## 14. The Confession at the Fire

There is a particular kind of cowardice that wears the costume of patience. I know it intimately. For eleven days I told myself I was waiting for the right moment to tell Ada Okonkwo the truth — that the right moment was a professional courtesy, like waiting for a level stretch of track before applying the brakes. In fact I was simply afraid, and fear is very good at drafting memos in favor of its own delay.

The storm made the decision for me, as storms do.

It came down the gorge at dusk with no manners whatsoever — one minute the river was running gold and quiet below our camp on the shelf of old ballast, the next the sky turned the color of wet slate and the rain arrived sideways, drumming on the canvas fly we'd rigged between two cuttings of the dead line. The porters hauled the boats above the waterline and doubled the lashings without being asked; Ngozi directed them with the economy of a woman who has argued with weather before and lost only the arguments she deserved to lose. Ada stood at the edge of the fly with her father's compass open in her palm, watching the river vanish into gray roar, and I watched her, and I understood that my window was closing faster than the light.

Because she had found the numbers. Of course she had. She'd spent three evenings cross-checking elevations by lamplight, her lips moving faintly over figures like a woman praying in a language only engineers speak, and this morning she had looked up from her ledger with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the expression of a person who has caught someone lying and is deciding how much it will cost to say so aloud.

She knew her father's survey had been falsified. She did not yet know *by whom*, or rather, she believed — reasonably, damningly — that the falsification was his. That he had cooked the figures out of incompetence or corruption or some private madness, and that the debt she carried was the interest on his disgrace.

The truth was worse. The truth was that the company had taken an honest man's honest numbers and bent them like rail iron, and then billed his daughter for the privilege of carrying the shame.

* * *

We ate under canvas — cassava and tinned fish and the last of the decent tea, hoarded against exactly such nights. The porters turned in early beneath the overturned boat hulls. Ngozi sat sharpening her machete with long, unhurried strokes, which I have learned is what she does instead of sleeping when something is wrong. And Ada sat across the fire from me with her notebook closed on her knee, and said, without preamble:

"Tell me about the orders, Mr. Vance."

"I don't—"

"Don't." Her voice was quiet, which from Ada is far more alarming than loud. "You've been polite for three weeks and careful for two, and today you couldn't meet my eyes over the elevation tables. I've been lied to by better liars than you, Elias. They were all wearing uniforms. So either you tell me what headquarters sent you up this river to do, or I'll spend tomorrow doing what I should have done at the depot — reading your behavior instead of your instruments. And I promise you my conclusions will be less flattering than your confession."

Ngozi stopped sharpening. She did not look up. She had the stillness of a person who has already chosen her side and is merely waiting for the rest of us to catch up.

I looked into the fire. It seemed only fair that if I was going to burn, I should at least study the flame.

"You're right," I said. "I'm carrying sealed orders. I've carried them since the depot."

"Read them."

"They're not written down. They're verbal. Which means there's nothing to confiscate and nothing to quote, which was rather the point."

Ada's jaw tightened. "Then say them."

And here is the thing I want set down plainly, because whatever else history makes of me, let it record that I did not lie to her when it mattered. I took a breath, and I gave her the truth in the order I had received it, because the order itself was part of the obscenity.

"Three months ago," I said, "the Hydroelectric Commission convened a closed session on the highlands scheme. Not the old line — everyone knows the old line failed. A new scheme. A dam at the upper gorge, reservoir flooding the valley above the falls. Forty miles of new track to service it. The Commission's projections depend on one document: the original 1909 survey of the valley floor. Elevation data, catchment figures, gradient profiles. The work of a surveyor named Okonkwo."

Her face did not move. Her hands, folded on the closed notebook, went white at the knuckles.

"The Commission needed those figures to be wrong," I continued, and my voice sounded very far away to me, like a man reporting on his own funeral. "If Okonkwo's elevations were accurate, the reservoir sits four meters shallower than projected, the output falls below viability, and the scheme dies in committee. If they were erroneous — if the surveyor was careless, or worse, dishonest — then the Commission can dismiss the old data entirely, commission its own survey, and proceed. So they needed the errors verified. Independently. By an engineer with no prior connection to the original crew, whose findings could not be dismissed as sentiment."

"Sentiment," Ada repeated.

"Your father," I said, "was respected. That was the problem. You cannot discredit a man's numbers while crediting the man. So they discredited both. There are files, Ada. There is a version of your father's story that has been circulating in headquarters for twenty years — that he falsified his own survey to cover cost overruns, that the closure of the line was a scandal hushed up to protect him, that the debt he died owing was the price of his fraud being forgiven rather than prosecuted."

"That's not—" She stopped. I watched her reach for the arithmetic she trusted more than any comfort I could offer, and watch the arithmetic betray her. Because she had found discrepancies. Real ones. Between her father's field books and the published figures. She had spent three nights assuming the fault was his.

"It isn't true," I said. "You already know it isn't true, because you've held his field books in your hands. His numbers were clean. I've seen the originals now — the ones you've been checking. The published figures were altered after the fact. Someone at headquarters took an honest survey and bent it, and then spent twenty years telling the world the bend was the surveyor's."

The rain hammered the canvas. Somewhere down the slope the river kept up its low, indifferent argument with the dark.

"And your orders," Ada said at last. Her voice was terrible in its steadiness. "Your orders were to verify the errors."

"My orders were to verify the errors," I said. "And to confirm the source route was passable for a new survey party. And—" I made myself finish, because half-truths were how I had gotten here "—and to establish, discreetly, whether any of Okonkwo's original materials survived upriver. Field books. Instruments. Correspondence. Anything that might contradict the official account."

"Anything that might *support* it, you mean."

"No." I met her eyes. "Anything that might contradict it. Because the official account requires his guilt, Ada. If his true surveys surfaced — if anyone independent ever saw that his figures matched the ground and the company's didn't — the dam scheme doesn't just die. It dies loudly. With inquiries. With names attached. Headquarters doesn't want his materials preserved. They want them accounted for. Lost. Burned. Absorbed into the general ruin of an abandoned line. My report was to state that nothing recoverable remained."

The silence went on so long that I heard the fire settle, heard Ngozi's blade resume its slow whisper against the stone.

"So," Ada said finally. "They didn't send me upriver to reopen a line. They sent me upriver to bury my father twice. Once in the files and once in the flood. And you—" and here her voice cracked, just once, like ice under load, and I would have taken that crack into myself gladly if she'd let me "—you read his field notes beside me. You handed me his maintenance log at the tunnel works. You watched me find him alive in those pages, Elias. You watched me understand that he stayed, secretly, after the closure, and you said *remarkable dedication* and poured the tea."

"Yes."

"Why?" It was almost gentle, which was the worst thing she could have done to me. "Why tell me now?"

"Because you found the discrepancies first," I said. "Because you're a better engineer than I am and you proved it in front of me every day for three weeks. Because at the tunnel works I watched you stand where your father stood and I understood that I wasn't traveling with the company's instrument anymore. I was traveling with the one person in the world who can end this, and I had been sent to make sure she ended it the wrong way." I looked at my hands. They were steady, which struck me as obscene. "And because I am tired, Ada. I have been tired since the depot. There is a kind of obedience that feels like integrity right up until you have to say out loud what you've agreed to, and tonight I had to say it out loud, and I find that I cannot carry it back down the river with me."

* * *

Ngozi spoke for the first time. "What happens to her. After. If the report goes the way they want."

I should have lied. Some part of me, the well-trained part, reached for the lie the way a hand reaches for a banister. But Ngozi was looking at me with the flat patience she gives rivers she intends to run anyway, and I found I had no banister left.

"There's contingency language in the expedition brief," I said. "Standard for remote assignments. If the senior engineer becomes unable to complete the survey — illness, accident, hostile country — the overseeing engineer assumes full authority and reports accordingly. The disappearance of an engineer with her history would be..." I searched for the administrative word. "*Regrettable but explicable.*"

"Disappearance," Ada repeated.

"Or death," I said, because we had passed the point where softening it served anything. "The country is dangerous. The radio is dead. Two porters have vanished. No one at headquarters will press hard on questions about a disgraced engineer lost above the falls. And her absence would close the last file on Okonkwo. Grieving daughter, follows father into bad country, pays the family's price. It ties the whole narrative off. There are people who would call it elegant."

"Elegant," Ngozi said, and spat past the edge of the canvas into the rain.

Ada had gone very still. When she finally moved, it was to open her notebook — not the ledger of debts, the other one, the working book — and turn to the pages of elevation figures she'd been checking. She laid it on the packing crate between us like a card played face-up.

"These are his true numbers," she said. "From the tunnel works log and the field books. I've checked them against every benchmark we've passed since the lower gorges. They're not just accurate, Elias. They're *beautiful*. He surveyed that valley with a care most men reserve for cathedrals. And somewhere in a filing cabinet in the capital there's a typed summary saying he was a fraud." She looked up. "Who signed the alteration? Do you know?"

"I have a suspicion. Suspicion isn't evidence, and I won't hang a name on it without documents. But the alteration is competent. It's the work of a senior man with access to the archives and authority over the closure file."

"Okoro," she said.

I didn't answer, which was an answer.

"He signed my dismissal papers too," Ada said, almost conversationally. "Two years ago. He wrote *lacks the temperament for field command* in the margin, and I hated him for a year, and now it turns out he was tidying me out of the way of something larger. Isn't that always how it works? The small cruelties turn out to be load-bearing."

The rain eased slightly, settling from assault into siege. The fire had burned down to a red heart. I became aware that I was cold, and that the cold was not entirely meteorological.

"Say the rest of it," Ngozi said to me. "Whatever you're still holding. A man confessing in installments is a man keeping change back."

So there was no hiding behind the canvas, no hiding behind the rain, no hiding behind the professional cadence of a report. I put my hands flat on my knees and finished it.

"I was also instructed," I said, "that if original materials were located, I was to take custody of them. For return to headquarters. For, quote, archival review, unquote. And I want to be precise about what that phrase meant, because I have thought about little else since the tunnel works. Archival review, in this context, means the documents never reaching daylight again. It means your father's true survey going into the same darkness where they put his reputation. I accepted that instruction, Ada. I sat across from you at the depot and shook your hand and accepted that instruction, and I told myself it was administrative housekeeping. I told myself a great many things. I was very good at telling myself things. It may be the only skill headquarters actually tested for."

Ada was quiet for a long time. Then: "The cache. At the source. You think that's what he left."

"I think a man who stayed secretly at a condemned tunnel works, maintaining a log in his own hand years after the official closure, was not doing it for exercise. I think he was preparing something. And I think everything we've seen — the charges never fired, the log, the route he kept passable — points upstream." I hesitated. "There's one more thing. About the porters."

She looked up sharply. "You know something."

"I know that the supply crate that went missing contained the survey instruments' spare cases. Empty cases, Ada. Waterproof, lockable, government-stamped. Somebody wanted containers that look official and seal tight. And I know that the night they vanished, I woke and heard movement near the boats, and I chose to believe it was animals, because believing it was animals required nothing of me." I rubbed my face. "I don't know who. I don't know if they're ahead of us or behind us or one of the twelve people sleeping around this fire. But I know that headquarters does not send one man to do this work. They send insurance. And I have begun to suspect that I was never the insurance. I was the alibi."

* * *

The fire burned low. Ada rose, walked to the edge of the canvas, and stood looking out at the drowned dark where the river ran. I let her stand there. Ngozi resumed sharpening the machete, which I understood now was less maintenance than meditation, or possibly threat display aimed at the entire situation.

When Ada came back, she sat down across from me and looked at me with an expression I will be turning over for the rest of my life — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but assessment. The look of an engineer evaluating a structure under load.

"Here's what I believe," she said. "I believe you were sent to do a filthy job and you did it competently for three weeks, and I believe competence is the most dangerous form of betrayal because it looks exactly like friendship from the inside. I believe you confessed tonight partly because you're tired and partly because you're frightened and mostly because I cornered you, and I am not going to pretend that makes it noble." She held up a hand before I could speak. "But. I also believe that a man who lies well can choose to stop, and that stopping is worth something even if starting was worthless. And I believe that right now, on this river, with a dead radio and half our supplies and someone in this party possibly working against us, I cannot afford to waste an engineer. Even one with your résumé."

"That's more mercy than I've earned."

"It's not mercy. It's logistics." Almost, almost, a smile. "Elias. If we find my father's cache at the source, and it holds what you think it holds — the true surveys, proof of the alteration — what do you imagine happens to the man who delivered it to headquarters? Do you think Okoro shakes his hand? The moment those documents exist in the open, everyone who touched their suppression is exposed. You wouldn't be the faithful servant. You'd be the witness. They burn witnesses last, but they do burn them."

"I've considered it."

"Have you considered what happens to *me* if you deliver them? Because your contingency language has me disappearing above the falls either way, doesn't it. Whether you find the cache or not. Whether I help you or fight you. The report writes itself; you told me so yourself." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the compass scar on her knuckle — a childhood story she hadn't yet told me, filed away with everything else I no longer had the standing to ask for. "So here is my proposition, Mr. Vance. Help me instead. Not because you love me — you don't know me well enough, and I certainly don't know you. Help me because it's the only move on this board that leaves anyone standing. Take your confession and aim it. Your testimony that the published figures were altered — your knowledge of the closed session, the contingency language, all of it — that's worth more than my father's field books alone. Documents can be stolen. A senior engineer of the corps testifying against the Commission cannot be quietly disappeared. They need you respectable for this to work. So stop being respectable."

The rain had thinned to a whisper. Down in the dark the river ran on, patient as arithmetic.

"Ngozi," Ada said, without turning. "You've heard all of it. Say your piece."

Ngozi set down the machete. "My piece is short. The white man talked like a man drowning, and drowning men grab whatever floats, and sometimes what they grab is you. I say we watch him. I say he carries nothing, knows nothing, and walks in the middle of the line from here to the source. And I say if he so much as hums the company anthem in his sleep, the river gets him and I write his mother a very polite letter." She picked the machete back up. "Also, we leave at first light. The water is dropping. We have maybe five days to reach the basin and get back to the falls, and I will not spend them listening to speeches."

"That's the whole of your counsel?" I asked.

"That's the whole of my mercy," Ngozi said. "Be grateful it exists. Most rivers don't offer it."

Ada looked at me across the dying fire, and extended her hand — the same hand I had shaken at the depot, in another life, under other flags.

"Well, Mr. Vance? Second chance. It comes once, it comes tonight, and it does not come with a receipt."

I took it. Her grip was dry and strong and utterly without warmth, and it was the most honest thing anyone had given me in years.

"One condition," I said, as we broke the shake. "When we find the cache — if we find it — I read everything first. Before you decide what to do with it. Because if there's anything in your father's papers that changes the picture, I'd rather learn it from the documents than from an ambush."

"Fair," Ada said. Then her eyes went past my shoulder, toward the black wall of the gorge, and narrowed.

"What is it?"

"Probably nothing," she said slowly. "A light. Upstream. Above the falls." She paused. "There shouldn't be anyone above the falls."

Above the roar of the falling water, faint and intermittent, something flickered where no camp should be — and Ngozi was already on her feet, machete in hand, counting heads in her sleepless, unhurried way, and coming up one short.
