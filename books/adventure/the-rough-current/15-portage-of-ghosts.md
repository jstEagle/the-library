## 15. Portage of Ghosts

The falls announced themselves a full day before we saw them — first as a tremor in the riverbed that came up through the soles of my boots, then as a sound like the sky clearing its throat and deciding not to stop.

"Great Falls," Ngozi said, when I asked what the maps called them. The maps called them nothing, actually; the company survey ended at a dotted line labeled *impassable — further investigation not recommended*, which is cartographer's language for *we were frightened*. "My mother's people call it the place where the river argues with itself."

"And who wins?"

"The river." She spat over the side, ceremonially, an offering to something. "Always the river."

We beached the remaining boat at dawn on a shingle bank below the thunder, and I stood looking up at what should have been a wall of white water and was instead a brown, muscular cataract the color of strong tea, throwing spray forty feet into the air. Somewhere above it, the river we had been climbing for five weeks went on being a river. Between us and it lay four miles of jungle, granite, and — according to the porters — ghosts.

I want to say I didn't believe in the ghosts. I want to report that I, Ada Okonkwo, daughter of a man who trusted numbers more than people, dismissed the whole business as superstition and got on with the hauling. But after everything — the dead radio, the vanished porters, the burned boat, Elias's confession still sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal — I had become a woman who listened carefully when people talked about spirits. Disbelief is a luxury of the well-defended. We were none of us well-defended anymore.

* * *

The portage, properly organized, is a problem in load distribution, gradient, and friction. I have spent my life solving problems in load distribution, gradient, and friction. So I did what I knew: I drew a plan in the dirt with a stick, assigned carrying teams, marked rest stations every eight hundred paces, and calculated that with twelve men — no, nine now, grief having subtracted three from our roster along with the boat and the fuel — we could move the boat, the crates, and ourselves around the falls in two days.

The porters looked at my plan the way men look at weather they intend to ignore.

Musu, the eldest, who had carried loads since before I was born and whose shoulders were a topography all their own, said gently, in the trade tongue Ngozi translated for me: "The engineer has made a map of the ghost road."

"It's not a ghost road," I said. "It's a game trail and an old porter route. My father's crews used it."

That landed harder than I intended. Musu looked at me for a long moment, and then he laughed — not unkindly, but with a sound in it like gravel shifting. He said something to Ngozi, long, and she translated:

"He says your father also made maps of this road. And he says your father walked it differently from other engineers. Other engineers counted paces. Your father stopped and greeted the old ones by name." A pause while Musu added something else. "He says you have his face when you are stubborn."

"I don't have a face when I'm stubborn," I said. "I have a face always. It's the same one."

Ngozi's mouth twitched. "He says that too is like your father."

So we went, and because there was no arguing with either the terrain or the dead, we went the way Musu wanted: slowly, with greetings called out at certain trees, with a handful of meal scattered at the base of a split boulder, with pauses at places that looked to my eye identical to every other place and were, apparently, not identical at all. I kept my own count of paces privately, in my head, because I am who I am, but I stopped saying so out loud. There is a kind of engineering that happens in ledgers and a kind that happens between living people and whatever else is listening, and I was beginning to understand that my father had been fluent in both languages, and that I had spent twenty years pretending the second one didn't exist.

* * *

The trail climbed away from the water through country that got older as it rose. The trees here were vast and patient, buttressed like architecture, hung with vines thick as hawsers. The light came down green. Underfoot, the path was worn smooth in a way that no game makes smooth — only human feet, thousands of them, over years. This was a road. It had simply been allowed to forget it was one.

Elias walked beside me for the first hour without speaking, which for Elias was a considerable discipline. Finally he said, "Your father came this way."

It wasn't a question. He'd read the maintenance log too, there in the tunnel dark — the entries dated years after the official closure, the careful hand recording timber inspections and drainage checks on a line that officially did not exist.

"For how long?" I asked. "The log runs six years past closure. Six years he came back to a dead tunnel and checked the drains."

"Longer than that, maybe. The log just stops. Stopping isn't the same as finishing." Elias ducked under a fallen trunk and held the vine aside for me, an old-fashioned courtesy that would once have irritated me and now felt like ballast — something steady in a wobbling world. "Ada. What I can't work out is why. If he'd wanted to stop the line, the sabotage did that. Why keep tending it?"

"Because sabotage decays," I said, and heard my own voice go strange. "A blown culvert washes out. A cracked bridge gets found by floodwater and finishes the job. If you want a line to stay closed quietly — to stay closed *plausibly* — someone has to maintain the ruin. Someone has to keep the lie in good repair." I stopped walking. The green light moved on the leaves. "He wasn't keeping the tunnel alive. He was keeping the story alive. The story that the valley beat the railway, that the ground was bad, the falls impassable, the project a noble failure. Every year he came back and renewed the argument."

Elias was quiet. Then: "Alone. All those years. Headquarters thought him incompetent or corrupt, and he let them think it, and he paid their price, and he never told anyone why."

"He told the river," I said. "And the river doesn't gossip."

* * *

We camped that night on a granite shelf above the roar, close enough to the falls that the spray reached us as a fine cool rain and conversation had to be conducted at a shout or not at all. Not at all, mostly. The porters built the fire in a ring of stones Musu arranged with particular care, and set a bowl of food beside it before anyone ate — for the old ones, Ngozi signed to me across the flames, the ones who walk here.

I sat apart, as I usually did, with my father's compass open on my knee. The brass case was worn bright at the hinge where his thumb had worked it ten thousand times. I had carried this instrument through disgrace and debt and half a continent, and I had always understood it as a tool. Sitting there in the fire-spit and the thunder, I understood it differently. It was a letter. It was the only letter he'd ever written me, and it said: *the needle points true even when the map is wrong. Learn which way north really is.*

"You're doing the thing again," Ngozi said, dropping down beside me. She had a way of arriving without arriving; I was learning not to startle.

"What thing?"

"Sitting alone with your face doing arithmetic." She took the compass from my knee without asking, turned it over, read the worn hinge with her thumb exactly where his thumb had been. "My mother died when I was small. For years afterward I argued with her. Out loud, on the water, whenever the river did something she would have handled better." She handed the compass back. "Then one year I noticed I'd stopped arguing and started answering. That's when I knew she'd finished teaching me."

"I never met his ghost," I said. "I've been walking beside him for days and he hasn't said a word."

"No," Ngozi agreed. "Ghosts of the dead are cheap. Every river has those." She nodded toward the falls, toward the black country beyond. "The expensive ones are the living you carry. He's not haunting you, Ada. You're haunting him. There's a difference, and the difference is that you can put him down whenever you're ready."

"I don't know if I want to put him down."

"Good," she said. "Then don't. Carry him properly instead. A load carried properly teaches you its weight. A load carried badly only teaches you resentment." She lay back and looked at the spray drifting silver through the firelight. "Tomorrow the hard portage. Sleep."

I slept, and dreamed of drains and culverts, of my father on his knees in the dark of the tunnel with a lantern, tightening a bolt on a lie, humming to himself, patient as masonry. In the dream I knelt beside him and handed him tools, and neither of us spoke, and it was the best conversation we had ever had.

* * *

The second day was the one the porters had been warning us about, though they had done it obliquely, the way you warn about a thing you cannot name directly. The trail left the river entirely and crossed a shoulder of country where the trees grew wrong — leaning, sparse, their roots gripping bare rock — and the silence between the falls' distant thunder had a texture to it, a held-breath quality. Twice, Musu stopped the column and stood motionless for a full minute, head tilted, listening to nothing I could hear. Both times we went on.

At midday we came to the place.

It was a clearing, or had been one once. Now it was a stand of young trees growing in unnaturally straight rows, and beneath them, half-swallowed by root and moss, lay iron. Rails. Dozens of rails, stacked in the pattern of a siding yard, rusted to the color of dried blood. And sleepers — hundreds of them, black with age, furred with fungus. An entire stretch of track, dismantled and carried up over the falls and abandoned here, four miles from anywhere, a century's argument about logistics ending in this green room full of dead steel.

"They brought it up," Elias said. His voice had gone flat, the voice he used for figures. "They hauled the whole extension over the portage. To build the highland section beyond the falls."

"And then they carried it back down," I said, "or most of it. Look — the stacks are half what the footings suggest. They built partway, and then they undid it." I walked the rows, counting automatically, my father's son in that moment if ever I had been. "This is where the company's story dies, Elias. Officially, the line failed below the falls. Ground conditions. Cost overruns. But they got the materials *up here*. Which means the failure wasn't the country. The failure was a decision."

"A decision someone made very quietly," Elias said, "and then buried under four miles of jungle and twenty years of paperwork."

"Two someones," I said. "One decided to build. One decided to unbuild. And the one who unbuilt it signed his initials on the original survey so that someday, someone like me would come looking and find the thread." I stood among the rusted rails with the young trees growing up through them, trees that had been seeds when my father last walked here, and I felt something in my chest come loose — not grief exactly, grief I had carried so long it had become structural, load-bearing. Something adjacent to grief. Recognition, maybe. He had known, when he falsified those elevations, that he was spending everything: career, name, his daughter's inheritance, his daughter's *opinion of him*. He had known and he had done it anyway, alone, and tended the secret like a fire through all those years, and never once written to tell me why, because telling me would have made me complicit, and he wanted me free.

He hadn't wanted me free. That was the thing I finally understood, standing in the ghost yard. He had wanted me *armed*. The debt, the disgrace, the compass — he had let me inherit the whole weight of it because a woman with nothing left to lose is the only kind of person who can carry the truth up a haunted mountain and decide what to do with it. He hadn't abandoned me. He had *loaded* me, the way you load a boat for hard water: everything essential, nothing extra, balanced for the current ahead.

"Ada?" Elias said. "You've gone somewhere."

"I've gone home," I said, which made no sense yet, and I didn't try to explain it. "Help me move these rails off the trail. The porters shouldn't have to step over them."

We worked together in silence, heaving the nearest rails aside, and the porters watched us do it, and something passed among them — I saw it, the small nods, the loosening of shoulders. Later Musu brought me a drink of water in his own cup, which Ngozi told me quietly was a thing you did for family. I drank it. It tasted of the river, of course. Everything here did.

* * *

We topped the shoulder in the late afternoon, and the country opened, and there it was: the upper river, wide and calm and golden in the slanting light, running easy through a valley of such ordinary, unbearable beauty that several of us simply stopped and looked. Beyond the falls, the river forgave everything. It spread itself thin and slow over gravel bars, braided and rejoined itself, carried egrets on its surface like paper boats. Somewhere up that gentle water was the source. Somewhere up there was my father's cache, and the truth, and the end of every road I had been walking since the coastal depot.

Ngozi stood at the edge of the shelf with her arms folded, looking at the valley with an expression I had never seen her wear — soft, and old, and guarded all at once.

"My mother's water," she said. "Two days up. Maybe less, if the dry season holds off." She glanced at me. "If it doesn't hold off, none of this matters, because we'll be standing here until the rains, explaining ourselves to fish."

"How many days do we have?"

She looked at the sky, at the water, at the light on the gravel bars, reading instruments I couldn't see. "Four," she said. "Maybe five. Up and back both." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. Four days to reach the source, find the cache, and return to the boats before the falling water stranded us on the wrong side of a hundred miles of rapids that were, even now, turning to stone.

That night, camped above the falls with the upper river glimmering below us like a promise, I took stock of what remained: nine porters who had earned my trust and whom I would trust with my life, one pilot who argued with rivers and won, one engineer who had burned his orders and chosen us, one boat, half a fuel store, four days, and a truth heavy enough to drown in.

Once, that ledger would have read to me like ruin. Lying under the stars with the compass warm against my sternum, it read like something else entirely. It read like a crew. It read, God help me, like a family — the kind you don't get given but get *made*, out in the hard water, where nobody can pretend anymore.

Above us, far upstream, lightning flickered once, twice, over the highlands — silent at this distance, a rumor of weather.

Ngozi saw it too. "Dry season's coming early," she said. "Whatever we're going to do, Ada — we do it fast."
