## 16. Dry Season Rising

The river told us before anyone else did.

I woke on the fourth morning above the falls to a sound I had learned to dread: the water speaking in a lower voice. A river dropping is not like a river rising. Rising water shouts—it roars, it shoves driftwood at you, it makes its opinions known. Falling water whispers. It pulls back from the banks like a guest making excuses to leave, and it exposes things you'd rather not see: rocks that had been polite enough to stay submerged, sandbars where none were charted, the pale scars of old channels that had died years ago and were now being resurrected in reverse.

I stood at the water's edge with my father's compass in my hand—I still carried it out of habit more than navigation—and watched the mark I'd scratched on a stone the evening we arrived. The scratch sat a full hand's width above the waterline. Two days ago it had been under by three fingers.

"Ada." Ngozi's voice behind me. She didn't say good morning. She hadn't said good morning since the sabotage; nobody had. Mornings now began with inventory instead of greetings, which tells you everything about how an expedition ages.

"I know," I said. "It's dropped."

"It has dropped *fast*." She crouched beside me and put two fingers in the current, reading it the way Elias read blueprints—quickly, completely, with a kind of proprietary irritation when it failed to behave. "This is not dry season. This is dry season with a grudge. My mother's people have a word for a year when the river leaves early. It translates roughly as 'the year you should have stayed home.'"

"How long?"

She was quiet for a moment, which from Ngozi was worth more than most people's speeches. "Coming up, this stretch took us five days against the current. Going down, with the water falling every hour? Three days to the lower gorge, if we run it hard and nothing breaks. Four if something breaks. Five if something breaks badly."

"And the source?"

"Two days up, maybe three, if the portage paths are passable. They may not be. The falls country is bad walking even in wet season." She stood and wiped her fingers on her trousers. "So do your arithmetic, engineer. You're better at it than me."

The arithmetic was ugly and I did it anyway, because that is what I am for. Upstream and back: five or six days minimum, assuming the cache—if it existed, if my father had left what the tunnel log suggested he'd left—could be found quickly. Downstream to safety: three to four days, shrinking daily. The window between them was closing like a door with a slow leak of air behind it. We could go up, or we could go home. We could not do both. Not with one boat, half our fuel burned, eleven mouths to feed, and a radio that had been singing static into the void for over a week.

I did the arithmetic twice. It came out the same both times, which is the cruelest thing about arithmetic.

* * *

We held council on the gravel bar, which had grown noticeably larger overnight—the river giving ground all along its length, conceding territory it had held for months. The crew sat in a rough crescent: me, Elias, Ngozi, and the nine porters who remained after the two vanished near the tunnel works. Their names I will not write here, because their families' names are not mine to publish, but I knew all nine by then. I knew which ones sang on the portages and which went silent, who hoarded tobacco, whose laugh could be heard across two gorges. An expedition strips you down until what's left is just people, and then, if you're lucky, you find you like what's left.

Elias spoke first, because Elias always spoke first—he had been raised in rooms where silence was considered a form of losing.

"The responsible decision," he said, "is downstream. We've lost the boat, the fuel, contact with base. Every day we wait, the descent gets harder. If we run the upper rapids at low water—" He stopped himself, which was new for him. He looked at his hands. "At low water, some of those channels simply won't be there."

"He means we die," Ngozi translated pleasantly.

"I mean the margin goes to zero," Elias said, with the patience of a man who had learned, somewhere between the storm canvas and the burning boat, to stop performing for her. "And I mean that whatever is upstream can wait. Stone waits. Paper waits. The company's schemes will still be schemes next season."

"Next season," Ngozi repeated. "You think they give us a next season? You think your headquarters writes us a second expedition after we come back with no boat, no radio logs, and a report full of words like *sabotage* and *falsified*?" She shook her head. "This is the season. There is only ever the season."

All eyes came to me. I had noticed this happening over the past week—not because I'd earned it, exactly, but because leadership abhors a vacuum, and both official leaders of this expedition had spent the last several days being visibly, catastrophically human. Elias had confessed his orders at the fire like a man coughing up glass. And I had stood in the ruins of our fuel store doing sums aloud while the others watched, and apparently that was enough. Command, it turns out, is mostly just being the person willing to say the numbers first.

So I said them.

"We have food for twelve days at half rations," I said. "Eleven people. One boat, patched twice. No radio. The descent window closes in"—I looked at Ngozi—"call it four days of safe running, five of desperate running, and after that the upper gorge becomes a staircase of rocks with a trickle between them."

"Generous," Ngozi murmured. "I'd have said three and four."

"Then call it three and four." I made myself look downriver, toward home, toward the coast, toward the hearing room where my testimony waited and my career would end in careful sentences. Everything I had come upriver to reclaim was down there. My name. My future. The debt paid off, the ledger closed, the brass weight lifted. And everything I had discovered—the falsified figures, the dam scheme dressed as a rail line, my father's secret kept for twenty years at the price of his reputation and very nearly mine—all of it pointed upstream, to a spring-fed basin where a dead man might have hidden the truth in a waterproof case, if he was the man I was beginning to understand he'd been.

My father had carried this alone. That was the thought that kept returning, the splinter under the skin. Twenty years of knowing, twenty years of letting the corps call him careless, letting them strip his name from the honor rolls, letting *me* grow up ashamed of him—and he never once put the truth down, because putting it down would have flooded the valley. He had chosen the river over his own good name, and I had spent my whole life resenting him for a sacrifice I hadn't known was being made.

Duty said: go down. Duty, as the corps defined it, had never meant anything but obedience wearing a clean shirt. I had been dutiful my entire life and it had bought me a demotion, a debt, and a posting nobody wanted.

But duty in another sense—the older sense, the one my father had practiced in secret and Ngozi practiced openly and the porters practiced by simply continuing to show up each morning—duty in that sense pointed up.

"There are villages up there," I said. "Ngozi's mother's people. Graves. Fields. Everything the reservoir would drown, and everything that would drown *with* it if the company's figures stand unchallenged." I turned the compass over in my hands. Its needle swung lazily, pointing north with the serene confidence of an instrument that had never once had to make a difficult choice. "If we go down without reaching the source, we testify about what we've seen, and the company calls us liars, and the valley floods anyway, and my father stays ruined forever. If we go up and find his cache, we come back with something that cannot be called a lie. Something in his own hand, with his own seals."

"And if we don't come back at all?" one of the porters asked. Musa, the eldest, who had walked beside me through the haunted country without complaint and who had earned the right to ask any question he liked.

"Then we don't," I said. "But I don't believe we won't. And I'll tell you why." I stood, because some things you have to say standing. "The river brought us this far. It dropped early to tell us the terms. I intend to meet them."

* * *

The vote, such as it was, took less time than breakfast. Nine porters, one pilot, one disgraced engineer, one penitent spy: ten for upstream. Elias raised his hand last, and I watched him do it, and understood what it cost him. He was voting against the institution that had made him, against every corridor and clubroom that had polished him into the man I'd resented on sight at the dock. He caught me watching and gave me a small, crooked smile—the first unguarded expression I'd seen on him since the confession.

"For the record," he said, "I think this is strategically insane."

"Noted for the record," I said.

"And I'm doing it anyway."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point of you, I've decided."

Ngozi snorted. "Decided. Like a route through a gorge. You engineers—you decide things about people the way you decide things about water, and then you're surprised when either one drowns you." But she said it while already moving, already rolling the tarpaulins, already giving the boat's patched hull a kick to test it, and I have learned that Ngozi insults you most tenderly when she agrees with you.

* * *

We spent the day preparing, and preparation, on an expedition that has lost half of itself, is a strange liturgy. We cached what we could not carry—sealed in oilcloth, weighted with stones, marked on my map in pencil that I would later have cause to bless: spare boots, the broken sextant, Musa's extra blanket, a tin of tea we could not afford the weight of. Each item buried felt like a small promise to the river: *we mean to come back for these.* The porters understood caching the way farmers understand seed. They worked without being asked, quick and silent, and once—only once—I heard one of the younger men humming, low, a walking song, and Musa hushed him gently, the way you hush a child at a graveside, and I understood that we were burying more than boots. We were burying the version of this journey that ended safely.

Elias and I checked the boat together in the late light. She was a good hull once; now she was a good hull with two canvas-and-resin scars and a personality. He ran his palm along the larger patch the way you'd touch a scar on someone you loved.

"She'll hold," he said.

"You sound surprised."

"I sound like a man managing his expectations." He glanced at me sideways. "Ada. If we get up there and the cache isn't there—if your father's last survey is a story we told ourselves to make the walking bearable—what then?"

I had asked myself the same question at three that morning, staring at the canvas overhead, listening to the river shrink in the dark. What then? Then we would have walked five days into dying country for nothing. Then the valley would flood on schedule and my father's ghost would keep its silence another century. Then I would walk back down and testify anyway, on faith alone, and be destroyed for it.

"Then we come back with nothing," I said, "and I tell the truth anyway, and it costs me everything, and at least I'll know I chose it instead of drifting into it." I looked at him. "That's the whole difference between my father and me, Elias. He never got to choose. Someone else held his ledger. I'm holding mine."

He was quiet a moment. "When did you become the sort of person who says things like that?"

"Somewhere around the second rapids," I said. "It was the capsize. Very clarifying, nearly drowning."

* * *

We launched at first light, going against the current one last time, and the river met us like an old adversary who has decided, provisionally, to respect you. Low water changed her character entirely. Where she had been deep muscle, she was now bone and sinew—channels narrowed to slots, boulders standing exposed like the vertebrae of something enormous, back-eddies forming in places the charts swore were clean water. Ngozi stood at the stern reading it all aloud in her flat pilot's voice, and her voice was the only calm thing on the river: *left of the white stone, hard right past the snag, paddle, PADDLE, ease—*

By midday we had made four miles that would have taken two hours in flood season. By evening, camped on a shelf of rock above a chute that roared even at low water, I got out the map and did the arithmetic again, because I am incapable of not doing it, and the numbers had worsened by half a day. The river was leaving faster than we were climbing.

Ngozi sat beside me on the rock, her knees drawn up, watching the water fail.

"My grandmother saw a year like this," she said. "Before I was born. The fish came late and thin, and the elders said the river had gone upstream to argue with its source." She smiled faintly. "I used to think that was just a story. Now I'm going to go ask the source myself whether it's been arguing."

"Do you think we'll make it?" I asked. "Both ways?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions about water. "I think the river doesn't care whether we make it. I think caring is our job." She bumped her shoulder against mine—an intimacy she permitted perhaps twice a year. "But I think your father chose this valley for a reason, and I think rivers remember the people who choose them back. So. Yes. I think we make it. Ask me again tomorrow."

Above us the stars came out, indifferent and magnificent, and below us the river ran away southward, lowering hour by hour, taking the safe way home with it. Somewhere upstream, two days' hard walking through the falls country, a spring-fed basin held—or did not hold—a dead man's truth in a waterproof case.

In the morning we would go and collect it.

I slept, eventually, with the compass in my pocket and the arithmetic finally, mercifully, finished. There was nothing left to calculate. There was only the walking.
