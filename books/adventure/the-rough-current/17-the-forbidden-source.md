## 17. The Forbidden Source

The river did not end so much as it forgot itself.

For three days above Ngozi's country the current had been thinning, spreading, losing its temper in shallows where we walked beside the boat more than rode in it. And then, on the morning of the fourth day, the channel simply gave up pretending, and we pulled the hull into a screen of ferns and went on foot, the way my father had gone, twenty years ago, along a track that was not a track.

I want to say I felt triumphant. I have read enough expedition journals—most of them written by men who never carried their own kit—to know that's what the reader is owed. But what I actually felt, climbing through that last green mile with my boots sucking at the mud and my lungs filing formal complaints, was a kind of dread dressed up as anticipation. Somewhere ahead of me was a basin of spring water and, if my father's cipher had not lied to me, a cache of papers that could hang a company or hang his name. Possibly both. Truth, I had learned, is not a lantern. It is a live wire, and it does not care who is holding it when it decides to bite.

Ngozi walked ahead of us both, and I noticed she had stopped talking.

This was, in itself, an event of geological significance. Ngozi argued with rivers the way other people argue with in-laws—constantly, fluently, and with an underlying affection that made the whole performance bearable. But since the falls, since the portage through country her mother's people called haunted, she had gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with tiredness. She moved like a woman entering a church where she knew everyone buried.

"Say something," I said, mostly to hear a voice.

"You first," she said, without turning. "You're the one walking toward your father."

That was Ngozi all over. She could gut you with nine words and keep rowing.

Behind me, Elias said nothing at all, which was its own kind of eloquence. Since the confession at the fire—since he had told me what headquarters wanted done to my father's memory, and what they had sent him to do about it—he had been a changed instrument. The polish was still there; the polish was structural, I suspected, like lacquer on cheap wood. But underneath it something had been re-tuned. He carried the last of the fuel on his back and he had not once suggested, in the manner of engineers, that we turn back and reconsider our arithmetic. The arithmetic was bad. We all knew the arithmetic was bad. The dry season was eating the river a finger's width a day, and every hour we spent going up was an hour we would pay for, with interest, coming down.

"Tell me again," Elias said, "why we didn't bring the porters."

"Because," I said, "whatever's in that basin, it was meant for family. And because twelve exhausted men make a lot of noise, and I'd rather not announce ourselves to whoever burned our boat."

"Charming. I'd hoped you'd say something noble."

"Nobility is what people call it afterward, when it worked."

He laughed—short, surprised, real. I was beginning to collect those laughs the way a miser collects coins, and I was not proud of it, but I was not going to stop either.

* * *

The track ended where all tracks end: at water.

The basin opened out of the forest without warning, the way a room opens behind a door you've been leaning against. One moment we were fighting through liana and the next the trees stepped back and there it was—a bowl of water perhaps two hundred paces across, fed by springs that rose through the sand in silver columns you could see from above, the whole floor of the thing lit and trembling like a held breath. The water was so clear it was almost rude about it. Every stone on the bottom had a color and an opinion. Where the springs surged, the surface dimpled and shivered but never broke, and out of the far side, calm as a man leaving a meeting he's decided to be bored by, flowed the river. Our river. The whole bad-tempered, magnificent argument of water we had spent six weeks climbing.

It did not look like the beginning of anything. It looked like the middle of forever.

"There," Ngozi said.

She didn't point. She didn't need to. On the near shore, half-swallowed by a fig tree, stood a cairn of stones—and not a village cairn, not a grave-marker. I knew the geometry the way you know your own handwriting, because I had grown up watching it being made. My father built his cairns like he built his embankments: three courses, keyed corners, a capstone set with the long axis pointing at whatever mattered.

The capstone pointed at the water's edge, at a fallen log, and under the log—

"Give me a minute," I said, and my voice came out strange, and neither of them pretended not to notice, which was its own mercy.

I knelt in the shallows. The log was soft with rot and I lifted it in pieces. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with pitch, lay a tin box the size of a bread loaf, and on the lid, scratched with a scriber in the neat capitals he'd used on every drawing I'd ever seen of his:

A.O. — FOR A.O. — WHEN SHE COMES

When. Not if. He had known. He had sat on this shore with his tin box and his falsified figures and his ruined name, and he had written *when she comes* the way other men write *return address*, as if his daughter arriving at the forbidden source of an unnamed river were simply a matter of scheduling.

I did not cry. I want that on the record. The water was in my eyes because I was kneeling in the shallows, and the shallows, as everyone knows, are notorious for it.

* * *

The oilcloth had done its work. Inside the tin, dry as the day they were sealed: a notebook in my father's hand, three rolled sheets of survey paper, a set of leveling tables, and—this was the detail that stopped my breath—a company letterhead, original, with a signature I recognized from the files in the survey office. The director of works himself.

I spread the papers on a flat rock at the basin's edge, and the three of us knelt around them like conspirators, which, in the eyes of the company, we now unambiguously were.

"Read it out," Ngozi said. "Slowly. I want to hear it in the air."

So I read.

The notebook was the true survey. Not the published one—*that* one, the one I had grown up ashamed of, the one with the "errors" that had ended my father's career and started my debt. Here were the real elevations, cross-checked three ways, dated, initialed, and annotated in a hand that grew smaller and angrier as the pages turned. The company's dam scheme—the one the new line was meant to serve, the one the reservoir would drown Ngozi's mother's country for—required a catchment wall at a height that would flood the valley to the gorge. My father had measured the valley. The valley did not care for the company's arithmetic. The true figures showed the reservoir would overtop its proposed wall in the first serious wet season, drown twice the land on record, and kill, in the company's own internal estimate—there it was, in the director's own memorandum, initialled—a number of people the memorandum described as "acceptable displacement, subject to revision."

"Subject to revision," Ngozi repeated. She said it the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "They wrote it down. They *wrote it down*."

"The figures were never wrong," I said, and heard my own voice shake. "His were right. The company's were wrong. They didn't falsify his survey—they published their own and buried his, and when he refused to sign off, they ruined him for it. The 'errors' were the truth. The debt—the debt was the price of him not signing."

Elias had gone very still. I had learned to read his stillnesses by now; this one was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he does not like.

"If that's true," he said carefully, "then the file I was shown—the 'verified discrepancies'—"

"Was manufactured," I said. "Or cherry-picked. They needed the old survey discredited twice, Elias. Once to bury the line, and once more, now, to justify the new dam. A dead man's errors are very convenient. Dead men don't file objections."

The basin said nothing. The springs rose and trembled. Somewhere in the fig tree a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge and thought better of it.

Ngozi put her hand flat on the memorandum, on the director's signature, as if she could hold the man's hand to the fire through the paper. "My mother's village," she said quietly. "The graves. The whole of it. 'Acceptable displacement, subject to revision.'" She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "You understand what you're holding, Ada. This isn't a document. It's a verdict. Whoever carries it downriver owns the future of this valley—and the company will do whatever companies do to people who own things."

"I know."

"And if you give it to the coast, to the newspapers, to whatever tribunal they invent to look busy—it comes out in some hearing room, in some language nobody here speaks, ten years from now, after the wall's already built. I've seen how justice travels, Ada. It travels by rail, and it always arrives after the flood."

"Then what would you do with it?"

"Leave it here." She said it without hesitation, and that frightened me more than anything in the tin. "Leave it with the people it's about. Let it come out when it can come out *with* them, not *to* them. A truth that arrives early is just another flood."

* * *

We argued for a long time. I will not reproduce it all; some arguments are private even when conducted in company, and this one was mostly me arguing with a dead man while two living people watched.

Because here is what the tin box did not contain, and what I kept turning over like a stone looking for what lived under it: absolution. I had carried his debt for eleven years. I had walked into the survey office with my head down and my hands out and let men who couldn't read a contour map call me *the saboteur's daughter* to my face. I had come up this river half to finish the line and half, if I am honest, which I am trying to be, to find some version of my father that I could forgive. And what I had found instead was a man better than the one they'd ruined and more stubborn than the one I'd remembered, a man who had chosen the valley over his own name and then, with a father's particular cruelty, left the whole unbearable weight of it in a tin box addressed to *when she comes*.

"You could take it down," Elias said at last, into the silence. "The testimony route. Your report, my corroboration. It ends both our careers, and maybe it saves the valley, and maybe it doesn't. Or—" he hesitated, and I saw him choose his next words with the care of a man defusing something, "—or there's a third thing. There's always a third thing. There's a thing I haven't told you."

Ngozi's head came up. Mine did too.

"Elias," I said, in the voice I use on bridges that are making sounds they shouldn't.

He looked at the tin box. He looked at the basin, at the springs rising through the sand, at the far side where our whole river walked out into the world wearing its innocence. And when he spoke, his voice was not the polished one.

"Before you decide," he said, "you should know what I was actually ordered to do with that cache. Not verify. Not retrieve." He drew a breath. "Ada—there's a part of my orders that changes everything, and I should have burned them at the falls, and I am going to burn them right now, in front of you, and then you can decide what to do with me."

From inside his coat he took the oilskin packet I had watched him guard for six weeks like a second heartbeat.

The springs rose. The river left. And Elias Vance, the most careful man I have ever known, knelt down at the water's edge and struck a match.
