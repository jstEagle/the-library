## 19. The Choice at the Source

The cache was a tin box—nothing more than that. A biscuit tin, rusted at the corners, the kind of thing a clerk might keep pencils in, and it had waited twenty years in a dry hollow beneath an overhang of granite while empires rose and fell and my father's name rotted in company ledgers two thousand miles downstream.

I sat with it on my knees for a long time before I opened it.

The basin around us was so quiet I could hear the spring itself breathing. That's what a source is, when you finally reach one—not a dramatic gush from a mountainside, not the picture on a schoolroom map—but a slow seep of water rising through moss and gravel, gathering itself into a pool no wider than a dining table, then spilling over a lip of stone to begin its long argument with gravity. The river that had tried to drown us twice, that had carried us and battered us and taught Ngozi every word she knew, began here as something you could cup in both hands.

Elias stood a few paces off, hat in his hands like a man at a funeral. Ngozi had climbed to the lip of the basin and was looking back down the valley we'd come up, reading the country the way other people read newspapers.

I lifted the lid.

Inside: a canvas roll of survey sheets, wrapped in oilcloth and still supple after two decades. A leather notebook gone stiff as board. And beneath them, a small brass instrument case holding a theodolite lens and—my throat closed—a second compass. Not mine. Mine had been pressed into my hands at fourteen, along with the ledger of debts. This one had stayed here, hidden, waiting. Two compasses, two lives, one man split between them.

"He meant for someone to find this," Elias said quietly. "Or he meant for no one to. He'd have known either way was possible."

"He meant for the truth to outlive him," I said. "That's different."

I unrolled the sheets in the sun. My father's hand was unmistakable—the same forward-leaning numerals I'd grown up watching march across kitchen-table calculations, the same habit of underlining a figure twice when it mattered. But these figures were not the ones in the published survey. The published survey, the one the company had built its reservoir scheme on, showed the valley floor sitting comfortably below the proposed dam crest, the drowned land measured modest, the displaced villages few. These sheets showed the truth: contours running higher, catchment broader, the flood line climbing into country where people lived and buried their dead. And in the margins, in pencil, dated entries that tracked month by month as the company's own engineers revised their numbers downward—falsified them downward—to make the drowning look small enough to sell to a distant board.

They hadn't just inherited his errors. They had manufactured their own, and planned to hang them on a dead man.

"You understand what this is," Elias said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because I needed to hear myself say it.

"It's the end of my career or the beginning of my name. Depending entirely on who reads it first."

* * *

We made camp that night above the pool, because descending by dark was how men died on this river, and because none of us, I think, wanted to leave yet. The porters from Ngozi's mother's village—who had appeared that morning out of the high forest as if summoned, four young men with ropes and easy laughter, greeting Ngozi in a language full of soft consonants—built a fire and argued cheerfully about whose fish-trap would feed us. The tin box sat between me and the flames.

Ngozi came and squatted across from me. She looked at the box the way she looked at water: assessing, unhurried, certain there was more in it than met the eye.

"So," she said. "Now you carry it down."

"Do I?"

She raised an eyebrow. "You walked three weeks up a dying river to find it. You don't walk all that way to leave a biscuit tin in the bushes."

"Maybe I do," I said. "Maybe the walking was the point."

"That is a very engineer thing to say." She fed a stick to the fire. "Ada. Listen to me, because I will only say this once and then I will go eat fish and let you brood, which you are very good at. If you take those papers down the river, what happens?"

I had been playing it through all afternoon, the way you play a gradient, and I knew the arithmetic cold. "I deliver them. To the Resident, to the press, to whoever will stand still long enough. There's a scandal. For perhaps a season. Then the company lawyers arrive, and the papers are called forgeries, and the man who forged them is conveniently dead and cannot defend them, and the woman who delivered them is already disgraced once, which makes her a reliable liar in any court that matters. The dam gets built anyway. The valley drowns. And everyone who helped me carries the mark of it."

"And if you leave them here?"

"Then nothing happens. Nothing happens for years, maybe forever. The dam gets built anyway, probably. The valley drowns anyway." I turned the brass compass over in my fingers. "Unless—"

"Unless," Ngozi agreed, "the people it belongs to keep it. My mother's people. The villages upstream. They know every grave that would go under that water. They know the old men who remember your father walking this country with his instruments. Papers rot, Ada. Memory doesn't, not here. One day—and I don't know when, but one day—there will be a hearing, or a new government, or a flood too big to hide, and someone will need to say *we told you, we always knew, here is the proof.* And they'll be able to say it. Because a stubborn woman left it with them instead of spending it on her own name."

"It could be lost," I said. "Floods, fire, termites, one careless grandson."

"It could be spent and wasted," she said. "Which is worse. Evidence used too early dies. Evidence kept waits." She stood, brushing off her knees. "You are not the only one who can read a river. Think about where the current actually goes."

She went to eat her fish, as promised, leaving me alone with the fire and the tin and twenty years of my father's silence.

* * *

Elias found me later, after the porters had rolled into their blankets and the fire had settled into red coals. He sat down without asking, which was new; three weeks ago he'd have asked permission to breathe near me. The mountain air was thin and cold and the stars were close enough to insult.

"I've been thinking about my orders," he said.

"The ones you burned."

"Burning them didn't burn them out of my head, unfortunately. I have an excellent memory for things I'm ashamed of." He drew a breath. "Headquarters' plan, if I'd delivered the cache, was elegant. The documents surface, they're authenticated just enough, and the failure of the original line is laid at your father's door—his falsifications, his sabotage, his desertion of the works. Grief for the poor deluded engineer. Sympathy for the company he wronged. And then, with the record 'corrected,' the new dam proceeds on the corrected—meaning falsified—figures, and nobody alive has standing to object. Your family name becomes the foundation stone. Literally poured into the concrete."

"You've mentioned this."

"I haven't mentioned the part that's been keeping me awake since the tunnel works." He looked into the coals. "There was a contingency. If the cache couldn't be retrieved—if it was destroyed, or lost, or never found—then the expedition's misfortune would serve nearly as well. An expedition lost upriver, engineer Okonkwo presumed dead alongside her disgraced father's secrets, closes the question even more cleanly. No evidence, no witness, no scandal. The dam proceeds on schedule." He paused. "You notice the shape of it. Either outcome serves them. Deliver the papers and they bury you with them. Fail to deliver and they bury you without them. The only move on the board that isn't theirs is the one where the papers simply never enter their game at all."

I stared at him. "How long have you known that?"

"Since the rainstorm. Since before the confession. Since—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—since roughly the moment I accepted the assignment, if I'm honest, though I dressed it up as duty for a remarkably long time. Duty is wonderful that way. It lets you do terrible arithmetic and call it loyalty."

"And now?"

"Now I think Ngozi is right, and I hate it, because I am a company man to my bones and everything in me wants to carry that tin downriver in triumph and watch the boardrooms burn." He turned to face me fully. "But triumph isn't on offer, Ada. It never was. What's on offer is a choice between spending the truth cheaply now, on our terms, where it will be crushed—or banking it where it can't be reached, on the valley's terms, until the day it can't be ignored. I know which choice the man who hid these papers would want. He didn't hide them downstream. He hid them here, among the people he was protecting. He already made your decision twenty years ago. All you're doing is honoring it."

"That's a comfortable way to think about it," I said. "That I don't get to choose at all."

"No," Elias said. "It's the hardest way to think about it. Because choosing to spend yourself on your own redemption—that's easy. Any fool can be a martyr with an audience. Choosing to be forgotten, on purpose, so that other people can one day speak—that takes a kind of courage I genuinely don't know if I have." He smiled, tired. "I'm rather hoping you do, because I intend to follow your lead, and I'd hate to follow it somewhere cowardly."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I took the survey sheets out one at a time and read them by coal-light, and what I read was my father—not the diminished man of the debt ledger, not the silent figure who'd come home smelling of river mud and secrets, but the engineer he'd been before the company taught him what his skill was worth to them. Every sheet was a love letter to this valley. He hadn't just recorded elevations; he'd recorded the villages, drawn the paths between them, noted the fishing grounds and the ford points and the sacred groves with the same precision he gave to gradients. Somewhere in the middle weeks of that last survey, he had stopped working for the company and started working for the country. The falsified figures weren't a betrayal of his profession. They were the most professional act of his life: a lie so carefully constructed it had held for twenty years, bought with his reputation, his livelihood, and finally, I understood now, with the debt that had been transferred to me like an inheritance of chains.

Except it wasn't chains. It had never been chains. It was a relay baton. He couldn't finish the race, so he passed it forward and trusted the current to carry it to someone with strong enough arms.

He'd trusted me. Before I'd earned it, before I'd even understood, he'd looked at his sharp-tongued daughter who read track gradients at the dinner table and thought: *she'll come up the river one day. She won't be able to help herself.*

Well. He wasn't wrong. I resented it thoroughly, and he wasn't wrong.

Near dawn I did the last piece of arithmetic, the one I'd been avoiding. If I went downriver with the cache, the best case—the absolute best case, requiring honest officials, a free press, and weather in the courtrooms—was a delayed inquiry, a suppressed report, and my name half-restored in a profession that had already eaten me once. The worst case was the dam built on my father's corpse. If I left the cache here, the best case was a future I would likely never see: the valley speaking for itself, with proof in its keeping, on some morning none of us could predict. The worst case was the same dam, built the same way—but with the truth still alive somewhere, waiting, patient as water.

An engineer learns early that you don't design for the best case. You design so that the structure survives the worst one. My father had designed for survival. Twenty years in a tin box, and the paper still supple.

I closed the roll and tied the oilcloth the way he'd tied it, corner over corner, and I knew.

* * *

The village above the falls—Ngozi's mother's people—received us in the middle of the next day, and I made my request to the headman with Ngozi translating, though halfway through she stopped translating and started simply talking, because it turned out I'd been saying it wrong and she knew how to say it right. What I asked was this: that they keep my father's work, not as a gift and not as a weapon, but as a seed. Buried, tended, and brought out only when the ground was ready—when there were ears that would hear, a court that would listen, a moment when the truth could grow instead of die. I asked them to add to it, year by year: names, graves, memories, photographs if such things ever came upriver. A living archive. Proof that grew instead of faded.

The headman was an old man with a voice like the bottom of the river. He listened to all of it, looked at me for a long moment, and then said something to Ngozi that made her laugh in surprise.

"What?" I demanded.

"He says your father made the same speech. Nearly word for word. In 1909." She shook her head slowly. "He says the Okonkwo family argues with rivers and hides papers, and that they are welcome here, and that the tin will be kept in the place where the ancestors keep their things, and that when the time comes, the valley will speak." She paused. "He also says you owe them a visit every dry season, to check the oilcloth. Apparently that part is non-negotiable."

"Tell him," I said, and found my voice had gone rough, "that it's the first appointment I've ever accepted gladly."

We buried the cache that evening in the ancestor place—a stone chamber in the hillside, cool and dry, guarded less by locks than by the fact that no stranger would ever find it and no friend would ever betray it. Elias stood beside me with his hat off again. When the stone rolled back into place, he said, quietly, "Your father would have liked that it was engineers who brought it home."

"My father would have liked," I said, "that it was family."

And then, because the moment was too large to survive unbroken, Ngozi clapped her hands and announced that if we intended to run the falling water back down her river, we had perhaps four days of margin and no business wasting them on ceremony, and the solemnity shattered into packing and rope-coiling and the beautiful ordinary noise of people getting ready to fight a river together.

That night I took out my own compass—my father's first gift, the one that had started all of this—and set it on my knee by the fire. It pointed north, as it always had, faithful and stupid and true. But for the first time since the coastal depot, since the ledger and the humiliation and the long gray corridor of my disgrace, it didn't feel like a debt.

It felt like a heading.

Above us, the stars wheeled over the source of the river, and somewhere in the dark stone of the hillside, my father's truth slept in the keeping of the people he'd chosen over the company—the same choice, I realized, drifting toward sleep myself, that I had just made. The descent would try to kill us. The low water was coming fast, and the rapids below the falls ran harder falling than they had rising, and Ngozi had already said, with a pilot's flat cheerfulness, that the river would be "opinionated."

Four days. One damaged boat. Three hundred miles of falling water.

I tightened the straps on my kit and went to help load the boat.
