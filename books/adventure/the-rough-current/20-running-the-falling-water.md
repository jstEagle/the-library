## 20. Running the Falling Water

The river had dropped a foot overnight. I knew it the way you know a fever in your own body—by feel, before any instrument confirms it. The waterline on the rocks below camp sat exposed and pale as bone, a clean band of stone that hadn't seen air since the last dry season. Ngozi stood at the edge of the water with her arms folded, reading the current the way I read a gradient chart, and whatever she saw there made her mouth go thin.

"That channel's gone," she said, pointing to the left-hand run through the first gorge. "Two days ago it had four feet over the sill. Now it has rocks."

"Then we take the right channel," Elias said.

"The right channel has a hole in it the size of a church."

"A hole we went around coming up."

"Coming up," Ngozi said, "we had water to spare. Going down, the river doesn't forgive arithmetic errors." She turned to me. "Engineer. You want to argue with her too?"

I looked at the gorge, then at our boat—the one surviving boat, patched with canvas and pitch and a length of copper sheeting we'd hammered flat from the wreck of the second—and did the arithmetic she didn't need help with. Six days of food if we were careful. Four if we weren't. The porters who remained would walk the lower trail; they'd done it before, and they wanted no part of what the river was about to become. That left three of us and one wounded boat against sixty miles of falling water that was falling faster every hour.

"We run it," I said. "All of it. Today, while there's still enough water to float us over the ugly parts."

Ngozi almost smiled. "The engineer finally learns: when the river offers you a bad choice, take it quickly."

* * *

We loaded light and wrong-footed everything on purpose—cargo forward, weight low, the patched hull sitting deep where the old damage was so the new seams wouldn't work open. It was the kind of loading you do when you expect the boat to be underwater more often than not, and I tried not to think about how naturally the calculation came to me now.

Elias took the bailer. He'd earned it honestly; his hands had been on that tin pail for two days straight, and he'd stopped complaining somewhere around the third hour of the first day, which I counted as a kind of moral progress for a man raised in carpeted offices. Ngozi took the stern. I took the bow paddle, because someone had to tell her what the water was doing ahead, and because—if I'm honest—I wanted my hands busy when the fear arrived. Idle hands have time to shake.

The first gorge came up fast, the way gorges do, all shadow and noise, the walls closing until you could hear your own breathing come back off the rock. The right-hand channel was exactly as advertised: a chute of green water funnelling toward a seam of white that marked something bad underneath.

"Hole," I called back. "Left of center. Hard left after the seam."

"I see it," Ngozi said, which was her way of saying *stop telling me my job*, and I shut up and paddled.

We hit the seam and the boat did what boats do—it went where the water told it, and the water told it left, straight into the hole. For one long, unhurried moment we hung on the lip of it, the bow lifting, the whole grey-green face of the drop visible beneath us like a wall of moving glass, and I remember thinking, absurdly, of my father's compass in my shirt pocket, and whether brass could drown.

Then the world went to laundry. Water from everywhere, noise like a train yard, Elias bailing before we'd even finished shipping it, and Ngozi's voice cutting through it all—not shouting, never shouting, just *there*, level as a spirit gauge: "Paddle. Left. Now."

I paddled. The boat, half-swamped, sluggish as a drowned animal, answered anyway. We came out of the tail race sideways, rolling, shipping water by the barrel, but *out*—into a long pool where the current gentled and the gorge walls fell back and the sun found us again like it had been waiting with its hat in its hands.

Elias lifted the bailer, regarded the inch of water still sloshing in the bilge, and said, with tremendous dignity, "I've had worse baths."

"You haven't," Ngozi said.

"No," he admitted. "I haven't."

* * *

The middle miles were the cruel ones, because they weren't dramatic. They were shallow. The river spread itself thin across gravel bars that had been drowned on our way up, and now we scraped and ground and poled through channels barely deeper than a lie, the patched hull singing a grinding note against stones that I felt in my teeth. Twice we got out and hauled, waist-deep in current that pushed like a crowd leaving a fire, Elias and I leaning into the gunwales while Ngozi walked ahead reading the bottom with a pole, calling depths like soundings off a ship's bridge.

It was during one of these hauls that I understood something about her I hadn't let myself understand before. She wasn't fighting the river. She never had been. Every argument I'd heard her win against the water was really an argument she'd had with herself first—pride against prudence, speed against survival—and the river simply got the benefit of her having already settled it. My father had been like that with track, I realized. He'd walked grades at midnight because he couldn't trust the numbers other men gave him, and everyone called it stubbornness when it was actually just care, performed loudly.

"You're smiling," Elias said, hauling beside me. "Nobody smiles while hauling a boat."

"I'm having a thought."

"Dangerous. Keep hauling."

* * *

*

The great rapids—the ones Ngozi had named on our way up with a flatness that should have frightened me more than it did—came at mid-afternoon, when the light was going long and gold and lying about how much daylight we had left. She beached us above them and walked the bank for twenty minutes, alone, and came back with her jaw set in a way I'd learned to translate.

"It's runnable," she said. "Barely. One line through, down the right of the island, then hard across before the second drop or the water pushes you onto the sieve." The sieve was a shelf of jagged rock the river poured through like gruel through cloth. On our way up, it had been a roaring white line a hundred yards wide. Now it was exposed, black and wet and hung with debris, and somehow that was worse—visible evidence of what the water intended.

"And if we miss the crossing?" Elias asked.

"Then the river keeps us," Ngozi said simply. "That's the arrangement. It always was. We've just been lucky about the terms until now."

We ate the last of the smoked fish sitting on the shingle, none of us saying anything about it possibly being a last meal, all of us thinking it. Then Elias did something I didn't expect. He put his hand over mine on the gravel and said, quietly, "Whatever happens down there—I don't regret a mile of this. Not one."

"You will if we hit the sieve."

"Ada."

"I know," I said. "Me neither. But if you make me say it plainly I'll cry, and crying is poor ballast."

He laughed, and the laugh broke the thing that had been tightening in all three of us, and we pushed off.

* * *

I will not pretend I remember those rapids the way they happened. Memory in whitewater comes home in pieces, like cargo after a wreck. Here is what survived:

The island, sliding past on our left, its trees bent downstream like men listening.

The first drop, taken perfectly, Ngozi's paddle making small corrections that looked like nothing and were everything, the boat running the tongue of water so cleanly that for three seconds it was flying.

The crossing. God, the crossing. The current hitting our beam and shoving us sideways toward the sieve while Elias bailed and I dug the bow paddle in until my shoulders screamed, and the boat crabbing across that terrible smooth green slope inch by inch, the black rocks sliding closer, close enough to see the water combing through them in silver threads—

—and then the eddy behind the island taking us, catching the stern, swinging us round like a partner, and suddenly we were in slack water behind the drop, rocking gently, alive, the roar of the sieve receding behind us as though it had lost interest.

Ngozi laid her paddle across her knees. Her hands were shaking. It was the first time I had ever seen them shake.

"Well," she said. "She argued with me at the top. But she let us go."

Elias sat down slowly in the bilge water, which he had failed entirely to keep out, and began, very quietly, to laugh—a cracked, exhausted, magnificent laugh—and after a moment Ngozi joined him, and then I did too, all three of us laughing like lunatics in a leaking boat in the middle of a country no map admitted to, and I understood that this was what people meant when they talked about being born. Not the first time. Some later time, when the river decides you're worth keeping and changes its mind at the last instant.

* * *

We ran the last gorges in the dusk, by feel and by Ngozi's memory, and camped on a sandbar with the boat hauled high and the fire small. Downstream, maybe a day's travel now, the river widened into the country of the lower villages, and beyond that the depot, the telegraph wire, the world of ledgers and sealed orders and men who falsified figures in comfortable rooms. Everything we carried that mattered wasn't in the boat anymore. It was in the valley behind us, in the keeping of people who would hold it until the holding could do some good.

I lay awake a long time, listening to the water. It sounded different going down than it had coming up—or perhaps I was different, which amounts to the same thing. A river isn't an obstacle. My father had known it; Ngozi had always known it; I'd only just caught up. It's a ledger of its own, every drop accounted for, every debt flowing downhill toward the sea, and no one—not the company, not a dam, not a disgraced engineer with her father's compass—gets to balance it by force.

Near dawn I heard Elias turn over on the sand and say, sleepily, "What will you do? After?"

"Teach," I said. "Upstream. There are children in that valley who should learn to read water better than the company ever did."

A pause. "There's room in that plan for a man who can survey?"

"There's room," I said, "for a man who can bail."

His laughter drifted across the sandbar, and the river carried it away toward the coast, toward testimony and ruin and the plain truth I still had to tell—and for the first time since I'd boarded a boat at that coastal depot with a ledger of debts in my bag, the future didn't feel like a sentence.

It felt like a current. And I knew which way it ran.
