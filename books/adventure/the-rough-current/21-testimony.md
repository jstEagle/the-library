## 21. Testimony

The harbor at Port Meridian smelled of diesel and salt and other people's money, which is to say it smelled like every place I had ever been dismissed from. I stood on the quay with my report in a leather satchel against my ribs and watched the fishing boats come in, and I thought: this is the last morning I will ever be an engineer of anything.

There is a particular lightness that comes with that knowledge. I don't recommend it, but I won't pretend it isn't real.

Six weeks on the river had stripped me down to something simpler than the woman who had arrived at the coastal depot with her father's compass and a ledger of debts she hadn't incurred. The river does that. It takes your vanity first—it took mine somewhere around the second gorge, when Ngozi threaded us through a chute so narrow the boat's gunwales sang against stone—and then, if you're lucky, it takes your fear too, or at least teaches it some manners.

What it leaves behind is a person who can stand in front of a room full of men in linen suits and tell them the truth without her hands shaking.

Mostly without her hands shaking.

* * *

The hearing was held in the survey office where, eleven weeks earlier, a clerk with damp palms had slid sealed maps across a table to me and refused, three separate times, to explain why the highland line had closed. The same room. The same maps, unsealed now, spread out under the same buzzing lights. The same brass fittings gone green at the edges. Someone had hung a new calendar; otherwise the room had not moved in twenty years, which struck me as about right for this company.

They gave me a chair. They did not give me water, though someone had thoughtfully provided a carafe for the directors, who numbered five and sat along the far wall like a jury of well-fed crows. Director Ashcombe presided—I remembered him from my dismissal hearing two years ago, a man whose voice never rose above the volume of a man reading a menu. He had been the one who signed off on sending me upriver. I understood now that I had not been sent upriver to reopen a line. I had been sent upriver to disappear politely, and Elias Vance had been sent along to make certain the disappearing went according to plan.

"Miss Okonkwo." Ashcombe folded his hands. "You've submitted a written report. It is—forgive me—unusual."

"In what respect, sir?"

"It contains no recommendation."

I let that sit for a moment. Around the table, pens hovered. Stenographers sat with their heads bent, recording everything, which was the only reason I had written what I'd written: because a spoken accusation could be denied, but a filed document entered into testimony has a way of surviving even the men who wish it hadn't.

"The line cannot be reopened," I said. "That is my finding. A recommendation would imply there is a choice to be made. There isn't."

"Engineer Vance's preliminary assessment suggested—"

"Engineer Vance's preliminary assessment was written before he reached the tunnel works." I opened the satchel. "Before he saw the charges that were never fired. Before he read the maintenance log kept in my father's hand, dated fourteen months after the official closure. Before either of us walked the grade and found it sound. The line didn't fail, gentlemen. It was stopped. And the people who stopped it knew exactly why."

Ashcombe's face did something small and expensive—a tightening around the eyes, the look of a man watching a debt come due that he had believed forgiven.

"You are aware," he said, "of your position."

"I am aware of several positions, sir. Mine is currently unemployed."

Someone at the end of the table coughed to cover what might have been a laugh. I chose to believe it was a laugh.

* * *

I had rehearsed the report on the descent, running the falling water with the pages wrapped in oilcloth inside my shirt, and again in the porters' compound at Meridian while Elias sat across the fire sharpening nothing in particular, because a man needs his hands busy when he is deciding whether to burn his own life down. I had cut it twice. The first draft named names. The second named fewer. What remained was worse than either, because what remained was arithmetic.

I read it aloud. My own voice sounded strange in that room—river-roughened, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had spent two months being argued at by a current.

I told them the published elevations for the highland route differed from the original field survey by figures no instrument error could account for. I told them the discrepancy ran in one direction only: the company's numbers made the valley floor appear lower than it was, which made the proposed reservoir appear smaller, which made the dam appear cheaper, which made the scheme appear, on paper, worth doing. I told them my father's private surveys—the ones recovered from the cache at the source, the existence of which I described without describing their contents or location—proved the true figures. I told them the true figures meant the reservoir would drown not the empty country shown on the official maps but forty square kilometers of inhabited valley: villages, graves, the whole living architecture of a people the company had simply declined to put on paper.

I told them the line had been closed twenty years ago not because it failed but because my father, its chief surveyor, refused to certify a route whose completion would serve the dam—and that the debt recorded against his name, the debt I had spent two years working off, was not a debt at all. It was a price. Paid quarterly, in my labor, for his silence.

When I finished, the room was quiet in the way rooms are quiet when everyone present is calculating.

Director Okoro—youngest of the five, and the only one who had ever looked me in the eye—turned a page. "These claims regarding your father's debts. You have documentation?"

"My father's ledger. The company's own disbursement records, which I have cited by date and entry number. Cross-check them. I did."

"And the original survey data itself. Where is it?"

"Gone," I said pleasantly.

"Gone."

"Lost overboard during the descent. The river keeps what it keeps, gentlemen. Ask any pilot."

This was, strictly speaking, a lie. The truth was sleeping in a waterproof case in a stone chamber above Ngozi's mother's village, in the keeping of people who had lost a boat, a fuel drum, and nearly two lives making sure it got there. But the hearing was not entitled to the truth entire. The truth entire was a weapon, and weapons belong to the people who will actually fire them. I had given the valley its evidence back. What I gave the company was the confession they couldn't suppress: that the fraud existed, that it was documented, that copies existed beyond their reach, and that any future attempt to revive the dam would be answering questions they could not control.

It is possible to destroy a man with careful language. I had learned that from watching them do it to my father.

* * *

They called a recess, which meant they needed twenty minutes to decide how much of me to feed to the record and how much to bury. I stood in the corridor outside with my back against warm brick and watched dust move in the light from the high windows, and Elias came and stood beside me without saying anything, which is a skill he has genuinely mastered.

"Well," he said finally. "That went better than my forecast."

"Your forecast?"

"I gave it sixty percent odds they'd have you removed before the recess." He glanced at me. "I forget sometimes that you enjoy losing arguments slowly in front of audiences."

"I don't enjoy it. I'm just good at it. There's a difference, and the difference is how I feel afterward."

"How do you feel?"

I considered. The honest answer was hollowed-out and strangely clean, like a channel after floodwater. "Like a bridge that's been demolished on purpose," I said. "Structurally unsound for years, technically. Everyone knew. It's almost a relief to fall."

He nodded as if I'd said something reasonable instead of something that would have concerned a normal person. Then: "They'll call me next. I want you to know what I'm going to say before I say it, so it doesn't look like coordination."

"It will look like coordination. We traveled together for eleven weeks."

"It will look like the truth, then. Which is more dangerous."

* * *

Elias Vance resigned in that room, and I want to set it down properly, because whatever else history makes of him—and history, if it bothers with him at all, will make him a footnote to a scandal—he did not hedge.

He corroborated every figure. He confirmed the maintenance log, the unfired charges, the discrepancies, the dates. And then he placed his own sealed orders on the table—his actual orders, retrieved from the lining of his kit bag, the ones instructing him to verify the old survey's "errors," secure any recovered documentation, and file a report attributing the line's failure to the senior surveyor's incompetence, posthumously—with the specific contingency that Miss A. Okonkwo's disappearance upriver be treated as evidence of the same negligence.

He read the contingency aloud himself. He made the stenographer read it back.

Then he said, "I am resigning my commission effective immediately, and I am stating for the record that these orders constitute fraud, that I intended to execute them, and that I did not, and that the reason I did not is standing in the corridor and will deny having influenced me, because she is stubborn about things like credit."

Director Ashcombe looked at him for a long moment. "Mr. Vance. You understand that this statement ends your career."

"Yes," Elias said. "That was the general idea."

"You had prospects. Considerable ones."

"I did the arithmetic," Elias said, and I heard in his voice the echo of my own cold accounting on the night after the sabotage, when the fuel was burning on the water and the dry season was closing like a door. "The prospects were priced in currency I no longer accept."

* * *

Afterward we walked out through the depot yard, past the coastal line's rolling stock rusting in ranked rows, past the weighbridge where I had once, at nineteen, proudly certified a consignment of rails. The gulls were making their usual arguments overhead. Somewhere a ship's horn moaned, long and low, like the river's distant cousin calling from salt water.

"They'll bury the transcript," Elias said. "You know that."

"Of course. But transcripts have a way of leaking. Okoro took notes. The stenographers heard everything. And somewhere in this town there is always a journalist bored enough to dig." I shrugged. "I don't need it printed tomorrow. I need it to exist. Existence is enough. The valley can wait twenty years. It's already waited twenty."

"And you? Can you wait twenty years for your name back?"

I stopped at the yard gate and looked back at the sheds, at all that iron going quietly to ruin, and I found—to my genuine surprise—that I didn't want my name back. Not theirs. Their version of my name was a thing they issued and recalled like equipment, stamped with serial numbers, charged against ledgers. I had spent two years trying to earn back a badge and had walked out of the hearing lighter than I'd walked in.

"I'm not waiting for anything," I said. "That's the trick of it, Elias. They think they've taken my future. They've actually returned it. Unopened."

He laughed—not his polished drawing-room laugh, but the shorter one, the one I'd first heard on the water above the gorge when Ngozi called his navigation "competent, for a man who reads books about rivers." He was quiet a moment, looking at the harbor.

"Ngozi's boat leaves Thursday," he said. "She's taking cargo as far as the lower village. After that—" He turned. "After that I have letters to write and a family to disappoint thoroughly, and then, when the season turns, a river to get back to. If the invitation stands."

"It stands," I said. "Bring your own paddle. Ngozi charges passengers double."

* * *

Thursday came gray and early, the way departure mornings do, as if the world is embarrassed to watch you go. I stood on the quay with one canvas bag—everything I owned now fit in one canvas bag, and I had checked twice to make sure the weight felt right, and it did, it felt like freedom measured in kilograms—and my father's compass in my coat pocket, its needle swinging lazy and sure toward the water.

Ngozi was already aboard, arguing with a stevedore about the proper angle for loading crates, winning, as she always won, by being louder and more correct simultaneously. She saw me and jerked her chin toward the bow.

"You're late, engineer."

"I'm punctual. Your clock is ambitious."

"Same thing." She spat over the side, ceremonially, at the harbor. "Last chance. Salt water's soft living. Warm beds. Bread that isn't weevils' bread. You could teach in the city. Be respectable."

"I tried respectable," I said. "It didn't take."

"No," she said, and grinned, wide and white in the gray morning. "It never does, with our kind."

Elias came down the gangplank carrying two bags and a crate of books, looking like a man who had packed for a journey he'd been planning his whole life without admitting it. He handed me up over the gunwale, and his hand stayed on mine a half-second longer than balance required, and neither of us remarked on it, because some things survive precisely by not being said out loud.

Ngozi cast off. The current took us the way it always takes everything—without ceremony, without permission, indifferent and generous at once—and the harbor began to slide away behind us, the cranes, the sheds, the survey office with its green-brass fittings and its buried transcript, all of it shrinking into the haze where it belonged.

I stood at the bow with the wind coming upstream to meet us, salt giving way gradually to green, and I thought about my father, and the debt, and how it had been paid at last—not in money, not in years of labor, but in a single plain sentence read aloud to five men who wished they hadn't heard it.

The river widened ahead of us, brown and patient, running home.

Somewhere up in the highlands, in a stone chamber above a village that wasn't on any map, the truth lay sleeping in its oilcloth, waiting for the season when the valley would need it. It could wait. We had learned to wait. Everything that mattered, the river had taught us, moves at its own speed—

and always, always, finds its way down.
