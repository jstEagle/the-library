## 22. Where the Current Begins

The river did not remember me. I had counted on that.

I had spent the months since the coast rehearsing my return—on trains, in cheap boarding houses, in the long grey interval of unemployment where a disgraced engineer drinks too much tea and reorganizes her father's papers for the fortieth time. In my head, the valley would greet me like a courtroom: every face a verdict, every glance an accounting of what I'd cost and what I'd saved. But when Ngozi swung the boat out of the current and into the slack water below the village landing, the river simply took us in, the way it takes in rain, and the children on the bank were far more interested in my surveyor's tripod than in my reputation.

"Your fame is holding up well," Ngozi said, cutting the engine.

"They're pointing at the tripod."

"They're pointing at you. The tripod is incidental." She grinned at me over her shoulder. "Don't flatter yourself about the equipment."

I had missed her the way you miss weather—a constant you only notice by its absence. Four months of letters from Elias had been excellent company but poor substitute for someone who argued with rivers and won.

* * *

The school—if that was the word for a roofed platform with blackboards salvaged from the old line's way-stations—stood on the rise above the village, close enough to the water to hear it working all night. Amara, the headman's sister who had organized its building before I ever arrived, met me at the landing with the particular expression of a woman assessing whether the person she'd hired could actually do the job.

"You surveyed the whole valley," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I surveyed parts of it. My father surveyed more."

"And now you will teach our people to do what your people did with the surveys?"

I thought about how to answer that honestly. "I'll teach them to measure," I said finally. "What they do with the measurements is theirs."

Amara considered this for a long moment, the way she considered everything, as though weighing it against some private ledger of her own. Then she nodded once and picked up one of my crates. "Good. Because the men from the company came again last month. Asking questions. Writing things down." She glanced back at me. "They wrote down wrong numbers. On purpose, I think. So we would like to know the right ones."

That was the whole of my commission, stated better than any contract I'd ever signed. I carried my crate up the hill behind her and felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since the coastal depot—the day I'd walked in with my father's compass and a ledger of debts, offering myself to the lowest bidder.

* * *

Teaching, it turned out, was harder than engineering. Engineering forgave you if you were right; teaching required you to be right *out loud*, repeatedly, in front of teenagers who had no interest in pretending my chain-and-theodolite routine was fascinating when there was fishing to be done.

But there was Kofi, sixteen, who took to levelling the way I'd once taken to it—with the hunger of someone discovering that the world could be interrogated and would answer. And there was little Efua, who couldn't have been more than ten and followed the survey crews around asking why we always measured twice.

"Because everyone lies once," I told her, "including instruments. Especially instruments."

She looked at me with enormous seriousness. "Even your father's compass?"

I hadn't mentioned my father to her. The village had; that was the thing about this place. His name lived here—not as the saboteur the company would have made him, not yet as anything official at all, but as the engineer who stayed, who walked these banks after the line closed, who left things in the ground for reasons the elders still debated. To Efua he was simply a story with a compass in it.

"Especially his," I said. "He taught me to check everything. Including him."

* * *

Ngozi appeared most evenings, running goods and passengers between the villages upstream, her boat loaded with everything the company's maps said this country didn't contain. She'd take her supper on the school platform and critique my day without being asked.

"Your students staked the boundary wrong on the east field."

"They staked it exactly as I showed them."

"Yes. That's the problem. You showed them the coast way. Upstream boundaries follow the flood line, not the ridge. Your father knew that." She ate her fish with the serenity of someone delivering a verdict. "It's in his notes. I've read them."

"You've read his notes?"

"Ngozi's mother kept copies. From the year he stayed here." She shrugged. "You think you're the only archivist on this river?"

I should have been stung—my inheritance, my private grief, handled by other hands—but the truth was it felt like relief. For twenty years my father had been a sealed room in my life. Now he was distributed: part of me, part of Ngozi's family, part of the valley's memory, part of the cache still hidden where only three people alive knew to look. A man spread across so many keepers was harder to lose.

"The company men," I said. "Are they still coming?"

"Twice a month. Smiling. Measuring." She looked at me sideways. "They'll come less when the rains start. They don't like the falling water any better than we do." A pause. "Elias writes you?"

"Every fortnight. When the post boat runs."

"What does he say?"

I could have deflected. Instead I handed her the latest letter, because keeping secrets from Ngozi had proven, over one harrowing season, to be physically impossible anyway.

* * *

*My dear Ada,* the letter began, in handwriting that had improved since he'd stopped signing reports for headquarters. *London is grey and full of men who want to explain the highlands to me. I have taken a position with a firm that does drainage work in the fens—unglamorous, honest, and mercifully free of reservoir schemes. I told the interview panel I wanted work where nothing gets drowned. One of them laughed. I got the post anyway.*

*I have been asked—I am asked constantly—to consult on the upper valley project. I decline each time, and each time the fee offered increases, which tells you how worried they are. The villages' petition has reached the Colonial Office. Amara's people write a better letter than half the engineers I know. There is talk of an independent commission. Talk, in my experience, is water finding its level—it goes nowhere until pressure is applied, but it does not go backward.*

*Keep the true figures safe. Keep yourself safer. I have not forgotten what the dry season looks like from the inside, or what I promised you at the source. When the season turns and the commission convenes, I will come upriver. If you'll have me. If the river will have me—I am under no illusions about which of those approvals matters more.*

*Yours, E.V.*

Ngozi read it twice and gave it back with an expression I couldn't parse.

"He burns his own orders," she said, "and then he asks permission for everything afterward. That man is very slow to learn freedom."

"He learned it once at great cost. He's being careful with it."

"Mm." She stood, brushing fish bones from her hands into the current, where they vanished instantly, claimed. "And you? What will you tell him when he comes?"

"That the school needs a second teacher. That the fens can spare him." I looked out at the water going bronze with evening. "That I checked everything, including myself, and I'm certain."

Ngozi snorted. "Certainty. You engineers." But she was smiling as she went down to her boat.

* * *

The dry season came early that year, and hard. The river dropped through October like a held breath releasing, exposing gravel bars that hadn't seen sun in a decade, and the falls below the gorge began their annual transformation from thunder to murmur. The porters who'd run with us—the ones who'd stayed after the two were lost, who'd hauled the boat through the country they called haunted—came up to the school to see the maps I was drafting with Kofi and Efua and a dozen others.

We were mapping the valley ourselves now. Every village, every grave site, every flood line and fishing weir and footpath the company's surveys had never bothered to record. Amara had understood before I did what the documents were for: not a weapon, exactly, though they might serve as one, but a *record*. Proof that the country the dam would drown already belonged to someone. It is difficult, in a hearing room, to drown a place that has been measured, named, and signed for by its own people.

Kofi ran the transit now better than I did. I mostly checked his arithmetic and pretended not to be proud.

One evening in late November, he found me re-reading my father's maintenance log—the one from the tunnel works, dated after the closure, the document that had started all of this.

"Why did he stay?" Kofi asked. He'd earned the right to ask; he'd heard the whole story twice. "After the line closed. Why hide in the tunnel instead of just going home?"

I turned the question over the way I'd turned it over a hundred times, walking beside his ghost through the haunted country above the falls.

"Because going home meant letting the lie stand," I said. "And staying meant no one would ever know he hadn't. Both choices cost him everything. He picked the one where the river won." I closed the log. "I used to think that was defeat. Now I think it was the only survey he ever did correctly."

Kofi nodded slowly, the nod of a student filing something away for later use, which is the best kind of teaching there is.

* * *

The post boat came upriver on the first week of December, riding the last navigable water before the shallows closed the channel. Ngozi brought it in herself—she'd gone down to meet it, which she never bothered to do—and she came up the hill carrying not mail but a man.

Elias Vance stood on the school platform in a coat cut for London and boots entirely wrong for the mud, looking around at the blackboards and the staked fields and the children hanging off the railings, with the expression of a man checking elevations against a survey he'd memorized and finding, against all expectation, that the figures held.

"You look terrible," I said. "The fens agreed with you too much."

"The fens are flat, Ada. I've discovered I can't live somewhere flat." He set down his bag. Inside it I could see the corner of a drafting case, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, something boxy that I suspected was a theodolite he'd bought with his own money. "The commission sits in February. Amara's petitioners present in March. I've resigned my consultancy—there was nothing left to resign from, they'd stopped asking—and I find I have a skill set that happens to match a valley's needs." He paused, and for a moment the polished company man flickered through, uncertain, waiting to be assessed. "If the position's open."

"It's open," I said. "It's been open since the first letter. I was waiting for you to stop asking permission."

Ngozi, who had absolutely been listening from the base of the steps, called up: "He can bunk on my boat until the dry season ends. If he argues with the river, the river wins. House rules."

Below us the water ran low and clear over the stones, quieter than I'd ever known it, patient as a held promise. Somewhere upstream, in a spring-fed basin no map recorded, my father's true surveys slept in their cache, waiting for the moment the valley would need them—waiting, perhaps, for the commission, for March, for whatever pressure would finally make the talk flow downhill. We hadn't dug them up. We hadn't needed to. Some evidence keeps best where the current begins.

Efua tugged my sleeve and pointed at the compass on its lanyard around my neck. "Is that yours now? Really yours?"

I looked at it—brass worn smooth, needle steady, pointing not north but simply *true*, which is not the same thing and took me forty years to learn the difference.

"It's mine," I said. "Come on. The light's good. Let's go measure something."

And we went down together toward the water, where the current begins, and does not end.
