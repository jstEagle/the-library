## 1. Salt Debt

The freighter had been dead for eleven years, and Coral Ashgrove had been robbing it for three.

She thought of it less as robbery and more as estate work. Somebody had to sort through the belongings of the departed, and the departed — a rust-streaked coastal hauler called the *Merrow's Grace*, sitting in forty feet of green water off a coast whose name never appeared on charts without the word *restricted* stapled to it — had no next of kin to complain. Just Coral, her dive gear held together with prayer and neoprene cement, and a debt to the Harrow syndicate that compounded like a rumor in a small harbor.

She went down the anchor line hand over hand, the way she liked, no fancy descent. The water was cold enough this morning to make a point about it. Forty feet down, the *Grace* loomed up out of the murk like a cathedral that had lost an argument with God — hull on its side, superstructure collapsed into a heap of orange rust and soft shadow, a school of silver baitfish pouring through the broken wheelhouse windows like the ship had finally learned to bleed properly.

Coral hung there a moment and let the fish get used to her. That was the trick of salvage nobody put in the brochures: patience. The sea didn't reward strength or cleverness. It rewarded the person willing to be boring longer than everyone else.

"Boring," she said into her regulator, and her own voice came back to her as a wet, cheerful gargle. "That's your brand, Coral. Own it."

She worked the stern cargo hold first, because that was where the brass lived. The *Grace* had gone down carrying machine fittings, valve housings, a whole consignment of ship's instruments that the insurance company had written off and the navy had, for reasons it never explained to anyone, taken a sudden and passionate interest in keeping people away from. Which, in Coral's experience, was the surest sign in the world that a wreck was worth diving. The navy didn't patrol carefully around things that were worthless. Care was a form of confession.

The first valve housing came free with a grunt and a cloud of silt. She stuffed it into the mesh bag on her hip. The second was fused to a bulkhead by eleven years of coral, and she had to chisel it loose with the short pry bar she'd named, in a moment of sentiment she'd never admit to, *Persuasion*.

Persuasion did its work. The housing came away, and with it a fist-sized chunk of the bulkhead, which Coral decided to interpret as the ship's blessing rather than its objection.

An hour in, she had six housings, two porthole rings, a ship's bell bracket that made her heart do a small greedy dance, and a lamp fitting the size of a dinner plate. Brass, all of it. Brass was honest money. It didn't rust to nothing like steel, didn't rot like wood, didn't ask questions like the navy. It just sat in the dark being heavy and valuable, waiting for someone patient enough to come collect.

She was working the lamp fitting loose when the shadow went over.

It came across the water above her as a long, flat darkness, and Coral went very still against the hull, the way she'd learned to go still — not frozen, because frozen things got noticed, but *settled*, just another piece of wreckage, another soft gray shape in the green. The patrol launch. She could feel it more than hear it, the engine note coming down through forty feet of water as a deep, patient thrum, like the sea itself clearing its throat.

The navy ran this stretch twice a day, sometimes three. Never close enough to catch her. Never far enough to let her relax. Just careful. Careful in the way of a man who keeps checking that a door is locked, which tells you there's something behind it worth locking.

The shadow passed. The thrum faded toward the headland. Coral counted to two hundred, because counting was free, and then went back to the lamp fitting.

"Half," she told the ship. "Today I get you to half."

The *Grace*, being a ship and therefore a good listener, said nothing.

* * *

The arithmetic lived in a waterproof notebook in Coral's chart table, and she knew it the way other people knew prayers. Fourteen thousand marks owed to the Harrow syndicate, original principal nine, the rest interest and what Bram Harrow's clerks called *administrative fees*, which Coral suspected was a fee for the administration of continuing to exist near them. She'd been paying it down for two years at whatever the sea gave her. Some months the sea gave generously. Some months the sea gave her a torn net, a fouled prop, and a lesson in humility, and the notebook's numbers went the wrong way.

Today's haul, if the brass buyers at Port Callow paid fair — and the Delacroix brothers paid fair because they were terrified of Coral's pry bar in a way she found flattering — would come to something north of seven hundred marks. That took her to half. Half. The word had a taste to it, like the first sip of clean water after a day of salt.

She surfaced alongside her boat, the *Tern*, which was twenty-six feet of stubborn timber that leaked with the steady dignity of an old man complaining about his knees, and hauled herself up the ladder with the mesh bag clanking against her hip like a very profitable ghost.

The morning had gone soft and gold while she was under. The headland stood black-green against the light, and beyond it, somewhere out past the shipping lanes, the navy had its yard, its cranes, its careful patrols, and whatever it was the navy was so careful about. Coral wrung out her hair, spat over the side — a habit, not an insult, though the sea was welcome to take it however it liked — and got the little kerosene burner going for coffee.

The *Tern*'s cabin smelled of kerosene, mildew, and the particular staleness of a woman who'd been living aboard so long that land had started to feel like a rumor. Coral had owned her for six years, inherited along with the debt from a father who'd been better at loving her than at bookkeeping, which she supposed was the correct order of operations even if it had bankrupted them both. The bilge pump kicked on with its familiar asthmatic wheeze. She patted the bulkhead.

"I know, I know. We'll both feel better after the brass buyers."

The run to Port Callow took two hours, and she spent them doing what she always did on the run in: talking to the boat, because the boat didn't interrupt, and rehearsing the numbers, because the numbers were the one conversation where she couldn't afford to be charming instead of correct. Coral was good at charming. It had taken her years to learn that charm was a tool like Persuasion, and like Persuasion it worked best on things that were already cracked.

Port Callow at midday was all tar and fish and argument. She tied up at the long public pier, nodded to old Marta who ran the chandlery and had opinions about Coral's rigging that were, annoyingly, always correct, and carried the mesh bag through the lanes to the Delacroix brothers' shed, where the smell of salt and copper hung so thick you could nearly chew it.

Yannick Delacroix, the older brother, weighed her brass on a scale older than the harbor and made a face like each item was a personal disappointment.

"Corroded," he said.

"Yannick. It came out of the sea."

"Everything comes out of the sea. Not everything comes out *corroded*." But his thumb was already stroking the lamp fitting with the unconscious tenderness of a man who'd been fondling scrap since before he could read, and Coral bit down on her smile.

They settled at six hundred and eighty, plus a bonus of twenty for the bell bracket, which Yannick claimed was out of the goodness of his heart and which Coral knew was because his younger brother Pasco had lost a bet about whether she'd bring back anything over a hundred pounds. She took the coins in a drawstring pouch, counted them on the spot — trust was for people who could afford the interest on being wrong — and walked back down the pier feeling seven pounds lighter and one hundred percent more solvent.

Half. She sat on the pier's edge with her boots dangling over the water and let the number settle into her chest. Half. Two more good seasons and she'd be free of Harrow. Two more seasons of cold water and careful patrols and the notebook's arithmetic marching, at last, in the right direction.

"You look pleased with yourself," said Marta, passing with a crate of line.

"I'm solvent," Coral said.

"Solvent." Marta said it the way other people said *contagious*. "That's a condition. It passes."

"Thank you for the encouragement. Truly, the harbor is lucky to have you."

"We are," Marta said without a flicker of irony, and went on.

* * *

That evening, Coral did the ritual. She sat at the chart table with the notebook open, the kerosene lamp turned low, and crossed out the old number and wrote the new one beneath it in her careful, diver's hand — a hand trained to write on slates underwater, where every letter had to be legible or it didn't exist. The old figure looked small and dead above the new one. She allowed herself the luxury of looking at it for a full minute.

Then she did the second part of the ritual, the part she never skipped. She took out the little tin box — tobacco tin once, now home to a bent photograph of her father squinting into a sun that was already making him squint in the picture, a lucky coin, and a lock of hair from a dog who'd been dead nine years and was still, in Coral's private accounting, the finest creature ever to walk a deck — and she put the day's savings in with the rest.

"You'd laugh at me," she told the photograph. "You'd say, Coral, girl, you're saving pennies against a flood."

Her father had died owing money and owing apologies, mostly the same people. Coral had inherited both, and had spent six years learning that the money was the easier debt. The apologies she paid in a different coin — in showing up, in being the diver who'd pull a stranger's fouled anchor for free, in remembering names and birthdays and which of Marta's grandchildren was teething. Small survivals. Small kindnesses. She'd built a whole life out of small things, the way a wren builds a nest out of scraps, and the remarkable thing, the thing she told herself every night with the lamp low and the pump wheezing, was that it held. It held against wind and debt and the navy's careful patrols. It wasn't much. But it was hers, and it held.

She was good at surviving small. She told herself that was enough, and on most nights, telling herself was all it took.

Tonight, though, the telling went a little thin, because of the letter.

It had come on the morning tide, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet her eye, on paper heavy enough to be expensive and plain enough to be deliberate. No syndicate seal. No threat. Just an invitation, in a clerk's immaculate hand:

*Mr. Bram Harrow requests the pleasure of your company aboard the Fair Wind, Thursday, at your convenience.*

*At your convenience*. That was the part that had been sitting in her stomach all day like a swallowed fishhook. Bram Harrow did not do convenience. Bram Harrow did appointments, and his appointments were weather events — you didn't schedule them, you survived them. An invitation at her convenience meant he wanted something, and Bram Harrow wanting something was a great deal more frightening than Bram Harrow taking it, because taking it was simple and wanting it meant there were layers, and Coral had learned to fear layers the way sailors fear a swell with no wind behind it.

She looked at the letter, then at the notebook with its beautiful new number, then out the porthole at the harbor, where the lights of the navy yard glittered beyond the headland, patient and careful and locked.

Half. She was at half. And tomorrow she was expected to walk up a syndicate gangplank and smile at the man who owned the other half, and pretend — the way she'd been pretending for six years, smoothly, easily, in a language she'd never meant to learn — that she was exactly the small, harmless salvage diver she appeared to be.

Coral closed the notebook, blew out the lamp, and lay in her bunk listening to the *Tern* complain about the tide.

"Small," she reminded the dark ceiling. "We survive small."

The ceiling, like the ship, like the sea, said nothing. But out beyond the breakwater, far past where her lamp could reach, a navy launch sat idling on the black water with its lights doused — and it was idling, Coral would notice in the morning, exactly where a boat would sit if it were watching hers.
