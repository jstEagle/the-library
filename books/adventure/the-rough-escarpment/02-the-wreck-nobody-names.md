## 2. The Wreck Nobody Names

The message came at dawn, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Coral Ashgrove knew her handler's habits the way she knew the tide tables. Odell Quist sent work orders at noon, when the harbor office opened and the paperwork could be properly shuffled, and he sent them in triplicate because he believed — sincerely, almost religiously — that a job without paper wasn't a job at all, it was an accident waiting to be blamed on somebody. So when her radio crackled to life at first light with nothing but a set of coordinates and the words *come alone, come quiet*, Coral lay in her bunk for a long moment and listened to her own heartbeat, which had picked up the way it always did when trouble arrived dressed as opportunity.

She got up anyway. That was the second thing about it.

Her boat, the *Salty Liar* — named by a previous owner with more honesty than sense — sat low on her mooring lines, taking on water at the usual insulting pace. Coral bailed her out while the coffee boiled, watching the harbor turn from pewter to gold, and tried to think of reasons not to go.

There were plenty. The coordinates put the dive site well inside the navy's restricted zone, that stretch of coast where the patrol cutters ran so regular you could set your watch by them, where the charts showed nothing but clean blue water over clean empty bottom, and where every old-timer in every bar along the waterfront would tell you, if you bought enough rounds, that the charts were lying. Nobody fished there. Nobody anchored there. Once, three years back, a trawler had drifted in with engine trouble and been escorted out so fast and so politely that the crew still told the story like they'd survived something.

And yet: triple pay.

Odell hadn't said triple pay on the radio. Radios were ears, and everyone in this harbor had one pointed at everyone else's business. But when Coral reached the office — a shack wedged between a chandlery and a fish smokehouse, smelling of tar and old ambition — Odell slid a single sheet across his desk, face-down, and turned it over with one finger like a man revealing a card he already knew would win.

Triple pay. Salvage rights on an unlisted wreck. No manifest, no survey, no questions filed afterward.

"Odell," Coral said, "you know I love you like a bad uncle. But nobody pays triple for brass fittings."

"Nobody's asking for brass fittings." He was a small man made smaller by worry, with hands that never stopped moving, straightening papers that were already straight. "Deck cargo. Sealed crates. You bring up what's inside, you don't open anything yourself, and you bring me a list of serial numbers. That's all."

"Serial numbers." She let the word sit there, ugly as a hook. "On what?"

"On a freighter that isn't on any list I've ever seen, sunk in water nobody's supposed to be diving, and I'd thank you kindly not to make me say any more of it out loud." He rubbed his eyes. "Look, Coral. I didn't find this job. This job found me, through channels I'm not going to describe because describing them would be bad for both our healths. The client wants discretion. Discretion costs triple. That's the whole arithmetic."

"And if the navy comes asking why my boat was sitting inside their fence line?"

"Then your boat wasn't. Your boat was doing a routine hull inspection off Marker Nine, and I have paperwork that says so, because paperwork is the only country where any of us are actually free." He pushed the sheet toward her. "You need the money. I know you need the money. Half of Harrow's debt gone last month was a miracle; the other half is still sitting on you like a barnacle. Triple pay clears it. All of it. You'd be free, Coral."

Free. The word did something to her chest that she didn't enjoy examining.

She thought about Bram Harrow's enforcers, who smiled too easily and counted too slowly, letting the arithmetic of interest do their violence for them. She thought about the *Salty Liar*'s weeping seam, patched twice and praying for a third chance. She thought about how good she'd gotten at surviving small — small jobs, small margins, small victories measured in pounds of brass and inches of freeboard — and how a person could spend a whole life telling herself that small was enough, right up until it wasn't.

"Bottom conditions?" she asked, which was as good as saying yes.

"Deep," Odell admitted. "Deeper than you like. And the current's contrary down there, or so I'm told by people who tell me things." He hesitated, then added, quietly, "One more condition. Whatever you find down there — whatever doesn't fit — you report it to me before anyone else. Not after. Before."

"Define 'doesn't fit.'"

"I can't." He spread his hands. "That's the honest truth, which I know will come as a shock to us both. Just — if something down there looks wrong, Coral, you'll know it. Things that shouldn't exist have a look about them."

She laughed, because laughing was cheaper than shivering. "Everything underwater looks wrong, Odell. That's half the job."

"That," said Odell, "is exactly what worries me."

* * *

The *Salty Liar* cleared the breakwater at mid-morning under a sky the color of worn tin, and Coral spent the two-hour run telling herself stories.

The first story was the sensible one: it was a smuggler's wreck, some syndicate freighter that had gone down running contraband years ago, and the client was the syndicate itself, tidying up loose ends before the navy stumbled over them. Boring. Manageable. Brass and rust and a fat payday.

The second story was less sensible, and it kept surfacing like something drowning. It was about the way the patrol cutters ran that coast — too regular, too careful, the way a man walks past a room in his own house he doesn't want guests looking into. It was about the old-timers' talk, the wrecks nobody names, the year the charts got redrawn and nobody would say why. It was about Ede Latch, actually, though she didn't know yet that it was about him — she only knew him as the old prospector who drank at the Salt Cellar and told rambling stories about the war years, and who had once, drunker than usual, gripped her wrist and said, *Girl, the sea keeps accounts. Everything the powerful throw away, the water writes down.* She'd bought him a beer and forgotten it.

The sea keeps accounts. She shook the phrase off and checked her gauges for the fourth time.

The site, when she found it, looked like nothing. Clean water over a plain of pale sand, no debris field visible from above, no oil sheen, no telltale scatter of wreckage that forty years of storms should have smeared across miles of bottom. Which meant either the charts were right and there was nothing here —

— or someone had cleaned up very thoroughly indeed.

She went down the anchor line into water that went from green to gray to a darkness that pressed on her like a hand. At sixty feet the sand appeared below her, rippled and blank. At eighty, the sonar's promise resolved out of the gloom: a shape. Long. Straight-sided. Too straight for a reef, too big for a fishing boat.

A freighter, sitting upright on the sand as if it had been set down there by something careful.

Coral hung in the water above it and felt the fear arrive, punctual as rent. Wrecks settle. Wrecks list, break, collapse into themselves over the years; the sea is a sloppy housekeeper. This ship hadn't settled. And its hull — she swam closer, running her glove along the plating — its hull was scorched. Not rusted-through, not storm-battered. Scorched, in a pattern that started high near the waterline and swept downward, the signature of fire on the surface followed by a fast, hard sinking.

No mine damage. No torpedo hole that she could read. Just fire, and depth, and silence.

*Freighter that isn't on any list,* Odell had said. *Water nobody's supposed to be diving.*

She found the cargo hold hatch open — already breached, long ago, by salvage crews who'd come and gone without leaving a trace except the absence of everything worth taking. Inside, the hold was a cathedral of shadows, ribs of steel going up into blackness, and the deck cargo Odell's client wanted was stacked amidships: sealed crates, steel-banded, their stenciled markings sanded off with a thoroughness that told its own story. She worked methodically, the way her father had taught her — *the sea rewards patience and punishes pride, little fish* — lifting each crate's lid just far enough to read the serial number stamped inside the frame, logging them on her slate.

The numbers were wrong.

Not illegible — legible, crisp, freshly struck. Wrong in sequence. Naval ordnance serials ran in blocks she knew from years of salvaging navy-surplus scrap, and these sat in a gap, a range that had never been issued, that existed nowhere in any catalog she'd ever thumbed through on slow evenings. Serial numbers for things that officially were never made.

She wrote them all down anyway. Triple pay, she reminded herself. Free, Odell had promised. She was good at surviving small, and this was a small thing, really — numbers on crates, a scorch mark, a ship the charts pretended wasn't there. None of it was hers to understand. Her job was the list, the lift, the surface, the invoice.

She was halfway through the third crate when she saw the compartment.

It was aft, past the collapsed bulkhead, a door that didn't match the rest of the ship — newer steel, newer hinges, welded shut around its edges with seams too neat for a working freighter. Someone had built this compartment *into* the ship, or rebuilt the ship *around* it. And someone else, more recently, had tried to force it: the welds were scored with tool marks, fresh ones, bright metal showing through, abandoned partway.

Coral hung there in the dark water and listened to the two voices that lived in her skull and had never once agreed on anything.

*Leave it,* said the first voice, the sensible one, the one that had kept her alive through twenty years of cold water and cold arithmetic. *It's sealed for a reason. Tool marks mean other divers, recent ones, and divers who cut into navy secrets don't come back to finish. Surface. Log your numbers. Get paid. Survive small.*

*But it's sealed,* said the second voice, which sounded suspiciously like her father, and also like greed, and also like the eleven-year-old who'd once pried open a locked chest on a beach because the not-knowing was worse than any consequence. *Sealed means hidden. Hidden means valuable. And valuable means free.*

She told herself she was only going to look. Every diver in history has told themselves that exact lie, and every diver in history has known it was a lie while telling it.

The welds were nearly cut through on one side — whoever came before her had done most of the work and lost their nerve, or their air, or their lives elsewhere. Her pry bar finished the job in four minutes that felt like forty. The door groaned, resisted, and gave with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard, a deep iron sigh, like the ship exhaling after holding its breath for years.

Inside: a small space, dry-ish, lined with rubber matting gone brittle. A desk bolted to the deck. A dead lamp. And on the desk, centered, deliberate, placed there by a human hand that had wanted it found — or wanted it kept — sat a compass.

Brass. Heavy for its size, dense as a knuckle of gold. No glass over the dial, or rather the glass was intact but beneath it there was no needle at all — no swinging point, no north, nothing that answered to the earth's magnetism the way every compass since the dawn of drowning sailors had answered. Instead, where the needle should have pivoted, there was a ring. A ring of engraved numbers, fine as hair, circling the dial completely: two digits, then three, then two again, then a gap, then more, arranged in no order she recognized — not degrees, not bearings, not any numbering system she'd ever seen on an instrument.

It didn't point anywhere. It just *counted*, somehow, or held its count, like a lock waiting for a key, like a question carved in brass that had been waiting years for someone to ask it correctly.

Coral floated in the dark of the sealed compartment, breathing her own noise, and felt the fear and the greed grapple inside her like two men fighting in a phone booth.

Because here was the thing she understood instantly, the way a diver understands depth without being told: instruments like this didn't get built for ships. They got built for *places*. A compass with no needle wasn't broken — it was specialized. It answered to something else. Coordinates, maybe. A location that mattered enough to machine into brass and hide behind welded steel in a ship full of impossible serial numbers, in water the navy patrolled like a wound.

Things that shouldn't exist have a look about them, Odell had said. He'd been right. It had a look. The look was the look of a door left open in a house where all the doors were supposed to be shut.

*Report it,* said the sensible voice. *Before anyone else. He asked. He begged.*

*He works for someone who pays triple for silence,* said the other voice. *And whoever cut those welds before you didn't report it either.*

She thought about her debt. She thought about freedom. She thought — and this was the thought that decided her, though she wouldn't admit it for a long time — about the old man in the Salt Cellar, gripping her wrist: *everything the powerful throw away, the water writes down.* Whoever had scuttled this ship had thrown something away and expected the sea to keep it forever. The sea had kept it. Now the sea, in its sloppy, generous way, had handed it to her.

Coral Ashgrove was many things — broke, tired, one bad season from losing her boat — but she had never in her life handed anything back to a powerful person who wanted it buried.

She unclipped the pouch at her belt, wrapped the compass in her spare dive glove, and stowed it against her skin, under the wetsuit, where no manifest would ever find it. Then she closed the compartment door as best she could, finned back up through the cathedral of ribs, and rose through the gray-green water toward the tin-colored light, feeling the brass weight against her ribs like a second heart.

On the surface, the wind had come around. She logged the crate numbers for Odell, lifted two crates of the least incriminating-looking salvage for appearance's sake, and ran for home with the *Salty Liar*'s bow knocking up spray and her mind moving faster than the boat.

It wasn't until she was tied up at her mooring, dusk coming down, that she allowed herself to take the compass out and really look at it. In the cabin's lamplight the engraving caught fire — fine lines, precise, machined by someone who cared. Two digits, three, two, a gap. Numbers that pointed nowhere.

Numbers that looked, she realized, with a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air, almost like coordinates.

Almost like a map written in a language she couldn't read yet.

Out beyond the breakwater, where the harbor light didn't reach, a navy launch sat idling in the dark. Engines barely turning over. Not patrolling — patrols moved. Just sitting there, facing her mooring, patient as a debt.

Coral closed her fist around the compass and blew out the lamp.
