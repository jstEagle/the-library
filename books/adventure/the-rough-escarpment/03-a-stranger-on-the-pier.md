## 3. A Stranger on the Pier

The office of Meridian Coastal Assurance smelled of mildew and someone else's ambition. Wren Vale had been sitting in it for eleven days, and in that time had learned three things: the ceiling leaked directly onto the filing cabinet marked *Claims, 1971–1974*, the coffee was less a beverage than a threat, and nobody — not one soul in the entire harbor town of Saltmere Quay — had ever once asked to see their credentials.

That last part bothered Wren more than they cared to admit. They'd forged a very good credential. Laminated. Embossed. There was a seal on it that Wren had drawn freehand and was quietly proud of, a little anchor wreathed in laurel, and it sat in a drawer gathering dust because the world could not be troubled to check.

"Vale!" barked Mr. Osgood from the inner office, where he conducted his empire of paperwork. "The Pellworth file. Did you log the Pellworth file?"

"I logged the Pellworth file," Wren called back, which was true as of four minutes ago, when they'd invented it.

Osgood's head appeared around the doorframe, spectacles fogged, expression hovering somewhere between suspicion and indigestion. It was, Wren had come to understand, his only expression. "Good man. This is why I hired you."

This was not why Osgood had hired them. Osgood had hired them because Wren had walked in off the street with a firm handshake, a plausible smile, and a résumé that claimed five years at a firm that did not exist, and Osgood — who was lonely, cheap, and drowning in unfiled claims — had grasped at them like a man grabbing driftwood. Wren sometimes felt a flicker of guilt about this. Mostly they felt grateful that guilt was such a small, portable thing. You could fold it up and put it in a pocket and forget which coat it was in.

Wren checked the window. The harbor lay flat and silver under a high white sky, boats knocking gently against their moorings like impatient horses. And there, coming through the breakwater gap with all the grace of a floating toolbox, was the *Kestrel*.

Coral Ashgrove.

Wren set down the pen they hadn't been using anyway, picked up a clipboard for authenticity, and strolled out of the office with the unhurried gait of a man whose only concern today was hull insurance premiums.

* * *

She docked badly. That was the first thing Wren noticed, and the thing they would remember longest: she came in fast, threw a line that caught on the second try instead of the first, and stepped onto the pier with the particular economy of motion of someone who had stopped wasting anything — effort, words, hope — years ago. She was smaller than the dossier photograph suggested, or perhaps the photograph had simply been taken by someone who wanted her to look larger. Wren made a mental note to have a word with whoever wrote these dossiers about flattering lighting.

Her boat was wrong, too. Not wrong in any way an insurance clerk would flag — though Wren filed away the weeping seam along her waterline for later, because it was going to matter — but wrong in the way a person is wrong when they're being watched. She moved around the deck checking lines, and every third movement wasn't a movement at all. It was a glance. At the pier. At the rooftops. At Wren, specifically, for exactly one and a half seconds, before dismissing him as furniture.

Furniture. Good. Furniture was safe. Furniture didn't get people killed.

"You're moored crooked," Wren said, because he was supposed to be friendly, and friendly people said things like this.

Coral looked at him. Up close her eyes were the flat gray-green of deep water, and they performed a quick, professional inventory of him — shoes, hands, clipboard, face — in roughly the time it took most people to blink. "I'm moored efficiently," she said. "It looks crooked so nobody borrows my spot." And she walked past him toward the harbormaster's shack without waiting for a reply.

Wren stood there holding his clipboard and experienced the novel sensation of having been assessed, catalogued, and shelved by a woman who thought he sold boat policies.

*Well,* he thought, *you do sell boat policies. Technically. You have sold two boat policies. Mr. Osgood says you have a gift.*

He followed her at a distance that was respectful rather than suspicious, and told himself the distance was calibrated, and told himself a lot of things before lunch.

* * *

The radio lived in the back of the office, behind the filing cabinet that caught the leak, wedged between a box of dead pens and Osgood's collection of commemorative beer steins. It was a battered field unit, older than Wren, with a crack across the casing that let the static out like something breathing. Using it required crouching in an undignified squat among the steins, which Wren had decided early on was actually excellent tradecraft, because no one who saw them down there would ever believe they were doing anything important.

They waited until Osgood went out for his noon pie — a ritual so fixed it could have been used to calibrate clocks — and then keyed the handset twice, low power, short burst. The crackle came back almost immediately, that familiar dry voice with its accent from nowhere in particular.

"Go."

"She's back," Wren said. "Saltmere Quay, berth fourteen. Solo. Boat's taking water at a seam aft of the keel — she'll need careening inside a week, monsoon season or not."

A pause. Static filled it like sand.

"And?"

And. There it was. The word that had been following Wren around for eleven days like a dog hoping for scraps. *And what did she bring up? And did she find it? And is the compass on her, in her boat, in her pockets?*

"No visual confirmation of salvage beyond scrap brass," Wren said. "She offloaded to the brass dealer on the north quay. Standard tonnage. Nothing unusual."

"Nothing unusual," the voice repeated, in the tone of a person tasting soup and finding it insufficiently seasoned. "You're certain."

"I'm certain she's tired, broke, and diving wrecks she shouldn't be able to afford to dive." Wren turned a commemorative stein absently in their hand. It said *SALTMEERE REGATTA 1968* and depicted a sailboat rendered by someone who had clearly never seen one. "Whoever paid for that restricted-zone job paid her well enough to keep the *Kestrel* afloat another month. That's not nothing."

"That's not confirmation either."

"No," Wren admitted. "It isn't."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came back it had shifted registers, gone softer, the way it always did when it wanted to sound reasonable, which was how Wren knew to brace.

"You understand why we're doing this, Vale."

Here it was. The liturgy. Wren knew it by heart now; they could have recited it in their sleep, and some nights, honestly, they thought they did.

"The wreck is evidence," Wren recited dutifully. "Evidence of a crime that, if it surfaces in the wrong hands, gets used by the wrong people. Syndicates. Press. Governments with grudges. We're not hiding the truth, we're *containing* it until it can be handled properly. Through channels. With oversight."

"With oversight," the voice agreed warmly. "You've been reading the briefing again."

"I read everything. It's a flaw."

"It's why you were chosen." A pause, and then, lightly, almost kindly: "Stay close to her. If she found something down there, she'll want to move it soon. People like her always move things too soon."

"People like her," Wren repeated.

"Broke divers. Desperate ones. Desperation makes people predictable, Vale. Remember that. It's the whole job."

The channel clicked dead.

Wren crouched among the beer steins for a while after that, listening to the static breathe out of the cracked casing, and tried to locate, with clinical precision, exactly where in their chest the uncomfortable feeling was coming from.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing Wren had been carefully not looking at since day one, the way you don't look at a loose tooth: the briefing was *true*. Every word of it. There really was a crime buried in that wreck — Wren had seen enough of the file to know the shape of it, if not the details, and the shape alone had given them nightmares for a week. There really were syndicates who would pay fortunes for it and use it as leverage, and press who would burn it down to a headline, and the navy really would torch half the coastline before letting it surface raw. Containment was *necessary*. Wren believed that. Wren had signed things, figuratively speaking, believing that.

What the briefing did not mention — what no briefing ever mentioned — was Coral Ashgrove herself. The briefing described a *vector*. A broke diver, a delivery mechanism, a risk profile. It did not describe a woman who moored crooked on purpose so nobody would steal her spot. It did not describe the way she'd inventoried Wren's shoes and hands in half a second, or the gray-green flatness of her eyes, or the fact that she was diving rusted freighters in restricted water to pay off men named Harrow, which meant she was already being eaten alive by exactly the kind of people the containment was supposedly protecting everyone from.

Desperation makes people predictable. Fine. But nobody in the briefing had mentioned what desperation looked like up close. It looked like a woman throwing a line twice because her hands were shaking from cold and debt. It looked like a hull seam weeping into the harbor while its owner spent her money on diesel instead of repairs because diesel got her to work and caulk just kept her alive.

*Necessary,* Wren told themselves, standing up out of the stein-crouch with knees that complained. *This is necessary work.*

It was at that moment — and Wren would remember this later, the precise moment, the way you remember the exact plank that broke — that they realized they had started saying *necessary* the way other people said *sorry*. Automatically. As a substitute for thinking.

Mr. Osgood returned smelling of meat pie and gravy, found Wren at the window with the clipboard, and beamed at them with the closest thing to warmth his face could produce, which resembled a man wincing at good news.

"Anything interesting out there?" he asked.

"Fishing boats," Wren said. "One sloop coming in. Crooked."

"Hah! Amateurs." Osgood settled into his chair with a groan. "Pellworth follow-up this afternoon. And Vale — there's a diver works out of berth fourteen, Ashgrove, the name is. Rough customer, word is. If she ever comes asking about coverage, you'll handle it. Hull policy, standard rates, but check the survey yourself. I don't trust her boat and I don't much trust her."

Wren felt something turn over slowly in their chest, like ballast shifting.

"I'll handle it personally," they heard themselves say. "I'll go down this afternoon. Introduce myself. Build rapport. It's better practice — clients renew when they trust their clerk."

Osgood blinked at them over his spectacles, visibly astonished that his new hire was volunteering for work, and decided, wisely, not to examine the miracle too closely.

"Well," he said. "Good man."

* * *

Berth fourteen, mid-afternoon. The light had gone gold and long, and the harbor smelled of tar and salt and frying fish from the chandlery two doors down. Coral Ashgrove was on her deck with a hand pump, working it in slow, weary strokes, coaxing water out of a bilge that clearly intended to keep making more. She didn't stop pumping when Wren's shadow fell across her deck. She just looked up, and waited, and pumped.

"Meridian Coastal Assurance," Wren said, and produced the laminated credential with its beautiful hand-drawn anchor, and held it up, and for the first time in eleven days actually hoped someone would ask to see it. "We cover hulls in this district. Yours is — forgive me — actively trying to sink."

"Most days it loses the argument," Coral said. Her voice was roughened, sea-worn, and there was a fresh bruise darkening along her jaw that hadn't been there that morning. Wren noted it and immediately wished they hadn't, because noting it felt like a step down a road with no map.

"A survey's free," Wren said. "No obligation. I can tell you whether that seam is repairable or terminal, and if it's repairable, I can get you a quote. If it's terminal—" they hesitated, and heard themselves add, honestly, "—then you should know before the season turns, not after."

Coral pumped twice more, considering. Then she stepped aside, jerked her chin at the hatch, and said:

"Coffee's terrible. Come aboard, clerk."

Wren climbed down onto the deck of the *Kestrel*, into the smell of brine and diesel and old rope, and understood — with the calm, total clarity of a person watching the tide go out and knowing it will not come back the same — that they had just crossed a line the briefing had never mentioned, drawn on no map, and that they were not going to report it.

Necessary, they told themselves one more time, out of pure habit.

It sounded, for the first time, like a question.
