## 4. The Prospector's Bargain

The Split Anchor was the kind of bar where the floorboards had opinions and the beer had a criminal record. Coral Ashgrove sat at the end of the counter with her back to the wall—old habit, cheap insurance—and pressed a bar rag against her eyebrow while she waited for the bleeding to slow.

It wasn't even her fight. That was the insulting part.

She'd come in for two things: a hot meal that hadn't been rehydrated on a boat, and the name of a welder who worked Sundays. She'd gotten as far as the stew when three men from the *Kestrel's Pride* crew decided that anyone who dove wrecks inside the navy's fence line must be either a liar or a ghost, and both kinds deserved a lesson. Coral had explained, reasonably, that she was neither. They'd explained, less reasonably, with a bottle.

She'd gotten one good hit in—the big one, across the knee with a stool leg, which was why they'd left rather than carried her out—but the arithmetic of three against one was never in her favor, and now her eyebrow was leaking and her ribs had filed a formal complaint.

"You want ice for that or whiskey?" asked Marta, who owned the place and had stopped asking how Coral got her injuries somewhere around the third year.

"Whiskey. For the wound."

"And for you?"

"That too."

Marta poured without comment and moved off to argue with a fisherman about the price of ice. The bar hummed around Coral—low voices, a radio playing something tinny about love and loss, rain starting against the shutters. Outside, the harbor lay gray under a sky the color of an old bruise, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the navy's patrol cutters drew their patient lines across the water, guarding nothing anyone would admit to.

Coral sipped and took inventory. Eyebrow: split, would scar, matched the one on the other side so at least she'd be symmetrical. Ribs: bruised, not cracked—she could still breathe deep enough to dive, which was the only medical test she trusted. Wallet: thin. Debt: enormous. Evening: ruined.

Surviving small. That was her whole art form these days. She was very good at it, and every year it cost more.

That was when the old man appeared beside her, the way old men in coastal bars sometimes did—suddenly, silently, as if he'd been assembled from the shadows behind the stove.

He was gaunt as driftwood, with a beard gone the color of sea-foam and hands that looked like they'd been carved by someone in a hurry. He wore a canvas coat older than Coral and smelled of woodsmoke and iodine. His eyes were pale, sharp, and currently fixed on her face with the frank appraisal of a man pricing salvage.

"You're bleeding," he said.

"I'm aware."

"On the wrong side. You favor your right hand, but they caught you turning left." He tapped his own temple. "You dropped your guard because you were watching the door instead of them. Smart habit. Wrong night."

Coral looked at him properly then. "Do I know you?"

"No. But I know divers. Knew a few. You sit like one—shoulders loose, feet ready for a deck that moves." He eased onto the stool beside her with a sound like rope creaking under load. "Ede Latch."

"Coral Ashgrove."

"I know that too." He said it mildly, no threat in it, just fact, the way you'd say the tide comes in. "Salvage. Small jobs. Good hands. Bad luck with money."

"Who's been talking about me?"

"Everyone talks about everyone here. It's a small coast. We're like crabs in a bucket—all claws, nowhere to go." He signaled Marta with one crooked finger. "Two whiskeys. The real one, not the paint thinner."

"The paint thinner's what I gave her," Coral said.

"The paint thinner's what you deserve," Ede said, and there was the ghost of a smile in the wreckage of his face. "But I'm dying, so I'm drinking well tonight, and I don't drink alone."

* * *

They talked for an hour, and it was the easiest hour Coral had spent ashore in months.

Ede Latch had prospected the coast for forty years—not gold, mostly, though he'd chased it once and it had cost him a marriage. He'd charted coves, mapped currents, found water in places the government swore there was none. He'd worked salvage crews before Coral was born, back when the wrecks along this shore were just wrecks, before certain sections of coastline grew fences and patrol schedules and a silence so official it practically had letterhead.

"You've noticed," he said, turning his glass slowly, "that the navy guards some water nobody wants."

"I've noticed they guard it carefully."

"Carefully is the word. Not heavily—carefully. There's a difference. Heavy means they're afraid of invasion. Careful means they're afraid of being seen." He drank. "Forty years, girl, I watched them be careful. And I got curious, and curiosity is a fine quality in a young man and a fatal one in an old one."

His cough arrived then—a wet, grinding sound from deep in his chest, the kind of cough that had already made its decisions. Coral slid him her untouched water and he waved it off with dignity.

"How long?" she asked. Divers learned to ask directly. Euphemism wasted air.

"Doctor says spring. Doctor's an optimist; I say the first cold storm." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Which is why I'm buying whiskeys for strangers with good hands and bad luck."

"I'm flattered, but I'm not going to rob you, if that's where this goes."

"Ha!" The laugh turned into another cough, and he rode it out with his eyes watering, grinning through it. "No. Nothing so simple. Come outside. My eyes are bad in this light, and what I have to show you needs better than bar-light and worse company."

Outside, the rain had settled into a fine persistent drizzle, the kind that didn't fall so much as accumulate. Ede led her down the alley behind the Split Anchor to a lean-to shed he apparently rented from Marta, unlocked a padlock with fingers that trembled only slightly, and lit a lantern.

The shed held a cot, a stove, a crate of tools worn to silver, and a sea chest that had traveled farther than most people. Ede knelt at the chest—it took him a long time, and Coral pretended not to notice, offering a hand he ignored on principle—and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

Inside was a map.

Not a printed chart. A hand-drawn thing, ink on waxed paper, dense with coastline rendered in a draftsman's careful strokes: headlands, soundings, little annotations in a cramped slanted script. And along one margin, running like a spine down the edge of the paper, a column of numbers.

"What am I looking at?" Coral said.

"A cache." Ede laid it flat on the chest with the tenderness other men reserved for photographs of their children. "Coastal. Dry. Sealed since before you were born. I built it myself, over two winters, when I was young enough to think I was clever and old enough to know I should keep my mouth shut."

"A cache of what? Gold? You said you never found—"

"Not gold." His voice changed. The humor drained out of it like tide out of a channel, leaving something flat and dark underneath. "Girl, sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit down anyway. It's my shed. I get to make the rules."

Coral sat on the cot. The springs complained. Ede lowered himself onto the crate opposite, lantern light carving his face into ridges and hollows, and for a moment he just looked at her—that same appraising stare, but weighted now, deliberate, a man checking a knot one last time before trusting it with his life.

Or someone else's.

"There was a ship," he said. "Thirty-one years ago. A passenger ship, running at night, no lights, no registry anyone will confirm. It went down inside the waters the navy now guards so *carefully*. Officially, it was never there. Officially, whatever happened that night was a training exercise that went wrong, miles away, involving no civilians." He paused. "Unofficially, I was fishing those waters that night. Unofficially, I saw things. Pulled things out of the water. Things the navy paid me to forget and I was too stubborn to forget."

Coral felt the hair rise on her arms, and it had nothing to do with the damp. "What kind of things?"

"Papers. Photographs. A manifest with names on it—more names than any training accident ever produced." He tapped the map. "I kept what I could carry. Sealed it away. Told myself someday I'd find someone worth handing it to, and then I told myself that for thirty years, because it's easier than doing anything." His mouth twisted. "Now the storm's coming early, for me personally, and the telling-myself days are over."

"Why me?" The question came out harder than she intended. "You don't know me. You heard my name in a bar."

"I know exactly what I heard." Ede leaned forward. "A diver who works alone, owes money to people who'd sell her own teeth back to her, has no family on this coast, no politics, no cause—and who, three weeks ago, went down on a wreck inside the restricted zone and came up with something in her pocket she didn't report."

The shed went very quiet. Rain ticked on the tin roof. Somewhere down the alley, a dog expressed an opinion about the weather.

Coral kept her face still, which took effort, because her pulse had jumped into her throat. The compass. The brass compass with no needle, only that ring of numbers, sitting wrapped in a sock at the bottom of her gear bag aboard the *Magpie*. She'd told no one. She'd barely admitted it to herself.

"How—" she started.

"Harbor rats see everything, and half of them owe me favors from before you were born." He waved it off. "Doesn't matter how. What matters is: you found something strange down there, and you didn't run to the navy, and you didn't run to the syndicate. You sat on it. That tells me you've got sense and distrust in equal measure, which is the exact recipe required." He pushed the map an inch toward her across the chest. "And it tells me something else. Something important."

"What?"

"That compass you're sitting on isn't decoration. It's a key. And this map"—he laid two fingers on the margin of numbers—"is the lock. They were made as a pair, thirty years ago, by a very frightened woman who trusted me with one half and trusted the sea with the other. I've spent three decades knowing the numbers meant something and not knowing what. Then a diver pulls its twin out of a wreck the navy forbids, and suddenly the halves are on the same coast again." He smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile. "The sea has a sense of humor. It's cruel, but it's *good*."

Coral stared at the map. The numbers marched down the margin, and even in lantern light, even upside-down-ish, something in her hindbrain stirred—a pattern recognition, a shape she'd seen recently, etched in brass around an empty ring.

Her heart was doing something complicated.

"This is a lot," she said finally. "This is a *lot*, Ede. You're a stranger. You found me in a bar. And you want to hand me a map to evidence of a navy cover-up based on—what? My posture?"

"Based on the fact that I'm out of options and you're the first honest coward I've met in twenty years."

"Honest coward."

"The best kind. Honest cowards survive. Heroes die beautifully and their causes die with them. Cowards who can't lie to themselves—" he tapped his own sunken chest, "—they live long enough to finish the job." He coughed again, shorter this time, and when he came up from it his eyes were wet and furious. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to be *reliable*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"What exactly do you want?"

"Two things. A promise, and a choice." He held up one crooked finger. "The promise: this gets delivered. Either to my daughter, Sera—she lives rough, south coast, past the old lighthouse at Grayle Point, you'll find her or she'll find you—or, if you can't deliver it, if it's going to be taken from you, if keeping it means losing it—" the second finger rose, "—then you burn it. In front of witnesses if possible. It never reaches a syndicate, never reaches a navy desk, never becomes leverage in anybody's game. Deliver it or destroy it. Those are the only two endings I'll accept."

"And the choice?"

"The choice is whether you say yes." He sat back. "I won't pressure you. Well—I'll pressure you a little, I'm dying and entitled—but the door's right there. Walk out, finish your whiskey, and tomorrow this never happened. I'll find someone else. Or I won't, and it burns with me, and maybe that's the ending the dead deserve anyway."

Coral looked at the map. At the old man folded onto his crate like a piece of driftwood the sea had given up on. At the padlocked door, the rain, the whole shabby small life waiting for her outside—the debt, the dives, the brass she'd sell tomorrow to pay half of what she owed and call it victory.

Every instinct she owned said walk away. People who carried things like this ended up at the bottom of harbors, or in the holds of syndicate boats, or in navy detention cells with very polite men asking very patient questions. She owed the Harrow syndicate more than she'd earn in five years. She had a boat with a weeping seam and a monsoon season coming. She was, by her own honest assessment, barely keeping herself alive, let alone a dead man's secrets.

And yet.

Refusing felt impossible. Not because he'd guilted her—though he'd done a professional job of it—but because of the way he'd said *the dead deserve it anyway*. Because of thirty-one years of names on a manifest, sleeping in a sea chest in a rented shed behind a bar. Because somebody had to be reliable, and the world seemed chronically short of volunteers, and she was, God help her, standing right there.

Besides—and she'd examine this later, or more likely never—there was the compass. The lock to his key. Whatever mechanism had put that brass ring in a forbidden wreck and this map in a dying prospector's hands, it had chosen her twice now. She didn't believe in fate. But she believed in patterns, and this was becoming one.

"If I take this," Coral said slowly, "people will come after it. You know that. The navy, the syndicate—maybe worse. You're not handing me a map. You're handing me a target with a promise attached."

"Yes," Ede said simply. "I know. I've thought about that every day for thirty years, and I've decided that a target with a promise attached is worth more than a secret that dies warm and safe in a chest." His voice roughened. "Those names on that manifest, girl. They were people. They had tickets. Somebody sold them passage and the navy wrote them out of existence, and the only reason it worked is that everybody who knew stayed quiet and comfortable." He looked at her, and his pale eyes were terribly clear. "I stayed quiet and comfortable. Don't be me."

The rain thickened against the roof. Down the alley, the dog had lost interest. Coral became aware that she'd stopped breathing normally and made herself start again.

"Sera Latch," she said. "South coast, past Grayle Point. Deliver it—or burn it."

"Deliver it or burn it," Ede agreed. "And the compass stays with the map. They're married. Separate them and you've got a widow and a corpse."

He held out his hand. It was lighter than she expected, all tendon and old scar, and it gripped hers with surprising ferocity, a man transferring weight.

"One more thing," he said, not letting go. "When you find Sera—and you will, she's her father's daughter, she finds things—you tell her I'm sorry. Not for the map. For the thirty years. Tell her her father was a coward who ran out of time to fix it, and that the last useful thing he did, he did right." His grip tightened. "Can you remember that? Word for word?"

"Word for word."

"Good." He released her hand and sagged back, suddenly, enormously tired, as if the promise had been the last cargo aboard and now the ship rode high and empty. "Then get out of my shed. Dying men need their beauty sleep, and you're dripping on my floor."

Coral stood. Wrapped the oilcloth back over the map with more care than she'd ever shown her own possessions, and tucked it inside her jacket, against her ribs, next to the ones that ached.

At the door she paused. "Ede. Why me? Really. Last chance for a sensible answer."

He was already lying back on the cot, hat tipped over his eyes, and his answer drifted out from under the brim, dry as sand:

"Because you sat with your back to the wall and still got hit. Means the wall's not enough anymore, girl. Time to stop surviving small."

* * *

She walked back through the drizzle to the harbor, and the map rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The *Magpie* sat low and patient at her mooring, riding the gray swell, her hull holding—for now—the way Coral's ribs were holding, the way her finances were holding, the way everything in her life held: barely, temporarily, on borrowed time. She climbed aboard, went below, and dug the sock out of her gear bag.

The compass sat in her palm, brass gone green at the seams, its empty ring catching the lamplight. She turned it until the numbers faced her, then unwrapped the map and held the two together for the first time.

The columns matched. Number for number, mark for mark, like two halves of a sentence finally spoken aloud. Thirty years apart, and they fit as if they'd been separated that morning.

Coral sat down hard on the bunk and laughed—one short, disbelieving bark that hurt her ribs and was worth it.

Then, through the salt-fogged port above her berth, she saw it: a navy launch, idling beyond the breakwater, lights dimmed, sitting in the rain with the patience of something that had all night and knew exactly where to look.
