## 5. Numbers That Point Nowhere

Coral Ashgrove had a theory about her house, which was that it was less a house than a suggestion of one — a fisherman's shack that some architect had dreamed about and then abandoned halfway through the dream. It sat on stilts above the tidal flats at the edge of Saltmere, leaning east the way old men lean into arguments. The floorboards had opinions about your weight. The roof had opinions about the rain. The only thing in the whole structure that worked without complaint was the kettle, and Coral suspected it was only biding its time.

She came home the way she always came home: by water, running her boat — the *Maren*, twenty-two feet of stubbornness held together with paint and prayer — up the channel at half tide, nosing into her private mooring, which was a rope tied to a piling that had been dead longer than she'd been alive.

The bar fight's damage had stiffened up on the ride home. Her ribs announced themselves every time she breathed past a certain depth, and the cut above her eye had scabbed into a small dark country that she kept catching in her reflection and finding unfamiliar. Ede Latch's bandages were better than anything she'd have done herself, which was its own kind of unsettling. She kept hearing his voice — *deliver it to my daughter, or burn it, girl, one or the other, no third thing* — and kept deciding, with the practiced ease of a woman who was very good at not thinking about things, that she'd get to it.

First, tea. First, dry socks. First, the small ceremonies of a life that fit in one boat and one shack.

She climbed the ladder to her porch, and the porch did its usual impression of a creature deciding whether to hold her, and she went inside and put the kettle on and stood in the middle of her one room with wet hair dripping on the floorboards, feeling the particular exhaustion that comes not from work but from *almost dying in an interesting place*.

The compass was in her jacket pocket. It had been there all the way home, a small hard weight against her hip, and she had spent the whole ride not thinking about it, which had required so much effort that she was now more tired than if she'd actually thought about it the entire time.

She took it out and set it on the table, next to the salt dish and the tin of matches and the photograph of her mother squinting into a sun that had stopped existing twenty years ago.

The kettle began its slow climb toward a scream.

The compass was beautiful. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the way your tongue circles a chipped tooth. Everything else she'd lifted off that wreck had been rust and rot and the particular sadness of drowned things. But this — brass, heavy, the case machined so fine that the lid closed with a sound like a whisper being decided against. And the face: no needle. No cardinal points. No *N* where an *N* should be, no rose, no degrees. Just a ring of numbers, etched around the inner rim, small and precise:

**7 – 19 – 4 – 22 – 11 – 3**

And in the center, where the pivot of a needle would sit, a socket. Empty. As if something had been removed. Or as if something were *missing* — waiting.

Coral turned it over in her hands. On the back, stamped so faintly you'd miss it unless you were a diver, a diver being a person professionally trained to notice the thing everyone else swam past: a naval foundry mark, and beneath it a date. Thirty-one years old. Older than the official sinking of the freighter she'd found it on by a full decade.

She put it down. She picked it up. She put it down again and made tea, because a woman cannot have a crisis and cold hands at the same time, and one of those things is fixable.

* * *

It was the map that did it.

Ede's map had been riding in the same jacket pocket, folded into eighths, soft as cloth from decades of handling. She'd looked at it once, on the prospector's floor, long enough to see that it was a coastal chart of a kind — hand-drawn, ink faded to the color of weak tea, with depth-soundings scribbled along a shoreline she half recognized and half didn't. She hadn't studied it. Studying it would have meant admitting she'd accepted a dying man's errand, and she'd been busy not admitting things all week.

But now it was midnight, the tea was gone, the rain had started — a soft, businesslike drizzle, the monsoon's advance scouts — and there was nothing left between her and the map except the ceiling she kept staring at.

She unfolded it under the lamp.

Ede had drawn the coast from the headlands down past Saltmere and beyond, into water Coral knew as well as her own hands. He'd marked wrecks — of course he had, the man had been a prospector, a scavenger of drowned history, a beachcomber of the deep — little crosses with dates and names, some of which Coral had dived, some of which she'd heard of, and some of which made her sit up straight because *nobody* knew about those.

And along the margin, in the same careful hand, a column of numbers.

**7. 19. 4. 22. 11. 3.**

Coral's breath went out of her like she'd been hit — which, in fairness, she had been, recently, and her ribs were in no condition for surprises.

She looked at the compass. She looked at the map. She looked at the compass again, and the numbers sat there in their ring, patient as a held note.

"No," she said to the empty room. "No, that's — that's a coincidence. The sea is full of coincidences. The sea is *made* of coincidences."

The sea did not answer, but the compass sat there looking smug, insofar as brass can look smug, and Coral, who had spent thirty years learning to read water and wreckage and the small tells of men lying about cargo weights, read this instead: the map and the compass were not two things. They were one thing, broken in half and carried by two people who had never met until tonight. Ede had carried the numbers in ink. Someone — the navy, presumably, because who else machined brass like that and then sank it — had carried the numbers in metal.

She turned the compass slowly, holding it over the map, and when the ring of numbers aligned with the column in the margin — when *7* sat over *7* and *19* over *19* — the socket in the compass's center came to rest directly above a point on the chart. A point Ede had drawn no cross on. No name. Just a small, deliberate circle, and beside it, in letters so small she had to hold the lamp close:

*The cistern. Where the water keeps what the water took.*

Coral sat back. Her heart was doing something complicated.

Because here was the thing, and she made herself look at it squarely, the way you look at a storm front: she was holding half a mechanism that pointed at something a navy had sunk a freighter to hide, and a dying man had handed her the other half in exchange for a promise, and neither of those facts was innocent. Things like this did not happen *to* Coral Ashgrove. Things like this happened to people in the kind of trouble that ended with names in newspapers and boats that burned at their moorings.

And yet.

And yet her hands were shaking, and it was not entirely fear.

She thought about her debt. Fourteen thousand to the Harrow syndicate, give or take the interest that grew in the dark like something in a bilge. She thought about the *Maren*'s hull, the seam aft that wept a little more every month, that she bailed with affection and lies. She thought about triple pay for a forbidden dive, and about how she'd taken it, and about what that said about where she'd already gone.

"Where the water keeps what the water took," she read aloud, and the words sat in the lamplight, and she found — this was the part that frightened her more than the navy, more than Harrow, more than any of it — that she was smiling.

Then she looked out the window, and stopped smiling.

* * *

The launch sat beyond the breakwater like a held breath.

It was a navy hull — Coral knew navy hulls the way she knew her own pulse, knew the particular arrogance of their lines, the way they sat in the water as if the water should be grateful. Gray, low, running no lights except the legal minimum, and positioned — this was the detail that turned her stomach from excitement to something cold and slow — positioned exactly where it could watch the channel. Exactly where it could watch *her mooring*. Not patrolling. Patrolling meant movement, meant a route, meant a boat going somewhere. This boat was going nowhere. This boat was *looking*.

Coral did not move from her chair. This was a skill, and she was very good at it: the skill of being furniture. She sat in the lamplight with the map under her hands and let her eyes do the work, tracking the launch through the rain-streaked glass while her body performed the role of a tired woman in a shack, which required no performance at all.

Thirty years on the water had taught her the difference between a boat that hadn't seen you and a boat that was pretending not to see you. This was the second kind. It sat at the edge of the breakwater light with the patient stillness of a heron over a fishhole, and every few minutes its searchlight made a slow, lazy pass across the flats — never quite touching her shack, never quite not touching it.

They knew she'd been in the restricted zone. Of course they did. The handler who'd offered her the job had been too smooth, the pay too good, the whole arrangement the kind of trap that only works on people who already believe they deserve traps. She'd walked into it with her eyes open because fourteen thousand reasons had walked in with her, and now the navy was sitting off her porch in the rain, and the question was not *do they know* but *how much do they know, and what are they waiting for*.

Waiting, she thought. That's what this was. A launch doesn't board a shack at midnight. A launch watches. A launch waits to see who comes, who goes, who visits, who the diver talks to. A launch is the navy's way of saying *we are a patient institution and you are one tired woman with a leaky boat*.

Coral turned the lamp down to a coal. In the reduced light she looked at the compass again, at the ring of numbers, at the empty socket, and felt the shape of the trap close around her with a gentleness that was worse than force. Because here was the elegant part: they didn't need to take it from her. They only needed to let her carry it. A compass that pointed nowhere was harmless in a drawer. In the hands of a diver with a map and debts and a boat, it was a leash — and whoever held the other end was watching to see where it pulled.

Unless Ede had known. Unless the old man, dying on his floor with his blood coming up like a tide, had looked at a bar-fight-battered salvage diver and seen exactly this: a woman the navy would watch, a woman with nothing to lose and a boat to lose it in, a woman who could carry the numbers where a careful person never could.

"You chose me," she said quietly, to a dead man, to a rain-dark window, to the gray shape beyond the breakwater. "You sneaky, dying old devil. You didn't give me a map. You gave me a *job*."

The searchlight swept the flats again, closer this time. Close enough that its edge grazed the *Maren*'s mast and slid along the wet rope of her mooring like a finger testing a knot.

Coral sat very still in her dark kitchen, with a compass that pointed at nothing and everything, a map to a place where the water kept what the water took, a promise to a dead man burning a hole in her pocket beside fourteen thousand reasons to hand it all over and beg.

Beyond the breakwater, the launch's engine turned over — a low, deliberate sound, like a decision being made — and began to move.

Not away.

In.
