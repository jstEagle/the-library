## 6. Harrow Comes Calling

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was the first bad sign. Bram Harrow was a man who believed that fear worked best before coffee.

Coral knew it was him before she opened the door because her neighbor's dog — a fat, cowardly creature named Admiral who barked at gulls, bicycles, and his own reflection — went suddenly and completely silent. Even the animals understood the food chain on this pier.

She opened the door with a mug in one hand and an expression of mild, hungover inconvenience on her face, both of which she had rehearsed in the mirror for ten minutes after spotting his black car parked outside the chandlery.

"Bram," she said. "You're a long way from the office."

"The office travels." He filled her doorway the way weather fills a window. Bram Harrow was not a large man, exactly, but he had the particular density of someone who had never once hurried anywhere he didn't want to go. He wore a linen jacket too clean for the harbor and shoes that had never touched a deck. "May I come in? Or would you rather conduct business on the step, where the whole pier can enjoy it?"

"Conduct what business?" She stepped aside anyway, because refusing him felt like refusing a tide. He entered her cabin, looked at the chart table, the drying dive suit, the single chair, and sat down on the bunk as though it were a throne he was being gracious about borrowing.

Her home was eleven feet by nine and smelled of diesel and salt and yesterday's stew. She watched him take it in — the peeling varnish, the patched porthole, the debt ledger she kept nailed to the bulkhead out of some grim sense of honesty — and she watched him decide, comfortably, that she was exactly what she appeared to be: a small woman living a small life above a small boat, drowning slowly in numbers.

Good, she thought. Be comfortable.

"I'll be direct," Bram said, folding his hands. "I'm told you took work last week. A job off the point. Restricted water."

"News travels fast."

"News is my business." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like the rest of him. "The syndicate hears things. Boats talk. Divers talk. Barmaids talk, especially when they're paid. You dove somewhere you shouldn't have been, Coral, and I find myself curious about what you brought up."

"Brass," she said. "Fittings, valves, a ship's bell with the name ground off it. The usual."

"Mm." He looked at her the way a jeweler looks at a stone he suspects has been swapped. "And where did this brass go?"

"Voss. On the quay. Paid me Thursday." This was true. She'd sold most of it to old Voss, who asked no questions because questions cost him concentration he preferred to spend on rum. "Half of it went straight to your people, if you check your ledger. Which I assume you do, since checking ledgers appears to be the closest thing the Harrow family does to religion."

Something flickered behind Bram's eyes — not anger. Interest. That was worse.

* * *

Here was the thing about lying, Coral thought, pouring him coffee she didn't want to share: it had gotten easy. That was the part that bothered her. There had been a time, not so long ago, when a lie stuck in her throat like a fishbone. Now they came up smooth as breath, polished and ready, and she could lie to a man's face while making him breakfast.

Her father used to say a diver's word was the only thing that held value down there, where nobody could see anything but bubbles. She wondered what he'd make of her now. Probably he'd understand. Probably that was the saddest possibility of all.

Bram accepted the mug without thanks and drank like a man sampling wine he already owned the vineyard of.

"You know what I admire about you, Coral Ashgrove?"

"My punctual payments."

"Your arithmetic." He set the mug down. "Most people in your position panic. They run, or they blab, or they do something stupid and sentimental. You don't. You do the math and you survive small. I respect that. It's why you still have a boat."

"And why you still have interest payments."

"Just so." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the polish dropped and she saw the thing underneath, the thing that had collected every debt on this coast for twenty years: patience with teeth. "So let me offer you some better arithmetic. A clean slate."

Coral kept her face still. Somewhere below them, in the bilge, wrapped in oilcloth inside a false panel under the water tank, a brass compass with no needle sat in the dark like a secret with a heartbeat.

"Go on."

"Whatever you lifted from that wreck — whatever it was, however strange, however valuable — you give it to me today, and the ledger comes down off your wall. Every number. Gone. You start at zero." He spread his hands, magnanimous as a priest. "That's not an offer I make twice."

And there it was. Freedom, sitting on her bunk, wearing a linen jacket. Fourteen years of debt, gone, in exchange for a hunk of metal she'd found in a sealed compartment on a wreck the navy pretended didn't exist.

It would be so easy. That was the trap of it. Bram Harrow did not make easy offers; he made expensive ones dressed as easy ones. If the compass were worthless, he wouldn't be here. If it were merely stolen goods, he'd have sent one of the twins who broke thumbs. He'd come himself, in his own car, before eight in the morning. Whatever she'd pulled off that freighter, the syndicate wanted it badly enough to forgive a fortune.

Which meant it was worth more than a fortune.

"No," she said.

Bram raised his eyebrows, genuinely pleased. "No?"

"No." She crossed her arms. "I sold everything I lifted. Brass is brass, Bram. There was nothing down there but rust and dead men's plumbing. If you've heard otherwise, somebody sold you a story."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed something insulting. Inside, Coral counted her heartbeats and kept her breathing slow, the way she did at forty meters when the air turned thin and the dark pressed close and panic meant death. Lying, she was discovering, was a kind of diving. Same discipline. Same cold clarity. Same risk of never coming back up.

"You understand," Bram said at last, conversationally, "that I can't simply believe you."

"That sounds like your problem, not mine."

"It becomes yours." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and retrieved his jacket from where he'd draped it over her stove — a liberty designed to remind her how casually he could touch her life. "I'll tell you what I know, free of charge, since we're friends. Three days ago, a navy launch sat beyond the breakwater watching this berth for six hours. Yesterday, two men I've never seen before asked Voss what you'd sold him. Last night, someone went through your mooring lines." He paused at the door. "Not my people. I don't search boats I intend to buy from. So whoever else wants what you found, Coral — they're closer than I am, and they're less polite."

"Is this the part where you threaten me?"

"This is the part where I warn you." He smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Threats are for people who owe me money. You don't owe me money anymore — not until Friday, anyway, when the next payment's due and I'm afraid the discount expires." He tapped the doorframe twice, a little farewell knock. "You have until then. Clean slate, Coral. It's the best deal anyone will ever offer you, and we both know it."

Then he was gone, and the Admiral resumed barking at a bicycle as though nothing had happened, and Coral stood in her tiny kitchen holding a cooling mug and hating herself.

* * *

She hated it — hated how smooth it had been, how the lies hadn't even needed fetching, how they'd lined up and marched out of her mouth in formation while she poured coffee. Sold it to Voss. Nothing but rust. Somebody sold you a story. Each one delivered with the flat calm of a woman reading a tide table.

When had that happened? When had lying become her native language, the tongue she dreamed in?

She thought of her father again, and of the first rule he ever taught her on the boat, age nine, her hands too small for the gear: *Never lie to yourself underwater, love. The sea checks.* Everything else you could fudge — depth, time, how much air you had left — but lie to yourself down there and the sea would collect.

Okay, she told herself now, standing over the false panel with a screwdriver. Check.

What am I actually doing?

She unscrewed the panel and lifted out the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped it on the chart table, and there it was in the morning light: the compass, beautiful and wrong. No needle. Just that ring of fine engraved numbers circling the face like a clock that refused to tell time, and beneath the glass, faint as breath, a second ring of markings she still couldn't read.

She was doing this, she realized. She was gambling her life — not her comfort, her actual life — on a piece of brass she couldn't explain, from a wreck the navy patrolled like a grave, against a syndicate enforcer who'd offered her freedom and a navy that had started searching her boat. And the truly mad part, the part she'd never admit to anyone, was that some stubborn diver's instinct in her chest said *keep it*, the same voice that said *one more meter* when the light was dying and the good salvage was just below.

Greed, probably. Her father would have called it greed.

But it didn't feel like greed. It felt like the compass was pointing at something, even without a needle.

She was rewrapping it when she heard footsteps on the dock — quick ones, wrong rhythm, not the twins' heavy tread — and she slid the bundle into the flour tin by the stove just as a shadow crossed the porthole.

A knock. Light, almost apologetic.

"Coral Ashgrove?" A young man in a courier's jacket, holding an envelope. "Delivery. No signature needed."

She took it. The paper was cheap; the seal was wax, which was not. Inside, a single line in a spidery, failing hand:

*Girl with the steady hands — you still owe me a visit. Come tonight. Bring nothing. Tell no one. — E.L.*

Coral stared at it. Ede Latch. The old prospector who haunted the end of the pier, who bought her tea sometimes and talked nonsense about the war and the coast and things sunk off the point that official charts swore were empty water. She hadn't seen him in a week. She'd assumed he'd finally drunk himself into the harbor.

Bring nothing. Tell no one.

She looked at the flour tin. She looked at the ledger nailed to the wall, fourteen years of numbers, three days left on Bram's clock. She looked out the porthole at the breakwater, where — right on schedule, patient as sin — a grey navy launch idled in the morning haze, facing her berth.

Everyone wanted something from her at once, and none of them were telling her the whole truth, which meant somewhere in all of it was a whole truth, and it was hers if she could reach it first.

"Tonight, then," she said to the empty cabin, and began packing a bag.
