## 7. Cutting Loose

Coral had always believed that leaving a place was a skill, like welding or holding your breath, and that she'd gotten good at it the way you get good at anything—by doing it badly for years first.

She packed in the dark. That was deliberate. Light invited inspection, and inspection invited questions, and questions were the one cargo she couldn't afford to take aboard. So she moved through her cabin by feel and by memory: the oilskin roll behind the bunk, the tool bag with the false bottom where the compass now lived wrapped in a sock, Ede's map folded into a waxed packet and tucked inside her shirt, against her skin, where it felt less like paper than like a second heartbeat.

The map had been there all night. She'd slept with it, or near enough to sleeping—she'd lain on her bunk listening to the harbor slap against the hull and thought about an old man's cracked hands pressing it into hers, and about the promise she'd made, which she still couldn't recite back to herself without feeling like a woman signing something she hadn't read.

*Deliver it to my daughter, or burn it.* That was the whole of it. Simple words. The kind of simple that meant nothing simple at all.

Outside, the sky over Harrow's Reach—that was what the locals called the east end of the harbor, though whether it was named after the syndicate or the syndicate had simply grown into the name like a tree growing into a fence, nobody could say—was the color of wet slate just beginning to think about gray. Dawn came late and grudging this time of year. Good. She wanted every minute of dark the season would sell her.

She loaded the last of it in silence: water cans, the dented kettle, a crate of tinned peaches she'd been saving against some better day because hope, in Coral's experience, kept best in syrup. She paused over the shelf above her bunk, where a row of small things stood sentry—a chipped ceramic gull her mother had won at a fair before Coral was born, a brass fitting polished smooth from handling, a photograph curled soft with damp of a boat she no longer owned.

The gull went into the bag. The photograph went into the bag. The brass fitting she held for a moment, turning it in her fingers, then set back on the shelf.

Some things you took with you so you wouldn't forget who you were. Some things you left so you'd have a reason to come back. She wasn't sure yet which category this was. She suspected she'd know by nightfall, and that knowing would hurt either way.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Grace* sat low at her mooring, riding the black water like a tired animal standing in a stream. Forty feet of salvage trawler, older than Coral and honest about it, her paint gone the gray-green of old copper, her deck scarred with a decade of dragged anchors and dropped tanks. Her portside seam—weeping since spring, worsening since the restricted-zone job, when Coral had pushed her harder than any boat deserved to be pushed—had been fed a steady diet of putty and prayer.

"Morning, ugly," Coral said, stepping aboard.

The boat answered with the particular groan she made when someone's weight hit the deck, which Coral chose to interpret as affection. You learned to interpret a lot of things as affection in this life. It was cheaper than the alternative.

She worked the engine room first, because if the engine wasn't going to start, better to learn it now, in the dark, with time to swear properly. The Perkins turned over on the fourth try, coughed, caught, and settled into its familiar uneven rumble—the sound, Coral thought, of an old man clearing his throat before telling a long story. She let it run and went forward to check the bilge.

An inch of water. Not catastrophic. Not nothing. She ran the manual pump until the level dropped, then wedged the pump handle where she could reach it from the helm, because the electric one had died in March and she'd never fixed it, partly for lack of parts and partly because pumping gave her something to do with her hands when the worrying got loud.

There was a version of this morning where she didn't leave. She'd rehearsed it lying awake: stay, dive the safe wrecks, pay Harrow his percentages, grow old small and solvent-ish in a harbor town that barely knew her name. It wasn't a bad life. It was, in fact, the only life she knew how to live, and there was a real comfort in that—in being exactly the right size for your own existence, like a key cut precisely for one lock.

But Bram Harrow had stood in her wheelhouse two nights ago smelling of expensive tobacco and cheap violence, and he had smiled at her with the patience of a man who already knew how the story ended, and he had said, *Whatever you lifted out there, Coral, I can be reasonable about it,* and she had lied to him—smoothly, easily, fluently—and hated herself for how little it cost.

Lying used to cost her something. Now it was like breathing. And a woman who could lie that easily to a man like Bram Harrow was a woman who had already left, whatever her body happened to be doing.

So. Leaving.

* * *

The harbor at half-light was a theater between shows. The fishing fleet wouldn't move for another hour; the syndicate's boats were dark along their quay; the customs office wouldn't open its shutters until someone official had finished a second coffee. There was a narrow window here, and Coral had spent twenty years learning windows.

She cast off the bow line first, then the stern, coiling them neatly on the dock because even fugitives shouldn't leave messes—it was a point of professional pride, and besides, tidy lines said *I'll be back*, and she wanted anyone watching to believe it.

Nobody was watching. Probably. The navy launch beyond the breakwater had been there yesterday evening, idling, patient as a cat outside a mousehole, and when she'd gone out to check her anchor light it had still been there, a darker shape against the dark water, close enough to see but not close enough to hail. It might have moved on overnight. It might not.

She climbed aboard, took the helm, and eased the *Kestrel's Grace* out of her berth on idle, threading the channel she could have steered blindfolded, past the fuel dock, past Old Tammo's derelict ketch that everyone kept meaning to scrap and nobody ever did, past the fish market where the first lights were just coming on and the smell of ice and salt hung over everything like a blessing.

And there—her chest did something complicated—there was the pier where she'd sold her first catch at nine years old, lying about her age to a buyer who'd known perfectly well and paid her fairly anyway because he liked her father. There was the seawall where she'd kissed a boy named Ferren Delacourt at fifteen and gotten seasick immediately afterward, which she'd always taken as an omen of unusual clarity. There was the chandlery window where Marta Osei displayed, every winter, a hand-lettered sign reading NO CREDIT, CORAL, AND DON'T GIVE ME THAT LOOK, which was the closest thing to love the woman had ever committed to writing.

She was going to miss a town that expressed its affection through signage. That surprised her. She'd assumed she'd miss nothing.

The breakwater came up on the port side, its lighthouse blinking its slow, unhurried blink, and Coral looked hard at the water beyond it, where the navy launch should have been.

It wasn't.

Either it had gone—which meant she'd worried herself sick over nothing—or it had moved somewhere she couldn't see, which was worse, and she decided, with the discipline of long practice, to believe the first thing. Fear was a currency and she refused to spend more than necessary.

She opened the throttle.

The old Perkins roared, the bow lifted, and the *Kestrel's Grace* came up onto plane with a shudder that rattled the teacups and probably loosened something else somewhere, and Coral Ashgrove cleared the breakwater at dawn running from a debt, a promise, and a compass with no needle, and the wind hit her face and—

She laughed. Out loud, alone, into the wind, like an idiot.

That was the part nobody warned you about, the part she'd forgotten between departures: that running felt *good*. That underneath the fear and the grief and the water seeping quietly into her bilge, there was this—speed, salt spray, the whole gray world opening up ahead of her like a page. She was thirty-one years old and broke and hunted and she had never once in her life felt as alive as she did steering a leaking boat toward trouble at seven knots over the harbor bar.

"We're idiots, you and me," she told the boat.

The boat, being a boat, agreed.

* * *

By full light the coast had become a rumor behind her—a green smudge under a bruised sky, the escarpment rising inland the way it always did, sudden and improbable, like the land had changed its mind halfway through being flat. She'd named the book of her life after that ridge without ever meaning to. The rough escarpment. The wall you didn't climb so much as survive falling down.

She steered southeast, toward the coordinates Ede Latch had pressed into her hands with his dying breath, and let the boat run while she worked the pump every twenty minutes and tried not to do arithmetic about the bilge.

The arithmetic did itself anyway. An inch of water per hour, maybe a bit more in a chop. The manual pump could beat that, comfortably, as long as her arms held out, as long as the seam didn't worsen, as long as the weather held, as long as nothing else went wrong. Four assumptions. In Coral's experience, you were lucky to get two.

Around midmorning the sky began to organize itself. Clouds stacking in the west, fat and gray-white, building with the unhurried confidence of something that knows it has all week. Monsoon season was supposed to be ten days out. The barometer on her bulkhead—Marta Osei's Christmas gift three years ago, inscribed *SO YOU'LL STOP ASKING ME FOR FORECASTS*—had been falling since dawn, slowly, steadily, like a stone sinking in honey.

"Ten days," Coral told it. "You're early."

The barometer declined to comment.

She ate a tin of peaches at the helm, spooning the syrup straight from the can, and spread Ede's map on the chart table, weighting the corners with the kettle and a wrench. In daylight, with the compass beside it, the thing looked less like a treasure map and more like a confession—Ede's shaky hand marking depths, bearings, a cistern, a phrase in the margin she'd read forty times now: *They never found all of us.*

She touched the letters lightly, the way you'd touch a scar.

"I'm going," she said. "I don't know what I'm delivering or to whom, but I'm going."

Somewhere out there was a daughter who may or may not want anything from her father's ghost. Somewhere out there was a cache that a dying man had traded away for a stranger's word. Somewhere out there, very possibly, was a navy launch that hadn't actually left.

And behind her—she checked the mirror of the horizon twice—behind her was everything else.

The harbor was gone now, folded into the haze. Her whole shabby, hard-won, insufficient life, dissolved into weather. She found she was gripping the wheel too hard and made herself let go, finger by finger.

Grief, she reminded herself, was just love with nowhere to put itself. She'd learned that from her mother, who'd learned it from nobody, who'd simply known things like that the way some people knew weather. You didn't get over leaving. You just got far enough away that the missing had to shout to reach you.

The *Kestrel's Grace* thumped through a swell, and beneath her feet, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of water finding its way in.

Coral sighed, took up the pump handle, and began to work.

* * *

It was nearly noon, the sun burning through the cloud in patches, the coastline long gone, when she saw the sail.

Small, single-masted, well out on her starboard quarter, running the same heading she was. At this distance it was impossible to tell anything about her except that she was there, and that she was there *here*, on the same empty stretch of sea, at the same hour, pointed the same way.

Could be a fisherman. Could be a trader running coastal. Could be coincidence, and coincidences happened every day, and most of them were harmless.

But Coral had spent a lifetime noticing when the sea got crowded, and the sea, today, was getting crowded.

She altered course five degrees south, casual, unhurried, the way a woman would who merely preferred a different line. Then she watched the mirror of the horizon over her shoulder and waited.

The sail altered course five degrees south.

Coral stood at the helm with the pump handle in one hand and her heart going like the engine, and she thought about the compass in the sock in the tool bag, and the map against her skin, and the promise she'd made to a dead man, and she said, to the boat, to the sea, to whoever was out there watching:

"Well. Come on, then."

The *Kestrel's Grace* ran on southeast, trailing a thin ribbon of weeping seam-water, toward the monsoon and the coordinates and whatever was waiting at the end of Ede Latch's map—and five miles astern, patient as tide, the strange sail followed.
