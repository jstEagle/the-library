## 8. The Stranger Aboard

Wren Vale had been many people in their life, but never, until this morning, a person who had lost a boat.

It was remarkable how much of a personality could be built out of nothing at all. A stolen boat required only the right slouch of the shoulders — defeated but not broken, the posture of someone whose whole world fit on the water and now didn't. A marine insurance clerk named Fen Ostrander required a lanyard, a clipboard, and a way of speaking that made every sentence sound like it was being filed in triplicate. Wren had worn Fen for three days now, docked at Port Calloway with a forged credential card and a rented room that smelled of mildew and other people's cigarettes, watching Coral Ashgrove's boat sit at its mooring like a tired dog waiting to be kicked.

The boat was called *Sparrowhawk*, which Wren found quietly funny, because there was nothing of the hawk about her. She was forty feet of patched steel and stubbornness, a salvage tug that had clearly been loved by someone who couldn't afford to love anything properly. Rust bloomed along her waterline like lichen. Her crane arm had been rewelded at least twice. And her owner — Wren had watched her through binoculars from the breakwater, cataloguing details for the report they were supposed to send — moved around her deck with the particular economy of a person who had learned that wasted motion was wasted money.

Coral Ashgrove dove like she was trying to make peace with the sea one brass fitting at a time. That was what the file said, more or less. What the file did not say was that when she came up the ladder with her mask pushed back and her hair plastered flat, she looked at the horizon the way other people looked at unpaid bills.

*You're doing necessary work,* Wren told themselves, the way they'd been telling themselves for eleven days.

The word *necessary* had begun to develop a smell. Like fish left too long in a bucket.

* * *

The radio crackled at dawn, as it always did. Wren sat on the floor of the rented room with their back against the bed frame, the handset cupped close to their mouth so the neighbors wouldn't hear.

"Ostrander. Status."

"Subject still at anchor," Wren said. "She's been running dive ops inside the restricted zone. I have the coordinates logged." They did. Three sets of them, transcribed in Fen Ostrander's tidy clerk's hand, each one a small betrayal written in pencil.

"And the item?"

"No confirmation yet. She's been selling scrap through the Harrow brokers. If she lifted something off that wreck, she hasn't tried to move it through normal channels."

A pause on the line, long enough that Wren could hear the wind on the other end, somewhere far away where the weather was already turning. Monsoon season was nine days out by the almanac, six by the look of the sky, and everyone in Port Calloway was either battening down or making one last run.

"She'll run before the storm," the voice said. "They always do. When she does, you're aboard. You get to the cache coordinates before she does, or you get them from her. Either way, we need what's in her possession. Understood?"

"Understood," Wren said.

"You understand what's at stake. This isn't salvage. This is containment."

"I understand," Wren said again, and hung up, and sat for a while looking at their own hands, which belonged to neither Fen Ostrander nor whoever the voice thought they were talking to, but only to Wren, who had signed up for this two years ago because it had seemed clean then. Necessary. The navy had sunk something it shouldn't have, and the truth of it needed to stay buried, and burying things was a job like any other.

Except Coral Ashgrove kept being a person. That was the problem. Files didn't prepare you for the way she'd argued with the harbormaster about mooring fees, all dry humor and flint, winning a concession she probably hadn't even wanted just because losing wasn't in her vocabulary. Files didn't mention that she fed gulls off her stern rail and pretended not to, flicking scraps over her shoulder while scowling at them for existing.

Wren looked at the radio. Then at the window, where the light was coming up gray-green and wrong, the color of a bruise deciding whether to form.

Nine days. Maybe six.

*You're doing necessary work.*

"Sure," Wren said aloud, to nobody. It sounded less convincing every time.

* * *

The plan, as originally drafted, had been elegant. Wren would simply be at the fuel dock when *Sparrowhawk* took on diesel, would strike up a conversation as Fen Ostrander, insurance clerk, would learn what could be learned, and would part ways with a handshake and a business card that led nowhere. Clean. Painless. Nobody hurt.

The plan had not accounted for Coral Ashgrove leaving harbor at four in the morning without filing a float plan, without buying fuel, and without, apparently, any intention of telling anyone anything.

Wren woke to the sound of her engine — a low, laboring chug that no amount of maintenance would ever sweeten — and made it to the pier in time to watch her running lights slide past the breakwater into the dark. No goodbyes. No provisions visible loaded aboard beyond a couple of jerry cans. She was cutting loose, and cutting loose fast, and every instinct Wren had said: *let her go, report it, let the professionals handle it.*

But the professionals, Wren knew exactly what the professionals did with loose ends. And besides — and this was the thought that Wren stepped over carefully, the way you step over a hole in a rotten deck, refusing to look down into it — besides, if she ran alone, into monsoon season, in a hull that Wren had personally watched weep rust-stain from a seam below the waterline, she might not run very far at all.

So Wren did the only thing left. Wren stole a boat.

Well. Borrowed. It was a fourteen-foot skiff belonging to a fisherman named Doss who owed Wren — owed Fen Ostrander, rather — a favor involving an insurance claim and some creative paperwork. Wren took it out past the breakwater with a borrowed outboard that coughed like a consumptive, and pointed it at the last smudge of *Sparrowhawk*'s lights, and began composing the lie.

That was the thing about lies. People thought they were hard. They weren't hard; they were just heavy. You carried enough of them and your shoulders learned the shape.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. The channel between Port Calloway and the outer islands ran three miles wide here, and *Sparrowhawk* was making maybe five knots, which told Wren two things: she was conserving fuel, and she was in no hurry to arrive anywhere, which meant she didn't entirely know where she was going yet. Good. A person without a destination could always be persuaded to take on passengers.

Wren brought the skiff alongside at a respectful distance and stood up in it, arms raised, the universal posture of *I am not a threat, please do not run me down.* The outboard chose that exact moment to die, which was, Wren reflected, either terrible luck or the single most persuasive piece of theater they had ever accidentally staged.

*Sparrowhawk* slowed. A figure appeared at the rail — Coral Ashgrove, hair tied back, a boathook in one hand and an expression on her face that could have opened oysters.

"You're a long way from the fuel dock, Ostrander," she called.

"The name's actually Vale," Wren called back. "Fen Ostrander is a work alias. Insurance fraud investigation, mostly boring, occasionally dangerous, currently extremely inconvenient." They gestured down at the skiff, which was shipping water over the transom in a manner that supported the next line beautifully. "My boat was stolen this morning. This one I borrowed to chase the thief. The thief got away. The outboard has given up. And you, Ms. Ashgrove, are the only vessel in the channel."

Coral looked at the skiff. At Wren. Back at the skiff. The silence stretched long enough that Wren had time to notice the gulls wheeling behind her stern, and the rust stain down her hull, and the fact that her dinghy davits were empty, meaning she had no tender, meaning she genuinely could not send Wren back even if she wanted to.

"That's a lot of story," she said, "for a man standing in a sinking rowboat."

"It's a lot of story because it's true. True stories usually are. Lies are tidier."

Something shifted at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An assessment. Wren had seen that look before, on customs officers and syndicate accountants and once, memorably, on a woman deciding whether to shoot them. It was the look of a person weighing a stranger against the cost of the alternative.

"Monsoon's coming," Coral said. "Six days, maybe less. I'm short-handed, my pump is temperamental, and I don't have time to babysit a castaway."

"I can pump. I can cook. I can read charts, fix electrical, and I know these waters better than most pilots." All true, as it happened, which was the other thing about lies: the best ones were load-bearing walls built out of true bricks.

"And why," Coral said, "would an insurance investigator know these waters better than most pilots?"

"Because investigating sunken claims means diving them," Wren said, "and I've been diving since I was fifteen."

Another silence. Somewhere behind them, thunder muttered, a long way off, practicing.

"If you're lying to me," Coral said finally, "I'll find out. I find everything out eventually. It's the one skill my life has taught me." She swung a rope ladder over the side. "Come up. Bring your bag. Leave the skiff — it's doing everyone a favor by drowning."

Wren hauled themselves up the ladder with a duffel that contained, among other things, a transmitter wrapped in oilcloth, a set of lockpicks, and a paperback romance novel they had bought genuinely for pleasure and now felt vaguely guilty about. As they came over the rail, Coral was already walking away toward the wheelhouse, calling over her shoulder:

"There's coffee if you make it yourself. I don't cook for strangers."

"You said I could—"

"I said you could come aboard. Cooking was implied to be your department. You offered."

And there it was — the first real smile, quick and crooked and gone, like a fish surfacing. Wren stood dripping on her deck, holding a bag full of betrayals, and felt something in their chest do a slow, treacherous roll that had absolutely nothing to do with the swell.

*This is fine,* Wren told themselves. *You're doing necessary work. You'll get the coordinates, send the signal, and walk away before anyone gets hurt.*

They went and made the coffee. It was terrible. Coral drank two cups and complained about both, and Wren found themselves memorizing the exact pitch of her complaints for no operational reason whatsoever.

* * *

By noon they had fallen into something that a generous observer might have called a rhythm and a honest one would have called mutual surveillance conducted through chores.

Wren pumped the bilge — twice an hour, as instructed, and the water coming up was greasy and persistent, confirming everything about that seam. Coral conned the boat south along the coast, consulting a chart she kept folded to show only half its surface, which Wren noted and did not comment on. Half a chart was a chart with secrets, and secrets were, professionally speaking, Wren's favorite food.

"So," Coral said, late afternoon, when the coast had become a green smear astern and the first real wind of the front was worrying the wave tops into whitecaps. "Vale. First name?"

"Wren."

"Wren." She tried it like tasting a coin. "Nobody's christened a child Wren since the war."

"My mother had a sense of humor and a bird book."

"And Ostrander? Was he in the bird book too?"

"Fen Ostrander is in the Port Callay insurance registry, third drawer down, along with a laminated badge and a very convincing letterhead." Wren kept their voice light, kept their hands busy coiling line. "You want the long version or the short version?"

"I want the version that explains why a fraud investigator chased me out of harbor in a leaking skiff instead of, say, reporting me to the actual authorities. Because that's the part of your story with a hole in it, Vale. Everything else fits. That doesn't."

Wren had prepared for this question. Had prepared three answers, ranked by plausibility. What came out instead was none of them.

"Because the authorities would impound your boat and put you ashore somewhere with nothing," they said, "and I've watched that happen to people, and I didn't want to watch it happen to you."

Silence. Coral looked at them across the wheelhouse, and the wind hummed in the rigging, and Wren understood with sudden clarity that they had just gone off-script in a way that could not be walked back — because the sentence was true. Every word of it. It had simply bypassed the checkpoint where Wren usually vetted such things.

"That's either the most honest thing anyone's said to me this year," Coral said slowly, "or the best lie I've ever heard."

"Could be both."

"It could." She turned back to the wheel. "Bunk's aft, port side. Pump schedule's chalked on the bulkhead. And Vale —"

"Yes?"

"When we make landfall wherever we're making landfall, you and I are going to finish this conversation. The version with the hole in it."

"Looking forward to it," Wren said, and meant it, and hated that they meant it, and went aft to pump the bilge.

* * *

Night fell early and ugly. The barometer had been dropping all day, faster than the almanac promised, and by full dark the wind had backed around and begun to shove the sea at them in long, impatient shoves. Coral stood her watch at the wheel with her collar up, and Wren sat in the wheelhouse doorway pretending to study charts and actually studying her — the set of her jaw, the way she talked to the boat under her breath, small encouragements, *come on, girl, hold together* — and felt the guilt in their chest sharpening, honing itself, turning into something with edges.

Because here was the trouble. Here was the whole trouble, laid out plain.

Two years ago, this assignment had been a shape on paper. *Asset embedded with salvage operator. Recover sensitive material. Containment protocol.* Shapes on paper didn't feed gulls. Shapes on paper didn't complain about coffee they'd made themselves and drink it anyway. Shapes on paper didn't stand four hours at a wheel in rising weather because they trusted no one else to hold the course — and then, at midnight, without ceremony, hand the wheel to a stranger they'd known for sixteen hours and say, "Wake me if the pump falls behind," and *sleep*, actually sleep, curled in the wheelhouse chair with her boots off, trusting them.

Trusting them. With the boat, with the night, with whatever half-chart secret she was carrying south.

Wren sat with the transmitter in their lap for a long time, the dial glow the only light, the wind screaming thinly through the stays. One message. Thirty seconds of code. *Subject underway south-southwest, coordinates to follow, recommend interception at—*

Their thumb rested on the key.

Below them, faintly, the bilge pump chugged its stubborn rhythm, keeping the sea out of the boat, keeping everybody afloat. Coral murmured something in her sleep. A gull — impossibly, absurdly, miles from shore — cried once overhead and was gone.

Wren closed the transmitter case without sending anything, and told themselves it was tactical. Better to establish trust first. Better to learn what she carried before calling it in. Better, better, better — the old litany, worn smooth as sea glass from handling.

But underneath the litany, in the place where Wren kept the things they refused to examine, something had already changed sides. Something had looked at Coral Ashgrove asleep in a storm and thought, not *target*, not *asset*, not *loose end* —

but *person*. *Her*. *Don't you dare*.

Ahead, dead ahead, low on the black horizon, lightning stitched the clouds together in one long silent seam — and then another, closer, and the rain arrived all at once like a door slamming, and Coral was awake instantly, boots on, hand on the wheel, shouting over the wind:

"Vale! Get forward and lash that crane arm — this is going to be a rough one!"

Wren went.
