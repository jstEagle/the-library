## 9. First Squall

* * *

**Coral**

The sky told her first.

Coral had spent eleven years reading water the way other people read letters from debt collectors—looking for the bad news hiding in the margins. The sea off the southern channel had gone the color of old pewter, flat and shining and wrong, and the wind had died so completely that the sails hung slack as laundry on a line. Her father used to call it "the held breath." He'd learned it from his father, who had presumably learned it from some ancestor who'd been drowned by weather and wanted the family to do better.

"Barometer's falling like my credit rating," she muttered, tapping the little brass instrument bolted by the wheel. It had been her grandfather's. It was, she reflected, the only thing of value she owned that nobody was currently trying to repossess.

Behind her, Wren Vale sat on the cabin roof doing an extremely poor job of pretending not to watch her.

They'd been aboard two days now. Two days of Wren being relentlessly, aggressively helpful—coiling lines that didn't need coiling, wiping down surfaces that were already clean, asking questions shaped like small talk but weighted like ballast. *Where did you learn to dive? Where were you before Harrow? Do you have family?* Each one delivered with that mild insurance-clerk smile that Coral trusted approximately as far as she could throw the engine block.

She hadn't answered any of them honestly. She was fairly sure Wren knew that. She was equally sure Wren appreciated the professional courtesy.

"Winds backing around to the northeast," Coral said aloud, because silence had started to feel like a chess game and she was tired of playing. "We'll want the second reef in before noon."

"Is that a lot of wind?" Wren asked. There was something almost endearing about how badly they hid their ignorance of boats. Almost. Endearment was a trap and Coral knew all its teeth.

"It's enough."

The squall came at them an hour later, exactly as promised, and it came like a bill collector: no warning you could use, arriving all at once, and interested only in what you owed.

The horizon went black-green in the space of ten minutes. Then the wind hit—not gradually, not politely, but like the whole sky had leaned over the rail. The boat heeled hard enough to slide everything on the chart table into the bilge, and Coral found herself hauling the wheel against thirty knots of very personal animosity.

"Reef it!" she shouted. "Main halyard, then the tack! Move!"

Wren moved. To their credit—and Coral noted the credit grudgingly, the way you note that a pickpocket has nice hands—they didn't freeze, didn't ask questions, didn't panic. They scrambled forward along the pitching deck, clipped on, and hauled when she yelled haul and cleated when she yelled cleat, and if their knots were the knots of a person who'd learned sailing exclusively from diagrams, they held.

Rain came sideways. The world shrank to gray noise and the screaming of the rigging and the wheel fighting her like a live thing. And through all of it, one thought kept surfacing in Coral's mind with terrible clarity:

*The compass is in my jacket pocket.*

She'd taken to carrying it. Not in the cabin, where Wren might find it, but against her body, where it sat like a second heartbeat—a ring of numbers etched in brass, pointing at nothing, meaning everything. Ede Latch's map was hidden under the floorboards. The compass rode above her breast pocket, and right now she was being thrown around a wet deck in a rising sea wearing a jacket with a button that had been loose for three weeks.

You can see this coming. So could she, actually, in the half-second it happened—the boat slamming off a wave's shoulder, the wheel kicking, her hand flying out for the shroud and missing, the jacket flapping open—

The compass left her pocket in a bright brass arc, spun once against the rain, and began the short, irreversible journey toward the sea.

Coral made a sound she would later deny making. It was not a word. It was possibly several words fused together under pressure.

And then Wren Vale—soaked, graceless, clinging to the lifelines with one hand—did something physically impossible-looking. They threw themselves flat across the tilting deck, slid six feet of rain-slick fiberglass on their stomach like a child on a banister, and caught the compass out of the air an arm's length above the scuppers, where the water was already reaching up for it with greedy white fingers.

Then they lay there, cheek against the deck, holding the compass aloft in one dripping fist, looking like a man who had just caught a grenade and wasn't yet sure whether throwing it back was an option.

"GET US FLAT," Wren bellowed, "AND I'LL CONSIDER GIVING THIS BACK."

It might have been a joke. It might have been a negotiation. In the roar of the squall, Coral genuinely could not tell, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed fishhook.

* * *

**Wren**

Later—much later, when the squall had spent itself and moved off east to ruin someone else's afternoon—Wren sat below with the compass still in their hand and tried to work out what exactly they had done.

Their handler's voice crackled faintly in memory, patient and reasonable as ever: *Your job is observation, Vale. Position, heading, contacts. Nothing more. You are a pair of eyes, not a pair of hands.*

Well. Their hands had just caught the single most important object in the entire operation, quite literally out of the sky, and now those hands were refusing, quietly and stubbornly, to put it anywhere except back in Coral Ashgrove's palm.

That was the part Wren couldn't explain. They'd had four full seconds. Four seconds lying on that deck with rain hammering their spine, during which a competent operative would have palmed the compass, waited for dark, and radioed home. Four seconds in which Wren had instead thought—with a clarity that felt less like thought and more like vertigo—*she looks at this thing like it's the last honest thing she owns.*

So they'd handed it back. Held it out across the soaked cockpit, brass glinting dully in the washed light, and watched Coral take it with both hands like someone receiving a verdict.

"You could have kept it," she said. Flat. Watchful.

"I could have," Wren agreed. "But I've seen what happens to people who steal from you. You make that face."

"What face?"

"The one you're making right now."

Coral looked down and discovered she was, in fact, making a face. It seemed to annoy her considerably. "I don't have faces."

"Everyone has faces. Yours are just better defended than most." Wren wrung out their shirt over the cockpit drain, which was the closest thing to a victory lap available. "Besides, I couldn't have used it. I don't know what it does. It doesn't point north. As a compass it's arguably worse than nothing."

"It points somewhere," Coral said, and then closed her mouth with the finality of a vault door.

Wren smiled and let it go. That was the technique, anyway. Let it go, let her think you weren't collecting, let her relax. Except somewhere between the second day and the squall, the technique had stopped feeling like technique and started feeling like conversation, and Wren was beginning to suspect that the difference mattered more than their handler would ever understand.

Because here was the thing Wren had not reported, and would not report, and was running out of ways to justify not reporting: Coral Ashgrove was not what the file said. The file said desperate. The file said indebted, isolated, predictable, motivated purely by money. The file had not mentioned that she sang—badly, tunelessly, under her breath—to the engine while working on it, apparently believing the vibration helped. The file had not mentioned that she'd given half her fresh water to a fishing skiff in trouble on day one without mentioning it to anyone. The file had definitely not mentioned the way she'd looked at Wren when they handed back the compass: surprised, and then immediately furious at herself for being surprised, as though kindness were a currency she'd stopped accepting years ago because the exchange rate was always robbery.

*Observation,* the handler had said. Fine. Wren was observing. They were observing extremely thoroughly. They were observing so much that the observations had begun to feel like a weight they'd have to carry ashore someday, and they had no idea what port would take the cargo.

Above them, Coral's boots crossed the deck, checking things, tightening things, being competent in that unglamorous way that Wren was coming to like more than was operationally advisable.

"You can come up," she called down, without turning around. "I know you're not seasick. Seasick people don't organize the tool drawer."

"I was organizing it alphabetically."

"Nobody alphabetizes a tool drawer, Wren. That's not a thing people do. That's a thing spies do when they're nervous."

Wren climbed up into the rinsed gold of late afternoon and decided, privately, that being seen through was not entirely unpleasant, provided it was her doing the seeing.

* * *

**Coral**

The trouble with nearly dying together, Coral discovered, was that it created a debt. And Coral Ashgrove had strong opinions about debts, most of which involved never owing anyone anything, an opinion her creditors had historically failed to respect.

Wren had saved the compass. Not her—the compass. She kept repeating this to herself while they ate salted fish and hard bread in the cockpit as evening came down purple and calm, the sea smoothed back over its tantrum like a child pretending it hadn't cried.

"That was decent work today," she said finally, because owing a debt silently was worse than acknowledging it. "On the deck. With the—" she gestured vaguely at the air where the compass had flown. "Fast."

"I grew up catching things," Wren said. "Three younger cousins. If food fell off the table, it was gone forever. You learn."

"That's the saddest origin story for good reflexes I've ever heard."

"It comes with a free tragic backstory. Very fashionable."

Despite herself—despite every instinct honed by years of syndicate men smiling at her across tables—Coral laughed. It came out rusty, like a winch that hadn't turned in a season, but it came out. Wren looked absurdly pleased about it, sitting there with salt-crusted hair plastered at angles that suggested their haircut had also been self-administered, and something in Coral's chest did a small, inconvenient maneuver that she chose to classify as indigestion.

"Why insurance?" she asked. It was a test question, lobbed casually. "Marine insurance. Of all the covers a person could wear."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered behind Wren's eyes. Calculation, or guilt, or maybe just the effort of keeping a lie oiled and running. "Because it's the one job where everyone expects you to be boring," they said. "People tell boring people everything. They assume you're not listening."

"And are you listening?"

"To you?" Wren turned the question over honestly, which was somehow worse than a smooth answer. "Constantly. It's becoming a problem."

The light was going. Out past the breakwater of the evening, the water lay long and pewter-colored, and Coral thought about the navy launch she'd seen idling beyond her own harbor two nights ago, watching, waiting, patient as arithmetic. She thought about Bram Harrow's voice on her dock: *Triple pay, Ashgrove. Just a dive.* She thought about Ede Latch dying in a chair with blood on his lips, pressing a folded map into her hands like a man passing a lit fuse.

Everybody wanted something. That was the ocean she actually lived in; the wet one was just where she worked.

But Wren had caught the compass. Had held it out instead of holding onto it. Had organized her tool drawer alphabetically, which no thief bothered to do, because thieves traveled light.

"Tomorrow we make for the Vexholme Channel," she said, before she could stop herself, before the sensible Coral who survived small could clamp a hand over the reckless Coral who was apparently still alive in there after all these years. "There's a cove on the north shore. Good holding, fresh water. We'll need to provision before the monsoon proper sets in."

Wren went very still, in the way of a person being handed something fragile. "You're telling me your route."

"I'm telling you where we're anchoring tomorrow night." Coral met their eyes across the little circle of lamplight. "If you've got a radio hidden somewhere and a handler waiting on coordinates, tonight would be the night to use it. I'd rather know now than later."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the hull ticked and settled, and the sea breathed against it, patient.

"No radio," Wren said quietly. And then, with the smallest hitch, the smallest fracture showing through the mild clerk's surface: "No handler. Not anymore. I'll tell you everything eventually—I'm working up to it. But not tonight. Tonight I'd rather just—" they gestured at the sky, the water, the lamp, the improbable fact of being alive and dry and fed, "—sit here. If that's allowed."

Coral studied them for a long moment. Every survival instinct she owned lined up along her ribs and voted no.

She passed them the bread anyway.

What she didn't see—what neither of them saw, wrapped in their careful new truce—was the shape riding the darkening water three miles astern. Low, slow, running without lights. A patrol cutter settling onto their wake like a shadow deciding whose door to stand outside.

And what Coral especially didn't see was the map under the floorboards, and the way the compass's ring of numbers matched the third line of Ede Latch's spidery handwriting—and what that match meant about whose waters they were sailing into, and why a dying man had chosen *her*, specifically, to carry it there.
