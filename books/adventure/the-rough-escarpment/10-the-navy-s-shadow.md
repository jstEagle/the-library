## 10. The Navy's Shadow

The cutter appeared an hour after dawn, and it did not appear by accident.

Coral spotted it the way she'd learned to spot everything that mattered—peripherally, while pretending to be interested in something else. She was coiling rope on deck with the exaggerated diligence of a woman who had never once done anything suspicious in her life, and beyond the port rail, maybe three miles off, a gray shape sat on the horizon like a held breath.

"Company," she said.

Wren looked up from the engine hatch, a smear of grease across one cheekbone that made them look younger and considerably less trustworthy. "Where?"

"Don't look."

Wren looked. Of course they looked. Coral was beginning to compile a mental list of Wren Vale's habits, and *doing exactly what you tell them not to* was climbing steadily toward the top.

"That's a patrol cutter," Wren said.

"It is."

"They don't patrol this stretch. There's nothing here to patrol."

"There's us," Coral said, and tied off the coil with more force than the rope deserved.

* * *

For two hours the cutter held its distance—close enough to count her flag halyards if it wanted to, far enough to claim it wasn't following anyone. It didn't hail them. It didn't change course when they changed course, which was somehow worse; a ship that matched your every turn without acknowledging you existed was either a coincidence or a message, and Coral had never once been anybody's coincidence.

She steered. Wren fidgeted.

"You could wave," Wren offered eventually. "Friendly-like. Show we're just two honest salvagers out for a morning cruise."

"With what? We're flying no colors, our registration numbers are painted over, and half our deck cargo is brass I lifted from a wreck inside a restricted zone. If I wave at that cutter, the next thing that happens is a boarding party, and the thing after that is a cell."

"So we don't wave."

"We sail boring," Coral said. "Boring is armor. Boring is the most valuable cargo on this boat." She adjusted the wheel a degree, unhurried, a woman with nowhere particular to be. "You'd be amazed how much trouble a person can avoid simply by being too dull to remember."

"I'll take your word for it."

"You should. It's kept me alive through three syndicate audits and one wedding."

Wren's mouth twitched. "Whose wedding?"

"Mine. Long story. The marriage lasted eleven days and the debt lasted six years, which tells you everything about my judgment back then." She said it lightly, because she'd had years of practice saying it lightly, but Wren went quiet in a way that suggested they'd heard the weight under it anyway. That was another item for the list: Wren listened like someone cataloguing. The question was whose catalogue.

The cutter shadowed them past a line of low green keys, past a shoal marked by a leaning stake bleached white as bone, past the long slow swell where the shelf dropped away into blue-black water. Then, at midday, as casually as a cat losing interest in a mouse that had proven insufficiently entertaining, it turned away and shrank back into the haze.

Coral watched it go until there was nothing to watch.

"Well," said Wren. "That was friendly."

"No," Coral said. "That was *patient*. Friendly would have been better."

* * *

They ate below decks—salted fish, hard bread, and the last of the coffee stretched until it was less a beverage than a rumor of one—and Coral spread Ede Latch's map on the chart table and weighted its corners with mugs.

She had looked at the map before, of course. Many times. She knew its coastlines, its soundings written in a prospector's cramped hand, the little inked anchor symbols marking safe anchorage. What she hadn't done—not properly, not with the patience the thing deserved—was read it against the compass.

She took the brass instrument from its oilcloth now and set it beside the map. No needle. Just that ring of numbers around the rim, etched fine as watchwork: 7, 19, 3, 44, 12, 8... She turned it slowly under the lamp. On the underside, almost invisible unless you caught the light right, a second ring of numbers moved within the first when the outer case rotated—a mechanism, not a decoration. Someone had built this thing to *do* something.

And Ede's map, she saw now, was covered in small circled figures. She'd taken them for soundings. Soundings were numbers; her eye had filed them away and moved on. But soundings didn't repeat. These did. The same clusters, again and again, scattered along the coast like a sentence written in a language made entirely of digits.

Her skin prickled.

"Wren," she said. "Come look at this. And touch nothing."

"Touching nothing is my best skill."

She set the compass on the map, aligned its zero-mark with the map's north arrow, and began matching numbers. The circled figures on the map weren't random. They were ordered—she could see it once she stopped looking for depth readings and started looking for pattern. A sequence. And the sequence matched the ring on the compass, number for number, in order, if you started from the mark Ede had drawn as a tiny star near the top of the sheet.

"It's a key," Wren said slowly. "The map tells you how to set the compass, and the compass—" They stopped.

"The compass points somewhere," Coral finished. "Or it *would*, if I knew what it pointed at." She rotated the outer case, setting the ring to match the map's sequence, and felt the mechanism catch—tiny brass teeth finding each other with a click she felt in her teeth. When she'd entered the last figure, the inner ring stopped moving entirely. Locked. Set.

Set to what?

She looked down at the map again, really looked, tracing the coastline Ede had drawn against the charts she knew by heart—the same coast, the same bays, the same barrier islands. Except. Her finger stopped on a stretch of shoreline drawn in slightly different ink, older ink, and annotated in Ede's hand with a single word she'd skimmed past a dozen times because it had seemed like a place name:

*Escarpment.*

Below it, smaller: *old range lights. decommissioned '09.*

Old naval territory. Decommissioned gunnery ranges, the kind the navy never actually gave up—the kind they still closed to traffic "for safety," year after year, decade after decade, safety being eternal. The cache wasn't hidden in some anonymous cove. It was tucked inside a wound the navy had stitched shut and posted guards around, and Ede Latch had known it, had drawn it plain as day, and had handed the whole business to a stranger bleeding on his floor.

Coral sat back. The cabin felt suddenly smaller.

"He chose me," she said.

"What?"

"Ede. He didn't give me a treasure map. He gave me—" She gestured at the compass, the map, the neat little circled numbers. "He gave me a job he couldn't do himself, aimed straight into the one place on this coast the navy watches closest. He picked a salvage diver with debts and a boat and no family to ask questions. That's not generosity, Wren. That's *procurement*. I'm not his heir. I'm his tool."

The words came out steadier than she felt. Inside, something was tilting—that particular vertigo of standing on a deck you'd thought was solid and feeling the whole structure sway beneath you. She had told herself a comfortable story these past days: dying old man, last request, good deed done cheap. Stories were ballast. You carried them so you didn't capsize. And now here was the truth sitting on her chart table, and the truth was that a stranger had looked at her drowning life and seen a delivery mechanism.

"You don't know that," Wren said.

"I know the navy followed me out of harbor. I know a cutter spent four hours learning my heading. Whatever Ede hid out there, other people already died getting close to it, or close to him, and he handed the coordinates to the first diver who patched his ribs." She laughed, short and unamused. "I keep waiting to feel angry. Mostly I feel *flattered*, which is worse. He made me feel chosen. That's how you get people to carry bombs."

Wren was very still. Coral noticed the stillness, noted it, filed it. There was a version of this moment where she demanded answers—where she grabbed the front of their shirt and asked who they radioed and why a marine insurance clerk knew what a decommissioned gunnery range meant. She wasn't ready for that version yet. Information was worth more than confrontation, and besides—selfishly, shamefully—she wasn't sure she wanted to hear the answer while trapped on a thirty-foot boat with the monsoon coming.

Instead she said, "You knew about the ranges. When I mentioned the escarpment, you didn't ask what it was."

"Marine insurance covers coastal hazards," Wren said. "It's in the literature."

"Mm." Coral folded the map along its old creases, careful, deliberate. "Well. Here's what's in my literature, Wren. Somebody wants whatever's at those coordinates badly enough to put a warship on a salvage scow. Which means it isn't treasure. Treasure makes people greedy. This makes people *nervous*. Nervous is expensive." She looked up. "So before we get any closer to that escarpment, you and I are going to have a conversation about what you're actually doing on my boat. Not tonight. But soon. And I'd start practicing your answer now, because I'm very good at telling when people lie. I do it professionally."

Something flickered across Wren's face—guilt, maybe, or relief, or the strange gratitude of a liar who has finally been caught lying well enough to respect the catcher.

"Fair," Wren said quietly.

"And Wren—if you're going to betray me, do it after we fix the hull seam. I need the hands."

That startled a real laugh out of them, tired and crooked. "Noted. Betrayal scheduled for Thursday."

"Thursday's no good. I've got a rendezvous with a storm."

* * *

She took the last watch alone, as she always did. Wren slept in the lee of the cabin, curled like a comma, and Coral stood at the wheel under a sky rinsed clean by the afternoon wind and let herself think.

Ede Latch, dying, breathing through wet cloth lungs, pressing a map into her hands: *deliver it to my daughter, or burn it.* She'd thought the promise was the errand. Now she understood the promise was the payload. Sera Latch wasn't a daughter to be found; she was a destination. Everything—the map, the compass, the dying man's trembling urgency—had been aimed at her like a round down a barrel, and Coral was the powder charge, and somewhere ahead the navy's old escarpment waited like a target painted on the world.

A weapon doesn't get to choose what it fires at. But a person does.

That was the difference, she decided, watching the dark water slide past. That was the whole difference, and she intended to keep hold of it. Ede had chosen her because she was desperate and alone and easy to aim. Fine. Desperate people were also the only ones with nothing left to lose, and alone people were very hard to threaten, and divers, as a class, were excellent at going places nobody wanted them to go.

Behind her, in the cabin, Wren murmured something in their sleep. A name, maybe. Or a number.

Coral checked the compass in its oilcloth one more time—locked, patient, pointing at nothing she could see yet—and then she put it away, and steered into the night, and tried not to think about how the cutter had peeled away so easily, so *confidently*, as if it already knew where she was going.

As if it didn't need to follow her at all.

By morning the barometer had begun to fall, and the horizon to the east wore a bruise-colored smudge that hadn't been there yesterday, and Wren came on deck, looked at it, and said, "That's early."

"Yes," Coral said. "Everything is."
