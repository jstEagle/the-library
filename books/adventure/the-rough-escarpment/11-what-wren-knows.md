## 11. What Wren Knows

The radio lived in the false bottom of Wren's duffel bag, beneath a layer of spare clothes that had never once been worn for their stated purpose. It was a good hiding spot. It had been a good hiding spot on three previous assignments, and Wren had begun to suspect that the problem wasn't the hiding spot at all.

It was eleven o'clock at night, Coral was asleep in the cabin with the particular boneless abandon of someone who'd spent the day hauling pump handles and arguing with an engine, and Wren sat on deck with their back against the wheelhouse wall, knees drawn up, radio balanced on their thighs like something fragile and venomous.

*The mission was supposed to be simple.* That was the phrase they kept returning to, turning it over like a stone looking for the soft underside. *Locate the diver. Confirm she has the item. Report position. Extraction follows.* Four steps. Even Bram Harrow's people could count to four, though Wren had privately doubted it during every meeting they'd ever endured in that smoke-scented back office with its map of the coast pinned up like a butcher's diagram.

Simple. Locate, confirm, report.

Wren keyed the transmit switch and spoke into the dark.

"Kestrel to Homeplate. Kestrel to Homeplate. Sitrep."

Static answered first — the long, patient static of a frequency that belonged to nobody, the sea hissing its indifference through the speaker. Then, faintly, the click of a channel opening.

"Homeplate reads." The voice was dry as old rope. Voss. Of course it was Voss. The man took the night shifts because he claimed sleep made him stupid, which Wren had always felt explained rather than excused his personality. "Report."

Wren looked down at the radio. Then out at the water, black and breathing under a sky half-scoured of cloud. Somewhere below the waterline, the hull seam they'd been fighting all day was holding by stubbornness and a length of rubber sheeting and Coral Ashgrove's refusal to lose.

"The asset is mobile," Wren said. "Heading south along the coastal shelf. Condition of vessel: marginal. Condition of asset:"

They stopped.

*Condition of asset.* That was what the file called her. ASSET — SALVAGE DIVER — HARROW DEBT — PROBABLE CUSTODIAN OF ITEM. Two pages, typed, with a photograph stapled to the corner showing a woman squinting into harbor light, hair tied back with what appeared to be electrical tape, expression suggesting the photographer was about to be thrown off the pier.

Wren had memorized the file in an afternoon. They had not memorized the way she hummed while working — badly, tunelessly, a habit she clearly didn't know she had. They had not memorized the fact that she talked to her boat's engine when she thought no one was listening, coaxing it in a low voice like a sick animal: *"Come on, you ugly old thing, you've survived worse than me."* They had not memorized that she'd given Wren the last of the coffee this morning without comment, without even appearing to notice she'd done it, pouring the cup and handing it over the way you'd hand someone an oar. As if it were simply what hands were for.

"Condition of asset," Wren repeated, "is functional."

A pause on the line. Voss's pauses had texture. This one contained skepticism.

"That's not a condition report, Kestrel. That's a weather report. Does she have the item?"

"I believe so."

"You believe." Another pause, longer, saltier. "Kestrel, I'm going to say this once, because it's late and I'm charitable at hours when I shouldn't be. Harrow's people are moving. You've been dark for two days. When a fast boat shows up alongside your little dinghy — and one will — the only thing between you and a very short career is whether we know where you are. So give me a position."

Wren's thumb rested on the transmit key. The sea made its small sounds against the hull. Below deck, Coral turned over in her sleep, and the bunk frame creaked, and Wren found themself listening to that creak the way other people listened to music.

Here was the thing Wren had not put in any report, had barely admitted to themself except in the small hours when honesty crept aboard like a rat: *the mission had stopped being simple the moment Coral Ashgrove had said yes.*

Not the moment they'd met — that had been theater, rehearsed, the stolen-boat story delivered with just the right crack of desperation. Wren was good at theater. Wren had been good at theater since before they'd had a name anyone used twice. No, the theater hadn't cracked anything. What had cracked things was smaller and stupider. It was the morning she'd caught them lying about knowing how to cook rice — they'd burned it, spectacularly, into a single fused carbon brick — and instead of being angry she'd laughed until she had to sit down on the deck, actually sat down, wheezing, and said, "You're useless, Vale, absolutely useless, and I've never been less worried about anything," and then shown them how to do it properly, standing behind them, guiding their hands, close enough that Wren had forgotten entirely what rice was for.

That was the moment. Or maybe it was the squall, when the compass had gone skittering across the tilted deck toward the rail and Wren had caught it — pure reflex, no decision in it at all — and seen her face in the lightning-flash: white, wide-eyed, and then, unbelievably, grateful. Trusting. She'd taken the compass back from their hand like it was nothing. Like Wren was nothing dangerous at all.

People kept doing that. Treating Wren like a person. It was disorienting. It was, Wren suspected, how you got spies killed — not by counterintelligence, but by kindness.

"Kestrel." Voss again, sharper now. "Position. Now."

And here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: give the position, and within six hours a cutter or a syndicate boat would come alongside, and Coral Ashgrove would learn what Wren was, and everything after that would be the ugly part. Refuse, and Voss would flag them non-responsive, and the machinery would move without them, and the outcome would be the same only crueler, and Wren would be a loose end besides. Loose ends got collected. Everyone in this business knew the rhyme.

There was no third option. There was never a third option. That was the whole design of the work — options sanded away until all that remained was obedience.

Except.

Except that Wren had spent the evening watching Coral chart their course by lamplight, tongue caught between her teeth, and had noticed — because noticing people was the job, the one skill they'd never managed to unlearn — that she'd marked three different anchorages along the route, each annotated in her cramped handwriting. Not escape routes. *Shelter* routes. Places to run if the weather turned or the pumps failed. And in the margin beside the third one, small enough that she probably thought no one would ever read it, she'd written: *if W gets seasick again, stop here.*

She'd written *if W gets seasick again.* She was planning around Wren's weaknesses. Nobody planned around a target's weaknesses. You exploited them.

"Kestrel, confirm receipt. Position, or I log you dark."

Wren looked at the radio. The radio, being a radio, offered no counsel.

"Homeplate," Wren said, "be advised. Asset is not traveling alone."

Silence. Then, carefully: "Explain."

"She picked up a passenger mid-channel. Stranger, vessel lost, standard story. I've assessed the passenger as low threat and high utility. Passenger is handling navigation support." A breath. "Passenger is me, Voss. I'm the passenger. I'm aboard. I've been aboard for four days."

The static seemed to lean in.

"You're telling me you've gone native."

"I'm telling you the situation changed."

"The situation never changes, Kestrel. That's the point of situations. People change, and then they call it the situation." A sound on the line that might have been Voss lighting whatever he smoked at night, some terrible local cigar he pretended was a pipe. "Listen to me. Whatever you think you've found out there — a person, a conscience, a reason — it isn't real. It's weather. It passes. What doesn't pass is what happens to you when Harrow decides you're worth more as an example than as an employee. Give me the position, Wren. I'll even spell it kindly in the file. *Delayed by circumstances.* You know how the words work. You've hidden behind them your whole career."

Wren closed their eyes.

The truth was that Wren didn't fully know why they'd stayed past the point of professional necessity. Oh, they had reasons — reasons were cheap, the mind manufactured them like a bilge manufactured slime. *Better to monitor from inside. Better to control the timeline. Better to steer her toward the cache and let the navy's own coordinates do the work.* All plausible. All, Wren now understood, scaffolding erected around a much simpler structure: *I didn't want to leave yet.*

The mission was supposed to be simple. Locate, confirm, report. But nobody had warned Wren about the part where the target fixed your rice and remembered you got seasick and slept ten feet away, breathing slow, trusting, while you sat above her with a transmitter in your lap deciding whether she was a person or a pin on somebody's map.

"Voss," Wren said quietly. "I can't give you the position."

"Can't or won't?"

There it was. The question with teeth. Wren turned it over honestly, because Voss deserved that much, and because lying to yourself was the one lie that always compounded interest.

"Won't," Wren said. "I'll get you the item's status. I'll get you the cache location when I have it confirmed — that much serves everyone, including the people who deserve it least. But her position stays mine. If Harrow wants her, Harrow can find her himself, the old-fashioned way, with effort."

"You understand what you're saying."

"I understand exactly what I'm saying. I've understood it for about two days. I've just been waiting for my mouth to catch up."

Another pause. When Voss spoke again, the dryness had gone out of his voice, replaced by something almost gentle, which from Voss was more frightening than any threat. "Wren. I've read your file. All of it, including the parts that aren't in your file. You were built for this work because there was nothing in you to compromise. That was the assessment. I signed it." He drew on the cigar; the exhale crackled the speaker. "So either the assessment was wrong, or you've grown something new out here in the salt air, and I genuinely don't know which possibility disturbs me more. Either way: you have until dawn. After that, I report you dark, and dark assets get handled. You know how. You've done the handling."

The channel clicked closed.

Wren sat with the radio humming its dead-air hum, and the night sat with them, and for a long time neither moved.

Then Wren did the thing they'd known they were going to do since the moment Voss had said *dawn*, possibly since the moment Coral had handed them the coffee cup, possibly — if they were honest in the ruthless way the trade demanded — since the photograph in the file, since the electrical-tape hair tie, since the first flicker of a thought that had felt dangerously like recognition: *I know that tiredness. I've worn that face.*

They opened the duffel. They lifted out the false bottom. And they set about destroying the radio with the same methodical care they'd once used to maintain it.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the strange part. No ocean burial, no hammer, no fire — drama left evidence, and evidence left questions. Wren simply disassembled it. Antenna coiled and snapped into thirds. Battery casing cracked, cells dropped overboard separately, fifty yards apart, where no dredge would ever marry them again. Circuit board folded until the solder joints surrendered with tiny clicks, like knuckles cracking. The housing went into the bilge scrap bin, where it would rust among honest junk within the week. Twenty minutes, start to finish. By the end there was no radio. There was a memory of a radio, distributed across the seabed, deniable forever.

Wren sat back and wiped their hands on their trousers and discovered, somewhat to their surprise, that they were shaking. Not with fear. With the vertigo of a man who has just sawed off the branch between himself and the ground and realized the ground is very far away and also, somehow, home.

No more reports. No more coded updates. From this minute forward, whatever Wren Vale did next would be done as themselves — whoever that was, this unnamed thing growing in the salt air, this person who caught compasses and burned rice and had apparently decided, somewhere between the squall and tonight, that Coral Ashgrove was not a target.

*She keeps being a person,* Wren thought, and almost laughed. That was the whole disease, right there. Every handler's nightmare, every spy's undoing, compressed into one absurd sentence. The file had said ASSET. The world kept saying CORAL. And Wren had discovered they could no longer hear the first word without the second one correcting it.

Below deck, the bunk creaked again, and then — footsteps, soft, bare feet on boards. The cabin hatch opened and Coral leaned out into the night, hair loose, eyes half-shut, wearing the expression of someone who has woken for no good reason and resents it mildly.

"You're talking to yourself up here," she said. "I heard voices."

"Just the radio," Wren said, before their brain could stop their mouth, and then, smoothly, seamlessly, with the ease of long practice: "Reception's garbage out here anyway. I chucked it in the scrap bin. Wasn't getting anything but static and my own bad decisions."

Coral squinted at them. In the lamplight spilling up from the cabin, her gaze was sharp despite the hour — salvage divers learned to assess things fast, hulls, weather, strangers — and Wren felt the look travel over them like a hand searching for a seam.

"Huh," she said at last. "Well. If you were secretly reporting our position to the navy this whole time, I suppose I should thank you for quitting." She yawned enormously, unguarded, utterly unafraid. "Come sleep sometime tonight, Vale. Dawn comes early and the pumps don't pump themselves."

The hatch closed. The footsteps faded. And Wren Vale sat alone on the deck of a leaking boat, having just lied fluently to the one person they'd resolved never to lie to again, and understood with perfect clarity that the hardest part of this business had never been deceiving enemies.

It was this: that the people you came to love believed you, easily, completely — and that every hour you didn't tell them the truth, you spent the trust they gave you like money you'd never be able to earn back.

Dawn was coming. Voss had given them until dawn.

Beyond the breakwater of the sleeping cay, far out where the black water met the blacker sky, Wren thought they could see it already — a running light, steady and patient, closing slowly with the tide.
