## 12. The Leaking Hold

The seam gave up its secret at half past two in the morning, in the manner of most secrets: all at once, and at the worst possible time.

Coral woke to a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat—the wet, apologetic *slap* of water where water had no business being. She was out of the bunk before her eyes finished opening, bare feet finding the cold film on the deck boards, and by the time she'd thrown open the engine compartment hatch she already knew what she'd find.

She found it anyway. Some part of her always insisted on looking.

The seam along the port side of the bilge—her old enemy, the one she'd caulked twice and cursed nightly—had opened like a bad wound. Water came through in a steady, muscular pulse, silver-black in the light of the single working bulb, pooling around the pump housing with an air of settled ownership.

"Wren," she called, not loudly. There was no need for loud. The boat itself was shouting.

Wren appeared in the doorway in four seconds flat, which told Coral something about how lightly they slept. Hair flattened on one side, shirt inside out, eyes already awake behind the sleepiness. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that we're not having this conversation standing still." Coral dropped to her knees beside the pump and yanked its cover off. "Hand me the wrench. Then start bailing with anything that holds water. Buckets, cookpot, your hat if it comes to that."

"You'd sacrifice my hat?"

"I'd sacrifice your whole wardrobe if it keeps us above the waterline."

The pump coughed, caught, and began its laboring rattle. It was a good pump. It was also a pump built for a boat that hadn't spent three weeks being asked to do things boats shouldn't do, and Coral could hear in its voice that it was negotiating terms rather than winning.

They worked. That was the thing about Wren Vale that Coral kept noticing despite herself: when work needed doing, they did it without ceremony. No questions about whose job was whose, no hovering. They found the second bucket, then the cookpot, then—when Coral's grim prophecy proved accurate—a battered canvas hat, and set up a rhythm at the companionway steps, hauling water up and over the rail into the dark sea that wanted it back so badly.

By four o'clock the arithmetic had stopped working. Coral sat back on her heels, arms trembling, and did the sums honestly for the first time. The pump moved maybe forty gallons an hour against its design. The seam was letting in more than that now, and gaining confidence. Dawn was still two hours off. The nearest charted harbor was six hours away, in the wrong direction, behind them.

"We're not making port," she said.

Wren paused mid-bail, pot dripping. "Where are we making, then?"

Coral pulled herself up the ladder to the cockpit and unfolded the chart across the wheel, weighting the corners with anything heavy within reach. Her finger traced the coast. Islands dotted the water like crumbs swept off a table—most of them named, which meant inhabited, which meant harbormasters, which meant questions she couldn't answer and fees she couldn't pay. But there, wedged between two larger islands like a pebble someone had overlooked: an unnamed cay. A smudge of green with no harbor, no village, no reason for anyone official to ever look at it.

"There." She tapped it. "It's a beach-and-pray. We run her up onto the sand before she decides to sink somewhere inconvenient."

"Is there fresh water?"

"Probably. Is there shelter? Probably. Will there be crabs judging us? Certainly."

Wren's mouth twitched. Even now—even with the bilge filling and the sky still black and both of them soaked to the elbows—something in their face wanted to laugh and was asking permission. Coral found herself, absurdly, granting it. She laughed too, short and rough, and the sound did something to the fear. Didn't kill it. Just made it share the boat instead of owning it.

"Point her at the smudge," she said. "I'll keep the pump company."

* * *

They ran for ninety minutes with the engine screaming a note it would bill them for later, Coral crouched in the bilge with one hand on the pump and the other pressed flat against the hull as though she could hold the sea out by stubbornness alone. Water rose past her ankles, past her shins. Twice the pump stalled and she had to coax it back like a tired animal, and each time it restarted she said thank you out loud, because superstition was free and mechanics were expensive.

The sky went from black to bruise to a thin, watery gray just as the cay resolved out of the haze ahead—a low green shoulder with a curl of white sand, backed by a scatter of wind-bent palms. No dock. No smoke. No footprints. A place the world had decided to skip.

"Sandbar runs shallow off the north point," Coral shouted up through the hatch. "Come in slow and straight. When I say now, cut the engine and let her drift."

"And when do we say now?"

"When I say now. Not before, not after. You'll know it because I'll be yelling it."

The keel kissed sand with a long grinding shudder that Coral felt in her teeth, and then the boat lurched, slid, and stopped with a finality that felt almost merciful. The engine died. The pump rattled on for a few seconds out of habit, then quit too, and in the sudden silence the only sounds were waves on the beach and water still dripping somewhere below.

Coral climbed out onto the deck and stood swaying, looking at the little island that was now, apparently, her address.

"Well," Wren said beside her. "It's not much."

"It's dry. Relatively speaking. In this economy, we take what we can get."

* * *

The morning organized itself around survival, which was the kind of morning Coral understood best. They bailed the boat down to a defensible level and stuffed the seam with sailcloth and sealant, a field fix that would hold until it didn't. They hauled the anchor line ashore and tied it to a palm, because a boat on a beach is a boat with opinions about leaving. Wren found a stream trickling through the rocks on the far side—fresh, cold, tasting faintly of iron—and Coral rigged a tarp awning between two trees with the competence of a woman who had lived rough before and expected to again.

By midday the crisis had become merely an inconvenience, and inconvenience, Coral could handle. She even caught herself humming while scrubbing the bilge slime off her hands in the stream.

That was when she saw the transmitter.

She'd gone back aboard to check the seam, dropping down into the emptied bilge with a lamp, and in the clean light after the water she noticed what the water had hidden: a gap behind the stringer plate under the aft locker, a gap that should have held nothing but rust and dead spiders, and instead held a case. Metal, olive-drab, the size of a thick book. Salt-crusted at the edges but recently stowed—she could tell by the way it sat, snug and deliberate, wedged with a folded rag to stop it shifting in rough weather.

Somebody had put it there. Somebody had put it there carefully.

Her own hands went cold before her mind caught up. She worked the case loose and carried it up into the daylight, and sat on the cabin sole with it in her lap for a long moment, feeling the weight of it, feeling the weight of everything rearranging itself around it.

She opened it.

A transmitter. Compact, professional, the kind of equipment that didn't come from pawn shops or chandleries. Beside it, a notebook—small, waterproof pages—and she opened that too, because apparently she was the sort of person who did that now. The handwriting was Wren's; she'd seen it on the shopping lists they'd started leaving, neat and slightly slanted. But these weren't shopping lists. These were coded entries, columns of letters and numbers, dates matching the days of their voyage. And one entry, dated the night before the squall, that wasn't code at all—just a fragment, half-erased, as if whoever wrote it had lost their nerve mid-sentence:

*—position approx. 14°—will confirm intent. C. is not—*

*C. is not.* Not what. Not hostile? Not to be trusted? Not what they'd been told?

Coral closed the notebook. Sat very still. Outside, the waves kept doing their patient, ancient work against the sand, and a bird somewhere made a sound like a rusty hinge, and none of it cared at all that the person sleeping ten feet away in her spare bunk had been carrying a radio in her boat since the day they'd come aboard begging passage.

She thought about the squall. About the compass nearly going over the rail, and Wren's hand closing around it—*an act that could be loyalty or theft*, she'd thought at the time, and filed the question away. Now the file opened itself unbidden. Had Wren been saving the compass, or marking it? Had the catch of the falling lantern been quickness, or training?

She thought about the patrol cutter that had shadowed them for hours and peeled away, unhurried, as if it already knew where they'd be tomorrow.

Cold moved through her, steady and thorough, like the water that had filled her bilge. This was betrayal. She recognized it the way you recognize weather—not by studying it, but by feeling it on your skin. Someone had taken her trust, her boat, her last stretch of open water, and used all of it as a road to wherever they were actually going.

And yet.

And yet Wren had bailed water all night with a cooking pot and a hat. Wren had learned how she took her coffee without asking. Wren had looked at her yesterday, sunburned and salt-stiff and laughing at the crab joke, with an expression Coral had privately, foolishly catalogued as *real*.

Half-erased sentences don't get written by people who are certain. *Will confirm intent. C. is not—* That was a person arguing with themselves. That was a person mid-turn, heading not yet chosen.

Coral looked down at the transmitter in her lap and made the coldest, calmest decision of her life, which was saying something, given the competition.

She wasn't going to confront them. Not yet. Confrontation ended the flow of information, and information was the only currency she had left that anyone wanted. If Wren was reporting to the navy, or to Harrow, or to some third party she hadn't even imagined, then every unsent message was a door she could watch. Better a spy she understood than an ally she didn't. Better the devil in the bilge than the devil she couldn't find.

Besides—some smaller, warmer voice added—there was the matter of the erased sentence. *C. is not.* She wanted to know how that sentence ended. She suspected, in some chamber of herself she wasn't ready to inspect, that she already knew.

She rewrapped the transmitter exactly as she'd found it, down to the fold of the rag, and slid it back behind the stringer plate. She wiped her fingerprints from the case with her sleeve. She climbed out of the bilge, sealed the locker, and stood on deck breathing the salt air until her face remembered how to be ordinary.

Then she went to find Wren, who was down at the stream washing the cookpot, sleeves rolled, hair dripping, humming something tuneless and cheerful—and who looked up at her with a smile so unguarded that Coral's chest hurt with the effort of not showing her a single thing.

"Stream's good," Wren said. "Tastes like nails, but good nails."

"Everything worth having does." Coral crouched beside them and cupped water into her mouth, watching Wren's reflection wobble in the current beside her own. "We'll need to think about water storage. And I want to take another look at that chart tonight—figure out our options once the hull's patched proper."

"Options are good. I'm fond of options." Wren wrung out the pot and grinned. "You know, for a woman whose boat tried to drown her last night, you're remarkably calm."

"I'm a diver," Coral said. "Calm underwater is the whole profession. It's up here—" she tapped her temple, lightly, keeping her hand steady, keeping everything steady— "that I've never once known what I'm doing."

Wren laughed, easy and warm, and handed her the pot to carry, and walked beside her back toward the boat through the hot white afternoon.

That night, Coral lay in her bunk listening to Wren's breathing go slow and even in the next berth, and stared at the deckhead, and waited. Somewhere below her, behind a steel plate, a radio sat silent, holding whatever Wren Vale would dare to say next. All Coral had to do was stay quiet, stay close, and stay smarter than the person lying to her.

She'd survived small her whole life. She could survive this.

The transmitter clicked softly in the dark, waking up.
