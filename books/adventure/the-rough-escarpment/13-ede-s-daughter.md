## 13. Ede's Daughter

The cay had no name on any chart Coral owned, which by now felt less like an accident and more like a theme. It was a low green thumbprint of land maybe four hundred meters long, ringed in white sand and guarded by a fringe of coral that had already chewed a strip of paint off her hull getting them in. The tide was going out. The boat sat in the shallows like a tired animal that had lain down on purpose and did not intend to get up again.

"Charming," Wren said, standing waist-deep beside the transom, holding it steady while Coral climbed over the side. "Rustic. Off-grid. The kind of place real estate agents describe as 'an opportunity.'"

"It has fresh water," Coral said. "There's a spring line under the east ridge — see how the green comes down to the sand in a stripe? That's not weeds. That's water telling you where to dig."

Wren looked at the stripe of green, then at Coral, with an expression she'd started to recognize: the look of someone recalibrating how much they were allowed to be impressed. "You read land the way other people read menus."

"I read menus better." She hauled the pump handle out of the bilge and slung it over her shoulder along with the tool bag. "Come on. We dig the seam inspection trench before dark, or we're sleeping in a boat that wants to be a submarine."

They worked through the late afternoon, Wren passing tools and asking questions that were half genuine curiosity and half interrogation wearing a friendly coat. Coral answered most of them. The ones about Ede Latch she answered least.

She hadn't told Wren everything. She'd told them the old man had died, that he'd left her a map, that she owed him a delivery. She had not told them what the map pointed at, mostly because she still didn't fully know herself, and partly because every time she imagined saying it out loud, the words sounded like the opening of a story where everybody dies in chapter fourteen.

By the time the light went amber, they'd shored the weeping seam with a canvas patch and a liberal application of epoxy that Coral privately considered less a repair than a negotiation. Her hands were pruned, her back was a single continuous complaint, and the smell of curing resin had gotten into everything, including, somehow, her hair.

"Food," she announced. "Then sleep. Then tomorrow we figure out where on this rock—" She stopped.

Wren had gone very still, head tilted, listening. "Smoke," they said quietly. "Not woodsmoke. Grease. Someone's cooking."

Coral smelled it a heartbeat later — fat and salt and something green, drifting on the evening breeze from the far side of the ridge. Her hand found the dive knife at her belt without her permission. "I thought this cay was empty."

"So did I," Wren said. "Which is either good news or the setup to a joke."

They climbed the ridge single file, Coral leading, moving on the balls of her feet out of habit. Over the top, tucked into a fold of land behind a windbreak of sea grape, was a camp so small and so neat it looked like it had been assembled by someone with strong opinions and no spare parts. A lean-to of driftwood and salvaged tarp. A fire pit ringed with stones. A rain catchment made from half a lifeboat. And a woman sitting cross-legged by the fire, turning fish on a flat stone, with a rifle across her knees.

She was perhaps forty, weathered brown, with gray coming in at her temples and eyes the pale, unimpressed color of shallow water over sand. She didn't startle when they crested the ridge. She simply looked up, took them in — Coral's knife, Wren's empty hands, the general air of two people who had been having a bad week — and went back to turning the fish.

"You've been watching us since we came through the reef," she said. Her voice was dry as beach gravel. "You anchor like tourists."

"We patched a seam for three hours," Coral said. "That's not anchoring like tourists, that's anchoring like drowning."

"Mm." The woman lifted one fish off the stone, inspected it, and set it aside. "Three hours. So the hull's bad. You'll want the eastern cove if another blow comes through — deeper water, lee of the ridge." She said it the way you'd tell a neighbor their gate was squeaking. Then she looked up again, and something in her face shifted, sharpened, like a lens finding focus. "You're the diver."

Coral kept her weight balanced and her voice level. "Do I know you?"

"No. But my father did." The woman reached into the lean-to and came out with a tin cup, filled it from a battered kettle, and held it out toward the firelight. "Ede Latch sent you. I can smell his map on you. He always rolled them with a sprig of dried sage so they wouldn't mildew. Twenty years I told him paper maps were madness. He said paper doesn't need batteries." She almost smiled. "Sit down before the fish gets cold. Both of you. If you meant harm you'd have come in the night, and you'd have brought more people and worse manners."

Coral did not sit. Neither did Wren. The rifle stayed across the woman's knees, casual as a cat.

"Ede Latch is dead," Coral said.

"I know. Eleven days now. The birds told me." At Coral's expression, the woman shrugged. "Frigatebirds follow dying things. There was a wheel of them over the far channel last week, circling and circling, and then there weren't any boats out there at all, and I thought: well. Either a whale or my father." She turned the remaining fish. "A whale would have been kinder to him. He hated the water near the end. Said it got too full of things he remembered."

"You're Sera," Coral said.

"Sera Latch." She finally looked directly at Coral, and the look had weight to it, the specific gravity of someone who had been waiting a long time for something and had learned to be patient about it in a way that frightened people. "And you are the person my father bled a promise out of. He described you. 'Small, stubborn, dives like she's apologizing to the water.' He said you'd come. He also said you might come angry, so let me save us both some time — no, I don't know exactly what's in the cache. Yes, I know roughly. No, I won't apologize for him sending you instead of me. Yes, I'm going to feed you first anyway, because decisions made hungry are bad decisions, and I suspect we're all about to make some enormous ones."

* * *

The fish was snapper, cooked over coals with some kind of wild pepper leaf, and it was so good that Coral briefly forgot to be suspicious, which she suspected was the point. They ate around the fire while the sky went from amber to violet to a deep bruised blue, and Sera talked the way some people row — steadily, without wasted motion.

"He was a surveyor, before," Sera said. "Before your time. Navy contract work, charting approaches, marking channels. He was good at it. Too good — he noticed things charts weren't supposed to notice." She poked the coals. "You've seen his map. You've noticed it's not really a map. It's a mechanism. Half of one."

Coral's hand went, involuntarily, to the pocket where the compass rode. Sera saw the motion and nodded once, unsurprised.

"The ring of numbers," Sera said. "No needle. Everyone who ever saw it assumed it was broken, or a decoration, or a toy. It's none of those. It's a key. And the numbers aren't directions — they're a sequence. Feed the sequence to the map, mark the points, and the points draw you a shape. My father spent thirty years collecting the pieces of that shape, one dive at a time, one lie at a time, because the navy that paid him to chart those waters was very interested in making sure nobody else ever mapped certain parts of them."

Wren had stopped eating. Coral noticed, distantly, that Wren's face had settled into careful blankness — the expression of someone listening to a story they might already know the ending of, and dreading it.

"What's in the cache?" Coral asked.

Sera was quiet for a moment. A wave folded itself against the sand somewhere below the ridge, unhurried, indifferent.

"A ship," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. A refugee run — forty-one people packed into a coastal freighter called the *Meridian Star*, running at night from the mainland troubles toward islands that were supposed to take them. The navy knew she was coming. She was in the wrong corridor at the wrong hour, and somebody in a command office decided that letting her through was a bigger risk than the alternative." Sera's voice stayed level, but her knuckles had gone white around the tin cup. "So they sank her. Officially, she never existed. No manifest, no distress call logged, no wreck surveyed. The incident report says a training exercise. The training exercise says nothing at all. Forty-one people went into the water and became a rounding error."

The fire popped. Coral realized she wasn't breathing properly and fixed it.

"My father helped chart the exclusion zone afterward," Sera went on. "Helped make the wreck disappear from the record. He was young and he followed orders, and then he spent the rest of his life trying to un-follow them. The cache isn't treasure, diver. It's the ship's logs — recovered from the wreck by a man who shouldn't have been able to reach it. Photographs. The real passenger manifest, written in the survivors' own hands before they boarded. Proof that the navy knew exactly what the *Meridian Star* was, and sank her anyway, and buried the paperwork under thirty years of classified sediment." She looked up. "He couldn't bring it out himself. Too old, too watched, too sick. So he built a map only a diver could use, and he waited on a dock for a small, stubborn woman to walk past bleeding."

Coral stared into the fire. Somewhere behind her ribs, the promise she'd made to a dying man in a smoky room was quietly changing shape — from an errand into a weight, settling onto her shoulders like a diving belt.

"He said deliver it to his daughter, or burn it," she said finally. "Those were the terms. Deliver or burn."

"And which do you think he wanted?" Sera asked softly.

"I think he wanted me to choose," Coral said. "I think that was the whole trick. He handed me something heavy enough that I couldn't pretend it was light, and then he gave me a door marked *burn* so I'd know staying was my idea."

Sera laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly mastered. "Yes. That was him exactly. He used to say the only promises worth anything were the ones you could have walked away from." She set down her cup. "For what it's worth, I don't want it burned. I've spent six years on this rock eating fish and talking to frigatebirds, waiting for someone to carry that proof out of the water. But I'm not the one carrying it. So it's yours to decide, diver. Freedom or truth. He'd have told you there's no third option. He was lying. There's always a third option. It's just usually worse than the first two."

* * *

Later, when Sera had banked the fire and retreated to her lean-to with the rifle and the unbothered dignity of a woman who had slept alone on unnamed rocks for six years, Coral sat at the edge of the camp with the compass open in her palm. Moonlight caught the ring of numbers. They looked the same as they always had — inert, decorative, a dead instrument. Except now they weren't numbers at all. They were names. Forty-one of them, maybe, arranged in brass.

Wren came and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. For a long time neither of them spoke.

"The *Meridian Star*," Wren said at last, very carefully. "When I was clerking — insurance, before all this — there was a file that crossed my desk once. A claim from a family. A woman looking for her brother, lost at sea, twenty years prior. No wreck on record, no route, nothing to pay out on. The file just... ended. I remember thinking it was strange that a person could vanish so completely. Like the sea had eaten the paperwork too." A pause. "It hadn't, though. Had it. People ate the paperwork."

"No," Coral said. "People ate the paperwork."

Wren was quiet. In the moonlight, Coral watched something move across their face — guilt, she'd have called it once, though lately it looked more complicated than that, more like a man watching the floor of a building he'd assumed was solid develop long cracks.

"Coral," Wren said. "If the cache holds what she says it holds—"

"Then everyone who's chasing us stops being greedy and starts being scared," Coral said. "Scared people with guns and budgets. Harrow. The syndicate. The navy." She closed her fist around the compass until the brass edges bit her palm. "Ede didn't give me a treasure map. He gave me a lit fuse and told me to hold it."

Below them, the tide whispered against the sand, patient as everything else the sea did. And out beyond the reef, far off in the darkness where the water met the sky, Coral saw it — a single running light, low and fast, cutting a line toward the cay. Not a fishing boat. Fishing boats didn't travel that straight, that quick, at that hour.

"Wren," she said quietly. "Get Sera. Tell her to put out the fire."

The light grew closer.
