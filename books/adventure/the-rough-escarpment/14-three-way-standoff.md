## 14. Three-Way Standoff

Sera Latch had learned to read boats the way other people learned to read faces, and the boat coming around the northern point of her cay was saying something ugly.

It was a fast boat — not fishing-fast, not smuggler-in-a-hurry fast, but money-fast, all black hull and low profile and an engine note that hummed like a hornet thinking about murder. It sat wrong in the water too, riding light and mean, the way a boat rides when it's carrying more men than it needs and fewer supplies than it pretends.

She'd been mending a net on the rocks above the little cove when she heard it, and by the time she had the net down and the binoculars up, Coral Ashgrove was already scrambling out of the tent they'd shared the night before, hair wild, one boot on.

"Tell me that's not who I think it is," Coral said.

"Depends on who you think it is."

"Black hull. Two hundred horsepower of bad intentions."

"Then yes," Sera said, "it's exactly who you think it is." She passed the binoculars over. "Three men I can see. Maybe four. The big one at the bow is doing that thing where he stands very still so everyone knows he's the dangerous one."

"That's Bram Harrow," Coral said, in the flat voice of someone confirming a diagnosis. "That's Bram Harrow standing on a boat two days' hard run from anywhere I owe him money."

Behind them, from the treeline where he'd been pretending to gather firewood for the better part of an hour, Wren Vale emerged with an armful of damp branches and the expression of a person doing arithmetic they didn't like. Sera watched them clock the boat, then the horizon south of it, and go very still.

"What," she said.

"Nothing," Wren said, which was how people said *something* in Sera's experience.

"Wren."

"I said nothing."

"You said nothing the way my father used to say nothing," Sera told them, "right before the mine shaft collapsed." She held out her hand. "Binoculars."

Wren gave them back. Sera swept south along the haze and found what Wren had already seen: a grey shape sitting fat and patient beyond the reef line, close enough to be interested, far enough to pretend it wasn't. A navy cutter, riding its anchor like a cat watching a mouse hole.

"Oh," Sera said quietly. "Oh, you absolute idiots. You led them both here."

"We didn't lead anyone—" Coral started.

"The transmitter," Sera said. "The one in your boat that you've been pretending isn't there because you decided I didn't need to know, which, by the way, we will be discussing." She was surprised at how calm her own voice came out. Her father had always said she got their mother's temper and his talent for sounding reasonable while furious, and right now both were working overtime. "You've been broadcasting our position since the channel."

Wren's jaw worked. "I haven't transmitted since the cay. I destroyed the radio."

"Then someone else has been listening to you transmit, and they don't need your help anymore." Sera pointed the binoculars south again. The cutter hadn't moved. It didn't need to. "Congratulations. Everyone's here. The whole family reunion."

* * *

There was, Sera reflected as they worked, something almost funny about three people who trusted each other approximately none of the way dragging a waterproof canister across a cay while two armed forces converged on them. Almost. If you squinted. From very far away. Through the wrong end of the binoculars.

The canister itself was heavier than it looked — Ede's old ammunition tube, sealed with wax and wire, holding inside it everything the navy had spent years insisting never happened. Sera had grown up on the edges of that insistence. She'd been eleven when her father came home from a season of prospecting with salt-white hair and wouldn't say why, and twenty-two when he finally started talking, and thirty-one when he'd handed a stranger a map because he was dying and out of time and out of options and Coral Ashgrove had happened to be bleeding in the right bar at the right moment.

Fate, her father would have said, is just luck with a work ethic.

"Under the net rack," Sera directed. "No — under the *rocks* under the net rack. If they search, they'll search the obvious caches first. Fishermen hide things in stupid places; make them dig for the stupid places."

Coral wedged the canister into a crevice beneath a slab of coral rock, packed the gap with rubble, and dragged a tangle of rotten netting over the top. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at it with the expression of a woman burying a dog she loved.

"It'll keep," Sera said.

"Everything keeps," Coral said. "That's the problem with things. They wait."

Wren stood at the treeline with their arms crossed, watching the black boat come on. "They'll beach on the north side. There's a sand spit there deep enough for that draft."

"You know this cay," Sera said.

"I read charts." Wren's voice was carefully, professionally bland. "It's my job."

"Your job is insurance clerking."

"My job," Wren said, after a pause that lasted about a year, "is complicated."

Sera looked at Coral. Coral looked at Sera. Something passed between them — the wordless shorthand of two women who had each, separately and recently, decided that Wren Vale was a problem to be solved *after* the men with guns arrived, and not one moment before.

"Here's how this goes," Sera said, straightening up and brushing sand off her knees. "We are three castaways. We have no canister, no map, no compass, and no idea what anyone's talking about. I live here. You two wrecked here. We've been sharing fish and grievances. If anyone asks me anything, I'm going to look confused, and I want you both to know I won't be acting."

"She's really good at looking confused," Coral offered.

"I've had practice. Men explain things to me constantly."

* * *

Bram Harrow came ashore the way he did everything: expensively and without hurrying.

He stepped off the black boat onto the sand spit in shoes that had no business on a beach — pale leather, spotless, an insult to the whole concept of coastline — and stood for a moment surveying the cay like a man inspecting property he intended to repossess. Behind him came two of his people: a narrow man with a shotgun broken open over his arm, casual as a picnic, and a broad woman with close-cropped hair whose hands stayed empty and therefore worried Sera considerably more.

Harrow smiled when he saw them. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which was the most frightening thing about it.

"Coral Ashgrove," he called, spreading his arms. "In the flesh. Do you know, I've been looking forward to this?"

"Bram." Coral walked down the beach to meet him, unhurried herself, hands loose at her sides. Sera followed at a distance calculated to look idle and stay useful, and felt Wren drift along behind her like a shadow that had opinions. "You're a long way from your office."

"I'm a long way from a great many comfortable things lately." Harrow stopped an arm's length away and looked her over with genuine, almost paternal warmth. "You look thin. Are you eating? Don't answer. Of course you're not. That's rather the whole tragedy of you, Coral — you'd survive a shipwreck on half a biscuit and a bad attitude, and you'd still call it winning."

"I'd call it Tuesday."

"There she is." He laughed, delighted, and turned the smile on Sera and Wren. "And friends! Introduce us."

"Sera Latch. Lives here. Wren Vale, insurance clerk, shipwrecked with me last week." Coral said it smoothly, easily, and Sera marveled again at how naturally lying sat on the woman — like a coat worn so long she'd forgotten she was wearing it.

"Insurance," Harrow repeated, tasting the word. His eyes rested on Wren for exactly one second too long. "How industrious. Well. I won't take up your morning." He clasped his hands behind his back and gazed out at the water with the contentment of a man on holiday. "I'm actually here on rather delicate business. You see, a mutual acquaintance of ours — a grubby old man with more secrets than teeth — apparently entrusted you with something before he died. And certain parties are extremely anxious to recover it. Parties with resources. Parties," he added pleasantly, "who are currently anchored off your southern reef and getting impatient with me."

Sera kept her face arranged into pleasant rural blankness. Beside her, Wren had gone very slightly rigid, and she could practically hear the machinery turning behind their eyes — *the cutter, the cutter, he knows about the cutter, which means he knows things he shouldn't know—*

"I don't know what you mean," Coral said.

"No, of course you don't." Harrow sighed, sorrowful. "You never do. That's what I admire about you. Here's the shape of it, though, since we're all friends having a friendly morning on a beach." He turned back to her, and the smile stayed fixed while everything behind it went cold and precise. "You give me what the old man gave you, and every coin you owe the syndicate becomes a story we tell at parties. Boat debt, dive debt, interest, penalties — gone. Poof. You walk away owing nothing to anyone, which I understand is a condition you've never once experienced in your adult life."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I take this lovely cay apart stone by stone, starting with the parts that belong to your friend Ms. Latch, and I find it anyway, and you owe me the debt *plus* the inconvenience." He spread his hands, regretful. "I'd hate the inconvenience part. I'm told I get creative."

Somewhere out past the reef, a bell buoy clanked. The silence afterward had weight.

"You'll have my answer by tomorrow," Coral said.

"Tomorrow." Harrow considered this, head tilted, like a man being offered a fish he suspected was off. "The navy's going to grow bored long before tomorrow, Coral. Bored navies do boardings. And when they board, they find *me*, a licensed salvage operator, standing on a beach chatting with a debtor and two strangers, one of whom—" the smile flickered, just barely, "—has a face I feel like I've seen on paperwork somewhere. Whereas they find *you* with a boat full of unmanifested salvage history and a restricted-zone dive stamp on your logbook. Ask yourself who they'll want to talk to longer."

"You're threatening me with the navy."

"I'm *introducing* you to the navy. Threatening comes later, if you insist on the full program." He checked an imaginary watch. "Now, if you'll excuse me, I should go stand on my deck and look legitimate. Do give my regards to the old man's memory." He paused, and for just a heartbeat the performance dropped and something older and uglier looked out through his eyes. "He was supposed to bring it to *us*, you know. Twenty years ago. He chose badly then, too."

He turned and walked back across the sand, spotless shoes and all, and Sera watched him go and thought: *he's afraid of them.* Not of Coral, not of her, not even really of losing the canister. Of the grey ship on the southern horizon. Harrow had come all this way smiling because the alternative was arriving with guns, and guns would give the navy a reason.

Everyone on this beach was smiling for the same reason, she realized. Nobody wanted to be the excuse.

* * *

They watched the black boat put back out to the spit and hold position a few hundred yards offshore, engine idling, patient as a hook.

"He knows about Wren," Sera said quietly, the moment they were clear. "Or suspects. 'Paperwork.' What paperwork has your face on it, Wren?"

"Employment forms," Wren said. "Very boring ones."

"You're the worst liar I've ever met, and I grew up around prospectors."

"I'm an excellent liar," Wren said, with the first flash of real feeling Sera had seen from them. "I'm just tired."

Before Sera could answer, Coral made a small sound and touched her arm. South, beyond the reef, the grey cutter had come alive. A launch dropped from its davits, filled with grey uniforms, and began the long, unhurried run toward the cay — the kind of unhurried that meant they already knew nobody on the beach could go anywhere.

"Well," Sera said. "Company."

* * *

The boarding party was four sailors and an officer, and the officer was young in the way that meant he'd been given a job slightly too big for him and knew it, and was compensating with posture. He came ashore with his cap squared and his chin set at the angle of a man reading from a manual in his head.

"Good afternoon." He surveyed the three of them, the tent, the net racks, the battered boat drawn up above the tideline. "This cay falls within a designated naval exercise area. I'll need names and purposes."

"Purposes plural?" Sera asked. "We only have the one between us. Fishing."

His eyes flicked to her, cataloguing: the rifle leaning against the net rack, the salt-cured fish, the woman living alone on a rock. "Name."

"Sera Latch. Born here, more or less. These two wrecked on the reef last week. I've been putting them up."

"Names of the castaways."

"Coral Ashgrove. Wren Vale." Coral's voice was easy, bored, faintly aggrieved — the voice of a woman with better things to do. "Our boat's holed. We're repairing it. Slowly. Mostly by arguing about it."

The officer wrote nothing down, which Sera noted with professional interest. Men who write things down are collecting records; men who don't are collecting impressions. This one was building a picture to carry back to somebody senior.

"You'll be aware," he said, "that salvage activity is prohibited within the exercise area."

"We're aware of everything," Coral said. "It's exhausting."

Something almost human crossed his face — the ghost of a smile, strangled at birth. He looked past them at the boat, at the tent, at the treeline, and Sera watched him do the arithmetic every searcher does: *three people, one island, where would I hide it?* She saw his gaze snag, briefly, on the net rack, and congratulated herself silently on the rubble pile.

"Where's your vessel, Mr. Vale?" the officer asked suddenly. "Ms. Ashgrove has a boat. You said you wrecked together."

"Mine was stolen," Wren said. "In harbor. Before we left."

"Stolen. Reported?"

"It was being reported."

"By you."

A pause. One heartbeat. Two. Sera could feel Wren deciding, could feel the whole fragile architecture of the morning balanced on whatever came next.

"Yes," Wren said finally. "By me. It's all quite tedious, officer. If you'd like, I can give you the insurer's reference number and you can spend your afternoon verifying it instead of standing on my friend's beach."

The officer studied them for a long moment. Out on the water, the black boat idled. Beyond it, the cutter waited. Somewhere under a pile of coral rocks, a dead man's truth waited too, wax-sealed and patient.

"Enjoy your repairs," the officer said at last. "You'll be seeing more of us. The exercise area is closed as of tonight — monsoon advisory. No vessels in or out until it passes." He put his cap back on with a small, precise motion. "All vessels. Including yours."

He walked back to the launch. The launch went back to the cutter. The cutter did not leave.

Sera stood on her beach between a syndicate enforcer's fast boat and a navy cutter, with a storm front building black on the western horizon like a bruise coming up, and beside her Coral exhaled slowly through her nose.

"Closed area," Coral said. "Storm advisory. Nobody in or out."

"Mm," Sera said.

"So everybody on this water is about to be stuck here together." Coral was staring at the horizon, and her voice had gone quiet and strange, the voice of a diver surfacing into weather. "Harrow. The navy. Us. All of it, on one rock, until the storm breaks."

Sera looked west. The cloud bank had organized itself into a single dark shelf, low and wide and coming on like something with intent.

"Then I suggest," she said, "we all get very good at smiling."
