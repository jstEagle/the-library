## 15. The Deal Offered

Bram Harrow came at dusk, alone, which was somehow worse than coming with men.

Coral had spent the afternoon watching the cay from the treeline the way a crab watches a gull—flat-eyed, motionless, doing arithmetic. Harrow's fast boat sat anchored in the lee like a knife laid on a table. Beyond it, farther out, the navy cutter hung on the horizon with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry. And between them, on her beach, her half-drowned boat listed gently against the sand with a canister of navy secrets sealed in its bilge.

She'd hidden it that morning while Wren slept, working by feel in the flooded dark of the hold, wedging the canister behind a frame joint where only someone who thought like a diver would ever put their hands. It had felt less like hiding and more like planting. Like burying something alive and hoping it stayed quiet.

Now Harrow picked his way up from the beach, hands visible, jacket open, wearing the smile of a man who had already won and was simply being polite about it.

"Coral Ashgrove." He said her name like he was tasting it. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"There wasn't an other guy. There was a bar fight, three witnesses, and a man named Doss who swears you went through a table." He stopped a few paces off, close enough to talk, far enough to be respectful of what she might do. "I've always liked that about you. You're economical. You never spend more violence than the situation requires."

"What do you want, Bram?"

"To talk. Just talk." He gestured at a driftwood log as if it were furniture in his own parlor, and sat, and looked out at the water with genuine appreciation. "It's a nice spot. I mean that. If things were different, I'd retire somewhere like this."

"Things are different."

"They are." The smile thinned but didn't leave. It just became more honest, which with Bram Harrow was its own kind of threat. "Here's my situation, Coral. I have a syndicate that wants results. I have a navy cutter sitting out there that wants jurisdiction. And I have you—a woman with no boat worth stealing, no money worth taking, and one item of extraordinary interest buried somewhere on this island or in that hull. So let's not insult each other. You have it. I know you have it. The question is only what it costs me."

"And if I say I don't?"

"Then we'll have a long evening, and I'll get sad, and sad men do clumsy things." He said it lightly, almost fondly. "But you won't say that. Because you're smart, and because lying to me has never once worked, and you know it."

Coral leaned against a palm tree and made herself comfortable, because the first rule of negotiating with Harrow was to look like you had all the time in the world even when your heart was going like a bilge pump. "So make your offer."

"I forgive it."

The words landed harder than she expected. Not the debt itself—she'd carried that number so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in her pocket—but the *idea* of it. A life without the weight. She tried to picture waking up and owing nobody anything, and found she couldn't quite render the image. It was like trying to imagine a color she'd never seen.

"All of it," Harrow went on, watching her face with the attention of a man reading weather. "Every coin. Clean slate. And passage—my boat, my route, anywhere you want to go. New harbor, new name, new life. I'll even throw in a stake. Enough to buy a sound hull and work honest wrecks for a year while you decide who you want to be."

"And in exchange."

"The canister. And the spy."

"Wren."

"Wren." He turned the name over like something he'd found under a rock. "You know what they are. Maybe you've known since the squall, maybe since the transmitter—I don't care when. They're an asset of an organization I have been politely tolerating for years, and they are currently sitting on my beach eating my goodwill. Hand them over with the canister, tell the navy whatever story keeps you clean, and walk away rich in every way that matters."

"And what happens to Wren?"

Harrow spread his hands. "That's above my pay grade. That's the beauty of it, Coral—it stops being your problem. Whatever happens after tonight happens to someone who lied to you, spied on you, and steered you straight into this mess. You'd be trading a traitor's fate for your own freedom. Any reasonable person makes that trade before I finish the sentence."

"I'm not reasonable," Coral said. "I'm a diver. We're trained to check what's underneath before we commit."

"Then check." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and became pleasant again, terribly pleasant. "I'll give you until dawn. The storm front's coming early—you can feel it, can't you? Even the air knows. At first light I want an answer, and I want the canister, and I want you standing next to me looking surprised when the navy takes your passenger into custody. Do that, and you're free. Truly free. No debt, no syndicate, no looking over your shoulder." He paused at the treeline. "Or refuse, and I'll take both anyway, and you'll learn what my patience looks like when it runs out. It isn't pretty. Ask anyone who's seen it."

"Anyone who's seen it still around to describe it?"

He smiled—the real one, brief and crooked, almost boyish. "No," he said, and walked down to his boat.

* * *

She didn't go straight back to camp. She walked the beach instead, the long way, letting the tide suck at her boots and the first fat drops of the coming storm spot her shoulders.

The arithmetic was simple and obscene. Her freedom on one side. Wren on the other. And the truth—that canister, those names, that ship full of people the navy had erased—sitting in her bilge like a heart she'd transplanted into her own boat.

Ede Latch had handed her this with his last breath, and she still didn't fully understand why. *Deliver it to my daughter, or burn it.* She'd done neither. She'd kept it, hoarded it, told herself she was waiting for the right moment, and now the right moment had arrived wearing Bram Harrow's smile and offering her everything she'd bled for.

*Surviving small,* she thought. *That was supposed to be enough.*

Wren was at the fire when she got back, feeding it sticks with the careful economy of someone who'd learned to cook on rations. Sera Latch had vanished into the interior at dusk with her rifle, watching the water approaches; Coral had stopped being surprised by anything that woman did. The firelight caught Wren's face and made them look younger than their lies deserved.

"We need to talk," Coral said.

Wren's hands stilled over the fire. "That's usually how the bad conversations start."

"This is the bad conversation."

They walked down to the tideline together, out of earshot of the empty camp, though Coral checked anyway—old habit, and lately not paranoid enough. The storm-light was going strange overhead, greenish, the pressure dropping like a held breath.

"Harrow offered me a deal," she said. "My debt forgiven. Passage anywhere. A stake to start over. All of it."

Wren nodded slowly. "In exchange for the canister."

"In exchange for the canister *and you*. I hand you to the navy as a spy, I play the innocent salvage diver who was deceived, and I walk away free." She watched Wren's face in the dying light. "And here's the part that interests me. You didn't flinch. You knew. You've known since he anchored, haven't you? You did the same arithmetic I did."

"I did the arithmetic," Wren admitted quietly. "I just got a different answer."

"Then explain it to me. Explain all of it. Tonight, Wren. No more halves."

So they told her. Standing in the surf-line with the storm gathering behind them, Wren Vale finally opened the whole ledger—and it was worse and better than Coral had guessed. Worse, because the mission had been exactly what she'd feared: find the diver who lifted the compass, track the map, secure the cache contents for the organization Wren served, an outfit that traded in leverage the way other people traded in fish. Better, because the radio was genuinely dead—smashed by Wren's own hand two nights ago, the pieces scattered in three directions along the cay—and because the coded update Wren had sent before that had contained nothing useful at all. A lie dressed in cipher. *Target lost. Asset non-viable. Recommend withdrawal.*

"You burned yourself," Coral said slowly. "For me. Before you even knew if I'd—" She stopped. "Why?"

"Because you caught the compass when it fell and you didn't sell it. Because you took a dying stranger's map for a promise you couldn't afford. Because you pump a leaking hull all night without complaining once, and you gave your last coffee to a woman who pointed a rifle at you." Wren's voice cracked on something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Because every report I wrote about you sounded like a person, and I couldn't feed a person to them anymore."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have."

"No." Coral stepped closer, close enough to see the firelight reflected small in Wren's eyes. "The organization. The handlers. The mission. That's the *what*, and you've given me the whole what, and I believe you. But there's one thing left, and it's the only thing I actually care about." Her voice dropped. "Why did you stay? After the squall. After the leak. After you could have taken the compass and gone—after any of a dozen nights when I slept like the dead and you could have ended this. You stayed. People don't stay out of guilt. Guilt leaves. Guilt gets on a fast boat and files its report. So why. Are. You. Still. Here."

The waves filled the silence. Somewhere inland, Sera's rifle bolt clicked—checking, always checking.

Wren looked at her for a long time, and Coral watched them arrive at the edge of something and stand there, toes over it, the way she herself stood at the lip of a dark wreck before committing to the drop.

"I can't," Wren said finally. "Not yet. I know how that sounds. But if I say it now, in the middle of this—with Harrow's boat out there and the navy closing and you holding a deal that buys your whole life with mine—it'll sound like a plea. Like a bid. And it isn't a bid, Coral. It's the one thing I own outright, and I'm not spending it to save myself."

"So you'd rather I hand you over at dawn."

"I'd rather you save yourself," Wren said simply. "Take the deal. Take the freedom. God knows you've earned it ten times over, and I'm the reason you're on this beach at all. If the price of your clean slate is my ugly little history catching up with me—" they shrugged, and the shrug cost them visibly— "then pay it. I signed up for risks. You never signed up for any of this."

"You absolute idiot," Coral said, and it came out rougher than she meant, warm and furious at once. "You confess everything except the one thing that matters, and then you tell me to sell you."

"I'm consistent."

"You're *impossible*." She turned away, dragged both hands through her hair, and stared out at the blackening water where Harrow's anchor light bobbed and the navy cutter waited and the storm front stacked itself against the horizon like a wall being built by something with a grudge. "Dawn," she said. "He wants an answer at dawn. And the storm's coming early—feel that pressure? By midnight this cay is going to be a very crowded place to stand."

"Crowded how?"

"How do you think? Harrow won't wait past his deadline, and the navy won't wait past the storm, and Sera won't wait past either of them, and none of them know the canister is twelve inches from their boots in the hull of a boat that barely floats." She turned back, and something had settled in her—not peace, nothing so kind, but the cold clarity she got forty feet down when the decision was made and the body just followed. "So here's what happens. Nobody decides anything until the weather does. We survive the night, all of us, and then—"

Lightning walked the horizon, silent, far off, counting down.

"—then I make my move," Coral finished. "Whatever it turns out to be."

Wren studied her. "You already know, don't you."

"I know the water," Coral said. "The rest is details."

Behind them, the fire spat as the first real rain arrived, big and sudden, and out past the breakwater the monsoon came on like a debt collector who was done being polite.
