## 16. Monsoon Rising

*Coral*

The sky turned the color of a bruise at two in the afternoon, which was wrong by half a day and wrong by every instinct Coral had ever trusted.

She'd been watching that horizon all morning, the way you watch a dog that's gone quiet in another room. The monsoon was supposed to be three days out. The fishing boats had cleared the channel on schedule. The barometer on her bulkhead—a brass thing she'd traded a working dive light for and never regretted—had been dropping so steadily it looked like it was trying to burrow into the deck.

Now the front came up over the water like a wall being pushed by an angry god, black-green at its base, boiling where it met the sky.

"Three days," she said to no one. "It said three days."

The cay had no one to argue with except Sera Latch, who sat on the rocks above the tideline cleaning a rifle with the unhurried competence of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and Wren Vale, who was hauling driftwood up past the storm line as if a pile of wet wood would save any of them from what was coming.

"Storm's early," Wren called, reading her face from twenty feet away.

"Thank you, clerk. I'd never have noticed."

"I'm just saying—"

"You're just saying something I already know because saying it makes you feel like you're doing something. I know the trick. I invented it."

Wren had the grace to grin. That was the trouble with them, Coral thought—not for the first time, not for the hundredth time. The trouble with Wren Vale was that they grinned when caught out, like honesty was a game they were only now learning the rules of, badly, on purpose.

Sera didn't look up from the rifle. "My father used to say the sea keeps its own calendar and lies about everything else."

"Your father," Coral said, "told me a lot of things before he died. Most of them were true. That's the part I keep tripping over."

* * *

*Wren*

Wren had been in places before a storm hit before. There was a particular quality to the hours—the way everyone got busier and quieter at once, hands moving while mouths stopped, the whole world narrowing down to rope and water and the next necessary thing. They found they liked it. Or they found it familiar, which might have been the same thing. In the service there had been drills like this: the klaxon, the scramble, the blessed simplicity of having exactly one job and no time to think about whether it was a good job.

They thought about it now anyway. They thought about it constantly, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth.

The radio was ash in a fire pit behind the dune, had been for two days, and Wren still caught themselves reaching for the pocket where it used to live. Every few hours a new message composed itself in their head—*asset stationary, target cooperative, situation deteriorating*—and every few hours they watched it dissolve, unsent, unsendable, and felt the strange weightless relief of a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Except the load wasn't set down. It was just redistributed. Now it lived in Coral's face when she looked at the boat. In Sera's silence, which was not the silence of trust. In the canister.

Wren hauled another armload of driftwood and tried very hard to think about rope.

"Clerk." Sera's voice, without her looking up. "You're stacking that wood like a man building a shrine."

"It's a windbreak."

"It's a monument to a windbreak. Storm'll take it inside the first hour. You want to help, help me get the tarp over the boat's cockpit and lash it down proper. Your knots are pretty. Mine hold."

Wren opened their mouth to say their knots held fine, considered the rifle, and went to hold down tarps.

That was the other thing about these hours before a storm. Everyone became honest by necessity. There wasn't room left over for pretending.

* * *

*Coral*

By four o'clock the light had gone strange and yellowish, the way it does when the sun is trying to shine through a lid, and the first gusts came through in long shoves that flattened the sea grass and made the palms hiss like kettles. All three of them worked the beach and the boats without discussing it. Harrow's fast boat sat anchored in the lee of the cay's northern spit, riding easy now, but Coral had watched two of Harrow's men re-secure it twice, and the navy cutter hung on the far horizon like a sentence nobody had finished saying.

Trapped. All of them. The word should have frightened her more than it did. What frightened her instead was how little time was left, and how much of it she'd spent waiting to feel sure.

She wasn't sure. She was going to do it anyway.

The canister sat in the bottom of her gear bag where it had sat since the standoff, wrapped in an oilskin, heavy as a confession. Logs. Photographs. A manifest with names on it—forty-one names, Sera had counted, forty-one people the navy had sunk in the dark and then erased from the dark, called it a training accident, called it nothing, called it weather. Ede Latch had carried the knowledge of it for thirty years like a stone in his chest, and at the end he'd handed the stone to a drowning stranger because drowning strangers, he figured, were the only ones desperate enough to swim it anywhere.

He hadn't been wrong. He also hadn't told her the stone would weigh this much.

Coral waited until the light failed and the wind rose to a steady roar that swallowed small sounds, until Sera was barricaded in the lean-to with her rifle and her father's map, until Wren was asleep sitting up against the hull of Coral's boat with their chin on their chest like a man too tired to finish the thought of lying down.

Then she took the canister and a diver's knife and went down into the bilge.

Her boat was old enough to vote, if boats voted, and corrupt enough to fit right in. Under the floor plates, below the waterline, in the space where the stringers crossed and the limber holes ran, there was a cathedral of filth and shadow that no surveyor had ever wanted to look at closely. Coral knew it better than she knew her own kitchen. She'd spent whole seasons of her life wedged in that dark with a scraper and a lamp, chasing leaks, chipping barnacles, learning her boat's body the way you learn a lover's—by touch, by trouble, by the long patient work of keeping it alive.

Only a diver would ever find it. Only a diver would ever think to look.

She worked by feel mostly, the lamp clamped in her teeth, the wind hammering the hull overhead like a fist on a door. She chose the spot under the engine bed where the stringers made a pocket—a steel box of a space, dry-ish, invisible unless you lay flat in bilgewater and reached in blind. She scraped the pocket clean, wrapped the canister in a second layer of oilskin, and lashed it down with waxed line, cinching each knot twice, testing each one with a hard pull until her shoulder ached. Then she took the knife and cut away the trim piece that had framed the access, trimming it close so the gap read as gap, as nothing, as the ordinary anatomy of an old boat falling honestly apart.

Her boat fell apart everywhere. That was the beauty of it. One more hidden seam in a hull full of them.

When she came up into the cockpit the rain had started—not the real rain yet, just the scouts, fat warm drops that struck the deck like thrown coins. Wren was awake. Of course Wren was awake. They watched her climb out of the companionway with wet knees and a knife in her belt, and Coral watched them back, and neither of them said anything, and the not-saying was so loud she almost laughed.

"Checking the bilge," she said finally. "Seam's weeping again."

"Before a storm. Naturally."

"Boats have timing like that. They wait until you need them most and then they get sentimental."

Wren nodded slowly, and Coral could see them deciding to believe her—or deciding to let her believe they believed her—and she hated it and loved it and filed it away, because whatever Wren Vale was, they were not stupid, and stupid would have been easier.

"Get inside," she said. "It's going to get ugly."

"Where will you be?"

"Right here. It's my ugly."

* * *

*Wren*

Wren did not sleep. They sat in the cabin of Coral's boat with the rain beginning in earnest on the deck above—a sound like applause, like static, like the end of the world rehearsing—and they turned the evening over in their mind the way you turn a puzzle box, looking for the seam.

Coral had come up from the bilge with wet knees and a knife and a face arranged into casualness so carefully it was practically architecture. Nobody checked a bilge at night in a rising storm with a smile like that. Wren had spent years reading faces for a living—reading them, reporting on them, sometimes ruining them—and Coral Ashgrove's face tonight had been the easiest read of their career and the hardest to know what to do with.

She'd hidden the canister. On her own boat. Where only a diver would find it.

Which meant she'd chosen. Not between Harrow and the navy—that choice was still open, still breathing—but something underneath both: she'd decided the evidence would survive the storm even if she didn't. She'd made herself expendable and the truth durable, and she'd done it alone, in the dark, without asking anyone's permission or forgiveness.

Wren found their eyes stinging and blamed the salt air, and then stopped blaming anything, because they were alone and it didn't matter.

The mission had been simple once. Locate the asset. Track the cache. Report. Somewhere between the pier and the squall and the night Coral had nearly lost the compass overboard and Wren had caught it without thinking—caught it the way you catch something that matters to someone you—somewhere in there, simple had died quietly, and Wren had buried it without ceremony, and now here they sat in the rain on a nameless cay with three armed factions and one honest woman, officially a liar, functionally a deserter, actually—

Actually what? They didn't have a word yet. They were working on it.

Above them the storm arrived properly, all at once, the way storms do—no more scouts, just the main army, the wind going from shove to slam, the rain turning horizontal, the whole cay disappearing into white noise. Somewhere out in that dark, Harrow's men were re-checking their anchors. Somewhere farther, a navy cutter was making its own calculations. And somewhere under Wren's feet, sealed in oilskin and waxed line in the filthiest corner of a dying boat, forty-one names waited for daylight.

Wren put their back against the hull, felt the sea talking through it, and for the first time since the pier, hoped—with their whole chest, no irony, no exit strategy—that the boat held.

* * *

*Coral*

Morning, if you could call it that, came gray-green and screaming.

The storm had settled in for the duration, a great slow wheel of wind and rain grinding over the cay, and the world had shrunk to the size of shelter. The lean-to leaked. The tarp over the cockpit held, barely, snapping like a live thing at its lashings. Sera brewed something over a spirit stove that claimed to be tea and tasted like hot pine needles, and they drank it together in a silence that had stopped being hostile sometime in the night and become merely crowded.

Harrow's boat was still there—Coral checked through a gap in the canvas, saw its anchor light burning stubborn through the murk. The navy cutter had closed to a gray suggestion on the southern horizon, holding position, riding it out like a patient animal. Nobody was going anywhere. The sea had locked the door and taken the key.

"So," Sera said, into the third cup of terrible tea. "Everyone's trapped. Everyone's armed. Everyone's waiting for the other two to blink." She turned her cup slowly. "My father would've loved this. He always said the best hiding place for a secret is inside an argument."

"He hid it inside me, actually."

"Yes." Sera looked at her then, straight on, Ede's eyes in a younger, harder face. "And what are you going to do with it?"

Coral thought about the bilge. About the pocket under the engine bed, the waxed line, the knots tested twice. About the fact that she had made her choice in the dark and told no one, and that the telling-no-one was the whole point—because a choice announced was a choice that could be argued with, bargained over, tortured out of you. A choice sealed in a hull was just weather. Just an old boat falling apart, same as ever.

"I'm going to keep it," she said. "Whatever happens on this rock, whoever asks, whoever offers—what my father carried stays carried. That's the promise. Not the map. Not the errand. The carrying."

Sera studied her for a long moment, then nodded once, and it was the first nod Coral had earned from her that hadn't been about tactics.

Outside, the wind found a new register, deeper, and the palms bent flat, and somewhere in the roar Coral heard her boat groan against its lines—a long complaint, an old woman shifting in her sleep—and she was on her feet and out into the rain before she'd decided to move, Wren a step behind her, both of them hauling on wet line in the white chaos, and through the sheeting water Coral saw the southern horizon flicker.

Not lightning. Lightning went up. This stayed low, along the waterline, steady, rhythmic.

A running light. Something small and fast was coming through the eye of the storm toward the cay, and it wasn't flying navy colors, and it wasn't flying any colors at all.
