## 17. Through the Eye

The storm came in like a debt collector.

Coral had watched monsoons build all her life—she knew the way the horizon went the color of a bruise, the way the air went thick and sweet before it turned mean. This one was early and it was angry, and by dusk the whole sky over the cay had curdled into something that looked less like weather and more like a verdict.

She stood at the waterline with Sera Latch's rifle slung across her back and the canister's weight a phantom ache against her ribs, even though she'd hidden it hours ago, sealed in the bilge where only a diver would ever think to look. The boat sat on the sand like a tired animal that had already guessed what was coming.

"You're not seriously considering going out in that," Sera said behind her. She had her father's voice—the same gravel-and-tobacco rasp, though Ede's had been softened by years of talking to himself, and Sera's was honed by talking to people she didn't trust.

"I'm seriously considering it," Coral said.

"Then you're an idiot."

"Probably." Coral turned. Sera stood with her arms crossed, rain just beginning to spit sideways off the surf, and there was something in her face that Coral recognized from the mirror on bad mornings: the particular fury of someone who has run out of options and hates being handed one anyway. "But Harrow's got a fast boat and the navy's got a cutter, and neither of them knows these waters the way I do. And when that eye passes over us—" she pointed at the black wall crawling down out of the north, "—there'll be maybe forty minutes of dead calm. Forty minutes where nobody can follow anybody. I aim to be somewhere else by then."

"And if the eye doesn't pass over us?"

"Then we drown," Coral said, "and honestly, Sera, given the week I've had, drowning is starting to sound restful."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, which Coral counted as a victory. Sera Latch did not give away laughter cheaply. She'd spent three years living rough on this nameless cay, armed and alone, waiting for a father who had died before he could come to her—and she still laughed at Coral's gallows humor, once, briefly, like a coin slipped under a door.

Behind them, footsteps in wet sand. Wren came down the beach carrying two jerry cans of fuel they'd scavenged from the wrecked skiff Harrow's men had left snagged on the reef, and Coral watched them walk—watched the careful way they moved now, like someone who had stopped pretending their body belonged to somebody else's purposes.

Wren Vale had confessed everything two nights ago, sitting across a driftwood fire with rain hissing on the embers: the insurance clerk story, the handlers, the coded reports, the radio smashed and sunk off the cay's north point. Everything except why they'd stayed. When Coral had asked, Wren had looked at her for a long moment with those gray eyes that never quite gave anything away, and said, "Ask me again when we're through the storm."

So they were going through the storm.

* * *

They loaded the boat in the last grey light, working in the rhythm of people who had learned each other's hands without meaning to. Coral took the helm because it was her boat and her water; Sera took the bow because she refused to do anything else; Wren worked the pump and the lines and said very little, which was normal, but there was a new quality to the silence now. It wasn't the silence of a spy conserving secrets. It was the silence of someone standing at a door they'd already decided to open.

"One condition," Sera said as they pushed off. She stood shin-deep in the surge, holding the gunwale while Coral fired the engine, and the first real gusts of the storm tore her hair loose around her face. "When you find whatever my father hid out there—you bring me with you. I don't care what it is. I don't care who else wants it. He sent it to *you* because he was dying and desperate, but he lived it for *me*. I'm coming."

Coral met her eyes over the engine noise. There was no arguing with that kind of arithmetic. "You're coming," she agreed.

Sera swung aboard, and the cay let them go.

The first hour was the worst thing Coral had ever done on water, and she had once spent six hours trapped in a flooded cargo hold listening to her own air run out.

The sea didn't have waves anymore so much as it had opinions—vast, moving walls of green-black water that picked the boat up like a toy and set it down hard enough to make her teeth click. Rain came horizontally, stinging as sleet, blinding as paint. The compass with its ring of numbers sat lashed inside her jacket, and the map was sealed in oilskin against her chest, and every time the hull slammed down between swells she felt the bilge canister shift and prayed to no god in particular that her patch job would hold.

"Wren!" she shouted over the wind. "How's she taking water?"

Wren appeared beside her, soaked to the bone, one hand white-knuckled on the rail. "Faster than I'm pumping!" they shouted back. "We've got maybe an hour!"

"Then pray for a short storm!"

"I gave up praying when I took this job!" But they were grinning—actually grinning, wild and alive, and Coral realized with a start that she'd never seen Wren grin before. Three weeks of careful, watchful, guarded faces, and here at the edge of drowning they finally looked like a person instead of a mission. It was, she thought absurdly, a good look on them. She'd have told them so if she'd had breath to spare.

Sera clung to the bow rail and called depths from memory—Ede had fished these waters before the navy closed them, and he'd drawn his daughter maps in the margins of old charts, and some of that knowledge had lodged in her like shrapnel. "Shoal off the port side! Two cables! Bear right or we're ground meat!"

Coral bore right. The boat groaned, wallowed, climbed a wave that seemed to go on forever, and for one suspended moment hung at the crest with the whole ruined world visible below—a chaos of white and black lit by lightning that turned the ocean silver from underneath—and then dropped into the trough like a stone down a well.

Something cracked aft. Not the hull. Worse, somehow: the mizzen step, the fitting that held the little auxiliary mast, tearing loose with a shriek of wood and iron. The mast went over the side trailing halyards, and the sudden drag slewed the stern around, and for ten seconds Coral fought the wheel with everything she had while the boat tried to broach and end them all.

"Cut it!" she screamed. "Cut the lines or it'll pull us under!"

Wren didn't hesitate. They went over the transom rail into the flooding cockpit with a knife already out, and Coral lost sight of them in the rain and spray, and for three heartbeats—three long, cold heartbeats—she thought the sea had simply taken them, quietly, without ceremony, the way it took everything eventually.

Then the drag eased. The boat came back around under her hands. And Wren hauled themselves up over the rail, knife between their teeth, hair plastered flat, looking like something the storm had decided to return.

"Mast's gone," they yelled, spitting saltwater. "So's my favorite knife. We're even."

"We are nowhere near even," Coral yelled back, and heard Sera laughing up front, high and half-hysterical, and found herself laughing too, because the alternative was screaming.

* * *

The eye found them a little after midnight.

It happened the way it always happens, which is to say impossibly: one moment the world was trying to kill them, and the next the world simply stopped. The rain thinned to a drifting veil. The wind died to nothing. Above them the clouds ripped open along a seam, and stars showed through—cold, indifferent, beautiful stars, wheeling in a circular cathedral of cloud with walls a hundred miles high.

The sea beneath was still heaving, all that stored fury rolling on without the wind to drive it, but manageable now. Rideable.

Coral killed the engine to half-throttle and let the boat wallow, and for a moment none of them spoke. The silence after that kind of noise has a texture all its own, like cotton packed in the ears. Sera slid down against the bow rail and put her head in her hands. Wren stood dripping in the cockpit, staring up at the ring of sky.

"It's like being inside a bell," Wren said quietly.

"My father used to say that." Sera's voice was muffled. "About the eye. Said it was the only honest part of a storm—the only part that tells you exactly how much trouble you're in."

"How much trouble are we in?" Wren asked Coral.

Coral checked the bilge—six inches of water, steady, pump keeping pace—and consulted the map by the dim red glow of the binnacle lamp. The coordinates sat inside the eye's track if the storm held its line, which storms never did, but she'd bet the boat on it, because the boat was what she had.

"Enough," she said. "Forty minutes of calm, maybe less. Then the back wall hits us harder than the front. So we use the calm."

"To do what?" Sera lifted her head.

Coral looked at her, then at Wren, and made the decision she'd been circling since the cay. "To get close enough to dive. The cache isn't on land, Sera. Your father's map doesn't lead to a cave or a cove. It leads to a flooded cistern on the seafloor—old naval construction, pre-war, marked on no chart I've ever seen. The coordinates put it under thirty feet of water, four miles east of here. I can't dive in that—" she nodded at the churning sea beyond the eye wall, "—but in the eye, if we can hold position, I can go down."

"In *this*?" Sera gestured at the swell, which even calmed was throwing the boat in lazy twelve-foot circles.

"I've dived in worse."

"You haven't," Sera said flatly. "Nobody has."

"She might have, actually," Wren said, and there was something odd in their voice—something almost fond. "I've read her salvage logs. Well. The ones her handler kept. She once brought up a propeller shaft in conditions the syndicate's own divers called impossible, and she billed them double for the insult."

Coral blinked at them. "You've read my logs?"

"I've read everything about you," Wren said, and then seemed to hear themselves, and went slightly pink even in the red lamplight. "Professionally. It was my job."

"Your job," Coral repeated.

"Which I quit," Wren said. "Smashed the radio. You were there."

"For the record," Sera muttered, "I liked it better when you two weren't flirting. It was less nauseating."

"We're not—" Coral began.

"Storm's back edge is going to eat us alive," Wren said quickly, "so perhaps we could flirt later?"

"There's no later unless we move now," Coral said, grateful and annoyed in equal measure, and put the helm over.

* * *

The four miles through the eye's calm were the strangest sailing of her life: running hard across a heaving, moonlit sea inside a tunnel of screaming cloud, the storm's roar audible on every side like a crowd baying at the edges of an arena. Twice the eye wobbled—storms breathe, shift, reconsider—and the wind gusted back in from the west hard enough to lay the boat over, and twice Coral wrestled her back upright with her arms burning and her teeth gritted and Sera shouting bearings from the bow.

And then, three miles out, the sea tried to take her.

It was a freak crossing swell—one of those accidents of reflected wave-trains that no forecast predicts, two walls of water meeting at a single point in all the ocean. The boat hit the seam broadside and rolled. Not a lurch—a full, sickening roll, past forty degrees, past fifty, rail in the water, and Coral felt her feet leave the deck.

There was no drama in it. That was the strange thing she remembered afterward. No slow-motion, no flashing of her life before her eyes. Just the deck tilting away and the black water arriving to meet her, casual as a handshake, and the idiotic clear thought: *the compass is in my jacket, it'll sink, Ede's map will sink, everything sinks, everything always—*

A hand closed on her wrist.

Not a gentle hand. A desperate one, wrenching her arm half out of its socket, and Wren's voice—raw, furious, terrified—right against her ear: "*I've got you—I've GOT you—*" and then Sera's weight landing across both their legs, anchoring them, and the boat slamming back upright with a concussion that threw all three of them into the cockpit in a tangle of limbs and rope and seawater.

For a moment they just lay there, breathing.

Coral became aware, gradually, that Wren had wrapped a line around their own waist at some point in the night—she hadn't seen them do it, hadn't noticed, but there it was, a safety line running to the main cleat, and their hands locked around her wrist with a grip that would leave bruises. Their face was inches from hers. In the red lamplight their eyes were wide and wet and completely unguarded.

"You tied yourself on," she said stupidly.

"Two hours ago," Wren said. "After the mast went. In case—" They stopped. Started again. "I spent eight months pretending I didn't care whether you lived or died. I'm done. Do you understand? I'm done pretending. If the storm wants you, it goes through me, and I am extremely annoying to go through."

Coral laughed. It came out cracked and salt-burned and more honest than anything she'd said in years. "That may be the worst declaration anyone's ever made to me."

"I'm a spy. We're trained in subtlety."

"You're the least subtle person I've ever met."

"And yet," Wren said, still gripping her wrist, still not letting go, "I fooled you for three weeks."

"You fooled me for nine days," Coral corrected. "I suspected you by day ten. I just wanted to see what you'd do."

"What I'd do," Wren repeated softly. They looked at her the way people look at a horizon they've been sailing toward. "What I did was fall in—"

"Landfall bearing!" Sera bellowed from the bow, pointing east, where a low dark shape had risen against the lesser darkness—the drowned spine of the old naval breakwater, just breaking the surface a quarter mile ahead, exactly where Ede's map promised. "Whatever you two are doing, do it faster! Back wall's twenty minutes out, maybe fifteen! Look at the west!"

Coral looked. Along the eye's western rim, the cloud wall had begun to lean toward them like a collapsing building, and beneath it the sea was already whitening, the storm gathering itself for the second act.

She squeezed Wren's wrist once—hard, deliberate, a promise with punctuation—and let go, and put her hands on the wheel.

"Hold us steady over the mark," she said. "However you have to do it. I'm going down."

"The cistern's flooded," Sera said, hauling on the anchor line they'd rigged, voice tight. "My father's notes say the hatch is jammed half-open. You'll have maybe ten minutes of light and no margin."

"Ten minutes is generous," Coral said, reaching for her tank rig. "I've done miracles on five."

Through the binnacle's red glow, Wren watched her with an expression she'd remember for the rest of her life—fear and faith in equal measure, worn openly at last, no handler's mask left anywhere on them.

"Come back up," Wren said. It wasn't a request.

"Wasn't planning otherwise," Coral said, and stepped off the rail into the black water, as the first fist of the returning storm slammed against the eye wall behind them like a door closing.
