## 18. The Cache

The eye of the monsoon was not the calm thing sailors promised each other in bars. It was a held breath, a ceiling of bruised cloud with a hole punched through it, and through that hole fell a light so clean and gold it looked like an apology from the sky.

Coral stood at the wheel of her half-drowned boat and steered into that light, because it was the only door out of the storm, and behind her came Sera Latch with a rifle wrapped in oilcloth and Wren Vale bailing for their life.

"Left side's winning," Wren called up, which was bailer-speak for *we are losing*.

"Then bail like you mean it."

"I am bailing like I *invented* it."

"Then invent faster."

It was, Coral thought distantly, the strangest conversation of her life, and she was having it while threading a leaking boat through a drowned channel toward coordinates drawn by a dead man on the back of a promise. The sea around them had gone strange in the storm's eye — flattened, glassy, lit from below as if the water itself were holding its breath along with the sky. Rain still fell at the edges of the world, gray curtains marching in circles, but here in the middle there was only the light and the sound of Wren's bucket and the small stubborn heartbeat of her engine.

"There," Sera said. She had climbed halfway up the mast with the oilcloth bundle slung across her back like a bandolier, and she pointed with her chin at a low green shoulder of rock ahead. "That's it. That's the escarpment."

Coral saw it and felt something turn over in her chest. A ridge of black stone ran out into the sea like the spine of some enormous animal surfacing after a long sleep, and where it met the water there was a notch — a slot of calm water between two walls of rock, barely wide enough for her boat, sheltered from every direction the storm could come from.

Ede Latch had drawn that notch from memory, twenty years ago, in pencil, on the back of a chart he'd stolen or earned or been given. He'd drawn it dying, in a room that smelled of iodine and rum, and he'd looked at Coral with eyes gone yellow as old brass and said, *You'll know it when you see it, girl. Water doesn't lie twice.*

She'd thought he was senile. She owed his ghost an apology.

"Slot's maybe four meters," she said. "I'll take us in under sail power alone. Kill the engine — noise carries over water even in a storm."

"You think they can hear us?" Wren asked, climbing up beside her, soaked to the bone, hair plastered flat, looking — Coral noted with some irritation — unfairly handsome in a way that suggested even drowning had standards.

"I think Bram Harrow has a fast boat and no patience, and I think the navy cutter out there has sonar and a grudge. So yes. Quietly."

They slid into the notch like a coin into a purse. The walls of rock rose on either side, black and streaked white with centuries of birdlime, and inside the slot the water lay still as bathwater. The wind simply stopped, cut off at the rim, and the silence that followed was so complete Coral could hear her own pulse.

Sera dropped down from the rigging. "My father used to talk about this place." Her voice came out rough. "He'd go quiet whenever anyone mentioned the northern coast. I thought it was grief. Now I think it was guilt."

"Ede didn't sink that ship," Coral said.

"No." Sera looked at the water. "But he watched it happen. And he spent the rest of his life carrying what he saw down here instead of saying it out loud. That's its own kind of drowning."

Wren said nothing. Wren had been saying very little since the outer squall, since the moment they'd hauled Coral back over the gunwale with both hands and held on a beat longer than rescue required. But Coral had caught them looking at the transmitter-shaped absence in their kit bag more than once, and she'd decided — provisionally, the way you decide anything in weather like this — that whatever Wren Vale was, they were hers now, or near enough.

"Anchor's set," Wren said. "What are we diving?"

Coral unrolled Ede's map on the cockpit sole and weighted the corners with brass fittings. In the clean light of the eye, the pencil lines stood out sharp: the notch, the escarpment, and then a series of marks — numbers, the same numbers ringed around the faceless compass in her pocket — leading inland from the slot to a symbol like a circle with a broken crown.

"Cistern," Sera said, before Coral could ask. "There was a garrison here once, before the navy moved in. They cut reservoirs into the rock. My father worked salvage on this coast his whole life; he knew every one of them." She tapped the crowned circle. "This one floods at high tide. Sealed entrance, underwater approach. Nobody hides anything in a dry cave. Everybody hides things where divers drown."

"Wonderful," said Wren. "I love a plan with a mortality rate."

"You don't have to dive," Coral said.

"Yes, I do." Wren said it simply, without drama, and then seemed surprised at themselves. "I mean — someone has to hold the line. And count your air. And not panic if you don't come up."

"That's surface work."

"It's *staying* work." Wren met her eyes. "I've done enough leaving for one lifetime."

Coral looked at them for a long moment, and then nodded, because there wasn't time for whatever else was rising in her throat, and because some things are better said by doing than by saying.

* * *

The water inside the slot was cold in a way tropical water shouldn't be — cold coming up from below, from deep channels under the escarpment, the whole coast breathing through hidden lungs. Coral went down the anchor line hand over hand, torch beam cutting a cone through water that turned from green to blue-black beneath her.

The cistern mouth opened in the rock wall at nine meters: a square-cut doorway, engineered, deliberate, its lintel still bearing chisel marks from men dead two hundred years. Beyond it, darkness. Her torch found the flooded gallery running inward and upward, a sloping tunnel of dressed stone, and she swam in with the compass in her left fist and Ede's map rolled in a waterproof sleeve strapped to her forearm.

The numbers on the compass ring weren't a heading. She'd understood that much weeks ago, lying awake in her bunk while a navy launch idled beyond the breakwater. They were depths and distances — a diver's instructions written in brass. *Nine meters to the door. Forty meters along the gallery. Ascend three. Turn at the mark.*

And there was the mark: a brass plate bolted to the stone, corroded green, stamped with the same crowned-circle symbol. The compass in her hand trembled — no, she trembled, the compass was steady as a stone — and she put her palm against the plate and felt, absurdly, like knocking.

Beyond the plate the gallery rose and broke the surface.

Coral came up slowly, torch first, into an air pocket that smelled of stone and salt and time. Her beam swept across a vaulted chamber the size of a chapel, dry above the waterline, its floor a shelf of rock littered with the small debris of decades — a rusted lantern, a coil of rope gone to red dust, a wooden crate stenciled with letters she couldn't read until she got closer and wiped away the silt with her glove.

*H.M.S. DEPOT — CONFIDENTIAL.*

Her heart was suddenly very loud in the quiet.

The crate's lid had been pried loose long ago and set back carelessly. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had kept better than any coffin, lay a metal document case, a canvas roll, and a photograph album with a leather cover furred green with mold.

She hauled them onto the dry shelf and knelt there, dripping, and opened the document case first because it was the smallest and therefore, in her experience, the most dangerous.

Logs. Ship's logs — but not navy-issue books. These were typed pages, carbon copies, the kind that get made when someone wants a record that will survive the official one being burned. The typing was neat and desperate. Coral read by torchlight, and the chamber got colder around her as she read.

*A civilian vessel, designated VESPER, carrying approximately one hundred and forty persons, intercepted in restricted waters during exercises. Orders given. Orders questioned. Orders repeated.*

*Vessel sank at 0340. Survivors: none recovered.*

*Official position: training accident, unmanned target hulk. All personnel present will sign the attached statement. Failure to sign will be treated as*

The page ended mid-sentence, torn away, as if whoever typed it had run out of either ink or nerve. The next page was the statement itself, a paragraph of lies, followed by a signature list — thirty-one names, ranks, dates. Some signatures were firm. Some shook so badly the pen had nearly torn the paper.

Coral sat back on her heels in the dark and let it land.

A hundred and forty people. Mothers, probably. Children, certainly. A ship full of somebody's everything, sunk in the dark by men following orders, and then folded up into language — *training accident, unmanned hulk* — the way you fold a body into a tarp. And the navy hadn't just buried it. They'd made everyone who touched it bury it too, signature by signature, until the lie had thirty-one roots.

Except one man hadn't signed. At the bottom of the list, where line thirty-two should have been, someone had written in pencil, in a hand Coral knew from the map: *I will carry it instead. — E.L.*

"Oh, Ede," she said aloud, and her voice bounced off the vaulted stone and came back smaller.

She opened the photograph album with hands that weren't quite steady. Black-and-white prints, water-spotted at the corners. A ship — modest, coastal, rust-streaked, the kind of vessel that carried families and goats and hope between islands nobody important lived on. Crowded decks. Faces looking at the camera with the particular patience of people who have waited a long time for anything, including photographs.

The last photograph was different. Taken from a distance, grainy, from the deck of another ship. The Vesper sat low in the water. There was smoke. There was, in the foreground, blurred, the rail of the warship taking the picture, and standing at that rail, small and unmistakable, a young man in salvage gear who had turned toward the camera as if he already knew what the picture would cost him.

Ede Latch, twenty years younger, watching a hundred and forty people die.

Coral closed the album. She stayed kneeling a while. Down the flooded gallery, far off, her own bubbles ticked against stone, patient as a clock.

When she finally moved, she moved like a diver: deliberately, nothing wasted. Logs, album, manifest, and the carbon copies went into the waterproof bag she'd brought for exactly this and had told herself was for salvage brass. She sealed it. She took one last look around the chapel of stone — at the lantern, the rope dust, the crate with its confident stencil — and thought about how many rooms like this the world must hold, all the little chapels where the truth gets stored by people too honest to burn it and too scared to shout it.

Then she went down into the water and started home.

* * *

She broke the surface in the slot at dusk-that-wasn't, because the eye had closed while she was under. The light had gone. Rain came hissing across the rock walls in sheets, and the wind had returned with a sound like the whole sea clearing its throat.

Wren hauled her up the ladder they'd rigged, hands sure, face white. "You were down ninety minutes. I counted every one of them twice."

"Find anything interesting while you counted?"

"A new respect for arithmetic." They got the tank off her shoulders and their hands were shaking, and they didn't pretend otherwise, and Coral found she liked them for it.

Sera took the waterproof bag without asking, weighed it, and looked at Coral with an expression that had stopped being armed hostility somewhere over the last two days and become something harder to name. "Is it all there?"

"All of it. He carried it instead." Coral pulled off her hood. "He wrote it in the margin where the signatures should've been."

Sera turned away. Her shoulders did something private. When she turned back her voice was level, mostly. "Twenty years. He sent me money every month and never once said why he couldn't come home."

"He was keeping your name off that list."

"I know." Sera hugged the bag to her chest. "That's the worst part. I know."

And that was when Wren, who had been scanning the slot's mouth out of habit, went very still, and said, in a voice Coral had learned to trust more than her own instruments:

"Coral. We have company."

Bram Harrow stood on the rocks at the top of the slot, forty feet up, where the escarpment broke against the sky. He had come alone down the cliff path — or appeared alone, which with Harrow amounted to the same threat. The rain streamed off him. In his right hand, held with the relaxed competence of a man who'd cleaned more guns than he'd read books, was a pistol, pointed generally downward in the manner of a man making a point rather than a corpse.

His voice carried down the slot easily, pitched under the wind the way only certain men can pitch it.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." He pronounced it like a debt. "You're a difficult woman to find. I want you to know I've enjoyed trying."

Coral stepped forward on the wet deck, barefoot, in her dive suit, empty-handed, and discovered she was not afraid. That was the surprise of it. Somewhere between the cistern and the rain, fear had gotten off the boat.

"Harrow," she called back. "You're a long way from your ledger."

"I go where the assets go." He tilted his head at the bag in Sera's arms. "That's a lot of paper for a salvage job."

"It's a lot of paper for a grave."

"Paper burns." He smiled, and even at forty feet, even in the rain, the smile reached her. "Come up the path, Mrs. Ashgrove. Bring the bag. Leave the Latch woman and your friend out of it, and we'll discuss your debt like civilized people. You have my word."

Coral looked at Wren. Wren looked at her. And Coral thought about the word *civilized*, and about thirty-one signatures, and about a man who'd chosen to carry the truth instead of signing it, and about the flooded gallery behind her where a hundred and forty names were waiting in the dark for someone to say them out loud.

"Give me a minute," she called up pleasantly. "I'm soaking wet and I've just been diving. I'd hate to negotiate while hypothermic."

"Take two," Harrow said agreeably. "I'm not going anywhere. Neither are you."

Behind him, far out past the escarpment, half-glimpsed through the rain, a gray shape was coming on through the storm — a cutter, running dark, closing on the slot like a verdict.

Wren saw it too. Their breath went out slow. "Coral," they murmured, "that's not Harrow's boat."

"No," Coral said, watching the gray shape rise and fall, rise and fall, closer with every swell. "It isn't."

She picked up the waterproof bag, and smiled at Bram Harrow through the rain, and began — unhurried, barefoot, thinking very hard and very fast — to climb.
