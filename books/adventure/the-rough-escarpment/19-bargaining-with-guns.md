## 19. Bargaining with Guns

Wren had always imagined that the moment of their betrayal—if it ever came—would feel like something. A drumbeat, a cold wind, a line of scripture. Instead it felt like sand in their boot and Bram Harrow's voice scraping across the rocks like a file on rust.

"Put it down," Harrow said, "and everyone walks away dry."

He stood on the black shelf of rock above the tideline with a pistol held low and casual, the way men hold guns when they've never once worried about needing them. Behind him, two of his people fanned out along the cay's spine, silhouetted against a sky gone the color of wet slate. The monsoon was still an hour out, maybe less, but you could taste it already—ozone and iron, the air thick enough to chew.

And beyond the breakwater, closing at a speed no fishing vessel had any business making, came the navy cutter.

Wren crouched beside Coral at the base of the rocks, close enough to see the canister strapped flat against her ribs under her jacket, close enough to count the breaths she was taking. Four in. Four out. The old diver's trick. She'd taught it to Wren three nights ago during the pump watch, back when they were still pretending to be strangers.

"That's Harrow?" Coral murmured.

"That's Harrow."

"He looks shorter than his reputation."

"He compensates," Wren said. "Boats, guns, volume."

The ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. Good, Wren thought. Stay angry. Anger keeps you sharp; fear just makes you heavy.

* * *

Here was the thing nobody had warned Wren about when they'd taken this job: that the arithmetic would stop working.

The original math had been beautiful in its simplicity. Locate Coral Ashgrove. Track the compass. Report coordinates. A clean transaction between a person who wanted to be useful and an institution that wanted to be thorough. Wren had told themselves it was necessary work—the kind of quiet, unglamorous diligence that kept worse things from happening. Necessary. They'd worn the word like a coat against the rain for eleven months.

Then Coral had laughed at their terrible knot-work and re-tied it without comment. Then she'd given them the last of the coffee without being asked. Then she'd stood in a flooding hold all night working a bilge pump beside them, salt-crusted and exhausted, humming some off-key salvage-yard song, and somewhere in that long dark the word *necessary* had quietly stopped fitting.

Now here they were, crouched behind basalt with a gun pointed at the woman they'd been sent to betray, doing arithmetic that refused to balance: one canister of truth against one life already half-spent against one navy cutter full of people who would very much like this story never to have been found.

Wren looked at Coral's profile—jaw set, eyes moving, always moving—and made the only calculation left that came out even.

They reached into their jacket.

Coral's hand snapped to their wrist. "What are you—"

"Trust me," Wren said. "One more time. I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like a eulogy."

"It's a plan." They pulled out the cracked radio handset—the spare, the one Coral didn't know about, the one they'd kept after smashing the first because old habits die ugly deaths. "I spent a year learning how these people talk. Let me talk to them."

* * *

Harrow's voice rolled down the rocks again, patient as tide. "Ashgrove! I know you can hear me. You're a practical woman. You didn't get this far by dying for other people's secrets."

Wren flinched at that, because it was true, or had been, and because they were partly the reason he knew so much about her. Every report they'd filed had been a small theft of her privacy, and now all those stolen pieces were being assembled into a weapon and aimed at her.

Coral stood up slowly, hands visible, and called back in a voice like weathered rope: "I'm listening, Bram."

"The canister. Hand it to my man and your debt dies tonight. Not deferred. Dead. And I'll put you on a plane anywhere you can name. Bali. Lisbon. The moon, if you've got a fare."

"And Vale?"

Harrow's pause was very slightly too long. "Vale is a separate conversation."

"There it is," Wren breathed.

"I told you," Coral said through her teeth, not looking at them. "I told you he'd do exactly this."

"He's not wrong about me, though," Wren said. "That's the part that stings. I am a separate conversation." They keyed the handset, thumbed it to the cutter's working frequency—they'd memorized the patrol bands in another life, back when they were still allowed to believe they were one of the good ones—and spoke low and fast into the grille.

"Cutter Meridian, this is Overwatch Six. Do you copy?"

A crackle. Then, clipped and suspicious: "Overwatch Six, authenticate."

Wren gave them the old call-sign suffix, the one from before the reshuffle, the one a desk officer three ranks too junior wouldn't know had been retired. It hung there for four endless seconds.

"Copy, Overwatch Six. Go ahead."

"Be advised, we have visual on multiple armed hostiles, Cay Nine-Seven sector, plus one vessel of interest anchored off the north spit. Hostiles are attempting to recover contraband material from the wreck site. Recommend immediate interdiction before the front closes. Say again: material is mobile. Window is short."

Silence on the band. Wren could picture the bridge of the Meridian: a young officer doing sums, a storm front on the radar, a chance to be a hero before lunch instead of a clerk who missed it.

"Overwatch Six, confirm hostile count."

"Four confirmed, possibly six. Armed. Moving now."

"Understood. Interdiction authorized. Overwatch Six, maintain observation. Do not engage."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Wren murmured, and killed the handset.

* * *

"You just—" Coral stared at them. "You just called a warship down on our heads."

"On *his* head. Ours is the problem we solve next." Wren jerked their chin toward the water behind the rocks, where the boat lay at anchor in a channel of green shadow. "The cutter will come around the point in about six minutes. Harrow has maybe four before he has to decide whether he's a smuggler or a businessman today. He cannot be found standing over you with a gun when that hull comes around the headland. Whatever else he is, he's careful. Careful men run."

"And us?"

"We go now, while everyone's watching everyone else." Wren finally let themselves say the rest of it, the part they'd been building toward since the pump watch, since the squall, since the moment catching a falling compass had felt less like duty than like instinct. "You dive the site. You put that canister where the dead can keep it. I'll keep the stage lit."

Coral looked at them for a long moment. Rain began—not the monsoon yet, just its advance scouts, fat drops ticking against the rocks.

"Wren Vale," she said slowly, "you are about to stand between a syndicate enforcer and a naval patrol with nothing but a dead radio and a good memory."

"And excellent posture."

"If this goes wrong—"

"When has anything gone right? We're overdue." They tried for lightness and mostly landed. "Go. Dive. Don't argue with me about decompression stops, I'll know if you skip them, I've been keeping notes."

Something moved across her face then—something that wasn't quite a smile and wasn't quite grief but lived in the neighborhood of both. She gripped their forearm, hard, once, the way divers grip things they intend to find again.

"Six minutes," she said. "Don't be a hero longer than that."

"No promises. Heroes bill by the hour."

She went over the rocks like water going downhill, stripped to her suit, fins in hand, and slid into the channel without a splash worth naming. Wren watched the white wake of her descent fade, then turned and climbed.

* * *

The trick, Wren had learned long ago in rooms where loyalty was a currency subject to inflation, was to look busier than you were and more dangerous than you felt. So they walked out onto the open shelf of rock with the dead handset in plain view, held up like a lantern, and let Harrow see them coming.

"Bram Harrow," they called. "We should talk before your evening gets complicated."

Harrow's gun came up—not aimed, but awake. His two men shifted on the ridge. "Vale. There's a name I expected to hear from my side of things. Funny place to run into you. On *their* side of the beach."

"Funny thing about sides." Wren stopped ten paces out, hands loose, heart hammering somewhere up around their molars. "They move. That's rather the definition."

"You were supposed to report in nine days ago."

"I did. You weren't listening to the right frequency." Wren tilted their head toward the sea, toward the gray wall of the approaching front, and pitched their voice to carry. "Because in about ninety seconds, a naval cutter is going to come around that headland looking for armed hostiles on this cay. Four to six of them, I said. Armed. Recovering contraband. I may have exaggerated the number by zero."

Harrow's expression didn't change, which was how Wren knew it had landed. Careful men did sums fast.

"You'd burn your own handlers," Harrow said. It wasn't a question. It was an appraisal.

"I'd burn anyone who pointed a gun at her," Wren said, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they had any right to expect. "That's the whole report, Bram. That's the final filing. Everything else is paperwork."

For a heartbeat—one long, salt-heavy heartbeat—Wren genuinely believed they might be shot on that rock, and discovered, with distant academic interest, that they could live with it. The canister would be at the bottom of the sea. Coral would be underwater, invisible, free. The math balanced. It had taken eleven months and a great deal of lying, but it finally balanced.

Then the cutter came around the headland.

It came the way Wren had promised it would—all bow wave and authority, running lights burning against the darkening sky, loudspeaker already warming up with a voice that could strip paint. Harrow's men melted backward off the ridge like ink in water. Harrow himself stood one second longer, pistol lowering by degrees, doing the last of his sums.

"This isn't finished," he said.

"It never is," Wren agreed. "That's what makes it a career."

And then, because the universe occasionally granted small mercies to people who least deserved them, the cutter's searchlight swung wide and caught the fast boat Harrow's crew had beached on the far spit—a sleek, expensive, deeply incriminating vessel—and every gun, every eye, every ounce of official attention on that water swung away from the black rocks and toward the money.

Harrow ran. Wren counted to ten, then twenty, then dove behind the rocks as the loudspeaker started issuing instructions nobody intended to follow, and watched the whole theater of it: the cutter bearing down on the syndicate boat, Harrow's people scattering like startled gulls, radios crackling, lights sweeping, the storm's first real gusts shoving everything sideways.

Nobody was watching the channel of green shadow behind the rocks.

Nobody was counting bubbles.

* * *

Wren got back to the boat with hands shaking so badly they dropped the mooring line twice, and was still cursing at the cleat when the water beside the hull broke and Coral surfaced, gasping, mask pushed up, one arm raised with the fist clenched.

Empty. The fist was empty.

For one obscene second Wren's whole chest went cold—then they saw her eyes above the mask, bright and fierce and almost laughing, and understood. Empty meant done. Empty meant placed. Empty meant the dead had accepted the delivery.

"Thirty-one meters," she rasped, hauling herself onto the ladder. "Your timing was—hah—your timing was *impeccable*. I could hear the loudspeaker through the water. Sounded like God reading regulations."

"How's the canister?"

"Snug. Quiet. Guarded." She pulled the mask off and looked at them—really looked, taking inventory the way she took inventory of wrecks, methodical and unsentimental and somehow, at the end of it, tender. "You gave them your own name. On an open band. Overwatch Six, broadcasting to a navy cutter. They'll have that recording by morning."

"I know."

"They'll disavow you. Worse than disavow. You'll be a loose thread with a biography."

"I've been a loose thread for months," Wren said. "Today I just made it official." They busied themselves with the engine, because their face was doing something they didn't authorize and they needed a moment to negotiate with it. "Besides, I'm told I have excellent posture. It'll carry me."

Coral caught their sleeve. Stopped them. The storm was arriving properly now, rain coming in slanted sheets, the cutter's lights strobing distantly through it, and in all that chaos she held their arm and said, quietly, under the noise:

"Why?"

Not *how*, not *what now*. Why. The question Wren had been dodging since the pump watch, since the coffee, since the knot.

Wren looked at her rain-wet face and told the truth, or the largest piece of it they could fit in their mouth: "Because somewhere between the bad knots and the bilge pump, you stopped being an assignment. And I found I'd rather be unemployed than accurate."

She held their gaze a moment longer. Then she shoved past them to the wheel, gruffly, ears pink in a way that absolutely was not the cold.

"Get us out of here, clerk," she said. "Before I start liking you, and we both do something stupid."

Wren took the lines, grinning like an idiot into the rain, and brought the boat about—south, into the teeth of the monsoon, toward the cay where a girl named Sera Latch waited with a rifle and her father's unfinished business, and toward whatever came after.

Behind them, the cutter's searchlight swept the empty rocks one last time, found nothing but storm, and gave up.

The water kept what it kept. For now.

But forty miles south, in the flooded dark of a cistern older than any of them, a brass compass lay in the silt beside a canister of photographs and names—and somewhere out in the rain, Bram Harrow's boat was running the same heading they were.

Wren saw its running light first, far off through the murk, and said nothing yet.

Some conversations, they were learning, you saved for calmer water.
