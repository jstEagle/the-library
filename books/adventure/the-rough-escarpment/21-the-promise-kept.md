## 21. The Promise Kept

The morning after the monsoon broke, the cay looked like something God had shaken out of His pockets and regretted.

Driftwood lay in windrows along the tideline. Sera's lean-to was half gone, its tarpaulin wrapped around a casuarina like a scarf on a drunk. The *Magpie* sat beached above the high-water mark with her stern cocked at an apologetic angle, and Coral stood beside her with a mug of instant coffee that tasted like hot salt and lies, watching Wren try to coax the outboard back to life.

"Any luck?" she called.

"It's thinking about it," Wren said. "It's a very thoughtful engine."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

Three days ago she had surfaced from the cistern with nothing in her hands and everything in her chest, and Harrow had looked at her across the rocks with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. Then the cutter's searchlight had swept the point, and Harrow had made his choice — the same choice men like him always made, which was to be somewhere else when the questions started. He'd backed his boat off the rocks and vanished into the rain, and Coral had swum ashore empty-handed, and it had felt — this was the strange part, the part she still hadn't found words for — it had felt like breathing.

The canister lay forty feet down inside the wreck of the *Vespera*, wedged behind a collapsed bulkhead where only someone who dove as long and as deep and as stupidly as Coral Ashgrove would ever find it. Let the dead keep their papers. They'd held them longer than anyone living had.

She drained the coffee, grimaced, and went to find Sera.

* * *

Sera Latch kept her father's ashes in an ammunition tin, which told Coral most of what she needed to know about how Ede Latch had raised her.

They sat on the seaward rocks while the light came up gold and forgiving over water that had tried very recently to kill all three of them. Sera had her knees drawn up and her arms around them, and she looked younger without the rifle, though not by much. Grief had already finished building her face years ago; the last week had just redecorated.

"He never told you anything," Coral said. "About why he moved out here."

"He told me stories." Sera turned the tin over in her hands. "About reefs that weren't on charts. About a ship he said he'd seen go down when he was young, and how nobody ever asked him about it, and how the not-asking was the loudest thing he'd ever heard." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "I thought it was the sunstroke talking. He had a lot of sunstroke in him."

"He wasn't lying."

"No," Sera said quietly. "I know that now."

Coral had spent the night deciding how to do this. There were a dozen ways to say it and every one of them cost something, so she did what she always did underwater: she went slow, and she didn't lie.

"Your father found the wreck. Years ago, before they sealed the records. He got into the officers' compartment and took what was there — logs, photographs, the manifest. He knew what it proved. He also knew what proving it would cost, and he wasn't a brave man, by his own account." She watched Sera's jaw tighten. "His own words. He told me he'd spent forty years being a coward about one specific thing, and the map was him finally putting it down."

"He drew that map for me?"

"He drew it for whoever could finish it. I think he hoped it would be you. I think he also hoped it wouldn't." Coral looked out at the water, at the pale line of reef where the *Vespera* slept under her blanket of silt and silence. "There are eighty-one names on that manifest, Sera. Families. Kids. A woman who wrote letters right up until the day they sank her, and the navy filed those letters under *training accident* and burned the rest. Your father carried that for forty years. He wanted it delivered. Not to you — through you. To everyone."

Sera was silent for a long time. A gull argued with another gull about something, lost interest, left.

"I promised him I'd deliver it or burn it," Coral said. "I'm not burning it. But it's not mine to hand out either. It's yours. He was your father. So I'm asking, not telling: what do we do?"

Sera looked down at the ammunition tin, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a small, crooked, exhausted smile that was so much Ede's smile that Coral's throat hurt.

"We do it slowly," Sera said. "He was a patient man about exactly one thing, and this was it. Copies. From different ports. Different months. No name attached, no face, no boat anybody can trace. Let it arrive like weather." She tapped the tin. "And when it lands, everybody who reads it is going to wonder who sent it. And nobody will ever know it was a dying old prospector who couldn't swim."

Coral laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than she could measure — and it came out wet, and she decided not to apologize for that.

"That's the promise kept, then," she said.

"The promise kept," Sera agreed, and shook her hand on it, grip like a mooring cleat.

* * *

Wren found them an hour later, standing ankle-deep in the surf by the beached *Magpie*, arguing companionably about whether the engine was salvageable or merely sentimental.

"—all I'm saying," Wren was saying, "is that a machine that starts on the fourth pull is a machine with opinions, and you should respect that—"

"It starts on the fourth pull because the third pull floods it, Wren. That's not opinion, that's a carburetor."

"You say tomato."

Wren came up the beach with the spark plug in one hand and oil to the elbows, and Coral watched them approach and felt the thing she'd been circling for two days finally come around and look her in the eye.

Because there was a radio frequency Wren could still recite from memory. There was a handler — Coral had gotten the whole confession out of them on the second night, piece by piece, like pulling splinters — a handler named Ostrander who would be waiting at three separate relay points, and a cover identity that could be resumed, and a career that could be salvaged if Wren reported in within the month and swore the mission failed through no fault of their own. Deserters who came home got paperwork. Deserters who stayed away got disavowed, which was a polite word for erased.

Wren had known all of that when they'd fed the navy false coordinates. Had known it when they'd stood between Coral and Harrow's gun without being asked. Had known it every hour since, and had said nothing, and had instead fixed the bilge pump and learned to cook rice without burning it and laughed at Coral's jokes, which frankly showed poor judgment.

"So," Coral said, when Wren was close enough. "Ostrander."

Wren went still the way people do when a wave comes in colder than expected. "You've been saving that one up."

"A little. Three relay points. You could make the first one by Thursday if we drop you at Cutter's Cay." She kept her voice level, easy, a working voice. "I'm not going to pretend I haven't thought about it. You'd be safer. You'd have a life again. Papers, pay, a bed that isn't a boat bench."

"And you'd have a bounty on your crew manifest and no one to pull the starter cord four times," Wren said. "No."

"No?"

"No." Wren set the spark plug down on a rock with great care, as if it were a decision being formalized. "I did the math a while ago, actually. Somewhere around the squall. Or maybe the night you caught me with the transmitter and didn't say anything, which, by the way, was terrifying, I aged a decade." They wiped their hands on their shirt, leaving it worse. "My handlers will disavow me. That's not a threat, that's a schedule. But here's the thing I figured out about necessary work: it's only necessary if somebody's left to need it done. Ostrander doesn't need me. He needs a radio that says yes." They looked at her, and there was nothing performed in it, nothing clerkish, nothing left of the insurance-man costume. "You need a fourth pull. I'm choosing the fourth pull."

Coral discovered that her eyes were doing something embarrassing and elected to blame the wind off the water.

"For the record," she managed, "the third pull floods it. It's a carburetor problem. You're not romantic, you're mechanical."

"Noted," Wren said gravely. "I'll file a complaint with the engine."

Sera, from up the beach, without turning around: "Just kiss and fix the boat, the tide's coming in."

* * *

They left the cay two days later, the *Magpie* riding low but honest, the engine starting — this was recorded for posterity — on the fourth pull.

Sera stayed. She had her father's tin and her father's patience and a network of reef-camps and crab-boats that had never appeared on any chart, and copies of certain documents began moving through that network like seeds on a current, each one unsigned, each one landing in a different port in a different month. Coral helped with the first two runs and then handed it off entirely, because Sera was right: it should arrive like weather, and weather shouldn't have a face.

On the last morning, Sera pressed something into Coral's hand at the gunwale. It was the compass — the brass compass with no needle, only the ring of numbers, the thing that had started all of it in a dark compartment on a wreck the navy forbade anyone to touch.

"It matches the map," Coral said. "Or it matched. The map's gone."

"So keep the other half anyway." Sera closed Coral's fingers over it. "Ede believed things pointed at truths even when they pointed at nothing. Worst case, it's a paperweight with good taste. Best case—" she shrugged, and her crooked smile came out again, "—best case, someday it points at something else worth finding."

Then she stepped back onto her rocks, and did not wave, because waving was for people who expected to see you again, and Sera Latch preferred certainty — so instead she shouted, "Fourth pull!" as the *Magpie* cleared the point, and Coral shouted it back, and then the cay was a green smudge and then it was gone.

* * *

They worked north along the coast, taking honest wrecks.

A coaster gone down on a sandbar, her cargo of cement bags long since turned to submarine boulders; brass fittings, a ship's bell, a week's decent money. A fishing trawler on a reef shelf, sad and shallow, more barnacle than boat. Wren ran the surface end — lines, lift bags, the logbook, the endless arithmetic of air and tide — and Coral did what Coral had always done, which was go down into the cold and the green and bring things back.

Her debt to the Harrow syndicate died quietly, the way debts do when the syndicate's enforcer decides a witness is worth more uncollected than collected. Bram Harrow sent no message, made no threats. He simply stopped existing in her ledger, and Coral understood that this too was a kind of payment — hush money in reverse — and accepted it without gratitude, because some currencies you take and never spend.

At night, anchored in coves with names nobody used, they cooked badly and ate well and argued about the engine, which had become less a machine than a third member of the crew with strong opinions. Wren was teaching themselves navigation from a stolen textbook and getting it wrong in confident new ways. Coral was sleeping more than she had in years, and dreaming less, and once — only once, half-asleep, in the dark of the cabin with the anchor light swinging overhead — she said, "You could still go back, you know. It's not too late to make it clean," and Wren, already mostly asleep, mumbled, "Clean's overrated. I like the rust," and pulled the blanket higher, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

One evening in a roadstead off a town too small to have a name, Wren came aboard with the week's supplies and a newspaper folded to the inside pages, and laid it on the chart table without a word.

Coral read it twice.

*A naval review board has been convened,* the article said, in the flat language institutions use when they are bleeding. *Questions have been raised regarding training exercises conducted in coastal waters some twenty years past. Records previously classified are under examination. A spokesperson declined to comment.*

Underneath, in smaller type: further documents received, sources unknown, verification ongoing.

"They did it," Coral said. "It's actually moving."

"Like weather," Wren said.

Coral touched the compass in her pocket — she carried it now the way other people carried lucky coins — and thought of eighty-one names, and a woman's letters, and a cowardly old prospector who had waited forty years for the tide to turn, and she found that her eyes were doing the embarrassing thing again and this time did not blame the wind.

"Well," she said, clearing her throat, folding the paper. "Tomorrow we've got a wreck off the escarpment. Honest work. Brass and a bell, if the charts are honest, which they never are."

"The rough escarpment," Wren said, trying the name on. "Sounds like a place where something begins."

"It's a rock formation, Wren."

"Everything's a rock formation if you're patient enough."

Coral laughed, and banked the stove, and went out on deck to look at the stars coming up over the headland — over the long, dark, improbable shoulder of land that the charts called nothing much and that she was beginning, against all her better judgment and every hard-earned habit of her heart, to call home.
