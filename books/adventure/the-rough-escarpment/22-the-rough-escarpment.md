## 22. The Rough Escarpment

The wreck was a coastal trader called the *Merrow's Grace*, and she had gone down forty years ago with a hold full of crockery, which meant that every dive was like excavating the world's most patient dinner party.

"Plates," Wren said, holding one up to the light that slanted down through ten fathoms of green water. They were speaking through the regulator mouthpieces, which reduced the word to something like *plah*, but Coral understood. After months of diving together she could read Wren's whole vocabulary through a mask: *plah* meant plates, *plah-plah* meant plates and also a question about whether plates were worth the effort, and a flat palm waggled side to side meant *I found something and I refuse to get excited about it until you confirm it's worth getting excited about.*

Coral confirmed it was worth it. Intact transferware from a Kilnaran pottery house sold to collectors for more than the *Merrow's Grace* had probably earned in her entire working life. They'd spent three weeks on this wreck under a salvage license Wren had forged so competently that Coral had briefly felt a flicker of professional envy. The name on the license was Mara Voss. Coral's name, these days, was Nell Ferris. The names had been Wren's idea. Wren, it turned out, was very good at making people exist on paper who had never existed anywhere else.

They surfaced into a morning so clear it felt like a personal favor. The bay was a sheet of hammered tin, the sky above it pale and rinsed, and their boat — a slow, ugly, utterly reliable old workboat called the *Patience*, because Coral had named her during a week when patience was the only virtue she could still afford — rode at anchor with the smugness of a vessel that had finally stopped taking on water.

"Eleven plates," Wren said, spitting out their regulator and hauling the catch bag over the gunwale. "Two of them chipped. The Kilnaran blue ones."

"Eleven plates." Coral counted them herself, because counting was a habit debt had carved so deep into her that it had survived the debt's death. Eleven plates. No brass to weigh, no syndicate percentages, no handler skimming the top, no navy cutter idling beyond the breakwater with its engines warm. Just plates. She found, to her mild embarrassment, that she was smiling at crockery.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at ordinary objects like they're a miracle. You did it with the fish last Tuesday. You did it with a rope coil."

"It was a very good rope coil."

"It was an adequate rope coil, Nell Ferris." Wren said her false name with a small private relish, the way some people say a lover's name. They had never once slipped and called her Coral in front of anyone else. In private, though — in the dark of the cabin, in the small hours when the anchor light burned — they still used her true name, carefully, like handling something recovered from deep water.

Coral coiled the dive line and looked out across the bay to where the coast rose in a long, tumbled wall of gray stone — the escarpment the old charts called the Rough, a spine of broken country that ran from the headland down to the sea. From up there, she knew, the whole bay laid itself out like a map: the anchorage, the fishing village with its smoke-houses, the channel, the deep water beyond. From up there, a person could see everything coming.

That was the point. That was the whole point now.

"Race you up," she said.

"You know I'll lose."

"I know you'll lose. That's why I said it."

* * *

The path up the escarpment was a goat's argument — a scramble of loose shale and wind-bent scrub that no sensible person climbed, which was exactly why Coral liked it. Wren followed, complaining with the cheerful thoroughness of someone who had long since learned that complaining was a form of participation. Halfway up they stopped at the flat shelf of rock where they'd first sat, months ago, the night after they'd come through the monsoon's eye with a boat full of salt water and a canister full of dead men's truth.

That night felt far away and very close at once, the way the worst nights do. Coral could still summon the sound of the storm if she let herself — the rigging screaming, Wren's arms locked around her in the cockpit, both of them soaked past meaning, Sera Latch wedged in the cabin with a rifle across her knees and a look on her face that said she had decided, provisionally, not to hate either of them. They had run the eye on a bet that the storm would blind the navy and blind Harrow both, and the storm had kept its side of the bargain. The boat had very nearly not kept hers.

And then the cistern. The flooded cistern under the old signal station, and the cache Ede Latch had spent his last years guarding with a dying man's stubbornness: the logs, the photographs, the manifest. The *Vesperine*. Three hundred and eleven souls, most of them children, on a ship the navy had sunk in the dark and then erased — called it a training accident, called it nothing, called it a rumor spread by grief-crazed survivors. Coral had held the manifest in her gloves underwater and read the names through the fog of her mask, and she had understood, in the cold center of herself, that she was holding three hundred and eleven people who had been told, by everyone with the power to say so, that they had never existed.

She had hidden the canister in the bilge of her own leaking boat while the storm came down like a verdict. She had let Harrow believe the cache was still on the cay. She had watched him tear the island apart while she sat on her hands and said nothing, and she had felt — she would always feel — the strange cold shame of that, right alongside the fierce rightness of it. Both things were true. She had learned, that season, that both things could be true at once and a person could still stand up in the morning.

The leak had been Wren's doing, in the end. Not the boat's — the *documents'*.

They'd never told her exactly how. She knew pieces: a dead drop in a port two hundred miles south, a journalist with a reputation for being unkillable, a package of copies made in a harbor-town print shop by a marine insurance clerk who no longer existed. Wren had destroyed their radio rather than send her location to their handlers, and the navy had written Wren down as a deserter, and a deserter with nothing left to lose turns out to be the most dangerous kind of witness. The documents had gone out into the world the way water goes through a hull — quietly, everywhere, unstoppable.

And the world, it turned out, had been waiting for exactly that water.

* * *

From the top of the escarpment, the bay was a coin of light and the *Patience* a fleck of rust at its center. The wind came up the cliff face carrying salt and the smell of the smoke-houses, and somewhere below, a dog was announcing its opinions to a gull.

"Look," Wren said, and pointed south, along the coast toward the navy station that had once, in another life, sent a cutter to shadow a leaky workboat through open water.

The station was still there. But these days it was there the way a man stands in a room where a scandal has just been spoken aloud — present, rigid, pretending nothing has changed while everything has. The inquiry had opened six weeks ago. Coral followed it the way she used to follow weather: obsessively, from whatever harbor bar had a wireless, with Wren translating the official language into what it actually meant. Three admirals had resigned so far. A fourth was testifying. The word *Vesperine* — a word that for forty years had been a rumor, a ghost story told by coastal people who were not believed — was now printed in newspapers in the capital, in type large enough to read from across a room.

There had been no names. Not hers, not Wren's, not Sera's. The leak had been built the way Wren built everything — carefully, with exits. But Coral knew, some mornings, a fear that arrived before she was fully awake: that the inquiry would dig, that someone would trace the documents back to a print shop, to a forged license, to a woman named Nell Ferris who had appeared from nowhere with salt-water hands. Then Wren would find her already up, already counting the plates, already doing the arithmetic of escape routes, and Wren would say nothing at all, just put the kettle on, and the fear would pass through her like weather and move on.

"Eleven plates," Wren said now, settling onto the rock beside her. "At Kilnaran prices, that's the license fee, the fuel, and — if the broker in Port Anselm doesn't rob us, which he will, but gently — about six weeks of eating."

"Seven," Coral said. "If we eat fish."

"We always eat fish."

"Because fish is free and I am a woman who respects free things."

Wren laughed — the real laugh, the one they'd had to relearn, because the person Wren had been before had laughed mostly as a tool, a small bright lie deployed at useful moments. Coral had watched that laugh get replaced, week by week, the way a wreck gets colonized: slowly, then all at once, until the new growth was the structure.

She looked at Wren — this person who had been sent to watch her, who had lied to her with a cracked radio and a forged smile, who had caught the compass out of the air in a squall with hands that might have been stealing or might have been saving and had turned out, impossibly, to be saving — and felt the old vertigo of it all over again. She had trusted a stranger because the monsoon gave her no choice. The stranger had turned out to be the truest person she knew. Life did that. Life handed you your worst decision and then, if you survived it, called it the best one.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The work. Before. Being — " she gestured vaguely at the horizon, at the machinery of it, at the handlers and the codes and the necessary things.

Wren considered this with the seriousness they gave all questions, which was one of the things Coral had decided she could live inside for a long time.

"I miss believing it was necessary," Wren said. "That was a comfortable feeling. Like a coat." They shrugged. "But no. I don't miss the coat. I spent years telling myself that what we did was the price of the world staying quiet enough to be safe. And then I met a woman who dives into wrecks the navy forbids, because her rent is due, and she's braver and more decent than anyone in my entire service, and the world stayed quiet by sinking a ship full of children and calling it weather." Wren looked out at the water. "Necessary turned out to just mean *someone else pays*. I don't miss it."

Below them, the gull and the dog had reached a détente. The light was going long and gold across the bay, the way it did at this hour, and Coral sat with her knees up and her hands loose and let herself do the thing she had once forbidden herself: the counting. Not of plates. Of costs.

The debt to Harrow — gone, dissolved in the chaos of the standoff, because a syndicate enforcer with a gun on a beach full of navy witnesses does not stop to collect ledgers. Harrow himself had retreated into the surf-dark that night and had not surfaced in their lives since; the inquiry had a way of making men like him very quiet. The *Vesperine*'s truth — given away, every scrap of it, to a journalist, because Coral had understood at last that evidence locked in a bilge is just ballast, and truth only weighs anything when you let it out where people can stand on it. Her old life — gone: the harbor, the handler, the small shabby competence of surviving small. Her name. Her boat, the first one, the one that had wept itself onto a cay and never left it.

And what remained. A partner who caught compasses out of storms. A friend on the coast — Sera, who had taken her father's map back with both hands and cried the way stone cracks, all at once and without warning, and who now ran a salvage outfit two ports down and sent them work when the pickings were honest. A boat named *Patience* that floated. Eleven plates. Mornings like this one.

Survival, she thought, had cost her everything except the things that mattered. She turned the sentence over, tested its seams, found it sound. It would do for a book, if she ever wrote one. She wouldn't. But it would do.

"Look there," Wren said softly.

Far out past the channel, where the deep water began, a ship was coming up the coast — a naval transport, gray and heavy, riding low. But she was flying no ensign at the mast, and her course bent wide around the station, and as they watched she slowed, hove to, and began lowering boats.

Coral went still in the way she went still underwater, when the thing you'd been looking for finally swam into the edge of the light.

"That's not a patrol," she said.

"No."

"That's a recovery operation. They're going down to the *Vesperine*."

Wren said nothing for a long moment. The wind moved the scrub at their backs. Then: "The inquiry ordered the site preserved. Sealed. Nobody dives it without a court writ." Their voice had gone quiet and flat, the old flatness, the one from before — but their eyes were bright with something that wasn't fear. "Unless someone's about to make sure the wreck says what it's supposed to say. Or make sure it doesn't."

Coral stood. Below them the *Patience* tugged at her anchor, impatient, ready. The plates were in the hold. The fuel was topped off. The tide would turn within the hour, and the channel would run them straight out toward the deep water and the gray ship and the truth, still down there in the dark, waiting to be guarded or sold or told.

She found she was smiling.

"Get the kettle off the stove," she said. "We're diving."
