## 1. The Man Who Keeps Moving

There is a particular sound a dead railway makes when you walk it, and after eleven years I know it the way other men know their wives' breathing in the night. It's a silence with a grain to it. The rails still hold the memory of steel singing, but nothing comes to make them sing, so they just sit there under the rust like harp strings nobody plucks, and your boots on the ties keep a rhythm that pretends to be purpose.

I was three miles up the Kettle Spur when the frost finally let go of my beard. That's the thing about the high interior — spring arrives here the way an apology does, late and half-hearted, and by then you've already made your peace with winter. The spur climbed through lodgepole pine that had been quietly reclaiming the right-of-way for a decade, saplings shoulder-high between the ties, one of them growing straight up out of a switchstand as if the mountain itself had decided to file a complaint about the railway and was doing it slowly, out of spite.

I checked the rails anyway.

That was the job, such as it was. Walk the closed lines, log the condition of track no engine would ever pull, note which culverts had washed out, which trestles had gone soft, which miles of steel were worth pulling up for scrap and which should simply be left to the pines. The Sable River & Northern had stopped running this spur four years ago, and every season since, some clerk in the head office had signed off on money to have a man walk it and write down what he saw. Nobody read the reports. I suspected nobody had ever read them. But the reports existed, filed in triplicate, and somewhere a ledger balanced, and that was enough for the company, and — though I'd have denied it under oath — it was enough for me too.

Because here is what a man learns about walking: a man who is walking always has a reason. Ask him why he's going and he'll point ahead of himself. He's checking the line. He's meeting a contact. He's heading home. Nobody ever says *I am fleeing the inside of my own skull at four miles an hour*, though that would be more honest than most survey notes I've written.

The Kettle Spur ended at a loading ramp above a creek that didn't have a name on any map I trusted. I stood at the end of the steel and looked at where the rails just... stopped. Not derailed, not torn up. Stopped, mid-tie, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what he was saying. Beyond it the ground went on without any opinion from engineers, and I found myself standing there longer than the job required, doing the arithmetic I always did and never said aloud: *if the line went on, where would it go?* Up the creek. Over the saddle. Down into country I'd only seen from ridgelines, the headwaters of the Sable, all that folded green nobody had bothered to measure properly because there was no profit in measuring it.

I wrote *end of track, condition fair* in my notebook, which was a lie, because the condition of the end of track was that it broke something in me a little every time I reached one.

* * *

You'll want to know about the bridge. Everyone eventually wants to know about the bridge, though most people are polite enough not to ask, which in the interior passes for kindness.

It was the Alder Crossing, eleven years ago this spring. A trestle over the Alder River gorge, two hundred and ten feet of timber and iron that I had inspected personally, twice, and passed both times. I want to be clear about that, because a man can live with a great many things but not with vagueness. I checked it. I walked every bent, sounded every pile, ran my hand along the chords looking for the soft spots rot leaves in timber. I found what I was told to find, which is to say I found what the schedule allowed me to find, because the ore contract was running and the general manager had made it plain — not in words, managers never use words — that a bridge closure would cost more than a bridge failure was likely to.

That's the arithmetic. Every railroad man knows it. You multiply the tonnage by the days and divide by the odds, and somewhere in that equation the lives of six men become a rounding error. I did the multiplication myself. I was good at figures. That's the part that keeps a man walking — not that he built the rotten thing, but that he signed the paper saying it would hold, and the paper was true right up until 6:14 on a Tuesday morning when it wasn't.

They were crossing light, deadheading back from the night shift, engine and caboose and six men whose names I still keep in the front of my notebook, before the blank pages, where I have to see them every time I open it. Harlan Voss. The Deel brothers, Petey and Sam. Old Marek, who'd taught me sounding timbers with the flat of a hammer. A boy called Finch who was nineteen and owed me a dollar from cards. And Tom Ellery, my fireman, who had a wife in the valley town and a laugh you could hear over a working engine.

The third bent from the north abutment had rot in it that my hammer should have sung against and didn't, or did and I didn't hear, and either way the difference between those two things is the whole of my life since. The span went down with the engine into the gorge, and the river took the noise, and by the time I got down the bank there was nothing left to do that hands are for. I held Finch's hand while he decided whether to stay or go, and he went, and his hand went slack around mine like a tool set down.

Afterward there was an inquiry, and the inquiry found that the bridge had failed, which was true, and that inspection procedures had been followed, which was also true, and those two truths together made a kind of lie that everyone agreed to carry. I was transferred. Then I volunteered for everything nobody wanted — the closed lines, the abandoned spurs, the surveys into country where the only thing to inspect was emptiness. The company thought I was devoted. Let them. A man who keeps moving can't be asked to stand in front of a room and say what he knows.

* * *

The waystation at Mile 41 had once been a water stop and was now a roofless suggestion of one, but the telegraph shed still stood, and that was where I found the letter waiting, forwarded through three offices like a rumor. Company stationery. The concession language alone told me it was trouble: *all unworked mineral claims within the Sable headwater district shall lapse upon expiry of the concession unless surveyed and certified prior thereto.*

In plain speech: the company had until the first of June to prove up its claims in the upper Sable country, or the government took the ground back. And the company wanted a man to walk the closed lines and the old survey routes and certify what was there before the clock ran out.

They wanted me specifically. My name was in the body of the letter, spelled correctly, which from that head office was practically a love letter. *Mr. Kavanagh's familiarity with the decommissioned lines renders him uniquely suited.* Uniquely suited. Eleven years of volunteering for emptiness will do that — make you uniquely suited for it.

I sat on the telegraph shed's one surviving stool and read it three times. The fee was generous. Generous enough that I noticed, and noticing is a profession in itself; a man who has done arithmetic for railroads knows when the figures are being friendly. They weren't paying for my legs. They were paying for my signature. Somewhere in the upper Sable there was ground the company needed certified worthless — or certified anything, really, so long as the certification came from a name with standing — and they intended to buy that name cheap before the concession lapsed and the whole question died.

A wiser man would have burned the letter.

I read it a fourth time instead, and what I felt wasn't greed and wasn't quite suspicion. It was the old pull, the one that had kept me walking for eleven years: the promise of a route, a reason, a line to follow upstream into the one part of the map I'd never had cause to enter. The headwaters of the Sable. Folded green country. Unmeasured.

I got out my notebook to accept, and my thumb fell across the front page, the names. Harlan Voss. The Deel brothers. Marek. Finch. Tom Ellery. Six men who had stayed still, in the end, forever, so that I could develop this habit of motion.

*Certification required within the district prior to June the first,* the letter said. There was a date in it, and dates are how companies count down, and I have learned — I have paid tuition, God knows, to learn — that when a company starts counting down toward a piece of empty country, the country isn't empty.

I signed the acceptance by lantern light, my handwriting steady, which surprised me. Then I spread my maps on the shed floor and weighted the corners with stones, and looked at the upper Sable, and saw what I should have seen sooner: a gap. Not blank — mapped, but thinly, lazily, the contours drawn by some office man working from a ridge traverse twenty years old. The headwater basin itself was guesswork dressed as cartography.

And penciled faintly along the margin, in a clerk's hand, almost apologetic: *Gov't survey party assigned this district last autumn. No returns received.*

No returns received. Four words that meant a team had gone up that river with instruments and authority, and nothing had come back down. Not the men, not the instruments, not so much as a report. And the company's answer to that silence was to hire a railroad man to walk in and certify the ground before anyone asked louder questions.

I sat back on the stool. Outside, the wind moved through the lodgepoles with that sound like a train passing on a far grade, the sound that isn't a train and never will be again.

Every instinct I'd spent eleven years building told me to fold the maps, pocket the fee advance, and put the letter in the stove. Keep moving. There was always another spur, another dead line, another report nobody would read.

Instead I found myself tracing the river upward with one finger, station by imagined station, into the gap.

Somebody had gone up there and stopped. And I have never in my life been able to pass a place where something stopped without needing to know what made it stop.
