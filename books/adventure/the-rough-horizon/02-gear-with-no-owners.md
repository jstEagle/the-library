## 2. Gear With No Owners

There is a particular kind of silence that comes off a waystation nobody uses anymore, and it isn't the silence of emptiness. It's the silence of a room where someone has just stopped talking. I'd been walking the Sable Fork spur for two days when I came up on Kettleman's Way — that's what the old crews called it, though no map ever printed it — and before I even saw the buildings I felt that silence sitting on my chest like a wet coat.

The waystation itself was what I expected: a plank platform gone silver with weather, a water tower leaning at an angle that would have given me nightmares in my working years, a tool shed with its door swinging in small, patient arcs. What I didn't expect was smoke.

Not much of it. A thin gray thread rising from behind the shed, the kind of smoke a single camp stove makes when somebody's keeping a fire just barely alive. I stood there on the ties and did the arithmetic every man who works alone learns to do: one thread of smoke means one person, maybe two. Two people I could handle. Three meant I turned around and walked back down the line and let the closed lines stay closed.

I went forward anyway. That's the thing about me you should understand right away, if you're going to read this account at all: I always go forward. It has never once been courage. It's more like a man walking downhill on ice — stopping is the dangerous part.

* * *

The camp was pitched in the lee of the shed, out of the wind, which told me whoever set it up knew country. A wall tent, government pattern, guy-lines dressed properly, corners staked square. Beside it, on a folding table, sat a transit on its tripod, leveled, the telescope capped. Next to the transit, a theodolite case, latched. Next to that, a kettle on a spirit stove, half-full of tea.

I want you to sit with that kettle for a moment, because I have been sitting with it for months now and I still haven't gotten all the way to the bottom of it.

The tea was cold. Not day-old cold, not rainwater-in-a-forgotten-cup cold. Cold the way tea gets when somebody pours it, takes two sips, sets it down, and walks away — maybe twenty minutes, maybe half a day, depending on the weather. The surface had a skin on it. There was no rain in the last week; I'd have walked through it. So the skin was time, not weather.

I looked at the transit again. Leveled — truly leveled, bubble centered in both axes, tripod feet seated on flat stones someone had placed deliberately. You don't leave a transit set up overnight unless you're coming back within the hour. An instrument like that costs more than a section hand makes in a year, and every surveyor I ever met treated his transit the way a priest treats his chalice. They don't leave them standing in the open. They especially don't leave them standing in the open in bear country in October.

I circled the camp twice, slow, reading the ground the way I'd been taught to read rail bed — looking for the story underneath the surface. Here's what the ground said:

Nothing.

No tracks leaving. None arriving, either, which was its own puzzle, but the ground around a camp gets churned by ordinary living, so absence of arrival tracks I could explain. Absence of departure was different. Around the tent, around the table, around the fire ring, the duff and needle-cover held the soft impressions of boots going about the business of a camp — and then, in every direction, they simply stopped. As if four or five men had stood in the middle of their own camp and been lifted off the earth like dust motes.

I found the latrine trench fifty yards out, properly dug, properly screened. Fresh use. Whatever happened here, it happened to men whose bodies were still obeying ordinary schedules right up until the moment they weren't.

I stood in the center of the camp and turned a slow circle, and the hair on my arms stood up under my coat, and I'll tell you honestly why. It wasn't fear of wolves or weather or men. It was that the camp looked exactly like the drawings I used to make in my head after the bridge went down — the ones where I replayed the morning and tried to find the version where everybody stepped back off the span ten seconds earlier. A scene interrupted. A sentence cut off mid-word. My whole life since the bridge has been built on the principle that if you never stop moving, nothing can be interrupted. And here was interruption itself, sitting politely in the wilderness with its tea going cold.

* * *

The clerk came out of the waystation while I was crouched over the fire ring, and I will grant him this: he moved quiet for a man in town boots.

"Morning," he said. "Fine morning."

It was not a fine morning. It was the color of dishwater and smelled like snow deciding. But I got up and shook the offered hand anyway, because he had the look of a man whose hand you shake now or hear about later.

"Kavanagh," I said. "Railway contract. Surveying the closed spurs before the concession lapses." I held up my permit book, because company men respect paper the way other men respect scripture.

"Merle Dott," he said. "Company agent, acting capacity." He said *acting capacity* the way some men say *acting alone*, which is to say, in a tone daring you to ask. He was a narrow man, gray as the morning, with a vest buttoned over a shirt that had been clean once that week. His eyes went to the camp behind me, then away from it, in the manner of a man declining to look at something he'd already looked at plenty.

"You know anything about this outfit?" I asked him.

"Government survey team," he said promptly. Too promptly. "Mineral assessment, upper Sable drainage. Five men, I believe. Six." He frowned slightly, as if counting were a moral question. "They came through the waystation heading upstream three weeks ago. Very professional. Very quiet."

"And?"

"And nothing, Mr. Kavanagh. Teams go into the field. Teams come out of the field. The intervals between are their own affair."

"Their instruments are still set up."

Dott looked at me with mild, reasonable eyes. "Are they? I hadn't noticed."

"You'd have to be blind not to notice. There's a transit standing twenty feet from your woodpile."

"I keep my attention on railway matters," he said. "Track, ballast, culverts. The things I'm paid to attend to." He produced a tin of mints from his vest and offered me one, and when I declined, took one himself with great care, as though the gesture were the entire purpose of the mints. "Now. Your contract. Closed-line survey, southern division, correct?"

"That's correct."

"Then your work lies south of here. Downstream." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind that comes in a box with instructions. "The upper Sable is outside your concession boundaries. Quite outside. In fact, I'd go so far as to say the upper Sable is outside most people's interest entirely. Poor country. No ore worth the name — the early assessments were very clear on that. Nothing up there but rock and river."

"Then it shouldn't trouble anyone that I walk up it."

"It wouldn't trouble me at all," Dott agreed pleasantly. "Trouble is such a strong word. I'd only note that the season's turning, that a man alone in gorge country in late October is a gamble against long odds, and that the company would feel responsible — genuinely responsible — if anything happened to a contracted surveyor on our former rights-of-way." He took another mint. "We've had enough misfortune on these lines. I'm sure you'd agree. Enough misfortune."

He said it lightly. He said it the way you'd mention weather. But there was a second sentence inside that sentence, the way there's a wire inside a cable, and both of us heard it hum.

I looked past him at the camp. At the transit standing patient as a gravestone. At the kettle.

"Someone should cover that instrument," I said.

"No doubt," said Merle Dott. "Someone should."

* * *

He watched me pack, which I'd expected, and he helped, which I hadn't. Handed me my haversack straps, pointed out where I'd dropped a peg hammer, made small talk about tie rot and frost heave. A courteous man. A helpful man. By the time I had my kit together I understood that the courtesy and the help were both forms of surveillance, and that he would stand on that platform watching until I was a speck going south down the old grade, and that he would then write a report containing the word *cooperative*.

So I went south. For about a mile.

Here's the thing about a map of closed lines: it's a map of everything the company decided wasn't worth guarding anymore. Every abandoned spur, every pulled switch, every trestle left to rot — those are the veins of the country the company has stopped watching. And a man who has spent eleven years walking those veins knows something a land agent in town boots doesn't: that the country doesn't care about directions. South of Kettleman's Way, the old Sable Fork grade swung west through a cut in the shoulder of the valley, and from the high side of that cut, a man with a surveyor's eye could see the upper river — the whole upper drainage, laid out like a diagram.

Which is how I came to be lying in the dead bracken above the cut at dusk, watching a lantern move along the far bank of the Sable, three miles upstream of the waystation, in country Dott had assured me held nothing but rock and river.

The lantern moved slowly. Methodically. It paused at intervals, the way a man pauses when he's searching — quartering the ground, checking the banks, checking the water. It was looking for something. Or someone. And it was looking upstream, toward the headwaters, toward the source country, which meant whoever held the lantern believed the missing team had gone further in.

Or it meant whoever held the lantern wanted anyone else who came looking to believe that.

I lay in the bracken until full dark, until my hands were cold enough that I had to flex them inside my gloves, and I thought about the bridge. Eleven years, and I still thought about it every day, but usually the way you think about a scar — a touch, a flinch, done. This was different. This was the old arithmetic unrolling itself in my head, the same cold ledger Crane-era accounting had run on that span: *cost of inspection versus cost of failure, probability of traffic versus probability of collapse.* Somebody had run that sum on five men — six, Dott couldn't decide — and had decided the tea could go cold.

I got up out of the bracken and turned north, upstream, off my own map, off my own contract, into the gap in the lines.

My permit covered none of it. My better judgment covered less. But I'd spent eleven years learning that the only thing worse than walking toward a thing you dread is standing still while it walks toward everyone else, and besides — there was a transit standing in the rain-country with nobody to cover it, and somewhere upstream there were men who had poured tea and never come back for it.

Somebody had to finish the sentence.
