## 3. Ilsa Refuses the File

The government office in Corben had a smell I will never forget: dust, ink, and the particular staleness of decisions already made. The clerk who received me — a small man named Petch, with the eyes of someone who had learned early that looking directly at things was bad for a career — took my letter, read it, and set it face-down on his desk as though it might bite.

"Miss Voss," he said, "the file has been closed."

"I can see that. It's stamped."

"Then you understand—"

"What I understand," I said, "is that my father has been missing for nineteen days, and your office managed to close his file in eleven. That is either remarkable efficiency or remarkable arithmetic, and I'd like to know which."

Petch looked at the stamp as if hoping it would speak for him. It said CLOSED in letters so red and so final they might have been written in something other than ink. He slid the file into a drawer instead of answering, which was its own kind of answer.

"The survey team's contract concluded," he offered. "All obligations discharged."

"My father is an obligation?"

"I didn't say that."

"You filed it."

He produced a handkerchief, mopped nothing from his forehead, and told me — kindly, in the tone one uses for children and the bereaved — that men went missing in the interior all the time, that the Sable country was dangerous, that grief made people imagine patterns. Then he gave me a copy of the closure notice, free of charge, and showed me to the door with the gentle firmness of a man ushering a cat out of a pantry.

I stood on the steps of the government office holding a piece of paper that declared my father legally finished, and I thought: they have not even looked for him. Nineteen days. Not one search party, not one notice posted north of Corben, not one question asked at the waystation where his team last resupplied. The paper in my hands wasn't a conclusion. It was a door, shut quietly, from the inside.

So I did what any reasonable daughter would do. I bought passage on the supply wagon to the end of the line, and then I walked the rest.

* * *

The waystation at Miller's Bend was less a building than an argument between a building and the weather. The roof had lost most of its arguments. Inside, it smelled of creosote and old coffee, and there was exactly one other person in it, sitting at the corner table with maps spread out before him like a card game he was losing alone.

I knew him by reputation before I knew him by name. Oscar Kavanagh. The railway's ghost — the engineer who walked dead lines, checking rails no train would ever ride. Men at the freight yards talked about him the way sailors talk about weather: with respect, and from a safe distance. They also talked about a bridge, once, and stopped talking when they saw each other listening.

He looked up when I came in. Grey eyes, a face that had been handsome once and had since decided against it. His hands were stained with pencil graphite, and he had the particular stillness of a man who is never actually resting.

"You're Kavanagh," I said.

"And you're dripping on my map."

It had started raining two miles back. I hadn't noticed. I hung my coat on a nail that seemed optimistic about its purpose and put my father's closure notice on the table between us, smoothing it flat.

"I need the Sable headwaters," I said. "You've surveyed every closed line in this district. You know the gorge country better than anyone the company still employs."

"I don't employ myself."

"Then you know it for free, which is better." I sat down across from him without being asked. "My father is Elias Voss. Government surveyor. His team went in six weeks ago to run levels up the Sable toward the source country. Nineteen days ago they stopped reporting. Yesterday the ministry closed his file without conducting a search. I intend to go up that river and find out why, and I am asking you to take me."

Kavanagh looked at the notice for a long moment. Then he looked at me, and what was in his face wasn't sympathy. It was recognition — the specific, unwelcome kind, like catching your own reflection in a window you'd thought was dark.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard my offer."

"There isn't an offer. That river above the falls is a gorge with opinions. The trail's washed out in three places. There's no road, no telegraph, and no reason—" he tapped the notice "—for a government that's closed the file to want anything found. Going up there alone is foolish. Taking a civilian is criminal."

"I'm not a civilian. I'm a surveyor's daughter. I've been reading a vernier since I was nine years old. I can reduce a traverse, run a level loop, and tell you within a chain how far we've come by the sound of the water. Your maps are missing their upper reaches because nobody who knows instruments will go. I'm offering you the only thing the company can't buy."

"Which is?"

"A reason to go that isn't money."

That landed somewhere it clearly wasn't welcome. His jaw tightened, and he began gathering his maps with the deliberate care of a man putting distance between himself and a conversation.

"Miss Voss," he said, "I walk these lines because they're finished. Nothing up a river is finished. Everything up a river is a question, and questions are how bridges fall."

"Bridges fall," I said, "because men sign off on them without looking."

The silence after that was total. Even the rain seemed to lean in. I hadn't known about the bridge when I said it — I'd only known the shape of the flinch, the same flinch I'd seen on the faces of officials all week, the flinch of men who had signed something. But I saw immediately that I'd cut deeper than I aimed, and part of me — the part I liked least — noted the location of the wound for future use.

"That's not fair," he said quietly.

"No," I admitted. "It's not. But neither is a closed file, Mr. Kavanagh, and I have spent eleven days being polite to men who keep telling me my father is a settled matter. I'm done being fair. Fair got me a stamp."

He sat back down. Not because I'd persuaded him — I could see that — but because some other arithmetic was running behind his eyes, some ledger I couldn't read yet.

"Why does the ministry close a file in eleven days?" he asked. Not to me, quite. To the table.

"That's what I intend to ask them. Loudly. In writing. With witnesses."

"And if the answer is that there's nothing to find? If your father walked off the job, or—"

"Then I'll have walked a river to learn my father was a coward, and I'll carry that home myself. But he isn't." I pulled the folded page from my breast pocket — his last letter, sent from the second camp, four lines in his cramped hand. *Levels don't match the published map. Off by forty feet and widening. Will re-run the loop from the source. E.V.* "Forty feet, Mr. Kavanagh. My father once rejected a township plat over six inches. Forty feet means either the map is wrong or the ground is lying, and my father did not believe ground could lie. Somebody else disagrees strongly enough to lose his file in a drawer."

Kavanagh read the letter twice. When he looked up, something had shifted — not softened; shifted, the way ballast shifts before it decides whether to hold.

"If I take you up that river," he said, "you do what I say when I say it. You turn back when I turn back. And if I tell you we're leaving, we leave, whether you've found your answer or not."

"Agreed."

"And you cook. I survey for a living. I eat like it's a punishment I've earned."

"Agreed," I said again, and meant it less, and he probably knew.

"One more condition." He folded my father's letter and handed it back with a strange gentleness, as though returning something recovered from a fire. "You don't ask me about the bridge."

"I haven't asked you about the bridge."

"You were about to. Everyone's about to, right up until they're not." He began rolling his maps, quick and precise. "We leave at first light. The patrol boat runs the lower Sable on Thursdays — company men, checking traplines they don't own. We stay ahead of it, and we stay off the water where the gorge narrows. If anyone official asks, you're hiring me to survey a fence line."

"A fence line," I repeated. "Up a river with no fences."

"People believe fences," he said. "They want to. It saves them from believing in rivers."

* * *

We spent the evening preparing in a silence that kept almost becoming conversation and then thinking better of it. He checked instruments I recognized — a level older than both of us, a compass with a hairline crack in the glass that he'd compensated for with a penciled correction taped beside it. Careful work. Grieving work, my father would have called it: precision as a way of holding the world still.

I cleaned my father's spare theodolite by lamplight, and I caught Kavanagh watching my hands more than once. Not rudely. The way a man watches a language he used to speak and thought he'd forgotten.

"Your father taught you?" he asked finally.

"Every summer from the time I was seven. He said a woman should know how to find true north because everyone else would spend her whole life trying to sell her a false one." I tightened the last screw. "He also taught me that a reading you can't repeat isn't a reading. It's a wish."

Kavanagh huffed — nearly a laugh, strangled at birth. "He sounds like a man I'd have argued with happily."

"He argues with everyone. It's how he loves you. If he ever agrees with you, check your figures."

Something moved through his face then — fast, quickly buried — and I understood that I'd just described a way of being in the world that he recognized from the inside. A man who keeps moving because stopping means counting what stopped with him. I had grown up with that man. I knew the architecture of the silence around a loss, how it gets load-bearing.

And I mistrusted it instantly, in him and in myself. Two griefs recognizing each other is not the same as two people doing so. Sometimes it's just the echo mistaking itself for a voice.

"First light," he said, and rolled into his bedroll facing the wall, conversation concluded by decree.

I lay awake listening to the rain finish its argument with the roof, and near midnight I heard him speak — low, half-asleep, or pretending to be. Just two words, addressed to no one in the room:

"Not again."

I didn't ask. I lay in the dark and memorized the sound of it, because I suspected that whatever he was refusing to do again was exactly the thing we were walking into — and that when it came, I would need to know whether the man beside me would stand, or run, or drown trying to do both.

First light came grey and cold. He was up before it, packing with the economy of a man who had left places often enough to be good at it. At the door he paused, looked back at me, and said the only honest thing either of us would say for days:

"For the record, Miss Voss — I think this is a mistake."

"Noted for the record," I said, and stepped out into the rain, upstream.
