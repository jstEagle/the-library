## 4. Upstream

The Sable River does not run so much as it argues. It argues with the rock, with the fallen timber, with anything fool enough to stand in its way, and by the second morning of pushing upstream I had begun to feel like one more grievance on a long list.

Ilsa Voss walked ahead of me along the gorge rim, pack straps creaking, her father's brass compass open in her palm like something she was afraid to look at too directly.

"You said this would be a trail," she called back.

"I said there'd be game trails."

"Game trails imply game. We've seen two squirrels and they were both unimpressed with us."

"That's honest country," I said. "It doesn't flatter you."

She snorted, but she kept walking, which by then I understood was her whole method: complain, then continue. Her father had raised her in survey camps, and it showed in how she moved — always checking her footing twice, never once looking at the view. People who grow up around instruments learn that scenery is what you get when the work is done, and the work is never done.

We had left the waystation at first light the day before, following the river into country where the maps went thin and honest about their ignorance. The official sheets showed the upper Sable as a blue line with confident little elevations printed beside it — 3,410 feet, 3,620, 3,780 — numbers some draftsman in a warm office had copied from an earlier survey and nobody had bothered to check since. The government team had checked. That was rather the point.

Every few miles we found their marks. A blaze on a spruce with the station number chiselled into the bark. A cairn of flat stones on a bench above the water. Once, wedged under a boulder, a wooden stake with a strip of zinc nailed to it, figures punched through in the neat, economical hand of a man who hated wasting strokes. Station 14. Station 15. The team had come up this same gorge, logging as they went, and each mark was a sentence in a conversation they'd been having with the land.

The trouble was that the conversation had stopped making sense.

* * *

By noon of the second day we reached Station 17, and Ilsa sat down on the cairn and did not get up for a long while.

She had brought her father's field book — the duplicate he'd mailed to her before the expedition went quiet, the one the government office had declared "closed" along with everything else. She read from it now, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"Elevation at 17," she said. "Three thousand nine hundred and forty feet."

"So?"

"The map says thirty-six twenty." She looked up at me. "That's not a rounding error, Kavanagh. That's three hundred feet. On a river. Rivers don't gain three hundred feet because a draftsman had a bad week."

"Could be his instrument was off."

"It's the same instrument I'm holding." She turned the compass over in her hands. "He calibrated it the morning he mailed me this book. He wrote the calibration in the front page. He always did. He used to say an instrument without a record of its own errors is just a very expensive opinion."

There was a family in that sentence, whole and intact, and I looked away up the gorge so she wouldn't see me notice.

We climbed on. At Station 18 the discrepancy was four hundred feet. At 19, logged on a bench of grey shale above a set of rapids, the figure didn't match the map at all — not approximately, not in spirit. And beside the elevation, in the margin, her father had written a single word in pencil, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Re-surveyed.*

Not *checked*. Not *verified*. Re-surveyed, as if the first answer had offended him and he'd gone back and demanded a better one from the rock itself.

"He only re-surveys when something frightens him," Ilsa said quietly. "He told me once. Most men re-measure because they doubt the instrument. He re-measures because he doubts himself, and he can't stand it."

"And what did he find?"

"I don't know. The rest is torn out." She closed the book with a gentleness that told me exactly how much restraint that cost her. "Someone tore them out carefully. Pages eleven through fourteen. You can feel the knife marks."

We stood there on the shale bench with the river roaring below us, and I thought about what kind of man tears pages out of a government field book with a knife instead of burning the whole thing. A careful man. A man who wanted the record dead but the evidence alive somewhere, in case.

Or a frightened man who hadn't yet decided which side of the truth he stood on.

"We should make camp," I said. "Light'll go in two hours, and this gorge turns into a well after dark."

Ilsa didn't move. She was staring downstream, back the way we'd come, her head tilted like a bird's.

"Do you hear that?"

I listened. Wind in the spruce. Water. My own knees, announcing themselves.

Then, faint and wrong, cutting across the river's argument: an engine. Not ours. Ours was a mule named Perpetua who was currently three days behind us eating someone's fence line. This was a motor, steady and purposeful, thudding up the valley from below.

We scrambled to the rim and looked down. Far beneath us, a speck on the brown water, a company patrol boat ground its way upstream against the current — searchlight housing empty in the daylight, two men visible at the rail, one of them sweeping the far bank with glasses.

Searching. Upstream. Toward us.

"No," Ilsa breathed.

"They're coming up the river," I said. "Same as us."

"No — look where they're *looking*." She grabbed my sleeve and pointed. The man with the glasses wasn't scanning the banks ahead of the boat. He was quartering the slopes behind it, methodical, thorough, and entirely aimed at the country they'd already passed. "They're searching downstream. They're going upstream and searching *backwards*. Nobody does that unless—"

Unless somebody had told them the missing men went downriver. Unless somebody wanted the search seen to be thorough and wanted it to fail.

"Crane," I said.

"You've met him one time."

"One time was educational."

We flattened ourselves behind a windthrown spruce and let the boat pass. It took eleven minutes. I counted, because counting is what I do instead of panicking; it's cheaper and it keeps the hands busy. Eleven minutes of watching a company boat grind past below us, close enough that I could see the coffee tin on the rail and the bored set of the helmsman's shoulders, and neither of us moved, and the river covered whatever small sounds we made, as if it had decided — temporarily, provisionally, for reasons of its own — to take our side.

When the engine faded Ilsa let out a breath she'd apparently been holding since Tuesday.

"They're searching the wrong direction," she said. "On purpose. Which means whoever sent them knows exactly where Papa isn't." Her voice cracked on the last word and she pressed on through it, jaw set. "Which means somebody knows where he *is*."

"Or knew where he'd be found," I said, "and arranged for him not to be."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she'd been refusing to since the waystation — and I saw her arrive at the same arithmetic I'd been carrying for two days. That her father was probably dead. That if he wasn't dead, it was because being found alive was worse for somebody than being found otherwise. That the polite clerk with the clean fingernails and the file marked CLOSED had known things a clerk shouldn't know.

"Gorge narrows ahead," I said, because somebody had to say something practical or we'd both stand there until the stars came out. "Half a mile on there's a side cut where the old tie-drivers camped. We'll overnight there."

"How do you know these cuts?"

"Because I surveyed this line before it was a closed line." I shouldered my pack. "Everything in this valley has been measured once already, Miss Voss. The question is whether anyone wrote down the truth."

* * *

The tie-drivers' cut was a shelf of level ground forty feet above the water, sheltered by an overhang of granite, littered with the rusted ghosts of their trade — a broken auger, half a keg hoop, the stub of a skidway rotting into the moss. Men had slept here sixty years ago, blasting roadbed for a railway that ran out of money before it ran out of mountain, and their abandoned works had a comfort to them that I've never fully explained to myself. Ruined ambitions make good campsites. They've already done their grieving.

I got a fire going small and smokeless in a rock hollow, and Ilsa spread her father's field book open between us, weighting the corners with stones.

"Read me the stations again," I said. "All of them. In order."

She did. Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen — and I made her stop at nineteen.

"Say the elevation again."

"Four thousand one hundred and ten."

"Now say what the map says."

"Thirty-seven eighty."

I took out my own notebook, the one I keep in an oilskin wrap against my chest, and drew fast: the river's course as we'd climbed it, the stations spaced along it, the mapped elevations on one side and the surveyed ones on the other. Two lines of figures. Then I connected them.

The mapped profile rose smooth and gentle, a river climbing politely toward its source. The surveyed profile rose in a series of steps — long flats, then sudden jumps, hundreds of feet at a time, like a staircase built by something enormous and unhurried.

"That's not error," I said. "Error wanders. This climbs."

"Climbs toward what?"

"A river doesn't climb in stairs, Ilsa. Water doesn't do that." I tapped the page. "But a fault does. Or a buried ridge. Or—" I stopped, because the next word was *ore body*, and saying it aloud in a gorge where a patrol boat had passed an hour earlier felt like shouting a debt collection notice into a church.

Ilsa had gone very still. She was better at arithmetic than she let on, or worse at pretending she wasn't.

"The mineral claims," she said slowly. "The ones the company filed. They're based on the old elevations, aren't they? On the map. If the real ground sits three hundred, four hundred feet higher than the paper says, then every claim boundary in this valley is drawn wrong. Everything the company says it owns—"

"—is sitting on top of something it sold twice," I finished. "Maybe."

"Maybe," she agreed, but her eyes said *certainly*, and mine probably answered.

We sat with that while the fire ticked and the river argued on below. Somewhere upslope a branch snapped — deer, most likely, or the wind, or a man with a rifle and a company wage slip. I put another stick on the fire and told myself it was the wind.

"There's one more thing," Ilsa said. She turned to the last legible page, the one before the knife-work began, and held it toward the light. Beneath Station 19's figures, in her father's hand, half the ink smudged by weather:

*Elevations confirmed ×3. Ground truth contradicts all prior surveys. If anything happens, the proof is at the*

And there it stopped. Torn. The sentence amputated mid-word, the rest of it living or dying in the pages somebody had carved out with a blade.

"The proof is at the what," Ilsa whispered. "At the *what*, Papa?"

I looked up the gorge, where the walls leaned together and the last of the light was draining out of the stone like water out of a cup. Above Station 19 there were no more marks. No blazes, no cairns, no zinc tags. Whatever came next, the survey team had stopped writing it down for people like us to find.

Above us, faint but unmistakable, a sound rolled down the valley that had nothing to do with water: a single shot, flat and deliberate, followed by a long silence in which the river suddenly seemed very loud.

Ilsa's hand found my arm in the dark.

"That wasn't a hunter," she said. "Hunters shoot twice."
