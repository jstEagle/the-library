## 5. The Second Camp

My father taught me to read a camp the way other fathers taught their daughters to read faces. A camp, he said, tells you what a man intends. Where he pitches his fly tells you the weather he expects. Where he sets his boots tells you how fast he means to leave. Where he puts his instruments tells you whether he trusts the night.

So when we came around the bend of the river at mid-morning and saw the second camp sitting in its clearing like a man interrupted mid-sentence, I knew before Oscar said a word that something was wrong with it. Not ruined — wrong. There is a difference, and the difference had my stomach turning before I'd taken ten steps.

"Stations are logged right up to here," Oscar said behind me. He'd been walking with his eyes down all morning, reading the blazes on the trees the way I read tide tables, each one cut neat and numbered in my father's hand. Station forty-one. Forty-two. The numbers climbing like rungs on a ladder that simply stopped.

The camp stood on a gravel bench above the water, sheltered by a stand of spruce that kept off the worst of the wind. Two tents. A fire ring, cold for days by the look of it. A canvas mess table with a tin plate still on it, and on the plate, a fork laid across half a bannock gone grey with mould.

Nobody walks away from a bannock. That's not sentiment; that's arithmetic. Flour out here costs more per pound than silver.

Oscar circled the clearing slowly, hands in his pockets, touching nothing. He had a way of moving through a place like a man wading into cold water — testing everything twice before committing his weight. I watched him stop at the instrument line, where the theodolite case sat open on its tripod, and I watched his face do something complicated.

"Theodolite's still levelled," he said. "Bubble's centred. Somebody left this set up on a station."

"People pack in a hurry."

"Not past levelling." He crouched, squinting along the telescope without touching it. "You centre a bubble because it takes thirty seconds and you're trained to hate leaving it wrong. This isn't a hurried camp, Miss Voss. It's a *tidy* one. Tidy except—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We were both looking at the same absence: the tent my father used as an office, its flap hanging open, the folding table inside swept bare. No field books. No log sheets. No pencil stubs, no spare nibs, no blotting paper. Every scrap of paper in a camp that had housed four men for two weeks, gone.

But the compasses were there. The watches were there. There was a rifle racked against a tree with dust on the stock, and a cash box under the mess table that Oscar flipped open with one finger to reveal a roll of banknotes fat enough to make a honest man blush.

"Ransacked," I said, "but not robbed."

"Robbed by somebody who wanted paper and nothing else." Oscar straightened up. He looked at the treeline the way a man looks at a room full of people after being told one of them is lying. "Miss Voss, I've been robbed on the line. They take the money, the gun, the boots if they're better than yours. Nobody robs a survey camp of its notebooks and leaves the payroll."

*Unless they weren't robbers.* I didn't say it. Saying it made it a fact, and facts have a way of becoming permanent once spoken aloud between two people.

I went through my father's tent instead, because grief is a kind of work and I would rather do it with my hands. His bedroll was there, rolled tight — he never let anyone else touch it, not even me, a habit from his army years that I'd stopped teasing him about somewhere in my teens. His spare shirt. His tin of tobacco, nearly full. And underneath the cot, wedged where a man might shove something in a hurry or might hide something with care —

Nothing. Dust, and the pressed-flat ghost of grass.

I sat back on my heels and let myself feel it properly for three seconds, which was two seconds more than I could afford. Papa was methodical. Papa was so methodical that when Mother died he produced, within the week, a written inventory of her things for the solicitor, because order was how he loved people. If he had left this camp, even running, even frightened, there would be a system to it. A note sewn somewhere. A mark on a tree. My father communicated in marginalia the way other men communicated in speech.

The camp had no marginalia. Which meant either he hadn't left it —

No. I put that thought down like a hot iron.

* * *

We worked the clearing until the light went long and gold. Oscar walked the perimeter twice, then a third time, counting. "Four bedrolls," he reported. "Four plates. But only three sets of tracks going down to the water, and those are days old, going upstream. Nothing coming back."

"And nothing leaving toward the trail."

"Nothing leaving anywhere." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I keep telling myself the river ate the rest. Gravel bars shift. But gravel doesn't eat four men and spit out their laundry."

It was while he was doing that — standing at the edge of the water, arguing quietly with the geography — that I opened the theodolite case.

Not to steal it. To check it. Because if the company wanted notebooks, and the notebooks were gone, then whatever my father thought worth protecting would be somewhere the company wouldn't think to want. And a theodolite case is the one object in a survey camp that no thief touches, because to a thief it's just expensive junk, and to a stranger it's too heavy to bother with.

The fittings were brass, worn bright at the catches from twenty years of my father's thumbs. Inside, the instrument sat in its wool cradle, lens caps on, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. All correct. All exactly as it should be.

Except the oilcloth was folded wrong.

I know this the way I know my own hands. My father folded cloth in thirds, always, edges in, because he claimed loose edges caught and tore and a torn wrap was a dead instrument in six months. The oilcloth lay folded in halves, tucked over the objective end only, leaving the standards exposed — sloppy, careless, *not him*. Someone had opened this case and repacked it in a hurry, and that someone hadn't been my father, because my father would sooner sleep in the rain than fold cloth badly.

Which meant the real packing was underneath.

I lifted the instrument out with both hands. Beneath it, glued flat against the floor of the case beneath a false layer of felt — and I recognized the felt, God help me, it was cut from the lining of his old hatbox, the brown one, the one he'd sworn he'd thrown out — was a sheet of paper, folded to the size of a playing card.

My hands shook. I hated them for it.

I carried the page to the fire ring and knelt where the light was best, and Oscar came and crouched beside me without a word, close enough that I could hear him breathing, which was either an intrusion or a kindness and I decided, for the moment, to let it be a kindness.

The page was a field record. My father's hand, small and exact, the figures ruled in columns. An elevation reading. A bearing. A date eleven days ago. And below the columns, crammed into the margin in the same tidy script, as though he couldn't help himself, as though even hiding for his life he could not write a number without annotating it:

*Elev. disagrees with Company base map by 340 ft. Disagreement increases upstream. They will call it error. It is not error.*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the page behind it:

*THE SOURCE IS THE PROOF.*

I read it three times. The words didn't get any more sensible. Three hundred and forty feet. I turned the idea over and it refused every angle. A survey discrepancy of a few inches was sloppiness. A few feet was scandal. Three hundred and forty feet wasn't a mistake — it was a different mountain. Somewhere between the official maps and my father's instruments, the valley itself had been redrawn, and someone had decided the truth of it was worth more than four lives.

"They took the notebooks," I heard myself say. My voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone standing downstream. "They took every book, every sheet. But he hid this one. He hid it *before*, Oscar. Look at the glue. Look at the felt. This was planned. He packed this case knowing —"

"Knowing someone was coming for the records." Oscar took the page from me gently, held it to the light. "Knowing they'd come, and knowing they'd miss this. He didn't disappear, Miss Voss. He *prepared* to disappear. Or—" His jaw worked. "Or somebody prepared it for him."

"The source is the proof." I said it aloud to hear it in the air. "The headwaters. He went up. Station forty-two is the last blaze, and the last blaze points upstream, and the source is the proof, and the proof is alive because the proof can't be burned."

Oscar looked at me then, and I saw him arrive at the same arithmetic I'd already done, the ugly sum of it: a man who hides his evidence carefully is a man who expects to be separated from himself. You don't plan for your own capture unless capture is on the schedule.

"He's alive," I said. It came out fierce, and I let it. "A dead man doesn't hide documents. A dead man doesn't need the truth protected. Papa went up the river because up the river was the only direction the company's arithmetic didn't cover, and he's up there now, and he has been for eleven days, and every hour we sit here admiring his handwriting is an hour Crane's people have on us."

"Crane's people already came through here." Oscar nodded at the cold fire ring, the swept table, the tidy theft of it. "This wasn't done by the men who took the team. This was done *afterwards*. Housekeeping." He stood, and something in the way he stood had changed — the restless weight in him gone quiet, settled, aimed. I'd seen that stillness in him once before, at the waystation, when he'd found the kettle of tea and the levelled instruments, and I understood suddenly that it wasn't calm. It was the stillness of a man standing on a bridge he already knows is rotten, deciding how much time he has.

"How far to the headwaters?" I asked.

"Three days' walk if the gorge allows it. Five if it doesn't." He folded my father's page along its original creases and handed it back to me, handle first, like a tool. "And Miss Voss — if the company housekept this camp, they'll housekeep the trail behind them. Whatever's waiting upriver, it's been waiting longer than we have."

I put the page inside my shirt, against my skin, where the paper warmed immediately and became, absurdly, the most alive thing in the clearing. Eleven days. My father had been alone in the high country for eleven days with a secret worth killing for, and the last thing he'd trusted to paper was a message addressed, unmistakably, to whoever loved him enough to come looking.

*The source is the proof.*

"Hold the light," I said, and picked up my pack.
