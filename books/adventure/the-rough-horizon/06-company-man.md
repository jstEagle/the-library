## 6. Company Man

They came up on us at dusk on the fourth day above the second camp, and I will say this for them: they were quiet about it. No engine noise, no hail from the water, none of the theatrical arrival a man expects when he's been dreading something for days. Just the smell of woodsmoke where there shouldn't have been any, and then Crane's voice out of the alder thicket, pleasant as a dinner bell.

"Mr. Kavanagh. Might I trouble you for a moment of your evening?"

Ilsa was at the fire, boiling water for tea that would never get drunk. She looked at me. I looked at her, and did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the waystation: one land agent, four men in the trees by the sound of their breathing, two of us, and a river running fast enough to drown a thought.

"Come to the fire," I said. "It's cold, and lying is easier done sitting down."

Aldous Crane stepped out of the brush like a man stepping off a veranda. That was the first thing I noticed about him — he had dressed for this. Not for the bush. For the *meeting*. Grey wool suit, pressed despite four days of patrol boat and portage, boots polished to a shine that had no business surviving the Sable's gravel bars. He carried a leather document case under one arm the way a clergyman carries scripture, and he smiled with exactly half his mouth, the half that did the talking.

The other half watched.

"You must be Mr. Crane," Ilsa said, and her voice was flat as a rail spike. "You've been following us."

"Following is such an accusatory word." He accepted the rock I kicked toward him across the fire and sat down on it without checking whether it was wet, which told me either that he trusted his tailor or that comfort meant nothing to him. "I prefer to think of myself as a fellow traveler. The valley is narrow, Miss — Voss, isn't it? Ilsa Voss. My condolences regarding your father. A fine surveyor. Meticulous."

"Meticulous," she repeated.

"Painstakingly so." He opened the document case. "Which is precisely why we're all in this difficulty."

I let him talk. There's a trick to it — you let a man build his own gallows while you hold the rope and look agreeable. I'd learned it from bridge inspectors and claim agents, men who could look at a cracked pier and call it character. Crane laid it out the way those men laid things out: reasonable tone, unreasonable content, everything arranged so that refusing felt like the rude option.

"The company," he said, "values its relationship with the government survey office. We value accuracy. And we are aware — painfully aware — that certain preliminary readings taken in this valley may have been subject to instrument error. Altitude anomalies. Magnetic interference from the ore bodies themselves, which as you know can play havoc with a needle." He produced a paper, unfolded it, smoothed it on his knee. "What we require is a certified statement. From a licensed engineer. Confirming that the headwaters country contains no commercially viable mineral deposits, and that the earlier readings were compromised."

"And who signs that statement?" Ilsa asked.

"A qualified professional." His eyes came around to me, warm as a bank manager's. "Mr. Kavanagh comes highly recommended. Twenty years on the high lines. Surveyed more closed mileage than any man in the territory. A man whose word carries weight precisely because he has no stake here. No claims, no holdings, no history in the valley." He smiled wider by a degree. "A moving man. Men who keep moving understand that some cargo isn't worth carrying."

There it was. I'd been waiting for it since the clerk at the waystation warned me off with his soft voice and his clean fingernails — waiting for the polite version to put on its suit and make its offer. And here it was, sitting on a rock by my fire, offering me money to say the valley was empty.

"How much?" I asked.

Ilsa turned her head sharply. Crane didn't blink. "Five hundred now. Five hundred on filing. Plus a retainer contract — seasonal inspection work, closed-line certification, your own schedule. You'd never want for a contract again."

"Fifteen hundred total, plus the retainer," I said. "That's what a man's signature costs these days?"

"That's what a *reliable* man's signature costs," Crane said. "Reliability is the expensive part. Anyone can find a liar for fifty dollars. But a liar with your reputation — a man whose certificates the assayer's office accepts without reading — that's rare stock. We're not buying dishonesty, Mr. Kavanagh. We're buying credibility. Yours happens to be excellent."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that minute, because honesty is the only thing I've got left that nobody's offered to buy.

For about three seconds, I wanted to take it.

Not because of the money. Well — partly because of the money; I'm not a saint, and fifteen hundred dollars buys a lot of distance. But mostly because taking it meant the thing would be over. The valley, the missing men, the girl with her father's stubborn jaw sitting across the fire from me looking at me like I'd already decided — all of it would collapse into a signature and a train ticket and a life spent moving again, which was the life I knew how to live. Moving was safe. Moving meant no town knew my face well enough to blame it, no crew worked under my eye long enough for me to fail them, no ground stayed still long enough to crack beneath me.

And then I thought about the bridge.

People assume a man haunted by a collapse thinks about the falling. I don't. I think about the arithmetic before it. Three years of reports saying the piers were scouring. An inspector who signed off because signing off was cheaper than condemning a span that carried freight worth more than the repairs. A company that ran the numbers — cost of repair against probability of failure against value of human life, though that last column was always written small — and found the bridge profitable right up until the morning it wasn't. Eleven men went into the water because somewhere a man sat at a desk and decided that eleven lives weighed less than forty thousand dollars, and he didn't even need to hate them to do it. He just needed to keep the columns tidy.

Crane was that man's arithmetic wearing a good suit. Same columns. Same small handwriting. Somewhere behind that pleasant half-smile was a page where the value of six surveyors had been entered against the value of a fraudulent mineral claim, and the claim had won, and the difference had been paid out in river water.

"I knew a bridge once," I said.

Crane waited. He was good at waiting. It's a skill men develop when they're used to other people doing the deciding.

"It stood over the Alder Narrows. Carried the main line for thirty years. Every report said it was sound, because every report was written by men paid to say so. Then one morning in April it wasn't sound anymore, and eleven of my crew went into the water with it." I fed a stick into the fire. "The company paid out the claims. Quietly. Called it an act of God. And the man who signed the last inspection certificate got promoted, because his signature had held until the statute of limitations did."

"An unfortunate story," Crane said.

"It's not a story. It's a price list. And I've seen it before tonight — twice, actually. Once at the waystation, when your clerk told me the file was closed before anyone had bothered opening it. And once upstream, where a survey team left their instruments leveled and their kettle full and walked nowhere." I looked at him. "Six men don't vanish from a valley, Mr. Crane. They're vanished. There's a difference, and the difference is always a man with a briefcase deciding the numbers work better without them."

The pleasant half of Crane's mouth stopped smiling. The watching half didn't change at all.

"You're declining," he said.

"I'm declining."

"Mr. Kavanagh." He folded the paper along its original creases, unhurried, a man putting away a tool he expected to use again. "Let me describe the alternative to the retainer, so we understand each other fully. Your concession permit expires in nine days. When it lapses, you are trespassing on company land. Everything you carry — instruments, notes, maps — becomes evidence of unlawful entry. The government office has already declared the Voss expedition closed; there is no official search, no official record, and no official person to whom anything you find can be delivered. You are two civilians in a gorge with one trail, which my men can close behind you as easily as closing a book." He stood, brushed off his trousers, resettled the document case under his arm. "The valley erases things, Mr. Kavanagh. Rivers, trails, expeditions. All I do is help it along. Five hundred dollars says you're a sensible man. Think it over. I'll pass back through."

He nodded to Ilsa — "Miss Voss. Again, my condolences" — and the four shapes in the alders withdrew with him, quiet as weather, and in ten minutes the valley belonged to the river and the dark again.

* * *

Ilsa hadn't said a word through all of it. Now she crouched by the fire and fed it the stick I'd been holding, because my hand had stopped being interested in things.

"He offered you fifteen hundred dollars," she said.

"He did."

"And you said no."

"I said no."

She studied me across the flames, and whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it, because something in her shoulders came down half an inch — not trust, nothing so generous as trust, but maybe the suspension of its opposite. "My father told me once that there are two kinds of men who come into the bush. The kind who go in to take something out, and the kind who go in because something took them first." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "He said the second kind made better witnesses. They've got nothing left to protect."

"Your father sounds like an optimist."

"He sounds like a man who's been hiding in the source country for three weeks eating fish raw." Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and she covered it by poking the fire. "The notebooks, Oscar. Crane's men ransacked the camp and took every notebook but missed the page in the theodolite case. Which means they know the readings exist, they know they didn't get them, and they know we might. He didn't come up here to buy a signature. He came to find out how much we know."

"I know," I said.

"Then you know he'll be back through this valley whether we sign or not. And next time he won't bring a briefcase."

"I know that too."

She looked at me a long moment. "Then why are you smiling?"

Because the trap was finally visible, and a visible trap is just geography. Because nine days was a number, and numbers were the one language I'd spoken fluently my whole rotten life. And because somewhere below us in the dark, a man in a grey suit was floating back downriver believing he'd measured me and found me purchasable, and there is a particular cold satisfaction in being underestimated by the exact kind of man who killed your crew.

"Because he told us his timetable," I said, and pulled my field book out of my coat, and opened it flat on my knee. "Nine days, one trail, and a gorge he thinks he's already closed. Get your father's page, Ilsa. We're going to need every reading he hid — because tomorrow we climb past the last station mark, and we're going to find out what's at the top of this valley that's worth six men's lives."

Above us, far up the black throat of the gorge, something shifted — a slide, maybe, or water changing course, or nothing at all. But Ilsa went still, listening, and I caught myself listening too, and neither of us said what we were both thinking:

that up there, past the last mark, someone might be waiting.
