## 7. The Forbidden Source

My father taught me that a surveyor's last station is always the hardest to find, because the country conspires against you. The ground rises when the map says it shouldn't. The light fails an hour early. The river, which has been your companion for forty miles, becomes a liar, doubling back through country that swallows its own echoes. He taught me this over kitchen tables, with salt shakers for peaks and a length of string for the line, and I had nodded along thinking it was just the way fathers talked — filling silence with instruction because affection embarrassed them.

Above the last marked station, I understood he had been giving me a survival lecture all those years, and I hadn't taken notes.

The valley closed like a fist. For three days Oscar and I had climbed through gorge country where the Sable ran green and furious between walls that leaned in over the water, and the whole time the instrument marks had led us — blazes on trees, little brass pins in rock, each one numbered in his handwriting. Station forty-one. Station forty-two. Each elevation logged on the strip of paper Oscar carried in his oilskin wallet, each one wrong. Not carelessly wrong. *Deliberately* wrong, in the way that only a trained man's numbers can be wrong — consistent, confident, and pointing at a country that did not exist.

Then station forty-three, and the marks stopped.

Beyond it the gorge narrowed until we were walking in the river itself, boots in water to the knee, the walls so close that Oscar could touch both sides with his arms out. And then, around a shoulder of granite the color of old bone, the gorge simply ended — and the valley opened into a basin no map had ever promised us.

I have tried since to describe that place to people who weren't there, and I have failed every time. It was a bowl of perhaps two hundred acres, ringed by cliffs, floored with meadow grass gone silver with seed. And at the head of it, where the walls came together, the Sable did something no river is supposed to do at its source.

It rose. Out of a cave mouth forty feet wide, black water pouring from the dark of the mountain as though the mountain were bleeding from some deep wound, cold enough that the air around it had its own weather. Mist hung over the meadow in ropes. The sound was enormous and also somehow private, like the mountain clearing its throat and not wanting to be overheard.

"Nobody maps this," Oscar said quietly. He had that look he got — the one where his eyes went flat and distant, doing arithmetic I couldn't see. "I've pulled every sheet for this district twice. There's a contour here. One line. The cartographers gave up."

"Or were told to."

He didn't answer, which was its own answer.

We crossed the meadow with the water roaring at our left, and it was I who saw it first — because it was I who had spent my childhood learning what my father's camps looked like from two hundred yards. The lean of a tent ridge. The geometry of a fire ring. The particular way a theodolite case sits when someone has set it down meaning to come back.

Except this wasn't a camp. It was a den.

The overhang at the base of the east cliff had been walled in with stone and sod, low as an animal's burrow, and in front of it, laid out on a flat rock with the deliberation of an altar, were the instruments. Theodolite. Level. Staff sections. Chronometer. All of it cleaned, oiled, arranged by size. And beside them, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, a stack of books.

Field books. I knew the binding. I had bought that binding for him in the city, three years ago, a dozen at a time because he went through them like a man who ate paper.

"Papa," I said, and my voice came out in pieces.

The den moved.

He came out of it low and fast, and for one horrible second I did not know him. He was bearded to the chest, filthy, wrapped in hides and canvas, and he held a surveyor's stake in both hands the way a man holds a weapon — because to a man who has lived alone above a river for months, everything that moves is either food or death, and he had stopped expecting a third category. His eyes went over Oscar, over me, with the quick animal triage of the starving, and then they stopped on my face and the stake dropped from his hands and he made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a word. Something older than words, from before he had any, from before I did.

I was on my knees in the meadow grass and he was holding my face in hands black with grease and earth, and he was saying my name over and over like a man checking a reading he cannot believe, and I was saying his, and the river roared, and neither of us could stop.

When I could finally see past him, I saw Oscar standing a careful ten feet off, hat in his hands, looking away down the valley with the studied politeness of a man giving a family a room. There was something in his face, though. Something that looked, from the outside, like grief wearing a borrowed coat.

* * *

He would not come inside the den. He sat by the fire ring while I got my father eating — slowly, in small amounts, because Oscar had warned me that a starving man fed too fast will die of the feeding — and gradually my father became a man again instead of a creature. He talked in bursts. Months had worn holes in his sentences.

"They came at night," he said. "Not Crane himself. His men. Four of them, and a fifth who never spoke. They wanted the books. I'd been sending the readings down by runner to the waystation, and someone at the company was reading them before the government did — I know it, Ilsa, I *know* it, because the questions they asked were the questions my own numbers raised." His hands shook around the tin cup. "They took the boys. Marched them down the gorge trail. I was up on the cliff doing a night observation on the cave — the water level, the flow. When I came down, the camp was torn apart and the trail was empty and there was blood on a rock and I—" He stopped. "I ran. God forgive me, I ran, and I've hated myself every hour since, but I took the books."

"Why the books?" Oscar asked from the fire. Not unkindly. "Why not run light?"

My father looked at him for a long moment. "Because you haven't seen them yet, Mr. Kavanagh. Because every man who's seen them is either dead, missing, or sitting at this fire." He got up, stiff as an old hinge, and brought the oilcloth bundle to the rock, and unwrapped it with the reverence of a priest handling relics.

"Here." He opened the last book to a page dense with his small, exact figures. "The cave. The water. I ran a magnetic traverse through the basin and the needle lies — there's a mass under us pulling it, a big one. I sampled the float. Copper, and iron, and something in the copper that assays higher than it has any right to. Now follow me." His finger moved down the page. "The strike of the ore body. The dip. I projected it."

He looked up at us, and there was the surveyor again, whole, alive, burning.

"It runs out of this basin to the southwest. Under the ridge. Under the old Kestrel claims." He said the name the way you'd say the name of a grave. "The company sold the Kestrel ground twice, Ilsa. Twice. Once to the government as worthless — that's the mineral claim they certified barren, the one the whole concession is built on. And once to the investors as rich — that's the stock they floated in the spring. They needed this valley to be empty and worthless, and my instruments say it is neither, and if the readings reach the assayer, every man who signed those certificates goes to prison, and every man who profited goes down with him."

The fire cracked. The river roared. I sat very still, because I could feel the shape of what he was saying settling over us like the mist off the cave.

"That's why the file was closed," I said. "Not because they found nothing. Because they found everything."

"And that's why you're both in more danger than you understand." He gripped my wrist, and his hand was trembling now for a different reason. "Crane's men took my team to make the numbers disappear. The numbers are still here. Which means the next thing that disappears is whoever carries them out."

Oscar had come to stand beside us. He was looking at the field books, and I saw something pass over his face that I had seen only once before, the night he told me about the bridge — the night he told me about the crew, the rotten timber, the report that had been filed and buried, the arithmetic that had decided eleven men were cheaper than a delay. He had said it flatly, without self-pity: *I've seen what it costs when men who know the truth decide it's safer to keep walking.*

"Then we don't carry them out tonight," Oscar said. "We rest, we eat, and at first light we go down the river together. All three of us. The books, the instruments, and the man who took the readings — because a reading is only a number until the man who made it swears to it."

My father shook his head slowly. "You don't know that gorge. And Crane doesn't leave a thing like this to chance. If he knows I'm alive—"

"He doesn't need to know it," Oscar said. "He only needs to guess. And a man like that guesses in the direction of money." He looked up the valley, toward the cave mouth, where the black water came endlessly out of the mountain, and his voice went quiet. "We should be gone before his guess arrives."

I was wrapping the field books back in their oilcloth when my father caught my arm.

"Ilsa." His voice had dropped. "There's something else. The night they came — before they came — I heard the boat. The patrol launch, on the river below the gorge. It wasn't searching. It was *waiting*. Someone told them exactly where we'd be, and exactly when." His eyes went to the fire, to Oscar, and back to me. "There's a fifth man, sweetheart. There has always been a fifth man. And he's not up here in the mountains."

He didn't say the name. He didn't have to. We both knew the only soul below who had known our route, our pace, and our destination — the clerk at the waystation, who had been so very, very polite.

Down in the gorge, faint through the roar of the river, came the sound of an engine.
