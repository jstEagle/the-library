## 8. What the River Takes

I have lost things before. A crew, a bridge, a decade of sleep. I thought I'd developed a kind of immunity to loss, the way a man who's been struck by lightning stops flinching at weather. But watching smoke rise from the second camp — our camp, the one we'd slept in two nights ago, where Ilsa had laughed for the first time since I met her — taught me that immunity is just another word for not having been tested lately.

We were still above the falls when we saw it. The smoke came up through the pines in a grey column, too straight, too deliberate. Nobody's cook fire stands that straight.

"That's Crane," Ilsa said. Not a question. She'd learned to read the valley faster than I'd expected, and she read this correctly: the company man had come back upriver to tidy his accounts.

"Or a very ambitious squirrel."

"Oscar."

"I know." I took her arm and turned us both back toward the gorge. "The trail down past the falls is sealed. They've cut it."

She looked at me. "You can't know that from here."

"I can smell the pitch burning. They felled trees across the switchbacks and they're setting them alight so nobody comes down that way again without an axe and a week to spare." I'd done similar work myself once, closing a line nobody was supposed to use anymore. There's a particular smell to deliberate destruction, and it doesn't smell like accident. It smells like paperwork.

So there we stood, three days above the last marked station, holding a dead man's field books that proved the company had sold the same ore body twice, with Mr. Aldous Crane's men below us burning the only trail out of the basin, and the falls ahead of us roaring like a creditor.

"The river," Ilsa said.

"The river drops forty feet in half a mile over rock that eats boats."

"The river goes where we need to go."

"It also goes where dead men go. Same water."

She didn't argue. That was worse. She just looked at me with her father's patience, the same expression he must have used on junior surveyors who said a reading couldn't be right, and waited for me to arrive at the answer myself like a good student.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the Sable above the falls, because I had walked its banks years before anyone thought to close anything: the river narrowed twice between the basin and the drop, and in those narrows the current ran fast but honest — no undercuts, no keeper holes, just water going somewhere with conviction. Below the falls themselves there was a pool, and below the pool a stretch of shallow braids where the water spread out and forgot its anger. If a man could get through the falls — and I want to be clear that every instinct I owned said he could not — the rest was merely unpleasant instead of fatal.

"We don't run the falls," I told her. "We portage around them. There's a shelf on the east side, old game trail, maybe. Then we build a raft or find a way down to the pool and take the braids."

"And if the shelf isn't there?"

"Then we climb back up and explain to Crane that we've reconsidered his offer."

She almost smiled. In the last three days I had catalogued her smiles the way I once catalogued mileposts, and I am not proud of how much of my thinking they occupied. There was a duty in front of us — the field books, the assayer, the testimony — and I held it firmly in mind, the way you grip a rail in a rocking car. But underneath the duty, in the place I didn't inspect, something else was running on its own schedule.

* * *

The shelf existed. Barely. It was a seam of stone the width of a coffin lid, angling down along the gorge wall above the thunder of the falls, slick with spray and hung with deadfall. We roped ourselves together — my idea, and the first time in ten years I had tied myself to another person on purpose rather than by the accident of shared employment.

The field books went into a oilskin wrap, and the oilskin went inside my coat, against my chest. My instruments — the transit, the level, the rods I'd carried through every chapter of this misadventure — went onto my back, because a surveyor does not abandon his instruments, and because some part of me still believed that if I kept the tools, I kept the trade, and if I kept the trade, I kept the man I might have been before the bridge.

That part of me was about to learn something.

* * *

Halfway along the shelf, the mountain made its argument.

It came as a sound first — a crack like a rifle shot somewhere above us — and then as movement, a section of deadfall letting go, a pine trunk the size of a church pillar sliding off its ledge and coming down the gorge wall with the unhurried malice of geology.

There is no decision in a moment like that. There is only arithmetic, and the arithmetic is brutal. The trunk hit the shelf twenty feet behind us and took the shelf with it. Stone, timber, and ten thousand pounds of indifferent history poured past us into the falls, and the shelf under our feet shuddered, tilted, and began to go.

I remember three things with perfect clarity, which is more than I can say for most of my life.

First: the rope going taut, and Ilsa's weight swinging out over the void, and my boots skidding on tilting stone while my arms took a load they had no business taking.

Second: my pack strap sliding off my shoulder — I felt it go, felt the whole careful weight of my instruments peel away from me like a bandage — and the transit cartwheeling down into the white water, gone between one heartbeat and the next, without ceremony, the way everything important leaves.

Third: her hand finding mine.

Not the rope. Her hand. She had climbed halfway back up the rope toward me — insane, magnificent, exactly the wrong thing — and gotten her fingers around mine, and in the roar of the falls, with the shelf dissolving under my boots, hers was the only fixed point left in the world. Everything else was moving: the water, the stone, the smoke from the burning trail, the whole treacherous geography of a valley built to be abandoned. But her hand did not move.

I got my other hand on a root. I got my boots back under me. And then, inch by inch, swearing words I will not repeat here because Ilsa was present, I hauled us both back from the edge to the solid wall of the gorge, where we sat down hard against cold granite and shook like the last two leaves of autumn.

My map was gone. Gone with the pack, gone with the instruments, gone into the pool below the falls to keep company with every other thing the Sable had decided it wanted. Ten years of closed lines, every decommissioned spur and forgotten waystation I'd walked and measured and memorized, all of it ink on paper, and paper floats downstream.

"Well," Ilsa said finally. Her voice was ragged. "You always said the river was the real surveyor."

"I said no such thing."

"You said the river knows where everything goes." She was looking at my empty shoulder where the strap had been, and I watched her understand what it cost me, and I watched her decide not to say anything kind about it, which was the kindest thing she could have done. "The books are safe?"

I pressed my chest where the oilskin lay flat. "Safe."

"Then we're still ahead of him."

"We're alive above a waterfall with no equipment, no map, and a rope that's seen better centuries. Define ahead."

"Ahead means the truth is still in your coat and not at the bottom of the pool." She stood, wobbled, and glared at the falls as though it owed her money. "Come on, Kavanagh. The braids are waiting."

* * *

We went down the east wall on roots and prayer, and found the pool below the falls exactly as I remembered it: wide, green, deceptively calm, ringed with boulders like spectators. The water that came over the lip above us fell in a single white curtain, and the sound was so total it wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, a weight on the ears.

The raft was Ilsa's idea and Ilsa's construction — deadfall logs, lashed with the rope and a length of cord from her father's kit, ugly as sin and roughly as seaworthy. I told her it would sink. She pointed out that sinking was a problem for later, and that later was a country we would never reach standing on this bank.

She was right. I'm recording that fact for posterity, because I intend to deny it verbally for the rest of my life.

The braids were as honest as I'd promised — fast, shallow, loud, but straight. The raft took water constantly and cared nothing for steering, and twice we grounded on gravel bars and had to haul off by hand in water to our waists, freezing enough to make speech impossible and laughter nearly inevitable. Nearly. Ilsa laughed once, a short bark of pure disbelief when the raft spun completely around in an eddy and presented us backward to our own destination, and I found the laugh did something to my chest that the cold water couldn't touch.

And then the river widened one last time, and slowed, and went quiet, and I understood that we had come through. The valley opened below us into long meadows and the far blue suggestion of the railway town, and the sun was low, and the concession lapsed at midnight.

I looked at my hands. They were cut, shaking, and empty. No transit. No level. No map. Twenty years of being the man who keeps moving, and the river had stripped me down to what I could carry in my coat and hold in my fist.

Ilsa's hand was still in my fist. I hadn't let go since the shelf. Neither, I noticed, had she.

"There's a patrol boat on this stretch," I said, mostly to break the spell before it broke me. "Crane's men. We'll have to walk the bank from here, stay in the trees."

"All right."

"And when we reach the town, there'll be a room full of government men, and I'll have to stand in front of them and sign my name and say what happened, out loud, in front of everyone, and then not leave afterward." I heard how it sounded — small, and enormous. "I haven't stood still in front of a room since—"

"I know since." She squeezed my hand once, briefly, and let go, and started up the bank toward the tree line like a woman with an appointment. "Midnight, Kavanagh. Try to be on time. You're the only punctual man I've ever met who's afraid of arriving."

I followed her into the trees, lighter than I had any right to be, carrying nothing but the truth and her handprint still warm in mine — and behind us, far down the valley, a whistle blew. Not a train whistle.

A company boat, coming upriver.
