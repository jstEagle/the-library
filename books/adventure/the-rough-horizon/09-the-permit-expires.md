## 9. The Permit Expires

The last twelve miles to Corben's Landing we made in the dark, on a handcar that Ilsa pumped like she was trying to kill it. My arms were gone — burned through somewhere around the falls, and what the falls left of me the cold had finished — so she did most of the work while I steered with one hand and held the field books against my chest with the other like a man carrying an infant across a flooded creek.

"You could tell me," she said between strokes, breath ragged, "that we're going to make it."

"We're going to make it."

"Liar."

"Probably." The rail hummed under us, running true for the first time in forty miles. Somebody still maintained this stretch. That meant somebody still believed in it. "But it's good lying. The kind that keeps you pumping."

The moon was up, thin as a paring, and the rails threw back just enough light to steer by. Behind us the gorge was a black seam in the mountainside, and somewhere inside it Crane's men were doing whatever men do when they've been paid to make a valley forget itself. I tried not to think about the fire we'd seen from the ridge — the second camp going up like a struck match, orange light licking the pines. They'd burned notebooks once already. They were getting practiced at it.

What they hadn't counted on was a half-feral surveyor who'd spent three months sleeping with his field books sewn into his coat.

* * *

Corben's Landing at eleven o'clock at night looked like every railway town I'd ever drifted out of: water tower, coal chute, a hotel with its name painted on backwards so you could read it from the train. The difference was that this time I wasn't drifting out. This time I was walking toward something, and my legs kept trying to turn me around anyway. Old habit. A man can teach his body anything, including cowardice, and the body will hold the lesson long after the mind recants.

The government assayer's office sat above the general store, and there was a light in the window. I'd sent a telegram from the junction — three words, *coming tonight, bring* — and cut it off there because telegrams are read by everyone, and some things you don't say in front of everyone.

Ilsa hammered on the door like she was serving papers.

The man who opened it was sixty if he was a day, spectacles pushed up into white hair, dressing gown over union suit, holding a lamp like a man expecting bad news. Assayer Prosser. I'd heard the name on freight platforms for years, always attached to words like *thorough* and *impossible*, which in my experience meant honest.

"It's midnight in fifty-one minutes," Ilsa said. "I'd come in if I were you."

He came in. We all did.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about telling the truth: it takes longer than lying. A lie is a short story. The truth is a whole line surveyed station by station, elevation by elevation, and every figure has to be read aloud in a room where a man in a dressing gown writes it down in pencil and asks you to repeat yourself.

Prosser read until two in the morning. He read the source readings first — the ones Aldous Crane had offered me four thousand dollars and a lifetime contract to lose — and his face went through a series of expressions I'd last seen on a man reading his own death warrant. The ore body didn't just run under the company's claims. It ran under land they'd sold twice, under homesteads, under the town lots of Corben's Landing itself. Every fraudulent assay filed in the last six years rested on the assumption that no one would ever walk up the Sable and look.

"They knew," Prosser said quietly. "They knew what was up there, and they sold it anyway, and then they buried the survey that said so." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his dressing gown, which gave him somewhere to look that wasn't at us. "Mr. Kavanagh. You understand what happens now."

"I understand Crane doesn't stop."

"He doesn't stop. Men like Crane don't have convictions, they have employment, and employment survives scandal the way water survives a drought — it finds the low ground." He put the spectacles back on. "You'll be named. In the hearings, in the filings, in whatever they print. Your name next to theirs, forever. Some men find that worse than dying."

"I've died before," I said. "Bridge on the Kestrel Line, eleven years ago. Forty tons of rotten iron and me underneath it, and six men who didn't crawl out." The room went very still. Ilsa's hand found my sleeve, not gripping, just resting there, like a hand on a railing. "I signed off on that bridge. I certified timbers I should have condemned, because the schedule said condemn them later and the schedule was money. So no, Mr. Prosser. Being named doesn't frighten me. What frightens me is the other thing. The thing where I keep moving and let it happen again."

Prosser looked at me for a long moment over the tops of his spectacles.

"Well," he said. "Then we won't waste each other's time."

* * *

The signing happened at twenty minutes to midnight, which Ilsa informed us was dramatic and which I informed her was clerical. There is a version of this scene where a man stands tall in a pool of lamplight and speaks truth to power. The actual version involves a wobbly table, a pen that needed blotting twice, and an assayer saying *initial here, and here, and this is the affidavit page, read it first, no, all of it.*

I read all of it.

And then I stood there, pen in hand, over the line where my name went, and I couldn't do it.

Not because of doubt. Doubt I could have handled; doubt is just fear wearing arithmetic. It was the room. Four walls, a lamp, two people watching, and nowhere to move. Eleven years of keeping my feet on gravel and rails and riverbeds, always with a horizon to walk toward, and now the horizon was a signature line eighteen inches wide and I had to stand on it.

"You don't have to narrate it," Ilsa said softly. "Just stand there. Standing is the whole job."

So I stood. I thought about the bridge — not the falling part, the part before, the morning inspection, the moment I'd put my thumb over the soft timber and told myself the numbers would hold. I thought about Halloran laughing on the trestle, and young Pete with his new boots, and how none of them got a signature line. Their names went on a stone instead, and stones don't take testimony.

I wrote *Oscar Kavanagh* in letters that shook only a little, and set the pen down, and discovered that standing still in front of witnesses is not the opposite of courage. It's courage with nowhere to hide.

"There," Prosser said, sanding the page. "Witnessed and filed. And Mr. Kavanagh — the concession lapses at midnight. Whatever claim Crane holds on this valley dies with the permit. His mineral rights revert, his access clauses void, his little empire becomes paper." He smiled without much warmth. "Which means he has nothing left to protect except his reputation. Be careful. A man defending his reputation is more dangerous than a man defending his money."

"I've noticed," I said. "We met."

* * *

We walked out past midnight into a street gone quiet and silver. The concession was dead. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in the capital, a clerk had stamped a date, and a hundred miles of mountains had changed owners — or rather, stopped pretending to belong to anyone.

Ilsa walked beside me with her father's theodolite case under her arm, the one with the hidden page still taped inside the lid. She hadn't let go of it since the basin.

"He'll come for the books," she said. Not a question.

"He'll try. But he can't burn a government office, Ilsa. He can't buy Prosser — I watched the man read those figures, and you can't buy that kind of stubbornness." I stopped at the corner where the main street met the rail yard, and looked down the line toward the dark. "He can make things difficult, though. Hearings get delayed. Witnesses get discredited. Men like me get described as unstable drifters with a grudge."

"Are you?"

"Thoroughly. But I'm a thorough drifter with documents."

She laughed — a short, tired sound, but real — and then went quiet again, looking at the town. At the lit window of the hotel dining room where someone was washing up late. At the water tower catching the moon. At the small, ordinary machinery of a place where people stayed.

"You could leave tonight," she said. "Freight comes through at four. You could be two hundred miles away before Crane's people even know the filing exists. Let the paper do the fighting."

Every muscle in my body voted yes. The body keeps its lessons.

"I used to think moving was the same as being free," I said. "Turned out it was just a slower way of running. And the thing about running—" I looked at her, at the soot still in her hair from the gorge, at the case she wouldn't put down, "—is that you always arrive somewhere eventually. Might as well arrive on purpose."

"So you're staying. For the hearing."

"For the hearing. For the valley. For—" I gestured at the town, the river, the whole improbable arrangement of lights and water and hills, and found I couldn't finish the sentence, and found also that she understood it anyway, which was worse and better.

Down the line, far off, a locomotive whistled — the four o'clock freight, early, or maybe not the freight at all. The sound hung in the cold air a long time, and neither of us moved toward it.

Behind us, in the assayer's office above the store, the lamp still burned. And on the street below, parked in the shadow of the coal chute where it hadn't been ten minutes ago, stood a black carriage with mud on its wheels — gorge mud, red and fine, the color of the upper Sable.

Somebody had driven all night to be here when the permit died.

Crane stepped down onto the boardwalk, adjusted his hat, and started toward us.
