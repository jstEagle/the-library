## 1. Debt of Clear Skies

The supercell came up out of the west like a rumor no one wanted confirmed.

Cyrus Nakamura saw it first as a bruise on the horizon, a smudge of violet-gray that didn't belong in a sky that had promised clear all morning. By the time he'd topped the ridge above the Salt Flats road, the bruise had grown teeth. The anvil spread overhead like a black wing, and beneath it the wall cloud turned with the slow, patient rotation of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use every second of it.

"Come on," he murmured to the sky, one hand on the warm hood of his chase truck, the other holding the anemometer readout against the wind. "Be beautiful. Be worth something."

Worth something. That was the arithmetic that governed his life now, though he'd once measured storms in awe alone—back when he was twenty-three and invincible and the only currency that mattered was proximity. He'd chased for the love of it then. Now he chased because the Vane Syndicate paid eleven hundred credits per verified mesocyclone dataset, fourteen hundred if the cell dropped a tornado inside their coverage grid, and because he owed them sixty-two thousand, four hundred and ten credits plus accruing interest, a number he could recite the way other men recited prayers.

He did the math while he drove, the way he always did, tires humming on cracked asphalt, dust boiling off the flats behind him like smoke from a burning world.

Eleven hundred per clean dataset. Maybe six good cells a season if the monsoon behaved, eight if it didn't. Call it seven thousand a season after fuel, after parts, after the roof repair from last year's hail event that had dented the truck like God's own ball-peen hammer. Seven thousand against sixty-two thousand. Nine seasons. Nine more years of this—of eating rice and tinned fish, of sleeping in the cab, of watching other men buy houses on flood plains and insure them through Vane subsidiaries at rates he couldn't imagine affording.

Unless the cell was violent. Unless it produced. A tornado inside the grid bumped him to nearly two thousand, and a long-track monster—one of those rare, sculptural beasts that carved a scar across the land for forty kilometers—could be worth three datasets stitched together, five thousand credits in a single afternoon.

The wall cloud ahead was promising exactly that kind of afternoon.

Cyrus took the turnoff onto the gravel section road doing eighty, the truck fishtailing, his stomach lifting with the old familiar lift, the one that felt like flying and falling at once. The air had gone green-black and thick, tasting of ozone and wet iron. Ahead, a ragged lowering spun beneath the meso—a funnel not yet committed, teasing the earth, sucking a column of dust into its slow gyre.

"Not yet," he told it, parking on high ground two kilometers out, killing the engine so the radio would come in clean. "Not until I'm set up."

He deployed the probe array with hands that knew the work better than they knew their own faces: tripod, sensors, uplink dish angled toward the relay satellite he rented time on from—of course—the Syndicate. Everything led back to Vane eventually. The roads, the weather satellites, the insurance actuarial tables that priced human lives by postcode. The Syndicate didn't cause the storms. It just owned the means of surviving them, which in the Kestrel Basin amounted to the same thing.

The funnel touched down at 4:17 in the afternoon, a rope of gray silk unspooling from the cloud base, and Cyrus felt the old joy rise in him like floodwater even as the colder part of his mind logged it: *tornado, on the ground, inside the grid, duration pending.* Data streamed from his probes. The uplink light blinked steady green. Somewhere in a glass tower in Meridian City, an algorithm was already converting this hour of his life into premiums and profit margins, and Cyrus Nakamura was converting it into maybe four thousand credits off a debt that grew back like grass.

The tornado crossed the flats in nineteen minutes, never touching anything but dirt and a fence line, dissolving with the modesty of a thing embarrassed by its own potential. The supercell marched on east toward the river country, trailing rain curtains like a bride's train, and Cyrus stood in the after-wind smelling the crushed sage and the lightning-burned air, and let himself feel, for exactly ninety seconds, the pure animal wonder of it.

Then he packed up his instruments and drove to the fuel stop at Halfway Well, where the ledger waited.

* * *

Halfway Well wasn't much: a diesel pump, a canteen that sold cold noodles and warmer beer, a row of rusted grain silos leaning together like conspirators. The monsoon season was still three weeks out, but the place already had that pre-storm tension, trucks gassed and gassed again, drivers checking radar on cracked tablets, everyone reading the same sky and seeing different futures in it.

Cyrus filled the tank and tried not to look at the pump total. Forty-one credits. He'd made four thousand today and spent forty-one of it before the sun went down. That was the thing about debt nobody warned you about when you signed—it didn't just take your money. It took your ability to enjoy what remained.

He was eating noodles at the counter, chopsticks moving without his attention, when the man sat down beside him.

Cyrus smelled him before he saw him: expensive cologne fighting a losing battle against diesel and dust. Gray suit, gray shoes, a face designed to be forgotten—medium everything, features arranged for anonymity the way some men arranged theirs for memorability. But the watch was real. Vane-issue, titanium, the kind that cost more than Cyrus's truck, and it marked the man the way a brand marks cattle.

"Nakamura." Not a question. "Good cell today. Clean track, right through grid sector nine. The office is pleased."

"The office," Cyrus said, "can wire me a bonus."

The man smiled without any part of his face joining in. He set a slate on the counter between them, face down, and laid one finger on it like a man claiming territory. "Delmar Quist. Collections and Acquisitions, Vane Syndicate. I've been driving since dawn to buy you a beer."

"I don't drink with collectors."

"You will with this one." Quist signaled the canteen woman anyway, ordered two beers, paid in hard scrip. Generous, performative. "Your account stands at sixty-two four-ten. Interest compounds monthly. At your current earning rate—and I've seen your rate, Nakamura, it's honestly impressive, top decile of our contract chasers—you're looking at nine, ten years to clear."

"Eight," Cyrus said. "If the monsoon's mean this year."

"Eight." Quist nodded slowly, as if savoring the number. "Eight years of hail damage and rollover risk and fuel costs, chasing a balance that grows faster than you can cut it. You know what we call men like you? We call you *annuities.*" He slid the beer across. "I'm here to offer you a chance to stop being one."

Cyrus put down his chopsticks. Outside, the light was going amber and long, the storm's leftovers smeared across the western sky like paint someone had given up on. He should get back on the road, shadow the cell east, maybe catch a redevelopment over the basin. Every hour mattered. Every hour was money.

But he didn't move. That was the trap of it, and he knew it even as he fell in. Desperation doesn't look like desperation from the inside. From the inside it looks like opportunity arriving at exactly the right moment, wearing a gray suit and buying you a beer.

"Talk," he said.

Quist flipped the slate. On it: a contract summary, dense with clauses, the Vane seal embossed at the bottom in silver ink that seemed to shift when Cyrus looked at it directly. "Meridian Scientific ran an expedition up the Kestrel River six weeks ago. Hydrographic survey, atmospheric research, the usual academic vanity project. They went quiet. Last transmission was thirty-one days ago." He tapped the slate; a waveform scrolled past, flatlining into static. "Vane has salvage rights on their equipment lease. The gear upriver is worth a great deal to us. Instruments, cores, data storage. We want it recovered intact."

"And you need me because—?" Cyrus gestured at himself, at the dust on his sleeves, the truck outside with its dented roof. "You've got enforcers. You've got boats."

"We have enforcers who are very good at making problems disappear and very bad at reading rivers. The upper Kestrel in monsoon season is not a place for amateurs. It's a place for a man who's spent fifteen years learning how water and sky negotiate." Quist took a slow pull of his beer. "You know the basin. You've run the lower river. Your file says you guided survey teams before your—situation."

*Before my situation.* Three words doing the work of a whole history: the flooded season, the bad contract, the boat he'd borrowed from Vane Marine to make one last big score, the storm that ate the cargo and left Cyrus alive with a debt sized like a mountain range. He'd been paying on it ever since, one storm at a time.

"One job," Quist said. "Recover the equipment, or verify its loss. Six weeks, maybe eight. You come back, and the slate is wiped. Zero balance. Nakamura, free and clear, no liens, no interest, no collections. You walk into next season owing nothing to anyone."

The words landed in Cyrus's chest like a probe hitting bedrock. *Owing nothing.* He tried to remember what that felt like and couldn't, and the inability frightened him more than the offer did.

"What's the catch?"

Quist looked almost hurt. "A catch. In a straightforward salvage contract."

"There's always a catch. Meridian went silent for a reason. Rivers don't eat entire expeditions."

"Rivers don't," Quist agreed pleasantly. "But the upper basin isn't entirely a river. There are people up there. Unlicensed settlements, toll-takers, the usual ecology of places the law forgets. That's why we're sending an enforcer with you. For security." He said it lightly, the way a man mentions an umbrella he happens to be carrying. "Marek Voss. You'll meet him at Lowmark. He reports to us, not to you. Think of him as ballast."

"Ballast that shoots people."

"Ballast that's never needed to." Quist finished his beer and stood, straightening a jacket that didn't need straightening. "The full contract is on the slate. You have until you reach Lowmark to sign. Boat leaves when the river does." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the amber light, and for a moment his forgettable face held something almost like sympathy—or its excellent imitation. "For what it's worth, Nakamura, I hope you take it. Men shouldn't spend their best years paying for their worst ones."

Then he was gone, into a gray car that hadn't been there an hour ago, driven by someone Cyrus hadn't seen either.

* * *

Cyrus sat at the counter for a long time after, the slate in front of him, the noodles going cold.

Outside, the last light drained off the flats. A pair of truckers argued quietly about radar returns over the basin—something about pressure readings that didn't match the models, storms forming where storms shouldn't. He half-listened, the way you half-listen to weather talk in a country where weather is the only news that matters.

He opened the contract and read it properly, because whatever else he was, Cyrus Nakamura was not careless. Most of it was standard salvage boilerplate: liability waivers, hazard acknowledgments, a clause assigning all recovered data to Vane outright. One clause made him pause—a nondisclosure provision covering "any phenomena observed within the upper Kestrel drainage," worded strangely, broadly, as if the lawyers had been told to protect against something specific. As if someone already knew what was up there.

And buried in the compensation schedule, a line item that didn't belong in a salvage job: *hazard premium contingent upon confirmation of source integrity.*

Source integrity. Sources were wells. Mines. Springs. What kind of expedition carried a *source?*

He thought about Dr. Ilsa Reyes then, unexpectedly, the way you think of someone when a place reminds you of them. They'd worked two seasons together, years ago, before her name got too big for fieldwork—her laughing at his instrument calibrations, her insistence that rivers were just atmosphere slowed down, that sky and water were one system pretending to be two. She'd gone academic, gone famous, gone upriver with Meridian Scientific. And now her expedition was a flatline on a syndicate slate, and the syndicate wanted its hardware back, and the man they'd sent to collect it bought beers like he was sorry about the whole business.

Cyrus did the arithmetic one last time, the way a gambler counts his chips before the hand he shouldn't play.

Nine years, or one job. A decade of his life, or six weeks of a river that ate expeditions. It wasn't a choice, not really. Choices required two live options, and one of these options was just a slower way of dying—dust in his lungs, hail in his roof, sixty-two thousand growing to seventy while he slept in his cab.

He pressed his thumb to the slate. The seal flared silver, and somewhere in Meridian City, a machine noted that Cyrus Nakamura had just become the Syndicate's property again, briefly, and on their terms.

"Free and clear," he said aloud, to the empty canteen, to the amber dark outside the window. The words tasted like ozone before a strike.

He walked out to the truck. The eastern sky over the basin was still grumbling, faint heat-lightning flickering inside a cloud deck that looked, from this distance, almost like a wall—like something standing between him and the horizon, waiting to be climbed.

He pointed the truck north, toward Lowmark and the river, and drove into the dark with the contract glowing on the seat beside him and the strange new feeling settling over his shoulders, heavier than any debt:

The certainty that he had just been bought cheap.
