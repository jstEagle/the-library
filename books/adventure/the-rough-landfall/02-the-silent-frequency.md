## 2. The Silent Frequency

The Vane Syndicate kept its offices the way it kept its ledgers: cold, precise, and lit just bright enough to make you feel examined.

Cyrus Nakamura had been in rooms like this before—collection rooms, mostly, where men with soft voices and hard arithmetic explained to him exactly how far behind he was. But this one was different. It sat on the ninth floor of a tower in Corvane's financial district, above the smog line, where the windows showed you the whole gray sprawl of the city and reminded you that none of it belonged to you. The carpet swallowed his boot heels. The air tasted of recycled nothing and someone else's money.

The man across the desk was called Aldous Wren, though Cyrus suspected the name was as manufactured as the calm on his face. He wore a charcoal suit without a single crease, which in this weather was less a fashion statement than a small miracle of wealth. Behind him, mounted on the wall like a piece of art, hung a speaker grille of brushed steel.

"You'll want to hear it," Wren said, "before you decide anything."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Of course not." Wren smiled the way a banker smiles at collateral. He touched something beneath the desk, and the speaker came alive.

Static first—a long, breathing hiss, like wind moving through a canyon—and then a voice. A woman's voice. Clipped, professional, tired around the edges but holding itself together with discipline.

*"Meridian Expedition, day forty-one, position logged at bearing two-seven-zero from the Kestrel narrows, eleven kilometers past the standing water mark. Instruments are reading—"*

A pause. Not static. A pause, deliberate, the sound of someone choosing their next words carefully.

*"—instruments are reading well beyond expected parameters. Repeat: beyond expected parameters. We're proceeding upstream to verify. Reyes out."*

Then static again, longer this time, and the recording looped back to its beginning.

Cyrus sat very still.

He knew that voice.

Not well—not the way you know a friend's voice, easy and unremarkable—but the way you know a voice that once mattered, in a season that once mattered. Dr. Ilsa Reyes. Three years ago, maybe four, up on the Sable Flats, when he'd been running atmospheric data for a university grant that paid in prestige instead of money, and she'd been the hydrologist who could read a river's mood from fifty meters away, who'd argued with him for six hours straight about evaporation rates and then bought him dinner because she respected a good argument more than she respected being right.

She'd gone quiet after that. Everyone did. People drifted out of your life the way weather systems drifted off the coast—you noticed the absence only later, when the sky felt wrong.

"She's missing," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question.

"The entire Meridian Expedition is missing," Wren corrected gently. "Eleven people. Forty-three days of silence. Their last confirmed position is somewhere in the upper Kestrel Basin." He let the recording play through another loop—the coordinates, the pause, the static—before touching the control again. Silence returned to the room, thick as floodwater. "You know her."

It wasn't a question either. Nothing Wren said was ever a question; everything was an observation waiting to become leverage.

"I've worked with her," Cyrus admitted.

"Good." Wren folded his hands on the desk. "That will make the recovery more efficient."

* * *

They gave him tea he didn't drink and a folder he wasn't allowed to keep.

Inside the folder: satellite imagery of the upper basin, grainy and cloud-veiled, the river a brown vein threading through green dark. A manifest of Meridian's equipment—survey cores, spectrometers, atmospheric arrays, a drilling rig light enough to be hauled by boat. An insurance valuation that made Cyrus's teeth ache. And at the bottom, a single page with the contract terms, printed in the Syndicate's preferred font size: small enough to hide things in, formal enough to pretend nothing was hidden.

"One salvage job," Wren said. "Recover the equipment. Confirm the fate of the expedition for the record. In exchange, the Syndicate considers your account settled in full."

"In full." Cyrus turned the page over as if the back might contain the truth. "My account stands at—" He stopped himself. Saying the number aloud in this room felt like undressing.

"We both know the number," Wren said pleasantly. "We also both know you cannot chase enough supercells in ten seasons to close it. Storm data pays what the market pays, Mr. Nakamura, and the market has decided your particular talent is worth less than your particular debts." He leaned back. "This is not charity. This is arithmetic. You have a boat, a reputation for getting upriver where others can't, and a passing familiarity with the missing party. Three assets. One job."

"And if I say no?"

Wren's smile didn't move but somehow got colder. "Then we continue our current arrangement. Collectors. Interest. The slow erosion of everything you own until the boat itself comes into our possession, and you go back to chasing storms in someone else's vessel, for someone else's margin." He spread his hands. "I'm told there's always work for a man who reads the sky. None of it dignified."

Outside the window, the city lay under its permanent lid of haze, and somewhere beyond it, out on the flats, Cyrus knew there were storms building—he could feel them the way amputees feel weather, an ache in old scars. He'd spent his whole adult life running toward the worst skies on the continent, and every mile of it had been mortgaged. Every lightning strike he'd ever recorded had a lien against it.

Freedom, offered in a folder, priced at one river.

"What happened to them?" he asked. "Meridian."

"Unknown. That's rather the point of the exercise." Wren rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking down at the city with his hands clasped behind him. "The Basin is closed territory. Permits suspended, traffic restricted, the usual precautions. Which means whoever goes up there goes quietly, and whoever recovers the equipment does so without fanfare." He glanced back. "You would be compensated for discretion as well as recovery. The terms reflect it."

Discretion. Cyrus had grown up around enough syndicate language to translate: *no witnesses, no records, no questions.* The word sat in his stomach like bad fuel.

"The equipment matters that much?"

"The equipment represents a significant investment by parties who prefer their investments not wash downstream." Wren returned to the desk and tapped the manifest. "Spectrometric arrays. Atmospheric sensors. Survey cores. Individually, curiosities. Together—" he paused, choosing, "—a picture. The Syndicate collects pictures."

"Of what?"

"Of the future, Mr. Nakamura. The future is the last monopoly worth owning."

The recording played again, unbidden—Cyrus realized Wren had set it looping softly through the room, a soundtrack to the negotiation. *Bearing two-seven-zero. Beyond expected parameters. Reyes out.* Her voice filled the office, calm and professional and forty-three days dead or worse, and Cyrus found he couldn't look away from the speaker grille, couldn't stop hearing the pause before *beyond expected parameters*, that half-second where a careful scientist decided how much truth a radio transmission could carry.

He'd heard that kind of pause before. In his own transmissions, during his own bad nights on the flats, when the storm had him and the data was impossible and he'd had to choose between honesty and composure. Ilsa Reyes had chosen composure. She'd chosen it all the way into whatever silence had swallowed her.

And now the Syndicate wanted him to go collect her ghost's belongings and call it a payday.

"It's a salvage job," Wren said again, softer. "Boats sink. Expeditions fail. Equipment gets wet. Someone has to go get it, and the Syndicate prefers to hire competence." He slid the contract across the desk, along with a pen. "The alternative remains available, of course. Debt has a wonderful way of clarifying options."

Cyrus looked at the pen.

He thought about the number. He thought about the collectors who'd started waiting outside the fuel stops, patient as vultures, polite as knives. He thought about the boat—his boat, the *Kestrel's Daughter*, the one asset they hadn't taken yet, the one thing between him and being entirely owned. He thought about ten more seasons of selling storm data at margins so thin they were practically theoretical, each season older, slower, closer to the day the sky finally collected on him instead.

And underneath all of it, quiet and cold, he thought: *this is wrong.* Not the debt—that was just weather, just the climate he'd been born into. Wrong was the discretion clause. Wrong was the way Wren said *pictures of the future*. Wrong was a syndicate that closed a basin, silenced a region, and sent a debtor upriver to do its digging, hiring his familiarity with a missing woman as though her friendship were a tool in the kit.

Wrong, and paying better than anything he'd ever been offered.

That was the trap of it, he understood even then, sitting in the cold light with the pen in reach. The Syndicate never offered you things you wanted. It offered you things you needed, wrapped in just enough shame that you'd sign quickly, before the dread finished settling.

His hand moved. The scratch of the nib on paper sounded obscenely loud in the quiet room.

"There will be a crew," Wren said as Cyrus signed, watching the signature dry with mild satisfaction. "A hydrologist—we've retained one already. A communications specialist, young, gifted. And a field coordinator to assist with logistics." A pause, measured to the millimeter. "Mr. Voss. You'll find him capable."

"Field coordinator," Cyrus repeated.

"Logistics," Wren agreed, "and accountability. Salvage work attracts... irregularities. Mr. Voss ensures the investment arrives home." He extended his hand; Cyrus took it because refusing seemed more dangerous than shaking. Wren's grip was dry and exact. "The river leaves in three days from Lowmark. Rest. Eat. Chase whatever weather you need to chase to say goodbye to it." Another smile, thinner. "You'll want the practice. The upper Basin has weather of its own."

* * *

He walked out of the tower into late-afternoon heat and the smell of hot asphalt and river rot, and for a long time he just stood on the steps, letting the city press against him, listening to the traffic and the gulls fighting over the fish market and the distant thunder of nothing at all.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He'd expected them to shake.

He found a fuel stop on the edge of the district—one of the old ones, with a canopy patched in three colors and a proprietor who charged fair prices and asked no questions—and bought a tank of clean diesel and a bottle of something amber and cheap. He sat on the tailgate of the *Kestrel's Daughter*, watching the sun go down orange and filthy behind the towers, and drank slowly, and made the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the ninth floor.

One job. One river. One slate wiped clean.

Against that: a silence forty-three days long. Eleven people. A woman whose voice he knew, saying *beyond expected parameters* like a prayer she didn't believe in. A syndicate that wanted pictures of the future and hired its photographers from among the drowning.

The bottle was half gone when he caught himself talking aloud, the way he did sometimes when the boat was the only company.

"Ilsa," he said to the darkening flats, "what did you find up there?"

No answer, obviously. Just the wind moving through the power lines, making that low moaning chord it always made before weather, and somewhere far off—maybe real, maybe memory—a roll of thunder rolling along the horizon like something clearing its throat.

He finished the bottle, screwed the cap back onto the tank, and went below to check his charts of the Kestrel Basin, spreading them across the chart table under the yellow lamp. The river ran up through the pages like a sentence he hadn't finished reading. At the top of the largest sheet, past the narrows, past the marked rapids, the cartographers had simply stopped drawing—blank paper, and a stamped legend in faded ink: **SURVEY INCOMPLETE — REGION CLOSED BY ORDER**.

Beyond expected parameters.

He traced the blank space with one finger, feeling the dread settle into place at last, heavy and familiar as ballast. Not the dread of danger—he'd lived with that since he was nineteen, and it had never once stopped him. This was the other kind. The kind that came from knowing you'd done the wrong thing for the right reasons, and that the reasons wouldn't hold the weight for long.

Three days to Lowmark. Then the river.

He killed the lamp and lay in his bunk listening to the city breathe, and just before sleep took him, he heard it again—Ilsa's voice, calm, professional, looping in the dark behind his eyes:

*Bearing two-seven-zero. Beyond expected parameters. Reyes out.*

*Reyes out.*

*Reyes out.*
