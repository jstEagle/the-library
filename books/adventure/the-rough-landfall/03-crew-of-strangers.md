## 3. Crew of Strangers

Lowmark smelled like every river town Cyrus had ever hated: diesel and rot and the sweet-sour breath of a place that lived off what the water gave up. The town clung to the last navigable bend of the Kestrel like a man clinging to a ledge, all corrugated roofs and pilings gone soft with decades of flood. Beyond it, the river went green and wide and then narrowed into country no barge captain would take money for, and beyond *that*—two hundred kilometers of unmapped channel, three warlords' worth of tolls, and whatever had swallowed the Meridian Expedition whole.

Cyrus stood on the dock at first light with the contract folded in his jacket pocket, reading the water the way he used to read sky.

The Kestrel was running high but honest. Brown-green, carrying silt from tributaries he couldn't name, moving at maybe four knots in the channel with an undertow you could feel through the soles of your boots if you stood still long enough. A good river for leaving. A bad one for coming back. He'd watched it from the syndicate office window two nights ago while Reyes's voice played on loop—*coordinates calm, coordinates calm, static*—and he'd signed his name on a line that felt less like ink and more like a hook going through his cheek.

One job upriver. One salvage. Debt erased.

Nobody erased debt that cheap. He knew that. He signed anyway.

The boat was waiting where the syndicate's advance payment said it would be: a thirty-year-old river freighter called the *Vela*, forty feet of riveted hull and a cabin superstructure that some previous owner had welded together from the bones of three other boats. She was ugly, overbuilt, and—according to the surveyor's report tucked behind the contract—sound below the waterline, which was the only soundness that mattered. Cyrus walked her length twice, thumping the hull with his knuckles, listening for the hollow note of rot. He found none. He found a spare-parts locker full of parts that didn't match each other, a galley that smelled of old kerosene, and a chart drawer containing charts of the lower basin only, as if whoever drew them had deliberately stopped drawing at the point where the maps turned to rumor.

He was checking the fuel lines when the first of them arrived.

She came down the dock road carrying a duffel over one shoulder and a hard-shell instrument case in both arms, walking with the particular posture of someone who had been told many times she carried herself wrong. Mid-thirties, close-cropped hair, skin darkened by fieldwork rather than sunbathing, and eyes that did a rapid inventory of the boat before they ever landed on him. When they did land on him, they stayed there like a held note.

"You're Nakamura." Not a question. "Cyrus Nakamura. You chased the Veymar supercell in 'nineteen. The one that killed the Corliss team."

"I didn't kill the Corliss team."

"No," she said. "You were eleven kilometers east when it took them. I've read the reconstruction. Your positioning data was the only reason anyone understood what happened." She shifted the instrument case to her hip. "Priya Osei. The syndicate says you need a hydrologist who can read the upper basin."

"The syndicate says a lot of things."

"The syndicate also says I'm unemployable everywhere else, which is true, so let's not pretend either of us has leverage here." She came up the gangway without being invited, set the case down on the deck plates with the care of someone handling something alive, and looked around. "Where do you want the flow meters?"

"Flow meters?"

"Current profilers. Sediment samplers. Pressure transducers." She ticked them off against her fingers. "You're going upriver into a basin whose hydrology nobody has mapped since before the Meridian Expedition, during monsoon season, in a boat older than I am. Either you brought instruments or you brought faith, and faith doesn't tell you where the sandbars moved."

Cyrus studied her. There was a hunger under the flat delivery—not ambition, exactly. Something more like a person who had been locked out of the only room she'd ever wanted to be in, and had spent years pressing her ear against the door.

"Aft cabin," he said finally. "Keep them dry."

"They'll be drier than your fuel lines," she said. "Your number-three injector line is weeping. I could smell it from the dock."

She picked up her case and went aft, and Cyrus stood on the deck of his own boat feeling, distinctly, like a passenger.

* * *

Tuck arrived at noon, which meant Tuck arrived whenever Tuck had finished doing whatever Tuck had been doing, because no schedule on earth applied to him.

Cyrus spotted him before he spotted the boy—a commotion at the harbormaster's shack, raised voices, and then a slight figure in an oversized rain jacket coming down the pier at a walk that was almost a run, clutching a canvas bag to his chest like a rescued animal. Sixteen, maybe. All elbows and collarbones, dark hair cut by someone with no training and no patience, a face that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to be a child's or an adult's and so split the difference badly.

"That man," Tuck announced, jerking a thumb back up the pier, "is a thief."

"He's the harbormaster."

"He's a thief with a license. He wants forty marks a week for dock space. Forty! I told him the going rate is fifteen and he said knowledge doesn't discount, and I said—" Tuck caught Cyrus's expression and deflated slightly. "I may have offered to read his mail."

"His what?"

"His radio traffic. Everyone in Lowmark runs unlicensed sets, half of them on frequencies they don't own. I can pull his callsign out of the noise floor in ten minutes. It's not even hard. That's the *point*, that's why it's insulting—he thinks he's hidden and he's not hiding anything." The boy hugged the canvas bag tighter. Inside it, Cyrus could see the outline of a receiver, and coiled antenna wire, and something that might have been a soldering iron wrapped in a rag. "I got us twenty-five a week. And he's throwing in fresh water."

"How," Cyrus said, "did you get us fresh water."

"Because I know his daughter's callsign and I know she's been transmitting to a boy in Delver's Reach at hours her father wouldn't approve of, and I would never, ever use that information, but he doesn't know that I would never, ever use it." Tuck's grin was quick and crooked and entirely without shame. "You're Cyrus Nakamura, right? The storm chaser? I've heard your traffic. You run a clean signal. Well—you *ran* one. Two seasons ago. It's gotten sloppy lately."

"My finances have gotten sloppy lately. The signal follows."

"Tuck." The boy stuck out a hand. His grip was surprisingly firm, calloused in the places a soldering iron builds callouses. "No last name. Last names are how people find you."

"And who's looking for you?"

"Nobody anymore," Tuck said, and the grin switched off like a breaker, and for one second Cyrus saw underneath it: a kid who had learned early that being findable was the same as being catchable. Then the grin came back, brighter than before, papering over the gap. "So. Where do you want the radio shack? Somewhere with good grounding. And not next to the engine—I'll hear it in my sleep and dream about bearing failures."

"Forward cabin," Cyrus said. "And Tuck. If the harbormaster comes looking for blood—"

"He won't. He likes me. Everybody likes me eventually." Tuck was already hauling his bag up the gangway. "It's my worst quality."

* * *

Marek Voss arrived last, and he arrived the way weather arrives: no announcement, just a change in pressure.

Cyrus was arguing with Priya about ballast distribution—she wanted weight aft for trim in the current, he wanted it forward for handling in debris—and Tuck was on the roof of the cabin running antenna wire with the balance of a cat, when the dock went quiet. Not empty. Quiet. The stevedores kept working but their voices dropped, the way birds go silent before a storm front, and Cyrus turned and saw a big man standing at the head of the gangway as though the gangway had been built for him personally.

Marek Voss was perhaps forty, heavy through the shoulders, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that had stopped expressing things a long time ago and now only reported them. He wore plain clothes—canvas trousers, a gray work shirt—but everything about him was wrong for the clothes, the way a wolf is wrong for a dog's coat. He carried one bag, small and dense. He did not look at the boat. He looked at Cyrus, and the look had weight to it, physical weight, like a hand on the sternum.

"Nakamura." The voice was low and even. "Voss. Marek Voss. Syndicate security liaison. I'm assigned to assist."

"Assist."

"That's the word on my orders." Marek came up the gangway without hurrying, and the deck plates seemed to register him individually. He set his bag down beside the wheelhouse and surveyed the vessel with a single slow sweep—the hull, the rigging, the sightlines, the exits—as though pricing it for scrap. "Small crew."

"It's a small river."

"It's a long river." Marek's gaze moved on: Priya, catalogued; Tuck on the roof, catalogued; back to Cyrus. "Four of us. Good. Small crews move fast and talk less."

"We haven't talked at all yet."

"We will." He extended a hand, and Cyrus took it, and the handshake was careful, calibrated, the grip of a man who knew exactly how much force a human hand could tolerate and used precisely one unit less. "I've read your file. Storm work, salvage work, some transport. You pay attention to weather and machinery and you leave people alone. That's a useful profile for this job."

"And yours?"

"Mine?" For the first time something moved behind Marek's eyes—not warmth, but the memory of warmth, like heat radiating off a wall long after the fire's out. "Enforcement. Logistics. Retrieval. I make sure things that belong to the syndicate come home."

"And things that don't?"

"Those too." He let go of Cyrus's hand. "Where do you want me?"

The question sat there, innocent-shaped, loaded underneath. *Where do you want me* — as if the placement were Cyrus's decision, as if the assignment weren't already written somewhere in a ledger Cyrus would never see. Cyrus thought about Reyes's voice on the loop, calm coordinates dissolving into static. He thought about the collector's smile at the fuel stop, the way he'd said *one job, clean slate* like a man quoting scripture he didn't believe.

"Aft watch," Cyrus said. "Nights. You get the ugly hours."

"Fine." Marek shouldered his bag. "I sleep light anyway."

* * *

The friction started before the sun did.

They cast off at dawn, and by mid-morning the *Vela* was working upstream against a current that pushed back like a tired animal, and the arguments had already begun. Priya wanted to stop at every confluence to drop a current profiler; Cyrus wanted to make distance while the weather held. Tuck wanted the transmitter's ground wire rerouted through the keel bonding, which Cyrus couldn't authorize because he didn't understand it well enough to say yes. Marek wanted nothing, visibly, and somehow got everything he wanted anyway—his gear stowed where he liked, his watch rotation accepted without debate, his presence at the chart table tolerated because objecting to it would require explaining *why* it shouldn't be tolerated, and none of them had an answer that wasn't just instinct.

By evening of the first day they had made sixty kilometers and made enemies of each other in small, efficient ways.

It came to a head over dinner—or over the idea of dinner, which Priya had reorganized without asking. She'd repacked the galley stores by caloric density and spoilage rate, which meant the food Cyrus had staged for easy access was now buried, and when he went looking for the rice he found instead a laminated card in her precise handwriting: CONSUMPTION SCHEDULE — DAYS 1–14.

"There's no consumption schedule," Cyrus said. "There's food. People eat when they're hungry."

"People eat when they're hungry until day nine, when they're suddenly starving because somebody burned through the fats early, and then morale goes, and then judgment goes, and then you're making decisions about rapids on an empty stomach." Priya didn't look up from the flow meter she was calibrating. "We have fourteen days of margin. After that, every meal is borrowed."

"Borrowed from whom?"

"From the return trip. Which is the part everyone forgets." She looked up then, and her eyes were steady and tired and absolutely certain. "You've done river work before?"

"Enough."

"'Enough' is what people say when they've done river work downstream. Upstream rules are different. Upstream, the river gets harder the farther you go, and your fuel gets lighter, and your margin gets thinner, and the thing you're chasing gets closer." She closed the calibration case. "I've lost colleagues to 'enough,' Nakamura. I keep schedules now."

Cyrus wanted to argue. The argument was right there, easy, familiar—the captain's prerogative versus the specialist's caution, a fight he'd had a hundred times with a hundred crewmates. But underneath the irritation he recognized what she was actually saying, which was: *I have been on boats where this went wrong, and I am trying to make sure it doesn't go wrong again, and I need you to let me do the one thing I'm good at.*

"Fine," he said. "Schedule stands. But rice stays accessible."

"Rice stays accessible," she agreed, and something in her shoulders came down half a centimeter, and that was the treaty.

* * *

The trouble with Marek came later, and it came quietly, which was worse.

Second night, past midnight, Cyrus came off his bunk for water and found the aft deck occupied. Marek stood at the rail with a cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, watching the black water slide past, and he didn't turn when Cyrus's bare feet hit the deck plates—which meant he'd heard them and decided not to, which was its own message.

"Can't sleep?" Cyrus asked.

"I sleep four hours a night. Have for twenty years." Marek took a sip of the coffee he wasn't drinking. "You want to ask me something. Ask it."

Cyrus leaned on the rail beside him. The river ran dark and endless, carrying its silt toward a sea neither of them would see. Somewhere ahead, upstream, in the dark, was a dead expedition and a live secret and the shape of his own freedom, priced and waiting.

"Why does the syndicate care about salvage equipment?" Cyrus said. "Meridian's gear is five years old. Depreciated. The recovery value doesn't cover the fuel we're burning."

For a long moment Marek said nothing. The engine muttered beneath them. On the bank, unseen, something large slid into the water.

"Ask your questions about the river," Marek said finally. "Fuel. Channels. Sandbars. Weather. Ask me anything about the job in front of you, and I'll answer straight." He set the untouched cup on the rail. "But don't ask me questions about the job behind the job. Not because I won't answer. Because I'll answer honestly that I can't, and then we'll both have to live with knowing that."

It was, Cyrus would think later, the most honest thing anyone said to him that week.

"Fair enough," he said, and took the cup, and drank the coffee himself, bitter and cold.

Marek nodded once, almost approvingly, and went below, and Cyrus stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the dark water carry them upstream toward whatever was waiting.

* * *

On the third morning, Tuck found the frequency.

Cyrus was on the bow checking the anchor winch when the boy came out of the forward cabin fast, radio headphones around his neck, and the look on his face stopped Cyrus's hands. It wasn't fear. It was the expression of someone who has just heard a voice they weren't supposed to hear—wonder and dread sharing the same face.

"Cy." Tuck's voice was low, pitched under the engine noise, and his eyes flicked once toward the wheelhouse, where Marek stood at the chart table. "Come here. Now. Please."

Cyrus crossed the deck. Behind him, the Kestrel ran green and strong, carrying them north into country that ate expeditions, and the morning was beautiful, and the sky above the tree line was beginning to stack clouds in ways that Priya, in the aft cabin, was already frowning at.

"What is it?"

Tuck pressed the headphones into his hands. Through the static, faint as a pulse, a woman's voice was repeating a bearing. Calm. Patient. Looping.

Cyrus knew the voice. He'd heard it on a loop in a syndicate office, played by men who believed she was dead.

She was not dead. Or something wearing her voice was still transmitting. And the bearing pointed upstream—deeper into the basin, deeper than the contract's maps went, deeper than any salvage job had any business reaching.

Tuck was watching him, sixteen years old and holding the biggest secret on the river.

"Who else knows?" Cyrus asked quietly.

"Just you," Tuck said. "So far."

Cyrus looked upstream, where the clouds were stacking, and made the choice he would spend the rest of the book paying for.

"Keep it that way," he said. "For now."
