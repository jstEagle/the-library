## 4. Upstream Rules

The river changed its mind three times before noon.

Priya Osei had learned to read water the way other people read faces—by the small betrayals at the surface, the way a current flinched before it committed. The Kestrel's lower reaches ran brown and easy, a working river hauling silt and timber toward Lowmark like a laborer with somewhere to be. But an hour past the last mooring post, where the banks pinched between hills of red clay, it turned green. Then glassy. Then, for the length of two heartbeats, it stopped moving altogether—a long slick of slack water that made her stomach drop as if she'd stepped off a stair that wasn't there.

Then it remembered itself and pushed on, faster than before.

"Slack seam," she said, mostly to herself, marking it in the waterproof notebook strapped open on her knee. "Pressure differential from a side channel. Nothing to worry about."

"Didn't say I was worried," Marek Voss said from the stern.

He hadn't looked up from the engine housing. His hands moved in the small economy of a man who had done this exact maintenance a thousand times—checking the fuel line coupling, wiping the fitting, testing the primer bulb with two fingers. Everything about him was like that. Not competent exactly; competence took years and left calluses and stories. This was something rehearsed. A competence worn like a coat someone else had broken in for him.

Priya wrote: *M. checks fuel line every 40 min. Same sequence, same order. Military? No—more like corporate security training. Doesn't improvise.*

She was cataloguing them all. It wasn't cruelty; it was habit, and survival, and the particular discipline of a woman who had once trusted a department full of colleagues and been handed her career back in pieces. People told you who they were in their bodies long before they told you in words. Cyrus Nakamura steered with his whole spine, leaning into bends like he could argue the river into cooperation, and his eyes did a constant arithmetic—fuel, distance, weather, debt—that she recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors during her own worst year. Tuck sat amidships in a nest of radio equipment, one earphone cupped to one ear, always leaving the other ear open to the boat. A child raised by listening. Priya hadn't asked yet what Tuck was listening *for*, but the answer was already visible in the way the kid flinched at engine backfire: something loud had happened once, and Tuck had decided never again to be the last to know.

And Marek. Marek watched the banks the way a customs officer watches luggage.

"You've run this river before," she said, not making it a question.

"No."

The answer came too fast and too flat, which was itself information. She let it lie there between them and turned back to the water.

* * *

By afternoon the basin opened around them like a held breath.

Priya had read everything written about the Kestrel Basin—all of it old, most of it wrong. The survey reports described a floodplain. What unfolded beyond the clay hills was less a plain than a drowned cathedral: channels braiding and re-braiding through forest that stood knee-deep in its own reflection, kapok and fig trees shouldering up out of black water, orchids hanging like chandeliers nobody had lit in a century. Birds went up in sheets when the boat passed. The air smelled of wet bark and something mineral underneath, faintly metallic, like the smell of a struck match just before it catches.

Cyrus cut the throttle to half and let the silence have them.

"Meridian's route log puts their first camp on the east channel," he said. "Past the split at the drowned ridge." He tapped the chart taped to the console, and Priya noted that his finger stopped a half-inch short of the actual coordinates—the map was annotated in a second hand, smaller, more precise, and she suspected those annotations were Marek's contribution, or Marek's employer's.

"The east channel silts out in dry season," Priya said. "We're early enough. Two weeks later and we'd be dragging the hull."

"Two weeks later we'd also be inside the monsoon."

"Yes," she said. "Which is the part I keep coming back to."

She didn't say the rest: that no legitimate expedition scheduled itself against a monsoon window this tight, and that Meridian hadn't missed their window by accident—they'd gone silent *before* the season turned, in fair weather, with a full complement and syndicate-grade equipment. Rivers didn't eat expeditions in fair weather. Something else had.

Tuck looked up from the radio nest. "There's a harmonic on the low band," the kid said. "Been there since the split. Faint. Could be atmospheric."

"Could be?" Cyrus said.

"Is atmospheric," Tuck amended, with the precision of someone who hated being wrong more than being ignored. "But it's *organized*. Storm static doesn't hold a pitch."

Priya felt the hair rise on her forearms and told herself it was the river breeze. She wrote the time, the frequency band, the bearing. Data first. Fear could wait its turn.

* * *

They made camp that night on a clay shelf above a side slough, and Priya lay awake in the hammock listening to the basin breathe.

It did breathe. That was the thing no report had captured. The forest exhaled after dark—a slow fog rolling off the warm water into the cooler air, filling the channels like smoke filling a room—and along with it came sound: fish striking, branches groaning, and beneath it all a hum so low she couldn't tell whether it lived in the air or in her own skull. She got up twice to check the barometer. Steady. Falling, maybe, by a hair. Too little to mean anything.

Too little to mean anything yet.

On her second trip back to the hammock she passed Marek on watch. He sat with his back against a root buttress, rifle across his knees, and he wasn't watching the water. He was watching the treeline to the north—the direction they'd be traveling—with the fixed attention of a man reading a document only he could see.

"Anything?" she asked.

"No."

"You're facing away from the approach."

A pause, measured out like fuel. "Wind's from the north. Sound carries."

That was true. It was also the kind of true that a person says when they don't want to give you the real reason. Priya filed it and went back to her hammock and did not sleep so much as hover near sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn she heard him move—not to wake Cyrus for the watch change, but first, quietly, to the supply crates. She heard the lid. She heard the lid again, softer, replaced with care.

In the morning she checked nothing, because checking would tell him she'd heard. She simply added a line to her notebook: *M. inventory check, 0430, unlogged.* And under it: *Count the fuel ourselves tonight.*

* * *

The drowned ridge announced itself as a change in the water's grammar.

All morning the channels had been conversational—splitting, rejoining, gossiping around islands. At the ridge the braids pulled together into a single throat between two lines of submerged stone, and the current leaned hard into the turn, and Cyrus took it standing, weight forward, reading the boils ahead the way she imagined he used to read storm cells: for the shape of the violence underneath.

"East fork past the ridge," he called back. "Reyes's log says camp one is four kilometers up, on the left bank, terrace above flood line."

"You've memorized their campsites," Priya observed.

"I memorize everything I'm paid to find." He glanced at her, and for a moment the arithmetic behind his eyes showed through, and it was not greedy arithmetic—it was desperate, which was worse. "Four kilometers. Then we'll know if the rumors are rumors."

The east fork narrowed. The canopy closed overhead until they ran in green light, the engine note flattening against the walls of trees, and Priya watched the banks with increasing unease because the banks were *wrong*. Not dangerous-wrong. Neat-wrong. The flood debris that should have been tangled in the understory—driftwood, plastic, the river's ordinary litter—was absent. The terrace edges were clean-cut. Water this high this recently should have left scars.

"When did this flood last?" she asked.

Cyrus shrugged. "Ask it."

"I'm serious. There's no high-water mark. No debris line. Either this channel hasn't flooded in years—which contradicts the whole hydrology of the basin—or—" She stopped, because the alternative required vocabulary she didn't want to use in front of Marek.

"Or the water goes down faster than debris can catch," Tuck said quietly, from the radio nest. "Drains fast. Like it's being pulled."

Nobody answered that. The engine talked instead, steady and small in the enormous green silence.

* * *

They found the camp at midafternoon, and Priya knew before they reached the bank that something was off, because the birds weren't.

For four days the basin had erupted in wings wherever the boat went—herons lifting like thrown linen, parrots screaming objections. Here, nothing. The terrace above the left bank stood in stillness, and the stillness had texture, the way a room has texture when people have only just left it.

Cyrus brought the boat in slow. Marek was over the bow rail before it touched, rifle slung, moving up the bank in a crouch that was smooth and correct and entirely unnecessary, because whatever had happened here had happened weeks ago. Priya followed, notebook out, and made herself do what she was actually for: observe, record, resist narrative.

The camp was intact.

That was the first fact, and it refused every story she'd carried upriver. Tents collapsed but folded—guylines wrapped, stakes bagged, poles bundled and lashed. A cook shelter with its tarp rolled and clipped. A fire pit scraped cold and buried. Supply crates stacked in ranks of three, lids closed, some marked with the Meridian star-and-soundings logo faded to a ghost. If she'd come upon this site blind, she'd have guessed a planned pullout, executed by disciplined people with time to spare.

Except.

"Fuel's gone," Cyrus said. He'd pried the nearest crate: empty jerricans, caps on, neatly nested. "Every can."

"And the instruments." Priya had reached the largest tent, the operations tent, and stood in its doorway looking at the shapes left behind in the mud floor—rectangles pressed into the earth where heavy cases had sat. Four large rectangles. Around each, the ground was scuffed in a pattern she recognized from her own field seasons: the shuffle of two people levering equipment onto a stretcher or hand truck. "They packed out their core science. Under load. In order."

"In order," Marek repeated, from across the camp. He stood at the edge of the terrace looking north, and his voice had gone very even, which she was learning was his tell for intensity rather than calm. "No panic. No fight."

"There's this, though." Tuck had drifted to the far tent, the smallest, and was holding up something delicate between two fingers: a radio antenna, snapped near the base. Not torn—snapped, with a twist, the way you break a thing deliberately when you're leaving in a hurry but not a *panicked* hurry. "They killed their own set. Why would you pack everything and smash the one cheap thing?"

"So no one could listen to what frequency they switched to," Priya said, and heard her own voice come out flat.

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere downriver a bird finally screamed, once, and thought better of it.

Cyrus crouched at the buried fire pit and brushed it with a stick, gentle as an archaeologist. Beneath the ash: a scatter of ration wrappers, a cracked pair of reading glasses, and a page torn from a field notebook, water-warped, ink mostly survived. He smoothed it on his knee. Priya read over his shoulder.

*Day 9. Gravimeter readings continue to exceed instrument ceiling. R. says recalibrate; recalibrated twice. The anomaly isn't drifting, it's growing, and it is not geological in character. Ilsa wants to push to the source before the window closes. Vote tomorrow.*

Below that, in different handwriting, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*If we vote no, I go alone. — I.R.*

Ilsa Reyes. The voice on the loop in the syndicate office, calm coordinates then static. Cyrus's face did something complicated and quickly hidden, and Priya pretended not to see it, because that was a debt he owed himself, not her.

"They voted yes," she said quietly. "Obviously. They pushed on."

"Or Reyes pushed on and the rest followed," Marek said. He had come to stand over them, and he picked up the page without asking, read it, and returned it with a care that didn't match anything else about him. "Organized withdrawal at camp one. Instruments and fuel consolidated. Moving upstream in good order." He recited it like a report. He probably was one; she wondered to whom, and how, with the nearest transmitter four days behind them. "That's not a mystery. That's a logistics trail."

"It's a trail," Priya agreed. "Of people doing exactly what your employers hoped they'd do."

Something crossed his face—fast, controlled, but not fast enough. Not surprise. Recognition. She'd scored a hit on something true, and he'd chosen not to deny it, which frightened her more than denial would have.

"Careful," he said mildly.

"Always," she said. "It's my entire discipline."

* * *

They camped that night on the Meridian terrace, because Cyrus argued—reasonably—that level ground above flood line was not to be declined on principle, and because none of them wanted to say aloud the real reason, which was that the camp felt safer than the dark water did.

Priya took second watch. Before turning in, Cyrus paused beside her hammock.

"Tuck says the harmonic's stronger," he said, low. "Same pitch. Holding steady since midday." He rubbed his jaw. "You're the scientist. Give me the odds this is nothing."

Priya looked north, where the channel bent away into black trees and the fog was rising off the water in slow banners. The hum was there tonight, unmistakable now, below hearing and inside it both. Her barometer, checked an hour ago, had fallen a full millibar in conditions that permitted no honest reason to fall.

"Cyrus," she said, "rivers don't hold a pitch."

She let that sit. Then: "Whatever Meridian found, it's not downstream of us anymore. It's upstream. And it's louder than it was yesterday."

Above the treeline, far to the north, lightning flickered—once, twice—inside a sky with no clouds to hold it.
