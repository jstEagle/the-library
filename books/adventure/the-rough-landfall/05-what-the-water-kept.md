## 5. What the Water Kept

The radio didn't sleep, and neither did Tuck.

That was the arrangement nobody had said out loud but everybody understood. The boat ran on diesel and distrust by day, and by night it ran on Tuck's ears. He'd claimed the aft bench as his station—blanket over his shoulders like a hooded monk, the receiver cradled in his lap, headphones clamped down so the world went to wool and static. Priya slept curled in the wheelhouse like a comma. Cyrus took first watch at the tiller, then Marek took second, and Tuck was supposed to sleep through both.

Tuck had never been good at supposed to.

* * *

The Kestrel Basin at night was a country of sound. That was the thing nobody told you about rivers—you thought they were water, but really they were noise wearing water as a coat. The current talked against the hull in low syllables. Frogs stitched the banks with their idiot sewing machines. Somewhere upstream, always upstream, thunder muttered like an old man arguing with a newspaper.

Tuck catalogued it all without thinking, the way other kids breathed. He'd learned radio before he'd learned long division, back at the relay shack where nobody asked his last name because nobody cared to. Frequencies had moods. A dead band had a specific emptiness, like a room just after someone leaves. A crowded one hummed. And a band that should be dead but wasn't—that had a texture all its own, thin and wrong, like hearing your own name called from a house that burned down years ago.

He swept the bands the way he did every night. Marine emergency: nothing but a fishing boat two days downstream complaining about its bilge pump. Shortwave numbers stations: the usual ghost alphabet, women reading digit strings for spies who were probably dead too. Weather fax from the coast, smeared into gray hieroglyphs by distance.

Then, at 4,712 kilohertz—a frequency so quiet it was practically a museum—he heard breathing.

Not his breathing. Not the river's. Human lungs, filtered through a thousand kilometers of night sky, arriving at his ears fainter than memory.

Tuck sat up so fast the blanket slid off his shoulders. He didn't move again. Moving made noise; noise made the headphones shift; and if he lost this signal now he would rather die than admit it. He eased his hand onto the fine-tuning dial and turned it a hair's width, the way you'd pet something wild.

The breathing resolved into a voice.

"—repeat, bearing three-one-seven—"

Female. Middle-aged. Clipped, tired, the diction of someone who'd spent her life being precise and had run out of patience for anything else. The signal rose and fell like something surfacing to breathe between waves.

"—three-one-seven true, past the standing water—do not follow the survey line—"

Static swallowed her. Tuck's hands were shaking. He pressed the headphones harder against his skull as if pressure could hold the voice together.

"—if anyone receives this, the readings are not instrument error—I repeat, they are NOT—"

Gone. Just carrier wave, just the hiss of the ionosphere doing its indifferent housekeeping.

Tuck sat there with his heart going like a drumline. He knew that cadence. He'd spent three nights in a syndicate office in Harrowgate listening to looped transmissions while men in good jackets watched him work, and one voice on those tapes had been different from the others—calmer, more exact, the only person on the Meridian Expedition who'd sounded like she was talking to the future instead of screaming at the present. Dr. Ilsa Reyes. The hydrologist. The one whose coordinates had dissolved into static on day eleven.

She wasn't dead.

Or she hadn't been, whenever this transmission had left her. That was the trap of radio, the beautiful terrible truth of it: signals outlived their speakers. Every night the sky carried voices from people who might be bones by now. Tuck had grown up knowing that better than anyone—the relay shack had kept a memorial tape of operators who'd gone silent mid-shift, played every year, and little Tuck had listened to dead men say *over and out* and felt closer to them than to anyone living.

But this voice had said *bearing three-one-seven*. Present tense. Instructions. You didn't give instructions to the future unless you expected the future to act on them.

Three-one-seven true. Upstream. Up-left. Into the dark part of the map.

* * *

He should have logged it. That was protocol—Cyrus's protocol, established the second night: *anything you catch, you write down, time, frequency, content, no exceptions.* Tuck had a logbook with a cracked spine full of careful entries in his cramped handwriting. This belonged in it.

His pencil hovered over the page for a long time.

Because here was the other thing, the thing coiled under his ribs like a swallowed hook: Marek Voss slept four feet away, and Marek Voss was syndicate, and syndicate men had sat in that office in Harrowgate listening to the same tapes Tuck had listened to. If Reyes was alive—if she was transmitting—if there was a bearing leading to her—then whoever held that bearing held her. And the syndicate hadn't sent an enforcer up a river full of warlords to recover *equipment*. Equipment didn't need watching. People needed watching. Witnesses needed watching.

Tuck was sixteen and had survived this long by understanding exactly one rule: information is the only currency that can't be taken off you, only traded away. Once you speak a secret, it stops being yours.

He closed the logbook without writing.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, the mist so thick the river seemed to be flowing through milk. Tuck hadn't slept. He'd spent the small hours doing arithmetic instead—not numbers, but people. Weighing them.

Priya: honest, probably. Honest people were safe but slow; she'd want to debate what to do, form a committee of one, lose six hours. Cyrus: complicated. Cyrus owed money to wolves and knew it, and Tuck had watched him do the math of desperation behind his eyes more than once. But Cyrus had also given Tuck the aft bench and the good headphones without being asked, and had stood between Tuck and Marek during the supply argument at Lowmark with a body language that said *not this one.*

Marek: no.

So it came down to Cyrus, and the question was whether telling him made the secret safer or just spread the risk. Tuck turned it over all through breakfast—hardtack and tinned fish, eaten in fog so dense they could have been eating inside a cloud, which technically they were—and finally decided the way he decided everything: by listening. When Cyrus said *we push on till noon,* his voice had a certain flatness. Worry, banked and burning low. Tuck recognized it. It was the sound of a man doing debt math with his life instead of his money.

After breakfast, Tuck found him checking the fuel lines.

"Cyrus."

"Mm." Cyrus didn't look up. His hands were busy, always busy; stillness made him itch.

"Last night. Four-seven-one-two kilohertz. Voice transmission." Tuck kept his volume exactly conversational, pitched under the engine noise, aimed at Cyrus alone. "Reyes."

Cyrus's hands stopped.

It was a small thing, the stopping. But Tuck had spent his whole life reading hands, because hands told the truth mouths were built to hide. Cyrus's hands stopped the way a man's stop when he hears his own name spoken somewhere he shouldn't be known.

"You're sure it was her." Not a question, really. A door opened one inch.

"The cadence matched the tapes. Same clipped delivery. She said—" Tuck recited it from memory, because of course he had it memorized, every word filed away in the vault behind his eyes: "*Bearing three-one-seven true, past the standing water. Do not follow the survey line. The readings are not instrument error.* Then the band closed."

"How long did it last?"

"Ninety seconds. Maybe less. It faded in and out—skip propagation, probably. She's using a low-power set and letting the atmosphere carry it, which is smart, because it means nobody can triangulate her easy." Tuck paused. "Which also means she doesn't want to be found. By most people."

Cyrus straightened up slowly. In the gray light his face looked older, the lines around his mouth cut deeper. "Who else could have caught that?"

"Anybody with a receiver pointed the right way at the right minute. Which is almost nobody." Tuck hugged the blanket tighter around his shoulders. "But Marek has a receiver. Military grade. I've seen it in his kit."

"And did Marek hear it?"

"I don't know. I was on watch. He was asleep." Tuck hesitated, and then gave Cyrus the hardest piece, the piece he'd been carrying since dawn like a stone in his shoe. "I didn't log it. Protocol says log everything. I didn't."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. The engine idled beneath them both, patient as a heartbeat.

"Why not?"

"Because a logbook can be read." Tuck lifted his chin. "Because the moment that bearing exists on paper, it belongs to whoever holds the paper. Right now it belongs to me. I'm offering it to you. That's the trade."

Something moved through Cyrus's expression—surprise, maybe, or the particular discomfort of a man realizing the kid he'd half-adopted had been running deeper calculations than anyone suspected. "You're sixteen," Cyrus said.

"I'm the one who heard her."

"Yeah." Cyrus exhaled through his nose. He glanced toward the bow, where Marek stood at the rail scanning the banks with binoculars, motionless, thorough, a man performing vigilance the way other men performed piety. When he spoke again his voice dropped even lower. "Three-one-seven. Past the standing water. She said don't follow the survey line."

"That's the part I keep turning over," Tuck admitted. "The survey line is the route the expedition filed. Everyone following their trail—including us, including whoever else is out here—is following the survey line. She's telling us the trail itself is wrong. Or watched. Or bait."

"And she's telling it to the sky, once a night, on a dead frequency, hoping the right ears are listening." Cyrus rubbed his jaw. "That's not a distress call, Tuck. Distress calls beg. That's a briefing."

The word settled between them. *Briefing.* As if Reyes had already decided someone would come, and had prepared for it, and was now conducting the arrival of her own rescue from inside whatever hole she'd crawled into.

"She knows the syndicate will come eventually," Tuck said. "She worked with them. She knows how they think. So she broadcasts on a frequency their gear wouldn't bother sweeping, at hours their operators wouldn't be listening, and she gives a bearing that contradicts their maps." He shrugged. "She's filtering. Only somebody who actually listens—who does the work—gets the message. Everybody else gets static."

"And we got it because of you."

"We got it because I don't sleep." Tuck allowed himself the smallest smile. It felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's hat. "First time being the one who knows first. It's heavier than I thought it'd be."

"It always is." Cyrus said it quietly, and for a second Tuck saw something underneath the debt-worry, some older sediment of experience, a man who had been the one who knew first about things that had cost him. "Okay. Here's what happens. Nobody logs it. Nobody speaks it aloud on this boat except you and me, and never within earshot of Marek. Tonight you sweep that frequency again, same window, and if she transmits, we get the full message. If she repeats the bearing, we treat it as real."

"And if Marek asks what we talked about?"

Cyrus looked at him. "We're checking fuel lines," he said. "That's what we talked about."

* * *

The day passed in fog and current. They passed the second abandoned campsite near noon—another ordered withdrawal, tarps folded, fire pit cold and neat, a single boot left upright on a crate like a signature. Priya photographed it with the grim attention of a woman writing a eulogy. Marek walked the perimeter twice and pronounced it clean, and Tuck watched him pronounce it and wondered what *clean* meant inside a man like that.

Tuck slept in the afternoon, actually slept, two hours of dreamless black, and woke feeling like he'd been recharged by a current only he could feel. He ate. He checked the antenna leads. And when full dark came down and the river turned to sound again, he took his place on the aft bench with the headphones and began to wait.

4,712 kilohertz. Dead. Dead. Dead.

The frogs sewed. The thunder argued upstream. Somewhere below the waterline, the river carried its cargo of silt and secrets toward a sea none of it would ever see.

At 0140, the band breathed.

"—anyone receiving, bearing three-one-seven true—"

Her voice, stronger tonight, or maybe just luckier in the skip. Tuck's hands stayed steady now. He'd paid for that steadiness with a sleepless day, and it was worth it.

"—past the standing water, white stone on the west bank—do not trust the second camp—"

*Do not trust the second camp.* Tuck's skin prickled. They'd passed the second camp twelve hours ago. Marek had walked its perimeter and pronounced it clean.

"—the readings are real—they are not instrument error—it steers the weather, do you understand, it STEERS—"

Static. Long static. Tuck leaned into it like a swimmer leaning into a wave, willing the voice back.

"—one survivor—come alone—"

And then the band closed like a door, and the frogs went on sewing, and Tuck sat in the dark with his heart hammering and the two new sentences glowing in his mind like coals.

*Do not trust the second camp.* Marek had walked it. Marek had pronounced it clean. Had Marek found something there? Taken something? Left something?

*Come alone.* Alone meaning without the syndicate. Alone meaning without Marek Voss.

Tuck pulled off the headphones. The night air was cool and smelled of diesel and wet rope. At the tiller, Marek stood his watch, a silhouette carved out of the darker dark, motionless as a post. Four feet of open deck between him and the aft bench. Four feet and everything.

Tuck waited until the watch changed—until Cyrus came forward rubbing his eyes and Marek surrendered the tiller and disappeared into the stern canvas without a word—and then he caught Cyrus's sleeve and pulled him gently, urgently, toward the bow, away from the sleeping shapes, downwind of everything.

"Same frequency?" Cyrus murmured.

"Stronger. And she added two things." Tuck kept his voice at the level of the river's murmur. "First: *do not trust the second camp.*"

Cyrus went very still. "We passed the second camp."

"I know."

"Marek searched it."

"I know that too."

They looked at each other in the dark, and Tuck watched Cyrus arrive at the same cold intersection he'd been sitting at since dawn: the possibility that the enforcer aboard them had already collected whatever the expedition had cached, or marked the site for the syndicate, or worse—left something. A beacon. A tracker. A little box whispering their position to men in good jackets a hundred miles downstream.

"Second thing," Tuck said. "She said *one survivor. Come alone.*"

Cyrus closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, the fog had come off the water and wrapped the boat, and his face in the gloom looked like a decision being made in slow motion.

"Alone," he repeated.

"She means without him." Tuck tipped his head, barely, toward the stern. "Cyrus—she knows he's coming. How does she know he's coming? She's a hundred miles upstream in a cave somewhere. Unless—" and the thought finished itself in Tuck's head even as he spoke it, arriving cold and complete, "—unless she already knows what the syndicate sends. Because she's seen it before. Because she knows exactly what kind of boat follows her voice."

The river talked against the hull. Upstream, the thunder stopped arguing and started something else—something longer, lower, a note held past all reason, like the sky clearing its throat.

Cyrus stared north into the dark, toward a bearing only two people on this boat carried, and Tuck watched him understand that the secret had just changed weight class. It was no longer information anymore. It was a live wire, and they were both holding it, and somewhere behind them in the canvas a man trained to erase witnesses was sleeping four feet from the only two people who knew he was the reason the witness had said *alone.*

"Tomorrow," Cyrus said finally, very quietly, "you and I are going to have a conversation about the second camp. Just you and me. Before we make camp. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Tuck—" Cyrus's hand landed on his shoulder, heavy and warm. "You did right, keeping it off the page. But it gets heavier from here. Knowing first always does."

Tuck nodded. He went back to his bench and put the headphones on over ears that weren't listening for anything anymore, because the important thing had already come through, and lay back and stared at a sky he couldn't see through the fog.

Upstream, three-one-seven true, a woman was waiting. Behind him, four feet away, a man was sleeping who couldn't ever know.

Tuck closed his eyes and discovered that being the one who knows first feels, in the end, exactly like being alone on a crowded boat—every sound doubled, every silence loaded, the whole night tuned to a frequency nobody else can hear.

The thunder upstream held its long note.

And held it.
