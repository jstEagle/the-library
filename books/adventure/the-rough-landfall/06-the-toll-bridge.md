## 6. The Toll Bridge

The river told them a day in advance.

Priya saw it first—a change in the water's color, the green going thin and chalky where the current slowed to drag itself over some submerged shelf of silt. She stood at the bow with her arms crossed against the morning damp and said, "We're coming up on something," the way another person might say *it's going to rain*, and Cyrus had learned enough in six days on the Kestrel to take her word as gospel.

Tuck heard it too, though what Tuck heard was not water at all. The kid had been running the receiver in sweeps since dawn, headphones clamped tight, one boot hooked over the rail, and around midmorning the kid pulled them off and frowned at the river like it owed an explanation.

"Traffic," Tuck said. "Way upstream. Short bursts, encrypted or just sloppy—I can't tell which. And a beacon on a loop. Old pattern. Pre-war coast guard stuff."

"Distance?"

"Hard to say. Sound carries weird in the gorge country." Tuck tapped the headset against a knee. "But if I had to guess? A day's run. Maybe less."

Marek said nothing from his seat by the engine housing, which was how Marek said most things. He was cleaning a pistol that never seemed to need cleaning, the cloth moving in small precise circles, and Cyrus caught himself watching those hands and wondering—not for the first time—what they were for when they weren't pretending to be idle.

They rounded a bend near dusk and there it was: the toll bridge.

It should not have existed. That was Cyrus's first thought—that nothing this permanent had any business standing above Lowmark, where the maps went vague and the river charts dissolved into rumor. It spanned the Kestrel where the channel narrowed between two walls of black basalt, a lattice of salvaged steel and cable and timber, three decks high, hung with lanterns that burned a soft amber against the coming dark. Nets sagged beneath it like the shed skins of enormous animals. At its center hung a platform on chains, lowered now almost to the water, and on the platform stood figures—six, maybe eight—holding long guns with the easy verticality of people who had never once needed to hurry.

Above them all, at the bridge's crown, a banner snapped in the wind. Grey cloth, no sigil, just a single stitched line of white that might have been a river seen from above, or a scar.

"Well," Priya said quietly. "That's new since my surveying days."

"You've been up here before?" Cyrus asked.

"I've been *near* here before." Her jaw tightened. "There wasn't a bridge. There wasn't anything. That's rather the point of the upper basin."

Marek finally spoke, without looking up from the pistol. "Cut the engine. Drift in. They'll shoot anything under power."

Cyrus looked at him. "You know this place."

"I know places like this place." Marek racked the slide once, a sound like a knuckle cracking, and set the pistol down on the deck between them, visible, deliberate. "There's always a toll. The only question is the currency."

* * *

They drifted in on the last of the current, engine cold, hands visible, and the platform rose to meet them on groaning chains until it hung level with their gunwale. Up close the bridge smelled of creosote and woodsmoke and, underneath it, something mineral and strange—the same faint sharpness Cyrus had noticed in the water for the past day, like licking a battery.

The guards were younger than he expected. Two of them couldn't have been more than nineteen, sunburnt, barefoot, cradling rifles older than they were. None of them spoke. They didn't need to; the muzzles did the talking, angled down with a courtesy that was somehow worse than menace.

And then the crowd at the back parted, and Mother Gant came forward.

Cyrus had built a picture of her on the way in—had to, the way he built pictures of storms—and every part of the picture was wrong. He'd imagined bulk, bluster, a warlord's theater. What stepped onto the platform was a small woman in her sixties, lean as a heron, wrapped in a grey shawl, with a face so ordinary it took a moment to understand why every guard on the platform kept glancing at her the way sailors check the sky. Her eyes were pale, nearly colorless, and utterly still. They did not move the way eyes move. They arrived where they looked.

"Cyrus Nakamura." She said it before anyone had introduced him, in a voice like dry paper. "Salvage contract, Vane paper, four souls aboard, running light on fuel since the flood." A pause, thin as a blade. "You lost your spare canisters at the confluence. Sloppy."

His mouth went dry. Behind him he heard Tuck shift, heard Priya's breath catch. Marek didn't move at all.

"How—" Cyrus began.

"The river tells me things." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone who found the joke funny in a way no one else ever would. "Everything that floats past my bridge is a message. Debris is a message. Fuel drums are a message. Dead men are very loud messages." She gestured, and a guard threw a line; Cyrus caught it on instinct and hated how obedient his hands were. "Tie off. Come up. All of you. My bridge eats no one who pays."

"And the ones who don't pay?" Tuck asked, before Cyrus could stop them.

Gant turned those still eyes on the kid, and something in her face softened—or rearranged itself into the shape of softness. "Ah. The radio child. I heard you sweeping my frequencies this morning. Very tidy work. You missed my low band, though." She let that sit while Tuck went pale. "The ones who don't pay, little one, are how the river gets its messages. Come."

* * *

The top deck of the bridge was a world unto itself—lantern-lit, hung with drying nets and wind chimes made of spent shell casings, a long table under an awning where tea steamed in dented tin pots. Gant sat at the head of it and had them seated around her like children at a lesson, which Cyrus slowly understood was exactly what this was.

She poured the tea herself. Six cups. Her hands were steady and quick and he noticed, with a professional's eye for weather, that she watched the sky through the awning's gap more often than she watched her guests.

"You're going upriver," she said. Not a question.

"We're a salvage operation," Cyrus said. "Recovering equipment from—"

"From Meridian." She set down the pot. "Yes."

The name landed on the table like a dropped tool. Priya went rigid. Tuck's hand drifted toward the headset hanging around their neck and stopped halfway, caught by some instinct about what reaching for a radio meant in this place.

"You know them," Cyrus said carefully.

"I know *of* them, the way I know of floods before they arrive." Gant sipped her tea. "Eleven months ago, eleven well-fed strangers came up my river with syndicate money and university manners. They paid their toll in information, as everyone does. They told me their instruments, their route, their schedule for return." Her colorless eyes lifted. "They were polite. They were thorough. Dr. Reyes had kind eyes. She asked me about the storms in the gorge—the standing one, the one that doesn't travel—and she listened to my answer the way few educated people listen, which is to say she wrote it down." A pause. "I liked her. I mention this so you understand that what happened next gives me no pleasure."

"What happened next?" Priya asked quietly.

"They went up." Gant set down her cup. "And the river ate them."

Silence, except for the shell-casing chimes turning overhead in the wind, a sound like distant, broken applause.

"That's a figure of speech," Cyrus said.

"Everything is a figure of speech until it happens to you." Gant folded her hands. "I have sat on this bridge for nineteen years, Mr. Nakamura. I have counted forty-one expeditions come up this river chasing one thing or another—gold, timber, rebels, science. Thirty-three came back down. Do you know what the eight had in common?" She didn't wait. "They all went past the gorge. Every one. The river above the standing storm does not want visitors. It bends the weather against them. It moves the channels in the night. It sends fog that has no business existing in this season. My own runners won't go past the second bend, and my runners would charge their own mothers for passage." She leaned forward slightly. "So. Salvage contract. Vane paper. Going upriver, past the gorge, toward the source." The words *the source* came out flat and careful, like she was setting down something fragile. "I will ask you plainly, because plainness is the only currency I respect: what do you expect to find of Meridian that the river left?"

Cyrus opened his mouth—and Marek's hand closed gently on his forearm under the table. Not gripping. Just resting there, a weight like a door being quietly shut.

"Ma'am," Marek said, and his voice was different—softer, rounder, a voice Cyrus had never heard him use, with a rural lilt in it that had no business living in that man's throat. "With respect, we're not after treasure. Half our gear's gone downriver already and the rest is held together with prayer. We're after bodies. The syndicate wants closure for the families. Names, effects, a manifest of what was lost so the insurers stop writing letters." He shook his head, rueful, tired, a man worn down by other people's grief. "Nobody upstairs cares about minerals or storms. They care about paperwork. I'm the poor devil sent to produce it."

Every word was smooth. Every word fit every other word like machined parts. Cyrus sat there listening to a lie being assembled in real time, seamless as poured concrete, and could not find a single seam—and the horror of it wasn't the lie. The horror was that he couldn't even tell what had been traded away inside it. Whether Marek had confirmed their route, or denied it. Whether he'd named the gorge, the source, Reyes. Whether he'd given Gant exactly enough truth to make the whole fabric load-bearing.

Gant studied Marek for a long moment. Then she laughed—a dry, rusted sound, genuine, awful.

"Oh, you're good," she said. "You're very good. Nineteen years I've sat at this table and I can smell a lie like smoke, and yours has almost no smell at all. Almost." She picked up her cup again. "Almost is enough. Toll's paid."

"Just like that?" Tuck blurted.

"Just like that." Gant's eyes moved to the kid, and again that terrible imitation of warmth. "Information is the only honest currency, child. Goods can be stolen back. Coin can be counterfeited. But a story, once told, is *spent*—and whoever holds it decides what it buys." She rose, and the whole platform seemed to rise with her, guards straightening along both rails. "You may pass at first light. My pilots will walk you through the narrows—they'll want paying, but that's goods, boring goods, fuel will do." She paused at the edge of the lamplight, a small grey woman against a big dark sky. "One thing more. Free of charge, since you've paid so handsomely."

She turned. The lanterns caught her eyes and made them flash white, like ice catching sun.

"When you find what's left of Meridian—and you will find it, the river always gives back the bones eventually—count the graves. Then count the boots. If the numbers don't match, turn around." A beat. "Because that means somebody walked out of the basin alive, and everything my runners and I have heard drifting down these eleven months says the river has been *quiet* up there. Too quiet. Quiet rivers are thinking." She pulled the shawl tighter. "Good night, salvage men. Sleep well. The bridge charges extra for nightmares."

* * *

They were given a corner of the lower deck, behind a net partition, with blankets that smelled of creosote and fish. The guards withdrew. The lanterns dimmed one by one along the bridge's length until only the crown light burned, and beneath them the Kestrel ran black and patient, carrying its messages.

Cyrus lay awake listening to the water and doing arithmetic he hated.

*Marek paid the toll,* he thought. *Not me. Not us. Marek.* And whatever he'd spent, Gant had accepted it as true enough to open the road—which meant either Marek had lied brilliantly about nothing, or he'd lied brilliantly about something, and Cyrus had no way of knowing which, and wouldn't, possibly, until the knowing was fatal.

Across the partition, he heard Marek turn over. Once. A man sleeping soundly, or a man practicing the sound of it.

Then, faintly, from the bow where Tuck had burrowed with the receiver despite explicit orders, a whisper of static—and threaded through it, so faint Cyrus might have dreamed it, a voice. Female. Calm. Repeating numbers.

A bearing. Pointing upstream.

He lay in the dark with his heart going hard and decided—consciously, deliberately, the first entry in a ledger he'd be keeping the rest of his life—not to ask yet.

The river ran on beneath the bridge, quiet, thinking.
