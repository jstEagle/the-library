## 7. Rapids and Reckoning

The river told lies in the morning.

Priya had learned that much in her first years of fieldwork, back when the journals still printed her name and the universities still returned her calls. Rivers lied the way rivers always had—by omission, by calm. A reach of water that looked like polished pewter at dawn could hide a drowned fence line, a collapsed bank, a whole village's worth of debris rolling beneath the surface like cargo. The Kestrel Basin was a better liar than most. It lay wide and brown-green under a sky the color of unpolished brass, and it gave away nothing.

She stood at the bow of the *Marrow*, one hand on the rail, the other holding her field notebook open against the wind, and she read the water the way other people read scripture: closely, skeptically, looking for the verse that would save them.

Behind her, Cyrus ran the engine at half-throttle, conserving what fuel they had left after Lowmark's inflated prices. Tuck sat amidships with headphones clamped over one ear, the radio's guts spread across an ammunition crate like a surgeon's tray. And Marek Voss stood at the stern with his arms folded, watching the banks slide past with the patient, appraising stillness of a man counting exits.

Four days upriver from Lowmark. Four days of heat and insects and the slow, grinding intimacy of strangers sharing twelve meters of boat.

Priya catalogued them the way she catalogued everything—because pattern was safety, because if you could name a thing you could survive it.

Cyrus Nakamura read weather the way she read hydrology, with his whole body. He'd stand on deck for hours doing nothing but watching cloud lines, and when he finally spoke—"storm by nightfall," or "clear through tomorrow"—he was right often enough that she'd stopped checking him against the barometer. There was something desperate in his competence, though. Men who were that good at reading the sky were usually running from something on the ground.

Tuck was sixteen and slept four hours a night and heard things in the static that Priya couldn't hear even when they were pointed out to her. The kid flinched whenever Marek moved too fast, then covered it with jokes. Smart. Watchful. Afraid of exactly the right person.

And Marek—

Marek was the problem she hadn't solved yet. Everything about him was correct. His knots were textbook. His watch rotations were disciplined. When they'd grounded briefly on a sandbar two days ago he'd been off the boat and pushing before Cyrus finished giving the order, and he'd braced the hull with a technique Priya had only seen in military salvage manuals. Too practiced. Competence like that wasn't learned on fishing boats. It was drilled into you somewhere with concrete floors and bad lighting, and then it followed you around like a smell.

He caught her looking at him now. He didn't smile. He just nodded once, courteous as a customs officer, and went back to watching the treeline.

Priya turned to the river and made herself useful instead of suspicious. Suspicion could wait. The water wouldn't.

* * *

The first wrong thing was the birds.

They'd been seeing egrets all morning, working the shallows in ones and twos, elegant and indifferent. Around midday the egrets vanished—all of them, within the space of twenty minutes—and their places were taken by swifts, dozens of them, flying low and hard *upstream*. Not feeding. Traveling. Moving against the current with the urgent, purposeful flight of animals relocating ahead of something.

"Storm birds," Tuck said, pulling off one headphone. "My gran used to say swifts flying hard means rain inside a day."

"Your gran was right about most things," Priya said, and wrote down the time, the species count, the direction. Then she did something she'd been doing more and more often since Lowmark: she checked the sky against her instruments, and her instruments against each other, hunting for the discrepancy she could feel but not yet prove.

The barometer was falling. That was normal; the monsoon season was building toward landfall somewhere south of them, and the whole basin breathed shallow under the pressure of it. But the wind was wrong.

The prevailing winds here ran east to west this time of year—the trade flow that pushed every storm system in the basin along its long track toward the interior. Every river bend, every tree's lean, every old settler's map confirmed it. The wind should have been at their backs, steady, carrying the smell of the lowlands they'd left behind.

Instead it came from the west. From upstream. From the direction they were traveling, faint but persistent, warm and smelling of wet stone and something else—something mineral, sharp, almost electrical, like the air after lightning finds sand.

"Cyrus." She raised her voice over the engine. "When did the wind shift?"

He'd already noticed. She could tell by the way he stood, weight forward, head tilted like a man listening to a conversation across a room. "Sometime last night," he said. "I figured it for a local eddy. Gorge country does strange things with airflow."

"It's not an eddy." She held out the notebook so he could see the entries. "It's been backing us for thirty hours. Steady gradient, no oscillation. Eddies breathe. This doesn't."

Cyrus took the notebook, read it, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done—the same reluctant, rising unease. "Wind opposing the trades for thirty hours," he said slowly. "That's not local. That's a system."

"That's my point. Where's the system?" She swept her arm at the sky. It was high, hazy, broken cumulus drifting lazily eastward with the trades above them, while the surface wind slid stubbornly beneath, upstream, contrary. "Look at the cloud deck. It's moving the wrong way from the wind we're standing in. Two layers, two directions. Something below is generating its own circulation."

"Like a firestorm," Marek said quietly.

They all turned. He was still at the stern, arms folded, but his eyes had changed—not duller, sharper, the look of a man who has just remembered something he was paid to forget.

"Big fires make their own weather," he went on, evenly. "Convection column, inflow winds, pyrocumulus. I've seen it burn whole valleys' worth of sky into a single cloud."

"Yes," Priya said. "Exactly like that. Except there are no fires up here. So what's burning?"

Nobody answered. The engine thudded. The swifts streamed past overhead, going where the birds went when the world stopped making sense.

* * *

They made camp that evening on a gravel bar backed against a bluff—a defensible spot, Cyrus said, though against what he didn't specify. Priya cooked rice and dried fish over a spirit stove while Tuck reassembled the radio and Marek cleaned a rifle that he claimed was for crocodiles, which fooled nobody, and Cyrus climbed the bluff with his binoculars and stayed there until full dark.

He came down looking older.

"There's a cell building northwest," he said. "Upstream. Isolated, rotating structure—it shouldn't be there. The shear profile doesn't support organization like that. It's like..." He paused, and Priya recognized the expression of a man reaching for language that didn't exist yet. "It's like watching a whirlpool form in a bathtub with the drain closed."

"The source region," Priya said. Everyone had heard the word by now; the syndicate's briefing documents had named it only as coordinates, a blank space on the map labeled SOURCE REGION — DO NOT SURVEY, which was the kind of instruction that guaranteed surveying. "Whatever Meridian found is up there. And whatever it found is doing *that*."

"To weather?" Tuck asked. "Weather doesn't have a source. Weather's just... the atmosphere arguing with itself."

"So is geology," Priya said. "Until you find the fault."

She slept badly, in the way of people whose life's work has begun whispering to them from a hilltop. Twice she woke to check the barometer, and twice the needle had fallen further, smooth and steady, a patient descent with none of the stuttering that preceded ordinary squalls. At some point in the small hours she heard Marek's watch change over with Cyrus's, heard the soft exchange of words, and lay still, memorizing the sound of it, filing it away.

* * *

The flood came at 3:40 in the afternoon the next day, out of a sky with not a single storm cloud in it.

Priya would replay those minutes for the rest of her life, and in every version the horror was the same: the *absence* of warning. No wall cloud. No distant thunder. No green-black light. Just the river itself, the broad, sluggish, trustworthy river, suddenly flinching.

She felt it before she saw it—a tremor through the hull, a shudder in the water like the whole river had been struck. Then the sound arrived, rolling up the channel from behind the next bend: a deep, grinding roar, the sound of a landslide given a voice.

"Off the channel!" Cyrus was already shouting, already hauling the wheel over. "Get us to the cutbank—NOW—"

The *Marrow* swung for the bank as the bend came around, and Priya saw it.

A tributary mouth opened on the western shore—a canyon slot so narrow she'd marked it that morning as a dry ravine, a wrinkle in the terrain, nothing. Out of that wrinkle came a wave.

Not a cresting wave, not a roller—a *wall*, brown and churning, thick with trees and boulders and the shredded canopy of some forest it had eaten on its way down, moving at the speed of a galloping horse and filling the tributary mouth from wall to wall. Behind it came more water, and behind that, the roar resolved into a thousand smaller sounds: rockfall, tearing wood, the river's own bed being rearranged by force.

An unseen tributary. A catchment high in the western ranges, rained on by a storm they'd never seen, in a sky that had shown them nothing. The flood had traveled kilometers of narrow canyon in minutes, gathering everything loose, arriving here as a moving hill of water in air without a cloud.

"Hold on!" Marek's voice, and then his hands—shoving Tuck flat against the deck plates, one arm hooked through the rail stanchion, anchoring the boy with casual, enormous strength. "Everyone down! Line and cleat!"

Priya got her wrist through the bowline loop as the wall hit.

The world became brown noise. The *Marrow* rose as if lifted by a god's impatient hand, surfed sideways down the face of the surge, spun twice—Priya felt the keel leave the water entirely, a sickening airborne lurch—and slammed down into the churned aftermath, shipping half a ton of water across the deck. Something huge and soft struck the hull—a waterlogged tree, a dead animal, she never knew. The engine screamed, coughed, screamed again. Cyrus fought the wheel with both hands and his whole furious will, reading the chaos the way he read clouds, finding the downstream seam in the turbulence, threading them between two spinning rootballs the size of cars.

Then, as fast as it had risen, the surge passed beneath them, spent, spreading, and the river settled into a new and higher mood, carrying them along in its wreckage-strewn wake.

For a long moment nobody moved. The *Marrow* wallowed, low in the water, listing slightly to port. Water sloshed ankle-deep across the deck. Somewhere aft, the engine ticked and knocked with a sound no healthy engine makes.

"Tuck." Cyrus's voice came out scraped raw. "Report."

Tuck sat up, pale, hair plastered with mud, and for once said nothing clever. He just pointed aft.

The spare fuel drums had been lashed to the stern rail. Two of the lashings had parted. The drums themselves were gone—swept overboard in the spin, swallowed by the brown water without so much as a ripple to mark them. Of the four they'd carried out of Lowmark, two remained.

Half their fuel. Gone in ninety seconds, into a river that didn't even keep it.

Priya knelt and put her hand flat on the deck, feeling the *Marrow*'s heartbeat through the planks, and did the arithmetic she knew Cyrus was doing too. Half their fuel meant half their range. Half their range meant the source region was now at the absolute edge of possible, with no margin for current, no margin for another flood, no margin for the return journey at all unless something changed.

"We can't go back the way we came on what's left," she said quietly. "Not against this current. We go forward or we don't go anywhere."

"I know," Cyrus said.

"And Cyrus—" She stood, wiped mud from her hands, and made herself say the rest of it plainly, because someone had to, and because saying it aloud was the only way to test whether it was real. "That flood came out of a clear sky. A catchment storm we never saw, on a day with no convective activity over the main channel. I've been running hydrometeorological surveys for eleven years. Floods don't come from nowhere. Rain comes from somewhere. That rain came from a storm system that *shouldn't exist*—fed by convection that's forming against the prevailing winds, in a region generating its own inflow, its own circulation, its own weather."

She looked upstream, toward the haze-shrouded gorge where the compass of the sky bent backward, and felt the awe move through her like the flood had moved through the river—vast, impersonal, magnificent—and then curdle, in her chest, into something colder.

Whoever had built the Meridian Expedition's instruments had built them to measure weather. Whatever Reyes had found up there, it was measuring *back*.

"Eleven years," she said, "and I've never seen a watershed manufacture its own storm. If that deposit does what I think it does—if it can seed convection, drive circulation, hold a standing system against the trades—then it isn't a mineral discovery. It's a lever. A lever big enough to move monsoons. And levers like that don't stay scientific for long."

Marek said nothing at all. But when Priya glanced at him, she saw that he was looking upstream too, with an expression she filed away with all the others: not wonder, not fear.

Recognition.

* * *

They ran the rest of the afternoon on the flooded river's new momentum, riding the elevated current while it lasted, because Priya had argued—hard, with numbers—that the surge water would carry them kilometers farther than their remaining fuel ever could. Cyrus had agreed. Even Marek had agreed, which unsettled her more than the flood had.

At dusk they tied off in the lee of a rock island, and Tuck, who had been silent for hours in the way of the young processing something too large, finally spoke.

"The transmission," the kid said. "Reyes's voice. The bearing she kept repeating." Tuck looked up, and in the last light the exhaustion and the resolve on that young face were the same thing. "It points straight into the gorge. Straight at whatever's making the sky up there run backwards."

Priya looked at Cyrus. Cyrus looked at the fuel gauge, then at the darkening northern horizon, where the isolated cell they'd seen the night before had grown a towering, anvil-capped silhouette, lit from within by silent, continuous lightning—flashing like a lighthouse for a harbor that existed nowhere on any chart.

"Well," Cyrus said at last. "Now we know where we're going."

Above them, against all sense, against every wind that should have governed that sky, the storm stood still.
