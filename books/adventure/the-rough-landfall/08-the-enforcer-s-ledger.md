## 8. The Enforcer's Ledger

The watch belonged to Marek from midnight until four, and he took it the way he took everything: without complaint, without ceremony, without leaving anything behind that could be read.

He sat in the stern of the *Kestrel's Mercy* with the boat tied off to a drowned tree, the river sliding past black and patient. The upper basin had gone quiet in the way that made his neck prickle—not silence exactly, but a held breath, as if the whole valley were waiting for permission to exhale. Insects droned against the lamp glass. Somewhere across the water a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

Marek Voss did not believe in omens. He believed in ledgers.

So he did what he always did on watch when the others slept: he opened the ledger in his head and reviewed the entries.

*Entry one.* Confirm the deposit exists. Simple. The Meridian Expedition had found something at the source of the Kestrel—something that bent weather around itself like light bending around mass—and the Syndicate needed to know if it was real before they committed ships and men and money to taking it. Marek's job was verification. Cores, readings, coordinates. Proof you could put in a case and carry downstream.

*Entry two.* Ensure no witnesses return.

He recited it the way he'd recited it for eleven years, in the flat interior voice that had once been a comfort and was now just a habit, like a rosary said by a man who no longer believed in the saint. No witnesses return. It was clean, as orders went. Cleaner than most. There was no torture in it, no theater. It was arithmetic: five people went up the river, and the number of people who came back down was zero. The river would do most of the work. The monsoon would do the rest. And whatever the river and the sky left unfinished, Marek would finish himself, because that was what he was for. That was the whole of what he was for.

He had killed men in alleys in Corvane and men on barges on the Serein and once, memorably, a man in a church, though the church had been empty except for God, and God had kept His counsel. He had never enjoyed it. Enjoyment was a defect, and defects got enforcers retired, and retirement in the Syndicate was a word with only one meaning. He had never refused an order either. Refusal was a larger defect.

Eleven years, no defects. A perfect ledger.

He looked upriver, toward the gorge he couldn't yet see but could feel—the way you feel stairs in the dark after the last one, that wrongness in the air where the world should keep going and doesn't. The clouds there were doing something clouds weren't supposed to do. Priya had said so that evening, her voice carefully stripped of wonder, which was how he knew she was full of it. Storms forming against the prevailing wind. Weather with a will. If it was true, then entry one was already half-written. The deposit existed. You didn't need instruments to confirm a thing like that; you needed only to stand under a sky that had stopped obeying its own laws and feel the hair rise on your arms.

Which meant entry two was coming due.

Marek turned the ledger page in his mind and found, for the first time in eleven years, that the ink wouldn't take.

It started, as these things apparently did, with something small. With Tuck.

The boy was asleep on the cargo deck, curled against a coil of rope with the radio headset still half over one ear, because Tuck slept the way Tuck did everything—attached to some machine, listening even while unconscious, as if the airwaves might whisper something worth waking for. Sixteen years old, maybe. No last name, no town that claimed him, no papers. A kid the world had issued to nobody, who had therefore issued himself to the first boat that would have him.

Marek had catalogued him weeks ago, the way he catalogued everyone: threat assessment negligible, utility moderate, loyalty purchasable. That was the entry. That was supposed to be the whole entry.

But somewhere between Lowmark and the abandoned campsite, the catalogue had begun to fill in with things that had no operational value. The way Tuck talked to the radio like it was a horse he was gentling. The way he ate—fast, defensive, one arm curved around his plate like a wall, a habit learned in some place where food was taken. The way he'd laughed three days ago at Cyrus's terrible impression of Mother Gant, a laugh that came out of him whole and unguarded, startling them all, startling Tuck most of all, as if he'd surprised himself by still owning one.

None of that belonged in a ledger. Ledgers held numbers and names and dates of expected death. They did not hold laughs.

Marek shifted his weight and the boat rocked and Tuck stirred, murmured something syllabic, and pulled the headset tighter over his ear like a child pulling a blanket.

*Ensure no witnesses return.*

Tuck was a witness. Tuck had seen the campsite, heard the transmissions, knew the bearing upstream. By the letter of his orders, the boy was already dead; he simply hadn't been informed yet. The date was in the ledger. Marek had written it himself, weeks ago, in the dry season, when Tuck was still just a line item with a radio.

He tried to read the order again and found he was instead reading the boy. Counting breaths. Noting the way his fingers twitched in sleep, chasing signals down in dreams.

This was new. This was a defect.

Marek got up quietly and walked the length of the boat, checking the lines, checking the fuel drums, checking the things a careful man checks, because motion was easier than thinking. Cyrus slept in the bow with his boots on, always, a man who'd learned somewhere that you might need to run before you finished waking. Priya slept sitting up against the instrument case, her notebook open on her chest, rising and falling. Ilsa Reyes wasn't aboard yet—but she would be, in a cave or a wreck or a grave, and when they found her the question would stop being theoretical. Witnesses multiplied as you went upstream. That was the cruel mathematics of salvage: every answer you found added a person to the list of people who couldn't be allowed to carry it home.

And Cyrus. Cyrus Nakamura, who owed the Syndicate enough to drown in, who had signed the contract knowing it was wrong because desperation makes honest men literate in their own corruption. Marek had watched him for weeks now, waiting for the man to break the way debtors break—mean, or cowardly, or both. Instead Cyrus had done something worse. He'd gotten decent. He'd given Tuck the good rations without announcing it. He'd stood beside Priya in the rain and listened to her impossible data like it mattered, like she mattered. He was paying attention to people the way Marek paid attention to exits.

That kind of man was dangerous, in the end. Not because he'd betray you, but because he'd make you want to be worth not betraying.

Marek crouched at the rail and let the river sound fill him. Below the surface, he knew, the current ran hard toward the gorge, toward the standing storm, toward the thing the Syndicate wanted the way empires want coastlines. Somewhere downstream, in offices with filtered air, men were already drafting the second expedition, and the third, and the annexes and the exclusivity contracts and the quiet arrangements with governments that would turn a canyon full of charged vapor into a lever long enough to move three nations' harvests. They didn't think of any of it as violence. They thought of it as ownership. Violence was just the paperwork of ownership done quickly.

He had been their paperwork for eleven years.

*Entry two,* he wrote in his mind, and his hand—his actual hand, resting on the cold rail—would not move.

Here was the truth he circled without landing on: he did not know what he would do. In eleven years he had always known, because knowing was the luxury of a man with no interior life, and now, watching a sixteen-year-old sleep with a radio clutched to his ear like a talisman, Marek Voss had discovered he possessed an interior life after all, and it was a ruin, and it was awake, and it was asking questions.

What if the boy lived. What if the hydrologist published. What if Nakamura burned the data and rowed home poor and clean. What if Marek Voss, whose name was on no document anywhere, whose existence the Syndicate could deny with a phone call—what if he stepped off the ledger entirely and became, retroactively, all those years, a man who had never existed, and let someone new walk out of the ruin?

Retirement. The word with one meaning. Or the other meaning, the one that didn't exist, the one he'd never let himself say even inside his own skull.

A fish broke the surface somewhere near the drowned tree. The lamp hissed. Tuck sighed in his sleep and turned his face away from the light, trusting, absolutely, the way only the young and the reckless and the orphaned trust a man on watch—that whatever stood between them and the dark would stand there all night.

Marek realized he was gripping the rail hard enough to hurt.

He made himself open the ledger one more time. *Entry two: ensure no witnesses return.* He looked at it for a long moment, the way a man looks at a bridge he has crossed ten thousand times, checking the planks.

Then, for the first time in eleven years, Marek Voss left an entry blank.

No decision. No date. Just white space where the boy's death was supposed to be written, and the pen hovering, and the night going on around him, and upriver—faint, at the very edge of hearing, or perhaps only at the edge of imagination—a low rolling sound that might have been thunder, except that thunder was supposed to come *after* the lightning, and this sound came from nowhere at all, out of a clear black sky, rolling down the gorge toward them like something answering.

Tuck's eyes opened in the dark.

"Marek," the boy whispered, sitting up, headset coming alive with static. "You hear that too?"

* * *

Downriver, in an office with filtered air, a man crossed out a name and wrote another. But that is a different ledger, and it can wait until morning.

Marek stood, and took the headset Tuck offered, and listened to the storm that shouldn't exist speak its first word to them through the dark.
