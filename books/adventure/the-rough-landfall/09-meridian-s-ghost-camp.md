## 9. Meridian's Ghost Camp

The river told them before the bank did.

Cyrus noticed it first as a change in the water's color—a thread of silt-pale green bleeding in from a side channel on the eastern shore, where the Kestrel split itself around a gravel bar and forgot about it. Priya saw it too, or something else: she had been standing at the bow rail for an hour without moving, her hair whipping loose from its knot, her eyes fixed on the treeline with the particular stillness of someone reading a language no one else could hear.

"There," she said finally, and pointed with her chin because her hands were busy with the flow meter. "That cutbank. See how the vegetation breaks?"

Cyrus followed her gaze. The forest along that stretch grew dense and indifferent, wall after wall of it, but at one point—maybe two hundred meters up the channel—the green went wrong. Too even. Too young. Secondary growth, the kind that came back fast over ground that had been cleared and then abandoned.

"Camp," he said.

"Camp," Priya agreed. "Big one. Cleared maybe three, four years ago, going by the alder." She turned to look at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before—not the guarded professionalism she wore like a shell, not the bitterness that leaked through when Marek spoke. It was hunger. Bare, unguarded scientific hunger, the look of a woman who had been starved of the thing she loved for years and could suddenly smell it cooking. "Meridian's last logged position was eleven kilometers downstream of this confluence."

"Rivers move," Cyrus said.

"So do expeditions." She was already moving toward the stern, calling for Tuck to bring the boat in slow.

Marek stood at the rail near the engine housing, arms folded, watching the cutbank come closer with an expression Cyrus had learned to distrust precisely because it revealed nothing at all. The enforcer had been quiet since the toll bridge—quiet in a way that felt less like calm and more like a man conserving himself between performances. Whatever lie he'd paid Mother Gant with, whatever information had changed hands at that fortified crossing, it sat between him and Cyrus now like ballast neither of them acknowledged.

"Could be anyone's camp," Marek said. "Loggers. Poachers."

"In the middle of the Kestrel Basin?" Tuck snorted from the helm, all sixteen years of him radiating contempt. "Nobody logs up here. Nobody poaches up here. There's nothing up here worth taking except what Meridian came for, and nobody knows what that is except—" He stopped himself, glancing at Marek, and finished lamely: "Except us, I guess."

Cyrus said nothing. He brought the boat alongside the gravel bar himself, taking the wheel from Tuck with a gentle hand, because his own nerves were singing and he didn't trust the kid's hands to stay steady. The hull kissed the shallows with a sound like a held breath being released.

They stood on the bank for a moment, four people in the dripping green silence, listening to the river behind them and the nothing ahead of them.

"All right," Cyrus said. "Rules. We go in together. We touch nothing we don't photograph first. And if anything looks wrong—if anything feels wrong—we walk back to the boat and we don't explain ourselves to each other until we're downstream. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Priya said immediately.

Tuck nodded, radio scanner clutched to his chest like a talisman.

Marek shrugged. "Your contract, your rules."

* * *

The camp announced itself gradually, the way ruins do—as absence first, then detail.

A clearing perhaps eighty meters across, hemmed by second-growth alder and the pale skeletons of trees felled four seasons ago and left where they lay. Tent platforms raised on pilings against the wet, six of them, arranged around a central structure that had once been substantial: a mess shelter built from salvaged timber and tarpaulin, its frame still standing though the skin had rotted to ribbons that moved in the breeze like something breathing. A generator shed. A drying rack for instruments. A fire pit swept clean, absurdly clean, the ashes raked flat as a garden bed.

And everywhere, order.

That was the thing that got under Cyrus's skin and stayed there. Abandoned camps he knew—he'd stumbled through a dozen of them in his chasing years, camps abandoned in panic or haste or death, and they all carried the same signature: scatter. Gear dropped mid-task. Meals half-eaten. Boots gone from beside bedrolls, or bedrolls gone entirely, dragged out into the weather by whoever needed them most.

This camp had been folded shut like a letter.

Bedrolls rolled and stacked under waterproof sheeting, tied down. Cookware nested and shelved. Tools hung on their nails. On the central post of the mess shelter, someone had carved a message into the soft wood with a knife, the letters deep and deliberate:

**MERIDIAN EXPEDITION — RELOCATING UPSTREAM — ALL WELL — DAY 47**

Day 47. All well. Cyrus read it three times and each time it frightened him more than the last, because it wasn't a distress signal and it wasn't a boast. It was a courtesy. A note left for whoever came next, written by people who expected someone to come next—or who wanted whoever came next to believe they'd left in good order, walking upright, unafraid.

"They didn't run," Priya said quietly. She had drifted to the nearest tent platform and was crouched over the stacked bedrolls without touching them. "You don't pack your bedding if you're running. You don't sweep your fire pit." She looked up at him. "Cyrus, this camp was struck by choice. Deliberate. They broke camp like they were moving to better ground."

"Or like someone told them to," Marek said.

Everyone looked at him. He stood at the edge of the clearing with his hands loose at his sides, surveying the treeline the way he always did—first the shadows, then the sightlines, then the shadows again—and when he felt their attention he gave them nothing but a small tilt of his head toward the instrument rack.

"Their core instruments are gone," he said. "Seismographs, magnetometers, whatever Reyes was running her atmospheric readings on. The racks are empty. But see the dust shadows? The outlines?" He pointed, and Cyrus saw it: on the galvanized shelving inside the generator shed, pale rectangles printed in grime where equipment had sat long enough to shield the metal beneath. Large rectangles. Heavy things. "They took everything that mattered and left everything that didn't. That's not evacuation discipline. That's a salvage operation."

"You'd know," Tuck muttered.

"I would know," Marek agreed, without heat, without irony, and that was somehow worse.

* * *

It was Priya who found the journal.

She called out from the mess shelter, and her voice had gone strange—careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile. Cyrus crossed the clearing and found her standing at the trestle table beneath the ruined tarpaulin, and on the table, weighted down with a stone, lay a field journal bound in oilcloth.

"It was under the table," she said. "Centered. Like it was meant to be found." Her hands hovered over the oilcloth without opening it. "Is it right, do you think? Reading another scientist's—"

"She left it for us," Cyrus said. "Or for whoever came next. That's the same thing." He hesitated. "Open it."

Priya unwrapped the oilcloth with the tenderness of a surgeon. Inside was a standard expedition logbook, water-stained at the corners, its pages dense with Ilsa Reyes's handwriting—which Cyrus recognized instantly, painfully, the sharp forward-leaning script of a woman who wrote the way she spoke, fast and certain, correcting herself only when the corrections were important.

He had known that handwriting across a hundred shared data sheets, five years ago, in another life, before the debt and the syndicate and the slow grinding-down. Seeing it here, in this dead place, hit him somewhere below the ribs.

Priya read aloud, because some things were too heavy to read alone.

*"Day 12. Baseline surveys complete. Local barometric behavior is anomalous beyond any margin I'm willing to call error. Pressure differentials forming and collapsing within minutes across distances of less than a kilometer. Wind vectors at the gorge contradict the synoptic pattern by up to 40 degrees. Okafor thinks instrument fault. I've triple-calibrated. It is not fault."*

Page turn. The paper whispered.

*"Day 19. We have located the source region. I will not record coordinates in this log; the syndicate's interest in our funding has grown too attentive, and some numbers should not travel. What I will record is this: the readings above the vent are impossible. Not improbable—impossible. Atmospheric charge densities that should ionize the air into continuous lightning. Thermal gradients that violate every model I have ever trusted. The mountain is exhaling something, and the sky above it is not our sky anymore."*

*"No atmosphere should produce these readings. No atmosphere does—except this one."*

Priya's voice caught slightly on that line, and Cyrus understood why. Every hydrologist spent their career trusting the atmosphere to be lawful. To find lawlessness, documented so calmly, in a dead woman's careful hand—

"She was alive when she wrote this," Tuck said from the doorway. He'd come in silent, the way teenagers did, and his face was pale. "Day 19. The transmissions we heard were day 30-something. So she made it almost two more weeks past this."

"Past writing this," Cyrus corrected quietly. "We don't know what she made it past."

But even as he said it, he remembered the voice on the loop in the syndicate office—calm coordinates, then static—and the static took on a new weight. Not an ending. Maybe just a pause. Maybe a woman still upstream, still breathing, still keeping a case of survey cores between herself and the world.

He didn't say that part aloud. Hope was a supply like any other, and you rationed it.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the long amber light that slanted through the alders, cataloguing the ghost camp. Priya photographed everything—the dust shadows, the carved message, the journal pages—while Tuck climbed the mess shelter's frame to rig a temporary antenna, muttering about elevation and line-of-sight. Marek walked the perimeter with a patience that Cyrus had stopped finding reassuring and started finding clinical, checking sightlines, noting approach routes, doing enforcer arithmetic on a camp full of ghosts.

Cyrus himself ended up alone at the fire pit, looking down at the raked ash.

There was a wrongness in it he couldn't name at first. Then he could. The ash hadn't been raked recently—but it also hadn't been raked four years ago. Rain should have pitted it. Leaves should have drifted across it. Instead it sat smooth and grey and undisturbed, sheltered by the mess shelter's broken roof, preserved like everything else in this camp that refused to decay at the proper rate.

Four years, and the camp looked like it had been struck yesterday.

"Hey, Cy." Tuck's voice, low, from above. "Come look at this. Don't make it obvious."

Cyrus crossed to the shelter and looked up. Tuck crouched on the ridge beam, holding his scanner, and his eyes weren't on the device—they were on the ground beyond the clearing's northern edge, where the forest resumed.

"What am I looking at?"

"Mud. After last night's rain. Ground's soft everywhere, right? Everything that walks leaves a print." Tuck's voice stayed conversational, pitched for Cyrus alone. "Now look at the trail heading north. Upstream. Toward the gorge."

Cyrus looked. At first the forest floor was just forest floor—leaf litter, roots, the general chaos of living ground. Then his eye found the rhythm in it. Boot prints. Fresh ones, pressed deep into the rain-softened mud, the tread pattern crisp-edged, unfilled with water. Made within hours. Maybe less.

Not one set. Two. Moving in step.

"They came through here," Tuck breathed. "Today. While we were on the river. They hit the camp, they read the carving, they probably read the journal—" His throat worked. "Did they take it? Did they—"

"It's still here," Cyrus said. "They left it."

"Why would you leave it? If you wanted what Meridian knew—"

"Because they already know it," Marek said.

Cyrus turned. The enforcer stood at the clearing's edge ten meters away, and Cyrus couldn't begin to guess how long he'd been listening. His face was unreadable, but his hands—his hands had moved, subtly, to rest near the jacket where he kept the pistol he pretended was for wildlife.

"Two sets of prints," Cyrus said. "Professional spacing. They didn't search the camp, they surveyed it. You want to tell me what that means, Marek?"

"It means," Marek said slowly, "that we are not the second expedition to follow Meridian's trail. We might not even be the third." He looked north, upriver, toward the distant wall of cloud that sat over the gorge like a lid on a pot—the standing storm, visible now even from here, a grey-green tower rotating with the slow patience of something that had been turning for years. "Gant knew Meridian by name. Gant's runners track everyone on this river. Whoever those boots belong to, they're ahead of us, they're fresh, and they're walking toward the same source we are."

The wind moved through the alders, and somewhere far off, so faint it might have been imagination, thunder rolled—not storm thunder, but the deep, arrhythmic pulse that Priya had been recording for days now, the heartbeat of the upper basin.

Tuck slid down from the beam. Priya emerged from the tent platform, journal hugged to her chest, and read their faces one by one, and Cyrus watched the understanding arrive in her eyes like cold water rising.

"We're being followed," she said. "Or preceded. Either way—"

"Either way," Cyrus said, "we're not alone out here. And everybody knows it now."

He looked north, at the storm that never moved, and made the only calculation that mattered: whatever waited upstream, it was better to meet it than to let it come to them in the dark.

"Break camp," he said. "We run the channel before nightfall."
