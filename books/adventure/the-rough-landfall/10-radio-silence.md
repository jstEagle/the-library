## 10. Radio Silence

Tuck found the silence at 0340, the way you find a hole in your tooth—with your tongue, without meaning to.

They were moored below the second bend, tied to a drowned cedar that leaned out over the water like a man listening for his name. Tuck had the night watch, which meant the radio shack—which meant the crate of salvaged gear wedged under the canopy aft, and the transmitter Tuck had rebuilt twice already from parts that had no business working together. The rig was ugly. It was hers. It had carried her voice from the flats to Lowmark to whatever ears were still tuned on this drowned green river, and it had never once gone quiet on her.

She keyed the mic out of habit, a half-second burst of carrier wave she'd been sending every hour since they passed Gant's bridge. *Still here. Still moving.* Nobody answered. Nobody was supposed to. It was just a heartbeat, a way of telling the dark: I exist, I exist, I exist.

The needle didn't move.

She keyed it again, longer. Pressed her palm flat against the case the way you'd press a hand to a sick animal's flank. Nothing came back through her fingers—no hum, no tremor of the final amplifier doing its work. The set was drawing power. The filaments should have been warm.

Tuck pulled the lamp close with her teeth and opened the back panel, and the smell hit her before her eyes made sense of it. Ozone and something sweeter, burnt-sugar wrongness. In the amber light she saw the coupling between the driver stage and the final—cracked clean through, not broken, *cut*, the fracture too straight, too deliberate, the ceramic scored first so it would snap where someone wanted it to snap. And beneath the chassis, tucked into the wiring loom like a spider in its web, a sliver of copper foil bridging two traces that should never touch. A kill switch anyone could remove in ten seconds. Someone had built this failure by hand, aboard this boat, while Tuck slept ten feet away.

She sat very still. The river talked to itself against the hull. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat and thought better of it.

Her hands wanted to shake and she wouldn't let them. Sixteen years old, no last name, raised on other people's frequencies—and she knew, with the flat certainty of a girl who had spent more nights listening than sleeping, that a cut coupling and a bridged loom were not an accident. Accidents tore. Accidents burned. This was surgery.

Someone on this boat had reached into her voice and pinched it off.

* * *

She didn't fix it. That was the first decision, and it cost her more than the cutting must have cost whoever made it. Every instinct screamed to solder, to restore, to make the world whole again—but a working transmitter would tell the saboteur their job was done badly, or worse, that Tuck knew. A dead radio told them nothing. A dead radio let Tuck watch.

So she closed the panel, wiped her prints off the copper foil with her sleeve—though if they'd been careful enough to score the ceramic, prints were probably a courtesy they'd extended to themselves—and sat back down at the watch position with her heart going like a telegraph key.

Four people on this boat. Five counting Reyes's ghost, but ghosts didn't cut couplings.

Cyrus slept in the bow, curled around his debt the way he curled around everything, one arm over his face. Priya had the midwatch hammock, her journal zipped inside her jacket even in sleep, like the river might steal her observations if she left them loose. Marek took the stern, as always, sitting up with his back against the gunwale, and here was the thing about Marek Voss that Tuck had catalogued in the first three days and never said aloud: enforcers didn't sleep sitting up with their eyes closed unless they wanted you to think they were asleep. She'd watched him do it six times now. His breathing changed when footsteps came near. Real sleepers didn't do that.

But Marek being syndicate wasn't a secret; it was a fact of the weather, like the humidity. You didn't need a cut coupling to know Marek would sell them all if the price was right. The question the copper foil asked was uglier: was it Marek? Or was it someone who wanted them cut off from the world for reasons of their own?

Or—the thought arrived cold and unwelcome—was it someone who wanted them cut off from each other?

Because that was the real payload of a sabotaged radio. Not the silence on the airwaves. The silence in the boat. The way four people who'd started eating together would start eating in shifts.

Tuck looked at the water and began, quietly, methodically, to test her crewmates.

* * *

Breakfast was rice and dried fish and the last of the honest coffee, and Tuck watched everyone's hands.

Cyrus ate like a man refueling machinery, efficient, eyes on the current line. He'd ask about the radio soon—he always asked, because the radio was their tether to Lowmark, to weather reports, to the thin fiction that somebody knew where they were. When he did ask, Tuck said, "Quiet last night. Probably atmospheric. The whole basin's gone strange." She watched his face take it. He frowned, nodded, said "Keep at it," and went back to the river. Either he was innocent or he was better than Marek, and Tuck didn't think anyone was better than Marek.

Priya ate slowly, reading the water over the rim of her bowl the way other people read newspapers. Tuck tested her sideways, casual as passing salt: "You ever notice how the static's been worse since the gorge?" Priya considered it seriously, because Priya considered everything seriously. "Static's not worse. There's less signal to separate it from." Then, sharper, the hydrologist surfacing through the woman: "Why? Did you hear something?"

And there it was—the flicker Tuck was fishing for. Not guilt. *Hope.* Priya wanted the radio alive because Priya wanted the world to still be listening, wanted data, wanted confirmation that the impossible storms she'd been logging had witnesses beyond this boat. Guilt doesn't hope. Tuck marked her down as clean, or clean enough, and felt something in her chest unclench by one degree.

Marek ate nothing. Marek drank tea and watched the banks with the patience of a man being paid by the hour, and when Tuck mentioned the radio was down, he said, "Equipment fails in the wet," in a voice with no opinion in it at all. Too smooth. But Marek was always too smooth; that proved nothing except what everyone already knew.

The trouble was that proving nothing was exactly what a clever saboteur would want. Tuck chewed her fish and hated how good the trap was. Whoever cut that coupling hadn't just killed the transmitter. They'd killed certainty itself, and now every silence in the boat had a second meaning, every kindness a possible motive, and Tuck—who had spent her whole life trusting signals over people, because signals never lied about what they were—had to sit in the middle of four human beings and wonder which one of them had steady enough hands to score ceramic in the dark.

* * *

Midday, rain like gravel on the canopy, and Tuck ran her tests properly.

She started with the obvious: she checked the tool roll. Her soldering iron, her wire snips, her ceramic file—all accounted for, all dry, all in order. Which meant either the saboteur had brought their own tools (plausible for Marek, implausible for almost anyone else) or they'd used hers and put them back so well that only a person who knew her own kit down to the wear pattern on the grips would ever catch it. She caught it. The snips had been resheathed a half-turn off true. Small thing. Everything about this was small things.

She tested Cyrus next, gently, the way you test ice. "Boss. If the radio stays dead—if we can't raise Lowmark before the monsoon—what's the play?" She watched his hands on the tiller. Cyrus Nakamura had a hundred tells and thought he had none. His jaw worked. "We've run dark before. Storm chasers spend half their lives unreachable." True. But then, quieter, almost to himself: "Nobody knows where we are." And his eyes went, just for a blink, to Marek at the stern, and away again, fast, like touching a stove.

Interesting. Cyrus suspected too. Or Cyrus feared, which on this river amounted to the same thing.

She tested Marek by giving him rope. "Found some water damage in the wiring loom," she said, watching his profile. "Could've shorted the whole final stage. Lucky it didn't."

"Lucky," Marek agreed, and drank his tea.

Not a flicker. Not a breath out of rhythm. And that—that was the most frightening result of the day—because Tuck had once talked a smuggler into confessing to a crime he hadn't committed just to see if she could, and she knew what human faces did when they lied. They leaked. Everyone leaked. Except, apparently, men who had been professionally trained not to, and men who genuinely had nothing to hide looked almost identical to them from the outside. The copper foil sat in her memory, scored and snapped, and she could not make it point at anyone. It just pointed at all of them.

Except—she turned it over one more time in the dark of the afternoon—it didn't have to point at a person. It could point at a purpose. Kill the transmitter and the crew couldn't call for help, couldn't report the deposit, couldn't warn Lowmark, couldn't coordinate with Gant's runners or anyone else. Kill the transmitter and the boat became a sealed room drifting toward the source with whatever the syndicate wanted sealed inside it. That wasn't paranoia. That was logistics. Somebody upriver had done arithmetic, and the radio was the remainder.

* * *

Evening. Rice again. The storm light made everything the color of old brass.

Tuck had decided, somewhere in the long gray hours, that she would tell Cyrus. Not tonight—tonight she'd watch one more watch cycle, confirm the saboteur didn't return to finish the job or gloat—but soon. Tomorrow. Because a secret like this rotted, and she could feel it starting to rot in her already, turning meals into interrogations, turning Priya's laugh into evidence, turning Marek's stillness into a confession written in a language she couldn't quite read. She was sixteen and tired and the river was full of thunder that behaved like no thunder she'd ever tracked, and she did not want to carry the cut coupling alone anymore.

Then, washing bowls, Priya said, lightly, to no one: "Strange, isn't it. No traffic on the bands at all. Even Gant's people chatter. Even smugglers. It's like the whole basin's holding its breath."

And Marek, drying his hands, said, "Maybe there's nothing left up there to talk."

And Cyrus laughed—too quickly, a laugh with a crack in it—and said, "Since when does empty stop anyone talking?" and the three of them stood in the brass light in a little triangle of almost-said things, and Tuck understood suddenly that she was not the only one keeping a ledger. That maybe everyone aboard was testing everyone else, quietly, kindly, with questions shaped like small talk. That the sabotage had already done its work whether or not its author ever touched anything again.

She looked at her bowl. She looked at the dead radio under its tarp, patient as a grave.

* * *

She took the midnight watch again, because she'd traded Priya for it and Priya hadn't asked why, and Tuck loved her a little for not asking.

At 0200 the storm upstream finally committed. Lightning walked the gorge walls in slow strokes, and in the strobes Tuck saw the river running backward for a heartbeat—swells moving against the current, against the wind, water climbing toward the source instead of falling away from it. Priya had written about this in her journal, muttering about pressure gradients that couldn't exist. The upper basin was making its own weather. Everybody felt it now, even Cyrus, even Marek. The air tasted like a struck match and a held breath.

At 0317, Tuck heard the footstep.

One. Soft. On the deck plating near the radio crate—not near her, near *it*. She kept her breathing even, kept her eyes half-lidded the way Marek did, and slid her hand under the blanket onto the wrench she'd placed there an hour ago with great care and no expectation of needing it.

In the lightning-strobe dark, a figure crouched at the crate. Broad shoulders. Moved like weight distributed on purpose. Lifted the tarp.

Tuck's heart slammed and she made herself count—two, three, four seconds of the figure's hands inside her radio, inside her voice—and then the figure straightened, replaced the tarp corner by careful corner, and turned.

Lightning filled the sky end to end, and in that white instant Tuck saw Marek Voss's face fully lit, three meters away, looking directly at her open eyes.

Neither of them moved. The thunder arrived, enormous, rolling down the gorge like a door closing.

"Couldn't sleep either," Marek said, in a voice as level and unreadable as standing water. He walked past her to the stern and sat down against the gunwale, facing the black upstream water, and said nothing else at all.

Tuck lay in the dark with the wrench in her fist and her mind tearing itself in half. Because she'd seen his face in the flash, and here was the thing, the unbearable thing: he hadn't looked guilty. He hadn't looked caught. He'd looked at her the way you look at a lock you're deciding whether to pick—or whether to leave locked, because locked is useful to you.

And she realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the rain beginning to fall, that she still didn't know if Marek had cut the coupling, fixed it, or simply come to check on work someone else had done.

The radio sat under its tarp, dead as a stone. Upstream, the storm turned over in its sleep, and the river climbed toward its source, carrying them all.
