## 12. Contact

The cave announced itself before we saw it.

Tuck heard it first—a sound riding beneath the gorge's endless thunder, thin and rhythmic, like someone striking metal against stone with the patience of the desperate. She had her headphones half-off one ear, the way she wore them when she was listening to two worlds at once, and her head came up from the radio bench with an expression I'd learned to read like a barometer falling.

"Cyrus." She tapped the earcup. "There's someone hammering. Been hammering. It's got a pattern—three strikes, pause, three strikes. Been going on since before dawn, I think. I just thought it was the rock settling."

"Rock doesn't keep time," Priya said from the tiller.

"No," Tuck said. "It doesn't."

We put in below a shelf of black basalt where the gorge pinched tight and the standing storm overhead seemed to lean closer, its rotation groaning through the stone like a hand run along a wet glass rim. The cliff face rose sheer for two hundred feet, seamed with vertical cracks that wept groundwater in bright mineral threads. And there, forty feet up a talus slope of shattered rock, a dark mouth opened in the cliff—narrow, man-high, half-veiled by a curtain of hanging moss that someone had clearly tried, and failed, to keep trimmed.

The hammering stopped the moment our boots touched stone. That was the tell. Whatever waited up there had been listening for us the way we'd been listening for it.

Marek went first. He always went first—it was in his blood the way reading water was in Priya's, the way frequencies were in Tuck's—and he climbed the talus with a low, economical grace that made my skin crawl now that I knew to look for it. Nothing wasted. Nothing human in the motion, either. Priya caught my eye at the base of the slope and said nothing, but her mouth was a flat line, and I knew we were thinking the same thing: *how many men had he climbed toward before, and what had happened when he reached them.*

I followed. Tuck stayed with the boat, radio in her lap, one ear on the world.

The cave mouth breathed cold air at us, mineral-sharp, tasting faintly of lightning—the same ozone tang that clung to everything above the gorge now. Inside, the passage bent twice and opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor littered with the archaeology of survival: ration wrappers sorted by type into neat stacks, a solar panel wired to a scavenged truck battery, a rain-catchment sheet funneled into a dented pot. And in the center of it, behind a barricade of expedition crates, sat a woman with a pistol in both hands and a case of gray alloy at her feet.

She was thinner than the woman in the syndicate office recording. Thinner, grayer, one arm wrapped in a filthy sling made from a shirt that had once been white. Her eyes were the worst part—not wild, not broken, but *counting*. Moving from my face to Marek's to the gun on his hip and back again, running arithmetic I could feel the weight of.

"Dr. Reyes," I said. "Ilsa. My name is Cyrus Nakamura. I chased storms with you out of Calloway Field, three years back. The dry season. You called my intercepts 'aggressively adequate.'"

The pistol didn't waver. "Aggressively adequate," she repeated. Her voice was a ruin, rusted from disuse. "You remembered that."

"I remember everything anyone ever said about my flying."

"Then you'll remember I also said you were the kind of man who'd fly into anything if the price was right." Her gaze slid past me and fixed on Marek, and something in her face went from counting to *certain*. "And you brought that with you."

Marek hadn't moved. He stood with his hands loose at his sides, in the doorway's shadow, doing his best impression of furniture. It was very good. It had always been very good.

"Dr. Reyes," he said. "You're mistaken—"

"Voss." She said the surname like spitting out a seed. "Marek Voss. You were at the Vane regional office in Harrowgate the winter Meridian filed its survey permits. You carried the denial paperwork yourself. You stood in the doorway and you watched us load our boats, and you had the same face you have right now. I spent four months in that cave learning to remember faces, Mr. Voss. I had nothing else to do."

The chamber went very quiet. Outside, the storm overhead turned and turned, its pressure pressing down through the stone until my ears ached with it.

"Everyone stop talking," I said, and I don't know who I said it to. "Ilsa. We came upriver because Meridian went silent. Because the syndicate bought a salvage contract and put my name on it. I didn't know—"

"You didn't know what?" She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a hinge that hadn't been oiled in months. "That the syndicate doesn't salvage? Cyrus. I filed those permits. I know what the Vane Syndicate is. I know what they sent you upriver to do. Ask him." The pistol shifted, two degrees, to cover Marek's chest. "Ask him what the contract says. The real one. Not the one they let you sign."

Marek's face did something I had never seen it do in the weeks since Lowmark. It *hesitated*.

And that hesitation was the answer.

"Get out of the doorway," I said to him. My voice didn't sound like mine. "Move to the wall. Both hands where she can see them."

"Cyrus—"

"*Move.*"

He moved. Slowly, without protest, without the coiled readiness I'd have expected—and that, more than anything, frightened me. Marek Voss did not comply with men who pointed guns at him. He *removed* them. But he went to the wall and put his back against the basalt and folded his arms, and he watched me with an expression I couldn't parse, and Ilsa Reyes kept her pistol trained on his chest and her back against her case of gray alloy like a mother over a child.

"Tell me," I said to her. "All of it. Start with the readings."

* * *

She talked for a long time. The storm overhead turned. The light in the cave mouth shifted from gray to the bruised violet of late afternoon, and Tuck's voice crackled once up the passage—*everything okay down there?*—and I called back that it was, and it wasn't, and both were true.

"The mineral's not a mineral, not really," she said. She'd lowered the pistol halfway, resting it on her knee, but her eyes never left Marek. "It's a charged lattice. It holds atmospheric potential the way a dam holds water—stores it, releases it, *steers* it. The vent below us is the biggest natural occurrence on the continent. The gorge storm isn't a storm, Cyrus. It's a *symptom*. The source generates its own weather, has for centuries, maybe millennia. We were sent to map it. We mapped it."

"And?"

"And then I did the math on what it would mean if someone *mined* it." She pulled a battered notebook from inside her jacket—Reyes's journal, though I wouldn't learn its final significance until much later—and held it up. "Stable steering of monsoon systems. You could bring the rain to one valley and starve another. You could hold a drought over a nation's breadbasket for a decade and nobody could prove it wasn't weather. Three countries' worth of agriculture, held in one canyon. The syndicate didn't send Meridian up here to study it. They sent us to *appraise* it."

"The rest of your team," Priya said from the passage mouth. She'd come up when the shouting started, and now stood with her arms crossed, water dripping off her, watching Reyes with the expression of a woman reading a river she didn't trust. "What happened to them?"

Reyes's jaw worked. "The syndicate's second boat came upriver eleven days after ours. Six men. Professional. They didn't announce themselves—they just started taking our instruments, our cores, our notes. Okafor argued. Okafor was our field lead." She stopped. Started again, flatter. "There was a disagreement about whether scientists need all their fingers. The others scattered into the gorge. I've heard nothing since. I've been here four months, eating moss-fish and rain, waiting to see which boat came next."

"And then ours did," I said.

"And then yours did." Her gaze went from me to Priya to Marek and back. "With him on it."

"I didn't know," I said, and heard how thin it sounded, and said it anyway. "Ilsa, I swear on every storm I've ever chased—I took a salvage contract. Equipment recovery. That's all I was told."

"I believe you." She said it without softness. "You were always a tool, Cyrus. The question was always who was holding you." The pistol came back up, leveled at Marek, steady despite the ruined arm. "So. Enforcer. Tell him what the contract says. Tell him what happens after the salvage. I've had four months to imagine it. I'd like to hear it from the source."

Marek was silent for so long that I could hear the battery hum, the drip of groundwater, the distant grinding of the standing storm. When he spoke, his voice was level, and that was its own kind of confession.

"The contract specifies recovery of survey data and mineral samples," he said. "Clause nine covers personnel. *Loose ends.*" He didn't look at me. "I was instructed to confirm the deposit and ensure no Meridian personnel returned downriver. That included the salvage crew, once the data was secured."

The words landed in the chamber like stones into still water. Priya made a sound—small, involuntary. Somewhere below, Tuck's radio hissed through a frequency sweep, oblivious.

"Ensure," I repeated.

"Ensure." Marek finally looked at me, and his face was the face I'd seen at the toll bridge, at Mother Gant's table, smooth and unreadable—and then it wasn't. Something moved behind it, something that had been moving for days, maybe weeks, cracking the finish. "You want the rest? The syndicate has an extraction operation staged at the basin's headwaters. Forty men, drilling rigs, a barge train. Gant is theirs—her toll bridge, her trackers, her runners. She's their upstream proxy and has been for six years. They plan to have the vent mined and producing refined lattice before the monsoon ends. Your boat, my boat, this contract—none of it was ever meant to go home."

"And you were going to do it," Priya said. Not a question. "The clause nine part. You were going to do it to us."

Marek looked at her. Then at me. And I watched a man stand at the edge of his own ledger and read the sum total, and I watched him decide not to lie, which may have been the first honest act of his life.

"I was going to do it," he said. "At the source. After the data was confirmed. I've done it before. It's what I'm for." He paused. "I'm not going to do it now."

"You'll forgive us," Reyes said dryly, "if we don't take your word."

"You shouldn't." He pushed off the wall, and every gun in the chamber came up—Reyes's pistol, my own hand drifting to the flare gun at my belt—and he stopped, hands visible, and said quietly: "You shouldn't take my word. But you should take my knowledge, because you're going to need it. Gant's runners found us at the bridge because the syndicate wanted them to. There's a tracker in this boat, or on one of us, and it's been reporting our position since Lowmark. They know where you are. They know you've found her. And the extraction team is three days downstream, moving up with the monsoon's first surge."

"The transmitter," I said, and the bottom dropped out of my stomach. "Tuck said the transmitter's been failing. Intermittent. She thought it was moisture."

"Moisture doesn't fail on schedule," Marek said.

The chamber seemed to tilt. I thought of Tuck at the radio bench, sixteen years old, coaxing dead frequencies back to life night after night, blaming herself for every failure. I thought of the sabotage chapter I hadn't known I was living inside.

"We go down to the boat," I said. "Now. All of us. And we find out exactly what's been listening to us."

Reyes was already moving, jamming the journal into her jacket, dragging her case of cores toward the passage with her one good arm. Marek stepped forward to help and she froze, pistol rising, and for a moment the two of them stood locked—her suspicion, his outstretched hands—while the storm overhead groaned like something waking.

"Dr. Reyes," Marek said. "I'm the reason you're down to one arm. I won't insult you by asking you to trust me. But I can carry the case, and you can watch me carry it, and when we reach the boat you can shoot me if I run."

A long moment. Then she stepped aside—two steps, no more—and let him take the handle, and followed him into the dark passage at gunpoint, and Priya looked at me with a face like flint.

"Cyrus," she said quietly. "Whatever you're deciding about him, decide fast. Because the sky's already deciding about us."

Above the gorge, the standing storm had begun, for the first time since we'd entered it, to *move*.
