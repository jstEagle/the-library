## 13. The Case for Burning It

The cave breathed.

Ilsa Reyes had learned its rhythm over four hundred days: the slow inhale of wind through the cliff mouth at dusk, the long exhale before dawn, the way the stone sweated when the standing storm above the gorge spun itself tight. She had named the rhythm the way you name anything you intend to outlive—quietly, so it wouldn't notice you leaving.

Nobody was leaving. That was the first thing she understood when the four of them crowded into her sanctuary, dripping river water onto the slate floor she'd swept smooth with a broken oar. The Nakamura man. The girl with the radio scarred into her fingertips. The hydrologist whose name Ilsa had read in three dismissed papers and one retraction. And Voss—the syndicate's long shadow, the man whose face she'd memorized from a personnel file she'd stolen in another life, back when Meridian Expedition had still been a word that meant *hope* instead of a word that meant *grave*.

She kept the shotgun where they could see it and the case where they couldn't.

"You're Reyes." Nakamura said it like he was checking a wound for a pulse. "The broadcasts. The bearing. That was you."

"Fourteen seconds of signal a night, on a frequency the syndicate's listening posts catalogued as atmospheric noise." She almost smiled. "I chose it because they'd already heard it and dismissed it. The best place to hide a voice is inside a sound they've decided doesn't exist."

"And the rest of your expedition?" Priya Osei asked. Her voice had the careful flatness of someone who had learned to ask questions whose answers she couldn't afford to feel.

Ilsa looked at the cave wall, at the tally marks she'd stopped carving after the two hundredth day, at the small shelf where six tin cups sat in a row—Meridian's dead, kept in drinking order because she couldn't bear to keep them in any other.

"Ask the mineral," she said. "It's the only one of us still telling the truth."

* * *

She opened the case because they would not leave without seeing it, and because—after four hundred days of talking to tin cups—she had forgotten how to want anything except an audience.

The survey cores lay in felt-lined channels like bones in a reliquary. Each cylinder was a meter of gray stone shot through with filaments that caught the lamplight and held it wrong—not reflecting it so much as *delaying* it, the way a storm delays its own thunder.

"Kestrelite," Ilsa said. "The expedition's name for it. The geologists wanted something Latin and dignified. I wanted something that sounded like a warning."

She lifted one core and held it up, and the light in the cave seemed to lean toward it.

"You've all felt it already. The storms that form against the prevailing wind. The cell over the gorge that hasn't moved in two years. The river that floods from tributaries that don't exist on any map." She set the core down gently, the way you set down a sleeping animal. "This is why. The deposit vents a charged mineral vapor—ionized particulate, stable in the boundary layer for hours. It seeds convection. It organizes it. It *steers* it."

"Steers," Priya repeated. The word came out of her like a fishhook coming out.

"Localized atmospheric manipulation." Ilsa gave them the sentence flat and clean, the way you hand someone a knife—handle first, so they can decide what to do with it. "Not weather modification the way the old cloud-seeding programs dreamed of it. Not hoping for rain. *Commanding* it. The vapor column above the source acts as a control surface. Shift the emission rate, shift the plume's density, and you shift where the atmosphere's energy wants to go. We measured it. I measured it." Her hand trembled, and she let it. "I built the model that proved it. I'm the one who wrote down the numbers that say a man with a valve and a weather station could walk a monsoon across a border like a dog on a leash."

The cave was quiet except for its breathing.

"Stable?" Nakamura asked. Always the practical question, always the man doing arithmetic. "How stable?"

"Stable enough to steer a monsoon," Ilsa said. "Stable enough to do it *every year*. Stable enough that whoever holds this canyon holds the rain."

She let that land. She watched it land differently in each of them—in Osei's eyes, the horror of a scientist seeing her discipline weaponized before her discipline had even finished understanding it; in the girl's face, something simpler and fiercer, the animal recognition of a cage being built; in Nakamura, the terrible arithmetic of a man calculating what the rain was worth and who had already sold it.

And in Voss, nothing. Voss looked at the cores the way a banker looks at collateral.

* * *

"Three nations," Ilsa said. She had rehearsed this part for four hundred days, to the tin cups, to the storm, to God if God still took calls from the Kestrel Basin. "The Kestrel feeds the lowland basins of three nations. The monsoon that waters them forms over the high plateau and funnels down this gorge. You've seen it. You've *sailed under it*. That cell overhead isn't weather. It's a demonstration."

She stood, because sitting made her feel like a defendant, and she was done being a defendant.

"Meridian's sponsors—the same people whose logo is probably stamped inside Voss's boot heels—funded us to find out if the effect was real. It is. I confirmed it with three independent methods and then I watched them kill my team to keep the confirmation quiet, and I understood what I had actually been hired to do. Not to discover it. To *appraise* it."

"The syndicate doesn't want rain," Nakamura said slowly. "They want the *valve*."

"They want the option on the valve. Think like a lender, Nakamura, because I know you can." She saw him flinch at his own name in her mouth, at how much she already knew about his debts, and she didn't soften it. "You don't have to make it rain. You just have to make everyone believe you *could*. Three nations' worth of agriculture, and every government, every grain exchange, every insurance market suddenly negotiating with the only company that owns the canyon the storms are born from. They wouldn't even need to use it. Ownership *is* the weapon. The famine is a rumor that never has to come true to starve people into obedience."

"And you?" Priya asked. "What did you do?"

"I ran," Ilsa said. "With the data. With the cores. I ran upstream because downstream was theirs, and I let six people die doing it, and I have spent four hundred days deciding what to do with what's in this case." She looked at each of them in turn. "So I'll tell you what I decided, and then we're going to find out which of you I'm wrong about."

* * *

"Burn it."

She said it plainly, and the cave took the words and gave them back with echo, so they seemed to come from the stone itself.

"Not the cores—the cores are just rock. Burn the *knowledge*. The model, the emission mappings, the vent survey, the shaft geometry. Everything that tells a person how to turn this canyon into an instrument. Collapse the vent shaft with a shaped charge at the throat, and the vapor column dies. The standing storm unravels within a season. The deposit stays where it is, buried under two hundred meters of rockfall, and no one—no syndicate, no government, no genius with a grant—steers the rain for a very long time."

"And the science?" Priya's voice cracked on the second word, and Ilsa recognized the crack. She'd made it herself, once, in a hotel bar in the capital, arguing with a man who had never held a core in his life.

"Gone with it. Yes. Twenty years of atmospheric physics, buried." Ilsa stepped closer. "You want to know what I've learned in this cave, Dr. Osei? I've learned that knowledge is not neutral. We tell ourselves it is—we tell ourselves the truth just *is*, and what people do with it is someone else's sin. But some truths are load-bearing in only one direction. Some knowledge can only be built into a weapon, the way some steel can only be built into a blade. This is that. I have read my own data every day for four hundred days, looking for the peaceful version of it, the version that only helps. It doesn't exist. I wrote the equations myself and I'm telling you: there is no page in that model that doesn't end in a leash."

"You'd blind everyone," Voss said.

His voice was quiet, which was how she knew he'd been waiting for his turn.

* * *

Voss rose and walked to the case, and Ilsa's hand found the shotgun, and he stopped—respectful of the weapon, unimpressed by the argument.

"You'd blind everyone," he said again. "Forever. On the theory that blindness is safety." He looked down at the cores. "It isn't. It never is. The deposit is here. It's *real*. I'm standing in the room with it. Which means the knowledge already exists—it exists in this cave, in my head now, in hers—" a nod at Priya, "—in the survey reports the first half of Meridian transmitted downstream before you cut their signal. You think burning a case of rock erases it? The knowledge can't be unlearned, Reyes. It can only be owned."

"By whom?" the girl—Tuck—asked, and her voice was younger than the rest of her.

"By someone accountable," Voss said. "That's the part of the fairy tale you all skip. You imagine the syndicate steering a monsoon into a rival's wheat belt. Fine. Imagine instead the drought year—there's always a drought year—when the rain fails on its own, and the only people who can bring it are the ones who understand this canyon. You'd rather three nations starve than owe a debt? Because that's the alternative you're proposing. Nature is not kind, it's just indifferent, and indifference kills more people than malice ever has. I've read the famine ledgers. Have any of you?"

"You've read the ledgers," Ilsa said. "Have you read the orders?"

The silence that followed had weight. She watched it press down on Voss, watched the man built for orders absorb the fact that his orders were now a matter of record in this room.

"I know what my contract says," he said.

"I know what it says too." She let him hear that she knew all of it. "Confirm the deposit. Secure the data. And ensure the survey team's findings never circulate—by any means necessary. That's the clause, isn't it, Voss? *Any means necessary.* You were sent to be the period at the end of my sentence." She turned to the others. "He's right about one thing, and you should hear it from me. The knowledge exists. It's in his head. It's in mine. Burning the cores doesn't erase that. But knowledge in one head is a secret. Knowledge in a syndicate vault is a *product*. There is a difference between a fire and a factory, and I am asking you to burn the factory."

"And the fire takes the cave with it," Nakamura said. "The vent shaft. Maybe the gorge."

"Maybe more than the gorge." Ilsa didn't flinch from it. "You don't collapse a charged mineral vent gently. You'll trigger a storm—the mother of them, everything the column's been holding back for two years, released at once. I won't lie to you about the cost. I've had four hundred days to lie to myself, and I've used them all up."

* * *

The argument went on for hours, because arguments about the end of the world always do, and because the cave was the only roof any of them had and no one wanted to step out from under it undecided.

Voss made the case for ownership the way he did everything—economically, without wasted motion. Accountability, he called it. Stewardship. He spoke of drought years and famine ledgers and the moral vanity of people who'd rather stay pure than stay useful. And the worst of it, Ilsa thought, was that he almost believed it. There was a version of Marek Voss, buried somewhere under the syndicate's stamp, who had talked himself into his orders one ledger at a time, and that version stood in her cave making her own arguments back at her with the serial numbers filed off.

She answered him with the dead. Not with speeches—with names. Six names, said into the cave's breathing, one for each tin cup on the shelf. *The knowledge can't be unlearned,* he'd said. *Then it can be unowned,* she said. *And that's worth a gorge. That's worth a storm. That's worth everything short of the three nations you're so worried about, because I promise you, Voss—those nations are already on the ledger. They've been on it since the day my sponsors read my first transmission. The only question left is whether the rain gets a master.*

Priya Osei came down on her side before the argument was half over, and said so, and said it with the fury of a woman who had been drummed out of the cathedral of science and had just been shown what the cathedral was being built into. Tuck said nothing for a long time, and then said only: "Whoever owns the valve owns everyone downstream. That's everyone. That's my whole map." Which was, Ilsa thought, a sixteen-year-old's way of arriving at the correct answer faster than the doctor had.

Nakamura said nothing at all.

He stood at the cave mouth with the standing storm churning above the gorge, and Ilsa watched him do his arithmetic—the only kind he trusted, the kind with lives in one column and debts in the other—and she understood that the fault line she'd drawn in the open air of her cave ran directly underneath him, and that which side of it he landed on would decide whether any of them walked out of the Kestrel Basin at all.

"Vane forgives debts," she said quietly, to his back. "I've read their contracts. They forgive them the way a flood forgives a fence."

"I know what they forgive," Nakamura said, without turning around.

Above the gorge, the storm turned, and turned, and waited—patient as a creditor, vast as everything they stood to lose—and far downriver, where none of them could see it, the first black walls of the early monsoon had begun to build over the coast, coming for the basin days ahead of any forecast.

Coming, Ilsa thought, like a collection.
