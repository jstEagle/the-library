## 14. Mother Gant's Bargain

The runners came at dusk, when the river turned the color of old pewter and the insects rose off the water in a humming veil. Cyrus heard them before he saw them—two long-tail boats running quiet against the current, engines muffled with rags, which told him everything he needed to know before the first hull kissed their gunwale. Nobody ran silent on the Kestrel unless they meant to arrive unannounced.

He was on watch. He let them come.

Six men, maybe seven in the failing light, all of them wearing the same oiled canvas ponchos that made them look like a school of dark fish surfacing together. In the bow of the lead boat stood a woman Cyrus had met once before, years ago, across a syndicate table in a city that smelled of diesel and rain—Gant's factor, a narrow man called Sef, whose smile never reached past his teeth.

"Nakamura." Sef said it like a man confirming a delivery address. "Mother Gant sends her regards."

Cyrus kept his voice level. "You're a long way from your bridge."

"The river is short, these days." Sef stepped aboard without invitation, his boots leaving wet prints on the deck plates. Behind him, two of the poncho men fanned out along the rail with the loose-limbed ease of people who had done violence often enough that it no longer required urgency. "She sent me because she thought you'd remember me. Sentiment. She's generous that way."

Marek appeared from below decks first—he always moved toward trouble like iron filings toward a magnet—and Priya came up behind him with a hand lamp she held lower than was polite. Tuck stayed in the wheelhouse shadow, and Cyrus could feel the kid watching, cataloguing, the way Tuck did everything.

"We paid your toll," Cyrus said.

"You did." Sef wandered the deck as if appraising it for salvage, trailing one finger along the rail. "And what you paid wasn't money. You'll have worked that out by now, or you're slower than your reputation. The manifest you signed at the bridge—the toll ledger, the nice little contract with the wax seal? Inked with a tracer compound. Every page of it. Your signatures are broadcasting this boat's position to Mother Gant's receivers every six hours, regular as a heartbeat. Have been since Lowmark's downstream cousin took your mark."

* * *

The silence that followed had weight. Cyrus felt it settle over the deck like the humidity, pressing down.

Behind him, he heard Priya exhale slowly through her nose—the sound she made when she was recalculating something fundamental. Marek said nothing at all, which was its own kind of confirmation; enforcers always knew more than they admitted, and Cyrus filed that away in the growing drawer of things about Marek Voss he didn't want to look at directly.

"So we're marked," Cyrus said finally. "Every boat on this river is marked somewhere. What does she want?"

"Ah." Sef smiled again, and this time it climbed a little higher, like a weed finding a crack. "Now we come to the part where I stop being a messenger and start being a merchant."

He produced a lacquered tube from inside his poncho, cracked the seal, and drew out a letter written on heavy paper, the handwriting small and precise as insect legs. He read aloud, though everyone present could see Gant hadn't written anything she expected to be read twice.

"Item: the woman Reyes, alive and functional. Item: the survey case she carries, contents intact. Delivery to Mother Gant's station at the confluence, or to her runners wherever intercepted. Consideration: full passage out of the basin ahead of the monsoon, escort guaranteed through the gorge, and—" here Sef paused, savoring it, "—the Vane Syndicate's interest in one Cyrus Nakamura, principal plus accumulated penalty, declared void. A clean slate. Signed under Gant's own seal, which on this river outranks anything Vane prints in the low country."

Cyrus looked at the paper without taking it. "And if I don't?"

Sef shrugged, a small economical motion. "Then we take them anyway, eventually, or the river takes you all, and Mother Gant loses nothing but patience. The offer is not a threat, Nakamura. It's arithmetic. She knows what you owe. She knows what you're worth clear. She's simply asking whether you can count."

* * *

They anchored that night in a side slough, backwater still as poured glass, the standing storm far upstream pulsing its dull violet light against the clouds like a bruise beneath skin. Sef and his runners withdrew to their own boats a hundred meters off, close enough that their lanterns bled across the water, far enough to make the point that nobody was going anywhere.

Cyrus sat in the wheelhouse with the offer spread on the chart table and did the thing he was best at, the thing that had made him a good chaser and a bad man by turns: he ran the numbers.

Passage out. Escort through the gorge, which had already cost them an engine and nearly the whole boat. Debt forgiveness—eleven seasons of his life, eleven seasons of chasing weather for other people's ledgers, gone with a signature. And all it would cost was Ilsa Reyes, a woman half-dead already who trusted none of them, and a case of cores that could steer the monsoons that fed three nations' fields.

It was such a reasonable trade. That was the shameful part. Not that he was tempted—temptation was honest, temptation at least announced itself—but that the arithmetic worked so cleanly. Eleven seasons weighed against one ruined hydrologist and a box of rocks. Any actuary in the low country would sign it without reading the fine print.

He thought about the debt differently then, the way he tried not to: not as a number but as a shape. His father's clinic, mortgaged into the Syndicate's hands when the flood year took the coast. The letters he'd stopped answering. The way every season of chasing had been less about the storms than about outrunning the accumulating interest, as if velocity itself could dilute what he owed.

He thought about Tuck asleep in the bow compartment, sixteen years old, no last name, who had signed onto this boat because Cyrus had seemed like the kind of man who didn't sell people.

He thought about Marek, and wondered—not for the first time, and with a new and colder clarity—whether the enforcer had known about the tracer ink all along. Whether the toll bridge had been arranged from the beginning. Whether *assist* had ever meant anything else.

The lantern guttered. Outside, the river breathed against the hull.

* * *

In the morning, Cyrus lied to his crew.

He'd decided sometime in the smallest hours, lying awake listening to the water, that he would buy a night. One night. Time enough to figure out a third option that didn't exist yet, or time enough to run, or time enough simply to be, for one more day, the man his crew believed they'd signed on with. So when Sef came alongside at first light, grinning his tooth-root grin, Cyrus stood at the rail and said:

"I need until tomorrow's dusk. One day. She wants a considered answer, she gets one."

Sef studied him with the flat patience of a man who had watched many people arrive at the same destination by different roads. "One day," he agreed. "But Nakamura—a kindness, since you seem like a man who appreciates kindness. Don't mistake stalling for strategy. Mother Gant has been trading on this river longer than you've been alive. She can smell a man deciding whether to sell his crew the way sharks smell blood in ballast water. Whatever you decide, decide it all the way."

Then the runners backed water and withdrew upstream into the morning mist, and Cyrus turned to face his crew with the lie already seated in his throat like a swallowed stone.

"They want the cores," he said. "Reyes's case. Gant's factor says hand over the survey materials and we get passage and escort out ahead of the monsoon." He paused, and the pause was the sin, measured precisely, calibrated. "No mention of Reyes herself. They didn't ask for her."

Priya frowned. "That doesn't track. Why leave the only living witness?"

"Because a witness is liability on the water," Marek said, before Cyrus could arrange his face. "Dead weight. Gant deals in goods, not people." His eyes found Cyrus's and held there, unreadable, and Cyrus understood with sick certainty that Marek knew—or suspected—and was saying nothing, and that this silence was either mercy or a trap, and he couldn't tell which.

Tuck said nothing at all. The kid just looked at Cyrus for a long moment with those quick radio-operator's eyes, the ones that could pull signal out of static, truth out of noise, and Cyrus felt the lie tremble between them like a frequency waiting to be tuned.

"Right," Tuck said finally, lightly, too lightly. "Just the cores, then."

* * *

The day passed badly.

They used it the way a condemned man uses his last morning—scrubbing the boat, checking lines, doing small necessary tasks with too much care. Priya spent hours over Reyes's journal, copying figures by lamplight, muttering about pressure gradients that shouldn't exist. Tuck rebuilt the transmitter's antenna mast twice, though it didn't need rebuilding. Marek cleaned weapons that were already clean.

Cyrus avoided all of them, and hated himself for the avoidance, because avoidance was its own confession. Every time Tuck glanced up from the radio bench, Cyrus found a reason to be elsewhere. Every time Priya asked a question about the route downstream, he answered a beat too fast.

Toward evening, Reyes herself surfaced from the cave shelter where they'd been keeping her, wrapped in blankets, hollow-eyed, sharp as broken glass even now. She looked at Cyrus across the fire they'd built on a gravel bar, and something in her gaze—a hydrologist's instinct for reading currents, perhaps, applied to faces—made him certain she could see straight through him to the lacquered tube hidden under the chart table.

"You've been offered something," she said quietly, when the others were out of earshot. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone upriver gets offered something eventually."

"Mm." She fed a stick to the fire. "I've met men weighing a betrayal before. There's a particular way they hold their shoulders. Like they're carrying water they don't want to spill." She looked at him, and her eyes were terribly kind, which was worse than accusation. "Whatever it is, Nakamura—you should know that I've been cargo before. Meridian sold me upriver piece by piece for three years of funding. I survived it. If you're doing the arithmetic tonight, do it honestly. That's all I ask. Do it honestly, and live with whatever it tells you."

She went back to her blanket, and Cyrus sat alone by the fire while the standing storm pulsed its bruised light upstream, and the shame sat in him like ballast, deep and permanent, shifting with every swell.

* * *

Dusk of the bought day came down the river like a lid closing.

Sef's boats reappeared out of the mist exactly on schedule, punctual as tide tables, and Cyrus walked down to the gravel bar alone to meet them. Behind him he could hear his crew moving about the main boat—Tuck's voice faint on the radio band, Priya's murmur over the journal—and the ordinary sounds of them made the gravel under his boots feel like it might give way.

"Well?" Sef asked.

"One more night," Cyrus said. "Tomorrow. I'll deliver the answer myself, at the confluence. Gant's terms stand as written?"

"As written." Sef's eyes glittered. "Though I notice you didn't bring the case."

"I notice you didn't bring my forgiveness."

Something flickered across Sef's face—irritation, or possibly respect. "Tomorrow, then. Dusk. Confluence." He turned to go, then paused, half-smiling. "A word of advice, chaser. The river above eats expeditions. The river below eats liars. Pick your water carefully."

The runners withdrew into the dark, and Cyrus stood alone on the gravel bar with the first fat drops of the coming monsoon spotting the stones around his feet, and understood that whatever he chose tomorrow, he had already lost something tonight. The lie was out of his mouth and into the world now, sitting in Tuck's memory, in Priya's careful silence, in Marek's knowing nothing-said. It had a weight and a frequency all its own, and it was broadcasting.

He looked upriver, where the storm glowed against the clouds like something buried and burning, and made himself a promise he wasn't sure he could keep: that whatever the arithmetic said, he would find a third option before dawn. Even if he had to invent it. Even if it cost him everything the forgiveness was worth.

Behind him, on the boat, a radio clicked, and Tuck's voice carried thin across the water—too low to catch the words, too deliberate to be accidental.

Cyrus stood in the rising rain and listened to his crew talking about him in frequencies he couldn't hear, and for the first time since Lowmark, he was afraid of his own boat.
