## 15. Fracture

Tuck had learned to read voices the way other people read faces. A voice told you everything if you listened long enough—the hitch before a lie, the flattening that meant someone was reciting something they'd memorized, the warmth that crept in when a person forgot to guard themselves. He'd spent sixteen years listening because it was safer than talking, and by now he could hear the truth in a transmission like a note held under water: warped, but there.

Which was why he couldn't sleep.

The camp sat on a gravel bar below the gorge's first throat, where the river ran green and fast between walls of wet black stone. The standing storm turned overhead, a slow gray wheel of cloud lit from within by lightning that never quite reached the ground. It hummed. That was the thing nobody else seemed to hear—or maybe they heard it and refused to name it—a low harmonic under the river-sound, like a tuning fork pressed against the world's teeth. Tuck lay in his bunk on the boat with his headset half on and let the hum fill him, and above the hum he heard Cyrus and Mother Gant's runner talking at the edge of the firelight, low, and he did what he always did.

He listened.

He hadn't meant to. That was the honest version, the version he'd tell himself later when it mattered. He'd been running a sweep on the shortwave band, hunting for Gant's traffic after Marek's warning that the warlord's runners were upstream of them, and the little receiver in his palm had caught the runner's burst transmitter—encrypted, mostly, but Gant was cheap where it counted and her people used a commercial scrambler with a known weakness, a repeating seed key that Tuck had cracked back in Lowmark out of boredom more than anything. So when the runner keyed up from fifty meters away, Tuck's handset translated it into plain speech, and plain speech said:

*Confirm package intact. Subject Nakamura accepts terms. Passage for four, debt voided, in exchange for the woman Reyes and the core samples. Handover at the narrows, dawn tomorrow.*

Dawn tomorrow. Which was now less than six hours away.

Tuck lay very still in the dark and felt the words go through him like cold water through a hull. *Subject Nakamura accepts terms.* Not *will consider.* Not *is stalling.* Accepts. And then, an hour later, when Cyrus had come back to the boat smelling of woodsmoke and someone else's tobacco, and Priya had asked what the runner wanted, and Cyrus had said—"toll talk, nothing settled, she wants more than we have"—Tuck had watched his face do something small and wrong. A hitch. The hitch before a lie.

He knew that hitch. He'd heard it in his mother's voice once, years ago, the last night she'd told him everything was fine.

* * *

Morning came up the color of dishwater, the storm-wheel overhead grinding slower, heavier, dropping its skirt of rain down the gorge walls in long gray veils. The air tasted metallic. Tuck had not slept. He sat in the wheelhouse with the transmitter panel open in front of him, all its colored guts exposed, and he held the handset like it might bite, and he thought about all the ways a person could say a true thing and all the ways a person could fail to.

Cyrus came aboard at first light with two mugs of tea and handed one through the wheelhouse door without asking, which was a thing he did, which was one of the hundred small reasons Tuck had decided somewhere on the river that this man was worth following. That was the trap of it. That was exactly the trap.

"Storm's building," Cyrus said, nodding at the sky. "Priya thinks landfall's coming early. We should be through the narrows before noon."

"Before dawn, you mean," Tuck said.

Cyrus went still. It was a small stillness, controlled, the stillness of a man who had spent seasons reading supercells and knew how much information lived inside a pause. "What's that?"

"The handover." Tuck kept his eyes on the panel. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was shaking like a loose antenna in wind. "Reyes and the cores. At the narrows. I cracked Gant's scrambler in Lowmark. I heard you accept."

The storm hummed. Somewhere behind them, up the gravel bar, Ilsa Reyes was coughing in her sleep, the wet rattle of a lung that had been drowning slowly for weeks. The case of survey cores sat lashed under the tarp like a coffin.

"Tuck." Cyrus set his mug down. Careful. Everything careful. "Whatever you think you heard—"

"I heard your name." Now Tuck looked at him, and it cost him, looking, because Cyrus's face was doing the thing faces did when the person behind them was scrambling for footing. "I heard *debt voided.* I heard *passage for four.* Four, Cyrus. Me, you, Priya, Marek. You didn't even ask them to count Reyes twice. You sold her and you didn't even haggle over whether I got a seat in the boat."

"I was buying time." The words came fast now, and Tuck heard the hitch again, worse, the lie fraying at the edges. "Gant's runners were going to take us apart on that gravel bar if I didn't give them something. A night. That's all I bought—one night to figure a way out that doesn't get anyone killed."

"You lied to us."

"Yes." Cyrus said it flat, and for a second the honesty of it was almost worse than the lie. "Yes, I lied. Because the truth was a loaded gun and everyone on this crew was already pointing fingers, and I thought—I thought I could fix it before anyone got hurt."

"You thought you could fix it *alone.*" Tuck stood up. The wheelhouse suddenly felt too small for both of them, for the storm overhead, for the word *sold* sitting between them like ballast. "That's the same as Meridian, isn't it? Everyone deciding alone what everyone else can bear to know. Look how that ended."

"That's not fair."

"No," Tuck said. "It isn't. Nothing about any of this is fair. But you don't get to decide what I can bear. Nobody gets to decide that but me."

And that was when the boats came.

* * *

Tuck heard them before anyone—of course he did, three diesel engines idling high, running quiet up the last bend of the narrows—and he was already shouting when the first hull slid around the rock spur, gunmetal gray, packed with Gant's fighters in yellow oilskins, a mounted autocannon bolted to the bow like a steel flower that only bloomed one way.

"They're here!" His voice cracked across the camp. "Narrows, both sides, they're here!"

The morning detonated into motion. Priya came off the bank at a run, hair plastered to her face, Reyes limping behind her with the journal clutched to her chest. Marek was already awake—Marek was always already awake—and Tuck saw him take in the boats, the cannon, the numbers, and do the arithmetic the way he did everything: instantly, without visible feeling.

"Sixteen armed," Marek said. "Two boats blocking downstream, one holding the spur. We're dead in the water unless we move now."

"We're not moving anywhere," said the runner from Gant's lead boat, a broad woman with a scarred jaw and a loudhailer she didn't need. Her voice carried flat across the water. "Nakamura. Terms are terms. The woman and the cores, and you four walk away rich in everything but conscience. You shook on it."

Every head turned to Cyrus.

Tuck watched him stand there in the gray rain-light, and he understood suddenly that the worst moment wasn't the lie itself—it was this, the moment the lie became load-bearing, the moment the whole structure leaned on it. Cyrus opened his mouth. Closed it. And Tuck, who read voices, heard the silence before the sentence and knew exactly which sentence was coming, and hated him for it, and hated himself more for the small animal part of him that wanted to hear it anyway—that wanted Cyrus to say *the deal's off* and make the world simple again.

"I made a bargain to keep us alive," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus." Priya's voice was low and terrible. "Tell me that's not what I think it is."

"It bought us a night—"

"A night to sell her." Priya stepped between Cyrus and Reyes, and Tuck saw the hydrologist plant herself like data, like a finding that would not be argued with. "You sat at our fire. You ate our food. You let her tell you about the vent shaft, about what the mineral does, about her crew dying one by one—and the whole time you were pricing her."

"I was pricing *all of us,*" Cyrus said, and his voice broke on it, and Tuck heard the truth in that at least, the awful arithmetic underneath: four lives against one, debt against conscience, the same ledger Cyrus had been keeping since Lowmark. "You think I wanted this? I owe these people my *life*, Priya. They own me down to the marrow. I thought if I could just get through this job—"

"That's what they count on." It was Reyes who spoke, pushing forward, gray-faced and burning-eyed. "Men like you, men who owe. The syndicate doesn't collect money, Nakamura. It collects *men.*"

"Enough." Marek stepped off the boat onto the gravel, and the sound of his boots was very loud. He walked to the waterline, unhurried, and Tuck's stomach dropped, because he'd seen Marek move like this before—on watch, checking lines, always competent, always too practiced—and he'd filed it away and said nothing, and now the file was open and spilling.

"Marek," Tuck said. "Don't."

Marek looked back at him. Just for a second. Something crossed his face that Tuck had no frequency for—something that looked almost like apology—and then it was gone, shuttered, and Marek raised both hands toward the boats in the old flat gesture of parley.

"The deal stands," Marek called. "Reyes and the cores for passage. I'm witness for the Syndicate side. Nobody else has to die today."

"You son of a bitch," Priya breathed.

"I'm the man who keeps his contracts," Marek said, without turning around. "Someone on this boat has to be."

The rain thickened. Overhead the storm-wheel ground down a notch, lower, and the hum rose through the gravel bar and up through the soles of Tuck's feet, and far to the south, past the gorge mouth, the sky had gone a bruised green-black that every chaser in the basin would know on sight. Landfall, early. Priya had called it yesterday and nobody had wanted to believe her.

Tuck stood in the wheelhouse doorway with the whole world splitting along a seam he could feel in his teeth. On the gravel, Cyrus and Priya and Reyes drew together like iron filings finding a new field. By the waterline, Marek stood facing the guns, his back to the crew he'd been sent to watch, or to erase, or—Tuck couldn't tell anymore, couldn't separate the man from the orders, maybe the man had never existed at all except in the small hours when he'd covered Tuck's watch and left tea where Tuck would find it.

And Tuck himself stood in the seam. Angry enough to spit at Cyrus. Needing him anyway. Hating that need most of all.

"Nakamura!" the runner bellowed through the rain. "Last call! The weather doesn't wait and neither do we!"

Cyrus looked at Tuck. Across thirty feet of rain and ruin, he looked straight at him, and his mouth shaped words the engines half-swallowed—but Tuck read lips the way he read voices, and the words were: *I'm sorry. Trust me one more time.*

Trust me. As if trust were a mug of tea you could hand through a door.

The first thunder rolled down the gorge—not the storm's thunder but deeper, older, a groan out of the canyon walls themselves, and the river beneath the boats shivered and began, very slightly, to rise.

Tuck picked up the handset.
