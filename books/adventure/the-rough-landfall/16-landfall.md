## 16. Landfall

The sky broke before the sky was supposed to.

Priya felt it first in her teeth—a pressure change so violent her fillings ached, the air itself turning dense as wet wool. She had been watching the gorge walls for an hour, reading the seep lines where groundwater bled from the sandstone, and every one of them had gone dark. The river wasn't rising. The river was *bracing*.

"Tuck," she called over the engine's laboring growl. "Get the barometer."

"I've been getting it." Tuck's voice came thin over the wind that funneled down the gorge ahead of schedule, wrong-schedule, three days early. "It's falling like it's got somewhere to be. Forty millibars in two hours. That's not a monsoon, Priya. Monsoons don't *sprint*."

Priya looked up. Through the notch of the gorge ahead she could see it: a wall of cloud stacked to the stratosphere, green-black at its base, rotating with a slow, obscene patience. It should have been a hundred kilometers east, still out over the lowlands, gathering itself according to every model she'd ever trusted. Instead it had turned. It had come for them—or for the thing beneath them, the vent, the source, whatever slept in the rock and breathed weather into the sky.

The standing storm overhead had been spinning for weeks, maybe years, fed by the mineral vapor bleeding from the canyon. Now the monsoon's outer bands were reaching down to mesh with it, gear into it, and the two systems were becoming one organism. She could see the seam where they joined—a ragged spiral of cloud descending into the gorge like a funnel being lowered by God's own careless hand.

"It's feeding on it," she said aloud, not caring who heard. "The source is feeding the landfall. We're sitting in the throat of something that shouldn't exist."

Behind her, Ilsa Reyes clutched the case of survey cores to her chest like a child. Marek Voss stood at the stern with his rifle slung and his face unreadable, watching the same sky, and Priya caught it—the flicker, half a second, of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

"Reyes," Marek said. "How bad?"

Ilsa didn't look up. "Worse than your employers planned for. Better than I hoped."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Ilsa said, "that everything we argued about in that cave is about to become academic. Hold on."

The river took the boat then, as if it had been waiting for permission.

* * *

Cyrus had run storms his whole life. He knew their grammar—the way a supercell announced itself, the way a gust front arrived like a slammed door, the particular green light that meant hail and the flat yellow light that meant worse. He had stood in the open flats of his debt-ridden youth and let tornadoes pass within four hundred meters because the data was worth it, because the syndicate paid per verified mesocyclone, because miles were money and money was freedom.

Nothing in his life had prepared him for this.

The gorge turned the storm into a weapon with their name on it. Wind came down the canyon walls in sheets, rebounded off the water, came back again from angles wind had no right to come from. Rain fell sideways, then upward, then in ropes thick enough to see individually. Lightning walked the rimrock in continuous stuttering steps, and each flash showed him the river transformed—a brown beast in full spate, carrying whole trees, carrying red silt torn from country that hadn't seen rain in a season.

"Priya! Can we make the bend?" He had to scream directly into her ear.

"If the bend still exists!" she screamed back. "Everything past here is new geography!"

That was the truth of it, Cyrus realized. The maps were dead. Every chart Tuck had digitized, every sounding Priya had taken on the way up—all of it described a river that no longer existed. They were running blind through a gorge that was being actively rewritten around them, and somewhere behind them Gant's boats were running the same blind water with guns aboard.

A shape loomed off the port bow—a floating island of debris, rootball and mud and a dead antelope tangled in fence wire, spinning slowly. Cyrus put the tiller over and they slid past it close enough to touch. The antelope's eye rolled at him, white and enormous, and he would carry that eye for the rest of his life.

Then the bend came, and the bend was wrong.

Where the charts showed a long right-hand curve around a sandstone spur, the flood had found a shortcut—a fresh channel scoured through soft rock, a chute of white water dropping two meters where none should drop. Priya saw it a half-second before he did; he felt her whole body flinch against his back.

"Left! LEFT—"

He went left. The boat went left. For one suspended moment they were airborne over water that hadn't been there yesterday, and then the world became noise—white roar, black water, the hull slamming something soft-then-hard, Tuck's radio case skidding across the deck, Ilsa's cry as the core case nearly tore from her arms.

Cyrus caught the case. He didn't decide to catch it; his hands decided, twenty years of salvage instinct overriding everything. And in the half-second his hands were full of Reyes's life's work instead of the tiller, the boat broached, and the river reached in and took what rivers take.

It took Tuck.

* * *

Tuck had been clipped to the deck rail—Tuck was always clipped to something, a habit from a childhood spent on boats with adults too distracted to notice a kid overboard—but the clip held and the rail didn't. One moment there was steel under small hands; the next there was only brown violence, cold enough to stop breath, loud enough to erase thought.

The river did not feel like water. It felt like being shaken by something vast and indifferent, a dog worrying a rag. Tuck tumbled, surfaced, gulped half the Kestrel Basin, went under again. The world below the surface was a churning amber murmur shot with bubbles and branches, and somewhere in it, absurdly, Tuck's mind kept working—it always kept working, that was the curse of it—cataloguing: *current velocity maybe six meters per second, entrained air reducing buoyancy, hypothermia onset in minutes, this is what drowning data feels like—*

A hand closed on the collar of the rain jacket.

Not Cyrus's hand. Cyrus was forty meters upstream, fighting the tiller. Not Priya's. This hand was huge, hard as dock pilings, and it hauled with a strength that seemed offended by the river's opinion.

Marek Voss had gone over the side without a line, without a word, without the calculation that had governed every second of his adult life. Later, lying exhausted on the deck while Priya compressed his hypothermic arm, he would not be able to explain it even to himself. There had been no decision. There had been a boy going under, and there had been water between, and the man Marek had spent thirty years building—the ledger-keeper, the order-taker, the closer of accounts—had simply not been consulted.

Now he pulled. Tuck clawed at him, all elbows and panic, and he wrapped one arm around the small chest and swam for the eddy behind the debris island with the flood trying to peel them apart at every stroke. His boots filled. His rifle, slung across his back, dragged at him like an anchor—he refused to let it go, some last vestige of the man he'd been—and the weight of it nearly finished them both.

They hit the eddy. The boat, Cyrus wrestling it around in a screaming arc, came alongside, and hands pulled them in—Cyrus's hands, Priya's, Ilsa's, all of them at once.

Tuck lay on the deck coughing the river back up, alive, furious, alive. Marek lay beside the boy and stared at the sky, at the rotating horror above the gorge rim, and understood with total clarity that he had just done something that could never be entered in any ledger. You couldn't balance it. You couldn't amortize it. A man who saves a child has told the universe what he is, and the universe does not accept revisions.

His hands were shaking. Not from the cold.

"That," Tuck wheezed, when breathing was possible again, "was the stupidest thing anyone's ever done for me."

"Noted," Marek said.

And something passed between them—not forgiveness, not yet, not anything with a name—but a recognition, terrible and permanent, that neither of them would ever be able to pretend the other was a stranger again.

* * *

Gant's lead boat came around the bend ten minutes later, riding the flood like it had been born to it, and there was nothing anyone could do.

Cyrus saw them first—three hulls, gunwales crowded with fighters in oilskin, Mother Gant herself standing upright in the bow of the lead boat with a rope in her fists and the storm light making her face look carved from wet wood. Her runners had tracked them from the toll bridge exactly as Marek had confessed: the toll had never been information. The toll had been a leash.

There was no running. The gorge narrowed ahead into the standing storm's rain core, water like hammered iron, and Gant's boats had the current's favor and numbers besides. Rifles came up on the lead hull. Cyrus put his body between the muzzles and the case of cores and waited to die, and discovered, strangely, that he wasn't afraid—only furious, furious at the waste of it, at the whole rotten arithmetic of a world where a thing like the source existed and men like these had come to collect it.

But Gant didn't want bodies. Bodies were overhead.

"The cores!" she roared across the water, voice cutting the rain like a blade through silk. "Give me the case and you float home! Refuse, and I take it and let the river sort the rest!"

"They're worthless without Reyes!" Cyrus roared back. "You can't read them! You can't sell them!"

"Girl Reyes reads them for me, then!" Gant laughed, and it was a horrible sound, joyful and cruel. "Everyone sells, chaser! Some just take longer to know their price!"

What happened next happened fast, the way catastrophes do—no deliberation, just a sequence of failures arriving in order. Ilsa Reyes, half-dead in the bottom of the boat, made her choice before anyone could stop her: she swung the case over the gunwale, toward the water, toward destruction—

And a grapnel thrown from Gant's boat caught the handle midair.

The line snapped taut. The case jerked, spun, tore free of Ilsa's grip, and went arcing across the brown water into the hands of Gant's fighters, who hauled it in whooping like fishermen landing a marlin. Ilsa screamed—a raw, animal sound, twenty years of work ripped away in three seconds—and lunged after it, and Cyrus caught her around the waist and held her while she beat her fists against his arms.

Gant's boats swept past, close enough that Cyrus could see the case lashed down in the lead hull, could see Gant looking at him with something almost like pity.

"Debt's cleared, Nakamura!" she called. "Syndicate pays for delivery! Consider yourself a free man!"

Then the rain swallowed them, and the sound of their engines faded downstream toward the source, toward the vent, toward the place where the sky was being born.

* * *

They regrouped in a slot canyon fifty meters up the bank, a dry fold in the rock where the wind couldn't reach. Nobody spoke for a long time. The storm raged overhead with the patience of geology.

Ilsa sat with her back against the stone, hollow-eyed. "Twenty years," she said finally. "Every core. Every calibration. In the hands of a woman who thinks data is cargo."

"We get it back," Cyrus said.

"How? Look at us." She gestured at the crew—Priya wringing out instruments, Tuck gray-lipped and shivering under every scrap of dry cloth they had, Marek standing apart with his ruined arm and his ruined certainty. "We have half a boat, no fuel to speak of, and the largest storm in recorded history coming down our throats."

Cyrus looked at his crew. At the hydrologist the academy had discarded and the river had proven right. At the kid who'd drowned and come back angry. At the enforcer who'd jumped into floodwater for a boy he'd been ordered to eventually kill, and who now stood with the look of a man whose every map had burned.

"No fuel," Cyrus said. "No data. No deal with anybody." He felt something settle in his chest, calm and absolute, the way the air settles before the worst part of a storm. "Just the five of us and a gorge to run. Gant's heading to the source thinking she's won. But she doesn't know what Reyes knows. She doesn't know what that vent does when you disturb it."

Ilsa lifted her head. "Cyrus. What are you saying?"

He looked at the black spiral above the rim, and heard himself answer with a steadiness that surprised him:

"I'm saying we follow her in. All the way to the source. And we finish what you started."
