## 17. What Marek Owes

The pain woke him before the rain did.

Marek Voss had been shot twice in his life—once in the ribs outside a counting house in Sallow Bend, once through the meat of his shoulder by a man whose name he'd never bothered to learn—and both times the pain had arrived like a professional: efficient, impersonal, almost courteous. This was different. The gorge water had hammered him against stone for what felt like an hour, and now every rib on his left side reported separately, each one a small mutiny. His left hand wouldn't close. There was a gash above his ear that kept filling with rain and emptying again, warm then cold, warm then cold.

He was lying under an overhang of black rock at the river's edge, on a slope of gravel that someone had dragged him onto. The monsoon had come down on the Kestrel Basin like a fist closing, and the standing storm above the gorge had swallowed it whole—the two weather systems married into something vast and screaming, a single organism of water and charge that turned the sky into a ceiling of strobing white.

Someone had covered him with a tarp. That detail reached him before any other did, and it lodged in him like a splinter.

*They covered me.*

He knew what he was to them. He knew what he had been sent to do. And they had pulled him out of the flood anyway, because Tuck had gone under and Marek had gone in after the boy without deciding to, the way a man's hand moves before his mind approves it, and the river had taken its fee from all of them.

Marek lay still and listened to the storm and understood, with the flat clarity of the badly injured, that the ledger of his life had come due.

* * *

The boat was wrecked above the tideline of debris the flood had left—a forty-foot river runner named the *Cormorant*, hull stove in along the port side where it had met a submerged fence post traveling at the speed of God's anger. Cyrus Nakamura stood over it in the rain, hip-deep in the shallows, running his hands along the wound in the fiberglass like a man checking a child for broken bones.

Priya Osei crouched beneath the overhang with Ilsa Reyes's journal sealed inside three plastic bags, reading by the light of a chemical stick, her lips moving as she worked through Reyes's dense notation. Tuck sat apart from everyone, wrapped in a space blanket, hair plastered flat, staring at the water with the thousand-yard look of someone who had drowned a little and not entirely come back.

And Reyes herself—Reyes was dead. Marek had watched her die at the vent shaft, arming the charge with Gant's fighters closing in, passing the journal to Priya with hands that were already letting go of everything else. He had heard the detonation sequence engage. He had seen the first tremor move up the canyon wall like a shudder through a sleeping animal.

The source would be collapsing even now. The standing storm would be dying or transforming into something worse. And the survey data—the thing the Vane Syndicate had sent four men upriver to acquire and six more to bury—was ash and vapor and a crater filling with rain.

Except the journal. Except Priya's memory. Except Tuck, who had heard everything. Except Cyrus, who had chosen.

Except Marek, who had been sent to make sure none of them came back.

He tried to sit up and the world tilted and he let it tilt him back down. The gravel was kind about it. Rain found the gap between tarp and rock and ran a cold finger down his neck.

Tuck looked over. Just looked. The boy's eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and there was no fear in them anymore, which was somehow the worst thing Marek had seen in twenty years of work. Fear he could use. Fear was leverage, and leverage was the only language he'd spoken fluently since he was younger than Tuck. But the boy looked at him the way you look at weather—something enormous and past and already survived—and turned back to the river.

Marek closed his eyes. Behind them, the ledger opened itself, unbidden, the way it always did when he was too hurt to keep it shut.

* * *

He had been nineteen the first time the Syndicate paid him to hurt someone, and the someone had deserved it, which was how they always started you. Deserving men. Men who had stolen, cheated, informed. You told yourself the arithmetic balanced. Then the arithmetic got looser. Then it stopped being arithmetic at all and became simply *instructions*, and instructions were a mercy, because a man who follows instructions never has to do the math himself.

Twenty-two years. He could count the bodies if he made himself, and sometimes, in the bad hours, he did. Eleven certain. Four probable. One—he thought of her face, a hydrologist's assistant named Wren who had talked too much to the wrong journalist—one that he still saw when he dreamed, which was rarely, because he trained himself out of dreaming the way you train yourself out of any expensive habit.

His orders for the Kestrel Basin had been simple and he had read them forty times, memorized them the way he memorized everything, because memory was the one vault no one could crack:

*Confirm deposit viability via Meridian's surviving documentation. Locate and secure cores if recoverable. Ensure no expedition personnel return downstream. Assets Nakamura, Osei, minor (radio operator) are expendable. Asset Voss is not.*

*Asset Voss is not.* He had read that line and felt nothing, the way you feel nothing reading your own name on a shipping manifest. Expendable people were cargo. He was the ship. Ships don't drown.

And then a sixteen-year-old with no last name had gone under the water in the gorge, and Marek Voss had gone in after him, and somewhere between the surface and the bottom the manifest had caught fire.

* * *

Cyrus came up the bank and crouched under the overhang, rain sheeting off his oilskin. He didn't speak right away. That was a thing Marek had catalogued about him across nine hundred miles of river: Nakamura wasted no words, but he also didn't spend silence cheaply. When he was quiet, it was because he was deciding something.

"You should be dead," Cyrus said finally. "By your own company's math."

"Yes."

"Your shoulder's dislocated, not torn. Ribs are cracked, maybe three of them. Priya taped you while you were out." He paused. "She didn't have to."

"I know what she didn't have to."

"Do you?" Cyrus sat back on his heels. Up close, in the strobe-light of the storm, his face looked carved out of something patient and unforgiving. "Because here's my problem, Voss. I've spent this whole river watching you. Every campsite, every watch rotation, every time we lost gear or signal. And now Reyes is dead, and Gant has whatever she didn't burn, and my crew is stranded fifty miles from anywhere with a holed boat and half a fuel can, and I'm sitting here next to a man who was sent to kill us. So talk fast, and talk true, because I'm done guessing."

Marek looked at him. The rain filled the pause.

"I'll tell you everything," he said. "But you should know it won't help you forgive me. It's not the kind of story that forgives."

"I'm not offering forgiveness," Cyrus said. "I'm offering a hearing."

That, Marek thought, was more than he'd offered anyone in two decades.

* * *

He told it in order, because disorder was a liar's tool and he was done lying, or wanted to be done, or wanted so badly to want to be done that the wanting itself felt like the first honest breath after drowning.

"The Meridian Expedition wasn't lost," he said. "It was audited. The Syndicate funded it through three shell companies—Kestrel Minerals, the Lowmark dredging cooperative, a church charity that does flood relief. When Reyes's early readings came back, the board understood what she'd found. Not a mineral deposit. A mechanism. A natural atmospheric engine, stable, localized, steerable. Weather prediction is worth billions. Weather *control* is worth nations. Three national grain belts sit inside the monsoon's reach. Whoever owns the source owns the rain, and whoever owns the rain owns the harvest, and whoever owns the harvest owns everything downstream of hunger."

Priya had stopped reading. She was staring at him over the green glow of the lightstick, and her face was doing something complicated—recognition, revulsion, and underneath both, the scientist's terrible appetite for confirmation.

"They didn't lose contact with Meridian," Marek went on. "They cut it. When Reyes refused to sell—she refused, Nakamura, she told their broker to his face that atmospheric physics belonged to everyone or no one—they cut the expedition loose and sent a cleanup team. Six men. I trained two of them." His voice did something he didn't authorize; it roughened. "I wasn't on that team. I was the follow-up. The one who comes later, when the first ones leave loose ends. My job was to find out what survived, retrieve anything usable, and retire anyone who knew enough to matter."

"Retire," Tuck said from across the gravel. The word came out flat and old.

"It's what we called it," Marek said. "I won't dress it in softer cloth. It was killing. It was always killing. The Syndicate doesn't run guns or product anymore—it runs information, and information has a shelf life measured in witnesses."

"And Gant," Cyrus said. "Mother Gant. The toll bridge. Was that yours?"

Marek almost laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that cost him a rib's worth of pain. "Gant is older than my employment. She was a Syndicate asset before I was recruited—river warlord, toll-taker, and eyes. Every boat that passes her bridge reports to Lowmark within the week. She doesn't just take payment in information. She *is* the information network. The Syndicate built her thirty years ago as a tripwire for exactly this scenario—an unauthorized party moving toward the source. Her runners tracking us off the bridge, her offer to buy you out—that wasn't opportunism. That was protocol. We were flagged the hour we paid her."

"So the flood," Priya said quietly. "The flash flood in the narrows. The storms forming against the wind. Was that—"

"No." Marek said it firmly. "That was the basin itself. What Reyes found isn't a weapon anyone built. It's older than the Syndicate, older than mapping. They didn't create it. They want to *own* it—cap it, meter it, sell the forecast. Gant's job upstream was to hold the approaches until an extraction team arrives. Heavy equipment, drilling rigs, a processing barge. They're already assembling it—I saw the manifests before I shipped out. Forty days, maybe less. When the monsoon season settles, they come upriver and they take the canyon, and everyone between here and Lowmark who knows what's under those cliffs becomes a line item."

The rain drummed. Somewhere up the gorge, thunder rolled with a new sound in it—deeper, structural, the mountain rearranging its throat.

"The extraction plan," Cyrus said. "Give me the shape of it."

"Two barges, a drill train, eighty personnel. They'll dam the lower gorge temporarily to control access. They'll build a landing at the old Meridian site. And they'll bring a man named Corvane—" Marek said the name and watched Cyrus go still. "You know him."

"I've met him."

"Then you know he doesn't leave witnesses either. Corvane's signature is total. Sites he closes stay closed. If the Syndicate takes the source, the Kestrel Basin becomes a hole in the map. Every village, every fisher, every kid with a radio. Gone or bought, whichever's cheaper."

Tuck had come closer without Marek noticing—the boy moved like that, quiet as static. He stood at the edge of the overhang, rain streaming off him, and looked down at Marek with an expression that wasn't hate. Hate would have been easier.

"You knew," Tuck said. "On the boat. All that time. You ate our food. You took watches. You fixed the engine with Priya. And the whole time you were going to—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "I checked everyone's kit after the transmitter died. Yours was clean. Do you know what that did to my head? Everyone was guilty except the guilty one."

"I sabotaged it myself," Marek said. "Third night out. Before I understood what any of you were."

"Why?"

"Because a working radio meant the Syndicate could track us, and tracked assets get closed faster. I told myself it was operational discipline." He held the boy's gaze. "It was also because I liked the way you laughed at your own jokes on watch, and I didn't want the listening posts to hear it. Both things were true. I've lived my whole life holding two true things that shouldn't fit together. That's what corruption is, kid. Not choosing wrong. Refusing to choose."

* * *

The storm shifted overhead while the words settled, and for a moment the rain slackened to a hiss, and in that hush they all heard it: a long, subterranean groan rolling down the gorge, the sound of the vent shaft folding, the source dying, the engine of the standing storms tearing itself shut. The ground under the gravel trembled and went still.

Priya spoke first, and her voice was careful, the way you're careful around a wound.

"The journal. Reyes wrote that the mineral vapor ionizes the boundary layer—that's why the storms form against the gradient. With the vent collapsed, the local weather engine is dead. But the monsoon is still making landfall, and the gorge is the only drainage for a hundred square miles of catchment." She looked at the river, which had begun, very slowly, to rise. "We need to be on the water before it peaks. And the *Cormorant* is done."

"Not done," Cyrus said. "Wounded. I can patch the hull breach with the tarp resin and the spare plate. She'll hold for calm water. This won't be calm water."

"Then we run it," Tuck said, and there was no shake in it at all.

Marek pushed himself upright against the rock, and the pain came with him like a debt collector, and he welcomed it, because pain was honest and he was finished with everything dishonest. He looked at the four of them—Nakamura with his ruined boat and his clean arithmetic, Osei with a dead woman's science in her arms, the boy with no last name and all the nerve in the world—and he said the last true thing he had.

"There's a supply cache," he said. "Gant keeps a forward depot twelve miles downstream, left bank, in a limestone cave behind a waterfall—fuel, patch kits, a skiff, and a shortwave rig. It's there for the extraction team. Coordinates are in my head, not on paper; that's how the Syndicate does things." He paused. "I can navigate the gorge below the collapse. I've run these waters twice before, on other people's orders. This time I'd be running them for you. Not because you owe me—you don't, none of you, not ever—but because it's the only payment I have left, and I intend to spend all of it."

Cyrus studied him for a long moment. The lightning gave his face to them and took it away, gave it and took it, like a man deciding whether to exist.

"If you steer us wrong," Cyrus said, "if one thing about that cache smells like a trap—"

"It will smell like a trap," Marek said. "It's mine. Everything I've ever touched does. But it's real, and it's the fastest way downriver, and Corvane's advance scouts reach the basin in six days. After that, the map gets erased." He met each of their eyes in turn, ending on Tuck. "I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is earned and I've spent mine. I'm asking you to use me. Point me at the work and let me pay until I'm spent."

Silence, except the river rising.

Then Tuck, who owed him nothing, who had every reason to let him bleed, tossed him the space blanket he'd been clutching, and said, "Then stop leaking on the rocks. Dead men don't paddle."

It wasn't forgiveness. Marek hadn't asked for it and wouldn't insult them by mistaking it. But he caught the blanket, and pressed it to the gash above his ear, and understood that he had just crossed the only border that had ever mattered—from a man who follows instructions to a man who chooses—and that the country on this side of it had no exit.

Upstream, the mountain groaned again, longer this time, and the first brown tongue of the flood surge slid past their shelter, thick with pine needles and the wreckage of the world above.

"We launch at first light," Cyrus said. "Or what passes for it under that sky."

Marek lay back against the stone, ribs burning, ledger open at last, and began—in the dark, silently, for the first time in twenty-two years—to count the things he could still give away instead of the things he'd taken.

Six days. One river. Four people who had every reason to feed him to the flood.

He intended to be worth the rescue.
