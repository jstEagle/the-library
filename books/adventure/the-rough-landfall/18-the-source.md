## 18. The Source

The river stopped being a river an hour before we understood it had.

Priya felt it first through the hull—a change in the current's voice, a deeper thrum beneath the ordinary rush of water, like something breathing under the bedrock. She called it out from the bow without turning around, and her voice carried that flat calm she saved for the worst news: "We're close. The water knows before we do."

Then the mist began.

It rose off the canyon walls in slow silver columns, not steam and not fog but something between, catching the bruised light of the standing storm above us and holding it, so that the whole gorge seemed lit from within by a lamp behind gray silk. Tuck stood at the radio rig with his headphones half off one ear, and I watched him go still in that particular way of his—total stillness, the stillness of a boy listening to something no one else could hear.

"Cyrus," he said. "You need to feel this."

"Feel what?"

He held out a bare hand over the gunwale. I leaned across and put my own hand into the air above the water, and there it was: a faint prickle against my skin, the static charge of a day about to break open with lightning, except it wasn't in the air ahead of the storm—it was *in* the air, everywhere, rising off the river itself like heat off summer asphalt. My arm hair lifted. The fillings in my teeth ached. Somewhere behind my eyes, a headache was being born.

"It's coming up out of the ground," Priya said quietly. She had her sample kit open on the thwart, vials rattling as the boat rocked. "Charged particulate vapor. It's been feeding the cell overhead for—I don't know how long. Years. Decades." She looked at me, and I saw something in her face I'd only seen once before, the night she told me why she'd lost her chair at the university: awe with nowhere to put itself. "Cyrus, this is the engine."

Marek stood at the stern with his arms crossed, saying nothing, watching the canyon walls the way he always watched terrain—for exits, for ambush angles, for the places men would come from if men were coming. He hadn't spoken more than ten words since the cave, since Reyes put a pistol against his ribs and made him say aloud what he'd been sent to do. But he steered us true through the last set of shallows, reading the water better than any of us, and I had learned in the gorge not to mistake silence for absence.

Ilsa Reyes rode in the middle of the boat with the case of survey cores locked to her wrist by a strap she'd fashioned from a pack frame, and she did not look at the mist. She looked at the walls. She had been here before, or near enough, and whatever she remembered of this place lived in her body now—the way she flinched when the thunder rolled down the gorge, the way her good hand kept finding the case and gripping it, knuckles white, like a woman holding onto a railing over a long drop.

The canyon narrowed. The walls closed in around us, black stone streaked with mineral bloom—veins of pale blue-white running through the rock like frost grown inward, glowing faintly where the mist touched them. The river shallowed, spread, slowed, and then, all at once, ended.

Not ended. *Opened.*

The boat drifted out into a flooded amphitheater perhaps four hundred meters across, ringed by cliffs, and at its center the water turned. Not a whirlpool—something stranger, a slow rotation of the entire pool, wide and patient, and at its heart a shaft of vapor rose from below the surface, thick as a cathedral column, climbing the full height of the gorge and vanishing into the storm base above. Where it met the clouds, the clouds *boiled*. Lightning walked inside the column like veins of fire in smoke, silent until the thunder arrived seconds later, rolling around the amphitheater and back again, never quite leaving.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Even the river seemed to have gone quiet, hushed by the thing in its midst.

Tuck broke first, because Tuck always broke first, wonder outrunning fear in him the way floodwater outruns everything. "It's a vent," he breathed. "It's a *chimney*. The whole storm is sitting on it like a—like a pot on a burner." He had his recorder out, capturing the sound, though I doubted any recording would hold it: the deep subsonic pulse underneath the thunder, the sound of the earth exhaling charge.

"That's Meridian Shaft Three," Ilsa said. Her voice came out scraped thin. "There were two others. Smaller. We mapped them in the first week." She unslung the case at last, worked the latches with her ruined left hand and her right, and lifted out a core sample wrapped in oilcloth. Even through the cloth, even at ten meters' distance, I could see the faint glow bleeding through. "Kestrelite. That's what Dr. Amara named it, informally. It holds atmospheric charge the way charcoal holds heat. Releases it slowly, steadily, over years." She looked up at the boiling cloud base. "This deposit isn't near the storm. It isn't under the storm. It *is* the storm. It has been running this cell for longer than anyone has been alive to notice, and nobody noticed because who studies weather that doesn't move?"

"The syndicate did," Marek said.

Everyone turned to look at him. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in two days.

"They've had geologists in the basin archives for six years," he went on, flat, eyes on the vent. "Quiet surveys. Mineral rights filings under eleven different names. I read the file before I was assigned. They don't want to study it, Nakamura. They want to *mine* it. Crushed kestrelite, dispersed from aircraft along a monsoon front—" he made a small gesture, almost delicate, "—and you steer the rain. You decide which provinces flood and which ones grow. You make drought a policy instrument." His jaw tightened. "That was the plan I was briefed on. There were slides."

"There are always slides," Ilsa said bitterly.

*

We beached the boat on a shelf of black stone at the amphitheater's eastern rim, wedging her between boulders, and climbed to a ledge thirty meters up where the vapor thinned enough to breathe without our teeth aching. From there we could see the whole impossible machine of the place: the rotating pool, the luminous column, the storm crouched on top of it like a predator that had found a kill too large to leave.

Reyes spread her journal pages on the rock and weighted them with stones, and for an hour she taught us what she had paid eight lives to learn.

The vent shaft, she explained, ran down through the limestone like a throat—open at the top, choked at depth with a plug of collapsed rubble that the expedition had sounded with sonar on their ninth day. The rubble plug was the only thing keeping the deposit from venting freely; the steady, metered release through the narrow throat was what made the storm stable, permanent, self-sustaining. A standing wave of atmosphere, fed from below.

"But the plug is unstable," she said, tapping a hand-drawn cross-section. "Seismically active. We recorded tremors every forty hours, regular as a pulse. If it ever let go naturally, the whole deposit would discharge at once."

"What happens then?" Tuck asked.

Ilsa looked at him for a moment, and I saw her decide that a sixteen-year-old deserved the same honesty as everyone else. "The largest single atmospheric energy release ever recorded," she said. "Every joule this system has stored and regenerated, dumped into one convective event. A superstorm unlike anything in the historical record. Winds over three hundred kilometers per hour at the surface. Rainfall measured in meters per day. And it would run for days, maybe weeks, until the deposit was spent." She paused. "It would erase the source, the gorge, and everything downstream for two hundred kilometers. Lowmark would drown."

The word landed on all of us. Lowmark. The river town where we'd hired on, where Tuck had a room over a chandler's shop, where Priya had left most of her books. Two hundred kilometers downstream.

"So we can't collapse it," Priya said slowly. "Even setting aside the moral question, the physics won't allow a controlled demolition. Any breach of the plug—"

"I didn't say we couldn't control it," Ilsa said. "I said the natural failure would be catastrophic. A controlled failure is different." She pulled another page from the journal—a diagram dense with her cramped handwriting—and laid it flat. "Meridian drilled four charge boreholes into the plug before the end. We intended to study the plug's response to small detonations. The charges are still in place. Still armed. All they lack is a firing sequence, and the firing unit is in the base camp, in the equipment locker, where we left it." Her voice roughened. "Where I watched them carry everything else out and leave it, because it was worthless to thieves who wanted data, not demolition."

"And if you fire those charges?" Marek asked. "In sequence? What does controlled look like?"

"A managed collapse. The plug comes down in stages, the throat seals progressively, the discharge stretches over weeks instead of minutes. Still enormous. Still a monster storm—but a monster we can see coming, one the basin can evacuate ahead of, one that spends itself against empty country upstream instead of drowning the delta." She looked around at each of us. "And when it's done, the source is gone. Sealed under fifty meters of rock. No mine. No monopoly. No weapon. Just weather again—ordinary, unpredictable, free weather."

Silence, except the thunder, which had become so constant it was nearly silence itself.

"The math says someone has to stay," Tuck said quietly.

We all looked at him.

"The firing unit's manual," he said. "You said it. Manual initiation, staged delays. Somebody has to set the sequence and hold the trigger through the first stage, because remote failsafes—" he shrugged, miserable, brilliant, "—remote failsafes are exactly how you get a partial collapse that leaves the vent half-open and makes everything worse. Somebody stays. Somebody fires it. And the first stage goes off before they can get clear."

"Not necessarily," Ilsa started.

"How long?" Tuck asked. "Between trigger and first stage?"

She didn't answer.

"How *long*, Dr. Reyes?"

"Ninety seconds," she said. "If the mechanism hasn't degraded. It's been sitting in the rain for five months."

Tuck nodded slowly, and I watched him do the arithmetic that sixteen-year-olds should never have to do, and I watched him arrive at the answer without flinching, and that—that was the moment I understood what this chapter of my life was going to cost, and that I would pay it myself rather than let a boy pay it for me.

"No," I said.

"Cyrus—"

"*No.*" Louder than I meant, and the thunder swallowed it anyway. I stood up on the ledge, and the charged air made my skin crawl, and I pointed back downriver, toward the drowned dark of the lower gorge. "Nobody's staying. Nobody's dying on this rock so the rest of us can write books about it. We find another way or we walk away. Those are the options."

"There is no other way," Ilsa said, not unkindly. "And walking away means Gant takes it. Her runners are already behind us—you know they are. And behind her runners is the syndicate's extraction team, and behind them, monsoon season, which arrives in—" she checked nothing, just knew, "—thirty-six hours, give or take. If we leave this place intact, it will be owned within a month, and the storms it makes will be aimed at people. I've seen the slides too, Nakamura. Marek described them well enough."

"She's right," Marek said.

"You don't get a vote," I snapped at him. "You were going to kill us all a week ago."

"Yes," he said simply. "And instead I'm going to stay."

The words dropped into our circle like a stone into the rotating pool below—no splash, just rings spreading outward, changing everything they touched.

"Marek," Priya began.

"I've read the borehole schematics," he said, cutting her off, calm as a man discussing cargo weights. "I'm the only one here who's handled military demolition sequencing. Tuck can build the delay circuit, fine, but someone has to verify the charge integrity after five months of flooding, and that means going down the shaft rim, alone, in the vapor, and staying there while the sequence runs. That's not a sacrifice, it's a job description. It's the job I was trained for." He looked at me, and for the first time since the cave, since the pistol and the confession, his eyes were steady and clean. "I owe a debt too, Nakamura. Mine's bigger than yours. Let me pay mine the way you're paying yours."

"I won't sign off on it," I said, and hated how much my voice shook.

"You don't have to. I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you the plan, so you can argue with it while we still have time to argue." He turned to Ilsa. "Show me the firing unit schematic. Show me the borehole map. And then show me the fastest route back to the base camp, because we have maybe twenty hours before Gant's boats round the last bend, and I'd like the charges set before she arrives."

*

We came down off the ledge as the light failed, and the vent lit our way—not the lightning, but the column itself, glowing faintly now as the dusk deepened, a pillar of cold blue fire standing in the middle of the roaring dark. Tuck walked beside me carrying the dismantled pieces of the firing unit wrapped in his jacket, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be either a lie or a goodbye.

At the boat, Priya caught my sleeve. She glanced back to make sure the others were out of earshot, and then she said, low: "He'll do it whether we agree or not. So we have one night to find a way to make ninety seconds enough." Her grip tightened. "I'm a hydrologist, Cyrus. Water finds paths. Shafts have side channels—we saw them on Reyes's sonar sheets, secondary conduits off the main throat. If we flood the lower galleries before the first stage fires—" she didn't finish it, because it wasn't a plan yet, only the seed of one, a desperate hydrologist's instinct that water might do what mercy couldn't.

Above us, the standing storm gathered the night into itself, and the first fat drops of the early monsoon struck the black stone around our feet like a countdown beginning.

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, Tuck's radio crackled to life in his hand: engines. More than one. Coming fast.
