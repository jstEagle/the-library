## 19. Gant's Last Toll

The storm had stopped being weather. That was Priya's first coherent thought as they climbed the last shelf of rock toward the vent, hunched against a wind that came from every direction at once. Weather had patterns. Weather could be read, anticipated, argued with. This was something else—a sky with intent, a monsoon that had arrived a full week early and then refused to move on, as though it had found what it came for.

Rain fell sideways. It fell upward. It fell in sheets so dense that the gorge walls dissolved into gray suggestion, and Priya clung to a wet granite handhold and thought, absurdly, of her dissertation defense, of a lecture hall in which a man with a pipe had asked her what she thought a river was. She had given him a good answer then. She was not sure she had one now.

*The river is a memory of the land,* she had said. *It remembers every slope it has ever crossed.*

The Kestrel remembered nothing anymore. The water below them had gone the color of wet iron and moved with the smooth, muscular indifference of something that no longer cared what it carried. Somewhere in that flood was the boat—what remained of the boat—lashed into an eddy behind a spur of rock where Tuck and Cyrus had fought it for two hours while the sky tried to peel it off the earth.

Above her, Ilsa Reyes climbed like a woman half her age and twice as stubborn, the survey case strapped to her back, her bad leg dragging on every third step. Marek moved behind her, close enough to catch her if she fell, and Priya had stopped being surprised by that. Whatever Marek Voss had been sent upriver to do, he had spent the last two days doing something else entirely: carrying, hauling, bleeding quietly from a gash on his scalp that he refused to let anyone properly dress.

"Two hundred meters!" Cyrus shouted, from somewhere ahead. The wind ate half of it. "Vent's over the saddle!"

Two hundred meters. Priya put her head down and climbed.

* * *

The vent did not look like an engine. It looked like a wound.

They came over the saddle into a bowl of shattered rock, and there it was: a shaft perhaps thirty meters across, breathing. That was the only word for it. The air above it shimmered and pulsed, and out of it rose a vapor so faint it was almost a rumor—a glittering haze that bent the light the way heat bends it over summer asphalt, except that here it rose in a slow, spiraling column, climbing two hundred meters, five hundred, until it fed the rotating belly of the storm overhead. The cloud base turned above them like a wheel seen through dirty glass, and every few seconds the whole gorge lit a bruised violet, and the thunder came not as sound but as pressure, a fist against the chest.

Priya's instruments—what was left of them—had been screaming for two days. She had stopped trying to record and started trying to understand, and even now, soaked and shaking, the scientist in her stood at the rim of that shaft and felt the old, shameful awe.

*Charge separation on a scale that shouldn't exist.* The mineral vapor rising from the vent was seeding the atmosphere itself, organizing the chaos overhead into something with a spine. Reyes had been right. This was not a storm cell. This was a machine, and someone had built the world a weather station without telling the weather.

"Reyes." Marek's voice, flat and loud. "Charge placement. Where."

Ilsa was already moving along the rim, favoring her leg, reading the rock the way Priya read water. "The shaft narrows at forty meters down. Old collapse shelf. We put the charges there, on the shelf, and bring the throat down on itself." She glanced at Cyrus. "Your demolition experience?"

"Oil rig salvage," Cyrus said. "Ten years ago. It'll do."

"It'll have to." Ilsa unslung the case and knelt, and her hands, when she opened the latches, were steady in a way the rest of her was not. Inside, nested in foam, lay the charges—salvaged, improvised, held together with wire and prayer—and beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, the last of her survey cores, the ones she had not destroyed, kept only because destroying the vent required knowing exactly where to strike.

Priya crouched beside her. "Ilsa. The timing on this—"

"Is terrible. I know." Ilsa looked up, and in the violet strobe her face was all planes and shadows, older than Priya had ever let herself see it. "We arm it, we get clear, and we hope the storm doesn't decide to discharge through the shaft before the charges fire. If it does—" She shrugged. "Then we'll be a footnote in someone else's science."

"And if Gant gets here first?"

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Then Gant owns the sky."

* * *

They worked in pairs, because the wind made solitary work a way of dying. Cyrus and Marek rigged the main line down the collapse shelf, rope and pitons and the kind of profanity that crosses all professional boundaries. Tuck stayed at the top with the detonator, cross-legged over the firing box like a small god at a console, radio headset clamped on, calling wind shifts in a voice pitched to carry.

"—another gust front, west side, big one—Cyrus, if you're on the shelf when it hits, you're a flag, get off the rope—"

Priya's job was the water. Someone had to watch the river, and she had appointed herself before anyone could argue, because watching water was the one thing she still knew how to do. She stood at the bowl's downstream edge with her gauge and her soaked notebook and watched the Kestrel climb.

It had risen four meters since morning. The tributaries above the gorge, all of them feeding off the same corrupted sky, were pouring themselves into the main channel, and the flood crest was still hours away. She did the arithmetic she did not want to do: charges armed at, say, midday. Collapse of the shaft, minutes. And the escape—a run down the gorge in a half-dead boat through floodwater that would be moving faster than anything she had ever guided a hull through.

It didn't matter. She wrote the numbers anyway, because numbers were her prayer, and then she heard Tuck's voice change.

"—contact. Contact! Boats, downstream side, two—no, three—"

* * *

They came up the gorge like wasps out of a struck nest.

Priya saw them first as movement against the gray, then as hulls—long, shallow raiding boats, the kind Mother Gant's runners used, each packed with armed figures in oilskin. They rode the flood with a skill that turned her stomach, using the current they should have feared, surfing the standing waves to the base of the bowl.

"Gant herself?" Cyrus shouted, hauling himself off the rope.

Tuck's head was down, headset on, and then up again. "Can't tell. There's a big one behind. Slower. She's on it—I can feel it, she's got a horn or something, they're signaling—"

"How many?"

"Twelve on the water. More behind. Cyrus, we can't fight twelve."

"We're not going to fight twelve." Cyrus looked at the charges, half-rigged, and at the shaft, breathing its glittering breath, and at the sky wheeling above it all. "We're going to finish the job and be somewhere else when they arrive. Marek—"

"I know." Marek was already moving. "Faster."

They worked faster. Priya watched the raiders' boats swing into the eddies below the bowl and the first climbers start up the rock, and she did her own arithmetic again, the other kind, the kind with people in it. Twelve against five. Rifles against wire and prayer. And somewhere in her chest a cold, clear voice that sounded like every professor who had ever failed her saying: *you were always going to die for someone else's discovery.*

She told the voice to be quiet and went to help Tuck with the firing box.

* * *

The first rifle shot was very small against the storm. That was the strange thing. Priya had expected violence to announce itself, and instead it arrived as a flat crack, almost polite, and a chip of rock burst a hand's width from Cyrus's shoulder.

"Down! Everybody down!"

They scattered behind the boulders at the rim, and the fight became what fights become in bad weather: a matter of gaps and chances. The raiders climbed in ones and twos, slowed by the same wind that slowed everyone, and the crew's only advantage was that they were already here, already positioned, and already desperate.

Marek fought the way he did everything—economically, terribly, without wasted motion. Priya saw him take a rifle from a man at the rim's edge with a movement she barely registered, saw the man go over the edge into the flood, and saw Marek's face afterward and understood that the ledger in his head had another line in it now, and that he would carry it.

Tuck, pressed flat beside her, was shaking. Not with fear, Priya realized. With fury.

"They're going to take it," Tuck said. "They're going to take it and steer the monsoon and decide who eats. Priya, they're going to *own the rain*—"

"Then we finish it." Priya looked at the firing box. The wire ran down to the shelf, to the charges, and the charges were set. All of them. She had watched Cyrus and Marek place the last one not ten minutes before the boats came. "Tuck. It's ready. It's been ready."

Tuck stared at her. "Then why hasn't—"

"Because someone has to hold the switch and be sure. Because if we fire early and the shaft doesn't close, we've told Gant exactly how to do it better." Priya's hands were steady on the box, and she was distantly amazed at herself. "And because Ilsa said she'd call it. It's her data. Her call."

* * *

Ilsa Reyes made her call standing up, in the open, with bullets in the air.

Priya saw her rise from behind the boulder where Marek had put her, saw her plant her bad leg and face the shaft, and for one suspended moment the storm lit the whole bowl violet and Priya saw her clearly: a woman who had lost an expedition, a career, a leg, and nearly her life to this place, standing at the rim of it like a scientist at a lectern.

"It's going to discharge!" Ilsa screamed, and Priya felt it too, then—the hair on her arms lifting, the glitter of the vapor column brightening, the whole atmosphere above them tightening like a held breath. The storm was reaching down. The machine was about to cycle. "Now! Fire it NOW, while the column's charged, it'll carry the collapse—PRIYA—"

Priya's thumb was on the switch.

She never remembered pressing it. She remembered Ilsa turning, in the strobing light, and pressing something into her free hand—oilcloth, journal-weight, warm from a body—and she remembered Ilsa's voice, very close, very calm, saying: "Publish it. All of it. Don't let them make it a weapon, don't let anyone make it a weapon. Make it *weather.* Make it belong to everyone."

Then the world went white.

* * *

The collapse was not an explosion. It was an exhale.

The charges fired on the shelf, and forty meters of throat came down in a single grinding roar, and the vent's breath—its glittering, charged, impossible breath—was cut off mid-spiral. For one second the column hung in the air, severed from its source, and the storm overhead seemed to lean down after it, a dog lunging at the end of a chain.

Then the discharge came, and Priya learned what lightning was for.

It came down the shaft in a pillar of white fire, every volt the sky had been hoarding for a week, and the sound was not thunder but the end of thunder, the sound that thunder dreams about. The bowl lit like the inside of a lamp. The rock under Priya's hands buzzed. And when her vision cleared, the shaft was a crater, the vapor was gone, and the great wheel of cloud overhead was already stumbling, losing its spine, unraveling into ordinary rain.

Ordinary rain. She was on her knees in it, laughing or crying, she couldn't tell which, and it was just *rain*, formless, meaningless, free.

Then she saw the rim. Where the discharge had grounded—where the shaft's edge had been—Mother Gant stood.

Priya never learned why she was there. Close, perhaps, to the thing she had come to own. Refusing, perhaps, to believe it could be taken from her. The big boat had reached the bowl's edge; her fighters were falling back from the crater's heat and light; and Gant alone walked toward the vent's rim with her arms slightly out from her sides, a warlord approaching a treasury that had just burned.

The ground under her feet was still settling. The shaft had gone down and kept going, the collapse shelf failing beneath the discharge, the whole throat unzipping into the dark. Priya saw the rim crumble in a long, silent-looking slough, saw Gant stop, saw her look down into the smoking depth with an expression that even at that distance Priya could read.

It was not fear. It was accounting. The look of a woman checking a ledger and finding, for the first time in her life, that the numbers did not balance.

The rim went. Gant went with it, without a cry, without a gesture, swallowed by the throat she had crossed a river to own, and the dust of the collapse rose up and took her the way the river takes everything, and the rain came down harder, and harder, and then—strangely, blessedly—softer.

* * *

They found Ilsa twenty meters from the rim, on her back in the shallows of a runoff channel, and Priya knew before she reached her.

There was no wound she could see. The discharge, perhaps. The rockfall. The storm's bill, presented all at once. Ilsa's eyes were open and fixed on the sky, on the unraveling cloud, and her face was peaceful in a way that Priya found unbearable, because it was the face of a scientist who had seen her hypothesis confirmed.

Priya knelt in the water and held her hand and felt it cool, and the journal was still in her other fist, oilcloth-wrapped, and around them the storm that had ruled the basin for a week was dying into weather, into mere weather, into the ordinary falling of ordinary rain.

Cyrus came and knelt on her other side. He said nothing. Marek stood at a distance, hat in his hands, a man at a graveside. Tuck sat down in the floodwater and did not pretend not to weep.

"She made it belong to everyone," Priya said finally. Her voice came out cracked. "That's what she said. Make it weather."

Cyrus closed Ilsa's eyes. "Then we'd better get downstream," he said, "before someone decides to disagree with her."

Priya looked up. The gorge was flooding—the collapse had dammed nothing, but the rain, freed from the machine's appetite, was coming down in warm, relentless sheets, and the river below the bowl had become a single moving idea of water. Their boat was somewhere in it. Gant's raiders were scrambling for their hulls, leaderless, suddenly only men in boats.

And the flood crest—the one she had been dreading all day—was coming down the gorge to meet them.

"Cyrus," she said. "We have to run the river. Now. All of it."

He looked at the water, and then at the journal in her hands, and then at the three people he had left, and Priya watched him do the arithmetic she had watched him do since Lowmark—except that this time, she realized, the numbers were all people, and every one of them was still alive.

"Then we run it," Cyrus said, and turned for the boat.
