## 20. Running Before the Sky

The world ended with a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

Cyrus felt it through the soles of his boots before he heard it—a deep subsonic shudder rolling up through the rock of the gorge, through the water, into the hull of the boat, as if the river itself had flinched. Then came the roar: not thunder, not the standing storm they'd lived beneath for two days, but something vaster, a single continuous detonation that had no beginning and no end, only pressure.

"Downstream!" he screamed, though his voice was nothing against it. "Everyone in—*now!*"

The vent shaft behind them was collapsing. He could see it even from here, even through the curtain of rain turned horizontal by wind that no longer blew so much as *shoved*: the canyon wall above the source bulging, sloughing, whole shelves of limestone peeling away into the white chaos below. The charged vapor that had fed the standing storm for who knew how many centuries was venting all at once now, a geyser of ionized mist rising half a mile into the monsoon ceiling, and the sky drank it and went mad.

The cloud above them rotated. Not drifted—*rotated*, a slow terrible wheel visible even in the dark, lit from within by flicker after flicker of lightning that didn't flash so much as breathe.

Priya hauled Ilsa's journal against her chest inside its oilskin wrap. Tuck was already at the engine, hands flying over the housing. Marek stood at the bow line, soaked to the bone, face turned up toward the rotating sky with an expression Cyrus had never seen on him—not fear exactly, but recognition. The look of a man meeting something he'd been promised all his life and never believed in.

"Marek!" Cyrus shoved him. "*Line off!*"

Marek moved. The cleat came free. And the river took them.

* * *

There was no steering in the first mile. There was only survival arithmetic: keep the bow roughly downstream, keep everyone low, keep breathing between the waves. The gorge had become a chute. Every tributary that fed the Kestrel was in spate simultaneously—the induced storm wasn't content with the canyon anymore; it was reaching out with both hands, wringing every watershed dry into the main channel—and the river had risen six feet in twenty minutes, brown and boiling, carrying entire trees end-over-end like kindling.

A log the size of a man loomed out of the spray dead ahead. Marek didn't shout a warning; he simply put his shoulder against the tiller bar and leaned, and the boat yawed just enough, and the log passed close enough on the starboard side that Cyrus could have touched it, could have counted the rings if there'd been time, which there wasn't, because the next wave was already lifting them.

"How—" Cyrus got one knee braced against the thwart, both hands locked on the gunwale. "How do you know where it's going?"

Marek didn't answer. His eyes were moving constantly—bank to water to bank to the sky to the water again—reading the current the way Tuck read frequencies, the way Priya read sediment lines. Reading it fluently. Reading it like a language learned young, somewhere with a river in it, somewhere he had never once mentioned in all the weeks aboard.

Cyrus filed it away. There was no room in the moment for anything but filing.

The first rapid hit them like a decision made by God. The gorge narrowed around a bend where an old slide had dumped a fan of boulders into the channel, and the river went from fast to vertical, and for three seconds that lasted a year the boat was less a vessel than a leaf in a drain. Water came over the transom in tons. Tuck, lashed to the engine, disappeared entirely under the flood and came up coughing, still working, still working—one hand clearing the fuel line intake while the other held the housing, mouth open in a scream that the river ate whole.

Then they were through, spat out into a long churning pool, spinning, shipping water, alive.

"Bilge!" Cyrus roared. "Bail or we sink!"

Priya was already moving, snatching the dented aluminum pot they cooked rice in, hauling water over the side in great arcing throws, her hair plastered to her skull, her glasses long gone, her eyes squinted nearly shut and still fixed on the water ahead because even drowning she would be reading the river. Tuck got the engine to catch—sputter, die, sputter, catch—and they had steerage again, barely, a wounded animal limping through a country trying to kill it.

Behind them, the sound continued. It did not fade. That was the thing Cyrus couldn't stop cataloguing even as he bailed with his cupped hands: the collapse should have been ending by now, settling into rubble and echo. Instead the roar held steady, sustained, *fed*. The storm above was feeding itself on what the vent had released, and the vent, collapsing, was releasing more, a chain reaction with no floor to it. They hadn't destroyed the source. They had uncorked it.

Ilsa had known. He understood that now, with the clarity that arrives too late to be useful and too sharp to be refused. She'd said the charge might trigger the mother of all storms, and she'd armed it anyway, and she'd stayed, and the last thing Cyrus had seen of her was her silhouette at the shaft collar, small against the rising glow of ionized air, one hand raised—not waving, he decided, not waving, *measuring*—before the first shockwave took the gorge and everything in it.

He bailed water and grieved in the same motion, grief and adrenaline fused into a single compound that had no name, that tasted like copper and rain.

* * *

The second hour was the trees.

The gorge widened briefly into a drowned terrace where the flood had jumped its banks, and the channel became a matter of interpretation rather than fact. Whole cottonwoods sailed past them, root balls like severed heads. One struck a rock ahead and exploded into spinning debris, and Marek threaded them through the wreckage without slowing, calling angles in a flat clipped voice—"left of the fork, hard left, then let it carry"—and Cyrus obeyed because there was nothing else to do, and because the alternative to trusting Marek Voss right now was dying, and he wasn't ready for that yet, though he couldn't have said precisely why.

"You've run water like this before," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question. The boat dropped into a trough and climbed the far side and the words came out sideways, torn.

Marek's jaw worked. Rain sheeted off his chin. "Kamchatka," he said finally. "Before the Syndicate. My father ran freight down the Bystraya when the salmon runs flooded the banks." A pause. A wave. A correction. "I haven't thought about it in nineteen years."

"You never said."

"There was never a reason to." His eyes flicked to Cyrus, and in the strobing half-light Cyrus saw something raw in them, something the enforcer's usual armor had been washed away from. "There's a lot I never said. Most of it's going to stay unsaid. But the water—" he turned back to the channel, hands steady on the bar, "—the water doesn't lie, and neither do I on it. Left of the fork. *Now.*"

They went left of the fork. The boat scraped stone along its keel with a shriek that Cyrus felt in his teeth, and then they were through, and the gorge closed in again, and the sky above went green-black, the color of hail, the color of tornado weather, and began to drop things.

Not rain. Ice. Hail the size of eggs came down in a barrage that sounded like rifle fire on the hull, and Tuck curled over the engine housing with his jacket spread across it, shielding the fuel line, taking hits on his own shoulders and skull without a sound except a thin involuntary whine he probably didn't know he was making. Cyrus grabbed the boy by the collar and dragged him down into the bilge, into the relative shelter of the gunwales, and held him there, both of them crammed among the sloshing water and the spare line and the empty fuel cans, while Marek and Priya rode it out above like figureheads.

It lasted four minutes. It felt like four seasons.

When it eased, Tuck pushed back from Cyrus's grip and looked up, and his face in the lightning-light was bloodied at the hairline and absolutely ferocious.

"The radio," he said. "I can get the radio back."

"Tuck—"

"I *can*." He was already crawling aft, already pulling the wrecked transmitter unit out of its bracket, and Cyrus saw that sometime during the hail—during the hail, this kid, sixteen years old, bleeding—he had been diagnosing. "The antenna mast snapped, that's gone, but the set itself is intact, it's just dead because the power feed shorted when the panel flooded. If I can isolate the short and rig a whip antenna off a piece of the broken mast, I can get receive. Maybe transmit on low power, close range." His hands were shaking—from cold or adrenaline or the blows, impossible to tell—and they were also perfectly certain. "If anyone else is on this river tonight, I want to know about it before they know about us."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. The gorge roared. Somewhere upstream, the world continued to end.

"Do it," he said.

* * *

They ran the third hour blind.

Night had come down—or the storm had simply swallowed the day so completely that night and noon were indistinguishable—and they navigated by lightning alone, each flash a single frame of film: *bend, cliff, hole, rock garden, clear water left.* Between flashes there was only sound, and Marek's voice, and trust. Cyrus had spent his whole life measuring distance in miles and risk in percentages, and here both instruments were useless; the numbers had burned off like fog. What remained was simpler and more terrifying: three other people in a boat, and whether the boat held together, and whether he deserved to be in it.

Twice more they shipped water. Once they spun full circle in a hydraulic below a ledge and sat in the recirculating foam for eight endless seconds, the engine screaming, before the current deigned to release them. Priya stopped bailing at some point and started talking instead, low and constant, naming features as they passed—"scour line's at twelve feet, that's a hundred-year stage, Cyrus, a *hundred-year* stage, this valley has never seen water like this"—because naming was how she kept her hands from shaking, and Cyrus let her talk, and the numbers she gave were horrifying and steadying at once, because horror with units attached was at least a horror you could hold.

Tuck got the radio alive at the top of the fourth hour.

They heard it before he announced it—a thin thread of static resolving, miraculously, into structure—and then the boy's head came up from the guts of the set like a diver surfacing.

"I've got traffic," he said. "Two stations. Maybe three." He pressed the headset hard against one ear, hunched over the unit, rain still finding him through every gap. "One's Gant's people. Their net's still up—relay station downstream must've survived. They're calling boats in to the lower gorge, emergency muster." His eyes lifted, and there was something new in them, something older than sixteen. "And there's another signal. Weak. Repeating. It's not Gant's encryption. It's not Meridian's either."

"Who, then?" Priya asked.

"That's the thing." Tuck pulled a scrap of pencil and wrote on the back of his forearm because there was no paper left in the world that was dry. "The bearing's downstream. Same as us. And the transmission pattern—" he hesitated, and Cyrus watched a kid decide, in real time, how much truth the moment could bear, "—it's a syndicate handshake protocol. I've heard it on smuggler bands before. Lowmark channels. It's a *recovery team*, Cyrus. Somebody's coming upriver."

The gorge carried them onward through the dark while the words settled.

"Coming for what?" Priya asked quietly. "There's nothing left to recover."

"Us," Marek said.

Everyone looked at him. Lightning filled the gorge white, and in that frozen frame his face was grim and calm and terribly awake.

"The Syndicate doesn't spend recovery teams on minerals," he said. "The deposit's gone. The data's gone. Reyes is gone, Gant's gone, the source is a crater filling with rain." He shook his head slowly. "But three witnesses just destroyed a proprietary asset worth three nations' agriculture, and one of those witnesses is mine—an enforcer who went dark. They're not coming for salvage." His hands tightened on the tiller bar. "They're coming to close the account."

Thunder rolled down the canyon, long and continuous, indistinguishable now from the collapse still grinding away behind them.

* * *

Near dawn—or what the ruined sky offered as dawn, a bruised gray suggestion of light—they shot the last of the big water and the gorge opened its jaws and let them out.

The lower basin spread before them: flooded forest to every horizon, treetops standing in brown water like a drowned congregation, the monsoon wall advancing from the east in a front fifty miles wide, lightning walking along its leading edge on legs of smoke. The boat wallowed in comparatively gentle swell, listing to port, engine gasping, every soul aboard hollow-eyed and salt-white with exhaustion even in fresh water.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Cyrus looked back once, upstream. The gorge mouth framed a distant column of cloud rising where the source had been, vast and slow-rotating, a monument to what Ilsa Reyes had chosen and what it had cost. The standing storm was gone—that strange permanent cell that had hung over the canyon for years uncounted—but its energy hadn't died. It had been *spent*, poured into the atmosphere like a ledger being emptied all at once, and the whole basin was going to pay the balance for days.

"She'd have wanted to see the readings," Priya said softly. Her arms were wrapped around the oilskin bundle at her chest. "She'd have hated that she can't."

"Yeah," Cyrus said.

Tuck suddenly stiffened, one hand flying to the headset. "Contact. Close. Downstream bend, maybe two klicks." He turned, and his voice dropped into a register Cyrus had never heard from him—flat, urgent, adult. "Running lights. Two boats. They just went dark, like they heard me listening."

Marek straightened at the bow. Through the gray drizzle, far down the flooded channel, two points of light winked out, one after the other, deliberate as a held breath.

"They know we're coming," Marek said quietly. "Question is what they think is coming." He glanced back at Cyrus, and for the first time since Kamchatka had surfaced from his past, the enforcer looked like a man asking permission instead of giving orders. "They'll expect a hostile boat. Four strangers running dark through floodwater. Give me the radio, Tuck. Let me make the call."

"And say what?" Cyrus asked.

Marek's jaw tightened. "Say whatever keeps us alive long enough to decide who we are now."

Ahead, the drowned forest waited, and somewhere in it, two silent boats.
