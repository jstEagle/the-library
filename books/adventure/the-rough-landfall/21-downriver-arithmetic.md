## 21. Downriver Arithmetic

The river gave them back to the world the way a tide returns what it has taken: slowly, grudgingly, and changed.

Priya felt the shift before she saw it. For nineteen days the Kestrel had spoken to her in a language of violence—eddy lines like knife wounds, boils of water rising from nowhere, the constant low argument of a river carrying too much mountain too fast. Now, somewhere in the grey hour before dawn, the current gentled. The boat stopped fighting her hands. She woke with her palms still curled around the tiller bar and realized the water had forgiven them, or at least stopped trying to drown them, and that the difference between those two things was probably the whole of her profession.

She sat up under the tarp. Marek was on watch, or what passed for watch now, sitting with his back against the engine housing and his wounded arm strapped across his chest, watching the banks slide past in the fog. He'd taken the tiller from her at midnight without asking, the way he'd started doing things without asking—small usurpations of labor that were, she understood, his only available dialect of apology.

"Lowmark by midmorning," he said. "Maybe sooner, if the flood's pushed us."

"I know."

"You've known since yesterday."

"I like saying it." She rubbed her eyes. "It doesn't feel real until it's said out loud."

Marek nodded, and the fog took his nod, and for a while there was only the river and the soft percussion of rain on the tarp and Tuck's breathing in the bow, curled around the radio case like it was a large and temperamental animal that needed guarding even in sleep. Maybe especially in sleep. Tuck had not let the case out of reach since the gorge. Priya suspected the boy slept with one hand inside it, on the transmitter, the way other people slept with a hand on a wound.

The journal was in her coat. It had been in her coat for six days, pressed against her ribs, and she had caught herself more than once resting her forearm over it as they ran the flooded channels, as though the river might reach in and take it. Ilsa Reyes's handwriting, tight and slanted, filling page after page with numbers that no atmosphere should have produced and then, in the last third, with something rarer: doubt. Wonder. The handwriting of a woman who had realized she was holding a loaded weapon and was writing down, as carefully as she could, where she had found it.

Priya had read it four times. She would read it forty more. She already knew, with the certainty she usually reserved for river hydraulics, that she was going to spend the rest of her life with those pages.

* * *

Lowmark announced itself by smell before sight—diesel and drying fish and the green rot of the floodplain—and then by sound, the clang of the cable ferry, the dogs, the particular argumentative music of a river town waking up. Then it came out of the fog all at once: the sagging wharf, the stilt houses leaning into the current like tired men at a bar, the corrugated roof of the fuel depot flashing rust-orange through the mist.

There were people on the wharf. That was the first hard thing. Word traveled faster than boats on the Kestrel, and the flood had been a spectacle even down here, and so a knot of dockhands and gawking children had gathered to see the boat that everyone had already decided was a ghost. Priya heard the name before they were close enough to make out faces.

*Meridian.* Someone said it. Then someone else. Then it was a murmur running along the wharf like water finding a crack.

"They think we're Meridian," Tuck said, standing up in the bow, and there was something awful in how young the voice sounded. "They think we're coming back."

"We're not Meridian," Marek said flatly.

"No," Priya said, and brought the boat in against the pilings with hands that had stopped shaking days ago and now simply ached. "We're what's left of the people who went looking for them."

She tied off. She stepped onto the planks. A dockhand, a heavy woman with a fish-gutting knife still in her belt, looked past Priya into the boat, counting, and Priya watched her do the arithmetic—four faces, not eleven—and watched the woman's face close like a door.

"You're not them," the woman said.

"No."

"Then what are you?"

It was, Priya thought, the only honest question anyone had asked her in weeks. She had no answer that fit in a sentence. *We are the people who destroyed the most valuable discovery in the history of atmospheric science, and we did it on purpose, and we would do it again, and we are broke.* She said, instead, "We need fuel, food, and a place to dry out. We can pay for some of it."

The woman spat into the water, which Priya chose to read as a yes.

* * *

The syndicate found them by evening, because of course it did. Priya had imagined it would take longer—imagined weeks of anonymity, of drying nets and mended hulls before the long hand of the Vane Syndicate reached this far downriver—but she had underestimated, as she had been underestimating all trip, the speed of money.

There were two of them, and they did not look like Marek. That was the first thing she noticed. Marek, whatever he had been, had looked like a man doing a job he believed in, back when he'd believed in it. These two looked like clerks. They wore dry, pressed clothes that had no business in Lowmark, and they set up in the back room of the fuel depot office with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no by a room.

They invited Priya in first. Alone. She went, because Cyrus was still at the wharf arguing with a chandler about a warped propeller shaft, and because she wanted to hear it herself before it was repeated to her secondhand.

The older clerk had a folder. Of course he had a folder. He opened it on the desk between them and turned it so she could read it, which she supposed was meant as a courtesy.

"Dr. Priya Osei," he read. "Formerly of the Calloway Institute of Hydrology. Doctoral work on monsoon dynamics in the eastern basin. Dismissed following the Halloway data dispute." He looked up. His face was kind in the way that furniture is kind. "The Syndicate has reviewed your circumstances and wishes to make you an offer."

"I know what kind of offer."

"I don't think you do." He slid a single sheet across the desk. "The salvage contract was voided when the vessel and cargo were lost. That's standard. However, the debt instruments held against Cyrus Nakamura are separate, and they survive. They are substantial. They are, frankly, unpayable at his current rate of income. The Syndicate is prepared to void them entirely—today, in this room—in exchange for one item."

"The journal."

"The survey materials. Cores, logs, the Reyes field notes. Whatever was recovered."

Priya sat with her hands flat on her knees. She thought about how to say the next part, and decided there was no good way, and said it plainly, the way you'd tell a family about a drowning.

"There's nothing to hand over. The cores went into the gorge with Gant's people. The instruments went with them. And the journal—" she watched his face for the flicker, and got it, a small tightening around the eyes that told her he already knew, that the folder in front of her was theater— "the journal is going to be published. All of it. As science. Open, cited, free to anyone who can read. There won't be a commodity. There won't be anything to own."

The clerk was quiet for a moment. Then he closed the folder, and something in his posture changed—not softer, exactly, but more honest, the mask of courtesy set down like a heavy case.

"You understand what you're describing," he said. "The mineral deposit is gone, yes. We've confirmed that. The vent collapsed; the readings from our remaining assets upstream are—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—inconclusive. But the *knowledge* isn't gone, Doctor. The methodology. Reyes's notes describe how to find such a deposit again. There are other basins. There are other vents. Whoever holds her methodology holds a head start measured in decades."

"And you think I'd trade that for a dead man's debts."

"I think you're a disgraced scientist being offered, in one sheet of paper, the career they took from you. Reinstatement. Funding. The Calloway Institute's board contains three men who would very much like to be the ones who publish the Kestrel findings *with* you, under proper auspices, rather than watch you do it from a river town with no institutional affiliation and no credibility." He folded his hands. "You've been ruined once, Dr. Osei. You know exactly how the world treats a woman with no institution behind her. We're offering to be your institution."

And there it was. Not the crude offer—the real one, the one with teeth, the one that had taken her apart once before. She thought of Halloway. She thought of the committee room, the eleven men, the way her data had been taken apart not because it was wrong but because it was *hers*, and how she had sworn, walking out of that building with her box of belongings, that she would never again let anyone else hold her work.

She thought of Ilsa Reyes, in a cliff cave, guarding a case with a rifle and half a lung, because she had understood before any of them what ownership meant.

"No," Priya said.

The clerk sighed, and it was almost genuine. "Then the debts stand."

"The debts are against a salvage contract for equipment that no longer exists. Void your own contract. I've read the terms—Marek's copy, before he burned it, and Tuck's memory, which is better than paper. There is no cargo. There is no expedition. There is no *consideration*. Your own law clerk could tell you the instruments are dead." She stood. "You don't want the debt. You want leverage, and you've just watched your leverage burn up in a gorge. So take your folder, and your sheet of paper, and go back to whoever sent you, and tell them the arithmetic didn't work."

He studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled—a thin, tired, almost human expression, the smile of a man who had delivered a great many refusals and had learned to recognize the ones that were final.

"The Syndicate disavows the expedition," he said. "Formally, as of this afternoon. Any claims of affiliation, any debts, any obligations—void. You'll find it in the coastal registries by the week's end. Nakamura is a free man, in the sense that we no longer consider him worth the cost of pursuing." He stood, buttoned his jacket. "You should understand that this is not mercy. This is accounting. You've destroyed an asset worth more than his debt, and pursuing you costs more than you're worth. That is the only reason you're all walking around."

"I know," Priya said. "It's still the best news I've had in a month."

At the door, he paused. "The journal, Dr. Osei. When you publish. The Syndicate will read it. We read everything."

"Good," she said. "I wrote the appendix for you. It's the part where the deposit kills everyone who tries to hold it."

* * *

They gathered that night in the room above the ferry office, because it was the only room in Lowmark with a lock and a table, and because none of them could bear to be alone and none of them would have admitted it. Rain on the tin roof. A lamp turned low. Cyrus at the table with the syndicate's voidance notice spread out in front of him, reading it for the fourth time, running his thumb along the seal as if checking it for a pulse.

"It's real," he said. "Tuck checked the registry feed. It's filed. I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "Twenty-two years. I've owed them twenty-two years, since I was younger than Tuck. And it's just—" he made a small motion with his hand, opening it, letting something invisible fall through the fingers. "Gone. Because there's nothing left to collect against."

"Because you burned it," Marek said from the corner. He said it without judgment, the way you'd state the weather. "Because there's nothing left to collect against *because you burned it*."

"Because *we* burned it," Cyrus said. "Don't start handing me the whole weight. You were there."

"I was there for the wrong reasons for most of it." Marek's arm was strapped, his face grey with the fever of a healing wound, and he sat the way men sit when they've decided to stop performing strength. "I'm just saying the arithmetic out loud. You paid a debt worth more than money. Somebody should say it, or you'll spend the rest of your life thinking you got away with something."

Priya watched Cyrus absorb that. Watched him look down at the voidance notice, at the cold language of it—*all claims void, all affiliation disavowed, the party known as Cyrus Nakamura holds no standing*—and watched him understand that the syndicate had, in the manner of all large cruel things, found the most painful possible way to let him go. Not forgiveness. Not even enmity. *Irrelevance.* He had spent his whole adult life being worth something to them, and now he was worth nothing, and the nothing was freedom, and the freedom was its own kind of wound.

"I don't know what I do tomorrow," Cyrus said at last. It came out quiet, almost boyish. "I've known what I do tomorrow since I was nineteen. Chase the storm, sell the data, pay the interest. Every day for twenty-two years, tomorrow was already scheduled." He looked up, and the lamplight caught the lines around his eyes, and Priya thought he looked like a man standing on a riverbank with no boat. "I don't have a tomorrow scheduled."

"You have a boat with a warped shaft," Tuck said, from the floor, where the boy sat cross-legged with the radio case open, soldering iron in hand, repairing something no one had asked to be repaired because the repairing itself was the point. "And a chandler who'll give you parts on credit if you fix his pump barge. I heard him say it."

"You were listening."

"I always listen. That's the whole job." Tuck held a wire up to the lamp, squinting. "You could stay. Lowmark needs boats fixed. Boats are how everyone here measures everything—boats and rain and who you'd trust in one." A pause, the soldering iron hissing. "That's what I'd measure in. If I was starting over. Which I'm not, because I'm staying regardless, so it's not really a question for me, but."

The room was quiet. Rain. River. The distant clang of the ferry cable, keeping its own time.

Priya took the journal out of her coat—she was doing that more and more, taking it out, letting it exist in the light—and set it on the table between the voidance notice and the lamp. Eleven days of Reyes's hand. Numbers and doubt and wonder, ending in the last entry, written in the cave, in letters gone shaky: *If anyone finds this, it belongs to everyone. That was always the point. That was always the whole point.*

"I'm going to need help with it," Priya said. "Translating it. Checking it. The atmospheric sections are beyond me, and the mineralogy is beyond everyone, but the methodology has to be verified before anyone will print it, and verification means—" she looked around the table, at the chaser with no tomorrow, the boy with the radio, the enforcer with one good arm, "—it means a team. An unpaid one. A discredited one. The kind of team nobody funds and everybody should be afraid of."

Marek laughed. It was a rusty sound, a hinge unused for years, but it was a laugh. "You're describing us."

"I'm describing us," Priya agreed. "God help the discipline."

Cyrus reached out and put his hand flat on the journal, not reading it, just touching it, the way you'd touch a keel to check for rot. Then he looked at Tuck, and Tuck looked back, and whatever passed between them was old already, though it was only weeks old—some current established between them that would outlast the river that made it.

"Tomorrow," Cyrus said slowly, testing the word like a new tiller, "I'm going to go look at that pump barge."

And outside, far upstream, where the gorge had been, the rain kept falling—ordinary rain now, unclaimed, belonging to no one—coming down on the whole basin at once, on the living and the drowned and the burned, on Lowmark and its leaning houses, falling the way it always had before anyone learned to want it, and would fall, Priya thought, long after everyone in this room was gone: free, unowned, and impossible to bill.
