## 1. Salt on the Wind

The salt flats began where the world's patience ended.

Vesper Pemberton had crossed them before — twice in her first year as a ranger, once running from a debt she'd rather not name, and now this ninth day, running toward something instead. The difference mattered less than she pretended. Out here the distinction between fleeing and pursuing dissolved into a single white glare, a horizon that offered nothing to hold against, and a man on foot ahead of her who had made nine days of mistakes and was still somehow alive.

Idris. That was all anyone called him. A thief with a courier's stride and a smuggler's luck, who had lifted a sealed oilskin packet from the survey office at Karst Landing three nights after Vesper had signed off a patrol there, and who had then done the one thing no sensible thief ever did: he had run across open country instead of along it.

She'd asked herself why a hundred times since. Thieves ran for cities, for harbors, for crowds where a pursuer's face meant nothing. Idris had run for the flats — flat, featureless, lethal, a place where a man's water told him exactly how long he had left to live and the sky gave him nowhere to hide from that arithmetic. Either he was the stupidest criminal on the coast, or he wanted to be followed.

Vesper preferred not to think about which.

* * *

On the morning of the ninth day the wind came off the eastern ranges carrying the smell of brine and old stone, and Vesper crouched at the lip of a dry wash and read the ground like a letter from someone she used to know.

His prints were two hours old. She could tell by the way the salt crust had already begun to powder back into the hollows, by the faint bloom of dust in the lee of each heel-strike. Two hours. She was gaining — had been gaining since the fourth day, when his pace had begun to shorten and hers hadn't, when the discipline of a decade of desert patrols started paying its slow dividend over whatever desperation drove him. He drank carelessly. She'd found the evidence at every camp: a wet ring on stone, a splash mark where he'd rinsed his hands, once — extravagantly, almost insultingly — the damp ghost of a face-washing at a seep that would have taken her an hour to find and him thirty seconds because he'd simply blundered onto it.

He wasn't rationing. He wasn't counting. A man who counted his water walked differently, stopped differently, chose his camps for shade rather than distance. Idris walked like a man whose water would last exactly as long as he needed it to, which was either excellent planning or a lie someone had told him.

Vesper took a mouthful from her own skins and made herself swallow slowly. Three-quarters gone. By any sane measure she should have turned back two days ago; the ranger's manual said so, in the flat bureaucratic voice of men who had written rules after burying colleagues. *Never pursue past half your water. Never pursue into the deep flats without a second party. Never—*

She had stopped reading the manual years ago, around the same time she'd stopped sleeping more than four hours a night, and for roughly the same reason: both left too much room for quiet, and quiet was where the thinking lived.

Because here was the thing about stopping. Here was the thing she had learned the hard way, on a night ten years gone, and relearned every time she let her feet go still: the moment the chase ended, the memory started. It came in sideways, patient as tide. Her father's hands on the wheel of the Marlowe's longboat. The sound the sea made going down his throat. The way the flare light had turned the water the color of old brass, and how she had swum anyway, because he'd told her to swim, and how she had spent every day since telling herself that obedience was survival and survival was enough.

It wasn't. She knew it wasn't. But motion was a kind of anesthesia, and the flats were the purest form of it she knew — a landscape with nothing in it, no ruins to remind, no graves to pass, just glare and salt and the small animal business of putting one boot in front of the other until the sun went down and she could sleep the dreamless sleep of the genuinely exhausted.

So she didn't stop. She never stopped. And if some small voice at the base of her skull observed that a woman who never arrived anywhere was also a woman who never had to be anywhere, in particular, ever again — well. The wind out here was loud enough to drown most voices.

* * *

Midday came down like a lid. Vesper walked through it in the long shadow of her own body, hat brim pinned low, lips split and tasting of salt that wasn't only from the ground. The flats changed character as she went deeper: near the coast they'd been hardpan, cracked into polygonal plates you could hear your own boots ringing on; out here they softened into something worse, a crust over mud the color of bone, ground that took a print and kept it like a grudge. Twice she crossed stretches where the surface had collapsed into shallow brine pools, each one fringed with crystalline bloom, each one a small mirror in which a woman looked back at her who looked too thin and too old around the eyes to be thirty-four.

In the third pool she saw something else reflected: smoke. Far off to the northwest, a thread of it, gray-brown against the bleached sky, rising from behind a line of low mesas that broke the horizon like the knuckles of a buried hand.

Canyon country. The flats ended there — everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the sea ended at cliffs. The Saltreach Canyons: ruined sandstone labyrinths where an old river system had cut itself into the plateau and then died, leaving slot gorges and overhangs and, according to the surveys she'd helped update, at least eleven springs. Water. Shade. An end to the terrible openness that had protected her pursuit for nine straight days.

He was heading for cover. Which meant, finally, that he was doing what a thief did.

Or — and Vesper turned this thought over with the reluctance of a hand turning a card it doesn't want to see — he was heading for the place he'd always intended to arrive, and everything before it had been theater. Nine days of careless water and sloppy camps, nine days of letting her gain at exactly the rate a good tracker gains, nine days of leading her deeper and deeper from anywhere she could call for help.

She stood at the edge of the brine pool a long moment, watching the smoke thread waver, and felt the old restlessness shift gears under her skin — not quieter but sharper, the difference between a dull ache and a blade.

If it was a trap, she should turn back. If it was a trap, turning back was the only rational act, and she had water enough to make the coast in five days if she pushed.

Vesper looked at the mesas for a while longer. Then she adjusted her pack straps, checked the angle of the sun, and started walking northwest.

*Tell yourself it's duty,* she thought, and the thought had her father's dry humor in it, which was the cruelest thing the flats had done to her all day. *Tell yourself anything. Just keep moving.*

* * *

She caught his camp at dusk — abandoned, still warm in the way of things recently left, a scatter of salt-crusted cloth and the greasy ash of a scrub-fire. He'd burned something. She knelt and sifted the ash with two fingers and found fragments: heavy paper, waxed, the charred edge of what might have been a seal. Not his provisions, then. Something he'd carried and decided, nine days in, that he no longer needed whole.

That troubled her more than the smoke had. Thieves stole things to sell them; you didn't burn merchandise in the middle of a salt desert unless the thing was worthless to anyone but the person following you — or unless you'd already taken from it what you needed.

She cast around the camp and found his trail resuming toward the mesas, and beside it, pressed into the soft crust where he'd paused, a single print facing back the way he'd come. Standing. Looking. For how long?

The moon rose fat and white as she climbed the first rampart of broken stone, and the temperature fell through her sweat-damp shirt like a door opening onto winter. Desert nights. Another thing the manual warned about, another thing she ignored. She made cold camp in the shelter of a fallen slab, ate half a strip of dried fish she didn't want, drank two measured swallows, and lay watching stars through the gap in the rocks while her body begged for sleep and her mind, unsupervised for the first time in hours, began its slow familiar work.

*Ten years ago tonight,* said the part of her she couldn't outrun, *the Marlowe went down.*

*No,* said the other part, the one that had gotten very good at this. *Not tonight. Tonight is the ninth of the month. It went down on the eleventh.*

*You've been counting.*

*I count tides. It's a professional habit.*

*You count everything. You counted the days since you last wrote to Sefa. You counted the money you haven't sent Bram. You counted—*

Vesper rolled onto her side and made herself name constellations until the voice wore itself out. The Diver. The Broken Mast — sailors' names, coast names, names that mapped the sky the way her father had taught her, lying on the deck of a ketch with his arm under her head, pointing up with a finger scarred from rigging wire. *That one's yours, Ves. The Diver. Always falling, never lands.*

She slept badly, and dreamed of brass-colored water, and woke before dawn with grit in her teeth and the absolute certainty that she would reach Idris today.

* * *

The canyon country took her in like a throat.

Sandstone walls rose on either side, striped rust and cream, sculpted by the dead river into flutes and hollows that the morning wind played like organ pipes. The light came down in shafts. In places the gorge narrowed to shoulder width and she moved through cool blue shadow with her hand on rock worn smooth by floods that hadn't run in a century; in places it opened into amphitheaters where fallen blocks lay tumbled like the furniture of giants, and old habitation showed itself in stair-steps cut into the walls, in grain bins tucked into cliff seams, in the faded pigment of figures on a sheltered wall — hunters, boats, a line of stick-armed people walking toward a spiral that might have been water or might have been the sun.

People had lived here once, waiting out their own hard seasons. She felt their patience in the stone and it sat wrong against her skin, this place built entirely of stopping.

His sign was easy to follow now — too easy. Boot scuffs on the sandstone dust. A handprint in old soot where he'd steadied himself. Once, absurdly, a strip of cloth tied to a dead branch at a junction of three gorges, fluttering like a courtesy, like a guide's marker.

Like an invitation.

Vesper stood at the junction with her hand on her knife and made herself think it through, because whatever else she was, she was still a ranger, and rangers died of enthusiasm more often than of thirst. He wanted her here. Every sign since the burned packet said so. The smart play was to circle wide, come at the canyons from the north, take him from an angle he hadn't chosen.

She even started to do it. She climbed the eastern wall, found the goat-track, made a quarter mile of flank movement with the wind in her favor.

Then she stopped, in the shadow of an overhang, and admitted the truth to herself with the flat honesty of a woman reading her own water level: she didn't want the smart play. The smart play added a day. The smart play meant another night of the voice, another dawn of arithmetic, another twelve hours of being nobody chasing nothing across a landscape designed to remind her that she had no destination that wasn't a grave.

Nine days was enough. Whatever waited at the end of Idris's trail — a trap, a buyer, a knife in the dark, an answer — it couldn't be worse than the tenth day of this.

She went back down to the marked junction and followed his cloth markers straight in, like a fool, like a daughter, like a woman who would rather chase than arrive and had finally run out of country to chase across.

* * *

The gorge opened at noon into a ruin.

It filled the head of the canyon the way a fist fills a palm: a fortress of the old river people, three stories of sandstone block stacked against the cliff face, towers rounded by a thousand years of wind, walls pierced with arrow slits that threw blades of light across a courtyard of drifted sand. A spring fed a stone basin at its center, still running, fringed with dark green moss — the eleven springs of the survey, and this one unrecorded, hidden, kept.

And there, sitting on the rim of the basin with his boots off and his feet in the water like a man at the end of a long holiday, was Idris.

He was thinner than the bounty sketch, darker, with a beard gone ragged and eyes that had the particular calm of someone who has finished a job. His pack sat against the wall behind him, small and light. No stolen goods in it, she'd have bet the Kestrel's hull. He watched her come down the last slope with his hands visible on his knees, deliberate, and when she stopped ten paces off with the sun behind her and her knife loose in its sheath, he smiled like a man greeting someone expected.

"Ranger Pemberton," he said. His voice was rough with nine days of salt air. "You're two hours faster than I estimated. That's flattering."

"You led me here." It wasn't a question, and she didn't waste breath making it one.

"I led you somewhere," Idris said. "Whether it's *here* depends on what you do next." He reached slowly — she let him, knife-hand ready — into his shirt and drew out a folded square of oilskin, travel-worn, salt-stained, and set it on the wet stone rim of the basin between them. "Half a chart," he said. "The western half. I was paid to carry it to a man in Karst Landing. Instead I'm giving it to you, because the man who paid me thinks you'll lead him to the other half, and I've decided I don't like the man who paid me."

The wind moved through the arrow slits, playing its low organ note. Vesper looked at the oilskin packet and felt, beneath the tracker's cold attention, something older stir — the diver, always falling.

"What's it a chart *to*?" she asked, though some drowned part of her already knew, already had known since the burned wax seal, since the smoke, since the first careless footprint on the flats.

Idris wiped his hands on his knees and met her eyes.

"The Marlowe," he said. "Wreck site and salvage bearing. Half of it." He nodded at the packet. "The other half belongs to Cassian Vane."
