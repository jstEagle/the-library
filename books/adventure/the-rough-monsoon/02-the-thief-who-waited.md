## 2. The Thief Who Waited

The canyon opened before her like a wound in the earth, and Vesper Pemberton stopped at its lip and let the wind read her face.

Nine days she had chased Idris across the salt. Nine days of white glare and cracked clay, of following a man's bootprints the way other people followed scripture — faithfully, obsessively, without ever quite believing there was anything holy at the end of them. Her water was down to two skins and a mouthful. Her lips had split twice. The salt had gotten into everything: her hair, her boots, the seams of her jacket, the small creases at the corners of her eyes where it would stay for weeks, glittering faintly when she cried, if she still cried, which she did not.

And now, at the end of all that white nothing, the land folded down into shadow — a slot canyon cutting into red sandstone, old walls streaked black by centuries of rare rain, and at its throat, half-swallowed by the dark, the ruins.

She knew them. Everyone who worked the desert coast knew the Saltgate ruins, though few could say what they had been. A caravanserai once, maybe, before the trade routes died. A customs house. A temple to some god whose name the wind had taken as payment. Now they were arches without buildings, doorways opening onto rooms made of sky, walls worn smooth as driftwood. Wind moved through them in long low notes, like breath drawn through the ribs of something enormous and dead.

Idris had gone in an hour ago. She'd watched him from the ridge, small against the red stone, moving not like a man fleeing but like a man arriving — unhurried, deliberate, pausing at the first arch to look back along his own trail. Looking back at *her*, or looking back to see whether she was close enough.

That was the thing that had been bothering her since the fourth day, the thing she kept pushing down under the simpler mechanics of pursuit: he wasn't running the way runners run.

Vesper had tracked men across this coast for ten years. Desert ranger, they called her, though the title was mostly hers to define; the settlements paid her to find lost wells and stolen stock and occasionally stolen people, and she was good at it because finding things was easier than being found. She knew the grammar of flight. Fleeing men wasted water. They doubled back stupidly, or didn't double back at all. They dropped things in panic and traveled through the worst hours of the day. Their trails read like screaming.

Idris's trail read like a letter. Neat, considered, addressed. He traveled at dawn and dusk and rested in shade. He never took the hard line when a soft one would do. Twice he had passed up chances to lose her that any competent runner would have seized — a lava field of broken basalt where tracks vanished, a dry wash with a gravel bed that swallowed footprints whole. Both times he had gone around, keeping himself legible, keeping the story of his passage readable on the ground like ink on paper.

He wanted to be followed. He just hadn't wanted her to catch him too soon.

Vesper crouched at the canyon rim and drank — two swallows, no more, warm water tasting of skin and canvas — and admitted the thought she'd been refusing for five days.

*Someone sent him.*

Not to escape her. To reach her. And whoever it was knew her well enough to know that Vesper Pemberton could not be approached directly, could not be summoned, could not be asked. She could only be *hunted* — gently, patiently, by a man willing to cross nine days of salt with her on his heels. Whoever it was understood that the only way to make her arrive somewhere was to make her believe she was chasing.

Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. Ten years ago her hands had shaken at everything; grief had lived in them like birds in a rigging. Now they were tools, and tools did not tremble, and somewhere in the last decade she had stopped being a woman carrying grief and become an instrument shaped around its absence.

She went down into the canyon.

* * *

The light changed at the bottom. Up on the flats the sun ruled absolutely; down here the walls cut it into ribbons, and the air came cool off the stone and smelled of dust and old smoke and, faintly, impossibly, of wet. There was water in this canyon somewhere. A seep, maybe, tucked under an overhang. That was why the caravans had come, once. That was why Idris had come now. He had chosen the one place in forty miles where she could not simply wait for him to die of thirst.

Which meant he knew her methods too. Knew she wouldn't fire on him from range, wouldn't take the shot she'd earned nine days ago on the open salt. Knew she'd want to look him in the face first, want to know *why*, because Vesper Pemberton had built her whole life on the principle that every theft had a reason underneath it, even if the reason was just hunger, even if the reason was just fear.

She moved through the ruins with her rifle slung and her knife loose in its sheath, boots silent on sand drifted deep between the fallen stones. The arches threw their shadows in long bars. In one sheltered chamber, someone had built a fire within the last day — ash still holding warmth when she pressed two fingers to it. He wasn't far.

She found him at the far end of the canyon, in what had been the heart of the place: a round chamber open to the sky, its walls carved with figures so eroded they were less pictures than suggestions — processions of people, maybe, or gods, or just the marks of hands that had needed, a thousand years ago, to leave proof they existed. A thread of water seeped from a crack in the rock and pooled in a basin worn glass-smooth.

Idris sat beside the pool with his back against the wall and his hands visible in his lap, palms up, empty. He looked tired in a way that seemed almost rehearsed — or honest; she couldn't tell anymore, and that itself told her something about how long she'd been alone. He was maybe fifty, lean as a whipstock, brown as the canyon, with a gray-shot beard and eyes that were doing something she hadn't expected.

They were sorry. Genuinely, specifically sorry, and aimed at her.

"Nine days," he said. His voice was rough with salt and disuse. "I told them you'd take eight. They said seven. Nobody bet on nine."

"Who's *they*?" Vesper stayed in the archway, out of arm's reach, out of the geometry of any lunge. The rifle hung easy across her body. "And start with why you stole from the Meridian post instead of walking up and knocking."

"Because you'd have turned me away at the door." He said it without flattery, without apology — a man reporting weather. "I've seen you do it. Three years ago, at Corvess Landing. A man came asking after you with a contract in his case. You looked at him for about four seconds and walked out the back of the tavern and were gone before his drink arrived. I was told—" he paused, chose the words like footing on scree— "I was told you don't answer letters. You don't answer messengers. But you will answer a trail."

Vesper said nothing. The wind played its low note through the upper canyon. Somewhere above, a raven commented and fell silent.

"So you made yourself a thief," she said finally. "Stole a chart case from a survey post. Made sure the description went out. Made sure it reached me." She let that sit. "You wanted a ranger hunting you, because a ranger doesn't stop."

"A ranger doesn't stop," he agreed.

"And now I'm here, and my water's nearly gone, and you have about thirty seconds to convince me this isn't the most elaborate ambush on the coast."

"It isn't." He moved — slowly, telegraphing every inch — and drew something from inside his coat. Not a weapon. A tube of oilcloth, sealed with wax, the kind surveyors used for charts. He set it on the stone between them and put his hands back in his lap. "I was hired to carry that to you. The theft was theater. The man who paid me is named Cassian Vane."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

She did not move. She had spent ten years becoming a person who did not move when the world tried to knock her over, and this — this was the world trying. Cassian Vane. The name was older than her grief and tangled all through it, a weed grown through the wreck of everything. She had heard it perhaps six times in a decade, always secondhand, always attached to money: *Vane's fleet lifted a cargo out of the Sable Shoals. Vane holds salvage rights clear down the Amber Coast.* A rival salvager who had risen fat and fast in the years after the Marlowe went down, as though the sea had paid him a commission on drowning.

"You're lying," she said, and heard how flat her voice came out, how much effort the flatness cost.

"I'm a courier," Idris said. "Lying's above my pay. Open it."

She didn't move toward it. "Why you? Why not bring it himself?"

"Because you'd have shot him." No hesitation. "He knows it. Everyone who's ever worked this coast knows it. You'd have shot him on sight, and you'd have been right to, and he—" Idris's jaw worked, and for a moment the courier mask slipped and she saw the man beneath it, a man who had crossed nine days of salt for money and had begun, somewhere around day four, to regret the shape of the job. "He needs you alive and he needs you willing. Those are different problems. The chart solves the first one. The rest is supposed to solve the second."

"The rest?"

"He'll come to you. Six days, he said. Maybe less. He gave me a place and a tide-table." Idris's eyes flicked to the oilcloth tube, then back to her face, and there it was again, that specific sorrow, and Vesper understood suddenly that the sorrow had a direction, that it wasn't for her exactly but for what he was about to do to her. "I'm going to tell you what's in that tube, Vesper Pemberton, because I carried it across the salt and I looked at it, even though I wasn't supposed to, because a man should know what he's delivering. It's half a chart. Western half. Soundings, bearings, a channel marked in an old hand. And across the top of it, in ink that's faded but not gone, a name."

The wind held its note. The water dripped into the basin. Drip. Drip. Drip, patient as a decade.

"Don't," she said.

"*Marlowe*," he said anyway, because she had known he would, because the whole nine days had been built toward this word like a keel toward water. "It's a chart to the Marlowe wreck. Half of one. The other half is in Cassian Vane's safe aboard his flagship, and he'll trade it for one thing."

Vesper became aware that she had crossed half the chamber. She had no memory of moving. Her hand was on the oilcloth tube, and the wax seal cracked under her thumb like a small bone breaking, and none of it felt like her own doing — it felt like the past reaching up through the floor of the world and taking hold of her ankle.

She unrolled the chart on the stone.

There it was. She knew the coastline before she knew she knew it — the drowned coast, the reef graveyard, the teeth of rock where the survey showed nothing but danger and her father's expedition had shown her everything. The soundings were in an old hand, careful and confident, depth numbers marching down into blue like a stairway. And the channel — a channel threaded through the reef field that appeared on no modern survey, that she had never found in ten years of ranging these waters, that existed here in ink because a man had sailed it and lived and written it down.

Her father's hand. She would have known it blind. She had learned her letters copying it.

The chamber tilted, very slightly, and righted. Or she did.

"He wants you to race him," Idris said quietly, from far away, from very close. "His fleet sails in six tides. If you reach the Marlowe first, the location's yours and he buys your silence. If he reaches it first—" the courier shrugged, helpless, honest— "I don't know what he wants there. But a man doesn't spend this kind of money unless he's afraid of what's down there. That's the truth of it, ranger. Fear. I've carried a lot of messages. This is the first one that smelled like fear."

Vesper knelt on the stone of a ruined chamber a thousand years dead, in a canyon that held water the way she had never learned to hold grief, and looked at her father's handwriting for the first time in ten years.

He had chosen the crew over the fortune. She didn't know that yet. What she knew was smaller and larger: he had written *soundings*, plural, methodical, the work of a man planning to come back and sail this channel again. A man who expected a future. The sea had disagreed, and here was his future surviving on goatskin and ink, delivered to his daughter by a stranger who smelled of sweat and regret.

Six tides.

She rolled the chart with hands that were, she noted distantly, perfectly steady. Tools. Instruments. She stood.

"Where does he expect to find me?" she asked.

"Port Meridian. The tide after next." Idris rose too, joints cracking, and looked at her with that unbearable directed sorrow. "Ranger — I'll say one thing more, and then I'm done and you can do what you like with me. He told me you'd say yes. He told me you couldn't help it. He said, *she's been running from that wreck for ten years, and the only thing faster than a running woman is one who thinks she's chasing.*"

The words went into her like the salt had gone into her boots — fine, invasive, certain to be there for weeks.

Vesper picked up her rifle and walked out of the ruins without a word, up the canyon, into the white glare, already calculating tides and distance and wind, already moving, and it was only halfway up the switchback trail, with the ruins shrinking below her, that she allowed herself to hear what Idris had actually said:

Cassian Vane hadn't sent her an invitation.

He had sent her a mirror.
