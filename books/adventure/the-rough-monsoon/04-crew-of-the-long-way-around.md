## 4. Crew of the Long Way Around

The harbor at Saltmere smelled of tar and old rain, and Vesper stood on the quay for a long time before she could make her feet move toward the Kestrel.

She had told herself, all the way down from the flats and through the canyon country and across the drowned coast road, that she would not say it. That she would carry Idris's half-chart home like a stone in her pocket and let it sit there, heavy and silent, until it wore smooth with disuse. She had told herself that the oath was an oath, that oaths were the only architecture some people had, and hers was built on a promise made over four graves.

But the chart was in her coat, against her ribs, and every step toward the mooring felt like walking with a lit fuse tucked under her arm.

The Kestrel sat low in the gray water where they'd left her, patched and patient, her hull streaked with salt bloom like frost that never melted. Sefa was already up in the rigging of the engine housing — she could tell by the particular violence of the hammering, the way Sefa worked when something in her head needed drowning out. Orla sat on the foredeck coiling line with the slow economy of someone who had learned to make stillness look like work. And Bram was in the wheelhouse, chart spread before him, doing what Bram always did: making the world legible so it couldn't surprise him.

They saw her come down the gangway one at a time, and Vesper watched three faces perform the same small theater — the flicker of relief, then the careful blankness, because none of them had ever learned how to greet her without first checking whether she'd brought trouble home in her teeth.

"You look like nine days of bad water," Sefa said, dropping to the deck. There was grease to her elbows and a wrench in her fist like a scepter. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine," Sefa repeated, flat as a becalmed sea. "Nine days chasing a thief across the salt, and you're fine."

"How—"

"Idris's cousin drinks at the Anchorline," Orla said without looking up from her coils. "Talks more than he rows. We knew where you were by day three." She set the coil down and finally raised her face, and Vesper saw the question in it, held carefully, the way you hold something with a broken edge. "We also heard what he stole."

The word *chart* went around the deck like wind through rigging. Nobody said it out loud. They didn't have to. Ten years, and the Marlowe was still the name they all kept in the same locked room, and here was Vesper standing on the threshold with the key jangling in her pocket.

Bram came out of the wheelhouse slowly. He was older than the rest of them by fifteen years, gray shot through his beard like weather into timber, and he moved now the way he always moved after hearing bad news — deliberately, as if the deck might betray him. "Tell me it isn't," he said. "Tell me the thief took a chart to somewhere else. Somewhere boring."

Vesper reached into her coat. Her hand hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and then she drew out the oilskin packet and laid it on the crate between them, and the sound it made was very small and very loud.

Nobody touched it.

* * *

Sefa spoke first, because Sefa always spoke first when silence became unbearable; it was her way of bailing water. "That's half a chart," she said, unrolling it with hands that weren't quite steady. "Whoever cut it took the western third. The approaches. You can't find anything with this except disappointment."

"Vane has the other half," Vesper said.

The name landed on the deck like a body dropped from height. Orla stopped moving entirely. Bram closed his eyes.

"Cassian Vane came to me in the canyon," Vesper went on, and heard her own voice go flat and official, the ranger's voice, the voice she used for distances. "He's offering a contract. Reach the wreck before his salvage fleet does. Six tides. He gave me the missing piece — or enough of it — and a purse I didn't take."

"And you're telling us this why?" Bram asked quietly. It wasn't hostile. It was worse than hostile. It was the voice of a man asking a question whose answer he already carried, the way sailors know a storm is coming from the smell of the air long before the barometer falls.

"Because it's not mine to decide alone."

"It was decided alone ten years ago," Sefa said. She said it without heat, which somehow made it heavier. "Four people decided it. My brother was one of them. And the rest of us have been living inside that decision ever since, like a hull we can't patch."

Vesper looked at her. Sefa had been twenty-two when the Marlowe went down, apprenticed in the engine rooms of the coastal trade, all elbows and opinions. Her brother Tomas had been the expedition's engineer — the man who'd taught Vesper, laughing, how to listen to a pump and hear whether it was lying. Tomas had gone down with the ship. Sefa had spent the decade since becoming the best engineer on this coast and never once taking a contract east of the reef line, and everyone aboard had understood the shape of that refusal without ever naming it.

"Sefa—"

"Don't." Sefa held up the wrench, not quite a weapon, not quite a plea. "Don't soften it. Don't do the thing where you make it sound reasonable. Just say it plain. Are you asking us to go?"

The harbor sounds filled the pause — halyards knocking, gulls arguing over fish heads, the far bell of the tide office counting out the hour. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world where the Marlowe was just a name in an insurance ledger.

"Yes," Vesper said. "I'm asking."

* * *

Orla stood up.

She was the youngest of them, though she carried herself like driftwood carries water — having absorbed too much of it to ever float high again. She walked to the rail and looked out at the harbor mouth, at the gray line where the sea began, and when she turned around her face had gone very still in a way Vesper had only seen twice before, both times in weather.

"My mother was a steward aboard the Marlowe," Orla said.

Vesper's breath caught. In ten years, Orla had never said it. Not once. Not in the nights after storms when grief got loose among them like something flooding the bilge. Not even drunk, and Orla had been drunk exactly twice. The crew had known — of course they'd known, you can't sail with people for years without learning the shapes of their silences — but knowing and hearing were different countries, and Orla had kept them separated by an ocean.

"She signed on for one last season," Orla continued, each word placed like a foot on wet rock. "One last run, and then she was going to come home and stay. I have her letters. Forty-one letters, and the last one says the salvage payout was going to buy us a house with a garden, and I would finally stop being passed around to relatives who resented me." Her jaw tightened. "So when you all swore off that job, I swore loudest. Because if we never go back, then it stays a story. A ship that sank. A mother who died. Clean. But if we go—" Her voice cracked and she mastered it, furious at it. "If we go, then it becomes a place. And places are real. Places have coordinates."

"That's exactly why we should go," Sefa said softly.

"No," Orla said. "That's exactly why I'm afraid."

Bram hadn't moved. He stood by the wheelhouse door with his hand resting on the frame, and Vesper realized he was holding himself upright by it. When he finally spoke, his voice was roughened, like a rope dragged over coral.

"My brother sailed on the Marlowe too."

The words seemed to cost him something physical. He looked at his own boots.

"Everyone thinks I signed on with you because I'm careful. Because I'm old and I like charts and I don't like surprises." A sound that was almost a laugh. "I signed on because your father's expedition had my brother's name on its manifest, and I thought maybe, someday, being near the people who survived it would teach me how he died. Ten years, Vesper. Ten years I've navigated us around that lagoon like it doesn't exist, adding three days to passages, lying about currents, and none of you ever called it a lie because you were all steering around it too."

"We were," Vesper said. Her throat ached. "All of us. Every heading we've ever plotted had a hole in it shaped like that ship."

"So what happens now?" Bram asked. "You sign Vane's contract, we sail east, and then what? We find her? We look at her? What is it you think is down there that we need?"

And there it was — the question underneath everything, the one Vesper had been outrunning across nine days of salt. She thought of her father's last letter, the one that arrived after he didn't. She thought of the night she'd swum away from the scattering lights of the expedition boats while the storm ate the sky, and how she had never once, in ten years, let herself finish the memory. She always woke up before the end. Stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant sinking, and she had built a whole life on the principle that motion was buoyancy.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But Idris didn't steal that chart to sell it. He waited for me in a ruin at the end of nowhere. Someone wants *me* to go back there. And I'd rather walk into that on my own feet than have it drag me by the hair."

* * *

The vote, such as it was, happened without anyone calling it a vote.

Sefa dropped her wrench into her tool roll and cinched it shut. "Tomas listened to pumps because pumps don't lie," she said. "People lie. Vane lies — everyone on this coast knows what he did or didn't do on that expedition, whatever the inquiries concluded. If there's truth down there, I want to hear it with my own ears." She looked at Vesper. "For Tomas. Not for you. Don't make it pretty."

"For my mother," Orla said, "and against my own better judgment, which I'd like noted in the log."

They all turned to Bram.

He had picked up the half-chart. He held it the way a priest holds a text he no longer believes in, and for a long moment the only sound was the harbor breathing against the hull.

"No," he said.

The word fell into the space between them and lay there.

"I've spent ten years keeping this crew alive by knowing exactly where we were," Bram said. "If we sail east, I won't know where I am anymore. That's not navigation. That's drowning with extra steps." He rolled the chart closed with terrible gentleness and set it down. "I'll help you provision. I'll check your stores, your water, your rigging, because I won't send you out short-handed on safety. But I'm staying ashore, Vesper. Some doors you lock from the outside."

"Bram—"

"Your father would understand," he said, and his voice broke on the last word, and he walked into the wheelhouse and pulled the door shut behind him, and through the glass they watched him stand over his charts with his back to them, shoulders shaking in the smallest possible way, a man grieving in the only privacy a ship allows.

* * *

They provisioned through the afternoon in a silence full of things unsaid. Sefa sang under her breath — old engine-room songs, the ones with numbers in them — while she checked the Kestrel's heart. Orla counted crates twice and then a third time, needing the arithmetic of survival. And Vesper stood at the rail watching the light go copper on the water, feeling the terrible relief of motion chosen, the same relief she'd felt at nineteen running from the funeral, at twenty-five running from the inquiry, at thirty running across the salt after a thief who had wanted to be caught.

Bram came out at dusk. He pressed a folded paper into her hand — a course, she realized, drawn in his own careful hand. The true route east, currents marked, hazards flagged, the liar's channel her father had used annotated in the margin with a single note: *He showed me once. I never forgot.*

"You said you wouldn't come," Vesper said.

"I said I wouldn't sail with you." His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I'm not coming. But nobody sails those waters without my arithmetic, and I won't have your deaths on top of everything else." He gripped her forearm, hard, sailor-fashion. "Six tides. Come back with answers, not just treasure. Treasure I don't care about."

He walked down the gangway and didn't look back, and Vesper watched him go, understanding suddenly that grief doesn't split crews — it reveals the fault lines that were always there, drawn years ago, waiting for pressure.

Behind her, Sefa fired the engine, and it caught on the second try with a sound like a coughing giant waking. Orla cast off the lines. The Kestrel leaned into the current, and the harbor mouth opened ahead of them, gray and wide and going dark.

"Course, Captain?" Orla asked, and the title sat strange and new between them, because Vesper had never claimed it aloud.

Vesper unfolded Bram's paper. East, past the reef graveyard. Toward the hole in every map she'd ever loved.

"East," she said. "Before the sixth tide turns."

And far out at the harbor's edge, where the water deepened from green to black, she saw — or thought she saw — a lantern answering theirs. One flash. Two. Deliberate. Someone marking their departure, someone counting them out of port.

Someone who already knew they were coming.
