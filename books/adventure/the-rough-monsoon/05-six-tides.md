## 5. Six Tides

The contract lay on the table of the Broken Meridian like something dead that hadn't finished dying.

Vesper had read it four times. The ink was good — Vane's ink was always good; he paid a scrivener in the capital to make his treachery legible. Six tides to reach the Marlowe wreck before his own salvage fleet. A quarter share of whatever they raised if they won. Nothing but passage money if they lost. And at the bottom, in a hand she recognized from a decade-old ledger she'd burned and regretted burning ever since: *Cassian Vane.*

"You've read it enough times to have memorized it," Sefa said from the doorway. "Reading it a fifth time won't change what's written."

"No," Vesper agreed. "But it might change what I do about it."

She pushed back from the table and went to the window. Port Callow at dusk was all lamplight and tar-smoke, gulls quarreling over fish guts on the breakwater, the harbor water lying flat and gray as hammered pewter. Somewhere out there, past the shoals and the drowned coast and the reef graveyard where her father's expedition had come apart like wet paper, the Marlowe sat on the bottom doing what wrecks did best: keeping its dead.

Her father among them.

"I want you to tell me I'm being a fool," she said.

Sefa came into the room the way she did everything — deliberately, with the weight of a woman who'd spent thirty years making engines obey her will and saw no reason people should be different. She was broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples, her forearms a lattice of old burns. She looked at the contract the way she'd look at a cracked boiler plate.

"You're being a fool," she said. "But you already knew that, or you wouldn't have asked me to say it. You wanted me to say it so you could hear how it sounds coming from someone who isn't you." She sat down across from the contract, not touching it. "It sounds like grief wearing a work shirt."

Vesper's jaw tightened. "That's not—"

"Ten years, Vess. Ten years you've taken any job that pointed you away from that coast. Smuggling runs up the Serpent's Teeth. Survey work in the Ash Reaches. You chased a horse thief across the salt flats for nine days last month because he *looked* like he was running somewhere." Sefa's voice wasn't unkind. That was the worst part. Unkindness she could have fought. "And now the one job we swore off walks in wearing a pretty coat, and you can't stop looking at it. Not because of the money. Because it's the only direction left you haven't run."

The lamp guttered. Outside, a ship's bell rang the evening watch, and the sound went through Vesper like a fishhook finding old scar tissue.

"He killed my father," she said quietly. "Or he stood on a deck and watched him die and sailed away, which is the same thing with better manners. And now he wants me to race him to the grave."

"Yes."

"And I'm going to sign it."

Sefa nodded slowly, as if Vesper had confirmed something she'd known before she walked in. "I know. I watched your face while Vane talked. You had the look of a woman standing at the edge of cold water." She turned the contract around with one finger and slid it across the table. "So here's what I'll tell you, since you're going to be a fool either way. If you go alone, you die. If you take strangers, they'll sell you. So you take us, or nobody."

Vesper stared at her. "You lost Tomas on the Marlowe."

"Tomas made his choices. I made mine after." Sefa's hand closed briefly, involuntarily, around the wrench she carried everywhere the way priests carried icons. "I swore off that wreck same as you. But swearing off isn't the same as being done. I learned that difference on my knees in an engine room one night, and I've never told anyone this, so listen: there are two kinds of stillness. The kind where you've made peace, and the kind where you're just holding your breath." She stood. "I've been holding mine ten years. Maybe it's time somebody breathed."

She left. Vesper picked up the pen.

Her hand didn't shake. That surprised her most of all. She signed *V. Pemberton* in a steady line, and the terrible thing — the thing she would carry like a stone in her boot for the rest of the voyage — was the relief. Warm and shameful and immediate, like brandy on a wound. Motion. Direction. Six tides and a destination, and no room left inside her for anything but the next mile of water.

She had spent a decade learning every way there was to keep moving. She had never once asked herself what she was moving toward.

Now she didn't have to. Now there was a heading.

* * *

Bram didn't even wait for her to finish talking.

They stood on the seawall above the careening beach, where the Kestrel sat propped on skids with her hull bared like a patient on a table. Bram had his arms folded and his eyes on the horizon, and he had that particular stillness he got when he'd already decided something and was simply waiting for the world to catch up.

"Six tides," Vesper said. "That's all I'm asking you to hear. After that, say what you like."

"I heard you the first three times you explained it at breakfast." His voice was flat as the harbor. "My answer hasn't changed since the second time."

"Bram—"

"No." He turned to face her then, and she saw what ten years of friendship had taught her to read in him: not anger. Fear, dressed in anger's clothes because fear was undignified and Bram had standards. "You want me to navigate you to that reef graveyard. To the exact patch of water where the Marlowe went down with my brother waving from her deck — did you know he waved? One of the survivors said he waved when the second wave took her under. Like he thought someone was coming." His knuckles whitened on the rail. "Nobody came, Vesper. That's the whole story. Nobody came, and now you're sailing out to prove it again with Vane's signature on your stern."

"Vane's fleet reaches her in six tides regardless. If it's not us, it's him. He strips her, sells everything that floats, and whatever's down there — whatever *truth* is down there — goes into his strongbox forever."

"And you believe that? You believe this is about truth?" Bram laughed, short and bitter. "This is about you. About the night you swam away and he didn't surface. You think if you get there first, you'll find some answer waiting in the hull. Some word from him. Well, I'll tell you what's down there, because I've dreamed it every night for ten years: dark water, and fish, and nothing. The sea doesn't keep answers, Vesper. It keeps *everything else*."

The words landed harder than he probably intended, or maybe exactly as hard as he intended. Vesper gripped the rail beside him and let the silence sit between them like a third person.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," she said finally. "I'm asking you to come."

"And I'm telling you no." He straightened. For a moment something moved behind his eyes — something older and sadder than refusal — and then it was gone, shuttered. "Some doors you don't open twice. My brother knocked on that one. I'm not going to stand outside it listening for him too."

He walked down the seawall steps without looking back, and Vesper watched him go until the crowd of the evening market swallowed him, and felt the strange double weight of loss and certainty. Losing Bram hurt. But some part of her — the part she despised — noted that a crew of three was lighter than a crew of four, that fewer voices meant fewer chances for someone to say *turn back*, and hated herself for noting it.

The relief again. It kept arriving uninvited, warm and awful, like a fever.

* * *

Orla found her at midnight on the Kestrel's deck, sitting against the mainmast with the signed contract in her lap and a bottle of rough plum spirit she wasn't drinking.

The deckhand came up the gangway without a sound — Orla moved like that, always had, a small quick woman with salt-bleached hair and a face that gave away nothing except when she laughed, which was rare enough to be an event. She settled onto the hatch cover across from Vesper and looked at the contract upside down.

"So it's true," she said. "Sefa told me. I didn't believe her."

"Why not?"

"Because you're the sanest person I sail with." Orla's mouth twitched. "Low bar, granted. But still."

Vesper huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Bram's out."

"I know. I watched him pack. He packed angry, which means he packed slow." Orla pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. For a long moment she just looked at the water, black and breathing beyond the breakwater. "You're going to ask me if I'm in."

"Are you?"

"That depends." Her voice changed — not softer, exactly, but closer to the bone. "Answer me honest, Captain. Why are you going? Not the speech you gave Sefa. Not the one about Vane and truth. The real one. The one you'd only say drunk, and you're not even drinking, so maybe you won't say it at all. But think it."

Vesper looked down at the contract. The lantern light made the ink gleam faintly, like something wet.

"Because if I stay ashore," she heard herself say, "I'll have to sit still. And if I sit still, it catches me. All of it. The night, the water, his face when—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Ten years I've told myself I was chasing work. Chasing thieves. Chasing anything. But motion isn't courage, Orla. I know that. It's just the fastest way to keep from hearing yourself think. And signing this is more of the same, and I know that too, and I signed anyway, because—" Her hands opened helplessly. "Because the alternative is standing still with it. And I don't know how to do that yet."

The honesty hung in the air between them, raw-edged. Vesper braced for pity.

Orla didn't offer any. She nodded once, slow, like a navigator confirming a bearing.

"All right," she said. "Then I'm in."

"Just like that? No reasons?"

"I have reasons." Orla unfolded herself and stood, and in the lantern light her face was unreadable again, the shutters drawn. "I'm just not giving them to you tonight. Ask me again when we're close. When you can see the water over her." She paused at the gangway, silhouetted against the harbor lamps. "One thing, though. Free of charge. You said motion isn't courage. Maybe. But sometimes it's the only boat that floats, and you row in it until you're strong enough to swim. I'd know."

She went below without another word, leaving Vesper alone with the mast at her back and the question of what Orla had meant settling into her like a splinter too deep to dig out.

Vesper tipped the plum spirit over the rail rather than drink it — she'd need a clear head — and looked east, toward the drowned coast, invisible beyond the dark curve of the world. Six tides. Somewhere out there, Vane's fleet was coaling and crewing, and the storm season was building behind all of them like a debt coming due.

She should feel afraid. She knew that. Instead, as she rolled the contract and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart like a letter from an enemy, what she felt was that same terrible warmth. Relief. Motion. A heading at last, even if the heading led straight down into the one place she'd sworn never to look.

Three days later, with the Kestrel patched and provisioned and slipping out of Port Callow on the pre-dawn ebb with her navigation lights dark, Vesper would remember this night on the deck — the relief, the shame of it, Orla's shuttered face — and understand that she had already made every choice that mattered.

What she couldn't yet know was that the Kestrel's hold held one more passenger than her manifest allowed, and that Bram's slow, angry packing had been a performance for watchers he'd spotted outside his window — watchers who weren't Vane's.

But that discovery was two tides away, and the gray water ahead was wide, and cold, and waiting.
