## 6. Departure in Gray Water

The rain came before dawn, thin and gray as dishwater, and Orla Kestrel-Brannigan — no, just Orla Brannigan now; she'd given the other name back to the sea years ago — stood at the harbor rail and watched it bead on the tarred rope like small glass seeds.

She had not slept. None of them had, really, but Sefa had at least lain down in the loft above the chandlery, and Bram had gone home to a wife who thought he was done with salt water forever, and Vesper — Vesper had sat on the Kestrel's stern bench all night with her father's compass in her fist, saying nothing, which for Vesper Pemberton was a kind of speech.

Orla counted the watchers instead of sleeping. That was her gift, if you could call it that: she noticed things. She'd been noticing things since she was seven years old, since the night the Marlowe went down and she learned that survival was mostly a matter of watching what other people failed to see.

There were three of them tonight. A fisherman mending nets that needed no mending under the far lamp, his hands too clean for the work. A woman in an oilskin two piers down who had walked past the chandlery four times without once looking in its window. And a boy on the customs house steps who could not have been more than fourteen and kept glancing at the Kestrel's patched mainsail the way people glance at something they've been paid to remember.

Vane's watchers. Of course. Cassian Vane did not spend coin on contracts he intended to lose.

"Three," Orla said quietly, coming up beside Vesper at the rail.

"I make it three." Vesper didn't look away from the water. "The boy worries me most."

"Because he's young?"

"Because he's bored. Bored men do stupid things, and stupid things make noise." Vesper turned then, and in the lamplight her face looked carved out of something patient and tired. "We go at slack water. Before the fishing fleet. Sefa says the tide turns at half past four."

"It's quarter past now."

"Then we're already late." Vesper pushed off the rail. "Get below. Check the bilge. If we're taking water out of this harbor, I want to know about it before the harbor knows about us."

Orla went. It was easier than arguing, and anyway she liked being below in the dark hold of the little ketch, where the smell of oakum and bilgewater and old canvas was so thick it felt like a blanket. She ran her palm along the hull planking as she moved aft, feeling for softness, for give, for the places where the sea had been quietly negotiating with the wood all winter. The Kestrel was twenty-two years old and had been sunk once — not by weather but by her previous owner's optimism — and every plank of her had opinions about the ocean. Orla had spent six weeks listening to those opinions with a caulking iron, and she trusted the ship more than she trusted most people, which was exactly how she preferred it.

Trust was a thing she rationed. Like water on the flats. Like air in a flooding compartment.

She checked the bilge — dry enough, an inch of honest seepage, nothing more — and then she did the thing she always did and never spoke of: she opened the small locker under the stern thwart where she kept her kit, and beneath the spare fuses and the signal flares she touched the tin box. Just touched it. Inside was a child's shoe, leather gone stiff and white with salt, and a hair ribbon, and a photograph of eleven people on a deck, squinting into sun, taken the morning the Marlowe sailed on her last voyage.

Orla was in the photograph. Second from the left, sitting on the coil of rope because her legs were too short to reach the deck. Seven years old. Smiling like the world was a place that kept its promises.

She closed the locker. She went back up into the gray.

* * *

They cast off at half past four in a rain that had thickened to a steady whisper, and they did it badly, on purpose.

Sefa killed the harbor lights on the pier side and let the Kestrel drift off her mooring stern-first, silent, using only the boathook and the tide's own pull. Orla crouched at the bow with a boat hook of her own, fending them off the pilings of the customs wharf by inches, close enough to hear the watchman inside snoring through the shuttered window. The boy on the steps had finally fallen asleep, curled against his satchel, and Vesper steered them past him so slowly that Orla could have reached out and pulled his cap over his eyes.

It was the fisherman who nearly ended everything. As they cleared the inner mole he stood up from his nets, and Orla saw him open his mouth — saw the whole shape of the shout assembling itself in his face — and she did the only thing her body knew how to do in moments like that. She made herself visible. Stood up straight in the bow, arms wide, and called across the water in a voice pitched to carry and to bore:

"Morning! Tide's early — we're running the oyster beds before the fleet wakes!"

The fisherman hesitated. Looked at her. Looked at the patched sail going up with a rattle Sefa couldn't quite smother. And then, blessedly, he shrugged — because a ketch going out at dawn to work the beds was the most ordinary sight in the world, and men are built to dismiss ordinary things — and sat back down among his nets.

Vesper exhaled through her nose. It was the closest she came to laughter before noon.

They were past the outer buoy when the woman in the oilskin appeared at the end of the breakwater, and this time there was no mistaking it: she raised a shuttered lantern, flashed it twice toward the headland, and was gone.

"She signaled," Orla said, climbing back into the cockpit, wringing out her sleeves.

"She signaled." Vesper's hands were steady on the tiller, but her jaw had gone tight. "Which means somewhere behind that headland there's a fast hull waking up, or a telegraph clerk being handed a coin. Either way, we've got maybe two hours of honest lead time."

"Then we don't waste them," Sefa said from the companionway, wiping her hands on a rag. Her arms were bare despite the cold, tattooed to the elbow with gauge-dials and pressure-marks from a life spent inside engines, and she looked at the gray horizon the way a surgeon looks at a wound she's decided to reopen. "I'll wake the engine. We'll motor-sail until the wind fills in from the southeast."

"The southeast's where the storm is," Orla said.

"The southeast's where the storm *isn't yet,*" Sefa corrected. "Give me the difference while I can still use it."

Orla went forward to secure the anchor. The motion of the ketch was easy, a long unhurried roll, and the harbor fell away behind them in layers: first the lamps, then the shapes of the warehouses, then the town itself dissolving into rain-smear, until there was nothing behind them but water and nothing ahead of them but the same, and the Kestrel sat between like a seed on a puddle.

This was the moment, Orla knew. This exact moment — land gone, sea not yet real, the crew alone with each other for the first time since the signing. She'd watched it happen on other boats, other voyages, other lives. Somewhere in the next hour, someone would say the true thing. Someone always did. The sea pried it out of people the way tide pried a mussel off a rock.

She just hadn't expected it to be her.

* * *

It happened at midmorning, when the rain stopped as abruptly as it had begun and the light came down flat and silver, and Vesper found her at the bow coiling line that didn't need coiling.

"You were on her," Vesper said.

Not a question. Orla's hands kept moving, over and under, over and under. "What makes you say that?"

"You check the bilge twice a day even though Sefa checks it three times. You sleep in the hold, not the cabin. You touch that locker under the stern thwart every single morning like it's a prayer, and you think nobody's seen you." Vesper leaned against the rail, forearms down, watching her. "And you signed on for the Marlowe without asking about pay. Nobody does that. Not one person in ten years has signed for that wreck without asking what's in it for them."

The line went through Orla's hands. Over and under. The sea breathed against the hull.

"My mother was the cook," she said finally. "On the Marlowe. Last voyage."

Vesper didn't move. Didn't breathe, for a second. Out here, with no land in any direction, the silence had weight enough to lean on.

"I was seven," Orla went on, because once the seal was broken there was no patching it, and maybe that was why she'd never let it break — not because she was ashamed, but because she'd known it would be like this, like pulling a splinter out of a wound ten years closed. "Cook's kids rode along sometimes. It was supposed to be a short run — salvage survey, two weeks, home before the monsoon. My mother packed my good shoes because there was going to be a dinner at the company house when we got back." Her mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "I still have the shoes. They never got worn."

"The night she went down." Vesper's voice was very careful, the way people walk on ice. "You were—"

"In the water. Yes." Over and under. The coil grew. "I don't remember much and I remember everything, if that makes sense. I remember it was dark and the ship was making a sound I can still hear when the wind comes wrong — like a giant breathing through a broken rib. I remember my mother putting me over the rail into a lifeboat that was already half swamped, and I remember her face, and I remember—" Her hands stopped. She stared at them as if they belonged to someone else. "I remember counting. There were nine of us in the boat when it hit the water. By dawn there were five. The sea just... took them. Quietly. One at a time. Nobody screamed. That was the worst part. People think drowning is loud, and it isn't. It's polite. It waits until you're too tired to hold on, and then it takes you like it's doing you a favor."

Vesper came around the rail and sat down on the foredeck hatch, close but not touching, the way you sit near a wild thing you want to trust you.

"I was sixteen," she said. "The expedition after. The one my father didn't come back from."

"I know."

"Do you." It wasn't sharp. It was almost gentle.

"Everyone in the trade knows. The ranger who won't stop moving. The daughter of Elias Pemberton who tracks thieves across salt flats for nine days on half a canteen." Orla finally set the coil down and looked at her directly. "I used to think you were running *toward* something. Some reckoning. But I've been watching you for three years, Captain, and I'll tell you what I see, because seeing things is the one skill the sea left me. You're not chasing anything. You're fleeing the quiet. Because in the quiet, it catches up."

The wind chose that moment to fill in from the southeast, exactly as Sefa had promised, and the sail snapped taut overhead and the Kestrel leaned into it and the whole conversation tilted with her. Neither of them moved.

"And what about you?" Vesper asked. "Why sign? Why come back to her?"

Orla looked south, toward the drowned coast, toward the reef graveyard where both their dead lay in different rooms of the same cold house.

"Because I've spent fifteen years being the girl who lived," she said. "Every job, every port, every crew — I'm the one who notices the rotten plank, the fouled rudder, the man who's lying about his charts. I keep everyone else alive. And it works, and I'm good at it, and I hate it, because keeping other people alive is just another way of never going back for the ones who didn't make it." She swallowed. "My mother's still down there, Captain. On the Marlowe. The ship sits upright in deep water — Idris's chart says so, and charts don't lie about depth. Which means somewhere in that galley, after fifteen years, there might be—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she let it crack anyway, because Vesper had earned the sound. "There might be something of hers. A ring. A button. A bone. I need to see it. I need to stand over that water and tell her I made it to shore and I stayed there, and that it wasn't her fault for putting me in the boat, and that it wasn't mine for getting in."

For a long moment Vesper said nothing. Then she reached out and put her hand flat on the deck between them — not touching Orla, just offering the wood, the ship, the shared footing of it.

"We go together," Vesper said. "When we get there. Whatever's down there — we look at it together. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

Orla put her hand next to hers on the planking. Two palms, a hand's width apart, on a patched ketch running southeast into weather.

"That's the first contract anyone's offered me that doesn't have a payout clause," she said.

"Best kind," Vesper said. "The ones you can't buy out of."

* * *

At noon Sefa called them aft, and the fragile warmth of the morning curdled in Orla's chest before Sefa even spoke, because Sefa's face had the particular gray look of a woman holding bad news in her teeth.

"Two things," Sefa said. She spread Idris's half-chart on the cockpit seat and weighted the corners with winch handles. "One: I've been working the chart since dawn, and the survey marks on it are old. Older than old. These depth soundings aren't in fathoms of the modern standard — they're in the old company measure, pre-reform. Whoever drew this drew it before the Marlowe ever sailed."

"So it's genuine," Vesper said.

"So it's genuine," Sefa agreed. "Which brings me to two." She tapped the eastern edge of the chart, where the coastline simply stopped, ink fading into blank paper like a road ending in fog. "This half shows us how to get *to* the lagoon. It does not show us how to get *out*. No return route. No fallback channel. Nothing." She looked up, and her eyes went around the circle of them — Vesper, Orla, the empty spot where Bram should have been standing. "Either Idris never had the other half. Or somebody made sure we'd only ever carry the half that leads in."

The wind gusted. The Kestrel leaned. Far to the southeast, past the edge of the silver water, Orla saw the first bruise-colored cloud standing up over the horizon like a wall being built by patient hands.

"And there's a third thing," Sefa added quietly, "that I haven't said yet, because I wanted to be sure. Our wake's been wrong since the mole. Half a knot off, steady. Something's dragging under us, or something's loose, and I want to dive the rudder before dark."

Orla looked at the cloud. At the chart. At Vesper's face, which had gone still and hard as the salt flats themselves.

Half a knot. In a race measured in tides, half a knot was a funeral.

"Then we'd better find out what's dragging us," Vesper said, "before whatever's behind us finds out we're slow."
