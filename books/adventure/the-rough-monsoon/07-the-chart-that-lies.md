## 7. The Chart That Lies

Sefa had never trusted a gift that arrived too easily.

She sat in the Kestrel's cramped stern cabin with the half-chart unrolled before her, pinning its corners with a brass oil lamp, a chipped mug of cold tea, and the flat of her palm. The lamp guttered in the swell — they were two tides out from port now, running south along a coast that smelled of kelp and old iron — and each time the light dipped, the ink lines seemed to shift, like something alive under vellum.

Idris's chart. Or Vane's chart, delivered through Idris's bleeding hands. Sefa didn't fool herself about whose work it was. A man like Cassian Vane did not give anything away; he lent it, at interest, and collected later with tools.

She pulled her loupe from the leather case at her throat and bent close.

The drafting was beautiful. That was the first wrong thing. Real salvage charts — working charts, the kind that got folded and sweated on and splashed with bilge — were ugly things, corrected in pencil, annotated in three hands, stained where someone had set a hot kettle down. This one was clean as a wedding invitation. Every line confident. Every depth sounding placed with a calligrapher's care.

Nobody drew a chart that pretty unless they wanted it admired rather than used.

"More tea?" Orla's voice came through the door, along with a slant of gray lamplight from the passage.

"Leave the pot," Sefa said, not looking up. "And tell Bram—" She stopped herself. Bram wasn't aboard. Bram had stood on the quay with his arms crossed and his jaw set and watched them cast off like a man watching a coffin go into the ground. Saying his name out loud still caught on something in her chest, a splinter she hadn't worked loose yet.

"Tell him what?" Orla asked, too quietly.

"Nothing. Never mind."

The door closed. Sefa exhaled through her nose and went back to the chart.

The half-chart covered the run from the drowned coast to the outer shoals — the reef graveyard, everyone called it, though nobody aboard said that name yet — and then it simply stopped, mid-water, at a place marked only with a small compass rose and the notation *bearing true from the twin heads*. From there, the Marlowe's position was supposed to live in Vesper's memory, or her father's papers, or wherever the other half of the truth had gone to die ten years ago.

Sefa traced the soundings with a fingernail. Fathoms, fathoms, fathoms — and here was the second wrong thing, the one that had been itching at her since the first night. The depths were *too* consistent. A real seabed breathed. It rose and fell, shelved and dropped, threw up pinnacles where no chartmaker expected them. But these numbers marched along in gentle increments, deepening by tidy degrees toward the channel, like a staircase built for a specific foot.

Like a route drawn backward from a destination, smoothed over, made navigable.

She'd seen this before. Not often — thank every salt god — but once. Years ago, back when she was an apprentice engineer on a merchantman out of Corvess, they'd been sold a harbor chart in a port whose name she'd mercifully forgotten, and the depths had looked exactly like this: plausible, elegant, and wrong by six feet in the channel, which was enough to open a hull like a tin of sardines. The harbormaster had sold false charts to competitors' ships on purpose. It was the oldest trick in coastal warfare. You didn't need cannons if you could sell a man his own grave in ink.

But this was subtler than that. The depths weren't fatally wrong. They were *incomplete* — and incompleteness, done well, was worse.

Sefa got up, knees cracking, and dragged her tool chest to the table. From the bottom tray, beneath the calipers and the spare gaskets, she lifted a sheet of tracing film and a grease pencil, and began to work the way she worked best: alone, methodically, with her hands.

* * *

An hour later she had overlaid the chart with everything she knew, and what she knew was considerable. Ten years of surveying derelict coastlines will teach you the shape of water the way a midwife knows the shape of labor. She plotted the soundings against the tide tables for the coming six days, and then she did the thing Vane presumably hoped no one aboard would think to do: she assumed the chart was lying, and asked what it was lying *for*.

The answer made her sit down slowly on the bunk.

If you followed the charted route exactly — trusting the depths, trusting the fairway, trusting that neat little compass rose — you would arrive at the outer shoals at precisely the wrong hour of the tide. Not catastrophically wrong. Not wrecked-on-the-rocks wrong. Just wrong enough that a vessel making the passage would find the channel gates closed, the reef awash, the water over the bar two feet shy of safe. You would have to heave to and wait. And a vessel heaving to outside the reef graveyard, in known waters, at a predictable position—

—was a lighthouse. A beacon saying *here we are, come and take us.*

Or, gentler version, the one Sefa almost preferred because it was less insulting: the chart was built so that anyone who failed to reach the Marlowe would be forced to broadcast their failure by their very position, visible from miles off, easy to find, easy to follow. Vane's fleet wouldn't need spies. It would only need to sail toward whatever ship sat stranded at the edge of the shoals and say, *ah, there's our pathfinder, thank you kindly.*

Either way, the arithmetic came out the same. The half-chart wasn't a map. It was a leash.

"And the other end of the leash," Sefa murmured to the empty cabin, "is held by whoever told him where we'd stop."

Because that was the part she kept circling back to, the part with teeth. For the trap to work — really work, not just inconvenience them — Vane needed to know when they'd pass the gate. He needed their departure time, their speed, their course. He needed information that lived aboard the Kestrel, in Vesper's head and Bram's replaced-by-nobody head and the logbook chained to the nav desk three feet from where she sat.

Someone ashore could account for some of it. Harbor gossip, watchers on the quay — Vesper had said Vane had eyes out, and Vesper was rarely wrong about being hunted; she had the instincts of a fox that had been shot at before. But departure time alone wasn't enough. Tide tables were public. Speed was guessable. What wasn't guessable was whether they'd take the direct line or hug the coast, whether they'd run dark or show lights, whether Vesper would push through the first squall line or shelter behind the headland and lose half a day being careful.

Those decisions were being made at this table. In this cabin. By four people, one of whom had a broken heart and another of whom had a secret she carried like ballast.

Sefa looked at the chart and felt the cabin get smaller.

* * *

She found Orla first, because Orla was easiest to find — the girl was always where the work was, coiling line or greasing blocks or, at present, perched in the shrouds re-serving a frayed stay with a marlinspike clenched in her teeth like a pirate in a penny dreadful.

"Your serving's too loose," Sefa called up. "You'll want to wind it tighter aft, or the salt'll get under."

"I know," Orla said around the spike, which meant *I know, don't teach your grandmother.* She finished the turn, dropped lightly to the deck, and spat out the spike. "You've been locked in the stern hole all morning. Chart trouble?"

"Maybe." Sefa handed her the pot of cold tea to hold and watched her face while she said it. "How much do you remember about Idris? Before the canyon."

Orla's eyes narrowed. She was twenty-three and had the kind of face that showed every thought like weather across a headland, which is why Sefa liked interrogating her — no craft to see through, just honest sky. "He waited for us. Vesper said that. He *wanted* to be caught."

"He did. Question is, caught by whom." Sefa took the pot back. "Vane hired him to carry that chart to us. Fine. But a courier who wants delivering doesn't run nine days ahead through salt flats. He runs just fast enough. He chose the canyon. He chose the timing. Which means somewhere upstream, somebody told him where Vesper would be — and Idris trusted that somebody's arithmetic with his life."

"You think Vane's that good?"

"I think Vane's been hunting Vesper Pemberton for ten years, and men who hunt that long learn their quarry's habits better than the quarry does." Sefa lowered her voice, though the nearest crewmate was forty feet away at the helm and deaf with wind anyway. "Which brings me to the uglier question. The chart has a flaw in it. Deliberate. It routes us to the outer shoals at slack-low water on the fourth tide, dead stop, sitting target. For Vane to time his fleet to that, he needs to know our speed and our start. Some of that he can guess. Not all."

Orla went very still. Sefa watched her arrive at it — watched the thought land on her face like a gull touching down.

"You think someone's signaling him."

"I think someone *could* be. I don't know. I hate not knowing." Sefa rubbed her thumb along the pot's chipped rim. "So we're going to make not-knowing expensive for him. From tonight, we run a false pattern. Lights shown when we mean to run dark, dark when we mean to show. Course changes logged one way and steered another. If there's a signaler aboard, they'll have to signal guesses, and guesses will put Vane's fleet in the wrong water."

"And if there isn't a signaler?"

"Then we've lost nothing but sleep." Sefa smiled without any humor in it. "Cheap price."

Orla didn't smile back. She was looking past Sefa's shoulder, toward the bow, where Vesper stood at the rail with the wind pulling her coat flat against her back, staring south at a horizon that held, for her, more ghosts than water.

"You're going to tell her," Orla said. Not a question.

"I'm going to tell her. Tonight, after the tide turns." Sefa paused. "There's a right way and a wrong way to hand a woman a reason to suspect her own crew, and I intend to find the right way, because that woman has spent ten years suspecting everyone except the people who actually deserve it."

* * *

She told Vesper at dusk, in the stern cabin, with the lamp turned low and the door shut against the wind. She laid it out the way she laid out engine faults: symptoms first, diagnosis second, prognosis last. The too-clean drafting. The stair-stepped soundings. The gate that closed at slack-low on the fourth tide. The leash, and the hand that held it.

Vesper listened without moving. That was the frightening thing about her — most people's fear leaked out of them in fidgets and swallowed words, but Vesper's fear went inward, condensing, until she was denser than the room around her. When Sefa finished, the silence lasted long enough for the lamp to gutter twice.

"The fourth tide," Vesper said finally. Her voice was flat as slate. "That's the passage through the reef graveyard. He timed his trap to the exact water my father died in."

"I hadn't gotten that far," Sefa admitted. "I was stopping at *trap*."

"It's worse than a trap." Vesper turned the chart ninety degrees, studying it upside down, the way her father had supposedly taught her to read water — from the sea's point of view, not the sailor's. "Look at the fairway. It bends east around the outer shoal. There's no reason for that bend. The straight line is shorter and deeper. Unless—" her finger stopped on the bend, pressed hard enough to whiten the nail, "—unless the bend takes you past a specific patch of reef. High ground. A place where a small boat could lie hidden and watch the whole approach."

"A picket post," Sefa said.

"A picket post." Vesper laughed once, a sound with no joy in it whatsoever. "He didn't just build us a leash. He built himself a window. If anyone sits on that reef on the fourth tide, they'll see us coming, count our guns, count our crew, and signal the fleet. We'd be sailing into a report before we ever sailed into a fight."

"So we don't take the fairway."

"We take the fairway." Vesper's eyes came up, and Sefa saw the thing underneath the flatness now — not fear, exactly. Appetite. The terrible engine that had driven the woman nine days across burning salt without enough water. "We take it one tide early. We run the gate at high water when his arithmetic says we can't, we pass the picket reef in the dark before his watcher's even posted, and we let his fleet sail to a position where we *used to be*." She rolled the chart closed with a snap. "Let him chase our ghost."

"That's if the rest of the chart is honest," Sefa said. "Which I don't promise."

"Nothing about this is honest. That's rather the point." Vesper stood, tucked the chart under her arm, and paused at the door with her back to the cabin. "Sefa. The signaler question. Do you have a name in your head?"

Sefa considered lying, decided the woman had earned better, and said, "No. And that's what worries me. Because I've been watching all of us for a day now, and every one of us has a reason, and none of us has a motive. Grief makes strange couriers."

"Grief makes everything strange," Vesper said. "It's the only honest cargo we carry." She opened the door onto the wind and the darkening water, and then stopped again, half in, half out, her silhouette cut by the passage lamp. "One more thing. The logbook. Starting tonight, the true course goes in code — my father's old salvage cipher. If someone aboard is copying our position, let them copy nonsense."

She stepped out. Sefa sat alone with the lamp and the cold tea and the sound of the hull breathing against the swells, and tried to decide which was heavier: the chart in her hands, or the suspicion she'd just planted in a ship too small to quarantine.

* * *

She couldn't sleep. Past midnight she climbed into the engine space — not because the Kestrel needed her, but because engines were honest company. Steel and fire and clear tolerances. No ambiguity. She ran her hands over the main bearing, warm and steady, and let the rhythm of the machinery slow her pulse.

It was there, in the smell of hot oil, that the last piece arrived — the piece she'd been circling all day without wanting to name it.

The chart was incomplete *by design*, yes. But incomplete charts had a second use besides traps and leashes. An incomplete chart required the missing half. And the missing half of the Marlowe's position existed in exactly one place: Vesper's memory, and her father's papers. Papers that had been sealed, catalogued, and stored for ten years in the harbor archive at Port Calloway — under a retrieval authorization signed, Sefa now remembered with a cold slide of her stomach, by the harbormaster's office. The same office Vane's money touched the way oil touches water.

Which meant Vane might already *have* the second half. Which meant the half-chart wasn't a leash at all.

It was a test. A rehearsal. Vane was checking whether the route he'd reconstructed from stolen paper matched the route Vesper's father had actually sailed — using the Kestrel as his proof-copy, comparing their wake against his forgery, correcting his fleet's charts with their blood.

Sefa sat down on an ammunition crate in the dark, engine heat pressing against her back, and understood that she had it backwards. The question wasn't *who aboard was feeding Vane.*

The question was whether anyone aboard needed to be — when Vane had already eaten.

Above her, through the deck plating, came footsteps crossing the stern cabin. Slow ones. Pausing at the chart table. Staying there a long time.

Sefa killed the engine-room lamp and listened, and did not move, and counted the seconds like a woman counting the gap between lightning and thunder.
