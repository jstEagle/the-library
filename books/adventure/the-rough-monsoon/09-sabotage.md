## 9. Sabotage

Sefa woke to the wrong silence.

A ship has a voice, even at anchor. The Kestrel murmured in her sleep — the tick of cooling copper, the hawser's slow complaint against the chock, water worrying the hull in small patient rhythms. Sefa had spent twenty years listening to ships talk, first as an apprentice with grease to his elbows and a lamp between his teeth, later as the woman who kept the Kestrel breathing through two groundings and a fire. He knew her every murmur the way he knew his own pulse.

This silence was the silence of a ship holding her breath.

He lay in his bunk in the dark of the engine flat and listened harder. Under the hull-sounds, faint but unmistakable: a dragging weight aft. A shudder that came and went with the swell, like something heavy turning over down there in the black water, reluctant, enormous.

The rudder.

He was up before the thought finished forming, bare feet on cold deck plates, lamp lit with hands that were already steady because his hands always went steady when things went wrong — it was the quiet hours that made them shake. Through the stern port he could see the anchorage: a shallow roadstead behind a drowned headland, gray under a moon strangled by cloud, the reef line showing as a long white whisper of surf half a mile out. Good holding ground. They'd taken the fourth tide's rest here deliberately, Vesper wanting the crew slept before the channel run at dawn.

Something was wrong with the way the Kestrel sat. He couldn't have said what, not in words, but she rode a hand's breadth down by the stern, and the wake of current around her rudder stock was broken, disturbed, carrying a thin foam that had no business being there on a night this calm.

"Vesper," he said through the companionway, not shouting, because you didn't shout on a ship at night unless the world was ending. "Wake up. Trouble aft."

She came up fast, hair unbound, knife already in her hand — of course it was; the woman slept like a cat with a history. Orla followed, one arm still bound from the squall, moving carefully. Bram appeared last, from the forward hold where they'd grudgingly let him sling a hammock after the stowaway business, and Sefa watched him come up the ladder with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to.

They gathered at the stern rail. Vesper leaned out over the black water, lamp held low, and Sefa saw her face change as the light caught what lay below.

The rudder was fouled. Not fouled with weed, not fouled with the tangle of drifting net any honest sea might throw at them. Wrapped. Chain — anchor chain by the look of it, forty fathoms of it at least, paid out from somewhere above and hung down around the blade and stock in great deliberate loops, with scrap iron lashed into the bights so the whole arrangement would hang heavy and swing with the swell, working itself deeper into the gudgeons with every hour. It had been done by someone who understood rudders. Someone who understood exactly how much damage a length of chain could do if it was given time and tide.

Someone who had done it while four people slept.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The surf whispered on the reef. Somewhere out in the dark, a fish jumped and slapped back down.

"That's not drift," Orla said finally. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman keeping herself very still. "That's work. That's an hour's work, maybe more, in cold water, at night."

"It's sabotage," Bram said.

And there it was. The word hanging in the salt air like something gutted and bleeding.

Sefa looked at him. He hadn't meant to; it was instinct, the same instinct that made you check the bilge twice when someone new came aboard. But once he'd looked, he couldn't stop looking, because everything about the last three days rearranged itself around that single glance. Bram, ashore when the chart went missing from the chart table for an evening and turned up again slightly damp. Bram, awake at odd hours, standing at the rail in the dark. Bram, whose brother the Marlowe had taken, whose grief they had all agreed to honor by letting him aboard — and who had been given the run of a ship that trusted him.

"I was asleep," Bram said, meeting Sefa's eyes, and his voice cracked on the second word. "You think I did this?"

"I think somebody did this," Sefa said quietly. "And I think we're a crew of five, and one of us is standing very close to the evidence."

* * *

They argued until the sky went the color of dirty pewter.

It was Vesper who ended it, the way she ended most things — not with volume but with a decision that fell like a hatch closing. "It doesn't matter right now who did it. What matters is that we've lost the night tide, and if we don't get that chain off before the morning wind fills in, we lose the day too, and the day after that we're dead in the race." She was looking at the water, not at any of them. "Six tides. We've burned one."

"The water's shark water," Orla said. "This whole coast is shark water. You know what comes into the shallows at first light to feed."

"I know." Vesper's jaw was set in a way Sefa recognized from ten years ago, from the reef graveyard, from the night the world broke. "I'll go down. I need hands to work the chain up as I cut it free. Two on the winch, one on watch with a boat hook and a gun. Whoever fouled the rudder knew we'd have to choose between diving and losing the race. So we dive."

"You'll want me on the winch," Bram said.

"No," Vesper said, without turning around. "You'll be on watch. Where I can see you."

The hurt that crossed Bram's face was terrible to see — not guilt, Sefa thought, watching him carefully, or not only guilt. There was something else underneath it, something rawer. But Sefa had learned long ago that grief wears whatever face it needs to wear, and he had no time to sort one kind of pain from another. He went below to rig the diving gear.

* * *

The water at first light was the color of old bottle glass, green going gold where the sun came through, and warm on the surface and cold beneath, and full of the particular menace of tropical shallows at feeding time.

Sefa went down first because it was his ship and his rudder and because he had never in his life asked anyone to do a thing he wouldn't do himself. The visibility was maybe fifteen feet, then ten, then less as his fins stirred the fine silt off the shoal bottom. The Kestrel's hull rose above him like the underside of a floating island, her copper sheathing glowing dull orange in the filtered light, and hanging off her stern, swaying gently with the swell, the chain.

Up close it was worse than it had looked from above. The loops had worked deep. One link had already bitten into the wood of the stock, and the scrap iron weights — old engine mounts, he saw, engine mounts from a marine yard, which meant bought or stolen rather than found — swung in slow pendulum arcs that could take a man's arm off if he misjudged them. And threading through the whole arrangement, glinting where the light caught it, a fine monofilament leader, the kind used for big-game fishing, tied off in a slipknot around the lowest bight.

Not a fisherman's knot. A booby trap. Pull the chain free fast, the way a panicked diver would, and the slipknot cinches, and the weights drop, and whatever is directly below them when they drop stops being a problem for anyone.

Sefa hung in the green water and felt the fear arrive, cold and clean, and let it pass through him the way he'd learned to let all things pass. Then he began, link by link, loop by loop, with a hacksaw blade strapped to his wrist, to undo the trap the way it had been built — slowly, patiently, in reverse.

Above him, muffled by water, he could hear the winch turning. Orla's voice counting. Once — just once — a shape slid past at the edge of visibility, long and gray and utterly indifferent to him, and his heart went into his throat and stayed there for the rest of the dive, beating against his teeth.

It took two hours. When he finally surfaced, hauling himself onto the boarding ladder with arms that shook, the sun was fully up and the morning wind was already filling in from the southeast, whitecaps starting beyond the reef, and Vesper crouched over him on the deck with a blanket and a question in her eyes.

"The chain's clear," he rasped. "But listen to me. It wasn't drift, and it wasn't opportunists. Engine mounts for weights. Monofilament slipknot rigged to kill whoever pulled it free. That's professional work, Vesper. That's Vane's work, or someone working for Vane." He got his breath back. "Which means either we have a traitor aboard, or Vane's people found us in the night, and I don't know which possibility I like less."

"And the monofilament?" Vesper asked. "Anything about it tell you anything?"

Sefa closed his eyes, running the memory of it back through his fingers. "Tied left-handed," he said. "I noticed because I'm left-handed myself, and you notice your own habits in other people's work. Small thing. Probably nothing."

But Vesper had gone very still, and Sefa watched her look, slowly, across the deck — at Orla, right-handed, working the winch with both hands. At himself. At Bram, standing at the rail with the boat hook in his right hand, staring out at the whitecaps, his shoulders rigid, his face turned away.

Left-handed.

* * *

They made the channel on the afternoon tide, running the Kestrel through the drowned coast with the wind freshening behind them, and nobody talked about the chain, and everybody thought about nothing else.

Sefa stood his trick at the wheel and felt the rudder answer — stiff, bruised, but answering — and tried to make the arithmetic come out. Five people aboard. Four alibis that amounted to sleeping, which was no alibi at all. One piece of professional sabotage, rigged by someone with salvage training and a left-handed habit with knots. And underneath all of it, the question he kept circling like a shark of his own: why?

If Vane wanted them dead, there were cheaper ways than chain and slipknots. If Vane wanted them slowed, the chain alone would have done it — the killing knot was a message, or insurance, or spite. And if it wasn't Vane at all, if it was someone aboard, someone grieving, someone who had followed them out onto this water for reasons of their own—

"Bram," he said, during the middle watch, when the two of them had the cockpit to themselves and the coast was a black line to starboard. "The night the rudder was fouled. You were asleep, you said."

"I was asleep."

"Wake at all? Hear anything? Even a splash, a scrape, anything?"

Bram was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Sefa's hands tightened on the wheel spokes.

"There was a sound," Bram said finally, and his voice was strange, hollowed out. "Sometime in the black hours. A splash, like you say. I put it down to a fish. I almost got up." He turned then, and in the low light of the binnacle his eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "I almost got up, Sefa. And I didn't, because I thought — what does it matter, what happens to this ship, what happens to any of us, when the Marlowe's down there waiting?" His breath caught. "Is that what you wanted to hear? That I heard it and let it happen? Because some nights I don't know what I'd have done if I'd gotten up and seen whoever it was in the water. Some nights I don't trust myself with what's left of my heart, and you all brought me out here anyway."

Sefa held the wheel and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to that, nothing that wouldn't be a lie or a cruelty.

But he didn't sleep for the rest of that watch. And near dawn, checking the lazarette for spare shackles, he found what he found: a coil of monofilament leader, salt-stiff, tucked behind the spare anchor warp. Enough for a dozen slipknots.

He stood in the dim little space with it in his hands and felt betrayal arrive in his chest like ballast, settling him, weighting him, making the ship of himself steadier and sadder and far more dangerous.

He didn't shout. He didn't wake Vesper. He carried the coil up on deck and put it in the chart table drawer, under the half-chart, and waited for morning, and watched Bram sleep in the forward hold with his face turned to the bulkhead and his fists clenched even in dreams, and thought:

Whoever you are, whatever you are — grief doesn't buy you my ship.

Then the lookout call came down from Orla, sharp and rising, and Sefa forgot the coil entirely for one full minute, because smoke was standing up off the southern horizon, tall and straight and black, the smoke of funnel stacks under forced draft, and there was only one fleet on this water that burned coal like that.

Vane had cut ahead of them.

And behind the smoke, low and purple and wide as the sky, the storm front was building.
