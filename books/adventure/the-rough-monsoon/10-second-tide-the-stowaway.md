## 10. Second Tide: The Stowaway

Orla heard him before anyone else did — not a sound, exactly, but the absence of one. The Kestrel had her own language at sea, a grammar of creaks and groans that Sefa could read like scripture, and for three days now there had been a wrongness in the rhythm of the hold, a silence where the ship's breathing should have been. A stowaway, Orla thought, listening in the dark of the second tide's dog watch, is a person who holds still. And holding still aboard a wooden ship is the loudest thing a body can do.

She didn't wake Vesper right away. She stood at the top of the hold ladder with a lantern unlit in her hand and let herself feel it first — the shape of another heartbeat down there in the bilge-dark, sharing their water, breathing their air, riding the same long swell toward the same drowned coast. There was something almost holy about it, she thought, and something obscene. She knew what it was to hide in a hold. She'd been small enough, once, to fit inside an emergency locker on a much larger ship, knees to her chest, salt water seeping through the seams above her head.

That memory decided it for her. No one should have to be found by surprise.

"Vesper," she said quietly, when she reached the deck. "We've got a rat in the hold."

Vesper was at the wheel, though Bram's charts had them on a broad reach that needed little steering. She'd taken to standing there anyway, hands on the spokes as if the ship were a thing that could feel being held. "Rats don't bother you," she said without looking over. "You told me once you'd share your bunk with worse."

"This rat wears boots," Orla said. "Size eleven. I've been counting his meals."

That got Vesper's attention. She turned from the wheel slowly, and in the gray-green light of the second tide's overcast morning, Orla watched three days of suspicion rearrange themselves into certainty on her captain's face. "Sefa said the stores were off. I told her she was miscounting."

"Sefa doesn't miscount." Orla set the lantern down. "I say we open the hatch now, quiet, before he hears us coming. Or—" she paused, because the other option had been sitting in her chest since midnight, heavy as ballast, "—or we let him come up on his own. He will, eventually. They always do. Hunger makes cowards of everyone but the dead."

Vesper looked at the hold hatch for a long moment. Rain had begun again, fine and needling, the front edge of the weather system they could all see building black behind them. "No," she said at last. "We do it my way. We've lost enough time to sabotage already. I'm not losing more wondering who's down there and what he's already done to my ship."

* * *

They came at the hold the way you'd breach a flooded compartment — Sefa with a pry bar, Orla with the lantern, Vesper last with her knife drawn and her face arranged into something that wasn't anger yet but had its address. The hatch hinges shrieked. Lantern light fell down the ladder well and across the shadowed tiers of crates and coiled line, and for a moment there was nothing — just the dark breathing back at them, smelling of tar and bilgewater and old canvas.

Then a crate shifted near the stern, and a voice came out of the shadows, low and wrecked with exhaustion.

"Don't. Please. Just — give me a minute. I'll come up."

"Bram." Vesper said the name flatly, the way you'd read a verdict. Of course it was Bram. It had been Bram since the rudder fouled; it had perhaps been Bram since the harbor. Orla saw the knowledge land on Vesper like a blow she'd been bracing against for days and had hoped never to receive.

The shadows moved. Bram came up the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, and when his head cleared the hatch coaming Orla nearly gasped. Three days in a closed hold had hollowed him. His eyes sat in bruised sockets, his lips were cracked, and his hands — his navigator's hands, precise and clean her whole life aboard — were filthy, black to the wrist, shaking. He stepped onto the deck and stood swaying in the rain with his arms slightly away from his body, the universal posture of a man surrendering, and waited.

"You fouled our rudder." Vesper's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. "We spent a full tide in shark water with Sefa half-drowned fixing what you broke. If she'd slipped—"

"I know what I did." Bram's voice cracked on *know*. "I know what it cost. I've lain down there listening to you talk about it every night. Every meal. Every watch change. I know."

"Then why?" Orla asked, before she could stop herself. It was the question under all of it, the one Vesper wouldn't ask because asking would mean wanting an answer. "Why follow us? Why break us if you wanted to come?"

Bram looked at her then, and Orla saw something in his face she recognized from mirrors she hadn't owned in twenty years — the look of a person carrying a grief so old it had become load-bearing, part of the structure, so that pulling it out might collapse everything.

"Because I couldn't decide," he said simply. "For eleven years I couldn't decide whether I hated the Marlowe or missed her. And I thought — if I stayed ashore, I'd never have to find out. And if I stopped you, I'd never have to find out either. So I came to stop you. That was the plan. Stop the Kestrel, turn her back, save everyone from—" His breath hitched. "From the place that took my brother."

The rain ticked against the deck. Somewhere aft, the sea made its long indifferent sound against the hull.

"And then?" Vesper said.

"And then I got down in that hold," Bram said, "and I listened to you all sail toward it anyway. Toward the reef graveyard. Toward the channel. And I lay in the dark thinking about Eamon — how he was twenty-two, how he went out on the Marlowe because the pay was double and he wanted to buy Mam a house with a roof that didn't leak — and I realized I hadn't come to stop anything. I came because I need to *see* it." His voice tore open on the word. "Eleven years, Vesper. Eleven years I've navigated ships around that coast like it was plague ground, giving it a berth of ten miles, twenty, drawing the exclusion on every chart I ever touched. I have never once looked at it. I need to stand over the place where my brother is and look down and — I don't even know. Say something. Say nothing. Be sick over the rail. I don't know. But I can't keep pretending the ocean swallowed him politely and it's fine."

Nobody moved. Orla found that her own eyes were burning, and she hated it, and she let it happen.

It was Sefa who spoke next, from beside the hatch, pry bar still in hand but hanging loose now, forgotten. "Eamon Bramwell," she said quietly. "Deck crew. Second voyage. I helped load the manifest the day she sailed. He carried his own crate aboard — insisted on it, wouldn't let the stevedores touch it. I remember thinking, there's a boy who'll work out fine."

"He did work out fine," Bram whispered. "Mam got the letter eight months later. Eight months. Nobody even knew where to send divers until—" He stopped. Looked at Vesper. And Orla watched him understand, in real time, watching her face, that the Marlowe had not taken only his brother. That the woman at the wheel had left more in that water than any of them had ever dared count.

* * *

They put him in the galley, chained by the ankle to the stove mount — Vesper's compromise, mercy with a lock on it — and Orla sat with him while the others argued in low voices on deck. She brought him water and hardtack and a strip of dried fish, and watched him eat like a man relearning the mechanics of it, and after a while she said:

"I was on her too."

Bram's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"The last voyage," Orla said. "I was nine. My father was bosun. When she started going down — I don't remember the sinking, people think I must, but I don't. I remember the locker. I remember the water coming up through the deck seams while I was folded up inside it, and I remember deciding that if I breathed in, I would die, so I didn't breathe in. For — I don't know how long. Long enough that when they pulled me out I'd bitten through my own lip." She turned her cup in her hands. "Everyone always asks survivors what they remember. Nobody asks what they decided. But that's the real cargo, isn't it. What you decided, down in the dark. I decided not to breathe. You decided not to look. Same trick, different door."

Bram set the spoon down. His hands had gone still for the first time since he'd climbed out of the hold. "Does it ever stop working?" he asked. "The trick."

Orla considered lying to him, and found she didn't want to. Not here, not with the drowned coast somewhere ahead under this same gray water, not with the storm stacking itself up behind them like a debt coming due.

"No," she said. "But it gets heavier. Until one day carrying it is harder than putting it down would have been. Then you put it down. Usually at the worst possible moment, in front of witnesses." She smiled, and felt it crack something loose in her chest. "Fair warning."

* * *

At dusk Vesper came below. Orla was mending net in the corner and pretended very hard to be absorbed in it, which fooled no one, least of all Vesper, who pulled the crate opposite Bram's stove-anchor around backward and sat on it facing him, forearms on knees, close enough that the chain between them was the only distance left in the world.

"Here's what happens now," Vesper said. "You're going to navigate us into the lagoon. The real approach, your way, no chart games — because whatever else you are, you're the best navigator I've ever sailed with, and I'd be a fool to sail blind into that coast with Vane's fleet behind me. You do that, and when it's done, you get your look. Over the rail, at anchor, as long as you need. And then—" she paused, and Orla saw her choose the words carefully, laying them down like stones across deep water, "—and then you and I are going to have a longer conversation about the rudder. But not tonight. Tonight I'm too tired to hate you properly, and you look like death's unpaid debts."

Bram laughed — a broken, wet sound, but a laugh. "That's the kindest sentence anyone's ever aimed at me."

"Don't get used to it." Vesper stood. Then, at the foot of the ladder, she stopped, her back to him, and said something so quiet Orla almost missed it under the creak of the rigging. "Eamon carried his own crate aboard. Sefa told me once, years ago. I'd forgotten." Her hand tightened on the ladder rail. "I forget most of them, Bram. That's what running is. You're not the only one who owes the Marlowe a visit."

She climbed up into the rain, and was gone.

Bram sat looking at the chain on his ankle, and then at the chart table bolted to the bulkhead two arm-lengths away — so close, and suddenly the farthest thing in the world, and the nearest. Orla watched him reach for it, watch his fingers hover over the blank paper Sefa had left there, and begin, at last, to draw.

Outside, the wind backed a point to the northeast, and the barometer needle slid down another notch, and somewhere beyond the gray curtain of the second tide, black sails were running before the storm toward the same lagoon — and none of them knew it yet.
