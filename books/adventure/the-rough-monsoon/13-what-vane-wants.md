## 13. What Vane Wants

The *Corbie's Pride* rode at anchor in the lee of Serrano Point, and Cassian Vane could not sleep.

He had owned the flagship for eleven years — black-hulled, triple-masted, her cabins paneled in teak taken from a prize he'd rather not name — and in all that time he had never once lain awake listening to the sea. The sea was a ledger to him, and ledgers balanced. Tonight they did not. Tonight the water against the hull sounded like breathing.

He rose from his bunk, dressed by feel in the dark, and climbed to the great cabin aft, where the stern lanterns threw amber light across charts weighted with brass. His steward had left coffee; it had gone cold hours ago, and he drank it anyway, standing at the gallery windows, watching the storm front stack itself along the southern horizon like a wall being built by patient hands.

Six tides. That was what he had given Pemberton's daughter. Six tides before his fleet reached the Marlowe.

He had told himself it was sport. He was good at telling himself things — it was, in truth, the finest skill he possessed, finer than navigation, finer than negotiation, finer than the particular smile that opened doors in every port from Corvane to the Salt Reaches. He had told himself for thirty years that what happened off the Drowned Coast had been weather. Had been bad luck. Had been Marlowe's own stubbornness, old Aldous Voss's miscalculation, the sea simply doing what the sea did.

But ledgers do not lie, and Cassian Vane had spent three decades making certain one particular ledger never surfaced.

* * *

He kept it in his memory, of course. The original had gone down with the ship — or so he had believed until four months ago, when a diver named Idris Fenn, half-starved and full of secrets, had come to him in a tavern on Corvane with a fragment of chart and a story: that the Marlowe had been found. That she sat upright in a deep lagoon behind the reef graveyard, preserved in cold dark water like a saint in glass. That her captain's safe might still be sealed.

Vane had bought the man's silence with coin and his loyalty with nothing at all, because men like Idris were loyal only to whoever held the next meal. And then Idris had done the one thing Vane had not paid him to do. He had run. He had taken the half-chart and vanished into the salt flats, and Vane had understood within a day exactly who would find him — because there was only one person alive who would chase a thief across nine days of white hell rather than let a chart slip loose into the world.

Vesper Pemberton. The ranger. The drifter. The girl who had swum out of the wreck of her father's expedition and never once looked back — or so the stories said. Vane had followed those stories for years, quietly, the way a man follows a wound he cannot stop touching. She had become a tracker of thieves and slavers on the desert margins, a woman with no port, no flag, no name worth speaking twice. She had buried the sea so thoroughly that even her own crew called her by a nickname earned on dry land.

And Vane had handed her the other half of the chart himself.

He turned from the window now, crossed to his writing desk, and unlocked the drawer beneath it with a key he wore on a cord around his neck. Inside lay a copy of a contract — the one he had pressed upon her crew in that smoke-filled room, all charm and deadlines, six tides ticking down like water into a drowning lung. He read it again though he knew every clause. It was a beautiful instrument. It gave them just enough chart to sail, not enough to arrive without his help if they faltered. It bound them to secrecy while guaranteeing his fleet would shadow their wake. If they succeeded, he took the salvage under terms they'd signed in desperation. If they failed, they died, and the location died with them, and the ledgers stayed drowned forever.

Either way, Cassian Vane won. That was the design. That had always been the design.

Except.

Except that the Marlowe had not carried treasure. Not primarily. Not in the way that mattered.

* * *

Thirty-one years ago, the salvage consortium out of Corvane had outfitted an expedition to raise the Marlowe — a merchantman lost with a fortune in alloy ingots and, in her captain's strongroom, the financial records of half the shipping houses on the coast. Insurance claims, cargo manifests, letters of credit. Paper. The kind of paper that made fortunes and unmade men.

Cassian Vane had been twenty-six years old, third partner in a small firm, hungry in the way that hollows a man out and leaves something else wearing his face. He had sold information to the consortium — depths, currents, the secret route through the reef graveyard that old Voss had spent years surveying. Sold it knowing the expedition was undermanned, knowing the monsoon season was closing, knowing that the man who held that route knowledge could dictate terms to everyone who survived it.

He had told himself he was selling maps, not lives.

Then the storm came early. Then the fleet scattered on the reefs. Then the reports came back — the Kestrel's namesake lost with all hands, the flagship holed, Aldous Voss refusing to abandon his divers, going down with the wreck he'd sworn to raise — and Cassian Vane sat in a dry office in Corvane and realized that the numbers he had sold had become corpses, and that the only proof of what he had done sat in a sealed safe at the bottom of the sea, in the strongroom of a ship whose captain had died rather than surface without his people.

He had built everything since on top of that realization. The fleet. The reputation. The name spoken with respect in every salvage court from here to the outer banks. A man can build a cathedral over a grave, Vane thought, watching the lightning walk the horizon, but the grave does not care how high the spire goes.

Four months ago, Idris Fenn had told him the grave had a door.

* * *

"Captain."

His first mate, Doran Hale, stood in the doorway of the great cabin — a slab of a man with a face like weathered rope, loyal in the way of men who had been rescued from debt-galleys and knew precisely what their rescue had cost. He had served Vane for nineteen years and had never once asked why the flagship always seemed to be sailing away from Corvane whenever certain archives were open for inspection.

"The fleet's formed up," Hale said. "Seven hulls. The *Iron Meridian* reports the strait passage is clear ahead of the front — we can cut two days off the long route if we run tonight."

"And the watchers on Pemberton's ketch?"

"Gone quiet. She slipped harbor two nights ago, gray water, no lights. My contact says the harbor officials were paid to look elsewhere — someone on her side has friends." Hale hesitated. "There's also word of a stowaway problem aboard. Unconfirmed."

Vane smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was how anyone who truly knew him could tell it was false. "A stowaway. How very human of them."

"You think she'll make the lagoon?"

"I think," Vane said slowly, "that Vesper Pemberton is the only person alive who wants to reach that wreck more than I want to reach it. Which makes her either my greatest obstacle or my finest instrument." He set down the cup. "We let her lead us through the liar's channel, Mr. Hale. Her father drew that route, and her father's blood remembers it even if her mind has buried it. We follow. And when the safe is open—" He paused, choosing the words the way a surgeon chooses a blade. "—we burn what must be burned, and we are generous with the rest. Generosity is cheap. Gold is heavy, but it is not dangerous."

Hale frowned. "You're saying the salvage isn't the point."

"I'm saying the salvage was never the point." Vane moved to the chart table and put one finger on the deep lagoon behind the reef graveyard, pressing hard enough to whiten the nail. "There is a strongroom on that ship, Mr. Hale, and in that strongroom there is a safe, and in that safe there are documents that would take everything I have built and feed it to the courts. Thirty years of reputation. Nine ships. A name my children will inherit." His voice did not rise; it lowered, which was worse. "I have spent three decades being respectable. Do you know what respectability costs? Everything. Every year, more. And all of it rests on the assumption that the bottom of the sea keeps its mouth shut."

Lightning lit the cabin white. In the flash, Hale saw something on his captain's face that he had never seen in nineteen years of service, and wisely never mentioned afterward: fear. Not the fear of storms or fleets or knives in the dark. The older kind. The kind that sits in a man's chest like ballast, that he has carried so long he mistakes it for his own weight.

"Signal the fleet," Vane said quietly. "We run the strait tonight, ahead of the front. Full press of canvas. I want the lagoon by the fifth tide."

"That's cutting it fine, Captain. That squall line—"

"Is the least dangerous thing in that water." Vane rolled up the chart with hands that were almost steady. "Storms kill bodies, Mr. Hale. I have made my peace with losing mine. But there is a piece of paper in a dead man's safe that kills everything else, and I will pass through any weather ever brewed to burn it."

* * *

They ran the strait through the small hours, seven hulls in line ahead, lamps doused, the *Corbie's Pride* leading her children into the narrowing dark between cliffs that had eaten better sailors than any of them. Vane stood on the quarterdeck through all of it, coatless in the spray, watching the white water boil off the rocks and feeling — for the first time in thirty years — that the sea and he were finally honest with each other.

It had never been about the ingots. He had let the world believe it, let Pemberton's crew believe it, let himself believe it on the good days. But a man who sells a route knows what the route is for, and Vane had known, even at twenty-six, even in his hunger, that the most valuable cargo the Marlowe carried was testimony. Aldous Voss had been meticulous — the whole coast had known it, had laughed at it, the old man and his logs and his copies and his seals. If Voss had scuttled the payout, if Voss had chosen his drowning crew over the consortium's fortune, then somewhere in that safe was a written account of why. Of who had sold the depths. Of who had fed the expedition to the reef graveyard with clean hands and a signed contract.

Of him.

And the girl — the girl who had been ten years old in the water that night, who had grown into a woman who chased thieves across salt flats because arriving anywhere meant remembering — the girl was sailing straight toward her father's handwriting, and Vane had handed her the map to it himself.

He laughed once, short and joyless, into the wind. Somewhere behind them the storm front gathered its patience. Somewhere ahead, in a lagoon of green-black water, a dead captain waited with the truth in his strongroom, and a living daughter raced to meet him.

"Mr. Hale," Vane said. "When we make the headland — no signals. No lights. We come around in silence and take the lagoon at slack water, before they've finished diving. Whatever happens, the safe opens first, and what comes out of it goes into the fire while the ink is still wet."

"Aye, Captain."

Vane looked south, where the lightning had begun to fall like slow white rain, and admitted to himself, in the privacy of his own skull where no court could reach, the thing he had refused to admit for thirty-one years:

He was not racing for treasure. He was running from a page.

And pages, unlike men, always surfaced eventually.
