## 15. Fifth Tide: The Wreck

Orla felt the lagoon before she saw it.

It came up through the soles of her boots first — a change in the Kestrel's motion, the long gray chop of the outer water smoothing into something gentler, rounder, as though the sea itself had drawn a breath and held it. Then the color changed. For five tides they had sailed through water the shade of old pewter, storm-fed and sullen, and now beneath the bow the sea turned suddenly, impossibly green — a deep, lit emerald, clear as bottle glass, as if the monsoon had never touched this place at all.

"Soundings," Vesper said quietly, and Orla heard the effort it took to keep her voice level.

Bram was already at the lead line, his broken arm bound tight against his chest, swinging the weight with his good one in a slow practiced arc. "Fifteen fathoms," he called. "Twelve. Ten." A pause. The line hissed out. "Ten. Holding ten."

Holding. As if the bottom had decided to be a floor instead of a grave.

Orla stood at the rail with her splinted arm cradled against her ribs and looked down into that impossible green, and something in her chest went very cold and very still. She had been seven years old the last time she saw water like this. She had seen it through a porthole no bigger than a dinner plate, from a bunk below decks on a ship called the *Marlowe*, on the last night of its last voyage, while rain hammered the hull like a fist demanding entry.

She had not thought of that porthole in years. She had made a discipline of not thinking of it. But here it was again, rising in her memory with the clarity of things drowned and perfectly preserved: the green light, the sound of her mother laughing somewhere above, the small tin whale on the shelf beside her bunk that her father had won for her at a harbor fair.

The Kestrel slid forward over the reef lip and into the lagoon, and the world went quiet.

* * *

It was Sefa who saw it first, though none of them needed telling once she pointed. She stood at the bow rail with her goggles pushed up into her gray-streaked hair, and she did not shout, did not even speak loudly. She simply said, "There," in the voice of someone naming a headstone.

Orla followed her finger down through the water.

At first there was nothing but light — shafts of it slanting down through the clear green like the beams of some drowned cathedral. Then the light found edges. A line where shadow met shape. A curve too long and too deliberate to be rock.

The *Marlowe* lay upright on the lagoon floor, forty feet down, whole.

Not broken across a reef the way wrecks were supposed to lie, not scattered, not gnawed open by decades of current and coral. Upright. Keel-down in the sand as if she had simply knelt there one day and stayed. Her masts had gone, snapped off somewhere near the deck, but her hull was intact, her rails unbroken, her stern windows — Orla's breath caught — her stern windows still held their glass, filmed green with growth but whole, and behind that glass, faint as a photograph developing, the shapes of furniture. A table. A chair. A lamp hanging exactly plumb from a beam, swaying gently in a current that had been swaying it for twenty years.

"She didn't sink," Bram said. His voice came out strange, thin. "She was *set down*."

"Scuttled," Sefa said. "Controlled. Opened her seacocks slow, even keel, nobody panicking." She shook her head slowly. "That's not a wreck. That's a burial."

Orla gripped the rail. The tin whale. The green light. Her mother's laugh cutting off mid-note when the first alarm bell rang — no, not an alarm, she realized now, understanding arriving twenty years late like a diver surfacing with something clutched in its fist. Not an alarm. A call to stations. Calm voices. Men moving with purpose, not panic, because someone had decided the ship would die gently, and everyone aboard would live to see it done.

Her father had carried her up the ladder himself. She remembered his heart hammering against her ear, steady as a drumbeat, a heart that was not afraid. *Hold on to me, little fish. We're going for a swim.*

They had all gone for a swim. All of them, alive. And the ship had lain down in this lagoon like a dog told to stay, and waited twenty years for someone to come back.

Nobody spoke. The only sounds were water against the hull and the creak of the boom overhead, and far away, the mutter of the storm front still building behind them, patient as debt.

Vesper stood apart at the stern rail, and Orla watched her look down at the wreck of the ship her father had died salvaging, and could not read her face at all. It wasn't grief. It wasn't awe. It was the expression of a woman reading a letter she had refused to open for a decade, holding it still sealed, knowing that the moment she broke the wax nothing would ever be the same.

"We anchor," Vesper said finally. "Then we go down."

* * *

They anchored in the lee of the northern headland, where the lagoon's rim rose in dark volcanic shoulders hung with dripping green, and the water inside was so sheltered that the Kestrel barely swung on her rode. The storm behind them filled the southern sky like a bruise coming up through skin, but here, inside the ring, the air was warm and unnaturally calm, and the light fell straight down into the deep.

Sefa rigged the dive lines off the mainmast stump, checking every knot twice, three times, running her hands along the ropes the way other people prayed. Orla worked beside her in silence, passing gear, and felt the strange intimacy of shared dread. They had not spoken much since the squall line, since Orla had come down from the rigging with her arm hanging wrong and Sefa had set it without either of them saying anything that mattered. Some things did not need saying. You survived a thing together and afterward you handed each other rope.

"I'll take the hull breach survey," Sefa said. "Bram, you're topside on the line. Orla—"

"I'm going down," Orla said.

Sefa looked at her. So did Vesper.

"My arm's broke, not my legs," Orla said. "And I've got more reason than any of you." She kept her voice flat, because if she let it bend even slightly it would break. "I was aboard her. Last voyage. I was seven years old."

The words dropped into the quiet like a stone into a well, and the well was deeper than anyone had guessed. She saw Vesper's eyes change — the ranger's flat assessment giving way to something rawer, recognition of another kind of orphan.

"You never said," Vesper said.

"Neither did you," Orla said. "About your father. About any of it. We're all of us the same kind of quiet." She took the dive knife off the rack and belted it on, awkward with her left hand. "I'm going down."

Nobody argued. There was nothing left to argue about. The wreck had sorted them all already.

* * *

The water took her like a hand closing around her, cool then cold then perfect, and the noise of the world above — the wind, the creaking, the human voices — fell away into a vast ringing silence broken only by the sound of her own breath rasping through the regulator.

Orla descended the shot line hand over hand, and the *Marlowe* rose to meet her.

That was how it felt. Not that she sank toward it, but that the ship came up — out of the green gloom, out of twenty years of patience — deck by deck, rail by rail, until she was hovering above the quarterdeck of the vessel she had slept aboard as a child, and the sight of it stopped her breath in her chest for two full seconds until the need for air forced it going again.

Growth covered everything, soft and pale, the whole ship wearing a shroud of pale anemone and drifting weed, so that she seemed less a wreck than a sleeping woman veiled. Fish moved through the open hatches in slow silver schools, unconcerned, permanent residents now. The wheel was gone from the helm but the binnacle remained, its brass long since greened, leaning at rest. And everywhere that eerie uprightness, that terrible orderliness. Coils of rope still flaked neatly on the deck. A lantern still hung by the companionway. A pair of boots sat side by side outside what must have been the galley, waiting for feet that had walked away and never come back.

Scuttled, Sefa had said. Set down gently. Every soul brought off alive before the seacocks opened.

Every soul but one.

Orla drifted along the deck, her fins stirring little ghosts of silt, and found herself counting doorways, remembering. That had been the galley where Cook Marta let her stir the pot. That hatch led down to the steerage berths where her family had slept, all four of them in a nest of blankets, rocking. That was the companionway her father had carried her up, and she knew it, knew it in her body, the way you know the staircase of a house you grew up in, and the knowledge hurt worse than her arm.

Ahead of her, Vesper hung in the water before the great cabin's stern windows, motionless.

Orla swam toward her slowly. Through the filmed glass she could see the cabin beyond: the table bolted to the floor, the chart drawers, the lamp. And a door, standing half-open, leading aft to the master's stateroom.

Vesper went in through the broken transom port, and after a moment Orla followed.

* * *

Inside, the cabin was a cathedral of suspended light. Shafts of green sun fell through the stern windows and the broken port, laying bars of brightness across the silted floor, and in that light the room's contents hung or rested with the stillness of things under glass. A cot, neatly made — made, after twenty years underwater, corners tucked, blanket smooth, and Orla understood with a chill that it had been made *before*, that whoever slept here had been the kind of man who made his bed even on the last night of the world. A shelf of books fused into a single block by time. On the desk, weighted down by a brass bar, charts — rolled, tied, patient.

And on the desk, centered, deliberate, placed rather than dropped: a compass.

Orla saw Vesper see it. Saw her reach out with a gloved hand and stop short, hovering, trembling, not touching, the way you hover over the face of someone sleeping whom you are afraid to wake. The compass card inside the glass had long since surrendered to the water, but it still pointed, faithfully, uselessly, in whatever direction it had pointed when the ship lay down to die.

Vesper's shoulders shook. Underwater, without sound, it was the loneliest thing Orla had ever seen — grief happening in silence, in green light, twenty fathoms from air, witnessed by no one but a dead man's room and a girl who had also been carried out of this ship in a father's arms.

Then Vesper turned, and her eyes found the safe.

It was set into the bulkhead beneath the chart shelf, iron, square, its dial crusted white. Someone had tried it before — Orla could see the old tool marks, rusted brown, decades old. Salvors. Her father's own crew, maybe, in the desperate weeks after the sinking, before the storms closed the coast. They had failed. The door was still shut. Whatever Elias Pemberton had locked away on the last night of the *Marlowe*'s life, it was still in there, still waiting, sealed in the dark with the same patience as everything else in this drowned, ordered room.

Vesper pulled the dive knife from her belt.

Orla wanted to surface. Wanted air, wanted sun, wanted to be anywhere but inside this room where the past had been waiting for them both like a trap sprung slow across twenty years. But she stayed. She owed the dead that much. She owed the living more.

It took Vesper a long time at the dial, working by feel through gloves, the knife blade levering, the mechanism fighting her the way it had fought every hand for two decades. Bubbles streamed from her regulator in silver strings. Once she stopped, forehead nearly touching the iron, and Orla thought she was praying, or cursing, or both.

Then something gave with a sound Orla felt through the water rather than heard — a deep iron click, like a door unlocking at the bottom of the world — and the safe swung open.

Vesper reached in.

What she drew out was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and even furred with twenty years of silt it was unmistakable to anyone who had lived aboard ships: a logbook. Heavy. Sealed. Whole.

Vesper held it against her chest with both arms, the way you hold a child, the way you hold the last thing left of someone, and looked up through the green light toward the distant bright ceiling of the surface, and Orla saw her mouth move behind the regulator. No sound. But Orla had spent a lifetime reading lips across noisy decks, and she caught it anyway.

*Dad.*

* * *

They surfaced together into a world on fire.

Orla broke the water gasping, her splinted arm screaming, and the first thing she saw was Bram at the rail of the Kestrel, hauling them in, and the second thing she saw was his face — and his face was wrong. White. Looking past them, southward, toward the headland.

"Sails," he said. "Half a dozen. Roundin' the point now." He swallowed. "It's Vane. It's the whole bloody fleet."

Orla turned in the water.

Beyond the southern rim of the lagoon, where all morning there had been only the green shoulders of the coast and the building bruise of the storm, the headland was sprouting masts. One, then three, then six — tall salvage steamers coming around the point in line ahead, black smoke pouring from their stacks into the low sky, driving hard for the lagoon mouth with the storm at their backs and murder in their bearing. Even at this distance Orla could see the derricks on their foredecks, raised and ready like gallows.

Beside her, treading water, the logbook wrapped tight in oilcloth and clutched to her chest, Vesper Pemberton went very still.

"They can't have the wreck," she said. The words came out low and even, and Orla had never heard anything so frightening in her life. "They can't have him."

The fleet rounded the headland, and the first gun-flash flickered at its leading edge like the storm learning to aim.
