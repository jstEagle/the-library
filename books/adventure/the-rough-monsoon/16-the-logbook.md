## 16. The Logbook

The safe was where he had always kept it.

Not in the captain's cabin — Vesper had already torn that room apart an hour ago, upending the chart table, prying at the false panel behind the bunk where her father used to hide the good tobacco, finding nothing but rust and the small pale skeletons of barnacles that had colonized every seam of the *Marlowe* like a second crew. She had come out of that cabin with salt water streaming off her dive gear and her hands shaking so badly she had to grip the ladder with both arms, and Orla, hauling on the winch above, had looked at her with those calm dark eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the water hadn't already said.

But the safe was in the chartroom. It had always been in the chartroom. She had known that the way she knew the scar on her own palm — a round iron door set into the deck beneath the chart table, bolted from below, invisible unless you knew to kneel and look. Her father used to let her sit on the table's edge when she was small, her heels drumming the wood, while he spun the combination wheel with one hand and pretended it was a magic trick. *What's inside, Papa?* she would ask, every time. *The ship's memory,* he would answer, every time, and tap her nose with one wet finger. *A captain keeps his promises where the sea can't reach them.*

The sea had reached everything else. But the safe had been built by a stubborn man, and it had held.

Vesper knelt on the deck of the drowned chartroom, her dive light cutting a yellow cone through water the color of old glass, and worked the wheel. Ten years of corrosion had welded the spindle nearly solid. She had brought a pry bar down in her kit — Sefa had insisted, Sefa who prepared for everything — and she wedged it into the handle and leaned her whole weight against it, her fins braced against the deck plates, her breath rasping loud in the regulator. The iron groaned. Somewhere above, faint as a heartbeat, she felt the *Kestrel* rolling at anchor, and below her, through the open hull, the lagoon floor waited in the dark like a held breath.

The wheel turned a quarter-inch. Then another. She worked it the way her father had taught her to work anything stubborn — patiently, angrily, without ever letting go.

*You always said I gave up too easy,* she thought, and the thought had no bitterness in it, which frightened her more than anything else down here had. *You said a Pemberton finishes what she starts. I spent ten years proving you wrong about me.*

The safe opened with a sound like a sigh.

* * *

Inside, the water had been kinder than the air above ever was. The ledger books of the salvage company had bloomed into pulp, their pages fused into a single soft brick of gray paper. The pay chest's lock had rusted through; inside, the coin had gone to green lace, delicate as coral, worthless as ash. Vesper lifted the bricks of fused paper out one by one and set them aside, and her heart was doing something strange — not pounding, but slowing, the way it slowed in the last minutes of a dive, when you knew what you would find and were only waiting for the sea to admit it.

At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work, lay a logbook.

She knew it before she touched it. She would have known it blind. The leather cover, the brass corners her father had soldered on himself because he distrusted glue, the pencil stub still tied to the spine on a length of tarred twine — she had watched him write in a hundred books like this one, at a hundred chart tables, his handwriting slanting forward as though the words themselves were impatient to get where they were going. This was the last one. This was the one that had never come home.

Her gloves were too thick for the pages. She stripped them, tucking them into her belt, and turned the book in the light, and for a long moment she only held it, letting the water move it gently in her hands like something sleeping.

*Open it,* she told herself. *You didn't dive sixty feet into your father's grave to stand in the doorway.*

The first pages were routine. Departure from Harrow Point, the manifest, the weather — and Vesper read her father's voice in every line, heard it so clearly that she looked up once, half expecting him to be kneeling beside her in the drowned chartroom, tapping the page. *Wind fresh from the northeast. Crew in good spirits. R. Pemberton, Master.* The salvage company's stamp on the contract page. The coordinates of the wreck they'd been sent to strip — a merchantman lost on the reef graveyard, nothing remarkable, a job like a hundred jobs.

Then the entries changed.

The handwriting stayed the same. That was the worst of it. The same forward-slanting letters, the same impatient loops, recording a thing that Vesper had spent ten years building her whole life on top of, the way you build a house on a grave you refuse to look at.

*Third day out. Vane's tender made signal at dusk. He wants the wreck's position kept between us until the payout is assessed. Says the company men would "complicate" the split. I don't like it. A man who wants to keep secrets from his own partners will keep them from you too.*

Vesper's breath caught in her throat and she had to consciously slow it, watching her gauge. *Vane.* Cassian Vane had been on the *Marlowe's* last voyage. She had known that the way she knew the date of her father's death — a fact worn smooth by handling, stripped of meaning. Vane had been the salvage broker's man, the charming one who'd come to their table in the Harrow Point tavern and bought the whole crew a round and told stories that made her father laugh until he coughed. She had been eleven. She had liked him.

*Fifth day. Found the wreck. It is not what the survey said. She's a company ship gone down heavy, and her hold is full of specie — more than the whole contract is worth. Vane came down with me himself. I watched his face when the light hit the coin. I have seen that face before, on men at cards, and I did not like it then either.*

The pages turned. The lagoon around her was utterly still, the *Marlowe* upright on the sand below like a church, and Vesper read on, kneeling in her father's chartroom, while the truth she had fled for a decade assembled itself line by patient line.

*Seventh day. Vane has been at the crew. Offering them private shares to help him run the cargo out quiet, before the company's assessor arrives. Two have taken his coin already. I told him to his face it was theft and he smiled at me and said, "Tom, the sea doesn't care who owns what. Neither should you." He is going to take the money and he is going to need the *Marlowe* gone to do it, and there are nineteen souls aboard who are in his way.*

*Eighth day. Storm coming. Barometer falling like a stone. Vane has ordered the tender away with the first of the coin — and with our spare pumps, our signal rockets, and half our fresh water, "for safekeeping." I have written to the company. I do not know if the letter will outrun the weather. If it does not, and if anything happens to this crew, let this book say plainly: Cassian Vane stripped this ship of her means to survive and took the money, and he did it with a smile, and I name him here so that no one may call it misfortune.*

Vesper's vision blurred. She blinked and the water took the tears instantly, as the sea takes everything, without comment. *Nineteen souls.* She had spent ten years carrying a different number. The number the company's inquiry had used, the number the harbor gossips had used, the number that had made her father's name a cautionary tale told to young salvagers: *the *Marlowe*, lost with all hands but one, her master's greed the cause.* All hands but one. She was the one. And the number had been a lie, and the cause had been a lie, and she had run from a lie for ten years as though it were her own face in a mirror.

* * *

The storm entries came next, and Vesper read them the way you read a letter from the dead — slowly, twice, with your whole body.

*Ninth day. The storm is on us. Vane's tender is gone over the horizon with the pumps. We have shipped water through the lazarette seam. The men are working the chain pumps in watches and I have never been prouder of any crew in my life. They are afraid and they are working. That is all any captain can ask and more than most get.*

*Tenth day. The pumps are losing. I have made a decision and I am at peace with it, and let whoever finds this book understand it fully, because I know what will be said of me otherwise.*

*The specie is in the hold. It is worth more than all our lives together, and that is exactly why it must go. The *Marlowe* sits low and the sea is making up fast. If I lighten her of the cargo we might yet limp for the coast, but the coin is what is tearing her open — every ton of it drives the seams wider, and every hour we spend fighting for it is an hour we do not spend fighting for ourselves. I have ordered the hold breached and the cargo scuttled. The company will call it the loss of a fortune. It is not. It is the purchase of nineteen lives, and it is the best bargain I will ever strike.*

*Vane's men protest. Let them protest. I would rather answer to a court than to a widow.*

Vesper made a sound into her regulator that had no name. Her father — the man the inquiry had painted as a greedy master who'd driven his ship too hard for a payout, the man whose name she had abandoned along with everything else, the man she had become a drifter and a ranger and a runner to stop being — had opened his own ship's hull and let a fortune pour into the sea to keep his people alive. *Including me.* She had been eleven years old, ship's girl in a storm, and she had never known — no one had ever told her — that the water rising around her ankles in the night had been let in on purpose, by her father's own order, to buy her out of the sea's arithmetic.

*Eleventh day. We are going down. The pumps are done and the storm has the mast and I have put the boats over. Vesper is in the second boat with Orla's people — the child is calm, God help me, calmer than the grown men. I am staying with the ship until the last boat is clear. A captain does not count himself among the saved until his crew is counted first. If the sea takes me, let it be recorded that I do not go as a man who lost a fortune. I go as a man who spent one.*

*The last I will write. Vane, if you live to read this — and men like you always live — know that the sea keeps books as well as captains. Mine is balanced. Yours is not.*

*For my daughter, if she ever reads this: Vesper. Little star. I did not leave you. I stayed so that you could leave. That was the whole of my life's work and I do not regret one hour of it. Be brave, but not the way I was brave. Be brave enough to stop.*

The entry ended there. The remaining pages were blank, and the blankness went on and on under Vesper's hands, page after page of nothing, the way the sea itself was nothing, the way the last ten years had been nothing — a long blank book she had filled with motion because she could not bear to fill it with this.

*Be brave enough to stop.*

She knelt in the drowned chartroom of the *Marlowe* and the truth came up through her like a diver coming up too fast, too much too sudden, and she let it, because she was done with shallow breathing, done with shallow everything. Her father had not died of greed. He had died of the opposite of greed, in a world that had no ledger generous enough to record it, and Vane had lived, and prospered, and built a fleet, and had spent the last ten years — she understood it now, all of it, the half-chart and the six tides and the charm like a knife wrapped in silk — trying to make certain this book never surfaced.

*He didn't send us here to beat his fleet. He sent us here because if we find it, he can take it from us and burn it, and the finding will be ours to answer for.*

The realization settled over her not with rage but with a terrible, clarifying calm. She tucked the logbook inside her suit, against her chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat, and she took one last look around the chartroom — the table where she had drummed her heels, the safe standing open, the barnacle lace, the green-rotted coin that her father had already given away once, to the sea, for love — and she spoke aloud, her voice a small silver blur in the water, the first words she had said to her father in ten years that were not an accusation.

"I read it, Papa. I understand. I'm bringing it home."

She kicked for the light above.

* * *

Orla hauled her over the gunwale with one arm, which should have been impossible and was not, because Orla had spent a lifetime doing impossible things quietly. Sefa had the hot kettle going and Bram was standing at the rail — Bram who had stowed away, Bram who had fouled the rudder and then bled for the repair, Bram who had lost his brother to this same water — and all three of them were looking at her, and Vesper realized she was crying openly, there on the deck in front of her crew, and found that she did not care.

"It's not what they said." Her voice came out wrecked. She sat down hard on the deck, the logbook clutched to her chest, and told them all of it — the safe, the entries, the scuttled coin, the nineteen souls, the naming of Vane. "He gave up the fortune. He gave it up for us. For *me*. And I spent ten years—" Her voice broke and she let it. "I spent ten years running from the best man I ever knew."

Sefa crouched and put her broad hand flat on the deck, the old engineer's gesture, feeling the ship's pulse through the planking. "Then we know what we're carrying," she said simply. "Not salvage. Truth. And truth's heavier than gold, so we'd best lash it down."

Bram had come to stand over her. His face was the face of a man looking at a grave he'd finally been allowed to visit. "Vane will come for that book," he said. "Not for the wreck. For the book."

"He's welcome to try," Vesper said, and the words came out of her with a steadiness she did not recognize as her own — it was her father's steadiness, inherited at last, ten years late and right on time. "He can have it when he pries it from—"

She never finished. Orla, at the bow, said quietly: "Smoke."

They all turned. Beyond the lagoon's headland, where the gray monsoon sky met the darker gray of the sea, a smudge of funnel smoke was standing up out of the horizon — one plume, then three, then five, the vanguard of Cassian Vane's fleet, come at last across the water to bury the past.

And behind the fleet, filling the whole southern sky like a second horizon, the storm had begun to build.

Vesper rose, the logbook against her heart, and looked at her crew — her father's crew, her crew, the family the sea had given back to her — and said, "All hands. It's time to finish what he started."
