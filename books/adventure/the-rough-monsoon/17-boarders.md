## 17. Boarders

Bram had spent thirty years reading water, and the lagoon was telling him a lie.

It lay too calm. The storm that had chased them for two tides had gone strangely quiet, as if it were drawing breath — the monsoon coiling itself above the headland like something patient. The Marlowe sat below them in her green cathedral of light, upright and whole, and the slack tide turned the surface to hammered pewter. Slack water. The one moment when the channel's current died and anything with oars could come through without being chewed apart.

He stood at the Kestrel's stern rail with his brother's knife at his belt — the one he'd carried since he was nineteen, the one he'd sworn never to use on a man — and watched the gap in the reef where the light kept flickering wrong.

"Boats," Orla said beside him. She said it flatly, the way you'd say *rain*. Her broken arm was splinted and bound across her chest, and she'd already wedged herself into the rigging with her good hand, because Orla did not wait to be told twice.

"How many?"

"Three. Maybe four. Running dark under the lee of the point." She squinted. "They cut their sails. They're pulling."

Vesper came up from below deck two steps at a time, the logbook wrapped in oilcloth and slung across her back like a child carried into a fire. Her hair was still wet from the dive. She looked at the channel, then at the sky, then at Bram.

"Slack water," she said. "Of course. He waited for slack water."

"He's had ten years to learn your father's routes," Bram said. "Same as we have."

"No." Vesper's jaw tightened. "He didn't learn them. He stole them. There's a difference, and in about four minutes he's going to find out what it is."

* * *

There was no time for a council. Sefa was already below, slamming the engine compartment shut and rigging the steam line to vent hot off the boiler — not enough to make way, the Kestrel's screw was fouled with cut line and they'd lost half a tide fixing the rudder, but enough to fill the aft deck with scalding fog if anyone forced the hatch. Orla stayed aloft with a boat hook and a coil of chain. Vesper took the bow, because Vesper always took the point where the fighting would be worst; Bram had learned that about her in three days aboard, and understood it in a way he suspected she didn't understand herself. You put yourself where the blow lands first when part of you is still trying to finish an old one.

That left the midships rail to Bram, and the gaff spar, and the boathook, and the truth he hadn't said aloud: that he had followed this crew out onto the sea against every promise he'd made himself, because the Marlowe took Tomas, and he needed to see the ship that ate his brother with his own eyes. He'd gotten his look. Down there in the green light, her rails still standing, her wheel still mounted, her name still legible in flaking gold — *Marlowe*, proud as a Sunday — he had seen her and felt nothing he'd expected. Not rage. Not closure, whatever the shore-side priests called it. Just a terrible tenderness, like looking at the face of someone who hurt you long ago and has grown old.

Now her killers' hired men were coming up the channel to burn whatever was left.

The first boat came out of the shadow of the headland all at once, eight oars, no lights, men crouched low. The second trailed it by fifty yards. The third hung back near the reef lip, and Bram marked it, because the third boat was the one carrying the officer — a big man in a yellow slicker who stood upright in the bow like he owned the rain that hadn't started yet.

"They'll board amidships and aft," Bram called down. "They want the ship, not us dead. Dead men can't be questioned."

"Then let's disappoint them," Vesper said.

The rain arrived the way a wall arrives. One moment the lagoon was pewter; the next it was white noise, a roaring gray curtain that erased the headland, the fleet beyond it, everything but the Kestrel and the boats sliding toward her through the smother. Bram heard the hull knock before he saw the grapnel — a soft wooden thud along the port side, then the bite of iron, then hands.

They came over the rail fast and professional, six of them in the first wave, knives and short hatchets, faces wrapped against the rain. Bram swung the gaff spar off its crutch and caught the second man across the throat as he cleared the bulwark, a full-armed sweep that put him backward into the sea with a splash the rain swallowed whole. The third landed well, rolled, came up inside Bram's reach with a hatchet.

Thirty years of harbor work is mostly waiting, and waiting teaches you things. It teaches you that a wet deck is a weapon if you give it to a man who trusts his feet. Bram stepped back, let the man commit his weight forward onto the streaming planks, and hit him once, hard, with the butt of the spar behind the ear. The hatchet skated away. The man went down and stayed down, breathing, which was more than Bram had promised himself he'd leave them.

Aft, the world went white and screaming. Someone had forced the engine hatch, and Sefa's boiling fog rolled out across the deck like a living thing, and through it came shrieks and the smell of scorched cloth. A figure burst from the cloud with his sleeves smoking and vaulted the starboard rail rather than stay another second. Orla dropped the chain coil from above. It caught the man across the shoulders mid-leap and took him into the water like a stone taking a coin.

"PORT SIDE!" Orla screamed down through the rain. "GRAPNELS! TWO MORE BOATS!"

* * *

The second wave was not professional. The second wave was angry.

Bram understood why when he saw the fire pot arc over the rail and shatter against the mainmast step — pitch oil, cheap and vicious, flame licking up the wet wood and dying, licking again and catching. They weren't here to take the Kestrel quietly anymore. Somewhere in the fog and the rain, the officer in the yellow slicker had made a decision, and the decision was that no log and no witnesses were worth the risk of either surviving.

"VESPER!" Bram's voice tore something in his throat. "THEY'RE BURNING US!"

She came aft through the smoke with the log clutched to her chest and her knife out, and Bram saw her take stock in one glance — mast step burning, Sefa coughing in the hatchway, Orla swinging down from the rigging one-handed like fury wearing a sling, three more boarders coming over the port side with axes going for the shrouds.

"Cut us loose or cut them down?" Orla shouted.

"Both!" Vesper said. "Bram — the lines. Take the lines!"

The grapnels. Of course. Three iron teeth buried in the Kestrel's port rail, each with a boat hauling on the other end, and the Kestrel was a patched ketch with a fouled screw and no way to run. If they pulled together, they'd tear her open or drag her onto the reef, and either way the Marlowe's secret died with her.

Bram went along the rail on his knees with his brother's knife, and for the first time in thirty years the blade found work worthy of it. First line: two strokes and the rope parted, and somewhere out in the white roar a boat load of men lurched and cursed. Second line: a boarder reached him before he finished, grabbed his collar, hauled him up face to face — young, terrified, eyes wild above the wet cloth over his mouth.

"You don't know what he does to—"

"I know exactly what he does," Bram said, and drove his forehead into the bridge of the man's nose, and cut the line.

The third grapnel was set deep, hooked under the rail timber itself, and the boat on its end was full of men pulling in rhythm. He could feel the Kestrel groan, feel the whole hull leaning, the deck tilting under the strain. Below him Sefa had got the boiler venting wide, and steam screamed from the hatch like a kettle the size of a church, and through the steam and the rain and the fire on the mast step the world had reduced itself to this: an old man on his knees with a dead man's knife, cutting his friends loose from drowning.

The rope parted. The Kestrel righted so hard Bram slid the length of the deck. And the yellow slicker's voice came across the water, amplified by a speaking trumpet, almost gentle beneath the monsoon's roar:

"Pemberton! Give me the book and I'll pull my men out and let the sea decide the rest! You have until the tide turns! You know what happens after slack water — even if I go, the reef will do my work for me!"

* * *

Bram got to his feet at the rail, chest heaving, and looked at Vesper through the rain.

She stood over the burning mast step with the wet tarpaulin in her hands, beating the flames down stroke by stroke, methodical as a woman doing laundry. The last boarders were retreating over the sides or swimming for the boats. The officer's launch hung off the reef lip, waiting. Beyond the headland, faint through the rain, the masts of Vane's fleet showed like a row of needles, and behind everything the storm's true body was coming — a blackness with lightning moving inside it, slow and total, the way grief moves when it finally stops being outrun.

"He's lying," Bram said. "About letting the sea decide."

"Of course he's lying." Vesper threw down the tarp and stood over the charred, smoking wood, breathing hard. "But he's also right about the tide. Look."

Bram looked. The lagoon's surface, which had been mirror-still at slack, had begun to move — a slow seaward creep, the channel waking, the current that had guarded this place for ten years stretching and opening its jaws. In an hour it would be a river. In two, a race that no boat could stem and no diver could survive. Whatever was decided in this lagoon would be decided now.

Orla dropped to the deck beside them, gray-faced, cradling the splinted arm. Sefa emerged from the steam with singed eyebrows and a wrench still in her fist, looking like a smith who'd walked out of her own forge fire. Four of them, soaked, burned, bleeding in small ways, holding a ketch with a fouled screw and a dead man's logbook, while a salvage fleet rounded a headland and the monsoon came down like the end of the world.

"Tide's turning," Sefa said. "We can't hold the lagoon and we can't run."

"We're not running," Vesper said.

Bram heard it in her voice then — the thing he'd been circling for three days, the reason she'd signed Vane's poisoned contract, the reason she'd swum down into the green light alone. It wasn't treasure in her voice. It wasn't even vengeance. It was the sound of a woman who had finally stopped moving away from something and was turning around.

"He wants the log," Vesper said, "because it names him. My father wrote it in ink ten years old, in a safe, in a ship Vane has spent a decade pretending doesn't exist." She put her hand flat on the oilcloth bundle at her back. "So we don't run, and we don't hide it. We carry it out to meet him. All of us. While the tide still lets us choose the ground."

Orla started laughing — a wild, cracked sound, half pain. "You want to sail *toward* the fleet."

"I want to sail toward *him*," Vesper said. "Before the storm takes the choice away from both of us."

Bram looked down at the knife in his hand — Tomas's knife, dry at last, having done at last the only honest work it had ever been given. His brother had drowned believing the Marlowe's loss was the sea's fault, or fate's, or God's. The log in Vesper's arms said otherwise. The log said a man had chosen, and chosen badly, and had spent ten years buying fleets to bury the receipt.

"Well," Bram said, and found his voice steady, which surprised him. "I've come this far to see the ship. Might as well see the man who sank her."

Ahead, through the thinning curtain of the leading edge of the storm, the flagship's lanterns were showing at the mouth of the channel — three lights in a row, coming on, riding the first of the turning tide straight toward them.

And behind those lights, filling the whole southern sky, the monsoon opened its eyes.
