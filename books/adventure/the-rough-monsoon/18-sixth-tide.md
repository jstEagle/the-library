## 18. Sixth Tide

The monsoon did not arrive so much as descend — a wall of gray-green water and wind that swallowed the horizon whole, and inside that wall the *Kestrel* ran with her decks awash and her rigging screaming like something alive.

Vesper stood at the wheel because no one else could hold it. Her hands were raw from the spokes, her shoulders burned from hours of fighting the sea's insistence that they turn broadside and die. Ahead, through the ragged curtains of rain, the lagoon opened like a wound in the reef — black water ringed by white foam, and in its center, faint as a ghost beneath three fathoms of storm-stirred sea, the long dark spine of the *Marlowe*.

Behind them, rounding the headland under bare poles and driven engines, came Vane's fleet.

"Three hulls!" Orla shouted from the bow, one arm bound tight against her chest, the other gripping the forestay. "The flagship's leading! He's not even waiting for the tide to settle!"

"He never waits," Vesper said, and found that her voice was steady, which surprised her, because everything inside her was not.

The logbook was lashed against her chest beneath her oilskin, wrapped in waxed canvas, tied with the same cord her father had used on his charts. She had carried it up from the wreck an hour ago — an hour that felt like another life — and since then she had felt its weight against her ribs with every wave, every heave of the deck, every breath. Not heavy. Her father had written in a small, precise hand; the book weighed less than a gull's egg. It was what it *said* that pressed against her like ballast.

*Scuttled the payout. Cut the lines myself. Twelve souls above water is worth more than twelve thousand below.*

And beneath that, dated the night before the end: *C. Vane has sold our position. If any hand finds this, know the fault was not the sea's.*

She had read those lines four times in the cabin of the drowned ship, by the light of Sefa's lamp, while the water around her father's desk swayed gently like a held breath. Four times, and each time the words rearranged themselves into something truer and more terrible. For ten years she had believed the Marlowe's loss was the sea's doing, or fate's, or — in her darkest hours, in the salt flats where she'd gone to stop thinking — her own. That if she had been faster, stronger, less afraid, her father would have surfaced beside her.

He had cut the salvage lines himself. He had sent the divers up empty-handed and flooded the pay-chest into the deep, because the storm was coming and the crew was scattered across the reef, and he would not spend lives buying gold. And Cassian Vane — young then, hungry, trusted — had already sold their position to a rival fleet and slipped away over the horizon, leaving them to drown in the dark.

Not the sea's fault. Not hers. His.

"Fleet's entering the channel!" Bram called from the stern rail. He had refused this voyage, had stayed ashore in his grief and his stubbornness, and then followed anyway, and now he stood soaked to the skin with a boathook in his hands like a man who had finally run out of reasons to stand anywhere else. "They'll be in the lagoon in twenty minutes!"

"The reef will have something to say about that," Sefa muttered at the engine. The old woman had both hands buried in the guts of the motor, coaxing it through spray and salt. "Channel's got teeth at this tide. I told him that ten years ago too."

Vesper looked at the lagoon, at the dark shape of the wreck, at the fleet driving toward it through the rain — and something settled in her chest, quiet and cold and absolutely clear.

For ten years she had run. Across salt flats and border deserts, through contracts and canyons, always moving, always chasing something just ahead of her so she would never have to turn and face what was behind. She had thought the running was cowardice. Standing at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with her father's log against her heart, she understood at last that it had been worse than cowardice. It had been a debt unpaid. Every mile she fled had left the truth sitting at the bottom of that lagoon, unclaimed, while the man who caused it grew rich and celebrated and slept soundly in silk sheets bought with her father's death.

No more.

"Sefa," she said. "Bring us about. We're going out to meet him."

Orla turned, rain streaming off her chin. "Meet him? Ves, there are three ships and one of us."

"There's one of me," Vesper said, "and that's all this needs."

* * *

They met the flagship at the mouth of the lagoon, where the reef's teeth lay just beneath the surface and the sea broke itself into white confusion on either side of a single winding lane of deep water. Vesper knew the lane. Her father had drawn it on the secret chart — the liar's channel, the route only a Marlowe man could thread. Vane's pilots had modern surveys. Modern surveys showed open water here, a safe passage there, and death in between dressed up as an invitation.

The flagship came on regardless, because Cassian Vane had never once in his life believed that anything could be denied to him.

Vesper brought the *Kestrel* across his bow, close enough that the two hulls groaned against each other, close enough to see the faces of his crew lined along the rail — hard men with boarding axes and muskets wrapped in oilcloth, and behind them, at the quarterdeck rail beneath a canopy that did nothing against the rain, Cassian Vane himself.

Ten years had silvered him at the temples and thickened him at the waist, but the smile was unchanged. She had seen it across auction houses and harbor taverns, that easy, charming curve of the mouth that made men want to trust him and made her want to put her fist through it.

"Pemberton!" he called over the wind, spreading his arms as if greeting her at a garden party. "You've made better time than I expected! And you've found it — I can see it in your face. Tell me, was it everything you dreamed?"

Vesper stood at the rail with the rain hammering her shoulders and said nothing for a long moment. Let him look at her. Let him see what ten years had made.

"You knew," she said. Her voice carried strangely well across the water, the way voices do in storms, as if the wind itself wanted to listen. "You sold them. You sold my father."

Something flickered behind the charm — quick, involuntary, quickly mastered. "My dear girl. Grief makes people invent villains. Your father's expedition was lost to weather—"

"I have his log." She pulled it free of her oilskin and held it up, small and dark and dripping, and watched the flicker come again and stay. "His own hand. Dated the night before the end. *C. Vane has sold our position.* Do you want to hear what else it says, Cassian? It says he scuttled the payout himself. There is no treasure down there. There never was, after that night. You've chased a fortune across ten years and six tides, and it's a chest full of seawater."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. In it she heard the wind, the sea breaking on the reef, the creak of two hulls grinding together — and beneath it all, from the flagship's deck, a low murmur rising among Vane's crew like water finding a crack.

Then Vane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm and rolling, and it fooled everyone except the people who had learned to read him. "A logbook. One old book, and you sail into my guns to wave it at me. Give it here, Vesper, and I'll make you rich anyway — richer than your father ever—"

"He chose the crew," Vesper said, and her voice cracked like a whip, and the murmur on the flagship's deck went still. "That's what your greed could never understand. The fortune was in his hands — the lines were cut, the chest was ready to lift — and there were twelve people drowning on that reef, so he let the money go and pulled them out instead. He chose *lives*, Cassian. You chose a buyer's purse. And when the storm took them anyway, you built a life on top of their bones and called it success."

"Careful," Vane said softly. The charm had drained away now, and what remained was the thing underneath, the thing she had glimpsed once, years ago, across a card table in Port Meridian: a small, cold, frightened animal wearing a rich man's clothes. "You're in my waters, on my prize, with my property in your hand. My men will board you in five minutes, and what happens to that log then is up to how sensible you decide to be."

"Your men should ask you a question first," Vesper said, loud enough for the whole deck to hear. "Ask him why the fleet needed six tides of secrecy. Ask him why the charts were sealed. Ask him why, if there's treasure in that lagoon, he's spent ten years telling everyone the Marlowe was a cursed loss not worth salvaging." She saw heads turning along the rail, saw doubt move through the ranks of hired men like wind through wheat. "There's no treasure. There's only proof. Proof that he murdered eleven people and stole their deaths to build his name. That book doesn't make me rich, gentlemen. It hangs him."

"Enough!" Vane's voice split the air, and his hand went up, and somewhere aft a whistle shrilled — the boarding signal. Grapnels arced out of the rain.

But Vesper was already moving. "Sefa! Hard about — the south lane!"

The *Kestrel* leapt away from the flagship's side like a horse feeling the spur, and the last thing Vesper saw clearly was Vane's face as the gap widened — not furious, not yet. *Calculating.* He looked past her, past the little ketch, to the dark water of the lagoon and the shadow of the wreck beneath it, and she understood with terrible clarity that he did not believe her. Could not believe her. A man who had sold eleven lives for a fortune could not afford to believe the fortune was gone; the belief was the only thing holding the architecture of his life upright.

He would go down to the wreck himself. He would see it with his own eyes.

Which meant he would take his flagship through the channel.

"Let them follow," she said to Sefa, hauling the wheel over as the rain closed around them like a curtain. "Every ship. Right down the lane."

"Ves—" Orla began.

"No." Vesper's hands were steady on the spokes. Her heart was pounding, but it was not fear anymore; she had left fear somewhere back on the salt flats, nine days and ten years ago. What filled her now was something her father would have recognized, something he had carried into the storm that took him: the clarity of a person who has stopped running and finally knows exactly which way to stand. "He wants the truth. The reef keeps it. Let him come and collect."

* * *

The monsoon broke fully as the fleets converged on the lagoon, and the world became water — falling, rising, roaring. Through it the *Kestrel* threaded the liar's channel like a needle pulled by a sure hand, Vesper calling the turns from memory and from blood: *hard to port at the broken coral, two lengths, then starboard where the water goes green.* Behind her, Vane's flagship committed to the same passage with far too much speed and far too much faith in a survey drawn by men who had never listened to the sea.

Vesper heard it before she saw it — a long, grinding shriek beneath the wind, deeper than any storm sound, the voice of living rock taking hold of a ship's keel.

The flagship struck the outer teeth of the reef amidships, at full drive, and the sound of her opening was like the world tearing paper. Masts went in seconds — the main first, folding slowly, almost politely, then crashing down across the deck in a tangle of canvas and rope and men. The sea poured in through the wound and the great ship leaned, groaned, and began to die exactly the way ships do: without dignity, without mercy, and very fast.

"Boats!" someone screamed across the water. "Cut the boats!"

Vesper brought the *Kestrel* around in the lee of the reef, close enough to watch, close enough to act. "Orla — lines and fenders. Bram, get the lamp lit. We pull survivors, nothing else. Nobody touches the strongbox, nobody boards for cargo. Survivors or nothing."

"And Vane?" Sefa asked quietly.

Vesper looked across the boiling water to the quarterdeck of the sinking ship, where a figure in fine ruined clothes clung to the rail, shouting orders no one obeyed, his fleet scattering in the storm like startled birds. Cassian Vane, who had sold her father for gold, was drowning in the wreckage of his own greed, and the sea offered Vesper the one thing she had sailed ten years to find: the choice.

Her father had faced this choice and chosen mercy at the cost of everything. That was the whole of the log. That was the whole of the man.

"Pull him out too," Vesper said.

Orla stared at her. "After everything—"

"That's what makes it ours and not his." She threw the wheel over and drove the *Kestrel* into the chaos. "We do it his way. All the way down."

They worked the wreck's edge for an hour that felt like a year — hauling half-drowned sailors over the rail, cutting men loose from the tangle of the fallen mainmast, Bram leaning out into the maelstrom with the boathook and pulling a boy no older than sixteen from the water by his collar. And near the end, when the flagship's stern was already under and the sea was closing over her name, they pulled Cassian Vane aboard the *Kestrel*, coughing seawater, his fine coat torn, his rings gone, his fleet gone, his fortune resting on the reef in pieces.

He knelt on the wet deck, retching, and looked up at her with the animal terror naked in his face at last, and Vesper stood over him with the rain streaming down and the logbook held against her heart, and said nothing at all. There was nothing left to say. The sea had said it. The reef had said it. Her father, dead ten years, had said it in a small precise hand, and it had proven truer than everything Vane had ever built.

The flagship slid beneath the lagoon's dark water with a long sigh, joining the *Marlowe* in the keeping of the deep.

And when it was over — when the last survivor was wrapped in canvas and the storm was blowing itself out toward dawn — Vesper sat down on the deck beside her crew, all four of them, soaked and bleeding and alive, and felt the fury that had carried her for a decade finally, quietly, let go of her bones.

Sefa put a blanket around her shoulders. "What now?" the old engineer asked. "The log. The location. The whole world will pay for what's in that book."

Vesper looked at the gray light beginning in the east, at the calm water of the lagoon where two ships lay together in the dark, and understood that the hardest choice was still ahead of them — harder than the reef, harder than Vane, harder than the storm.

"Now," she said, "we decide what the sea gets to keep."
