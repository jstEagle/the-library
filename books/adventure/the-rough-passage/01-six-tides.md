## 1. Six Tides

The silt in Bergen harbor held everything the city wanted to forget, which was why Lena Eriksen—currently invoiced as L. Marstrand, marine surveyor—had taken the contract. Four weeks of mapping a collapsed nineteenth-century wharf foundation, dredging mud by the ton, cataloguing nothing older than a boot heel and a broken gin bottle. It was honest work in the way that digging through someone's garbage was honest work: necessary, unglamorous, and unlikely to attract attention.

She'd been L. Marstrand for eleven months now. Before that she'd been K. Vollan in Trondheim, and before that S. Aune in Ålesund, and before that a name she didn't like to say out loud even to herself. Each alias had a paper trail thin enough to tear, a rented room, a bank account at a different regional branch, a story about where she'd trained—always vague, always plausible. *Freelance. Contract work. You know how it is.* Nobody in Norwegian coastal archaeology asked hard questions of a woman who charged fair rates and delivered clean reports.

Nobody had, for ten years.

The morning the papers came, she was forty meters down in the gray-green cold of the harbor basin, gloved hands reading the grain of old pilings by touch because visibility was measured in centimeters. Down there the world was simple. Pressure, breath, task. Her dive computer ticked against her wrist. The silt breathed around her when she moved, and she moved slowly, the way her father had taught her—*the seabed is a sleeping animal; wake it gently or it swallows you*—though she tried not to think about who taught her what, these days. That was the whole architecture of her life: a series of small, disciplined avoidances.

She surfaced into rain. Bergen did rain the way other cities did weather—constantly, comprehensively, without apology. The tender bobbed beside the survey barge, and the deckhand hauled her ladder line while she spat her regulator and looked up at the gray sky and the gray water and the crane arm swinging its load of dredged muck onto the sorting table.

"Message for Marstrand," the deckhand said, holding up an envelope in a plastic sleeve. "Came by courier. Guy said signature required."

"I'm mid-shift."

"He was pretty insistent."

Everything was pretty insistent lately. The harbor office had been asking about her liability insurance. The tax office had sent two letters to her mailbox at the marina, both forwarded from addresses that no longer existed. Debt didn't chase you all at once; it arrived in increments, each one polite, each one a little closer than the last.

She peeled off her gloves and took the envelope. Heavy stock. Real paper, not printout. A law firm's name embossed in the corner—*Vinter & Rask, Bergen*—and beneath it, in smaller type, a second name that made her stomach drop through the bottom of the boat.

*On behalf of C. Ashe Maritime Recovery Ltd.*

She stood there with water running off her drysuit into the scuppers and read the return address three times. Corinne Ashe. She knew the name the way everyone in salvage knew the name—the way you know a storm system forming over open water. Ashe ran recovery operations out of Stavanger and Aberdeen, fast ships, faster lawyers, a reputation for finding things other people had given up on and owning them absolutely. Lena had spent a decade making sure their paths never crossed. Ashe bought wrecks. Lena dug in mud. Different economies entirely.

The envelope was thick. She didn't open it on the deck. She waited until she was in the site office—a converted shipping container with a kerosene heater and a kettle—and she waited until the kettle boiled, and she poured tea she wouldn't drink, and then she opened it with a knife from the tool drawer, slitting the seam cleanly the way you opened anything when you might need to reseal it later.

Inside: fourteen pages. A notice of assignment of debt. Her debt—all of it, every loan, every credit line, every favor extended by every institution she'd ever signed her false name to, consolidated and transferred, effective immediately, to C. Ashe Maritime Recovery Ltd. Page after page of account numbers she recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. They'd found all of them. The Ålesund account. The Trondheim overdraft. The personal loan from a lender in Kristiansund she'd taken out as S. Aune with a fabricated employer reference. Even the six thousand kroner she owed a dive shop in Florø, which wasn't a bank at all, just a man named Bratteli who'd let her run a tab on gear because he liked her reports.

Someone hadn't just pulled her credit file. Someone had assembled her.

The last page was a letter. One paragraph, typed, unsigned except for a stamped initials block: **CA**.

*Ms. Marstrand—or should I say Ms. Eriksen? Your obligations have been acquired in full. I am prepared to discuss terms of settlement. There is one offer on the table and one only. Contact my office within seventy-two hours.*

Seventy-two hours. Three tides, roughly, if you counted the way harbor people counted—not by days but by the water coming in and going out, the clock that governed everything worth doing on this coast. Lena set the letter down next to the kettle and stared at it until the steam fogged the window and the container went dim with afternoon.

She should have felt panic. What she felt instead was something colder and more familiar: the particular calm of a person who has practiced being found. Ten years of aliases meant ten years of rehearsing this moment in the shower, on night watches, in the long gray pauses between contract payments clearing. *They've come for you. Now what.* And the answer, every time she'd rehearsed it, had been the same: move. New town, new name, new paper trail. She had a go-bag packed in her rented room above the chandlery. Passport under a third identity, cash, a drive with her certifications. She could be gone by morning tide. She'd done it before. It was almost routine.

Except.

Except they'd found the Florø tab. Except they'd spelled Eriksen correctly, with the right number of s's, in a country where half the population spelled it wrong. Except the letter didn't threaten. It *invited*. Threats were for people you wanted to punish. Invitations were for people you wanted something from.

She turned back to the assignment notice and read the fine print properly this time, the way she read charts—with suspicion, looking for what wasn't drawn. Clause 9(b). *The assignee may, at its discretion, convert outstanding obligations into contractual consideration for services rendered aboard vessels operated by the assignee.* In plain language: your debt can become wages. Work it off aboard our ships.

Aboard *our* ships.

Lena put down the papers and pressed both palms flat on the desk until her fingers stopped shaking. Outside, the crane swung another load of harbor mud onto the sorting table, and one of the graders called out that he'd found a coin, maybe, could be nothing, and the site supervisor said log it and move on, and the ordinary machinery of an ordinary dig ground forward without her, and she stood in the container breathing kerosene fumes and understanding, with total clarity, exactly which ship.

There was only one wreck on this coast that Corinne Ashe would spend money buying a stranger's debt to reach.

The *Vargr* went down nineteen years ago, off the shelf edge west of Stadlandet, with eleven crew and a cargo hold full of recovered artifacts from the Skerries expedition—the find of the decade, they'd called it, medieval trade goods, runestones, a merchant's hoard that would have rewritten chapters of the history books. The inquiry blamed ballast failure in heavy seas. The inquiry also blamed Magnus Eriksen, owner and master, for sailing into a forecast gale to save time and salvage fees. Magnus Eriksen died with his ship. His partner, Anders Halvard, survived ashore and spent the rest of his short life being sued, blamed, and ruined, because the contracts said the cargo was his responsibility and the cargo was gone. Halvard died four years later. His daughter would have been a teenager then.

And Lena Eriksen had changed her name and gone into the mud and stayed there for ten years, because the alternative was being the daughter of the man who killed eleven people and broke another family on his way down.

That was the story. That was the official story, the newspaper story, the story she'd built her whole exile on top of, the way you build a hut on a fault line and tell yourself the ground is stable.

She'd never believed it. Not entirely. She'd been twenty-three when the *Vargr* sank, old enough to have worked two summers aboard as a trainee diver, young enough to worship her father completely. And she remembered things the inquiry never asked about: the way Magnus had the strongroom hatch re-welded the week before departure, personally, refusing the yard's help. The way he'd moved the cargo manifests to his own cabin and locked them. The way he'd told her, the night before the final sailing, standing on the quay in the dark with the harbor lights doubled in the black water: *"If anything happens, Lena—if I don't come back—you never worked on this ship. You were never here. Promise me."*

She'd promised. She'd kept the promise so thoroughly she'd buried herself along with him.

Now a letter in heavy-stock paper had arrived with her real name spelled correctly and a single offer attached, and the offer was a berth on a salvage run to the *Vargr*, and whatever else Corinne Ashe wanted—cargo, claim, closure—Lena understood immediately that what Ashe actually wanted was *her*. Not her labor. Her knowledge. The compass. The thing she'd carried for nineteen years in a waterproof case, first in a rucksack, then in a safe-deposit box, then in a rucksack again, the brass-cased expedition compass issued to Vargr crew, serial number 004, the one her mother had handed her after the funeral saying *he'd want you to have it*, the one Lena had never once let out of arm's reach for longer than a bank visit.

Because it was wrong. She'd known since she was twenty-five, since the night in a Trondheim hostel when she'd finally sat down with it and a proper magnet and stopped avoiding the question. The needle didn't behave. It swung freely enough, but it settled heavy, always a few degrees off true, always the same few degrees regardless of how she turned the housing—and when she'd unscrewed the bezel, careful as surgery, she'd found the compensation weight had been altered. Added to. Deliberately. A bias built into the instrument, tuned by hand, by someone who understood magnetic deviation well enough to make a compass lie consistently in one direction.

Her father had modified his own compass. And the direction it lied toward, plotted out on a chart with a ruler and a sleepless night's arithmetic, ran west-southwest from the last known position of the *Vargr*—away from the coordinates in the inquiry report, away from where the search had looked, out past the shelf edge into deeper water nobody had ever surveyed.

He'd left a pointer. He'd left it with her. Whether he'd meant to or whether she'd simply stolen the map without knowing it—that was a question she'd had nineteen years to ask and had never once dared to.

Until now. Until a stranger bought her debts down to the last six thousand kroner in Florø and put a single sentence in front of her: *there is one offer on the table and one only.*

Lena cleaned up the site office, logged out of the day's records, wrote a note for the supervisor saying family emergency, which was true in a way that made her laugh once, alone, without any humor in it. She walked the rain-slicked quay to her room above the chandlery and took down the go-bag and didn't pack it. Instead she took out the compass case, set it on the narrow bed, and looked at it for a long time.

Seventy-two hours. She had until Thursday's tide to answer.

She could run. New alias, new town, deeper hole. But they'd found Florø. They'd found everything, which meant the aliases were finished, which meant running now was just drowning slower. Or she could call the number, take the meeting, hear the offer in full—and walk into the one place on earth she'd spent a decade avoiding: a salvage deck pointed at her father's grave, surrounded by strangers, with her name already known.

Dread sat in her chest like ballast. Underneath it, quieter, unwelcome, something else stirred—the same thing that had stirred at twenty-five when the bezel came off and the lie in the needle revealed itself. Not hope exactly. Proximity. After ten years of digging other people's mud, the thing she'd buried was surfacing on its own, and it was coming for her with paperwork.

She picked up the phone.

"This is Marstrand," she said, when the office answered. "Regarding the letter."

"Of course." A pause, crisp, professional. "Ms. Ashe anticipated your call. She asks me to tell you the *Kestrel* sails from Måløy on Sunday's tide, and that your settlement terms will be discussed aboard. Travel arrangements are enclosed. Welcome back, Ms. Eriksen."

*Welcome back.* Two words, spoken by a voice that had never met her, and they landed harder than any threat could have. Lena hung up and stood in the narrow room with the rain on the window and the compass on the bed between them—her and her father's instrument, her and her father's debt, her and the sea that had taken everything and was now, apparently, offering it back at interest.

Three tides. Then Måløy. Then the *Kestrel*. Then the water over the *Vargr*, and whatever her father had welded shut from the inside.

She picked up the compass and put it in her coat pocket, where it rode against her hip like a second heartbeat, and began, calmly, professionally, dreadfully, to pack.

* * *

The courier had come at low tide. By the next high tide, the whole harbor seemed to know something was moving—word travels fast in a port, faster than any wire. The site supervisor accepted her resignation with a shrug and a handshake and the observation that Marstrand had always seemed like a woman halfway out the door. The chandlery's owner pressed a thermos of coffee on her and refused payment. Bratteli in Florø would get his six thousand kroner, she decided, first, before anyone else got anything. Some debts you paid in order of decency rather than size.

On the last evening, she walked out to the end of the north quay and stood in the wind looking west, toward the open sea and the shelf beyond it, though from Bergen you couldn't see either—you could only feel the direction of it, the pull, the way the compass in her pocket felt like it was leaning.

Somewhere out there lay the *Vargr*, and inside the *Vargr* lay the truth, and somewhere between here and there stood Corinne Ashe, waiting to collect.

Lena Eriksen had spent ten years learning how to disappear. She had seventy-two hours to remember how to arrive.
