## 2. The Debt Collector

The office of Corinne Ashe Salvage & Recovery occupied the top floor of a converted fish warehouse on the north side of the harbor, and it was designed, Lena decided within thirty seconds of entering, to make visitors feel like cargo being appraised.

Floor-to-ceiling windows overlooked the quay. The furniture was expensive and arranged at angles that gave every seat a view of the water but none a view of the door. A model ship sat under glass on the credenza—a three-masted barque, museum quality—and beside it, framed, hung what looked like an original chart of the Norwegian shelf, its margins annotated in faded ink. Ashe's taste ran to objects with provenance. Objects that had survived something.

Corinne Ashe stood at the window with her back to the room, hands clasped behind her, watching a container ship nose out past the breakwater. She was smaller than her reputation. Silver hair cut short and severe, a charcoal suit that probably cost more than Lena's last three contracts combined. She didn't turn when Lena came in.

"Miss Voss." She said the name like she was testing a coin between her teeth. "Or should we do this alphabetically? There are eleven of them, I believe."

Lena stopped two meters from the desk and kept her voice level. "Ms. Ashe. You bought my debt. That doesn't buy my time."

"No." Ashe turned then, and her face was composed into something almost kind, which was worse than cruelty. Kindness from a creditor is a tool. "It buys your options. Sit down, Miss Voss. Or Miss Eriksen. I find I don't care which."

Lena didn't sit.

"I'm not taking the job," she said. "Whatever it is. Find another diver."

"The job," Ashe said, moving to the desk without hurry, "is a salvage run. Six tides, one wreck, a share structure that would make you solvent by spring." She opened a leather folder but didn't look at it. It was a prop. Everything in this room was a prop. "You're the best deep-water diver working the North Sea basin under any name at all, and I say that having checked all eleven."

"There's nothing to check. I pay my taxes. My licenses are clean."

"Under *Ingrid Voss* they are." Ashe smiled faintly. "Bergen, 2019 through 2022. Before that you were *Maren Skau* out of Ålesund—two seasons on the pipeline surveys, excellent references, forged references, incidentally, though very well done. *Kari Lund* before that, Tromsø. *Anna Eriksen*—" she let the name sit in the air for a moment, watching Lena's face, "—which I thought showed either great confidence or great contempt, using your mother's first name as a surname. Åndalsnes, 2014. Shall I go on? There's a *Britt Holm* I've never quite traced. The paperwork has holes. Someone helped you build her, and I'd like to know who, someday, but not today."

The room was very quiet. Out on the harbor a gull screamed once and gave up.

Lena had rehearsed this conversation on the walk over. She had prepared refusals: firm, professional, non-negotiable. She had not prepared for the sensation of standing still while someone read her life back to her like a manifest, alias by alias, year by year, each name a hull she'd built and sailed and scuttled.

"You've had me investigated," she said finally. It came out steadier than she felt.

"I've had you *understood*. Investigation is what you do to strangers." Ashe closed the folder. "I knew your father. Did you know that?"

Something cold moved down Lena's spine. "No."

"Everyone says no about Magnus Eriksen. He collected secrets the way other men collected debt, and he died owing me one." Ashe walked to the window again, and for a moment her reflection looked older than she did. "I financed his last expedition. Not publicly—I never put my name on paper where the sea could read it—but the *Vargr* sailed with my money in her hold and my requirements in her manifest. Eleven crew. One partner, Halvard, who took the fall for whatever went wrong, and one captain who went down with the ship rather than answer questions." She turned. "So when I learned that Magnus Eriksen's daughter was diving wrecks under false names within four hundred kilometers of where the *Vargr* lies, I confess I found it... suggestive."

"It's coincidence. I dive where the work is."

"Of course you do." Ashe's tone made it clear she believed exactly the opposite. "Here is what happens now, Miss Voss—or may I use Eriksen? It saves time. Here is what happens now. Option one: you sign aboard my survey vessel, the *Kestrel*, as dive lead. Six tides on the grid I give you. You work, you get paid, your debt clears, and your eleven names stay buried deeper than any wreck I'll ever show you."

"And option two?"

"Option two is that I sell your aliases to the maritime licensing board. All of them. Forged references, false registration, contract fraud across three countries. The board revokes everything, the tax authorities take a very close look at eleven identities' worth of income, and you spend the next several years proving you exist—which, given how carefully you've spent a decade making sure no one can prove that, should be entertaining." Ashe folded her hands on the desk. "There is no option three. People always ask if there's an option three."

Lena looked at her for a long moment. The fear she'd expected—the animal panic, the urge to run, change the name again, burn it all down and start a twelfth life—didn't come. What came instead was something quieter and much more unpleasant: the slow, sinking understanding that the running had never worked.

She had thought invisibility was a thing you could own. Build it plank by plank, alias by alias, maintain it, defend it. Ten years of forged references and rented rooms and contracts signed in borrowed names, and she'd believed she was building a fortress.

She'd been renting. Month to month. And the landlord had just walked in.

"When does the *Kestrel* sail?" she heard herself ask.

Ashe's smile widened by a single degree. "Thursday's tide. Ruiz will have your papers." She slid a single sheet across the desk—crew agreement, standard terms, a signature line waiting. "You'll want to read it. Everyone does. It changes nothing."

Lena picked up the pen because her hand wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. She read the agreement twice, fast, the way she read decompression tables—looking for the trap, knowing the trap would be legal rather than physical. Clause nine gave Ashe's liaison full authority aboard. Clause twelve waived salvage claims on anything designated *priority recovery*. Priority recovery meant whatever Ashe said it meant.

She signed anyway. *Lena Voss.* Let the woman choke on the alias; it was the only weapon left in reach.

At the door, Ashe spoke again, and her voice had lost its warmth entirely, gone flat and precise as a depth reading.

"For what it's worth, I don't think you're looking for the *Vargr* out of guilt, Miss Eriksen. Guilt is cheap. I think you want to know what your father was, and you're afraid the answer is: exactly like you." She returned to the window. "Thursday. Don't be late. The tide won't wait, and neither, anymore, will I."

Lena walked down four flights of stairs and out onto the quay without remembering any of it. The wind off the harbor hit her face and she stood there among the crates and mooring lines, breathing salt air, feeling the shape of her own name in her mouth like a foreign object.

Ten years of names, and the woman hadn't even needed the list memorized. She'd brought a folder.

That night Lena packed her dive bag in the rented room above the chandlery, laying out each piece of gear with the ritual calm she used before deep dives: check, check again, trust nothing. At the bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth, went the compass—her father's compass, brass-cased, needleless, useless, carried through eleven lives because throwing it away had always felt like a decision she wasn't ready to make.

She held it a moment under the bare bulb. It didn't point anywhere. It never had.

Thursday, she thought. The tide won't wait.

Neither, it turned out, could she.

* * *

The *Kestrel* was moored at the commercial pier, gray and utilitarian, a survey vessel with a crane aft and a dive station that looked recently rebuilt. Lena came aboard at 0500 with her papers and her bag and a face like weather, and the first mate met her at the gangway—a lean man in his fifties with gray in his beard and eyes that did arithmetic on everyone they landed on.

"Papers," he said.

She handed them over. He read them longer than the documents required.

"Lena Voss," he said. "Dive lead."

"That's what it says."

"Ruiz," he said, and gave the papers back, and held her eye one beat too long before turning toward the bridge. "Welcome aboard the *Kestrel*. We sail on Thursday's tide."

It wasn't Thursday yet. But the tide, Lena understood, had already begun to turn.
