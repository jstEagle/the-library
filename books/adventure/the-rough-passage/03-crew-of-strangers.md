## 3. Crew of Strangers

The Kestrel was smaller than her paperwork suggested. That was the first thing Lena noticed when the tender bumped against her hull at dawn—survey vessels always photographed well from above, all clean deck lines and purposeful cranes, but up close they were rust-streaked workhorses with paint peeling in long gray ribbons and a smell of diesel and old coffee baked into every surface.

She climbed the pilot ladder with her duffel on her shoulder and stood for a moment on the deck, letting the motion of the water settle into her legs. Somewhere below her feet, under forty fathoms of cold Norwegian sea, lay a ship she had spent ten years not thinking about.

"You're the new hire."

The voice came from a hatch amidships, out of which folded a woman built like the vessel herself—broad, functional, streaked with grease. She wiped her hands on a rag that had given up its original color years ago and looked Lena over with the frank appraisal of someone who judged people by whether their gear was maintained.

"Eriksen," Lena said, and heard the name come out of her mouth like a coin dropped into deep water. *Voss*, she'd told the agency. *Marit Voss.* But Ashe's contract had been explicit: real name on the manifest, no aliases, no exceptions. *I want you findable,* Ashe had said, with a smile that made the word sound like a threat. So Lena had signed as Lena Eriksen for the first time in a decade, and now the name sat in her chest like swallowed ballast.

"Okafor." The engineer didn't offer a hand—hers were still filthy—but jerked her chin toward the superstructure. "Ruiz will want to see your certs. Diving, sonar, the lot. He's particular."

"And you're not?"

"I'm particular about engines. People I let Ruiz worry about." She turned back toward the hatch, then paused. "One thing. Whatever Ashe is paying you, it isn't enough to touch my ballast controls. I've seen divers who think they know pumps. They don't."

"Noted."

Okafor grunted, which Lena would learn over the following days was the full range of her approval, and disappeared below.

* * *

Ruiz received her in a cabin that served as office, chart room, and shrine. The charts were the shrine. They covered three of the four walls, some of them so old the paper had gone amber, marked in faded pencil with soundings and hazards and, here and there, small annotations in a handwriting that made something clench behind Lena's ribs before she could stop it.

She knew that handwriting. She'd grown up watching it fill legal pads at the kitchen table, columns of numbers that never quite added to anything a child could understand.

"You're Voss," Ruiz said. "Or Eriksen. The paperwork disagrees with itself."

"The paperwork is current." She set her certification folder on his desk. "Lena Eriksen. Diver, survey tech, ROV-rated."

He opened the folder without hurry. He was perhaps sixty, gray at the temples, with a face like weathered rope—everything in it pulled taut around two pale eyes that did not blink as often as they should. He read her certs line by line, and Lena stood in the silence and felt each page turn like a slow search of her pockets.

"Ten years' experience," he said finally. "Under four different names."

"The industry uses contractors. Contractors use what names they're given."

"Mm." He closed the folder but didn't give it back. "I sailed commercial salvage for thirty years. I've met a hundred divers with four names. Usually it means debt, or an ex, or a licensing board in one jurisdiction or another." His eyes came up. "Which are you?"

"All three, if we're being thorough."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Honesty. That's rarer than the certs." He slid the folder across the desk. "You're rated for mixed gas?"

"Trimix, yes."

"Good. We'll be working deep. The grid Ashe bought runs along the shelf edge west of—" He stopped. Not dramatically; he simply stopped, the way a man steps off a path he's walked a thousand times because he's noticed, too late, where it leads. "West of the usual grounds," he finished.

Lena said nothing. The chart on the wall behind him had a section turned face-in, clipped shut with a brass fastener, and she would have bet her last krone she knew what waters it showed.

"There's a bunk forward, third door," Ruiz said. "Mess is at eighteen hundred. Pell cooks Tuesdays and Thursdays, God help us all. Don't go aft past the engine room without telling Okafor—she'll hear you anyway, but she likes to be told."

He was already turning back to his charts, dismissal written in his shoulders, and Lena was almost through the door when he spoke again, quietly, without looking up.

"The compass on your bag strap. Where did you get it?"

Her hand found the compass before she decided to move it—the old brass-cased thing she'd carried since she was nineteen, since the lawyer's office, since the single box of her father's effects that had survived the bankruptcy auctions. It hung from her duffel by a worn lanyard, case scarred, glass clouded.

"It was my father's," she said.

"Salvage man?"

"Something like that."

Ruiz nodded slowly, and for a moment his eyes went somewhere else entirely—to another deck, another year—and Lena watched grief pass over his face like the shadow of a bird. Then he blinked, and it was gone.

"Keep it dry," he said. "Salt gets into everything."

* * *

Pell found her that evening, or rather materialized beside her at the rail while she watched the harbor lights smear themselves across black water. He was younger than she expected—thirty maybe, with a diver's lean build and a nervous energy that seemed to leak out of him like air from a bad seal.

"You're the new one. Voss. Or—" he lowered his voice, delighted, "—Eriksen. Ruiz has the manifest locked in his cabin, but Okafor read it over his shoulder, so basically everyone knows."

"That was fast."

"Nothing on a boat stays secret. Physics." He stuck out a hand. "Pell. Diver, second class, keeper of the good masks."

"Lena."

"Lena." He tried the name out, chewing it. "That's a name with weight. You can tell by how people say it. Like *Ingrid*. Or *Magnus*."

The name landed between them and lay there. Lena kept her eyes on the water and made her voice do nothing at all. "You believe in names having weight?"

"I believe in everything having weight." Pell leaned on the rail beside her, close enough that she could smell the brine on him—he'd clearly been in the water already today. "You've heard about this job, right? What we're actually looking for?"

"Ashe's brief said a wreck of interest on the shelf edge."

"A wreck of interest." He laughed, short and unhappy. "That's one way to say it. My uncle worked the support fleet back then. Tenders and supply boats. He was tied up at Ålesund the night the *Vargr* went down, and he swore till the day he died that the weather report that night called for force five. Force five. The *Vargr* had ridden out force nine twice that season. And then she sinks in sheltered water with all hands?" He shook his head. "Ships don't just die, Lena. Something killed her."

"Bad luck happens."

"Sure. And compasses point north." He glanced sideways at her, and there was something sharp under the nervousness, something watchful. "My uncle also said Magnus Eriksen checked the bilge alarms himself the morning they sailed. Personally. Sent the whole engineering watch ashore for a run of shore leave nobody asked for."

The harbor lights blurred. Lena discovered her hands had gone cold on the rail.

"That sounds like gossip," she managed.

"It does." Pell pushed off the rail and stretched. "Most true things do. Anyway—welcome aboard. Watch out for Tuesday dinners. Okafor claims the fish stew is cursed, but really she just hates fennel."

He wandered off toward the bow, humming, leaving Lena alone with the water and the name sitting in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed so long ago she'd mistaken it for part of her body.

*Magnus Eriksen sent the engineering watch ashore.*

Eleven men. One ship. A daughter who had built a drowned saint out of the wreckage and prayed to him for ten years.

She stayed at the rail until the cold drove her inside, and even then she didn't sleep. She lay in her narrow bunk listening to the Kestrel breathe—hull ticking, pumps sighing, the endless small conversation of a living ship—and understood, with the clarity that only comes at three in the morning, that she had signed onto a voyage where every stranger aboard might be carrying a piece of the truth she'd spent a decade avoiding.

And that one of them already was.

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide.

Lena was on deck before the lines came off, running through her pre-dive checks with the mechanical comfort of ritual, and she watched Bergen shrink behind them with a feeling she couldn't name—part escape, part surrender. Ahead lay open water, gray-green and patient, and beyond it a search grid drawn on charts she hadn't been allowed to see.

Okafor ran the engine room like a submarine crew—one woman, two hundred voices of machinery, all of them answered. Pell tested dive gear with a devotion that bordered on liturgy, blessing each regulator with a tap of two fingers that he probably thought no one noticed. And Ruiz stood at the helm hour after hour, chart at his elbow, saying little, watching much.

It was on the second day out, in the grey afternoon light, that Lena found him waiting for her by the dive locker. Alone. The rest of the crew was forward; the wind carried their voices away aft, and Ruiz's voice came low beneath it.

"I need to show you something," he said, "before we reach the grid. And then I need you to tell me whether I've spent twenty years wrong about a man."

He held out his hand. In it lay a photograph, creased white at the folds, of a younger Ruiz standing on a deck beside a tall man with fair hair going gray at the temples—a man whose face Lena knew better than her own reflection, because she had spent her whole life deciding which parts of it to keep.

Behind them both, half out of frame, the ship's bell of the *Vargr* caught the light.

"His name was Magnus Eriksen," Ruiz said, watching her face with those unblinking pale eyes. "And I have a feeling, Miss Eriksen, that you already knew that."

The wind came across the water, cold and clean, and Lena stood very still on the deck of the Kestrel and understood that the voyage had truly begun—not at the sailing, not at the signing, but here, now, with nowhere left to hide and the horizon closing in from every side.
