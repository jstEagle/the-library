## 4. What the Charts Don't Say

Tomás Ruiz had been keeping the Kestrel's log for eleven years, and in that time he had learned that a ship tells you everything if you read it right. The engine's cough before a cold start. The way the hull groaned at certain swells. The crew was no different—people leaked their histories through small things, and his job, as he saw it, was to notice.

The new hire noticed nothing back. That was the first thing wrong with her.

She'd signed on as Lena Voss, diver, references thin but clean, and she'd come aboard with two bags: one of gear so worn it had been repaired more times than replaced, and one so light it barely qualified as luggage. No photographs. No letters. A woman traveling without ballast.

Ruiz watched her the way he watched weather.

* * *

It happened on the third day out of Bergen, while they were still running north through open water toward the search grid. Okafor had the engine room, Pell was up top nursing a headache and his superstitions, and Ruiz found the new diver in the aft mess, alone, cleaning something on a cloth spread over the table like a surgeon's field.

He almost walked past. Then he saw what she was cleaning.

A compass. Brass cased, gimbal-mounted, the kind issued in batches rather than bought retail—and Ruiz knew the batch, because he'd held one just like it twenty-two years ago in a harbor office in Tromsø, when Magnus Eriksen handed them out to his expedition leads with a speech about how a man who trusted instruments would never trust himself enough to be great.

The Vargr expedition compasses. Forty-one made. Forty-one engraved on the caseback with a serial number and the word *VARGR* in a typeface no other salvage outfit used, because Eriksen had commissioned it himself from a smith in Ålesund who'd died before the ship did.

Ruiz stopped in the doorway.

The woman didn't look up. She worked oil into the hinge with a toothpick, patient, thorough, the way you maintain something you intend to keep forever. And when she turned it over to check the card, the light caught the caseback, and there it was: the worn ghost of an engraving, polished nearly smooth by years of handling—but Ruiz had spent twenty-two years learning to read ghosts.

He could not make out the serial from the doorway. He didn't need to. What he needed was to not be standing there when she looked up.

He turned and went forward, and his heart was doing something stupid and old against his ribs.

* * *

That night he sat in his cabin with the door shut and took out the notebook he kept for things that didn't belong in the official log.

He hadn't written in it since the day they'd left port. Before that, the last entry was eight months old—a note about a charter client whose cargo manifest hadn't matched his containers, which had turned out to be exactly what it looked like. Ruiz wasn't a suspicious man by nature. He was a careful one, and the difference was that suspicion looked for trouble everywhere while care only wrote down what it actually saw.

He uncapped his pen and wrote:

*Diver, L. Voss. Carries a Vargr-issue compass. Caseback engraving defaced or worn past reading—or made to look that way.*

He stared at the line for a long time.

There were forty-one of those compasses. Most had gone down with the ship; the manifests said thirty-three were aboard when she sailed. Of the eight ashore, four belonged to men who'd sold them or lost them within a year, because a dead expedition's trinkets are bad luck and bad luck sells cheap. Two were in museums. One belonged to Halvard's widow, who'd kept it on her mantelpiece until the day she died, according to the newspapers Ruiz had read too many times.

And one—the ninth, the one that shouldn't have existed, the one Eriksen had carried aboard and therefore gone down with—was accounted for by the sea.

Unless it wasn't.

Ruiz set down the pen. He was doing the thing he hated, which was building a cathedral out of a single stone. A compass could change hands. Compasses got stolen, inherited, faked. There were replica smiths in Bergen who could age brass so well it fooled customs officers.

But she hadn't bought a replica. She maintained it like an heirloom.

He thought about her hands again. Not the compass—her hands. Steady, scarred across the knuckles in the pattern of someone who'd been working wrecks since she was young enough to be told she shouldn't be. And the way she'd checked the card: not reading it, confirming it. You don't confirm your own instrument unless you've already caught it lying to you once.

Or unless it isn't really a compass at all.

He wrote one more line, then closed the book.

*Watch.*

* * *

The trouble with watching someone is that eventually they feel it, and Lena Voss felt it on the fifth day.

They were on the grid by then, running survey lines over a seabed that gave them nothing but glacial till and the occasional rusted ghost of some other, older sinking. Ashe's fleet was still a rumor on the southern horizon—a smudge of masts that appeared each morning and vanished each night, patient as gulls. Everyone aboard knew what the smudge meant. It put a wire under everyone's movements.

Ruiz found reasons to be where she was. He asked her opinion on line spacing. He had her check the tender's fuel lines with him, though Pell had done it that morning. She answered in short, competent sentences and never once asked him a question back, and that asymmetry told him more than anything else. People who want something from you ask questions. People hiding don't spend words.

On the fifth evening he came onto the deck late, after the generator cycle, and she was standing at the rail looking south—not at the smudge of Ashe's fleet, but down, at the water, as if the answer were already below and she was just waiting for permission to go get it.

"You dive like someone who's done this before," Ruiz said.

"Everyone on this boat has."

"Not like this." He leaned on the rail beside her, leaving a careful arm's length. "I've watched a hundred divers work a grid. They hunt. You—" he gestured vaguely at the water, "—you wait. Like you know it's going to be there."

She was quiet long enough that he counted the generator's rhythm twice. Then: "Maybe I'm just tired."

"Tired people sleep." He said it mildly. "You haven't slept properly since Bergen. I hear you turning over above my cabin."

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear exactly. Recalculation. She was deciding how much of herself to show him, and the fact that she was deciding at all meant she'd understood his question perfectly.

"Eriksen," she said.

The name landed in Ruiz's chest like a dropped tool.

"What about him?"

"The Vargr. That's the wreck we're hunting, isn't it? Ashe's contract doesn't say, but the grid coordinates do. Everyone's pretending it's a routine salvage, but nobody runs six tides of survey for a trawler." She shrugged. "So. Eriksen. The famous one. I figured I should know whose grave we're digging up."

Ruiz looked at her and felt the strange vertigo of hearing a lie that was also true. Everything she'd said was correct. None of it was the reason she'd said it.

"He wasn't famous," Ruiz said. "He was loud. There's a difference."

"You knew him."

"I worked for him." Twenty-two years fell away and he was thirty again, standing on the Vargr's deck with a brass compass in his hand, listening to a speech about greatness. "One season. Survey lead. I got off the ship the week before she sailed and I've spent twenty-two years being glad and ashamed about it in roughly equal measure."

"Why both?"

Because Magnus Eriksen had shaken his hand at the gangway and said, *Smart man. The rest of us will make history and you'll make rent.* Because three weeks later the Vargr went down with eleven souls and no distress call, and Ruiz had spent a decade wondering if staying would have changed anything, and another decade knowing it wouldn't. Because grief is a debt you can't discharge, only service.

"Both is how it works," he said aloud.

She nodded slowly, and for a moment her face did something unguarded—something that looked, absurdly, like kinship. Then it sealed itself again, and she wished him goodnight and went below, and Ruiz stayed at the rail watching the water do its ancient indifferent work.

* * *

In his cabin, he opened the notebook.

*L. Voss asked about Eriksen tonight. Volunteered knowledge of the target. Asked why I felt both glad and ashamed. Who asks that?*

He sat with the pen hovering.

Here was the shape of the thing he couldn't say yet, even to himself: the compass might be coincidence. The questions might be professional curiosity. But the way she'd said *Eriksen*—flat, careful, a name handled with tongs—that was not how strangers said it. Strangers said it with awe or with scandal, the way you'd say *Titanic* or *Amelia Earhart*. She'd said it the way you say a name that lives in your house.

Magnus Eriksen had a daughter. Ruiz remembered her from the harbor office in Tromsø—a girl of maybe twelve, all elbows and silence, sitting in the corner of meetings she was allowed to attend because Eriksen believed children absorbed greatness by proximity. Ruiz had spoken to her once. She'd corrected his Norwegian pronunciation of a fjord name with the gravity of a harbormaster, and Eriksen had laughed with real delight and said, *That one will bury us all.*

What was her name? Ingrid—no, that was Halvard's girl, the other family ruined by the same sinking. Eriksen's daughter had a different name, a northern name, and Ruiz had known it once and let it go the way you let go of things that hurt to hold.

Lena. Was it Lena? Had it ever been Lena?

He wrote:

*If she is who I think she is, she's using a false name on my manifest, which makes me complicit in whatever she's doing. If she isn't, I'm about to insult a stranger by accusing her of being a dead man's daughter.*

*Either way: watch.*

He paused, then added, because honesty was the whole point of the private book:

*I keep thinking about Halvard. About what it cost his family when the inquiry blamed him and cleared Eriksen posthumously. If Eriksen's blood is on this ship, does she carry his guilt or his secrets? Are those different things?*

*Twenty-two years I've wondered what Magnus knew that made him sail into that storm without a distress plan. Maybe the answer just walked up my gangway with two bags and a compass that shouldn't exist.*

* * *

The next morning the smudge on the horizon resolved into hulls.

Ashe's fleet had closed to visible distance overnight—three vessels riding high and fast, running lights still lit in the grey dawn, which was either arrogance or a message. Pell spotted them first and came down the ladder saying a word he'd been saving since Bergen, and by full light the whole crew stood along the rail watching the competition arrive like weather.

Ruiz stood apart, as usual, and found that Lena Voss had drifted to stand near him. Near, not beside. Close enough to speak without carrying.

"They're early," she said.

"They're fast. Different thing." He raised his binoculars. Lead ship flying a Panamanian flag, freshly painted, the kind of money that didn't need to hide. Behind it, a workhorse salvage tender. Behind that, something low and dark that he didn't like the look of at all. "Ashe runs three classes of ship. Survey, salvage, and persuasion."

"And which one are we?"

"We're the ones with the honest paperwork." He lowered the glasses. "For now."

She was quiet, watching the fleet hold station at the edge of the grid—close enough to see, far enough to claim innocence. Then she said, without turning her head: "You never finished telling me about Eriksen. Why you got off the ship."

"I told you. Smart man. Rent instead of history."

"That's what he said to you?"

Word for word. Ruiz turned and looked at her fully for the first time in daylight, and she met his eyes without flinching, and there it was again—that flicker beneath the calm, the sense of a person holding very still around something alive.

"No," she said, answering a question he hadn't asked. "No, he wouldn't have said that to you. He'd have said—" and she stopped, jaw tightening, as if she'd bitten down on the sentence itself.

"Said what?" Ruiz asked softly.

"Nothing. Forget it." She pushed off the rail. "Line four starts in twenty minutes. I'll take the first dive."

She walked away, and Ruiz stood in the wind with his heart hammering, because he'd finally placed it. Not the compass. Not the name. The voice. Twelve years old, correcting his Norwegian in a harbor office, and now grown into a woman's throat but carrying the same precise, clipped certainty—

*That one will bury us all.*

He went below, took out the notebook, and wrote faster than he had in years:

*She quoted him. Or started to. She knows how Magnus Eriksen talked—knows it the way you know a parent's voice, not the way you know a biography.*

*Tomorrow I check the crew manifest against every alias I can pull from the registry offices in Bergen. If Tomás Ruiz spent twenty-two years running from one sinking, he'll be damned if he lets it sneak back aboard wearing someone else's name.*

*But God help me—I hope it's her. I hope it's his kid, coming back for answers none of the rest of us were brave enough to dive for.*

At the bottom of the page, after a long pause, he added a final line, the one he'd been circling since the mess hall:

*Note: the compass. Check whether the card actually settles north. Some of Eriksen's instruments were custom work, and the man loved a trick more than a tool.*
