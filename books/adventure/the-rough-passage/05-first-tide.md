## 5. First Tide

The grid was a lie drawn in straight lines.

Lena stood at the chart table in the Kestrel's cramped survey room and looked at the search area Ashe's people had bought with their money and their certainty: a neat rectangle of ocean forty kilometers square, south-southwest of the last known Vargr position, divided into numbered lanes like rows in a field waiting to be planted. The coordinates were plausible. They were even defensible—drift modeling, current data, the kind of analysis that looked rigorous in a report and cost nothing to be wrong.

She'd have believed it herself, ten years ago. Before she learned that the sea didn't keep tidy records.

"Lane twelve," Ruiz said behind her. He had a mug of coffee he never seemed to drink from, just carried, like a prop in a play about a man who drank coffee. "Ashe's liaison wants us to start at lane one and work east. Systematic."

"And you want?"

Ruiz set the mug down on the corner of the chart, precisely, so the ring of old stains wouldn't touch the paper. "I want to start where I'd start," he said. "Which is not lane one."

"You were on the original expedition."

Something moved through his face—not surprise; he'd known she knew; it had been in his file, or whatever version of his file Ashe's people had assembled for her benefit—and settled into something older and flatter. "Deckhand. Nineteen years old. I tied lines and hauled gear for men who talked over my head." He tapped the chart once, near the western edge of the rectangle, where the lanes ran out against open water. "Magnus Eriksen did not sail predictable. He sailed like a man who already knew where he was going and wanted everyone else to think otherwise."

The name landed the way it always landed, a weight dropped into still water. Lena kept her hands flat on the table. "Then why is the grid here?"

"Because Ashe works from documents," Ruiz said. "Documents are what people leave behind when they want to be found. Magnus left documents because he liked being looked for by the wrong people in the wrong places." He picked up his coffee, finally, and drank. "Start anywhere you like, Eriksen. It's your debt."

He said her false name without irony, without emphasis, and walked out, and Lena stood alone with the chart and understood that Ruiz had just told her two things: that he suspected the alias was an alias, and that he would wait, patiently, for the real one.

* * *

They started at dawn on the third day out, when the Kestrel came up on the grid's eastern boundary and the sea went from grey to a hard, glittering blue that meant shallow water over sand, the kind of bottom that gave back sonar returns clean as bell tones.

Lena suited up first. Not because she had to—there were three contracted divers aboard and Pell had seniority—but because if she was going to spend six tides dragging a rectangle of ocean, she was going to do it with her own hands, and because standing on deck watching other people descend into water that might hold her father's ship was a thing she could not have survived intact.

Pell checked her rig twice, which was one more time than necessary, and touched the little tin medal clipped inside his harness—a St. Nicholas, patron of sailors, dented and green with age. "Bottom's soft," he said. "Visibility maybe eight meters, less when you stir it. Current runs north along the lanes, so work with it, not against. And if the water goes cold all of a sudden—"

"It won't."

"It did for the Vargr boys," Pell said. "Cold spot off the stern, Okafor's cousin swore it, right up until—" He stopped himself, made the sign against bad talk with two fingers, and turned away to check his own gauges.

Okafor ran the winch and the comms from the deck, his voice in Lena's ear a low, unhurried baritone that made the descent feel almost routine. "Two meters. Five. Relax your breathing, you're gulping. Ten meters. Bottom in sight."

It wasn't sand. That was the first thing. The charts said sand, and the sonar said sand, but what lay under Lena's fins was hardpan and scattered stone, a seabed like a swept floor, scoured by currents that had been doing this work for centuries. She drifted down the shot line and felt the disappointment arrive before she'd seen anything at all: this was not a place where a ship had died. Ships died messily. They left furrows, debris fans, a scatter of coal and crockery and bone like a wound in the seafloor. This was a floor that had been cleaned.

"Lena Eriksen"—the alias, delivered in Okafor's neutral voice—"report."

"Bottom contact. Hardpan, no sediment cover. No debris field in visual range. Moving north along lane one."

"Copy. Pell's in behind you, ninety seconds."

She worked. That was the discipline her father had taught her without ever teaching it—her mother told stories about Magnus coming home from expeditions and putting seven-year-old Lena at the kitchen table sorting shell casings and coins from a bucket of mud, hour after hour, until the child's fingers knew the difference between value and shine. Work the grid. Touch everything. Let the site speak at its own speed.

For four hours, the site said nothing.

Rust, eventually. A scatter of modern wreckage—engine block, hull fragments, a winch drum furred orange with corrosion—that the sonar had read as a target and the eye read as junk, some coastal trawler's grave, decades dead. Pell found a boot sole. Lena found a porthole rimmed in barnacle, glass long gone, and held it up to the grey-green light and saw her own distorted face looking back, and put it down.

By the time they surfaced, the wind had come around and the swell had a chop on it, and the Kestrel's deck crew were quiet in the way crews get quiet after a first dive yields nothing—the superstition that day one sets the tone for the whole run settling over them like spray.

Ruiz met her at the rail as she stripped her hood. "Nothing?"

"Nothing that matters."

"Mm." He looked west, and Lena followed his gaze, and that was when she saw them.

Three ships, low and white and fast, running down the long swell with the particular arrogance of vessels that had been built recently and paid for recently. At their lead, a hull name in letters too far away to read but not too far to recognize by shape alone: the fleet of Corinne Ashe, arriving exactly when a well-funded rival arrives—not late, not early, but at the precise moment designed to remind you whose money was bigger.

"They'll anchor inside our grid by nightfall," Ruiz said. "Claim adjacency rights. By tomorrow they'll have ROVs in the water and lawyers on the radio."

"Is that legal?"

"Everything Ashe does is legal. That's the worst thing about her." He turned back to Lena, and for a moment the flat professional mask slipped and she saw the tired man underneath, the one who had been nineteen once on a different deck. "Get some sleep, Eriksen. Tide turns at 0400. We dive again whether they like it or not."

* * *

They dove again. Lane three yielded a refrigerator door and a length of chain. Lane five, nothing at all—just the swept hardpan going on and on under the lights like a ballroom floor abandoned mid-waltz. On the second day, Ashe's flagship anchored eight hundred meters off their port beam, close enough that Lena could see figures moving on her decks, close enough that the ROV launch crane swung out over the water like a slow accusation.

The liaison's name was Merrick. He'd introduced himself in Bergen with a handshake calibrated to communicate equality while establishing hierarchy, and he spent his days now on the Kestrel's bridge with a satellite phone and a smile, asking cheerful questions and writing down answers. "Any joy today?" he asked Lena at the evening debrief, pen poised.

"Refrigerator door," she said. "If Ashe wants it, we can negotiate."

Merrick laughed the way paid men laugh. "Ms. Ashe has deep pockets but shallow patience, Ms. Eriksen. Six tides. She expects results by tide three." He clicked the pen closed. "No pressure."

There was, of course, only pressure. Lena lay in her bunk that night listening to the Kestrel's engines idle against the anchor and thought about her father's habit of sailing unpredictably, and about the grid, and about how wrong it felt. Forty kilometers of carefully modeled ocean, and every meter of it empty. Either the models were wrong, or—

Or the wreck wasn't there. Or the wreck was somewhere the models couldn't reach, because someone had made sure the documents pointed the wrong way.

She got up, dressed, and went to the survey room, where the chart still lay under its lamp with Ruiz's coffee ring ghosted in one corner. She stood over it for a long time. West, the lanes ran out against deeper water, past the shelf edge, where the sonar data thinned to guesswork and the grid simply stopped, as if the ocean beyond were not worth modeling. As if whoever drew the rectangle had decided, in advance, what was possible.

Her father had taught her that trick too, though she hadn't known it was a trick until years later: hide things in plain sight by making the hiding place look like a boundary. Don't guard the door. Make people believe there is no door.

She put her finger on the western edge of the grid and held it there until the lamp buzzed and her hand ached, and then she went back to bed and did not sleep.

* * *

Tide three began in fog.

It rolled in off the sea before dawn, a thick grey wool that swallowed Ashe's fleet whole—Lena heard their generators, smelled their diesel, and saw nothing of them—and turned the Kestrel into an island. Merrick paced the bridge and made calls that didn't connect. Okafor pronounced the conditions marginal and the visibility underwater likely worse, and Lena suited up anyway, because the fog was a gift. Whatever she did in the water today, nobody ashore or afloat would see it happen.

She dropped down the shot line into green-black silence and worked west along lane nine, against the current, fins beating steadily, compass in her hand.

It was her father's compass. Brass cased, glass cracked in one corner, the needle housing clouded with age. She'd carried it through ten years and four aliases because throwing it away had been impossible and keeping it openly had been worse, and because some part of her had always known it wasn't quite right. The needle didn't settle. It wandered, sluggish, disagreeing with every other compass she'd ever held it next to, and she'd told herself it was broken, the way you tell yourself things about the dead.

In the fog-dark water, holding it now, she watched the needle swing and hold—steadily, stubbornly—west. Not north. West, out past the edge of the grid, toward the deep water nobody had bothered to model.

Later, she would tell herself she'd noticed it gradually. The truth was simpler and more frightening: the moment the needle steadied, she knew. Some inheritance doesn't arrive as knowledge. It arrives as recognition.

But that was later. Today she had a job to do, and a grid to finish, and a liaison counting tides.

Lane nine: rust and stone. Lane eleven: a tangle of net, decades old, home to a crab that regarded her with ancient indifference. Her air gauge fell. The current pushed. Somewhere above, muffled through forty feet of green water, the Kestrel's engine turned over, repositioning for the next lane, and Lena hung in the fog-dark with her father's broken compass pointing steadily, impossibly west, and made herself turn back.

Discipline. Work the grid. Let the site speak at its own speed.

But the site was speaking. It had been speaking since the first dive, and she had finally, on the third tide, in the fog, begun to understand the language: *not here. Not here. Not here.* A negative space shaped exactly like a lie.

* * *

She surfaced into grey soup and a deck crew coiling wet rope, and it was Pell who took her tank, and Pell who frowned.

"You're bleeding."

"I'm what?" She looked at her hands. A scrape across the left palm, shallow, salt-stung, from hauling herself along the shot line. "Netting. It's nothing."

Pell didn't let go of the tank. His eyes had gone strange—not frightened, exactly, but the way a man looks at weather turning. "Third dive," he said quietly. "Blood in the water on the third dive. My grandmother had a word for that."

"Pell—"

"She drowned, my grandmother. Off Lofoten. Said the sea keeps accounts." He hefted the tank onto his shoulder and looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse until she realized it was pity, and that it wasn't for her. "Whatever you're looking for out there, Eriksen—it's looking back. I've done this thirty years. I know the feeling."

He walked aft, and Lena stood dripping on the deck with her scraped palm stinging and the fog pressing in from every side, and told herself the old man was a superstitious fool.

Then she opened her fist and looked at the thing she'd found in the net tangle on lane eleven and hadn't logged, because logging it would raise questions she couldn't answer yet.

A hull plate. Small, torn free of whatever it had fastened, edges jagged with old force. Corroded nearly smooth, the metal bloomed orange and black, but stamped deep enough to survive the sea's housekeeping, and she'd scrubbed it with her thumb in the dark water until the letters came up through the rust like a face through frosted glass:

**HALVARD & CO. — BERGEN**

Not the Vargr. Not her father's name, not his company, not his ship.

Halvard. The partner. The man the histories blamed, the man who'd taken the fall for the lost cargo while Magnus Eriksen went down a hero, the man whose descendants—if he'd had any—had lived fifty years under the weight of a theft they hadn't committed.

His stamp was on a plate from a wreck that shouldn't be anywhere near this grid, unless the wrecks were connected, unless the story everyone had been told for half a century was built on a hull of lies, unless—

Unless her father hadn't just died with his secret. Unless he'd built the secret a room, and locked it, and sunk the key.

Lena closed her hand around the plate until the jagged edge bit into the scrape on her palm, and welcomed the pain, because pain was simple and the rest of it was not. Out in the fog, Ashe's generators hummed. On the bridge, Merrick was making calls. Behind her, Ruiz watched from the wheelhouse door with an expression she didn't turn around long enough to read.

Six tides. Three gone. And the compass in her cabin, she now understood, had never been broken at all.

It had been pointing somewhere all along.
