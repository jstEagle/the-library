## 6. The Compass Points Down

The compass sat in Lena's palm like a held breath.

She'd carried it for ten years without really looking at it. That was the trick of inheritance—you learned to stop seeing the things that hurt. The brass case was dented on one side where it had struck a rail, or a knee, or something worse, the night the Vargr went down. Her mother had been handed it at the memorial service by a man from the insurance consortium who couldn't meet her eyes. Lena had been fourteen. She'd taken it because no one else wanted to touch anything her father's hands had touched.

It lived now in the bottom of her kit bag, wrapped in a sock, riding from dig site to dig site under whatever name she was using that season. She hadn't opened the case in years.

But tonight the Kestrel rode at anchor inside the second tide line, and Ruiz had said something over dinner that wouldn't leave her alone. *Your father ran a tight grid,* he'd said, not looking at her, or looking at her in the way people look when they've decided not to say what they're thinking. *Magnus never dove blind. He always knew his water.*

She'd laughed it off. Gone to her bunk. Lay there listening to Okafor's engines tick as they cooled, and Pell coughing in the next rack, and the North Sea working the hull like a patient animal trying one more time to get in.

Then she'd gotten up and unwrapped the compass.

* * *

The thing about a compass is that it lies beautifully. It points north with such confidence that you forget north is an agreement, not a fact. Lena had spent a decade navigating by instruments she trusted absolutely, and somewhere in those years she'd stopped questioning the one instrument she'd inherited instead of chosen.

She opened the brass case under her bunk light and set it flat on the chart table.

The needle swung. Settled. Pointed—she checked against the wall compass mounted above the table—roughly northwest by west. Off true north by a good forty degrees. She'd noticed the deviation years ago and assumed the mechanism had been damaged when the ship went down. A bent pivot. A magnetized case. Salvage gear took knocks; you compensated and moved on.

Except.

She turned the compass ninety degrees clockwise. The needle didn't follow the case the way a free needle should, lagging and then catching up with the earth's field. It stayed exactly where it was relative to the table. She rotated the whole instrument back and forth, slow, then fast, and the needle barely trembled—a few degrees of lazy drift, nothing more, like a man shrugging off a question he'd already answered.

A magnetic needle doesn't do that. A magnetic needle is a slave to the planet. This one wasn't pointing north. It was pointing *somewhere*, and it didn't care which way Lena faced.

Her mouth had gone dry. She set the compass down carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening, and made herself think it through like a professional instead of a daughter.

Weighted. The needle—or whatever passed for one—was weighted asymmetrically, damped heavy, probably oil-filled behind the card. Built to hold a bearing regardless of the ship's heading. Not broken. *Purposeful.* Someone had machined this deviation into it deliberately, tuned it, tested it. And there was only one reason to build a compass that pointed at a fixed bearing instead of north:

So that a man standing on any deck, in any weather, holding this object, would know which direction lay home.

Or—which direction lay *there*. One specific place. A bearing burned into brass.

Lena thought of her father's last letters, the ones her mother had kept in a biscuit tin until the tin outlived her too. Magnus Eriksen wrote like a man leaving instructions between the lines. *The sea gives up only what you ask for properly.* She'd read that as philosophy. Now she wondered if it was arithmetic.

She pulled the Kestrel's current chart across the table and laid the compass on it, edge of the case kissing the pencil mark where they'd anchored tonight. Northwest by west. She drew the line lightly, barely touching paper, and stared at it running off toward the grid's western margin—the dead corner, the sector Ashe's survey had flagged as low-probability and skipped after the first pass.

Everyone was searching east. The echo returns were better east. The seabed profile matched the Vargr's known draft east.

And her father had built a pointer aimed west.

*He scuttled her,* Pell had said around the galley stove two nights ago, and the crew had groaned and told him to save it for shore. *You don't sink straight down in open water. You walk her out first. You pick your spot.*

Lena had dismissed it. Grief dressed up as ghost story.

Now she picked up the compass again and felt its weight differently—not the weight of a relic but of a tool. Her father had walked his ship out to a spot he chose, and he'd left a needle to tell anyone worth telling how to find it. Anyone. Or perhaps: one person. Perhaps he'd imagined handing it to her someday, the way he'd imagined a lot of things that never happened.

The thrill that went through her then was shameful and clean at once. Ten years of digging other people's mud under borrowed names, and here, finally, was something that was hers—not her father's shame, not the debt, not the alias—but a piece of knowledge sitting in her hand like a lit match. Inheritance as advantage. For one long moment she let herself feel it: she knew something Corinne Ashe did not know, something Ashe was spending a fleet's worth of fuel and money failing to find.

Then the shame caught up, because that was also exactly how Magnus must have felt. And look where his certainty had parked eleven men.

* * *

Footsteps on the companionway ladder. Lena closed the compass case with a click that sounded enormous in the quiet and slid it under the chart drawer as Wexler—the liaison, Ashe's eyes aboard, all pressed collar and corporate calm—leaned into the compartment.

"Working late." His gaze went to the chart, to the faint pencil line, to her face. He had the habit of reading surfaces and believing he'd read the book.

"Checking tomorrow's dive plan," Lena said. "The eastern returns are noisy. I want to re-run the grid spacing."

"You think it's east?" Something quickened behind his eyes. Ashe had promised him a bonus tied to a find; everyone aboard knew it, and everyone aboard pretended not to.

"The data says east," Lena said, and heard how easily it came, smooth as ballast sliding into a hold, and hated it, and kept her face still. "Data's usually right."

"It is," Wexler agreed. "Ms. Ashe values rigor." He lingered a beat longer than courtesy required, cataloguing her, then withdrew. His boots faded up the ladder toward the deck and the satellite phone he'd no doubt use within the hour: *the Eriksen woman is diligent, chasing the eastern anomaly.*

Lena sat in the lamplight and let out a breath she felt in her ribs.

That was the third lie she'd told aboard this ship. The first two had been small—her reasons for joining, her indifference to the Vargr. Each one had come easier than the last, arriving fully formed, professional, unhesitating. She'd spent a decade being nobody, and she'd told herself that hiding was survival, not skill. But lying to Wexler hadn't felt like survival. It had felt like talent.

*You can deceive as well as any Eriksen,* said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like Corinne Ashe. *The question is what you build with it.*

She opened the drawer, took out the compass, and held it level. The needle leaned faithfully northwest by west, patient as a dog at a door, asking nothing but to be followed.

Tomorrow was the third tide. Wexler would expect her on the eastern grid, and she'd give him a show of it—two dives, honest-looking effort, a full logbook page. And somewhere in the middle of the performance, while Pell ran the winch and Okafor grumbled about compressor pressure and Ruiz watched her with those old-expedition eyes that missed less than he admitted, she would slip west along the margin, past the skipped sector, following a dead man's needle down into water no one was watching.

Alone. Which was stupid, and she knew it was stupid, and she was going to do it anyway, because the alternative was waiting for committees and contracts and Corinne Ashe's lawyers to arrive at the truth by road while Lena stood holding a compass that had already arrived.

She wrapped the compass back in its sock. Before stowing it, she turned it once more in her fingers, thumb finding the dent in the brass.

"Hold on, old man," she murmured. "I'm coming. And I'm not you."

Outside, the tide turned, and the Kestrel swung gently on its anchor, bow easing around until it pointed—without knowing it, without meaning to—northwest by west.
