## 7. Halvard's Daughter

The envelope had no return address, which was the first thing Ingrid Halvard noticed about it, because everything worth trusting in her experience arrived with its origins visible.

She stood at her kitchen counter in Ålesund with the envelope flat under the window light, turning it once, twice. Ordinary brown paper. Her name typed on a label—*I. HALVARD*—in a font that belonged to no office she knew. Postmarked Bergen. Inside, a single sheet, also typed, no signature.

*Salvage operation departing for grid 62°41′N 4°12′E. Vessel Kestrel, out of Bergen, plus contracted fleet under Corinne Ashe. Target: wreck of the Vargr.*

Ingrid read it three times. Then she set it down, washed her coffee cup, dried it, put it away, and read it a fourth time, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Sixty-two degrees forty-one north. Four degrees twelve east.

She knew those numbers the way other people knew their mother's birthday. She knew them in her father's handwriting, cramped into the margins of letters he'd written from the *Vargr* in the last spring of his life, when he still believed the expedition would make him and not unmake him. *We're holding at 62-41, 4-12 until the weather lifts,* he'd written. *Halvard & Eriksen Salvage, working the shelf like our fathers before us.* He'd underlined the coordinates twice, the way he did with things he was proud of.

Her father had been proud of exactly one thing in his life, and it had killed him by inches over eleven years instead of all at once.

Ingrid poured herself a glass of water she didn't want and drank half of it. Outside, the harbor traffic moved in its slow gray procession, trawlers and feed barges and one white ferry cutting its neat line toward the islands. Somewhere out there, past the shelf edge, past the depth where the light gave up, lay the ship that had taken her father's name and dragged it through every maritime court and salvage registry and tavern conversation on the coast for a decade.

*Halvard.* The name that meant thief, in the trade's shorthand. The partner who'd signed the manifests, who'd been blamed when the cargo vanished from the ledgers, who'd drowned with his reputation while Magnus Eriksen drowned with his secrets—or so everyone said. Eleven crew dead, the ship down in deep water, the cargo unaccounted for, and the inquiry had needed someone to blame. It had needed someone whose body they could point at and say *there, that's where the money went.*

They'd never found her father's body either. Just his coat, eventually. Just enough.

And now someone—anonymously, carefully, deliberately—was telling her that two salvage operations were racing to the spot where the whole ruin of their lives had begun.

She thought about who might have sent it. Not Ashe; Ashe wouldn't warn anyone, she'd simply arrive. Not the Kestrel's owners; they'd have no reason. Which left someone aboard, or someone adjacent, someone who wanted Ingrid there the way you want a witness at an execution. Someone who understood that what was buried at 62-41 wasn't cargo.

It didn't matter, she decided. What mattered was that the door had opened, and she intended to walk through it standing up.

* * *

Her father's letters lived in a cedar box under the bed in the room that had been hers as a girl, in the house outside Ulsteinvik where her mother still kept the lights on in rooms nobody used. Ingrid drove out that evening through rain that came sideways off the sea, and her mother met her at the door with the particular expression of a woman who has spent eleven years watching her daughter carry something heavy and knows tonight is one of the nights.

"The box," her mother said. Not a question.

"I need the letters from the spring. The last ones."

"They're in order," her mother said. "I keep them in order."

Of course she did. Her mother had spent eleven years keeping everything in order—the estate papers, the inquiry transcripts, the clippings, the letters—as if perfect arrangement could substitute for justice. As if a well-organized archive were a kind of answer.

Ingrid sat on the floor of her old room with the box open and the radiator ticking and read her way through the spring of the *Vargr*'s final season. Her father's handwriting deteriorated across the months, she noticed—not the content, the hand itself. Confident loops in March. By May, pressed hard, the pen nearly tearing the paper, as though he were writing against something.

*Eriksen wants to move the manifest dates again,* one letter said. *I've told him no. I've told him the surveyor will catch it.*

*I don't like the new insurance arrangement. He says it's standard. Nothing about that man is standard.*

*If anything happens to me, look at the manifests against the dive logs. Look at them properly. Promise me you'll look.*

She had looked. She'd been nineteen, and grief-stupid, and the inquiry lawyers had looked first and explained to her patiently why the evidence pointed where it pointed: her father's signatures, her father's access, her father's debts. She'd been nineteen and alone against a narrative, and narratives win. She'd stopped looking. That was the wound she carried—not just the blame but her own surrender to it, the years she'd let the story stand because fighting it required a strength she hadn't yet grown.

She was thirty-one now. She'd grown it.

The last letter was dated nine days before the sinking. Four lines.

*Holding position 62-41, 4-12. Weather window coming. Eriksen is cheerful, which frightens me more than any storm would. A cheerful man with a secret is more dangerous than a frightened one with a knife.*

*Whatever they say after, remember the ship went down quiet. Ships don't go down quiet unless someone helps them.*

Ingrid closed the box and sat in the dark of her childhood room for a long time, listening to the rain, feeling the cold purpose settle over her like a coat that fit perfectly because it had been tailored for eleven years.

*Ships don't go down quiet unless someone helps them.*

Her father had known. He'd known right up to the end, and he'd written it down, and he'd sent it home to a nineteen-year-old girl who hadn't been able to do anything with it. Now someone was handing her a second chance at the only thing that mattered.

* * *

Chartering a boat took four days and most of what remained of the estate money, which was to say, most of what remained of everything.

She went through a broker in Kristiansund who didn't ask questions because she paid the questions fee upfront. The vessel was a converted fisheries tender called the *Måse*, forty feet, diesel, slow, honest, with a skipper named Torvund who was sixty and weathered and looked at her for a long moment when she told him the destination.

"Deep water," he said. "That's a watch-the-gauges run, not a fishing trip."

"I know what's out there."

"Do you." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime the previous decade. "People who say that usually mean they know what they hope is out there."

"Then let's say I know what I'm afraid is out there, and I want to see it anyway."

Torvund considered this. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to satisfy him, or at least satisfy the part of him that decided whether a charter was worth the risk. "Two days' steam. Weather window holds maybe five days. After that I bring us home regardless of what you haven't found. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And if there's trouble out there—and there will be trouble, because nothing good ever happened at those coordinates—I don't fight. I'm a skipper, not a soldier. We leave."

"We leave," she agreed, and shook his hand, and felt the small dishonesty of it settle somewhere behind her ribs. Because she did not intend to leave. Whatever was raised from that seabed—cargo, confession, or nothing at all—she intended to be present for it, publicly, unmistakably, with her father's name spoken aloud in front of whoever was doing the raising. If the *Vargr* gave up its secrets, the world was going to learn whose disgrace it actually was.

She provisioned that afternoon with methodical care: charts, flares, a handheld radio, a waterproof case for documents. Into the case went copies of everything—her father's letters, the manifest discrepancies she'd finally documented properly at twenty-eight, the inquiry transcript with her own marginal notes accumulated over a decade of sleepless rereading. If she went into that water, her evidence would stay above it.

On the last evening, her mother came to the dock. They stood together at the rail of the *Måse*, looking at the gray water and the gray sky and the gulls hanging motionless on the wind like punctuation marks.

"You could let it go," her mother said quietly. "Eleven years, Ingrid. You could just let it be."

"If I let it be, then it stays true. The version where Dad was the thief. It becomes permanent history." Ingrid watched a gull bank and slide away seaward. "He wrote to me, Mum. Nine days before the end. He told me to look. I'm done not looking."

Her mother reached into her coat pocket and brought out something small, wrapped in flannel. She pressed it into Ingrid's palm.

Inside was a brass pocket compass, old, dented, the lid engraved with a name worn nearly smooth: *HALVARD*. "His. From the boat. It came back with the effects." Her mother's voice was steady in the way that cost her something. "It doesn't point north anymore. Never has, since we got it back. But it pointed true for forty years before that, and I think—" she stopped, started again. "I think he'd want it going back to the water. Where it can finish whatever it's pointing at."

Ingrid closed her hand around it. The metal was cold and then warm, the way handed-down things are.

* * *

They cleared the breakwater at dawn on the fifth day, the *Måse* rolling gently into the long North Sea swell, Torvund at the wheel with a thermos of coffee and the resigned expression of a man who had made peace with a bad idea. Ingrid stood at the bow with her father's compass open in her hand, watching the needle swing lazily, aimlessly, pointing at nothing and therefore, she supposed, at everything.

Behind them, Ålesund shrank to a smudge. Ahead, two hundred miles southwest, two salvage operations were converging on a patch of empty blue water where a ship full of lies had gone down quiet.

She didn't know yet who else was out there. She didn't know that among the strangers on the *Kestrel* was a woman carrying the other name from her father's letters, the name that had walked away clean while Halvard sank. She didn't know that the daughter of Magnus Eriksen was already diving toward the truth with a weighted compass of her own.

She knew only what she'd come to do. Not to salvage. Not to claim. To watch. To stand on the water where her father's reputation had drowned and refuse, this time, to look away.

Torvund called from the wheelhouse, "Weather says five days. Make them count."

Ingrid turned the compass over in her hand, once, twice, the way she'd turned the anonymous envelope, and looked south toward the horizon where the weather was building.

"Hold your course," she said. "I'll tell you when we're close."

The needle swung, and swung, and settled—pointing, for the first time in eleven years, toward something.
