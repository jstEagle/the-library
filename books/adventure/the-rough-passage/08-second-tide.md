## 8. Second Tide

The barometer started falling at noon, and by three the Kestrel was running for shelter with her halyards singing like a woman being strangled.

Pell felt it in his teeth before the instruments confirmed it. Thirty years on the water did that to a man—gave him a second nervous system, one that lived below the thinking mind and spoke in pressure and ache. His left knee said storm. His right ear, the one the propeller took half of in '09, said worse than storm.

"Skjellvik cove," Ruiz called from the wheelhouse, one hand on the glass. "Two hours if we push."

Okafor grunted from the engine hatch, grease to his elbows. "Pushing costs us a fuel pump."

"Not pushing costs us a mast."

Pell said nothing. He never said much when the weather talked; it seemed rude to interrupt. He coiled line instead, working the wet rope through his hands in figure-eights, and watched the sky stack itself into slate over the western islands. The clouds had that boiled look, that bruised underbelly he'd learned to respect off Mauritania when he was nineteen and stupid and lost two shipmates to a sea that had looked, right up until it didn't, perfectly friendly.

They made Skjellvik with an hour of light to spare—a narrow bite in the coast, deep enough to anchor, ringed with black granite that took the swell's teeth out of it. Not comfortable. Nothing about a lee was ever comfortable. But the Kestrel swung at her anchor like a dog tied outside a shop, restless but contained, and Okafor got the generator settled, and Ruiz cooked something with onions in it because Ruiz believed onions solved most problems afloat.

Pell waited until the plates were empty and the coffee was poured. That was the tradition. Ghost stories came after coffee, same as they came after funerals—when the body was fed and the mind had nothing left to guard against them.

He didn't plan it. He'd sworn off telling the Vargr story years ago, after the last time, when a skipper in Stavanger threw him off the boat for lowering morale. But the storm pressed against the hull like a listener, and the cove was full of echoes, and Pell had spent the whole run from Bergen feeling the girl watching him—the Eriksen woman, the one with the compass she checked too often, the one whose face went strange whenever anyone said the old man's name.

She needed to hear it. He couldn't have said how he knew. He just knew, the way he knew weather.

"You've all heard I worked salvage before this," he began, turning his mug slowly on the table so the handle faced him, then away, then back. "Mostly true. What I haven't said is where I started."

Ruiz glanced up. Okafor kept cleaning his nails with a folding knife, which meant he was listening.

"Nineteen years ago. I was deckhand on a supply tender out of Ålesund. We ran provisions and dive gas to the survey boats—little runs, honest money." The mug turned. "One of the boats we serviced was the Vargr."

The name landed the way it always landed, like a coin dropped down a shaft. Nobody moved.

"Magnus Eriksen's ship," Ruiz said quietly. "The one that went down with all hands."

"All hands except the ones who weren't aboard," Pell said. "That's the part nobody asks about. Eleven men drowned in Havsul Basin, and everyone mourned them proper, and then everyone stopped asking why a ship as sound as the Vargr—one of the soundest hulls I ever stood on, and I stood on her, I loaded her, I know her lines—why she went down in weather no worse than we're sitting in right now."

Outside, the wind found its voice against the granite, a long low note like a struck bottle.

"She was sound," Pell repeated. "I helped paint her boot stripe. There wasn't a seam out of true on her. And Magnus Eriksen was not a careless man. Careless men don't last in this trade. So you tell me how a sound ship and a careful man end up on the bottom together with eleven souls."

"Equipment failure," Okafor said. "Happens."

"To ships that aren't maintained. To crews that cut corners. Not to Eriksen. He ran that vessel like a church." Pell set the mug down. "And here's what I never told any skipper who fired me for saying it. The night before she sailed, I brought out the last consignment of dive gas and stores. Magnus came down the gangway himself to sign for it—him, not his mate, which was unusual. And while his mate counted tanks, Magnus walked the deck aft, alone, and stopped at the ballast controls, and stood there maybe four minutes. Just standing. Looking down into that space like a man looking into a grave he hadn't dug yet."

"So he was moody," Okafor said. "Men get moody before a hard passage."

"He was *counting*," Pell said. "I've seen men count before. You count when you're deciding whether the numbers balance. Whether what you lose is worth what you keep." He looked around the table, and his voice dropped, not for drama—for accuracy. "Eleven men went down with her. And I have thought about those four minutes every year since, and every year I come back to the same thing: the Vargr didn't sink. She was *sent* down. By her own master. And whatever he wanted at the bottom of Havsul Basin, he wanted it there more than he wanted eleven men breathing."

Silence. The wind filled it, worrying at the rigging.

"That's a ghost story," Ruiz said at last, but his voice had a hairline crack in it, the kind you only hear if you're listening for flaws. "Sailors tell them to explain drownings. It's easier than admitting the sea doesn't need a reason."

"The sea doesn't," Pell agreed. "But men do. Men always have a reason. That's the difference between us and the water."

He expected the table to break apart after that—jokes, dismissal, someone putting music on. That was the ritual. Instead, Okafor closed his knife very slowly, and Ruiz stared into his coffee like it held coordinates, and the silence stretched long enough that Pell understood he'd done something worse than tell a ghost story.

He'd told a plausible one.

It was the girl who broke it. Lena Eriksen—or whatever her name was this month; Pell had signed enough crew manifests to know a false name when he bunked next to one—set her cup down with a small precise click.

"The ballast controls," she said. Her voice was level. Too level, the way ice is level right up until it isn't. "You're sure that's where he stood."

"Aft portside. The manual flood station." Pell shrugged. "Why?"

"Because that's not where you stand to check ballast." She said it flatly, factually, a diver stating depth. "The gauges are forward. Aft is where you'd stand if you wanted to open the floods yourself and watch them work."

Pell felt the hair rise on his forearms. He'd told that detail a dozen times over the years, to a dozen dismissive faces, and no one had ever once heard what the girl had just heard—that he hadn't described a man inspecting his ship. He'd described a man operating her.

"And how would you know that?" Ruiz asked, and there was nothing casual in the question. Nothing at all.

Lena met his eyes across the table, and Pell watched her decide something—watched it happen behind her face like weather changing, fast and total. Whatever she decided, she folded it away.

"I've read the accident report," she said. "More times than is healthy."

Ruiz held her gaze a moment longer than politeness allowed. Then he nodded, slow, and looked away, and Pell saw him reach into his shirt pocket—not for anything, just touching it, the way a man touches a letter he carries but hasn't read aloud.

Okafor stood, stretched, declared the watch rotation, and the spell broke into ordinary noise. Dishes clattered. The generator hummed. The world resumed being a boat instead of a séance.

But Pell caught the girl at the companionway, just the two of them, the storm pressing its shoulder against the hull.

"You believe me," he said. Not a question.

"I believe you saw what you saw." She paused on the bottom step, half in shadow. "My father used to say the sea keeps better books than any man. That everything balances eventually."

"Your father sounds like a wise man."

Something crossed her face then—something old and tired and furious, all at once, gone before Pell could name it.

"He was a careful man," Lena Eriksen said. "Everyone says so."

Then she was gone down the passageway, and Pell stood alone with his mug and the wind, and understood, with the certainty he usually saved for weather, that he had just told a ghost story to the ghost's own daughter—and that she hadn't flinched from it.

She'd recognized it.

* * *

Pell had the midnight-to-four watch, which suited him. Night watches were for listening, and listening was the only prayer he trusted.

The storm peaked around two. The cove roared with it—wind funneling down off the granite, rain coming sideways, the Kestrel yawing at her anchor and checking herself with a groan of chain that ran through the whole hull like a shudder. Pell stood in the wheelhouse with the radar sweeping its patient green arm and let the boat argue with the weather beneath him.

At half past two, a light showed on the aft deck.

Not a deck light. A handheld torch, hooded, cupped in a palm—the kind of light a man uses when he doesn't want to be seen using it. Pell watched it move along the rail, pause at the stern, and go dark. Then, faint through the wind, the sound of the swim step deploying.

He should have woken Ruiz. Procedure said wake Ruiz. But procedure also said the person on deck might be doing nothing worse than checking the anchor, and Pell had been the man dismissed from boats for talking too much, and some instinct older than procedure told him that whatever happened in the next ten minutes would either need witnesses or need none, and he wouldn't know which until it was over.

So he watched.

The figure on the swim step was slim, hooded against the rain. It knelt, and lowered something over the side on a line—slowly, carefully, paying out meter after meter, the way you lower something you can't afford to drop and can't afford to find. Then it stayed there, motionless, kneeling in the rain above black water, for what Pell judged to be twenty minutes. Not checking gear. Not testing a light. Praying, or grieving, or listening—the way you kneel over a grave.

Then the line came up, lighter than it went down, and the figure vanished inside, and the swim step rose, and the Kestrel was just a boat again, swinging at her anchor in the dark.

Pell stood a long time in the wheelhouse, the radar sweeping, the storm shouting, and turned it over. A weighted line lowered into a sheltered cove during a storm, by a crew member who shouldn't be awake, in water far too shallow for diving and far too cold for swimming.

Unless it wasn't water she was lowering something into.

Unless it was a test. Unless the compass the girl carried—the old brass one, expedition issue, the kind Ruiz had gone quiet over at dinner—wasn't pointing at magnetic north at all, and she'd been calibrating it against the cove's known bearing, checking the needle against landmarks she could see even in rain, learning its language before she trusted it out where it mattered.

Or unless it was simpler than that. Unless she'd been saying goodbye to something. Or asking permission.

Pell didn't know. He knew only that at dawn, when Ruiz relieved him and the storm had blown itself down to a long gray grumble, he made a decision he'd made twice before in his life and regretted both times.

He kept his mouth shut.

Some stories, he'd learned, you don't tell until you understand whose they are. And the girl kneeling in the rain over the side of the Kestrel, lowering her father's secrets into the dark like a woman feeding a well—

Her story was still being written. And Pell intended, this time, to be standing close enough when the ending came.

By morning the wind had backed around and the barometer climbed, and Ruiz stood at the chart table plotting their return to the search grid, and Lena Eriksen came up on deck with her compass in her hand and her eyes already west, toward Havsul Basin, toward the place where eleven men were waiting for someone brave enough or guilty enough to ask them why.

And Pell, coiling his lines in figure-eights, watched her watch the horizon, and thought: *the tide turns today.*
