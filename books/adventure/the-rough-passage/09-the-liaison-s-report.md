## 9. The Liaison's Report

The call came at 04:12, which told Corinne Ashe everything before the liaison said a word. Brandt did not call at four in the morning with good news. Good news kept until breakfast. Only complications woke her, and complications, in her experience, were almost always other people's consciences stirring.

She took the call standing at the window of her cabin on the *Meridian Star*, watching the Norwegian dark lie flat on the water like a lid on something kept.

"Talk," she said.

Brandt's voice came through compressed and salted with static, the satellite link doing its work of making every report sound like a confession extracted under duress. He was her man aboard the *Kestrel*—officially a communications specialist, unofficially her eyes, her ledger, her patience made flesh. He had been aboard six days. He had sent her four routine reports. This was not routine.

"She went west," Brandt said.

"West is where the grid goes. I paid for the grid."

"West of the grid." A pause, the sound of him checking something, though Ashe knew he had it memorized. He was meticulous that way; it was why she kept him. "Two hundred meters past the search boundary, into the dead zone the hydrography flagged as featureless. She dove alone. No buddy, no tender standby, told Ruiz she was running a magnetometer sweep. She wasn't. The magnetometer stayed on its cradle. I checked."

Ashe turned from the window. The cabin's low light made the chart on her table look like a wound—contour lines radiating from the search grid like the rings of something drowned.

"How long down?"

"Forty-one minutes. Came up on the far side of the hull, out of sight of the deck. Ruiz chewed her out for twenty minutes and she took it like weather. Didn't argue. Didn't explain. Just said the sweep was worth it and asked to move the grid west."

"And did they?"

"Voted on it. Okafor wanted no part of it—says the western edge is a fuel trap, bad holding ground, one more storm and the anchor drags. Pell voted with Okafor because Pell votes with whoever spoke last and loudest. Ruiz broke the tie. They're shifting the grid tonight." Another pause. "Corinne, there's something else. She's been standing over the chart table at night. I've watched her do it three times. She doesn't study the grid. She studies the blank space past it. Like she's reading something that isn't printed there."

Ashe sat down slowly in the chair at her chart table and let the information settle over her the way silt settles over a hull—layer by layer, until the shape underneath was clear.

The girl knew.

Not suspected. Not hoped. *Knew.* You did not burn forty-one minutes of bottom time diving alone past a survey boundary unless you already had a reason to believe the boundary was wrong. And you did not ask to move the grid west unless you wanted the move to look like science rather than what it was—which was a woman walking, on the bottom of the Norwegian Sea, straight toward something she had no legitimate way of knowing was there.

"Describe the compass," Ashe said.

Brandt hesitated. "The brass one? She keeps it in her kit. I've seen it on the chart table."

"Look at it again. Tonight. Tell me if the needle settles north."

"You think it's rigged?"

"I think," Ashe said, "that Magnus Eriksen did not spend thirty years at sea without learning every trick of the trade, and I think he taught his daughter more of them than she admits. Look at the needle."

She ended the call and stood for a while in the dark of her cabin, and what she felt was not surprise. Surprise was for amateurs. What she felt was the particular cold satisfaction of a bet beginning to pay—the feeling she had chased her whole life across auction rooms and wreck registries and the fine print of other people's failures.

She had bought Lena Eriksen's debt for a simple reason: debt was the cheapest key ever forged. People assumed you bought debt to squeeze it, to bleed interest, to strip assets. Amateurs did that. Ashe bought debt to buy *behavior*. A woman drowning in obligations would take the job. A woman hiding under false names would obey, because obedience was the price of remaining unseen. She had built the entire arrangement on the assumption that Lena Eriksen was a fugitive, and fugitives were predictable—they ran from the past, they never ran *toward* it.

But the girl had gone west. Past the edge of the paid-for world, alone, at night, toward the blank space on the chart.

Which meant the past was not something Lena Eriksen was fleeing.

It was something she was *hunting*.

* * *

Ashe poured coffee she didn't drink and spread the original expedition file across her table, the one she had paid too much for and never regretted. She had been twenty-nine when Magnus Eriksen's consortium came to her fund looking for backing—Vargr Expedition, salvage and recovery of the *Hesper*, a wartime freighter rumored to carry sealed bullion and, more valuable to Ashe even than gold, the *Hesper*'s manifest, which named names. Ashe had understood immediately that the manifest was the real cargo. Gold was money. Names were *leverage*. She had put up a third of the financing, taken a third of the claim, and waited for the world to hand her both.

Then the *Vargr* went down with all hands but two, and the claim dissolved into maritime law, and the names stayed at the bottom of the sea, and Corinne Ashe learned the second great lesson of her career: the ocean does not honor contracts.

The first lesson she had learned earlier, from her father, a shipping magnate of modest fleet and modest honesty: *never chase the thing itself. Chase the person who knows where it is, and let them do the swimming.*

She had spent eleven years believing Magnus Eriksen was dead. The wreck said so. The inquiry said so. But inquiries said what paperwork wanted them to say, and Ashe had never trusted a document she didn't purchase personally. Now, watching the pattern assemble itself—Eriksen's daughter, Eriksen's compass, a dive past the boundary into blank water—she permitted herself the thought she had been refusing for six days:

Magnus had not lost the *Vargr*. Magnus had *spent* it.

And if he had spent it, he had spent it to bury something. And if he had buried it, he had left a way back down. Men like Magnus always did. Not out of sentiment—out of the same cold arithmetic Ashe practiced herself. You did not sink a fortune without keeping a receipt.

The compass. It had to be the compass. The daughter carried it the way other people carried ashes, and Ashe had watched her handle it on the crossing—always in the left hand, always flat, always with that small unconscious tilt, like a woman checking a watch that told a private time.

"Let her lead," Ashe said aloud to the empty cabin, and heard how the words sounded like patience and were actually appetite.

* * *

She summoned her fleet master at dawn. Halvard Renn—no relation to the Halvard of the *Vargr*'s ruined partnership, though the coincidence had amused her when she hired him—arrived with the morning weather report and the morning fuel report and the expression of a man who had learned never to ask why.

"The *Star* holds station," he said. "The *Tern* is two hours behind us. The *Gannet*—"

"Will hold back." She tapped the chart, the blank western water. "We stop pretending to search. From this tide forward, the *Tern* shadows the *Kestrel* at eight hundred meters. No closer. No lights after dark. I want to know when Eriksen dives, where she dives, and how long she stays down—and I want it without her knowing we know."

Renn studied the chart. "You think she has the location."

"I think she has something better than the location. She has a *reason*." Ashe straightened. "Do you understand the difference? A location can be bought, guessed, stolen. A reason means she's not searching. She's *returning*. And people who return to a place move differently than people who search for it. No sweeps. No grids. No wasted bottom time. They go straight to it, and they go when they think no one is watching."

"And when she finds it?"

Ashe considered the question the way she considered all questions: as a matter of sequence. "Then we find it too. Immediately. Legally. Our claim predates her contract—she's a hired hand on a survey vessel, we're a registered salvage fleet with standing in this district. Whatever she touches, we file on by nightfall." She smiled, and knew it was the smile that made lawyers nervous. "Let her do the diving, the danger, the dying if it comes to that. Let her find her father's grave and open it. And when she surfaces with her hands full, we will be standing on the water above her, holding paper."

Renn nodded, but he lingered, and Renn did not linger. "There's the other matter," he said. "The Halvard woman. Ingrid. My contact in Bergen says she's been asking about charter boats. Deep-sea rated. She's been asking about *us*, too—our route, our schedule."

Ashe went still.

Ingrid Halvard. The other daughter in this story—the daughter of the man Magnus had left holding the blame, the partner whose name had gone down in the inquiry as the incompetent one, the embezzler, the reason eleven men were dead. Ashe had followed the Halvard file for years the way you follow a wound that won't close. Halvard had died broke and disgraced two winters after the *Vargr* sank, and his daughter had grown up in the shadow of a lie, and Ashe had always assumed that lie was a wall between the girl and the truth.

But walls, Ashe knew, were also doors. It depended which side you hammered from.

"If Ingrid Halvard charters a boat," Ashe said slowly, "she's not coming to salvage. She's coming to *watch*. To see it end." She turned the thought over and found she liked its weight. "Which means she's coming to see *Lena Eriksen*. Two daughters of the *Vargr*, meeting over the wreck. Do you know what that is, Renn?"

"Trouble."

"Opportunity." Ashe folded the chart. "Trouble draws witnesses. Witnesses draw press. Press draws the coast guard, and the coast guard draws lawyers, and lawyers draw *courts*—and a court is the one arena where my paper beats everyone's passion. Let the Halvard woman come. Let her stand on the water and glare at us. Every eye on the drama is an eye not on the manifest."

Renn left. Ashe stood alone with the chart again, the blank western water, and let herself do what she did best: nothing. Not yet. The instinct of every salvage man she had ever employed was to grab—to close the hand at the first glint of metal. That instinct had bankrupted three of them and drowned one. The ocean rewarded the patient. It kept everything, and it gave things back only to those willing to wait at the surface, hands open, while the desperate did the drowning below.

Lena Eriksen would lead. She would lead because she could not help it—because the thing she was hunting was the thing she had been running from for ten years, and no one runs from a thing that hard without turning, eventually, to face it. Ashe would let her. Would feed her just enough opposition to keep the hunt honest, would let Brandt report and report and report, would let the girl spend her father's secret like inherited money, right down to the last coin.

And when the strongroom opened—because there would be a strongroom; men like Magnus Eriksen did not weld a ship's guts shut around empty water—Ashe would be there with the claim, the fleet, and the law.

She looked at the compass point on her chart where the *Kestrel* lay, a small inked cross on the blank western water, and she thought of the girl asleep aboard her, dreaming, no doubt, of her father.

"Lead, little Eriksen," Ashe said to the dark water. "I'll follow you all the way down."

* * *

The report came the next evening, and it was everything she had hoped.

Brandt's voice, low, almost reverent, as if the satellite link itself might be bugged by the dead: "She checked the compass tonight. On the chart table, under a lamp, thinking everyone was asleep. The needle doesn't settle north, Corinne. It settles west-northwest, and it does it the same way every time, like it's weighted. She held it over the chart and it pointed past the grid edge, and she wrote down coordinates and burned the paper in the galley stove." A breath. "She's diving at first light. Alone again. And Ruiz is going with her—I saw him check the dive roster and add his own name. They think I don't notice things."

Ashe stood at the window a long time after the call ended, watching the first stars come out over a sea that kept, as it always had, everything it was given.

West-northwest. She marked it on her own chart with a pencil point so fine it barely tore the paper. Then she picked up the satellite phone and gave the order she had been holding for six days, the order that would move the *Tern* into the shadow of the *Kestrel* and put her divers on standby and her lawyers on retainer.

"Let her lead," she said again.

But this time, she added the rest: "And the moment she surfaces with something in her hands—take it."
