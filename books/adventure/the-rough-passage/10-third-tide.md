## 10. Third Tide

The tide turned at 03:40, and Lena was awake before it.

She'd learned to sleep in increments aboard the *Kestrel*—twenty minutes here, forty there, her body keeping its own ledger of rest the way she kept every other account. But tonight she hadn't slept at all. She'd sat in her bunk with the compass open in her cupped hands like a small dark heart, watching the needle hold its impossible bearing toward the western edge of the grid, where the charts said nothing lived but cold water and older failures.

It wasn't magnetic. She'd proven that on the second night out, after Ruiz's look in the galley had lodged under her skin like a splinter. She'd set the compass beside Okafor's ship's compass, beside a steel wrench, beside the iron frame of her bunk. A magnetic needle would have argued with all of them. This one didn't so much as tremble. It pointed west-northwest, steady as a held breath, and when she rotated the housing the card didn't swing—it stayed fixed relative to the case itself.

Weighted. Balanced. Built.

Her father had been many things she was still learning to name, but he had never built anything without intent. The compass wasn't an instrument for finding north. It was an instrument for finding one place, and one place only—a pointer machined to hold a bearing, disguised as something a daughter could carry through customs without a second glance.

He had given it to her the summer before the Vargr sailed. She remembered that now with terrible clarity: his hands closing hers over the brass case, the salt-rough warmth of them, and the odd gravity in his voice when he'd said, *Keep this close, Lene. Things get lost on boats.* She had been nineteen and had thought it sentiment. It had been instructions.

*Third tide,* he used to say about difficult dives. *Third tide is when the water tells you what it's hiding.* She didn't know if he'd actually believed that or if it was just something he said to make waiting feel like method. Either way, she was going to find out what the water was hiding, and she was going to do it alone.

Not brave. She made herself say it plainly, sitting in the dark with the deck plates humming under her spine. Not brave. Practical. If she told Ruiz, Ruiz would tell Okafor, and Okafor would tell the liaison—because Okafor answered to the contract, and the contract answered to Corinne Ashe, and Corinne Ashe bought debts the way other people bought coffee. If she told Pell, Pell would tell the whole North Sea. And if she told no one and found nothing, then nothing had happened, and she could go back to being a woman named Marta Voss who dug other people's mud for money and wanted nothing from the past except for it to stay sunk.

But the compass knew. And so, apparently, had her father. And the space between those two facts was exactly wide enough for one diver.

* * *

She went over the side at slack water, 04:12, using the old trick of timing her entry with the generator cycle so the splash vanished into the mechanical noise. The Kestrel's running lights made a smeared yellow ceiling on the black water; below ten meters there was nothing but the green-black interior of the sea, and her own breath, loud as confession.

The plan—if you could call it a plan—was simple. Descend along the shot line they'd placed at grid marker seven, then run a heading off the compass across the silt plain to the northwest. Two hundred meters, maybe two-fifty. Bottom time fifty minutes on the tables, sixty if she lied to herself about how fast she breathed, which she intended not to do. She'd done enough solo work in enough borrowed names to know the arithmetic of dying: it always started with someone deciding one more minute wouldn't matter.

The silt plain opened beneath her like a floor in an abandoned house. Her light carved a cone through the particulate—fine grey snow that never settled, the sea's permanent dust—and beyond the cone the darkness went on with the patience of geology. She checked the compass against her wrist slate, laid the heading, and swam.

Fifty meters. Eighty. The bottom changed character without changing substance—the same silt, but rippled now in long parallel waves, as though something enormous had dragged a comb through it. Current-scour, maybe. Or wake. She thought of the Vargr going down stern-first, or bow-first, or however a ship goes down when its own captain opens its veins, and she thought of the displacement of all that water, all that weight arriving at once on this soft floor, and she kept swimming.

At a hundred and forty meters her light caught a shape.

She stopped kicking and hung in the water column, letting her momentum bleed away, because the first rule of finding things is that excitement kills more divers than cold does. The shape resolved by degrees as she drifted closer: a slope of hull plating rising out of the silt, furred with eleven years of marine growth, anemones blooming across it in pale constellations like frost flowers on a window. Steel. Riveted seams. And along the curve of the plating, half-swallowed by growth but legible to anyone who'd grown up tracing letters on her father's knee:

**VARGR — BERGEN**

Lena's breath caught and came back ragged in her own ears. She put her glove against the hull. Cold came through the neoprene like a message. Eleven years underwater and the steel was still colder than the sea around it, or so it seemed; probably it was just her hand.

*Hello,* she thought, absurdly. *Hello, old man.*

Then the awe curdled, because awe always does, and she noticed what she should have noticed first: the angle of the hull. The bow section lay separated from the rest of the wreck by nearly thirty meters of scattered debris—plating, a winch drum, a rail stanchion bent into a question mark. The Vargr hadn't simply sunk. She'd seen enough wrecks by now, under enough false names, to read the grammar of a breakup. Ships that sink intact break at their weakest points, and the weak points are predictable. This break was clean. Deliberate. As if the ship had been persuaded to come apart along lines someone had chosen in advance.

Scuttling charges, she thought. Placed in the right frames, sized correctly, timed together. That's not panic. That's engineering.

Her father had been a mediocre husband and an absent father and, she was increasingly afraid, a thief. But he had never once been sloppy.

* * *

She followed the hull line aft along the starboard side, her light walking across plating and portholes and the ghost-lettering of cargo stencils, until she reached the point where the bow section ended in a torn lip of steel—and there, set back from the tear, was a structure that did not belong to any cargo manifest she'd ever seen.

A strongroom.

Not a strongroom the way passenger ferries had strongrooms—one welded box in the purser's corridor. This was built into the forward hold like a vault set into a bank wall: a rectangular chamber perhaps four meters by three, its walls doubling the hull plating, its single door facing aft. Growth covered everything, but growth couldn't hide geometry. She swept her light along the edges and saw the reinforcement ribs, the doubled hinges—or where hinges had been—and the door itself, flush-seamed, featureless.

Welded shut.

She hung there in the green dark and felt the cold move through her that had nothing to do with the water. Because there were two ways to weld a door shut on a sunken ship, and only one of them was possible.

If the Vargr had gone down and someone had come later—salvors, looters, the partner's people, anyone—they would have cut. Cutting is what you do from outside. Every illegal salvage job she'd ever worked had left the same signature: torch marks, plasma scars, the bright raw metal where someone had opened a wreck like a tin. She had made some of those marks herself. You cut inward, always. The tool comes from outside.

These welds ran outward.

She got close—close enough that her faceplate nearly touched the steel—and traced the bead with her glove. Continuous fillet weld, laid in a single disciplined pass, the crown smooth and even, unbroken around the entire door seam. No starts and stops. No hesitation. Whoever had done it had been good, and calm, and had worked from inside the room.

From inside a compartment on a ship that was going down.

The math arrived in her chest before her head agreed to it. To weld a door shut from within, you need power—you need a welding plant, cables, electrodes, all of it hauled into a sealed space. You need time—minutes, tens of minutes, while the ship takes water around you. And when the last pass cools, you are on the wrong side of your own seam, in a steel box at the bottom of the coming sea, and there is no version of that story where you walk out.

Someone had sealed themselves in. Or someone had sealed others in. Or—

Or Magnus Eriksen had stood in the forward hold of his own sinking ship with a welding torch in his hand and made absolutely certain that whatever was behind that door would stay there until the end of the world, and then he had turned off the torch and waited for the water.

Eleven crew. The number surfaced in her mind with its faces attached, the ones she'd seen in the memorial photographs over the years: young men mostly, a couple of women, one of them barely older than she was now. Drowned in open water or drowned in compartments, she'd never asked which, because asking meant caring and caring meant the name Eriksen, and she had spent a decade not being that name.

Now she hung in front of a weld her father might have laid, and the water pressed in from all sides like a question.

*What's in the room, Papa?*

Not treasure. Treasure doesn't need this. Gold sleeps fine anywhere; it has no enemies worth a man's life. Whatever was behind that door was something that could be read, or traced, or identified—something that pointed back up the chain to whoever put it there. Documents, maybe. Ledgers. The kind of paper that turns metal into evidence.

Something he stole, whispered the thought she'd been outrunning since the dockside office. Something he stole, and killed to keep.

She backed away slowly, her bubbles rising in a silver column, and forced herself to do the professional thing, the thing Marta Voss would do, because Marta Voss was still useful even now. Photographs. She shot the hull lettering, the break line, the strongroom face, the weld bead in macro, her strobe flashing silent white pulses into the dark like a lighthouse having a seizure. Slate notes: position, depth, dimensions, heading back to the shot line. Her handwriting came out steady, which surprised her. Underneath everything, some cold clerk in her head was already filing this under *assets*, and she hated the clerk, and she was grateful to it, both at once.

Air check. A third gone. Time to go.

She took one last look at the door. Four meters of welded steel, and behind it, whatever it was, had waited eleven years in the dark with the patience of the drowned. It could wait a little longer. It had waited for her specifically—that was the thought she couldn't shake as she turned for home. The compass in her dive bag, riding against her hip like a second heartbeat, had been pointing here since before the Vargr ever sailed. Her father had put the map in her hands six months before he sealed himself into a steel box with his crime. He had known. Some part of him had known even then how it would end, and he had chosen her—not Ruiz, not the partner, not the sea—to be the one who came looking.

That was either love or the last and largest theft: taking his daughter's future and spending it on his secret. She genuinely could not tell the difference, and she suspected, with a grief that had no floor, that no one ever would.

* * *

She surfaced at 05:31, two hundred meters astern of the Kestrel, in the grey hour when the sea and the sky are the same color and neither will admit to being anything. She'd swum the last stretch on the surface, regulator out, breathing air that tasted of diesel and dawn, giving her body time to off-gas and her face time to remember how to be a face. By the time she reached the boarding ladder her expression was set to neutral, and the woman who climbed aboard the Kestrel was Marta Voss, contract diver, nothing found, mud and rust, same as yesterday.

Ruiz was on watch. Of course he was. He looked at her from the wheelhouse window with eyes that missed nothing and said less, and she nodded at him the way colleagues nod, and carried her gear below.

In the head, she locked the door and shook for ninety seconds—hands, jaw, the works—letting the fear and the awe and the horror cycle through her body like bilge through a pump. Then she washed her face, straightened, and unlocked the door.

Okafor was in the engine room, and Lena had learned that information moved through the Kestrel the way heat moves through steel: slowly, predictably, and always toward the liaison. So she gave the liaison what the liaison expected. At breakfast she complained cheerfully about the western grid—"nothing out there but sand and old rope"—and watched Brandt write it down without writing it down, the little performance of note-taking that Ashe's man thought was invisible.

Let him report nothing. Let Ashe's fleet keep sweeping the eastern grid, where the sonar ghosts were, where the search pattern said a rational salvager would look. The compass had paid for itself already, and it hadn't even cost her anything yet.

Except, she thought, rinsing her mug in the galley sink, everything. It had cost her everything, years ago, in a hold filling with black water, and she was only now receiving the bill.

Pell shuffled past her in the passageway, headed for his bunk after the night watch, and paused. He looked at her wet hair, her dry eyes, the particular stillness she was wearing.

"Find anything down there?" he asked. Not suspicious. Just a superstitious man reading weather.

"Mud," Lena said.

Pell nodded slowly. "Mud," he repeated, in the tone of a man who knows that's what the sea says right up until the day it says something else. "Third tide, eh?" And he went on toward his bunk, leaving Lena standing in the passageway with her heart hammering, wondering what else the old diver knew, and who else on this ship was keeping secrets besides her.

Below her feet, two hundred meters down and three hundred meters west, a welded door held its silence. And somewhere above the horizon, she knew without knowing how, Corinne Ashe was already turning her fleet around.
