## 11. Welded From Within

Okafor had cut into forty-one wrecks in her career and she could read steel the way Ruiz read weather. Steel remembered everything. It remembered the temperature of the water when it failed, the direction of the force that bent it, the hour of its dying. Most people thought salvage was about treasure. Okafor knew it was about forensics. Every wreck was a body, and every hull plate told you whether the death had been an accident or an argument.

She sat in the Kestrel's cramped workshop at 0400, three days after Eriksen had found the bow section, with a sample of weld bead clamped in her bench vise and a magnifying loupe screwed into her eye socket, and she did not like what the steel was telling her.

The sample had come up on the last dive — a chunk of the strongroom door frame, sheared free when Pell's cutting torch had kissed the edge and then backed off, because Pell got superstitious about cutting into sealed spaces and nobody aboard had the patience to argue him out of it that night. The Kestrel had surfaced with nothing but a look at the seam through the ROV camera: a weld line running the full perimeter of the strongroom hatch, clean and continuous, laid down by someone who knew their trade.

Ashe's liaison, a narrow man named Voss who smelled of expensive cologne and cheaper motives, had wanted them to breach it immediately. Ruiz had stalled — gas mixtures, bottom time, the storm swell still working itself out of the water column — and bought them forty-eight hours. Okafor intended to use every one of them, because something about that weld had been itching at the back of her skull since she first saw the footage, and Okafor had learned long ago never to ignore an itch in her own head. Itches were how steel talked to you before it talked to everyone else.

She turned the sample under the lamp.

Here was the thing. There were two ways a hatch gets welded shut on a wreck. The first way is after — salvage crews do it sometimes, sealing a compartment to keep silt out or to keep looters from getting somewhere first, or a navy does it to bury something embarrassing. Post-mortem welds have a signature. They're laid in open water or in a dry dock, cold metal, often sloppy, often with electrodes that don't match the parent plate. You can almost always find a start-stop crater where the welder lifted the rod, because nobody welds a perfect continuous bead around a whole hatch perimeter without stopping. Human arms get tired. Rods run out.

The second way is during. And that one was rare. That one meant the ship was going down already, or the people inside knew it was going down, and someone made a decision with a welding torch while the water climbed their shins.

Okafor rotated the sample thirty degrees and looked at the heat-affected zone along the toe of the bead.

The grain structure told her what she'd been afraid of. This weld hadn't been laid in cold seawater. The cooling pattern was wrong for it — too slow, too even, the kind of crystallization you got when the parent metal was already hot when the arc struck. Hot from fire, or from friction, or from the simple physics of a ship tearing herself apart. And there, at the margin where the weld metal met the plate, was a thin band of oxide scale that had been trapped under the bead as it ran — scale that forms on steel exposed to air at high temperature, not on steel sitting at four degrees Celsius on the seabed.

Someone had welded this hatch shut while the Vargr was still burning. Or sinking. Or both.

Okafor set down the loupe and sat back on her stool, and the little workshop felt suddenly very small around her, the way rooms do when they fill up with a fact you didn't invite.

* * *

She ran the analysis twice more because that was who she was. She sectioned a second fragment, etched it, looked at it under proper magnification instead of a loupe. She measured the hydrogen content in the weld metal, which told her the rods had been low-hydrogen, professional grade, the kind carried in heated canisters by ships that took their work seriously. She checked the electrode composition against the hull plate and found they matched — same batch, near enough, which meant the welder had used the Vargr's own repair stock. Ship's stores. A man sealing his own ship with his own materials.

By 0730 she had a page of notes written in her flat engineer's hand, and by 0800 she had read the page six times looking for another explanation, and by 0815 she had given up finding one.

There wasn't one. The strongroom of the Vargr had been sealed from within, during the sinking, by a skilled hand using the ship's own supplies. Not after. Not by salvagers, not by the coast guard, not by anyone tidying up afterward. During.

Which raised a question that Okafor, who believed in machines precisely because they didn't lie to her, found herself unable to put down:

Who welds a door shut behind themselves?

* * *

She found Ruiz in the galley, doing what he always did at this hour: drinking coffee he didn't taste and reading the tide tables like scripture. The rest of the crew was scattered — Pell up top checking the dive lines against the swell, Eriksen in her bunk or pretending to be in her bunk, Voss the liaison in the cabin Ashe had assigned him, making whatever reports narrow men make.

Ruiz glanced up. "You look like a woman who's been awake all night."

"I have been."

"Find something?"

Okafor poured coffee, didn't drink it, held the mug for the warmth. She'd rehearsed this conversation on the ladder coming down and now that she was in it, she skipped the rehearsal entirely. That was another thing she'd learned about bad news: it traveled better without packaging.

"The weld on the strongroom," she said. "It's original. Laid down during the sinking. I'd stake my ticket on it."

Ruiz set down his cup. Slowly, the way men do when they want their hands to have something to do besides shake.

"Say that again."

"Sealed from inside. While the ship was going down. The metallurgy doesn't lie, Ruiz. Heat-affected zone, oxide entrapment, cooling rates — all consistent with welding done on a vessel in distress, in ambient temperatures well above seawater. Somebody stood on the wrong side of that hatch with a torch and closed it while the Vargr died around them." She paused. "And they used ship's stock. Low-hydrogen rods from the repair locker. This wasn't improvised. It was planned."

The galley was quiet except for the hum of the refrigeration unit and, somewhere above them, Pell's boots crossing the deck plates.

Ruiz stared at the table. Okafor watched him and understood that she wasn't delivering news to him so much as confirming something he'd already suspected in some wordless part of himself for twenty years. She'd seen that look before, on faces of men who'd survived things — the look of a man being handed the physical proof of a story he'd only ever been able to feel.

"Magnus went down with his own secret," she said quietly. "That's what the steel says. He sealed it from inside. He welded his own tomb shut over whatever's in that room, and he did it carefully, professionally, with the right rods and a steady hand, while eleven other people were drowning." She let that sit. "You don't do that in a panic, Ruiz. Panic welds are ugly. That bead was beautiful. He took his time."

Ruiz's jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice was rougher than she'd ever heard it. "I was supposed to be on that ship."

"I know."

"You know because—"

"Because everybody knows, Ruiz. You've been carrying it since the day we signed on. It's why you check the manifests twice. It's why you won't let Pell dive alone." She sat down across from him. "I'm not telling you this to hurt you. I'm telling you because you need to hear it from somebody who reads steel and not from that liaison, who'll spin it however Ashe wants it spun."

"Which is how?" Ruiz asked bitterly. "That Magnus was a hero? Sealing the treasure vault to protect it?"

"That's exactly how she'll spin it if it serves her. Or the opposite, if that serves her better. Ashe doesn't care what happened on the Vargr. She cares what the room is worth." Okafor leaned forward. "But here's what I care about, and what you should care about. We came out here thinking this was salvage. Find the wreck, raise the cargo, split the money, go home. That's not what this is anymore."

"No?"

"No. Salvage is when you take things off a dead ship. What we've got is a sealed compartment that a man welded shut around himself while his crew drowned, containing cargo that half the industry believes never existed, and a woman ashore who bought our contract the way you'd buy a lottery ticket." She tapped the table once, hard. "This isn't salvage. This is an exhumation. And we should all be clear-eyed about what that means before we cut that hatch, because once it's open, it stays open. Whatever's in there — truth, treasure, both — comes up into daylight, and daylight doesn't negotiate."

Ruiz was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed, short and joyless, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"You know what the worst part is? Twenty years I've wondered if Magnus Eriksen went down fighting. If he tried to save her. If he was in the water calling for help while we sat in port listening to the mayday relay." His hands dropped. "And now you're telling me he was down in the hold with a welding torch. Calm enough to lay a clean bead."

"The steel says calm."

"Then the steel knows something I don't." He stood, crossed to the porthole, looked out at the gray water. "Halvard. You remember the name? Halvard Sørensen, Magnus's partner. Took the fall for the missing cargo manifest. Died broke in Bergen with everybody calling him a thief. His family's still living with it." He turned. "If that room holds what I think it holds, then Magnus didn't just kill eleven people. He killed a man's name and left it rotting for twenty years."

"And his daughter's out there somewhere," Okafor said, "carrying it."

Ruiz looked at her sharply. "What do you know about daughters?"

"Nothing. Why?"

He hesitated — visibly, deliberately, the hesitation of a man deciding whether to open a door — and then closed it again. "Nothing. Forget it." He drained his coffee. "We tell the crew?"

"Pell will take it badly."

"Pell takes everything badly. That's his function." Ruiz almost smiled. "Eriksen?"

Okafor considered. She thought about the way Lena Eriksen had gone quiet at the western edge of the grid, the way she dove like someone following instructions only she could hear, the way she'd looked at the sonar image of the bow section — not like a salvager seeing payday but like a woman seeing a face.

"Eriksen already knows," Okafor said. "Or she knows something adjacent to it. Watch her when we tell the others. Tell me if I'm wrong."

"You're rarely wrong about steel."

"People aren't steel."

"No," Ruiz agreed, moving toward the companionway. "People are worse. People choose."

* * *

They gathered the crew at noon, all hands in the mess except Voss, whom Ruiz excluded on the grounds of a "structural survey briefing" — technically true, in the sense that the structure in question was a tomb. Okafor laid her samples on the table between the coffee mugs and the bread tin and walked them through it in language even Pell could follow: hot weld, ship's stock, sealed during the sinking, from the inside.

Pell heard the first two sentences and went the color of old rope.

"From inside," he repeated. "Somebody was in there. In the room. And they—" He stopped, swallowed. "They stayed."

"Somebody was at the door," Okafor corrected, because precision mattered, because she couldn't give the superstition more fuel than the facts required. "Whether they were inside when it closed, the steel can't say."

"It can say," Pell said, very quietly. "You just don't want to say it."

Nobody argued with him. That was the thing about Pell — half his ghost stories were nonsense, and the other half were things nobody else had the stomach to say out loud.

Eriksen sat at the end of the table and said nothing at all. But Okafor watched her, the way she'd promised Ruiz she would, and what she saw was not shock. It was confirmation. The woman looked at the weld sample the way you look at a photograph of someone you buried years ago — grief arriving late, catching up with news the heart had processed long before.

When the briefing broke up, Eriksen lingered, turning the weld fragment over in her fingers with a strange tenderness, and Okafor heard her murmur something too low to catch. Two words, maybe three. It might have been a question. It might have been a goodbye.

Ruiz caught Okafor's eye across the mess and gave a small nod: *you were right.*

Okafor began packing her instruments, and her mind was already running ahead to the problem that mattered now, the problem that would define everything that came next: how do you breach a door that a careful man sealed with the last of his skill, knowing that whatever he wanted hidden, he wanted hidden badly enough to die for it — and knowing that somewhere behind them, a faster fleet was closing on their position with no interest at all in doing it gently?

Above her, through the deck plates, she heard boots moving fast toward the bridge, and Ruiz's voice, tight and carrying: "Get me the horizon. Now."
