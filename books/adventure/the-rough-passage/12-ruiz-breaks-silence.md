## 12. Ruiz Breaks Silence

Ruiz had been waiting three days for the right moment, and the right moment turned out to be four in the morning, when the Kestrel rolled in a long Atlantic swell and everyone aboard was either asleep or pretending to be.

He found Lena in the galley. She sat at the bolted-down table with a chart spread out in front of her and her father's compass beside it, and she didn't hear him come in because she'd wedged a towel under the door to block the light—her first mistake, and one he'd made himself, once, on a different ship with a different secret.

He pulled the towel free.

She came up out of the chart like a diver surfacing too fast, hand going for the compass, and then she saw who it was and something in her face closed like a hatch.

"Ruiz."

"First mate's privilege," he said. "I can go where I want."

"It's four in the morning."

"That's when the honest conversations happen." He dropped into the bench across from her, deliberately heavy, letting the deck's motion do some of his work for him. The chart between them was the western edge of the grid—the edge the liaison had already flagged, the edge Ashe would be asking questions about by noon. Ruiz looked at it without touching it. "You've been diving off-chart."

"I've been diving carefully."

"You've been diving alone." He let that sit. "Twice now. Third Tide, you told Pell you were checking a transponder mount. You were down ninety minutes. Yesterday you logged forty minutes and came up with silt on your suit that doesn't match anything inside the grid."

Lena's jaw tightened. She was good—he'd give her that—but he'd spent thirty years reading divers, and a diver who's found something has a particular stillness. She had it now. She'd had it since the second day.

"So?" she said. "It's my neck."

"It's my ship." He said it flat, no heat, and watched her recalibrate. Good. She'd thought of him as crew, as background, as the man who ran the deck and kept the paperwork straight. Time to correct the file. "Ashe bought this run, but I signed the safety plan, and every hour you spend down there alone is an hour I answer for if you don't come back up. So before I decide whether to log it or lose it, you're going to tell me what you found."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I log it, and the liaison gets a very detailed report, and Corinne Ashe learns everything I know about you." He paused. "And I know more than you think."

That landed. He saw it land—a small shift behind her eyes, the kind of movement you only catch if you're watching for it.

* * *

He'd been watching since the first night.

The compass was the thing. A Vargr expedition issue, brass-cased, serial-stamped—Ruiz had handled two hundred of them in another life, had handed them out himself from a crate in Magnus Eriksen's cabin while the old man joked about never getting lost twice. He'd recognized it across the Kestrel's deck the day Lena boarded, before she'd said ten words, and he'd said nothing because saying nothing was a skill he'd perfected.

But he'd started keeping notes. Old habit. Date, time, observation, no conclusions. A page in the back of his maintenance log where nobody looked:

*Day 2 — E. carries Vargr-issue compass. Won't let anyone hold it.*

*Day 4 — E. studies western grid after hours. Asked Okafor about hull-cutting rates "hypothetically."*

*Day 6 — E. dives west edge, unlogged. Returned shaken. Hid it well. Not well enough.*

*Day 7 — Eriksen. Has to be. The way she stands at the rail is his stance. God help us all.*

He hadn't written that last line until last night, and writing it had felt like opening a door he'd welded shut himself, decades ago, from the inside.

Now he sat across from her in the galley's yellow light and took the folded paper from his jacket—not the notes, not yet—and laid it on the chart between them.

"What's that?"

"Read it."

She hesitated. Then she unfolded it, and he watched her face do what faces did when they met a ghost: the recognition first, the denial right behind it, the slow collapse of both into something rawer.

It was a letter fragment. One page, torn, water-stained brown at the edges, the ink faded to the color of old blood. Halvard's handwriting—she wouldn't know that, but Ruiz did, had seen it on manifests and requisitions for a year of his life. The fragment read:

*—cannot reconcile the manifest against the recovery logs. Forty-one items catalogued ashore that were never lifted from the site, or lifted and never logged. Magnus says the discrepancy is clerical. I have done the clerical work myself, twice. There is no clerical explanation. If these pieces surface on the private market I will have no choice but to take this to the insurers, whatever it costs me—*

It ended there. Torn.

Lena read it twice. Her hands were steady, which surprised him; his hadn't been, the first hundred times he'd read it.

"Where did you get this?" she asked. Her voice was careful. Too careful.

"The night the Vargr sailed," Ruiz said. "I was there."

* * *

Bergen, nineteen years ago. Rain like nails. He'd been twenty-six and hungry, the newest hand on the best-funded salvage outfit on the coast, and Magnus Eriksen had treated him like a son—or like what Ruiz had imagined a son got treated like, having no basis for comparison.

They'd loaded through the night. He remembered the crates going aboard under tarps, heavier than the manifest said they should be, and he remembered asking, and he remembered Magnus laughing and clapping his shoulder and saying, *You count what I tell you to count, Tomas, and someday you'll own a boat bigger than mine.* He remembered believing it. That was the part that still shamed him—not the loading, not the silence. The believing.

And then, two hours before departure, Magnus had called him into the cabin. No anger. That was the terrible thing, how calm it had been. Magnus had slid an envelope across the desk—severance, triple rate, cash—and said, "You're off the manifest. Take the morning train home. See your mother."

"My mother's dead," Ruiz had said.

"Then see someone. Anyone. Just don't be on my boat tomorrow." And when Ruiz had stood there gaping, Magnus had looked at him with something that wasn't quite kindness and wasn't quite threat and said, quietly: "Tomas. I'm asking you as a favor. Don't make me ask differently."

He'd taken the envelope. He'd taken the train. And two days later the Vargr went down with eleven souls, and the coast guard interviewed everyone who'd ever touched the operation, and Tomas Ruiz sat in a fluorescent room and said: I left the crew over a pay dispute. I know nothing. I saw nothing.

The envelope had lasted eight months. The sentence had lasted nineteen years.

"I told myself it was grief," he said to Lena now. "For years. Grief makes liars of everyone, and I'd earned mine honestly—I loved that man. But grief doesn't keep letters in a waterproof bag taped under a floorboard for two decades. Guilt does that. Guilt keeps receipts."

"You stole Halvard's letter?"

"Halvard gave it to me." Ruiz leaned forward. "Three days before the sailing. He came to my boarding house drunk, which he never was, and put it in my hands and said, 'If anything happens to me, this needs to survive.' I thought he was being dramatic. Salvage men are dramatic. Then he died, and Magnus died, and eleven other men died, and I understood that Halvard hadn't been dramatic. He'd been precise. He knew exactly what was coming, and he knew he couldn't stop it, so he planted a seed in the most junior person he could find who might outlive it."

Lena's eyes hadn't left the fragment. "Why didn't you go to the police? The insurers? Anyone?"

"With what? Half a letter accusing a dead man of theft, written by another dead man, held by a crewman who'd taken hush money?" He shook his head. "I was twenty-six and cowardly and the letter named no crime precisely enough to hang. And there was—" He stopped. Started again, because she deserved the whole ugly shape of it. "There was also the fact that if Magnus was guilty, then everyone who loved him was a fool, and I preferred being a grieving orphan to being a duped accomplice. People choose the story that lets them sleep. I chose mine for nineteen years."

"And now?"

"Now you showed up on my deck with his compass and his way of standing and his habit of deciding things for other people's own good." He tapped the chart, the western edge, the place she'd been diving in secret. "So here's how I see it, Eriksen. Either you tell me what you found out there, or I decide for you. And I promise you I learned decision-making from the worst possible teacher."

* * *

She was quiet a long time. The Kestrel groaned around them, that language old ships spoke in their sleep, and somewhere above them Pell turned over in his bunk and muttered at a dream.

"He scuttled her," Lena said finally. "The strongroom is welded shut—from the inside. Okafor confirmed the welds are original. My father sealed the evidence in with his own hands and took the ship down on top of it." She looked up, and Ruiz saw his own nineteen years looking back at him out of her face. "Eleven people, Ruiz. He killed eleven people to bury a theft."

"I know."

"You *know*?" Her voice cracked upward. "How long have you known?"

"Known-suspected. There's a difference, and I hid inside it for most of my life." He held her gaze. "I suspected the theft from the night I read that letter. I suspected the scuttling from the day the wreck report came back—engine intact, hull breached aft in a pattern the investigators called anomalous and I called deliberate. But suspicion isn't knowledge, and knowledge without proof is just a heavier kind of cowardice. So I carried the letter and kept my mouth shut and took jobs on safe ships and told myself the sea had already punished everyone who needed punishing."

"And now you want absolution from *me*?" She laughed, and it was an awful sound, young and old at once. "You want the daughter to forgive the witness?"

"No." He said it plainly. "I don't want forgiveness. I want a partner. There's a difference too, and it matters more."

He turned the letter fragment toward her, square in the middle of the chart.

"Think about what we're holding between us, you and me. You have the wreck. I have the document. You have his blood; I have his orders ringing in my ears. Neither of us can finish this alone—you can't prove anything from a welded box without a paper trail, and I can't prove anything from a paper trail without the box. Ashe has money and lawyers and a liaison who writes down everything. Ingrid Halvard has righteous fury and a charter boat. We have each other and a half-page of dead man's handwriting." He shrugged. "I've seen worse odds. I sailed under your father."

"Don't—" Her hand came down flat on the table. "Don't call him that like he's mine to defend."

"Then whose is he?" Ruiz asked softly. "Because that's the real question, isn't it? Nineteen years you've spent hiding from his name, and now you're diving his wreck alone at four in the morning, and I need to know which one you're doing. Avenging him? Or burying him?"

Lena looked at the letter. At the compass. At the space between them where a father should have been.

"I don't know," she said, and it was the first honest thing she'd given him, and it cost her visibly. "I built him out of nothing, Ruiz. A hero father, lost at sea, wronged by history. I lived inside that story for ten years because the alternative was living inside *this* one." She pressed her palm flat over the fragment, over Halvard's faded accusations. "And now the alternative has a body count."

"Welcome aboard," Ruiz said. "That's the same boat I've been rowing since Bergen."

Something moved through her then—not softening exactly, but recognition. Two orphans of the same lie, circling each other in a yellow-lit galley, finally close enough to touch. He watched her decide. It happened in her shoulders first, the way it always does with divers: the body commits before the mouth does.

"The strongroom's at frame sixty-two, port side, under the collapsed cargo deck," she said. "I can get us in through a stress fracture in the plating, but it'll take cutting gear and two divers minimum, and we'd have maybe twenty minutes of bottom time before the liaison notices we're off-station." She looked up. "Okafor and Pell. Can you sell them a reason?"

"I can sell Okafor the truth and Pell a ghost," Ruiz said. "Pell's been telling the crew the Vargr's haunted since day one. Turns out the superstition's the closest thing on this ship to a factual account." He began folding the letter away, then stopped, and gave her the last piece, because partners didn't ration. "One more thing. Ashe funded the original expedition. I found the routing in the old insurance filings—money laundered through three holding companies, but it traces back to her family. Whatever's in that strongroom, she's wanted it buried or owned for twenty years. When we open it, we're not stealing from her. We're taking back evidence."

Lena absorbed that. Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, the false dawn that meant the liaison would be awake within the hour, coffee in hand, notebook open, counting.

"Then we move tonight," she said. "Fifth tide."

"Fifth tide," Ruiz agreed, and stood, and at the doorway he paused with his hand on the frame—the same gesture, he realized too late, that Magnus had used leaving that cabin in Bergen, and he hated his own body for remembering.

"Lena. When we open it—whatever's in there, whoever it convicts—it goes to Halvard's daughter first. Before Ashe, before the insurers, before us. That's the price of my help. Sworn."

She met his eyes across the galley, and he watched her weigh it, and he watched her understand that he wasn't testing her. He was paying his own debt, the only way left, nineteen years late.

"Sworn," she said.

Above them, the Kestrel's wake stretched back toward the grid's western edge, toward the welded dark, toward everything they'd spent their lives not saying—and ahead, faint on the horizon where the grey water met the greyer sky, a second set of running lights had appeared that hadn't been there at midnight.

Ingrid Halvard was coming.
