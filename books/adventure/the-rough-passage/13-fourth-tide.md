## 13. Fourth Tide

The Kestrel's engine note changed at 0400, and Lena woke knowing why before she reached the wheelhouse.

She'd learned the ship's voice the way you learn a language—by immersion, by mistake, by lying awake in a bunk that smelled of diesel and other people's coffee. The Kestrel hummed when she was happy, knocked when the swell came from the northeast, and went thin and strained when Okafor pushed her harder than he liked to admit. This morning she was straining. Someone had opened her up.

Ruiz was at the helm when Lena climbed the ladder, a mug of something gray in his hand and his eyes on the horizon like it owed him money.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since two." He didn't look at her. "Three hulls. Same formation as yesterday, but closer. She's done pretending."

Lena found them without effort once she knew where to look. Gray shapes against a gray sea, running lights doused, holding station off the Kestrel's starboard quarter with the patience of animals that had learned exactly how fast their prey could go. Ashe's fleet. Two survey vessels and something longer and lower that carried too many men for its tonnage.

"She could have taken us in the night," Lena said.

"She could have taken us any time this week." Ruiz sipped his gray coffee. "That's what I keep asking myself. What's she waiting for?"

The answer arrived at 0630, riding a tender that cut across the swell with the arrogance of purchased authority: a coast guard ensign flying from a boat whose crew wore civilian jackets under their life vests, and at the bow, holding the rail like a woman posing for a photograph, the liaison—Marek, Ashe called him, though Lena doubted it was the name on whatever documents had made him official.

"Inspection," Marek announced from the water, voice lifted over the engines. "Environmental compliance and salvage licensing. Standard procedure."

"Standard," Ruiz repeated, flat as ballast.

Okafor came up from below wiping his hands on a rag that had been white once, took one look at the tender, and said nothing at all—which was, Lena was learning, the loudest thing the engineer ever said. Pell appeared last, hovering at the rail with his fingers working a knot of string, his private rosary, muttering the numbers of the dead like a man counting change.

"They bought an inspection," Lena said quietly.

"They bought *the* inspection." Ruiz set down his mug. "There's only one patrol boat within eighty miles, and I've drunk with its commander. He's honest. His fuel budget isn't. Somebody's covering his operating costs this month, and we both know who signs those checks."

* * *

They let the boarding party up the ladder because there was no version of refusing that ended well. Four men in mismatched gear, one carrying a clipboard with the solemnity of scripture, Marek behind them wearing the expression of a man attending a funeral he'd personally arranged.

"Captain Ruiz." Marek smiled with all the warmth of a laminated badge. "You'll forgive the intrusion. Regulations are regulations."

"Of course." Ruiz's voice could have chilled beer. "Manifests are in the wheelhouse. Dive logs are current. Waste discharge records are—" he paused, savoring it, "—boring beyond belief."

"I'm sure they are." Marek's eyes moved past him, across the deck, and settled on Lena with the unhurried precision of a man who had already found what he was looking for and was now simply enjoying the walk toward it. "Ms. Voss. Or should I say—"

"Lena Voss is fine," she said. "It's the name on my certification. You checked it yourself when I signed aboard."

"I did." He drifted closer, hands clasped behind his back, a tourist admiring the view. "Interesting career, Ms. Voss. So many contracts. So many harbors. You get around."

"I go where the work is."

"Don't we all." He stopped beside her, close enough that she could smell his cologne fighting a losing war against salt air. "Ms. Ashe sends her regards. And a question."

The deck seemed very still around them. Pell's fingers had stopped moving on his string. Somewhere below, Okafor's compressor had gone silent, which meant Okafor was listening.

"The grid," Marek said. "Your search pattern. It's been drifting west for three days. Off-specification. Inefficient. Ms. Ashe wonders why."

"Because the seabed out here doesn't match the survey data," Lena said. "Sediment drift from the shelf current. We're chasing where the wreck *should* be before we commit to where it might actually be." She heard herself say it—smooth, technical, bored—and felt something cold turn over in her chest. "I can send your office the bathymetric overlays if you'd like. They're tedious."

Something flickered in Marek's face—the small frustration of a man offered more paperwork than he wanted. "And the western edge? Your night dives?"

"Calibration," Lena said. "Bottom-reference checks. The compass variation up here runs wide; if we don't correct nightly, every fix we take is garbage by morning." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her nothing, and that was the part that frightened her. "Ask your own surveyors. It's standard practice above sixty degrees."

Marek studied her for a long moment. Then he smiled again, thinner now, and produced a folded sheet from inside his jacket—not a document, just paper, but paper with weight. "Ms. Ashe also wonders about your father."

The gulls were loud suddenly. The sea kept doing what the sea does, indifferent and enormous.

"Magnus Eriksen has been dead eleven years," Lena said. "If she wants to ask him questions, she'll need better divers than yours."

"He never recovered the body," Marek said softly. "Funny thing about that. A man lost at sea, no wreckage confirmed, no remains. Eleven years, and the sea keeps his secrets." He tucked the paper away. "Ms. Ashe is a patient woman, Ms. Voss. But patience is expensive. She asked me to remind you that debts accrue interest, and so do loyalties. Whichever you're paying with."

He turned before she could answer, clapped Ruiz on the shoulder hard enough to be an insult, and led his men back down the ladder. The tender pulled away trailing diesel rainbow. Within twenty minutes the three gray shapes had fallen back to their patient distance, and the Kestrel was alone again on a sea that felt smaller than it had at dawn.

* * *

Ruiz waited until the wheelhouse door was shut before he spoke.

"Compass variation," he said. "Nightly calibration dives."

"It sounded professional."

"It sounded like something Magnus would have said." He turned from the window, and his face had the look Lena had learned to read carefully—not anger, not quite, but the memory of it, worn smooth like a stone he carried in his pocket. "I stood on a dock in Tromsø eleven years ago and listened to that man explain tide tables to me like I was a child. He lied the way other people breathe. Effortless. Charming. You never caught the lie until weeks later, when you were already somewhere else, already committed." His eyes held hers. "Where did you learn to talk like that, Ms. Voss?"

The question sat between them. Lena thought about the truth—that she'd learned it in rented rooms under borrowed names, in harbor offices from Bergen to Reykjavik, smiling at clerks while her real self stood behind her shoulder holding its breath. That she'd been lying since she was nineteen and it had always felt like drowning.

What she said was: "My mother taught me to sail. My father taught me charts. Nobody taught me this. I taught myself."

Ruiz held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly, and she couldn't tell whether he believed her or had simply decided to file her answer next to all the others he didn't believe. "The western edge," he said instead. "Whatever you're actually doing out there—you have until the fifth tide. After that, even I can't stall her."

"You've been stalling her?"

"I've been filing paperwork," Ruiz said, and for the first time in four days, almost smiled. "In triplicate. There are forms for everything in Norwegian waters, and I intend to complete every single one correctly. It's the most subversive thing an honest man can do."

* * *

That evening Lena sat alone in the chart room with the door dogged shut and both truths spread out in front of her.

On the table: the official grid, the one Marek had seen, annotated in her own hand with the false logic of sediment drift and current shear—a fiction built to survive scrutiny, every line defensible, every number plausible. She'd constructed it the way her father must have constructed his whole expedition, layer by careful layer, each lie load-bearing.

Beneath it, folded into the chart tube where no inspector would think to look: the true coordinates. Not written—she hadn't dared write them—but memorized, burned in, recited nightly like Pell's string-knot litany. Depth forty-one meters. Bearing off the shoal at the western edge. The place where a weighted needle pointed down into the dark and told her, in her father's dead hand, *here*.

She memorized them again anyway. Fourth repetition. Fifth. She closed her eyes and walked the numbers like a corridor in a house she'd never lived in, and when she opened them, the chart room lamp threw her shadow huge against the bulkhead, and the shadow looked, for one unsteady second, like a man's.

"There it is," she said aloud, to nobody. "There's the family talent."

Because that was the thing she couldn't wash off, the thing the salt water wouldn't touch: it had been *easy*. Marek had stood three feet away with bought authority and bought questions, and she had fed him sediment drift and compass variation, and her pulse hadn't risen, her hands hadn't shaken, and some quiet competent part of her had even enjoyed it—the architecture of it, the fit of lie against lie, the little click when the story locked together and bore weight. Ten years of hiding, she'd told herself. Ten years of shame. But hiding was just lying with better posture, and the Eriksen gift had been in her blood all along, waiting, patient as the wreck itself.

Her father had scuttled a ship and eleven lives behind a wall of plausible words. She had scuttled nothing yet. But she recognized the blueprints now. They were hers by inheritance, drawn into her the way the compass's needle was drawn down—weighted, deliberate, pointing at one specific truth.

She hated how easily it came. She hated more that tomorrow she would need it again.

Pell knocked once and put his head in. "Skipper says weather window holds till morning. Fifth tide comes early." His eyes went to the chart, to her face, back to the chart. Whatever superstition lived in the man, it wasn't stupid. "You going to tell us what's out west, miss? Before the sea tells us first?"

Lena folded the false grid closed over the true coordinates and met his eyes.

"At dawn," she said. "All hands. Everything."

It was time, she decided, to stop being the only liar on board.
