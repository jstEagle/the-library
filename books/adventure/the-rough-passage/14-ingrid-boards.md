## 14. Ingrid Boards

The tender was never meant for open water.

Ingrid Halvard had told the charter man she wanted something small and quick, and he'd given her a RIB with a cracked windshield and an engine that coughed on cold starts. She'd paid cash for three days of it and asked no questions, which he'd clearly appreciated, because men who ask no questions get boats that answer none either. Now, forty minutes out from the shelter of the sound, the Kestrel sitting gray and patient on the horizon like a headstone that hadn't been carved yet, Ingrid gripped the tiller and thought about how her father would have laughed at this boat. Halvard Jørgen had loved proper vessels. He'd kept models of them on the shelf above his desk, each one named in his careful hand, and when he drank too much—which was most nights, toward the end—he'd tap their hulls with one finger and tell the room about the Vargr as if it were still afloat somewhere, still salvageable, still worth saving.

It wasn't the ship he'd meant to save. Ingrid had understood that by the time she was fourteen. It was himself.

She checked the bearing again against the coordinates she'd copied from her father's letters—the ones the anonymous tip had confirmed, folded into an envelope with no return address, typed on a machine that smelled faintly of salt even through paper. *They're diving your father's disgrace,* the note said. *Grid 7-West. Ask yourself who profits.* She'd sat with it for two days before she chartered anything, because two days was how long it took her to stop hearing the accusation in it and start hearing the invitation. Someone wanted her here. That should have frightened her. Instead it felt like being handed a key to a door she'd been pressing her palm against for twenty years.

The sea ran short and choppy, the kind of water that looked harmless and wasn't. Ingrid had grown up on this coast. She knew its moods the way other people knew a difficult relative's—intimately, unfondly, without illusion. Her father had taught her to read it before he taught her to read books. *Watch where the wind has been, not where it says it's going.* She steered into the old wind and let the new one argue behind her.

The Kestrel grew as she closed: a survey vessel, mid-sized, work-worn, flying no flag she could read at this distance. A crane arm folded along its deck like a sleeping bird. There were figures at the rail. One of them raised something—a hand, or binoculars—and Ingrid felt the particular loneliness of being watched by strangers who had power over you and hadn't decided yet whether to use it.

Her father had known that feeling too. He'd lived inside it for eleven years after the Vargr went down, until his heart gave out in a chair by the window with a half-finished letter on his lap, and Ingrid had found him there and read the letter before anyone else could take it from her.

*If they ever come back for it, Inger, remember: I signed nothing. Whatever they say I agreed to, I signed nothing.*

She'd spent twenty years wondering what *it* was. Today she intended to find out.

* * *

The Kestrel didn't try to run. That surprised her. She'd rehearsed the chase in her head all morning—the tender surging, the big ship wallowing, voices shouting across water—but instead the survey vessel simply slowed, turned its beam to the swell to give her a lee, and waited. Professional courtesy, or a trap. With money involved, Ingrid had learned, the two were often the same thing.

She came alongside with more force than she intended. The fender she'd rigged—a fender borrowed, like everything else about this trip—compressed between the hulls with a rubber groan, and then hands were reaching down for hers, and she was climbing a ladder bolted to steel that stank of diesel and old salt, and then she was standing on the deck of a ship that had nothing to do with the Vargr and everything to do with it anyway, because the Vargr was down there, under her feet, under forty fathoms of cold green dark, exactly where her father's letters said it would be if anyone ever got greedy enough to look.

Four people faced her. A big man with engineer's forearms and a face like a closed ledger. A younger one, wiry, superstitious-looking in the way sailors get when they've seen one thing too many. A woman with gray at her temples and the posture of someone who'd spent years giving orders on decks—first mate, Ingrid guessed, or captain. And behind them, half a step back, a fourth woman, brown-haired, unremarkable, whose eyes were doing something the others' weren't.

The others were looking at Ingrid. The fourth woman was looking at Ingrid's reaction to the ship.

"Ma'am," the gray-haired woman said. "You came alongside us at speed in an open tender. Want to tell us why?"

"I want to know," Ingrid said, "what you're diving."

Silence, of the specific texture that means everyone already knows the answer. The engineer shifted his weight. The wiry one glanced at the woman beside him.

"This is a contracted survey," the gray-haired woman said carefully. "Hydrographic work."

"Then you won't mind telling me the contract holder." Ingrid held up her phone, the tip message already open on the screen. "Because I received this four days ago. Grid seven-west. And grid seven-west is where the Vargr sank in March of—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she hated it, "—twenty-two years ago. Eleven men died there. My father was blamed for it. So before anyone says *hydrographic* to me again, someone is going to say the word *salvage*, and they're going to say it honestly."

The wind moved across the deck. Somewhere below, machinery turned over and settled into its drone.

"You're Halvard's daughter," the gray-haired woman said quietly. Not a question.

"Ingrid Halvard. Yes." She made herself stand straighter. "And you are?"

"Ruiz. First mate." Something passed over Ruiz's face—recognition, maybe, or the memory of recognition—and Ingrid filed it away. "You should talk to our employer about the contract. Corinne Ashe. We work for her, not the other way around."

"Then put me through to Corinne Ashe."

"That's not—" Ruiz stopped. Started again. "That's not how this works."

"It's exactly how this works," Ingrid said. "You're diving my father's disgrace. You're dragging up the worst thing that ever happened to my family and selling it by the kilo, and you want me to believe there's a procedure? There is no procedure. There's only what people do when they think no one is watching. Well." She spread her hands. "I'm watching now."

It was then that the fourth woman stepped forward.

* * *

Up close she was younger than Ingrid had first taken her for—early thirties, maybe, though the eyes were older, the way eyes get when someone has spent a decade practicing not being seen. She had rope-scarred hands and a diver's pallor and she stood the way her father used to describe certain crewmen standing: balanced for a deck that might turn on you.

"You said eleven men died there," the woman said.

Ingrid turned to her. "Yes."

"And your father took the blame."

"My father was made to take the blame. There's a difference, and I've had twenty years to learn every syllable of it." Ingrid studied her. "Why do you ask?"

"Because the expedition had two names on its founding papers." The woman's voice was level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Magnus Eriksen and Halvard Jørgen. Partners. When the Vargr went down, the inquiry couldn't reach Magnus—he'd gone to ground, vanished, left nothing but debts and a daughter. So it all landed on Halvard. Negligence. Falsified manifests. The works."

The deck seemed to tilt, though the sea was calm. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've read the same files you have." A pause. "Probably the same copies."

"Who are you?" Ingrid asked, and heard her own voice go thin.

The woman looked out at the water for a moment. When she turned back, her jaw was set like someone stepping off a ledge.

"Lena," she said. "Lena Eriksen."

The name went through Ingrid like cold water through a wetsuit. Twenty years of it—of the name spoken in bars by old salvagers, spat in court transcripts, whispered by her mother like a curse she couldn't stop repeating—twenty years of *Eriksen* meaning the man who ran, the man who hid, the man who let Halvard drown in blame because drowning in blame was safer than drowning at sea. And here it was, standing on a deck, wearing a diver's hands, looking at her with something that was almost—almost—apology.

"You," Ingrid said. "You're his."

"Yes."

"He let my father hang for what he did. He disappeared. He let us lose everything—the house, the name, all of it. My father drank himself to death reading letters to a partner who was never coming back to answer them." Her voice rose and she let it; there was no one out here to hear but gulls and the guilty. "And now you're diving his wreck for a woman who bought your debt, and you have the nerve to stand on this deck and say your own name to me like it costs you something."

"It does," Lena said.

"It should cost you more than that."

"Maybe." Lena didn't flinch, and Ingrid realized, with a strange sinking feeling, that she recognized the not-flinching. She'd seen it in mirrors. In her own shoulders, squared against twenty years of other people's pity. This was what happened to daughters of the Vargr: they learned to hold still while the past hit them. "Maybe it should. But I'm not here to trade apologies. I'm here because I know things about that wreck that aren't in any file, and some of them are going to help your father's name, and some of them are going to hurt it, and you deserve to be in the room when both happen. That's the only reason I'm saying my name out loud. Not because it's owed. Because it's useful."

"Honest, at least," Ruiz murmured.

"Shut up, Ruiz," Lena said, without heat.

Ingrid looked from her to Ruiz to the engineer, who was watching the exchange with the expression of a man watching weather change. The wind had picked up. The tender knocked against the Kestrel's hull, patient, ridiculous, her whole plan reduced to a rented boat and a borrowed fender.

"What things," Ingrid said finally. "What things do you know?"

Lena reached into her jacket and brought out a compass. Old brass, dented, a lanyard worn smooth by a thumb. Ingrid knew it instantly—not the object itself, but the type, the make, the expedition issue her father had described a hundred times, the one item every founding member carried and none of them were supposed to keep.

"That's a Vargr compass," she said.

"It was my father's." Lena held it flat on her palm. "It doesn't point north. It points at the strongroom."

"The what?"

"There's a compartment welded shut inside the wreck. Welded from the inside, Okafor thinks—before she went down. My father sealed it himself, with himself and eleven others around it." Lena's voice stayed level but her thumb had gone white against the brass. "Whatever's in there, he killed a ship to keep it buried. And whatever he stole to fill it, your father was blamed for losing. I think the proof of both is in that room. His theft and his confession, welded together."

The sea noise filled the silence. Ingrid became aware that her hands were shaking and made them stop.

"You're telling me my father was innocent."

"I'm telling you I don't know yet. I'm telling you there's a room down there that will say." Lena closed her fist around the compass. "Ashe wants the cargo. I want the truth. You want your father's name back. Those three things are pointing at the same piece of steel, and we have maybe two tides before Ashe decides she doesn't need any of us to be honest about why."

"And you're telling me this," Ingrid said slowly, "because?"

"Because you'd have found out anyway. Because you have a right to it that I'll never have. And because"—Lena hesitated, and for the first time looked, briefly, like someone's daughter instead of someone's ghost—"because when we open it, I don't want to be the only one down there carrying his name."

Ingrid thought of her father's last letter. *I signed nothing.* She thought of the shelf of model ships, each hull tapped by a drunk finger, each one a vessel that had never sailed anywhere except away. She thought of the anonymous tip, and understood suddenly, completely, who had sent it—who else could have known the coordinates, who else would want a witness on this deck who couldn't be bought.

Someone had wanted the two of them facing each other. Well. Here they were.

"All right," Ingrid said. "I stay aboard. I see what's in that room, or I go to the coast guard tonight and every newspaper tomorrow, and Ashe can explain to them why her survey vessel is hovering over a grave site. Your choice."

Ruiz made a sound that might have been a laugh. "She'll fit right in."

Lena looked at her for a long moment—assessing, Ingrid realized, the way you assess a line before trusting it with your weight. Then she nodded once.

"Welcome aboard the Kestrel, Ingrid Halvard." She turned toward the wheelhouse, then paused. "One condition of mine. When we breach that strongroom—you read the manifest first. Before me. Before Ashe. Whatever my father stole, your family sees it first."

"Why?"

"Because debt gets paid to whoever's holding it," Lena said. "And yours is the oldest."

Below them, forty fathoms down, the Vargr waited with its welded door and its eleven dead, and somewhere beyond the horizon, Ingrid had no doubt, Corinne Ashe was counting tides.
