## 15. The Confession Cylinder

The fifth tide came in gray and windless, the sea lying flat as poured metal, as if it too were waiting to see what we'd pull out of it.

We'd spent the fourth tide cutting. Okafor ran the plasma rig like a surgeon who hated his patient—slow, precise lines through the welded seam of the strongroom door, forty-one meters down on the Vargr's lower deck. The welds had been done in a hurry but done well, beads laid by someone who knew the work mattered more than his own breathing. Every pass of the cutter ate through steel Magnus Eriksen had sealed with his own hands, and I stood watch at the surface station and felt each meter of the cut like a stitch being pulled out of me.

Now the door hung open by a hand's width, propped with a spreader bar, and Ruiz said, "You don't have to be first."

"I do," I said.

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing with me somewhere around the third tide, when he'd shown me the letter fragment and I'd shown him my father's compass and we'd stood in the Kestrel's engine room like two people at a graveside neither of them was sure they had a right to mourn at. Since then he'd just looked at me sometimes, that old man's look, measuring how much of me was Magnus and how much was something else. I still didn't know the answer myself.

Pell checked my gear twice, then a third time without being asked. "Strongroom's dead air down there," he said. "Forty years sealed. You crack it wide, anything loose comes out at you—silt, cargo, God knows. Don't take your eyes off your line."

"Copy."

"And Lena." He hesitated, turning his knife over in his fingers the way he did when superstition and sense were fighting for the wheel. "Whatever's in there. It's been waiting a long time. It can wait ten more minutes for you to breathe right."

It was the closest thing to a blessing the Vargr's ghost would get from him, and I took it.

* * *

The water at forty-one meters was cold enough to feel like an opinion. My lights carved two pale cones into the dark and the dark gave back rust, rust, rust—the Vargr's bones furred orange, deck plates soft as old bread, everything wearing four decades of the sea's slow digestion. I'd dived wrecks on three continents under five names. I'd never dived one that was mine.

The corridor to the strongroom was choked with collapsed conduit. I went through sideways, exhaling carefully, and then the doorway was in front of me and the spreader bar held the gap and beyond it was blackness so complete my lights seemed to bend around it.

I put my head through the gap.

The silt came first—a slow gray bloom rising off everything, blinding for thirty seconds, my own heartbeat loud in my ears while Pell's warning played back: *it can wait ten minutes.* Then the water began to clear, and the strongroom showed itself.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked in tiers against the bulkheads, lashed down with webbing gone brittle as autumn leaves. Stenciled markings still legible on the near ones: *VARGR EXPEDITION — CATALOG 4 — HANDLE AS ARCHIVAL.* And beneath that, in smaller letters, the name that had started all of this and ended eleven lives:

*HALVARD.*

I drifted along the nearest tier. One crate had split along its seam during the sinking or the years after, and through the gap I could see packing straw, and in the straw the curve of something bronze—a shoulder, maybe, or the rim of a vessel, green with age, beautiful even half-glimpsed in a dying light. Catalog 4. The finds from the Skerries site, the recovery that had made the expedition famous before it made it infamous. The finds Halvard had supposedly sold off and pocketed the proceeds from. The finds that had followed him to his grave in disgrace while Magnus Eriksen sailed away a martyr.

They'd never left the ship. They'd been here the whole time, forty meters down, lashed in their crates, while a man rotted under a lie and another man became a legend on top of it.

My hands were shaking inside my gloves. Not cold. Something older than cold.

Because there it was, plain as the stencil: my father hadn't lost the cargo in the sinking. He'd locked it away. He'd carried it down with him deliberately, every crate, every piece, and welded the door behind him—and if the cargo was here, then the theft wasn't Halvard's. It had only ever been one man's.

I found the cylinder on the second tier, wedged upright between two crates in a bracket that had clearly been fabricated for it—welded in place, like everything else. Brass, thirty centimeters long, threaded cap, and etched around its circumference in a neat engineer's hand:

*M. ERIKSEN — FOR THE RECORD.*

For the record. As if the sea were a court. As if drowning were a notary.

I worked it free of the bracket. It was heavier than it looked, weighted inside, and when I turned it in my light I saw the cap had a small glass port, and behind the port, rolled tight, paper. Real paper, waxed against the water, and it had stayed dry for forty years because Magnus Eriksen had made sure of it, the way he'd made sure of everything—the welds, the lashings, the lies, the schedule that put eleven other people aboard a ship he intended to kill.

Eleven names. I knew them. Ruiz had spoken them to me once, below decks, in the flat voice of a man reciting a debt ledger: Aune. Brandvik. Collett. The list went down the manifest like a wound going down a body. My father had written those names onto a sailing roster and then written their deaths into the same voyage plan, and he'd had time—weeks, months—to think about it, and the thinking had led him here, to a brass tube and a glass port and the word *record.*

I clipped the cylinder to my harness. I didn't open it. Some part of me understood, even underwater, even alone, that whatever was written inside should be read in air, aloud, in front of witnesses—that my father had staged his confession for exactly that, and the least I owed the dead was to let the staging play out.

I took one last look at the crates. *HANDLE AS ARCHIVAL.* Handle as evidence, more like. Handle as restitution. Ingrid Halvard's inheritance, sitting in the dark all these years while she grew up on her father's shame.

I turned for the line and went up.

* * *

On deck, the light hurt. Pell stripped my mask and Okafor wrapped a blanket around my shoulders before I'd finished coughing, and Ruiz stood apart with his arms folded, watching the cylinder in my hands the way you'd watch a live shell.

"That's it?" he said. "That's what he left?"

"For the record," I read aloud, turning the etching toward him. "His words."

Ruiz came close. Forty years ago he'd been a young deckhand on the Vargr's final voyage, and Magnus had ordered him off the ship the night before she sailed—some pretext about papers, some errand ashore that had saved his life and ruined his understanding of it. He'd spent four decades not knowing whether he'd been spared or discarded. He reached out and touched the brass with one finger, briefly, like checking a stove.

"We read it on deck," he said. "All hands. And we signal the coast guard first—I've had the set keyed since the third tide, and today's the day I use it." He looked at me. "Your father built himself a courtroom down there. Fine. We'll give him one up here. But it gets heard by everyone, including the law. No private grief. No deals."

"No deals," I agreed. I thought of Ashe's liaison aboard—the smooth man named Ferro who filed his reports by satellite every six hours and smiled like a contract. "One problem. Ferro."

"Ferro's already on the radio," Okafor rumbled from the rail. "Been transmitting since you surfaced. Short bursts. Encrypted."

Of course. Ashe had let us lead her to the wreck, patient as a spider, and now the strongroom was breached and the find was real and her patience would be over. I'd known it was coming. I'd fed Ferro false coordinates twice and memorized true ones, and I'd discovered with self-disgust how naturally deception sat in my hands, how much of my father lived in my reflexes. But there was no false coordinate for this. We were on top of the Vargr. There was nowhere left to misdirect anyone to.

"Then we read it before her people can stop us," I said. "Now. While the tide's slack and their boats are still twenty miles out."

Ruiz weighed it for three seconds. "Pell, get everyone up top. Okafor, break out the good lamp—we're doing this in daylight if the sky will allow it." He turned back to me. "You read it. His blood reads it. That's how it should be."

"My blood wrote it," I said. "There's a difference."

"Not down here there isn't," Ruiz said quietly. "Up here, maybe, you get to be different. That's the whole point of surfacing."

* * *

The crew gathered in a rough semicircle by the main hatch. Pell had his cap off, which I'd never seen. Okafor leaned on the rail with his arms crossed, engineering judgment already written on his face—he'd run the analysis on the welds days ago, told Ruiz what we all now knew: *sealed during the sinking, not after; Magnus went down with his own secret.* Ingrid's charter boat sat anchored a cable's length off our starboard quarter—she'd come out on the tide, silent, watchful, the daughter of the blamed man come to see the ledger balanced. She stood at her rail with binoculars lowered, waiting. She didn't ask to come aboard. Not yet. There was a formality coming, and she meant to observe it.

Ferro hovered at the edge of the group with a recorder in his palm, and nobody told him to turn it off. Let him record. Let Ashe hear it from her own device. The truth was the one cargo on this ship that couldn't be stolen.

I broke the seal on the cap. Inside, the paper roll was dry and stiff and browned at the edges, ink faded to the color of dried blood but legible, every word laid down in that same neat engineer's hand I'd seen etched on the brass. My father's handwriting. I'd known it my whole childhood—from supply manifests, from birthday cards that always sounded like invoices, from the last postcard he'd sent me before the Vargr sailed, *back by the spring tides, keep the home fires boring.* I'd kept that postcard for ten years like a relic. Now the same hand was about to tell me what it had been hiding.

I unrolled the page and began to read aloud.

*"If this is found, then the sea has given up what I gave it, and I must accept its verdict..."*

My voice held. I'd read hard passages aloud before—my own drafts, other people's eulogies. What nearly broke me was not the words but the calm of them, the methodical way he laid it out, item by item, like a defect report. He'd taken the Skerries finds. He'd falsified the manifests. He'd let Halvard carry the suspicion because Halvard had signed the export papers and the signature was forgeable and Magnus had forged it—"because Arne trusted me, and trust is the easiest material in the world to cut." He'd planned to sell the collection abroad and disappear rich. Then the buyers had fallen through, the debts had mounted, and the expedition's backers had begun asking questions he couldn't answer, and so he'd arrived at the solution that solved everything at once: scuttle the Vargr in deep water, drown the evidence, become the tragic hero of his own disappearance.

He wrote about the eleven crew the way he wrote about ballast calculations. *"Their families will be compensated by the insurers. Grief is expensive but it is not permanent. Reputation is both."*

Reputation is both. I read the sentence twice because my eyes refused to believe it the first time. Eleven people. Aune, who had a daughter. Brandvik, who was saving for a boat of his own. Collett, who was twenty-three. And my father had weighed them against his reputation and found the arithmetic acceptable.

The father I had invented—the martyr, the lost hero, the man whose shadow I'd spent a decade hiding from under false names because I couldn't bear to stand in it—that father had never existed. The real one had existed all along, down here, in brass and waxed paper, and he was worse than anything I'd ever feared, and also, in some unforgivable way, more honest than the man I'd mourned. At least this one confessed. At least this one, at the very end, had welded a door shut on himself and sat down to write the truth for whoever came looking. It wasn't absolution. It wasn't even remorse, not really—it was vanity, the same vanity that had killed them, wanting the record straight because the record was the last thing he owned. But it was the truth, and the truth was the only thing in the strongroom that belonged to me.

I read to the end. *"To my daughter, if she ever reads this: I am sorry in the way that costs nothing. Do not salvage me."*

Do not salvage me.

The deck was absolutely quiet. Somewhere below, the Kestrel's pumps ticked. Pell had both hands pressed flat against his thighs. Okafor's jaw worked once. Ruiz stared at the compass rose painted on the deck plates, and I understood he was seeing a night forty years gone, a gangway, a man telling him to step off a ship.

Ingrid Halvard's tender bumped against our hull. She came up the ladder before anyone offered, walked to the center of the semicircle, and stood in front of me with her father's disgrace finally lifted off him and her eyes wet and furious at once.

"Say the eleven names," she said. "Out loud. All of them. They've waited long enough."

I opened my mouth to begin—

And Ferro's satellite phone rang. He answered it, listened for five seconds, and his smile, for the first time since Bergen, looked genuinely regretful.

"That was Ms. Ashe," he said. "Her fleet is eight minutes out. She says the find is hers, the ship is hers, and no one is going anywhere."
