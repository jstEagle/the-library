## 16. What Power Buys

The call came in at 0340, which told Corinne Ashe everything before the liaison said a word.

Voss did not make calls in the small hours unless something had gone wrong, and Voss did not report things gone wrong unless they were also going right. He was careful that way—careful the way hired men are careful, with an eye on the door and one hand always resting on his own usefulness. Corinne had chosen him for exactly that quality. A man who watched doors was a man who noticed who came through them.

She took the call standing at her window, forty-one floors above a harbor that belonged to her in every way that mattered except the paperwork. The city was a scatter of amber lights below her, and beyond it the black water where her money was currently sleeping on the seafloor.

"Say it," she said.

"The strongroom's breached." Voss's voice was flat, professional, pitched low as if the walls of the Kestrel might be listening through the satellite link. Maybe he thought they were. "Okafor ran the cutting analysis two days ago and sat on it. They went in on the fifth tide. Eriksen dove."

"She found the cylinder."

A pause. Long enough to confirm what he hadn't said yet. "They've got cargo on deck, ma'am. Crated. And the girl's been reading from something. Ruiz called a crew meeting about it. Whatever's in that cylinder, they're treating it like a will."

Corinne turned from the window. Her reflection moved with her—a woman in her sixties with silver hair cut short as a verdict, wearing a robe that cost more than the Kestrel's monthly fuel bill. She had been awake anyway. She was always awake at this hour; sleep had left her around the time the Vargr did, twenty-two years ago, and never fully come back.

"Inventory," she said. "What's on deck."

"Partial manifest from my camera feed before they covered the hold. Bronze fittings. Two sealed amphorae. What looks like a navigation instrument set—silver, monogrammed. And a strongbox I'd guess at thirty kilos." He hesitated. "There's more down there. The hold wasn't empty when they stopped pulling."

"No," Corinne said. "It wouldn't be."

She crossed to her desk and opened the drawer she kept locked, because some things you keep close not for their value but for their weight. Inside lay a ledger bound in green leather, its spine cracked white with age. She didn't take it out. She just rested her hand on it, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of someone you'd once loved and now merely owned.

Twenty-two years. She had been thirty-four when she signed the financing papers for the Halvard-Eriksen North Atlantic survey, sitting across a table from two men who believed—with the touching confidence of men who have never been audited—that the money was the least important thing in the room. Halvard had talked about science. Magnus Eriksen had talked about legacy. Corinne had talked about returns, because she was the only one being honest, and they had both smiled at her the way people smile at weather.

The expedition had cost her four hundred thousand in 1990s currency. It had returned nothing but a sinking, eleven dead men, a drowned partner, and a scandal that ate Halvard's name whole while Eriksen sailed off into martyrdom. The insurers paid out on the hull. Nobody paid out on the truth.

And then, three years later, a fisherman's trawl snagged on something off the old grid and brought up a bronze fitting stamped with a maker's mark that should not have existed outside a museum vault—and Corinne understood, with the cold clarity that had built her entire fortune, that the Vargr had not sunk empty.

That the cargo manifest Halvard filed had been fiction. That the real finds—the ones worth stealing—had gone into the hold unlisted, and gone down with the ship, and waited.

She had spent two decades and rather more than four hundred thousand keeping it that way.

"Ma'am?" Voss said.

"I'm here." She closed the drawer. "Who else knows about the breach?"

"Crew only. Six of them. The girl, Ruiz, Okafor, Pell, the cook, and a deckhand. Ingrid Halvard's tender is still shadowing them—she hasn't boarded again since the fourth tide."

"Ingrid Halvard." Corinne said the name the way you'd test ice. "Still there."

"Still there."

Of course she was. Corinne had read the file on Ingrid Halvard twice—once when the anonymous tip about the salvage run reached her office, and once more last week, after the girl chartered a boat and started trailing the Kestrel like a debt she intended to collect personally. A schoolteacher, of all things. Twenty-eight years old, born eight months after her father went down, raised on the story that Magnus Eriksen was a hero and Halvard was the man who'd lost the ship. And now she was out on the water watching strangers open her father's disgrace like a tin.

Corinne almost admired her. Almost. Admiration was cheap; she could afford any amount of it.

"Here's what happens next," she said. "You'll tell the girl nothing has changed. Contract terms stand. You'll be pleasant. You'll inventory everything they bring up, and you'll do it with their cooperation if possible."

"And if not?"

"Then not pleasantly." Corinne sat down at her desk and woke the terminal. "But hear me clearly, Voss. Nothing leaves that vessel. Not the cargo, not the cylinder, not one gram of scrap metal off that wreck. If they try to run for port, you signal me and we intercept. If they try to transfer anything to Halvard's tender, we intercept faster. Am I understood?"

"Understood. But—" He paused, and she could hear him choosing his words, which meant he'd already chosen them and was dressing them up. "Ma'am, the coast guard has been sniffing around the grid. Ruiz filed a survey extension that doesn't quite match our permits. If we seize a civilian vessel in open water without clean paper—"

"We won't be seizing anything." She pulled up the file she'd been building for three weeks, the one with the Kestrel's registration history, Okafor's expired pressure-welding certification, Pell's incident report from a Barents Sea job five years back, Ruiz's—Ruiz's was the thickest folder, and the most delicate. "We'll be conducting a contractual audit. The Kestrel is operating under a salvage agreement funded by Ashe Maritime Recovery. Every artifact recovered under that agreement is company property pending valuation. That's not seizure, Voss. That's bookkeeping."

He was quiet for a moment. "And if the crew objects?"

"Then the crew can explain to a maritime court why they breached a sealed compartment on a registered grave site without a heritage permit." She let that sit. "I've already made the call to the heritage authority. There's a protection order on the Vargr effective tomorrow morning. Anything recovered after midnight tonight is contraband. Anything recovered before—" she smiled at the terminal, at the neat timestamp she'd arranged with a man who owed her a favor older than Ingrid Halvard, "—is ours."

"That's retroactive."

"That's Tuesday," Corinne said. "Get some sleep. You'll want your hands steady."

She ended the call and sat alone with the harbor lights and the green ledger.

* * *

People misread her, and she let them. Let them call her greedy behind her back—she knew they did; greed was the story people told about women who won. Greed was simple. Greed wanted things. Corinne did not particularly want the artifacts. She had seen photographs of the Halvard-Eriksen finds from the first season: corroded bronze, cracked glass, the kind of museum pieces that photographed well and appraised modestly. If she'd wanted money she had a hundred cleaner ways to make it than chasing a wreck across six tides with a debt-ridden ghost's daughter aboard.

No. What Corinne wanted was the ending.

Every fortune is built on a room somewhere where the bodies are, and hers was no different. She had financed the Vargr expedition. She had structured the ownership so that the finds would pass through a holding company she controlled before anyone saw them—which was standard practice, which was how financing worked, which Halvard and Eriksen had signed without reading past the numbers. When the ship sank, the insurance claim, the inquiry, the lawsuits—all of it had landed on Halvard, because Halvard was the partner of record, because Magnus Eriksen had very carefully made himself the face and Halvard the signature. Corinne had watched it happen from her office and told herself it was tragedy, not architecture.

But she had known. Even then, half-knowing, she had known. Because three weeks before the Vargr sailed, Magnus Eriksen had come to her office alone—without Halvard—and asked her one question. He'd asked it casually, hat in hand, a big charming man playing at humility.

*If the cargo came home heavier than the manifest says,* he'd asked, *whose name goes on the receiving dock?*

And she had answered honestly: *Mine.*

And he had smiled, and shaken her hand, and thanked her for her time, and she had sat there feeling the shape of something wrong and done nothing, because whatever Magnus was planning, it would either enrich her or bury itself, and either way she'd profit. She had told herself that too. She'd been telling herself versions of it for twenty-two years.

Then the ship sank with eleven men aboard, and Halvard's name went into the water with them, and Corinne realized that Magnus hadn't planned to enrich her or bury himself. He'd planned to drown the evidence and surface clean, and everyone else—the partner, the crew, the financier whose money bought the expedition that killed them—could float or sink as the sea decided.

So no. Not greed. Greed would have been simpler and forgivable. This was closer to accounting. Somewhere in the dark off the shelf there was a hole in her books twenty-two years wide, and eleven names in it, and a family named Halvard living in the crater, and Corinne Ashe did not leave holes in her books. Not because she was good. Because a woman who left holes in her books was a woman other people could reach into.

The artifacts coming up meant the hole was opening. The confession cylinder meant it was opening with witnesses. And witnesses were the one asset class Corinne had spent her life shorting.

* * *

At dawn she made three calls.

The first was to the heritage authority, confirming the protection order and adding a rider: any party in possession of material from the protected site was to be considered in violation, effective immediately, retroactive to the date of the order's drafting. The man on the other end agreed quickly and gratefully. He had a daughter at university in Edinburgh and a mortgage shaped like a boat. Corinne remembered both.

The second was to her lawyers. "Prepare a claim of conversion against the Kestrel and a motion for emergency impoundment of all recovered materials," she said. "Name the vessel's charter company, name Ashe Maritime as injured party, and add the individual crew members as respondents. All of them. Especially Ruiz."

"Ruiz?" The junior partner's voice sharpened with interest. "The first mate? We have something?"

"We have a man who served on the original expedition and failed to disclose it on his crew certification. Which makes his testimony worth nothing and his presence worth a great deal." She tapped a pen against the desk. "I don't need him convicted. I need him quiet. A man facing charges will advise caution to everyone around him. Caution is all I require."

"And the girl? Eriksen?"

Corinne looked at the green ledger on her desk. "Lena Eriksen," she said, tasting the name. Ten years of aliases, ten years of rented invisibility, and Corinne had peeled every one of them back in a dockside office in Bergen with the ease of unwrapping a gift. The girl had looked at her then like a woman discovering the floor was painted on air. Corinne had enjoyed that. She wasn't proud of enjoying it, but she'd stopped requiring pride from herself a long time ago—it was inefficient, like conscience.

"The girl is leverage," she said. "Her debt is mine. Her aliases are mine. Her father's name is mine—I own the exclusive rights to the Eriksen disaster story, did you know that? Documentary option, two publishers, a streaming deal in negotiation. Everything the world knows about Magnus Eriksen, it knows because I permitted it." She paused. "If she surfaces with that cylinder and starts reading names aloud, she isn't just breaking contract. She's devaluing my asset. Add defamation exposure to her file. Cite the debt agreement's confidentiality clause."

"There's no confidentiality clause covering historical fact."

"There will be by Friday."

The third call was the shortest. She dialed a number she kept written nowhere, memorized the way you memorize a fire exit.

"It's me," she said. "The Kestrel. Tonight, if she moves. Nothing dramatic—I want her hulled and drifting, engines dead, forty miles from any port, with every excuse in the world to blame the sea." She listened. "Yes, before the protection order publishes. And the tender with her—Halvard's boat. Don't touch the girl. Just make sure neither vessel reaches anywhere with a courthouse."

She hung up and stood at the window again. The sun was coming up over the water, gilding the harbor, lighting the cranes and the container stacks and the ferries full of people who would read about all this someday in a story she would sell them.

Entitlement, someone had once called it, to her face—an ex-husband, actually, during the divorce, throwing the word like it was a knife. *You think the world owes you.* And Corinne had laughed, because he'd had it exactly backward. The world didn't owe her. She had simply learned, earlier than most, that the world keeps accounts badly—that it lets men like Magnus Eriksen scuttle ships and sail away as heroes, lets dead partners carry blame like ballast, lets eleven names sink without a hearing. The world's books were a mess. Someone had to keep honest ones.

Hers just happened to balance in her favor.

* * *

Voss called again at noon, and this time his voice had a hairline crack in it.

"They know," he said. "Eriksen confronted me an hour ago. She knows I've been reporting. She didn't threaten, didn't shout—she just told me she knows, and then she went back to cataloguing the cargo like I wasn't standing there." A breath. "Ma'am, these aren't salvagers anymore. Something changed on that deck. Ruiz had the crew sign a witness log. Actual signatures, actual timestamps. They're building a record."

"A record needs a court to read it."

"Or a press room." He lowered his voice further. "There's a journalist filing from Bodø. I traced the queries—one of them used the word 'Halvard.' Somebody gave him the name. Ma'am, I think it was the girl."

Corinne stood very still. Outside her window, a container ship eased toward its berth, obedient as furniture.

So the daughter of Magnus Eriksen had decided to become a witness. How novel. How unbearably naive. As if truth were a lifeboat—as if naming the dead aloud settled anything. Corinne had watched men testify before tribunals; she'd watched inquiries bloom and wilt; she'd watched the Halvard family spend thirty years shouting into a wind that returned only silence. Truth didn't win. Truth got impounded, redacted, priced, and shelved. Truth was just another asset waiting for someone with capital to acquire it.

Which was, when you thought about it, exactly what she was doing.

"Let her file her queries," Corinne said. "By the time any story runs, the protection order will be public, the Kestrel will be in violation, and every artifact on that deck will be evidence in my claim. She can't give the cargo to the Halvards without committing a crime, and she can't keep it without owing me. Either way it comes ashore through my hands." She picked up the pen. "One more thing. The cylinder—the confession. Has it left the ship?"

"Not that I've seen. The girl sleeps with it, near as I can tell."

"Good. Then it's simple." Corinne uncapped the pen and wrote a single line in the margin of her morning page, the way she did when a decision finalized itself: *Kestrel — recover all. No exceptions.*

"Tell the crew the audit team arrives within forty-eight hours," she said. "Tell them it's routine. And Voss—if the girl tries to move that cylinder to Halvard's tender, you stop her yourself. Whatever it takes. That document doesn't get read into any record I don't control."

"And if she dives with it instead?" Voss asked quietly. "Takes it down to the wreck? Puts it somewhere even we can't reach?"

Corinne considered the black water beyond the harbor lights, the cold miles down where a ship lay welded shut around its own guilt, where a man had once decided that drowning the truth was cheaper than confessing it. She had spent twenty-two years assuming Magnus Eriksen was the most dangerous man she'd ever financed.

She was beginning to suspect his daughter might be more dangerous still. He had scuttled a ship to bury the truth. The girl, by all reports, was trying to raise it.

"Then she drowns with it," Corinne said, "and the sea keeps my books balanced. It always has."

She hung up, and on the desk beside the green ledger, her phone lit with a new message from the heritage authority—and the timestamp on the protection order was wrong. Published early. Hours early. Already circulating.

Someone had gotten there first.
