## 17. Mutiny of the Small

Pell saw the enforcers before anyone else did, because Pell always saw the bad things first. It was a gift, or a curse, depending on whether you believed in gifts.

He was on the aft deck checking the tender's lashings when the black RIB came around the Kestrel's stern quarter, running dark, no nav lights, no hail. Two more behind it. He counted heads by the glow of their helmet lamps: six, eight, ten. Boarding hooks already out. They weren't coming to inspect anything.

"Ruiz," he said, not loud, because loud would have been wrong. "Company."

Ruiz came up the companionway with a mug of coffee he never finished and set it down on the rail with the care of a man putting down something he might need both hands without. He looked at the RIBs for maybe three seconds.

"How many?"

"Ten. Maybe twelve. Heavy gear."

"Okafor."

The engineer's voice came up from below before Ruiz finished calling, which meant Okafor had been listening on the engine-room intercom like he always did when the night went quiet. "I see them. Give me two minutes."

"Pell." Ruiz turned, and his face was doing something Pell had never seen it do in all the weeks aboard—nothing at all. Flat as a chart. "Get to the tender lines. If they board us, I want them boarding a ship that can't move and can't be moved. You understand?"

Pell understood. His hands understood before the rest of him did; they were already moving along the rail toward the cleats where the tender's painter and the dive lines ran. "And Lena?"

"Lena stays below. That's not negotiable and it's not a discussion."

* * *

Here is what Pell knew about Corinne Ashe that nobody had said out loud: you didn't get a fleet like hers by being lucky. You got it by buying things that weren't for sale, and the thing she'd bought this time was the law itself—or enough of it, bent at the right angle, to look like law. The liaison had papers. The liaison always had papers. Papers saying the Kestrel's find was subject to seizure pending adjudication, papers signed by people whose signatures cost money, and money was the one ocean nobody had ever successfully dived.

Pell's grandmother had worked the herring boats off Lofoten her whole life, and she'd taught him one thing worth carrying: *when the big boat comes, the small boats sink each other on purpose rather than be swallowed.* She hadn't meant it as strategy. She'd meant it as history. But Pell had learned young that history was just strategy that had stopped pretending.

The first RIB bumped against the hull. Grapples went over the rail with the soft clank of professional work. Boots on the ladder.

Above him, floodlights snapped on—not theirs. The Kestrel's own floods, thrown by Ruiz from the wheelhouse, washing the whole aft deck white so that everyone could see everything, which was its own kind of message: *we're not hiding, and neither are you.*

"Kestrel, this is Meridian Security aboard under writ of seizure," said a voice through a megaphone, smooth as varnish. "Do not interfere. Interference will be logged."

"Logged," Ruiz repeated into his own handheld, dry as sand. "By all means. Log away. My paperwork takes time. Everything legal does."

* * *

Pell got to the tender lines and started cutting.

Not the tender—the tender was Ingrid's, and Ingrid was aboard, asleep in the guest berth or pretending to be, and Pell wasn't touching her boat. He cut the dive lines first. The spare lift bags' rigging. The mooring pennant that had kept them hooked to the wreck site buoy, because if the Kestrel drifted off station, every hour Ashe's people spent re-anchoring was an hour the strongroom stayed shut. Then, with a smaller knife and a steadier hand than he felt, he went after the hydraulic lines to the crane winch—the ones that would be needed to lift anything heavy off the deck onto any RIB.

It was funny, in the way things are funny at three in the morning when your heart is going like a fish on a deck. All those years of superstition, all the salt rings and the coins under the mast step and never whistling in the wheelhouse, and it turned out the useful magic was just knowing which line held what.

A boot hit the deck behind him.

Pell didn't turn around fast. Turning fast was how men ended up over rails. He straightened slowly, knife still open in his hand, and found himself looking at a broad man in black kit with MERIDIAN stenciled across the chest plate, holding a flashlight that he pointed at Pell's face instead of his hands, which told Pell the man was new.

"You were cutting lines."

"I was maintaining them," Pell said. "Maintenance looks like that sometimes."

The light wavered. Behind the new man, others were coming over the rail in pairs, moving with the confidence of people who had done this to fishing crews and research vessels and never once to a ship whose crew had decided, quietly and completely, not to be taken.

"Where's the cargo?" the new man asked.

"What cargo?"

"The artifacts. From the wreck."

Pell spread his arms, knife and all, encompassing the empty aft deck, the coiled lines, the grey sea. "You see a hold full of treasure? I see a working deck. We're a survey vessel. Ask the liaison—he's been aboard a week, he knows what we carry, which is nothing, because we haven't lifted anything yet."

That much was true, and truth was the best rope in a knot like this. The strongroom's contents were still down there, forty fathoms deep, sealed behind steel that Magnus Eriksen had welded from the inside. Whatever Ashe thought she was seizing, she was seizing water.

The new man's radio crackled. A voice Pell recognized—the liaison, Halvorsen, with his office cologne and his clipboard voice—said something clipped. The new man's posture changed. He looked at Pell differently now, the way you look at weather coming.

"Engine room," he said. "They say your engine room is flooding ballast."

"Aye," Pell said. "She does that."

* * *

Down below, Okafor was conducting.

Pell heard it through the deck plates more than he saw it: the deep, arrhythmic groan of pumps running in combinations they were never meant to run in, the Kestrel taking on water in her wing tanks, port and starboard, deliberately uneven. The old girl began to list—two degrees, then three—and a listing ship is a ship no sane boarding party wants to stand on while she's rolling.

Okafor had explained it once, over cards, why he loved ballast systems the way other men loved women or football. *"Everyone thinks the engine is the heart. Wrong. Ballast is the spine. You control the ballast, you control whether the body stands up or lies down."* Tonight the Kestrel lay down. Not dangerously—Okafor didn't do dangerous, he did precise—but enough. Enough that the enforcers' second RIB, trying to come alongside on the low side, took green water over its bow and had to sheer off, engines screaming.

"Meridian, be advised," Ruiz's voice came over the external speaker, calm as a shipping forecast, "we've developed a stability issue. I'd recommend your people withdraw to a safe distance until my engineer assesses. Forcing a boarding on an unstable vessel creates liability. Yours, I mean. Ours too, but mostly yours, since you're the ones with lawyers on retainer."

The megaphone voice came back, less varnished now. "This is obstruction."

"This is maintenance," Ruiz said, and Pell, crouched by the severed mooring pennant, laughed out loud before he could stop himself.

* * *

They put four of the enforcers in the wheelhouse anyway, because papers demanded it, and Ruiz gave them papers. God, he gave them papers. Pell watched through the wheelhouse glass as Ruiz produced manifest after manifest—harbor clearances, insurance riders, the original salvage contract with its arbitration clause, the environmental compliance filing, a notarized statement about ballast procedures that Pell was fairly sure Okafor had typed up in the last twenty minutes—and laid them out one at a time, reading each aloud in full, in the voice of a man who had all night and knew they didn't.

Halvorsen stood by the chart table with his tablet, jaw tight, recording everything, and Pell caught the moment the liaison realized the trap he was in: every minute of delay was being documented, and every document was a wall. Ashe had bought speed. Ruiz was selling slowness, and slowness, tonight, was priceless.

In the middle of it, quietly, the wheelhouse door opened and Lena Eriksen came in.

Pell saw her come up from below with the cylinder wrapped in a dive bag slung across her back, and his stomach dropped through the hull. She was supposed to stay below. She was supposed to be nowhere. But she walked past the enforcers like they were furniture, set the bag on the chart table next to Ruiz's paper fortress, and said, to Halvorsen, in a voice that carried:

"You want to know what we found? It's not cargo. It's a confession. Eleven names. Your boss funded the expedition that put those eleven men on a ship her partner scuttled, and she's known for thirty years. Read your own company history, Halvorsen. Or don't—I'm told it's short."

The wheelhouse went very quiet. One of the enforcers shifted his weight. Halvorsen's thumb hovered over his tablet, recording, and Pell watched him understand that he was now recording evidence against his employer, and that the smart move—the move that would keep him clean when this all surfaced, and it would surface, things like this always surfaced—was to keep recording.

*That,* Pell thought, *is how you fight a bought thing. You make the purchase public.*

* * *

It didn't end there. Bought power doesn't leave because you embarrass it. The RIBs regrouped off the port beam, and the megaphone voice came back harder, promising a second boarding with coast guard escort—"civilian escort," the voice said, which was a lie wearing a uniform—and Pell climbed to the rail with his knife and his salt-ring calluses and waited.

But the crew was already moving, and this was the part he would remember for the rest of his life, the part he'd tell badly in bars for years: nobody had voted. Nobody had argued. Okafor had flooded the tanks without being asked twice. Ruiz had built his paper wall without being asked at all. And when the second wave of enforcers hooked grapples over the port rail, Pell cut the last line he'd saved—the RIB's own tow bridle, left carelessly looped on a bollard by a man in a hurry—and the inflatable swung wide on its own wake and slapped itself against the hull hard enough to tear its own pontoon seam.

Small mutinies. A cut line here, a flooded tank there, a signature delayed, a lie recorded. Nothing you could point to in a courtroom. Everything you needed.

The enforcer with the new face found Pell at the rail again, breathing hard, and for a long moment they just looked at each other across the floodlit deck.

"You people are insane," the man said.

"No," Pell said. "We're small. There's a difference. Big things can be bought. Small things have to be convinced, and you haven't."

The man looked past him—at Ruiz still reading clauses, at Okafor's silhouette in the engine-room hatch, at Lena standing over the brass cylinder with her hand flat on it like a woman keeping a door closed—and something in his face let go. He keyed his radio. "Meridian Actual, this is Board Team Two. Recommend withdrawal. Repeat: recommend withdrawal. Vessel is non-compliant in ways that will cost more than the cargo."

Static. Then, faintly, from far away, a woman's voice that Pell had only ever heard through Hull comms, cold and unhurried: *"Hold position."*

The enforcer looked at his radio. Looked at the sea. Held position.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and thin, and the RIBs still sat off the beam like dogs told to stay, and the Kestrel rolled gently on her deliberately uneven ballast, and nobody had won yet. But Pell stood his watch at the rail with the knife closed in his fist and felt something he hadn't felt since he was a boy watching his grandmother's fleet scatter before the buyer's boats: not safety. Solidarity. The difference mattered. Safety was weather. This was keel.

Below his feet, the cylinder sat in the wheelhouse with eleven names inside it, and somewhere south, if Ruiz's signal had gone out days ago the way he'd sworn it had, the real coast guard was coming with paperwork of its own.

Pell spat over the leeward rail, for luck, out of habit, out of respect.

Then he saw the masthead lights on the horizon—two sets, white-over-white-over-white, closing fast—and beside them a third shape that made his blood go cold and hot at once: a Meridian fast-response cutter, flying no flag at all.

"Ruiz," he called up. "Visitors. Three of them. And one of them isn't waiting for dawn."
