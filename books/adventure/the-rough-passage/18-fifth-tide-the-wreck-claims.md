## 18. Fifth Tide — The Wreck Claims

The enforcers came aboard at slack water, and the Kestrel answered with sabotage.

Lena heard it all from the dive deck: Okafor's ballast pumps cycling wrong, the ship listing just enough to make the boarding party stumble; Ruiz's voice, smooth as harbor oil, reading regulations aloud to men who hadn't brought any; Pell somewhere above, shears in hand, cutting mooring lines with the cheerful industry of a man finally doing what he was born for. Shouting. A generator dying mid-sentence. The particular silence of a radio being unplugged.

She didn't wait to see how it ended. She'd spent ten years learning when a scene was about to turn ugly, and this one had turned.

The cylinder was still down there.

They'd breached the strongroom on the fifth tide's first window—Okafor's cutter walking through the weld seam like a can opener, the sea pouring into a space that had been dry for eleven years. Inside: crates of Halvard's stolen finds, catalogued and crated by careful hands, and on a shelf built for it, a brass cylinder the length of her forearm, capped and sealed, her father's initials stamped into the collar.

ME. Magnus Eriksen. Even his vanity had been tidy.

Ruiz had wanted to bring it up immediately. Lena had said no—too much chaos topside, too many eyes, let the crew secure the artifacts first. It had seemed like prudence. Now, with Ashe's divers in the water and her own crew fighting a boarding action two decks up, it looked like the same instinct her father had followed straight to the bottom: hold the secret, control the story, trust no one.

She was done trusting no one. But she wasn't done retrieving the truth.

* * *

The water off the Vargr was green-black and cold as a held grudge. Lena went down the anchor line without a tender, without a spotter, in gear she'd rigged herself—single tank, no redundant anything, because redundancy took time and time was the only thing Ashe couldn't buy fast enough.

Forty meters down, the wreck resolved out of the murk in pieces, the way it always did: rail first, then the shadow of the superstructure, then the wound of the strongroom glowing faintly where Okafor's cut had left clean metal. Her light swept across the crates. Across the shelf.

The cylinder was gone.

Her heart did something complicated—dropped, then lifted, then dropped again—as her beam found the drag marks in the silt leading away from the strongroom, toward the stern section, toward the tangled dark under the collapsed mast housing. Someone had gotten here before her. One of Ashe's divers, working while the surface burned. They'd taken the confession.

No. Not taken. She followed the marks and understood: they'd tried. The cylinder lay wedged deep under a fallen deck plate, half-buried where its retriever had been forced to drop it—either spooked by something or called back up. Either way, it was hers now, if she could get under the plate.

She knelt in the silt and dug. Cold worked through her gloves. Her air gauge read a third of a tank, which was generous if everything went well and a lie if anything didn't. She got her fingers under the brass collar and pulled, and the plate shifted, and the cylinder slid free into her arms like something sleeping.

For one stupid moment she held it against her chest. Eleven years of not knowing, eleven years of building a father out of grief and scraps, and he weighed four pounds and fit against her ribs.

Then her line went slack.

Not drifted—cut. She knew the difference. She'd cut lines herself, once, working a site off Tromsø under a name that wasn't hers, when a rival crew got curious. A cut line has a specific limpness, an honesty about it. Hers floated away from her into the dark like a severed nerve.

Above her, silhouetted against the pale ceiling of the surface, three shapes descended in formation. Professional spacing. Fins that didn't waste a kick.

Ashe's divers. Come to finish the retrieval.

Lena did the math every diver does and hated the answer: a third of a tank, forty meters, no line, three hostiles between her and the anchor. Free ascent from this depth meant decompression she didn't have the air to sit through, and skipping it meant bubbles expanding in her blood like applause.

But the shapes weren't descending toward her. They were descending toward the strongroom. Toward the crates. Toward cargo.

Of course. Ashe hadn't sent them for the cylinder—she didn't know about the cylinder. She'd sent them for the artifacts, the things with auction-house numbers attached. The confession was worthless paper to a woman who thought everything had a price.

Which meant Lena had one asset they didn't know existed, and one chance to use it.

She swam—not up, but along the wreck's flank, into the shadow of the hull, dragging the cylinder tight against her body so it wouldn't catch their lights. Her breathing went loud in her ears, the old panic rising, the panic she'd been drowning since she was nineteen years old and watching a salvage barge burn on the horizon. She made herself count kicks instead of breaths. Ten kicks. Twenty. The divers' lights swept past behind her, methodical, greedy, blind.

At thirty meters she found the hawse hole—a rusted opening in the bow where the anchor chain used to run—and rose through it inside the wreck itself, using the Vargr's own bones as cover. Her father's ship sheltering his daughter. She almost laughed into her regulator, and the laugh would have cost her air, so she saved it.

Fifteen meters. Her gauge needle leaned into the red like a drunk against a doorframe.

She'd planned to ascend outside the wreck, clear of the hull, but the divers were between her and open water now, and her air was a rumor. So she did the thing the manual says never to do: she went up through the ship. Through the hawse hole into the chain locker, up a rusted ladder rung by rung, through a hatchway warped open by eleven years of pressure, into the black throat of the forward hold, kicking blind with one hand on the cylinder and one hand on the guide of her own memory—because she'd studied these plans until they lived behind her eyes, because knowing a dead man's ship room by room was the closest thing to a conversation they'd ever had.

Ten meters. Five. Her vision tunneled. The gauge read empty, which meant it was lying, which meant she had maybe sixty seconds of compressed screaming left in the tank.

The last hatch was jammed. Of course it was. She hit it with the cylinder, once, twice—the brass rang against steel, a bell tolling underwater—and on the third strike it gave, and she was through, and the surface was a white shimmering ceiling she climbed toward on fumes.

* * *

She broke the water gasping, and hands caught her before the gasping finished.

Not Ashe's hands. Ingrid Halvard's—strong from hauling lines on her chartered boat, sure around Lena's shoulders, hauling her over the gunwale of the tender like a sack of wet rope. Lena came down onto the deck plating and stayed there, coughing seawater, the cylinder still locked in her grip so hard her fingers had to be pried loose one at a time.

"I've got it," Ingrid said. "I've got you. Both."

Lena rolled onto her back. Above her, the sky was doing storm-colored things, and beyond the tender the Kestrel sat low in the water, listing, ringed by Ashe's boats like wolves around a limping stag. Somewhere aboard, Pell was probably still cutting things. Somewhere below, her lungs were filing complaints.

"You went down alone," Ingrid said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's coat.

"There wasn't time."

"There's never time. That's how my father died—everyone always out of time." Ingrid crouched over her, and her face was the face Lena had seen across a deck once already, hard and hungry and grieving, but something in it had cracked since then. Something had opened. "What is it? What's worth your life?"

Lena sat up. The world tilted and settled. She looked at the cylinder in Ingrid's hands—at the initials stamped in the collar, ME, the man who had scuttled his own ship with eleven souls aboard to bury what he'd stolen from her family—and she saw the moment arrive that she'd been diving toward since Bergen.

She could keep it. Read it alone. Control the story, shape the telling, decide who deserved which truths. That was the Eriksen inheritance, handed down like the compass: hold the secret, survive by it, drown by it.

Or she could do the thing her father never could.

"Read it," Lena said. Her voice came out scraped raw, half seawater. "Out loud. On the Kestrel's deck. Before everyone—your people, my crew, Ashe's liaison, whoever's listening." She pushed wet hair out of her eyes and met Ingrid's gaze and felt something in her chest unclench for the first time in eleven years. "He owes eleven families an explanation. He owes yours the most. I'm not going to decide what they get to hear."

Ingrid stared at her. "You don't even know what it says."

"No," Lena agreed. "I don't. That's rather the point."

The wind picked up, carrying shouting from the Kestrel—Ruiz's voice, raised for the first time Lena could remember, and beneath it the grind of hulls touching hulls. Ashe's people were coming alongside. Whatever happened next would happen fast, and armed, and bought and paid for.

Ingrid stood, cylinder in both hands, and looked from the wreck's dark shoulder breaking the swell to the besieged survey vessel to the woman dripping on her deck. Then she reached down and pulled Lena to her feet.

"Then we'd better get aboard," she said, "before someone else decides who gets the last word."

Behind them, the Vargr settled another inch into the seabed, letting go of its secrets one breath at a time.
