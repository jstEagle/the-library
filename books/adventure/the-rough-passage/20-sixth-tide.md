## 20. Sixth Tide

The tide came in at 06:14, and with it came the law.

Lena heard it before she saw it—two diesel engines running at a pace no salvage boat bothered with, that flat, purposeful drone that said *we are not here to negotiate*. She was on the aft deck with a mug of coffee she'd stopped drinking, watching the gray water do its gray water things, when Ruiz came out of the wheelhouse and said, without inflection, "They're early."

"You knew they were coming."

"I signaled them four days ago." He said it the way he said everything, like a man reading off a manifest. "Before Ingrid boarded. Before the strongroom came up. Before any of it got complicated."

Lena set the mug down. Four days ago. Before the confession cylinder. Before she'd stood on this deck and heard her father's voice read aloud in a dead man's cadence, eleven names counted out like coins. Before Ashe's divers cut her air line and left her to the cold arithmetic of the deep. Ruiz had been carrying the end of this story in his pocket the whole time, and he'd let the rest of them walk through the middle of it blind.

"You could have told me."

"You'd have told Ashe." He didn't say it cruelly. He said it like a man who'd watched her feed false coordinates to a liaison and memorize true ones, and understood exactly what an Eriksen did under pressure. "I needed the coast guard to arrive at a moment when everything was already on the table. Not before. Before, and it all goes into lawyers' drawers for a decade."

"And if I'd drowned?"

Ruiz looked at her then, and there was something old in his face, something that had been waiting eleven years for this conversation whether he knew it or not. "Then the coast guard would have arrived at a moment when everything was on the table, and you'd have been dead, and I'd have carried that too. I've been carrying things a long time, Eriksen. One more wouldn't have broken me. But it would have been a waste, and I don't like waste."

The first vessel came around the headland at 06:31—white hull, orange trim, the coast guard pennant snapping in a wind that had teeth in it. The second followed ten minutes behind. On the Kestrel, nobody moved much. Pell came up from the galley with his hair uncombed and stood at the rail like a man watching weather. Okafor didn't come up at all; Lena could hear him below, deliberately, loudly, doing something to a pump that did not need doing.

Ashe's fleet was already there. Two vessels holding station four hundred meters off since the previous evening, since her enforcers had come aboard and been met by a crew that had, in the space of one tide, stopped being her crew. The *Meridian* and the *Corvane*. Ashe herself had transferred to the *Meridian* at dawn—Lena had watched the transfer through binoculars, a small figure in a red parka crossing a gangway with the unhurried stride of a woman who had never once been on the wrong side of a transaction.

The coast guard vessel put its bow toward the *Meridian* first.

* * *

What followed was not dramatic. That was the thing Lena couldn't get over, standing at the rail with her hands in her pockets while the morning did its work. She had spent six tides expecting the end to arrive like a wave—some cresting, breaking moment when the truth finally had enough force behind it to sweep everything before it. Instead it arrived as a RIB, four officers in drysuits, and a clipboard.

She could hear fragments across the water when the wind allowed. The officer in charge—tall, gray-haired, a voice like a filing cabinet—spent forty minutes aboard the *Meridian*. Then the RIB came to the Kestrel, and the same forty minutes happened again, this time within earshot.

"State your interest in the wreck designated VGR-09."

Ruiz had the answer ready. Of course he did. "Survey vessel Kestrel, contracted salvage assessment. We located the wreck, breached the cargo compartment, and recovered artifacts and documents relevant to an open maritime fraud case from eleven years ago. The documents are aboard. The artifacts are aboard. Nothing has been sold, moved, or altered."

"You recovered these under whose authority?"

"Under the authority of anyone who'd listen," Ruiz said. "Which, until this morning, was no one."

The officer wrote that down. Lena watched his pen move and thought: *there it is. The whole war, decided by a pen.* Not the diving, not the storms, not the night she'd spent at forty meters with a dying torch and her father's strongroom yawning open in front of her like a mouth. A pen.

They photographed everything. The artifacts, laid out on tarps in the hold where Okafor had arranged them with a conservator's care—the silverwork, the instruments, the pieces of a collection that had been assembled by a man named Halvard and stolen by a man named Eriksen and drowned with eleven innocent crew because one of those men couldn't bear to be caught. The confession cylinder, sealed in an evidence bag that Pell carried with both hands, like a sleeping thing. The Kestrel's log, Ruiz's notes, the false coordinates Lena had fed the liaison—produced, to her surprise, by the liaison himself, whose loyalty to Ashe had apparently been a rental agreement too, and who had decided somewhere around the fourth tide that his interests lay with whoever was going to be holding the paperwork at the end.

The gray-haired officer came to Lena last.

"You're listed as salvage diver, contract holder Ashe Maritime. Name?"

And here it was. The moment she'd been running from for ten years, arriving not with a wave but with a question on a clipboard. Every alias she'd ever worn—Voss, Lindqvist, the two or three she didn't even count anymore—stood in a line behind her like old coats.

"Lena Eriksen," she said.

Her own voice. Her own name. It came out steady, and that surprised her more than anything else that morning. The officer wrote it down. He didn't look up. To him it was a string of letters matching a string of letters in a file, one more fact in a morning full of them, and the anticlimax of it nearly made her laugh. Ten years of hiding, and the hiding ended in a margin note.

"You're Magnus Eriksen's daughter."

"Yes."

"He's listed as deceased. The Vargr."

"He's listed as lost," Lena said. "It's not the same thing. We have his confession. He scuttled his own ship."

The officer looked up then, briefly. Not shock—she'd seen real shock on this deck, on Pell's face, on Ingrid's. This was something quieter. The look of a man adding a final figure to a column that had been running long before he arrived. "Then that's for the prosecutors," he said, and moved on down the list.

* * *

By noon, the *Meridian* had been placed under detention order. Lena watched it happen through the wheelhouse glass: the coast guard vessel holding station off the salvage ship's beam, the orange pennant running up, the small bureaucratic violence of a vessel forbidden to move. Ashe's crew worked the deck with the tight, mechanical efficiency of people whose employer had stopped answering her radio.

Ashe did not come aboard the Kestrel. Lena had half expected her to—had, if she was honest, wanted her to, wanted the confrontation, the scene, the chance to say the things she'd been composing since the first tide. But Ashe was not a woman who attended her own losses. At 13:40 the *Corvane* broke station and turned south, and Lena understood that somewhere aboard the *Meridian*, Corinne Ashe was already doing what she did best: recalculating. The artifacts were impounded. The claim was dead. But the woman who had bought Lena's debt and forced her onto this water had other vessels, other wrecks, other debts in other drawers, and she would be aboard one of them by the end of the month, red parka and unhurried stride, certain as tide. That was the thing about people like Ashe. They didn't lose. They diversified.

The summons would come later—Lena knew that too. There would be depositions. There would be the question of the false coordinates, the aliases, the decade of work done under other women's names. She had fed a lie to a liaison and memorized the truth, and she'd done it easily, and the coast guard would want to know how easily, exactly, and why. She'd answer. She'd answer all of it. But she already knew how the story would sound in a room with lawyers in it: *the daughter of the thief, working under false names, deceiving investigators.* The truth had a shape, and the shape was not entirely innocent, and no reading of a confession cylinder was going to sand that down.

Ruiz appeared beside her at the wheelhouse glass, following her gaze to the *Meridian*.

"You're thinking about the depositions."

"I'm thinking that I lied to that liaison for three days and it felt like breathing."

"It did," Ruiz agreed. "I watched. You were good at it. Better than your father, probably—he only ever lied to people who trusted him, which is easier." He let that sit for a moment. "You want to know the difference? He lied to keep things. You lied to give them back. It won't matter to the prosecutors. It matters to me."

Lena didn't answer. Below them, through the deck plates, she could hear Pell talking to Okafor, the two of them inventorying the impound manifest aloud, item by item, Pell reading numbers and Okafor confirming them with the solemnity of men who understood that they were, in effect, reading a funeral service. Eleven names had been spoken on this deck two nights ago, one per tide owed. Today the names were going into a file. It wasn't a resurrection. It wasn't even justice, not the kind the word promised in stories. It was a clipboard. It was a pen. It was a detention order on a ship and an evidence bag on a cylinder and a prosecutor in an office in Bergen who would spend two years on this before anyone saw a courtroom.

Unglamorous. Slow. Made entirely of paperwork and patience.

It was, Lena decided, the only kind of justice that had ever actually worked.

* * *

Ingrid came aboard at 16:00, cleared by the coast guard as an interested party, which was the bureaucratic phrase for *the daughter of the man they blamed*. She crossed the deck in the flat gray light with her coat buttoned to the throat and stopped in front of Lena the way she had the first time, on the day of the boarding—no greeting, no warmth, just the direct stare of a woman who had spent her inheritance of grief carefully and wasn't finished spending it.

"They're saying restitution," she said. "To the estate. To my father's estate." She said *my father's estate* the way other people said *the scene of an accident*. "Eleven years. And it comes down to an estate claim."

"It comes down to whatever survives," Lena said. "That's what I've learned. Not what's deserved. What survives."

Ingrid looked out at the water, at the buoy they'd left on the wreck site, bobbing its small orange head against the swell. "The artifacts. The Halvard collection. My grandfather's pieces. I saw the manifest." Her voice did something complicated on the word *manifest*. "They're real. After all these years of people telling me my father was a thief, they're real, and they're coming back."

"They're coming back."

"And you." Ingrid turned to her. "You're the one who found them. You're the one who brought up the confession. The coast guard officer told me it was you, alone, on the fifth tide, when Ashe's divers cut your line." Something moved behind her eyes—not softness, not yet, but the ground where softness might eventually be built. "Why? You could have kept it quiet. Sold it. Your father's name would have stayed dead and yours would have stayed hidden. You'd have been free."

Lena thought about the answer she'd been carrying since the reading on the deck, since the eleven names, since the cylinder's brass shell had come apart in her hands and her father's voice had come out of it flat and mechanical and unmistakably his. *I did what was easiest. I always did what was easiest. Lena, if you're hearing this, I was not the man you think. No one is the man anyone thinks.*

"Because I had his confession in my hands," she said, "and I realized I was going to do exactly what he did. Bury it. Keep it. Let someone else carry the blame." She met Ingrid's eyes. "I've been my father's daughter my whole life. I wanted, just once, to be someone else's."

Ingrid said nothing for a long moment. The water slapped the hull. Somewhere below, Pell dropped a wrench and swore at it with real feeling.

"The compass," Ingrid said finally. "The officer mentioned it. The weighted one. It belonged to the expedition."

"It did."

"I'd like to see it. When this is over. When the lawyers are finished." It wasn't a request, exactly, and it wasn't a demand. It was the opening of an account, and both of them knew it. "My father navigated that expedition. If there's a piece of it left that's honest, I'd like it to come home too."

"It will," Lena said. And found that she meant it, and that meaning it cost her something, and that the cost was the point.

* * *

They stayed on station through the evening while the coast guard completed its inventory. Lena stood her watch out of habit more than need, the Kestrel's running lights doubling themselves in the black water, the *Meridian* a cluster of sullen lamps to the north. At 21:00 Ruiz brought her tea she hadn't asked for and stood beside her in the wheelhouse, both of them watching nothing.

"Four days ago," she said. "You signaled them four days ago. Before I even knew what was in the strongroom."

"I knew what was in it." Ruiz turned his mug slowly in his hands. "I've known for eleven years. Not the confession—not the details. But I knew Magnus ordered me off that ship, and I knew why men get ordered off ships, and I spent a decade being the only survivor who wouldn't say it out loud." He looked at her. "You were the second person in eleven years to say it for me. So no, Eriksen. I don't regret the timing. I regret only that it took me this long to find the first one."

Lena drank her tea. Below them the impound manifest was finished; above them the first stars were coming out over the water, thin and cold. The sixth tide was turning. Somewhere under the gray swell the Vargr sat on the bottom with its strongroom open at last, empty of its cargo, empty of its secret, holding nothing now but rust and the shape of a lie that had finally been given a funeral.

Ruiz set down his mug. "There's a message for you," he said. "Came in on the coast guard relay an hour ago. From Bergen." He held out a printed slip, and his face, in the instrument light, was carefully, deliberately blank—the face of a man who had decided that whatever was on the paper belonged to her alone.

Lena took it. The paper was thin, official, stamped. Her eyes went to the signature block first—she was, after all, her father's daughter—and the name there stopped her breath.

It was a law office. It was a restitution notice. And it was addressed, in full, in type, in the permanent ink of the public record, to *Ms. Lena Eriksen*—the first document in ten years to call her by her real name, the first paper in a decade that knew exactly who she was and wanted something from her anyway.

She read it twice. Then she looked up at Ruiz, and the question was already forming, and the tide was already turning, and somewhere south of them Corinne Ashe's fleet was running dark toward whatever came next.

"They want me in Bergen," Lena said. "As a witness. Against her."

Ruiz almost smiled. "Then you'd better decide which name you're sailing under."
