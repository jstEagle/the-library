## 21. Restitution

The harbor office smelled of cold coffee and old varnish, the way every harbor office in Norway seemed to, as if there were a single national supplier of both. Lena sat with her hands flat on a scarred pine table and watched the clerk—Hovden, according to the little brass plate on his desk—work through her file one page at a time.

He was not hurrying. That was the point of him. The coast guard had spent three weeks untangling the Vargr affair, and what remained at the end of it was paperwork: manifests, claims, affidavits, and this—the quiet room where everything finally stopped moving.

"Salvage rights," Hovden said, tapping a form without looking up. "You understand you're not selling them. You're surrendering them. There's no payment."

"I know."

"The artifacts go into impound pending restitution to the Halvard estate. Your name comes off the claim entirely. If the estate's counsel later determines any portion of the recovery was yours by right—" he glanced up for the first time, gray eyes patient behind rimless glasses "—you'd have grounds to contest. Most people do."

"I won't be contesting anything."

He studied her a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded and turned the form around so it faced her. "Then sign where the line is red."

Lena picked up the pen. It was cheap plastic, the kind that came free with bank accounts, and she held it over the line for a moment longer than the act required.

Her father had signed things too. She'd learned that from the confession cylinder, played twice through on Ruiz's battered recorder before they surrendered it: Magnus Eriksen's voice, flattened by salt water and decades, reciting his crimes in the careful cadence of a man who had rehearsed them for years. He had signed manifests, insurance papers, a false logbook. Every signature had been an instrument. He'd used ink the way other men used crowbars.

She signed her own name—Lena Eriksen, full and unhidden—and felt nothing break.

That was the strange part. She'd expected something to break. For ten years the name had been a locked box she carried against her ribs, and now she'd set it down on a government form in a harbor office that smelled of cold coffee, and the world had simply continued. Hovden stamped the page. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither would ever win.

"There," Hovden said. "You're out of it."

Out of it. As if the Vargr were a tide pool she could step over.

* * *

Ingrid Halvard was waiting on the quay when Lena came out, standing at the rail with her hands in the pockets of a wool coat that had been good twenty years ago and had decided, since then, to stop pretending. She didn't turn when Lena approached. She was watching the water, or watching nothing, which amounted to the same thing with her.

"They told me you'd finished," Ingrid said.

"It took four minutes."

"Paperwork always does. It's the only honest part of the law." She turned then. Her face hadn't changed since the deck of the Kestrel—still that cold, level attention, still the look of someone who had learned young that grief was a thing you did alone and thoroughly. But something underneath it had shifted, the way ballast shifts in a hull. Less weight on one side. "The coast guard called my solicitor this morning. The impound order. They wanted to confirm the estate wouldn't challenge your surrender."

"And?"

"And I told them we wouldn't dream of it." A pause. "I also told them I wanted to see you before you left Bergen. They said you'd probably already be gone. I said I'd take that chance."

The wind came off the fjord and pushed between them, smelling of diesel and salt and the particular green rot of old pilings. Down the quay, a fisherman was hosing down his deck, the water running pink with blood and scales.

"You got what you came for," Lena said. "The artifacts. The truth. My father's voice on a tape admitting all of it."

"I got evidence," Ingrid said. "Evidence takes years. There will be hearings. The insurers will fight it—they funded half the original expedition through a shell company nobody's named yet, and they will not want their fingerprints read. My father has been dead thirty-one years, Lena, and the man who ruined him has been dead almost as long, and none of that matters to a courtroom. What matters is whether the paper trail survives, and paper trails are killed by patience more often than malice." She said it without heat, the way you'd report weather. "So no. I don't have what I came for. I have the beginning of what I came for."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." Ingrid looked back out at the water. "Do you know what my father's letters said about the last expedition? Not the official ones. The ones to my mother. He wrote that Magnus Eriksen was the only man he'd ever worked with who made him feel like the sea was survivable. That's a direct quote. My mother kept the letter in a drawer for thirty years and read it maybe six times, because reading it hurt." Her jaw tightened. "And then your father sank his ship with eleven people aboard and let Halvard's name carry the theft, and that letter became the cruelest object in our house. Because it proved how good the lie was. Even the man inside it believed it."

Lena had no answer for that. She'd spent weeks trying to build one—on the Kestrel, in the coast guard interviews, in the rented room above a bakery where she'd slept badly and woken early—but there wasn't one. There was only the fact of it, heavy and finished, like a stone at the bottom of a dredge net.

"He believed it," she said finally. "Right up until the end. Then he stopped believing it all at once, and welded himself in with the proof."

"That's not courage."

"No. It's arithmetic. Eleven lives against one reputation—he ran the numbers and chose the reputation, and then he couldn't live in the answer, so he sealed it shut." She shook her head. "I used to think finding the wreck would give me a father. Some version of him worth having. Instead I found a man who did the math."

Ingrid regarded her for a long moment. "You sound like someone who's done the same math."

"Everyone in my family does the math. It's the family trade." Lena reached into her jacket. "Which is why I'm done with it."

She brought out the compass.

It sat in her palm looking smaller than it had any right to—brass case gone dull green at the seams, glass scratched into a fine web by thirty years of salt, the needle frozen at some bearing that meant nothing to anyone anymore. Except it had never been a compass. That was the joke of it, the inheritance in miniature: a thing shaped exactly like navigation that pointed nowhere true. Weighted, rigged, built by her father's hands to find one specific place on the ocean floor and nothing else. A liar's instrument. A treasure pointer wearing a sailor's face.

Ruiz had recognized it the first week aboard the Kestrel—Vargr expedition issue, stamped on the case back—and had said nothing, because Ruiz was a man who understood that some recognitions should ripen before harvest. By the end, he'd stood beside her on the aft deck while she turned it over in her hands and said only: "Your father gave you a map and called it a direction. That's about right for him."

Now she held it out to Ingrid.

Ingrid didn't take it. She looked at it the way you look at a snake that might be dead. "What is that?"

"A compass. Technically. It's how I found the bow section. My father weighted the needle—it doesn't point north, it points to the strongroom. He built himself a way back to his own crime." Lena turned it so the light caught the stamp on the case back. "Vargr expedition issue. He carried it the whole time. Maybe he meant to come back for it. Maybe he meant it as a message. With him you can never separate the plan from the apology."

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because it's the last piece of him that exists outside a courtroom, and I've been carrying pieces of him for ten years under other people's names, and I'm finished." The wind pushed at her hand; she steadied the compass on her palm. "It belongs to the Vargr's victims. All of them. But eleven of them are dead and can't hold it, and you're the daughter of the man whose name they buried it under. So it should be yours. Not as a trophy. As evidence, if you want. Or as a paperweight, if that's better. It's a lying compass either way. The only honest thing you can do with it is decide what it means."

Ingrid was silent for a long time. A ferry crossed the far water, small and white, going somewhere ordinary.

"My mother threw his letters in the stove after the inquiry," she said at last. "All but one. She said she wasn't going to let a dead thief's handwriting warm the house." Her eyes stayed on the brass case. "I thought I felt the same way. When I chartered that boat, when I boarded the Kestrel—I told myself I was coming for justice. Cold purpose. That's what I called it in my own head, like it was a virtue." She reached out slowly and closed her fingers around the compass, lifting it from Lena's palm. It looked even smaller in her hand. "But I stood on that deck and heard your name, and something in me unclenched that I didn't know was clenched. Because you weren't a monster. You were just a woman doing the math. And I thought—if she can put it down, maybe I'm allowed to pick it up."

"That's not justice."

"No. It's better. Justice is for courtrooms." Ingrid slipped the compass into her coat pocket, and Lena watched the last relic of Magnus Eriksen disappear into the folds of a dead man's grief and a living woman's choice. "Thank you. I'll say that once and never again, so listen closely: thank you."

"Noted."

"And for what it's worth—" Ingrid's mouth moved toward a smile without fully committing "—your father's confession named my father innocent in the first ninety seconds. Thirty-one years of my family carrying a theft, cleared in ninety seconds by the thief himself. I've listened to the tape eleven times. I'll probably listen to it eleven hundred. But that first ninety seconds is the only part that ever gets easier."

* * *

They walked the quay together afterward, not companionably exactly, but in the way of two people who had survived the same storm and were checking each other for damage. Ingrid talked about the hearings to come—the shell companies, the insurer's lawyers, the slow grinding machinery of restitution—and Lena listened, and occasionally asked a question, and discovered to her mild surprise that she wanted to know. Not as a salvager scoping a rival's operation. Just as a person, following a story to its end.

At the head of the pier, Ingrid stopped.

"My solicitor asked if the estate intended to pursue a claim against you personally. Magnus's heir, technically liable for the wronged partner's losses." She said it lightly, testing. "I told him the heir had surrendered everything she had and given the estate its best evidence for free. He said that was legally insufficient. I said I didn't care."

"You could have taken it. The claim. Cleared your father's books off my account."

"I could have." Ingrid adjusted her collar against the wind. "But you gave me the compass. And I've decided we're not going to spend the rest of this doing math at each other. Someone in these two families has to stop." She extended her hand—brief, dry, firm. "Goodbye, Lena Eriksen. I hope the name fits."

"It's still breaking in."

"Everything honest does." She turned and walked away down the quay, coat flapping, a woman carrying a broken compass and thirty-one years of vindication in the same pocket, getting smaller against the gray water until the crowd took her.

Lena stood at the pier's end for a while after she was gone.

Below her, the harbor worked on—crates swung, ropes creaked, a trawler backed out trailing gulls. None of it knew or cared that a debt had died onshore that morning, or that a name had changed owners, or that a brass lie had found its final resting place in a wool coat. The sea kept its own books. It had taken eleven names on one bad night and given back two daughters on a cold morning, and the exchange rate was obscene and permanent and there was nothing anyone could do about it except refuse to pass the bill forward.

She checked her watch. She had a train at fourteen forty, south along the coast, and beyond the train a job—a real one, survey work, legitimate manifest, her actual name on the contract because Ruiz had vouched for her with a phone call and four sentences and the specific gravity of a man who'd served on the Vargr and lived. She'd start Monday. She'd be paid honestly. She'd sleep in bunks with her real name taped to the door, and someday, maybe, it would stop feeling like walking around without skin.

She turned to go, and that was when she saw the man.

He was leaning against the harbor office wall, out of the wind, dressed like a thousand other men on a thousand other quays—gray jacket, soft cap, hands in pockets. But he wasn't watching the boats. He was watching her, and he had been for a while; she could tell by the settled quality of his stillness, the way a heron settles before it strikes.

As he pushed off the wall and started toward her, Lena's hand went instinctively to her jacket pocket, to the space where a compass had been and wasn't anymore, and found nothing there to steady her.

The man stopped an arm's length away and took off his cap.

"Lena Eriksen," he said. "My name is Anders Vinter. I represent the insurers of the original Vargr expedition." He smiled, and it was a professional smile, smooth as a welded seam. "We should talk about what your father's confession is going to cost you."

The gulls went on screaming. The fourteen-forty train suddenly felt very far away.
