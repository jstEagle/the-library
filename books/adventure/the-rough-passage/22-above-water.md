## 22. Above Water

The harbor office in Tromsø smelled of coffee and toner, and the woman behind the desk had a nameplate that read H. SOLBERG and a handshake that lasted exactly as long as it needed to.

"Eriksen," she said, reading the form. "Lena Eriksen."

"That's right."

Solberg looked up at her over the rim of her glasses, and Lena braced for the flicker—the half-second of recognition, the mental search through news archives, *Eriksen, Eriksen, where have I—* The Vargr had been in every paper for a week that spring, eleven years ago, before the story drowned along with the ship. But Solberg only stamped the intake form and slid it across the counter.

"Certifications check out. Ruiz's reference is unusually detailed." A dry note entered her voice. "Three paragraphs on your line-handling alone. I've had captains write less about their wives."

"He's thorough."

"He's old-school." Solberg gathered the papers, squared them against the desk. "Survey work isn't glamorous, Ms. Eriksen. Bathymetry runs, cable-route mapping, harbor inspections. Cold water, long hours, and nobody writes songs about any of it."

"That's the part I liked best," Lena said, and meant it.

Solberg studied her a moment longer—not suspicious, just the professional habit of a woman who'd hired enough deckhands to know that some of them were running from something. Whatever she saw, she apparently decided it wasn't her problem. She held out a lanyard with a crew badge clipped to it: ERIKSEN, L. — SURVEY TECH, MV NORDLYS.

"Vessel's at Berth Fourteen. Ruiz said you'd want to see her before you signed anything final."

"He was right."

The badge was plastic and cheap and weighed nothing at all, and Lena carried it out of the office like it was made of lead and gold at once.

* * *

Berth Fourteen sat at the far end of the commercial quay, past the fish-processing plants and the chandlery with its window full of rope and brass. The Nordlys was nothing to look at—a forty-meter survey vessel, white hull gone gray at the waterline, a stubby A-frame aft and a sensor pod slung beneath her keel like a chin. Working boat. Honest lines. No history anyone cared about.

Lena stood on the quay for a full minute before boarding, and let herself feel how strange it was.

For ten years she had stepped onto vessels the way a thief steps into a house—quickly, quietly, under names that fit like borrowed coats. *Karlsen. Vik. Brandt.* Each one worn until it frayed, then folded away and abandoned in some port town's records office. She'd gotten good at it. That was the thing no one warned you about aliases: they get comfortable. You start answering to them in your sleep. You start forgetting which name is the costume and which one is the wound.

She climbed the gangway. Her boots rang on the steel, and no one challenged her, because the manifest at the top of the ramp had her real name on it, typed, official, hers.

Ruiz was waiting on the aft deck, leaning against the rail with a paper cup of coffee and the expression of a man who had been standing there long enough to enjoy it.

"You're early," he said.

"You're older."

"I'm allowed. You're not." He handed her the cup. It was terrible, machine coffee, scorched and thin, and she drank it anyway because it tasted like every survey vessel she'd ever worked and none of the ones she'd fled. "Solberg give you grief?"

"Three paragraphs on my line-handling."

"I could've done five. She'd have fallen asleep." He turned to face the water. Below them, the harbor moved with its morning business—trawlers coming in heavy, a pilot boat cutting its white seam toward the headland, gulls arguing over something at the fish dock. Ordinary noise. Ordinary light. "I told them everything they needed and nothing they didn't. That's the trick of a reference. Same as a confession, really. Just with better outcomes."

Lena looked at him. Eleven years ago he'd been the mate Magnus ordered off the Vargr the night she sailed, and he'd carried that order like a stone in his chest ever since—she'd seen it finally set down on the Kestrel's deck, the night they read the cylinder aloud, when his voice cracked on the fourth name and he kept reading anyway. He was thinner now than he'd been at sea. Lighter, too, though that didn't show in the body.

"They ask about the Vargr?" she asked.

"Coast guard report's public record. I gave them the short version: salvage dispute, resolved, artifacts impounded, restitution pending." He shrugged. "They asked if you were reliable. I said you were the best diver I'd worked with in thirty years, and that when the money got ugly, you chose the dead over the payout. In this industry, that's a stronger reference than any certification."

"It might also be a red flag."

Ruiz smiled, small and crooked. "It's the industry's problem, not yours."

* * *

She spent the afternoon learning the Nordlys from keel to masthead, because that was what she always did on a new vessel—only this time there was no one to hide it from, no reason to play the competent stranger instead of the expert she was. She crawled the engine room with Okafor's voice in her head (*check the bilge alarms first, girl, everything else is decoration*) and found them new-installed and clean. She ran her hand along the multibeam transducer housing in the hull, felt the solid click of good mounting. She stood in the dry lab while the chief scientist, a young man named Brekke who talked with his hands, walked her through the sonar suite with the pride of someone showing off a first apartment.

"—and the processing workstation's new this season, so if anything freezes, don't take it personally, it takes everything personally—"

"Brekke." She pointed at a cable run. "That's crossed. Port transducer's feeding the starboard channel."

He blinked, traced it, went faintly pink. "Huh. How did you—"

"Lucky guess." But she was smiling as she said it, and Brekke laughed, and re-ran the cable, and by evening the whole crew knew the new survey tech had eyes like a hydrographic chart. It was such a small thing. It was the largest thing in the world. To be known for what she could do instead of what she was hiding.

At dusk she went up to the wheelhouse. The night watchman nodded her through without question—her name was on the manifest; that was all a manifest was for—and she stood at the windows looking down the length of the deck, the A-frame silhouetted against the last orange band of sky, the hawsers slack and honest in their cleats.

Tomorrow the Nordlys sailed for a cable-survey run off Senja. Two weeks of grid lines and sediment cores and sonar returns of absolutely nothing worth stealing. She had read the contract three times, not because she distrusted it but because she wanted to memorize the feeling of signing her own name to something clean.

Her phone buzzed. A message from Ingrid:

*Restitution hearing confirmed for the 14th. Eleven families represented. My father's name comes off the maritime register's disgrace listing the same day. They're printing a correction.*

Lena typed back: *He would have hated the paperwork.*

Then, after a moment: *Good. Let it be paperwork. Paperwork is what justice looks like when it works.*

Ingrid's reply came slow, the way her messages always did, each word considered: *My mother used to say my father died of shame. I'm learning he died of someone else's. Thank you for the compass. It sits on my desk pointing north, like an honest instrument should.*

Lena put the phone away and stayed at the wheelhouse windows until the orange faded and the harbor lights came on, doubled and trembling in the black water.

* * *

She dreamed about the Vargr that night, in the bunk the Nordlys assigned her—narrow, clean, smelling of new paint. But the dream had changed shape. For ten years it had been the sinking: the list of the deck, the black water coming up the passageways, her father's silhouette in the wheelhouse glass, always turning away. Now she dreamed instead of the strongroom, welded shut from within, and in the dream she stood outside it on the seabed with her dive light steady, and she understood at last that the welds weren't a tomb. They were a letter. Her father had written the truth in steel because he couldn't bear to say it aloud, and it had taken eleven tides and two daughters to open the envelope.

She woke before dawn with salt-dry eyes and no weight on her chest.

On the quay, the morning shift was loading stores. Ruiz was already aboard, arguing cheerfully with the bosun about a winch. Brekke waved at her from the dry lab, a cable in one fist, coffee in the other. Solberg came down the ramp with the final manifest on a clipboard and held it out.

"Sign here," she said. "And here. And initial there."

Lena signed. *Lena Eriksen*, in her own handwriting, which she hadn't used on an official document in a decade and which came out steadier than she expected.

Solberg tucked the clipboard under her arm and paused, one foot on the gangway. "Ruiz says you're good in bad water."

"Ruiz says a lot of things."

"He also says you'll tell me if something's wrong with the plan." Solberg's eyes were level, unhurried. "That's rarer than diving skill. Most people who've been underwater too long stop believing the surface counts."

"The surface counts," Lena said. "I'm counting on it."

The Nordlys cast off at 0700 sharp. Lena took her station in the dry lab, headset on, screens glowing, while Brekke called the transit plan and the helmsman acknowledged and the harbor fell away behind them in the gray-gold light. Through the port window she watched Tromsø shrink to a skyline, then a smudge, then nothing but sea and sky and the long white wake unspooling behind them like a thread being paid out clean.

No alias in the logbook. No false name on the muster. No debt riding in the hold, no father's shadow bending over the charts, no rival fleet on the horizon. Just a vessel, a job, a crew that knew her name and needed nothing else from it.

Ruiz appeared in the lab doorway around noon, two coffees in hand. He passed her one and leaned against the doorframe, watching the sonar screen paint the seafloor in soft colors—sand, gravel, rock, nothing hidden, nothing buried.

"So," he said. "What does it feel like? Being above water?"

Lena considered the question honestly, the way she'd learned to consider everything since the sixth tide. The weight was still there—grief doesn't lift, it just changes density—but it rode differently now. Distributed. Shared among the people who'd earned a piece of it: Ruiz with his letter fragment, Ingrid with her compass, eleven names spoken aloud into the wind off the Kestrel's deck, one per tide owed.

"Lighter," she said. "Not empty. Lighter."

Ruiz nodded like a man filing something important, drained his coffee, and pushed off from the doorframe. "First survey line starts at 1400. Try to look impressed by the mud."

"I'm always impressed by the mud."

He laughed and went forward, and Lena turned back to her screens. The Nordlys drove north into open water, her hull humming, her manifest clean, and Lena Eriksen—diver, daughter, debtor no longer—stood her watch under her own name, going wherever the work was, staying exactly as long as she chose.

The sea ahead was gray and ordinary and entirely unhidden, and she sailed into it glad.
