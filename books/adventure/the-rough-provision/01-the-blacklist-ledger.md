## 1. The Blacklist Ledger

The ledger behind the bar of the Wayfarer's Rest was a small, greasy book that Oskar kept under the till, and Tamsin Okonkwo's name was in it twice: once for the tab they'd been carrying since the first frost, and once — in Oskar's careful, apologetic handwriting — as *paid in full, do not extend further credit*. Two countries had blacklisted them from the mountains. Halden's Rest had merely blacklisted them from beer.

Tamsin sat at the end of the counter anyway, nursing a tea that had gone lukewarm an hour ago, and watched the rain come down on the town like it was trying to drown something specific. Halden's Rest in October was a wet fist of a place: sawmills shuttered for the season, the border post glinting its indifferent red-and-white across the river, and everywhere the mountains rising up behind everything, huge and white-lit even through cloud, close enough to touch with your eyes and never with anything else.

They were beautiful. That was the cruelty of them. Four years since the Karakol incident, and Tamsin still caught themselves looking at the high country the way you look at a photograph of someone who died hating you.

"Tab's at forty-two," Oskar said, not unkindly, wiping the counter in front of them though there was nothing to wipe.

"I know what my tab is."

"Just keeping us both honest."

Honesty. There was a word with teeth in it. Tamsin turned the teacup a quarter-rotation, watching the tannin ring it left on the wood, and thought about the arithmetic of their life as it currently stood. Eleven months since the last paying client — a bird researcher who'd wanted the low valleys and hadn't asked questions, because the low valleys didn't require anyone to ask about Karakol, or about the eleven days Tamsin had spent descending alone with a dead man's pack and no body, or about the inquiry afterward where the word *abandoned* had been used, formally, into a microphone, by a man named Corrigan whose face Tamsin could still draw from memory, down to the small scar bisecting his left eyebrow.

Rent was two weeks behind. The boots needed resoling before winter, not after. And somewhere south of the border, in a country that had revoked their certification with a stamp so casual it might have been approving a fishing license, there was a mother who read the blacklist notices the way other people read obituaries.

Forty-two. Plus rent. Plus boots. The ledger in Tamsin's head was longer than Oskar's.

The door opened on a gust of cold air and a woman walked in out of the rain wearing a coat too good for this town — charcoal wool, tailored, the kind of coat that had never been rained on until tonight. She was maybe fifty, grey at the temples, with the flat, unhurried way of moving that Tamsin had learned to read as either money or government, and sometimes both. She scanned the room the way you scan a ridgeline: quickly, completely, dismissing most of it.

Then she came to the end of the bar and sat down one stool away from Tamsin, which in an empty room was a choice.

"Oskar," she said. "Whatever he's having. And one for yourself."

Oskar's eyebrows did a small, complicated dance. Nobody bought Oskar drinks. "Whisky?"

"For him. Water for me." She looked at Tamsin then, directly, and her eyes were pale grey and entirely without warmth, which Tamsin found almost restful. Warmth was what people used when they wanted something and felt guilty about it. This woman felt no guilt whatsoever. "Tamsin Okonkwo."

"Not buying."

"I'm not selling. I'm hiring." She set a folded paper on the counter between them, squared its edges to the bar with one finger, and left it there like a card player leaving a hand face-down. "Ilse Varga. Geological consultant. I need a guide into the Karveth foothills, upper drainage, seven days, possibly eight. Cash."

The whisky arrived. Tamsin didn't touch it. "Consultants don't go into the upper Karveth in October. The passes are already loading. Any geology worth doing up there is done in June."

"The deposit I'm interested in doesn't keep a calendar."

"Then find another guide."

"There isn't another guide." Varga said it plainly, without flattery, which was somehow worse than flattery. "I checked. Everyone certified within three hundred kilometers is either booked, retired, or unwilling. Which leaves you."

*Which leaves you.* The sentence landed in the old bruise and settled there, familiar as a stone in a boot. Tamsin picked up the whisky finally, not to drink but to hold, letting the glass fog under their fingers. "You know why there isn't another guide."

"I know what the official record says."

"And?"

"And I've read enough official records to know they're written by the people who benefit from them." She turned on the stool, unhurried, and Tamsin had the sudden, disorienting sense of being interviewed rather than interviewing — of being weighed by someone who had already decided the number. "I'm not interested in Karakol. I'm interested in whether you can read terrain, move fast above treeline, and keep your mouth shut. Everything else is weather."

Everything else is weather. As if the thing that had hollowed Tamsin's life out and salted the ground of it were a front that had blown through. Tamsin laughed — a short, ugly sound that surprised even them — and drank half the whisky, because apparently the body had decided to accept the bribe before the mind finished refusing it.

"How much cash?" they said.

Varga told them. It was more than the tab, the rent, and the boots together. It was, in fact, roughly what a certified guide would have charged, which meant she knew the rates, which meant she'd done her homework down to the last humiliating detail.

"For eight days," Tamsin said. "Into terrain nobody's surveyed since the war. With a consultant who won't say what she's consulting on."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

Varga stood, buttoned her coat, and left the folded paper on the counter. "Then I'll drink this water, admire your mountains, and hire the next worst option, and one of us will probably die of it. You strike me as someone who'd rather that happened farther away." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, rain silvering the light behind her. "Six a.m. I'll be at the bridge below the mill. Bring your own rope. I'll know if it's bad rope."

She was gone. The door swung twice and stilled. Oskar collected the empty glass, glanced at the folded paper, and had the decency to look away while Tamsin opened it.

Cash. A route marked in confident blue ink up the Sereth drainage and into the high cirque where the maps went vague — *glaciated, unsurveyed* — and, at the bottom, in type smaller than the rest, a line that made the cold walk up Tamsin's spine on small deliberate feet:

*Discretion essential. No radio contact. No check-ins.*

No check-ins. Every legitimate expedition filed them. Every rescue protocol depended on them. Tamsin had spent four years living inside the consequences of a trip where the check-ins stopped coming, and here was a stranger asking them to walk into the high country with the radios dark by design.

The sensible thing — the thing the person Tamsin had been before Karakol would have done without hesitation — was to fold the paper closed, slide it back across the bar, and let some other fool carry whatever this was up the mountain.

Tamsin looked at the mountains instead. Cloud had torn open along the Karveth massif, and the evening light was coming through sideways, gilding the icefields, laying the glacier's long blue shadow across the valley like something the mountain was pointing at. *Glaciated, unsurveyed.* Somewhere up in that white silence was whatever Varga wanted badly enough to pay a blacklisted guide full rate and ask for darkness instead of safety.

Their father had taken them up there once, when they were nine. Same drainage. He'd put his hand flat on the glacier's moraine and said, *the river remembers everything it's given*, and Tamsin had thought it was just a thing fathers said.

Forty-two plus rent plus boots. The ledger didn't care about omens. The ledger only cared about totals.

"Oskar," Tamsin said, pocketing the paper. "Put the tab on my room. I'm settling it Friday."

"You're going up there." It wasn't a question. Oskar wiped the counter again, slower. "Karakol wasn't your fault, Tam. Half this town knows it."

"Half this town," Tamsin said, standing, pulling on their jacket, "isn't the half that matters."

Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine drifting veil, and the cold came off the water sharp enough to bite. The bridge below the mill was a dark shape against the darker river, and Tamsin crossed it once, just to see, boots ringing on the planks, the current sliding black and patient underneath toward the lowlands — carrying, as it carried everything, silt and secrets and the melted leavings of the ice, down and down toward a sea that kept what it was given.

Six a.m., then. Rope, rack, the old tent, the stove with the temperamental pump. And the compass — they'd have to dig the compass out of the drawer where it lived under expired permits and a photograph they couldn't look at often. Their father's compass. Brass-cased, needle worn smooth, the kind of instrument that outlived its makers.

Tamsin stood on the bridge a moment longer, watching the water, feeling the pull of the high country like a hook set deep in the chest — shame and longing braided so tight after four years they'd become a single cord, impossible to tell which was holding which.

Then they went home to pack.

* * *

Packing took ninety minutes, because packing was the one ritual grief had never managed to take. Gear laid out on the floorboards in rows, checked by touch in the dark if it came to that: rope coiled over shoulder and elbow, eight wraps, tied off; rack racked; stove pumped and lit and killed again; tent poles run through their sleeves. The compass came out of the drawer last, as it always did, and Tamsin sat on the floor with it in their palm for a long time, thumb moving over the dented brass baseplate where a nine-year-old had dropped it on granite and been forgiven instantly.

*North is a promise,* their father used to say. *Everything else is negotiation.*

He'd gone into these same mountains twenty-six years ago with a survey crew and come back different — quieter, watchful, checking the locks twice — and eighteen months after that he was dead, officially of a road accident on a stretch of road where nothing had ever crashed before or since. Tamsin had been fourteen. They had built their whole life on the assumption that the mountains were honest, that altitude stripped people down to what they actually were, that the truth lived up where the trees gave out. It had taken a government inquiry and a man with a scar through his eyebrow to teach them that the biggest lies were filed, stamped, and archived at sea level.

The compass needle swung, found north, held it. Tamsin snapped the lid shut and packed it top-of-bag, where a hand could find it blind.

Sleep came thin and shallow, the way it always did the night before the first steps back uphill. In the grey hour before dawn Tamsin lay listening to the river through the wall, running fast with autumn melt off the high glaciers, and let themselves feel, for exactly one minute, the fear. Not of the mountain — the mountain was the easy part, the mountain was arithmetic and respect. The fear of being seen up there again, by other climbers, by whatever passed for authority in the high valleys now, by the ghost of who they'd been at twenty-nine with a clean certification and a spotless record and a partner named Dev who had trusted them with his life and lost it.

One minute. Then Tamsin got up, dressed, ate standing, shouldered the pack, and stepped out into a world rinsed clean and freezing, stars still burning hard overhead, breath smoking.

The bridge below the mill was iced at the edges. Ilse Varga was already there, standing mid-span with a small pack that was far too light for eight days in glaciated terrain, looking upstream toward the mountains with the stillness of a heron.

"You're early," Tamsin said.

"So are you." She turned. Her eyes went over the gear with quick professional economy — rope, rack, the repaired tear on the pack lid — and something in her posture eased by a degree she probably believed was invisible. "You pack like someone who's done this hungry."

"I've done everything hungry." Tamsin stopped an arm's length away. "Rules, since we're doing this. We move at my pace. We turn around when I say. If I say the route's dead, the route's dead, and no amount of consulting changes it. And when we're above treeline, you do what I tell you the first time I tell you, because up there hesitation is how people end up as names in inquiries."

Something flickered across Varga's face at the word *inquiries* — there and gone, too fast to name. "Agreed."

"One more thing." Tamsin shifted the pack higher on their shoulders and nodded upstream, toward the vague white places where the maps gave up. "Whatever you're looking for up there — ore, oil, old bones, buried money — when we find it, I don't want to know what it is. I guide, you consult, we come down, we're strangers again. That's the deal that gets you a guide at all."

For a moment Varga said nothing, and the river filled the silence, loud and cold and constant, running down out of the ice as it had run for ten thousand years, keeping every promise its source required of it.

"That's the deal," Varga said.

They shook hands once, brief and hard, and turned upstream together into the blue dark, two liars with a rope between them, walking toward the part of the map where the truth was supposed to live.

Behind them, far downstream, the lights of Halden's Rest went out one by one, and the ledger stayed open on the bar, waiting to be settled.
