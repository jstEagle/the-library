## 2. Gear With No Owners

The treeline gave up on us the way most things did in the Karveth — gradually, then all at once. First the pines thinned to scrub, twisted and salt-blasted by wind that came off the icefields like a debt collector's knock. Then the scrub shrank to krummholz, knee-high and stubborn, and then even that surrendered, and there was nothing left but stone and lichen and the long grey throat of the valley opening above us like a question nobody wanted answered.

Tamsin Okonkwo had walked this transition a hundred times in a dozen ranges, and it never stopped feeling like crossing out of one country into another — the green country of the living into the white country where things were kept. Bodies. Secrets. Old rope from other people's mistakes.

Behind me, boots crunched on scree with a rhythm I'd already learned to read. Ilse Varga walked like an accountant: measured, economical, no wasted motion, no pleasure in it either. Three days up-valley and she hadn't once asked how much farther, hadn't complained about the weight of her pack or the cold that got into the joints at night. She paid attention to her footing the way she paid attention to everything — too closely, and without ever seeming to look.

"Water," she said now, and it wasn't a request. It was a checkmark.

I pointed my chin at the river without breaking stride. It ran fast and pale beside us, braided with meltwater, loud enough that we'd been shouting our conversations for two days. The Karveth's main artery, feeding every farm and town downstream for two hundred kilometres, and here at its shoulder it was still young and violent, all spray and argument.

She crouched at the bank, filled her bottle, and while she drank I watched her watch the water. Not drinking it so much as reading it.

"You've done this before," I said. "High country."

"I've done a lot of things before." She screwed the cap back on. "Keep moving. I want the moraine before dark."

*She wants the moraine.* In three days she'd never once said *I'm tired* or *let's camp*. She gave coordinates and bearings like a woman reciting lines she'd memorized in another life, and if I deviated from the route she'd prescribed, she corrected me within minutes — not angrily, just precisely, the way you'd nudge a horse back onto a trail. I'd guided ministers' sons and film crews and one very famous cellist through worse country than this, and none of them had ever known the route better than I did.

Ilse Varga knew it better. That was the thing I kept turning over at night in the tent, listening to the wind work the fly. A geological consultant who knew the Karveth's unmapped tributaries by name.

But she was paying cash, in advance, in a currency that still spent, and Halden's Rest had exactly one person willing to walk into the high range in late autumn, and that person owed four months of back rent to a landlady who'd started leaving notes under the door that weren't notes so much as verdicts. So I walked.

* * *

We made the moraine by late afternoon, when the light had gone the colour of old pewter and the temperature dropped the ten degrees it always dropped between three and four o'clock, like the mountain closing a till. The lateral moraine ran along the valley's east flank, a rampart of grey rubble dumped by a glacier that had been retreating since before my father was born, and beyond it, higher, the ice itself showed its face — dirty blue in the crevasses, white and blind on the seracs, hanging over the upper valley like something deciding whether to fall.

That was when I saw the smoke.

Not smoke, exactly. Heat-shimmer. A thin vertical distortion in the air above the flat bench of ground where the moraine met the glacier's toe, the kind of shimmer a stove pipe makes on a still evening. My body understood it before my mind did; my feet were already slowing, my hand already up, and Ilse stopped behind me without asking why, which was itself an answer about what kind of consultant she was.

"Somebody's home," I said quietly.

"That's not possible." Her voice had changed. Not louder. Tighter. "There's no party scheduled on this drainage. There's no permit issued for this drainage. I checked."

"People don't always check with you first."

"They do," she said, "in this case."

I looked at her. She was staring up-valley at the shimmer with an expression I couldn't file — not surprise exactly, because surprise is a soft thing, and this had edges. It looked more like recognition. Like a woman seeing a bill arrive that she'd known was coming.

"We can go around," I said. "West flank, cross below the icefall, lose half a day."

"No." She said it too fast, then caught herself, smoothed it down. "No. We should look. If someone's in distress—"

"If someone's in distress they'll have a radio, and we'll hear them on the band. We haven't heard a voice on any channel in three days. That's its own kind of strange, up here. This valley's usually chatty. Survey teams, heli-tour operators, the lot."

"Then we look," she said, "quietly."

So we looked quietly. I took us off the trail and up the moraine's spine, using the boulders for cover out of a habit older than my blacklist, and Ilse followed with a silence that was beginning to frighten me more than the smoke did, because she moved like that naturally. Nobody learns to move like that doing geology.

* * *

The camp sat on the bench like a sentence waiting for its verb.

Four dome tents, pitched properly, guyed out against the wind with the neatness of people who expected to come back to them. A cook tent larger than the rest, its vestibule zipped halfway. Two stoves — a primus outside for melting snow, a wood-and-gas hybrid inside the cook tent whose pipe was the source of the shimmer. Gear laid out on ground cloths: coils of rope, racks of carabiners hung in careful order, drill casings stacked like cordwood, crates stencilled with a government survey mark I recognized because you learn to recognize the marks of the agencies that ruin you.

And no people.

I stood at the edge of the camp for a full minute before I let myself step into it, and in that minute I catalogued everything my eyes brought me, because that's the job. That's the whole job, when you get down to it: notice what's wrong before it notices you.

Wrong thing one: the stove was warm. I put my bare hand near the cook tent's pipe and felt heat radiating off it, real heat, recent heat. Someone had lit that stove within the hour. Maybe less.

Wrong thing two: the snow. Around the tents, the snow held boot prints — dozens of them, churned and overlapping, the ordinary chaos of a working camp. But at the perimeter, on every side, the prints simply stopped. A clean line, as if someone had drawn a border in the snow and everyone had stepped across it and vanished. No tracks led down-valley toward us. No tracks led up toward the glacier. No tracks led anywhere. Four days of weather wouldn't have erased them that evenly; we'd had flurries, not burial.

Wrong thing three: the radios. There were two of them, sitting in the cook tent on the folding table, and both were switched off. Not dead — I checked the batteries, and they were charged, antennae intact, sets functional. Switched off. Nobody switches off their radios at a working high camp. Up there the radio isn't equipment, it's a heartbeat. You leave it on in your sleep.

Wrong thing four, and this was the one that put the cold in me deeper than the altitude did: the food. On the table beside the radios sat three tin mugs of tea. Cold now, skin formed on the surface, but poured. Poured and abandoned. And beside them, a map spread open and weighted at the corners with stones, marked up in grease pencil — and the markings didn't stop at the glacier's toe the way every official map of the Karveth stopped. They went up. Into the accumulation zone. Along a route I'd never seen drawn, bearing northeast toward a col that didn't have a name on any map I owned, including the ones in my father's handwriting.

"Tamsin." Ilse stood at the nearest sleeping tent, and her voice had gone somewhere flat and careful, the voice you use around wounded animals and unstable cornices. "Come look at this."

Inside the tent: four sleep systems, rolled and ready. Four. But racked against the wall, five packs, five harnesses, five pairs of mountaineering boots in descending sizes. Five of everything, and bedding for four.

"One of them slept somewhere else," I said. "Or one of them never came back to camp."

"Or," Ilse said, "one of them left before the others did."

"And went where? Barefoot?" I nodded at the boots. "Look at the snow line again. Nobody walked out of this camp. Not one person, not five. People don't evaporate."

Ilse had gone pale. I mean that literally — the colour drained out of her face in a slow tide, and up until that moment I would have told you the woman didn't have a face that could do that. She'd watched me cross a swollen glacial stream on a rotten log two days ago with the mild interest of someone watching traffic. Now she looked like the log had broken under her.

"We're going down," she said.

"We just got here."

"We are going *down*." She was already moving, out of the tent, into the grey light, and there was something in her posture I'd only seen once before, years ago, on a different mountain, in a different life — the set of a person's shoulders when they understand, ahead of everyone else, exactly how bad a thing is going to be. "Right now. Pack nothing. Touch nothing else. Down."

"Hold on." I planted myself in her path, and I'll admit part of why was the money and part of why was pride and part of why — the largest part, though I didn't examine it yet — was that mug of cold tea sitting on that table like an accusation. "You hired me to reach the upper basin. You've got a route marked on that map that goes past anything your own agency publishes. You knew this team was up here. You knew before we left Halden's Rest."

For a moment she said nothing, and the wind filled the silence, moving down the glacier the way it always moves in the evening, cold and enormous, combing the ice for whatever the day had left loose.

"Okonkwo." She used my name the way you'd use a rope — deliberately, knowing it would hold, knowing it was the last piece of gear between us and something long. "In eleven years of fieldwork I have found two camps like this one. Do you know what I found inside them?"

"Tell me."

"The first time, nothing. Nothing at all, except the smell, after the spring thaw." Her eyes didn't waver, and that was somehow worse. "The second time, I was ordered to report that I'd found nothing at all. I did as I was ordered. I have regretted it in a low-grade way every day since, the way you regret a bad tooth. So believe me when I tell you that whatever happened here did not happen by accident, and whoever made it happen may still be close, and every minute we stand in this camp arguing we are standing in the open."

Something moved on the glacier.

It was small — a shift of light, a flicker at the edge of vision, the kind of motion your eye catches and your mind files as wind-blown spindrift. But I'd spent twenty years teaching my eye not to file those things, and I turned toward it, and for maybe two seconds I saw it clearly: a figure, or the suggestion of one, far up the ice among the seracs, dark against the blue, standing utterly still. Watching. Then the light shifted off the ice and it was gone, and I couldn't have sworn to it under oath, and I knew that too.

"Down," I said.

We went down fast, faster than was strictly safe on the moraine in failing light, and Ilse matched me stride for stride without a word, and neither of us looked back, and both of us listened — to the scree, to the wind, to the river rising beside us in the dusk, carrying its cold cargo of meltwater down toward the towns that drank it without ever asking what the mountains put in.

It was full dark when we finally stopped, two hours below the camp, in the last pocket of scrub pine where we could risk a stove flame shielded by rock. Ilse sat with her arms around her knees, staring into the small blue hiss of the burner, and I watched her decide something. I could see it happening, the way you see weather building over a ridge.

"There's a notebook," she said at last, not looking at me. "If we go down tomorrow — all the way down — there's something I have to show you in Halden's Rest. Something that will explain the camp. Explain the team. Explain me."

"You're a geologist," I said. "That's what you'll tell me. You're a geologist with excellent contacts."

"I'm not going to tell you anything tonight." She fed a twig into the stove, and the flame leaned toward it, hungry. "Tonight I'm going to keep watch until dawn, and you're going to sleep, and tomorrow you're going to wish you'd never taken this job. That's the honest forecast. It's the only one I can give you."

I lay back on my pad and stared at the underside of the fly, where the wind drew patterns in the fabric like water writing on sand, and I thought about five packs and four beds, and tea poured for someone who never came, and a figure on the ice that might have been nothing at all.

Sleep took its time. When it finally came, it came thin and shallow, and somewhere in the small hours I woke once to a sound I couldn't place — a single sharp crack echoing down from the glacier, like a door closing very far away.

By then, Ilse was already sitting up, fully dressed, watching the dark.

She'd heard it too.
