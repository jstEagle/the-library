## 3. What the River Keeps

The river had opinions, and all of them were bad.

Ilse Varga heard it before she saw it — a low grinding beneath the wind, like millstones turning somewhere under the world. By the time they rounded the shoulder of the moraine and the Karveth's upper gorge opened below them, the sound had become a physical thing, a pressure against the sternum, and she understood why Tamsin Okonkwo had stopped talking an hour ago. Some landscapes you narrate. This one narrated you.

*Keep it professional,* she told herself, for the hundredth time since Halden's Rest. *You are a geological consultant. You are mildly interested in quartzite. You have never in your life read a signals intercept or memorized a service weapon's field-strip sequence.*

She was growing tired of the lie. It sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Below, the river ran white through a slot of gray rock, churning with the particular violence of snowmelt funneled into bedrock. Spray hung in the air even at this distance, a fine cold mist that beaded on her eyelashes and froze there when she blinked. The morning sun came off the glacier fields above in flat silver sheets, and everything — the scree, the ice, the water — was the same color as her fear.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the drop with their hands jammed in their pockets, reading the gorge the way other people read documents: quickly, completely, with a face that gave nothing back. Ilse had watched them do it for two days now. Watched them count switchbacks without counting visibly, note cloud movement over the eastern cols, glance at the sun and then at their watch and then say nothing at all. She had filed each observation away in the compartment where she kept things she would need later, and she was beginning to suspect that Tamsin Okonkwo was doing exactly the same with her.

"You want to go down?" she asked. It came out wrong — too much like a question about more than the trail.

"I want to know what happened to them." Tamsin didn't look at her. "Camp was warm, Varga. Stove still holding heat. Radios charged and silent. Nobody walks away from a warm stove in the Karveth unless something walked them off it." They turned, finally, and their eyes were the color of the river's quieter pools — dark, deep, and moving fast underneath. "And you knew. Back at camp. You looked at those tents like you'd seen a ghost, and then you ordered me downstream like you were paying by the hour."

The lie shifted in her chest. She reached for the consultant's voice and found it wasn't there anymore.

"Downstream is where the answers are," she said instead. "If they went into the water—"

"Who said anything about water?"

"The river did." She nodded at the gorge. "Look at it. Six people vanish from a survey camp and every one of them goes *up*? In boots they'd worn for three weeks? No. Somebody went down. Somebody always goes down."

Tamsin held her gaze for a long moment. Wind pulled at their collar, snapped the loose strap of their pack. Then, without a word, they started down the goat track toward the water, and Ilse followed, and the lie stayed where it was, heavy and patient, waiting its turn.

* * *

They descended for two hours through country that got meaner the lower it went. The trail — if it deserved the word — was a suggestion of ledges stitched along the gorge wall, slick with meltwater, dropping away on the left into the roar. Tamsin moved like the mountain had personally trained them: weight always forward, hands finding holds before the eyes needed them, never once looking down. Ilse moved like what she was — competent, careful, and quietly terrified — and hated herself for the difference.

It was Tamsin who stopped first.

They didn't shout. They just went still, mid-stride, one hand rising in a flat gesture that needed no translation, and Ilse felt the day change temperature.

"There," Tamsin said.

At first she couldn't find it. The rapids below were a chaos of white — boulders wearing helmets of foam, water exploding between them and reassembling itself downstream. Then the foam parted around a shape that did not move with the water, and her stomach dropped through the floor of her body.

A man. Snagged in the V of rocks at the rapid's throat, half-submerged, his parka inflated around him like a failed parachute. One arm trailed downstream. His legs were pinned under a shelf of stone, and the river worked at him patiently, the way rivers do, with no malice and no hurry, because it had all the time the mountain would give it.

He'd been in the water long enough for the cold to keep him. Not long enough for the cold to finish him. Days, not weeks. The thought arrived with terrible precision, the way facts do when they're worst.

"That's Osei," Ilse heard herself say. Her voice sounded borrowed. "Kwabena Osei. Geodetics."

"You know him." Tamsin's voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than shouting. "By name."

"He's on the team manifest. I read it before we—" She stopped. The sentence had a cliff edge in it and she could feel her boots on the lip.

"Before we left," Tamsin finished for her. "You read the manifest of a missing government survey team before agreeing to guide a stranger into the range where they vanished. Consultants don't do that, Varga."

The river filled the silence between them, roaring, indifferent.

"We can't reach him from here," Ilse said, because the alternative was answering.

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But the river will. Give it a day, maybe two, and it'll hand him to the shallows below the bend. Or—" they squinted upstream, jaw working, "—or we go up. There's a ledge system on the far side. Old flood debris. If I traverse high and come down behind those boulders, I can get a line on him."

"It's suicide."

"It's Tuesday." And there it was — the first flash of something underneath the pragmatism, dry and black and human. "Stay here. Don't move off the rock. If I go in, throw the rope and don't be sentimental about the coil."

He crossed the gorge wall like a rumor, and Ilse stood alone on the ledge with the spray freezing on her lashes and her heart doing arithmetic it didn't like.

* * *

It took him forty minutes, and she spent all of them rehearsing.

That was the ugly truth of her profession — the lying was never the hard part. The hard part was choosing which truth to spend. She had been Ilse Varga, geological consultant, for eleven days, and she had been good at it, and every hour of being good at it had cost her something she couldn't itemize yet. She watched Tamsin's red jacket bob along the far ledges and she rehearsed versions: *I'm with the ministry's safety directorate; I was sent to assess; I never meant for anyone to get hurt.* All true. All worthless. All designed to protect the one person who had never once been in danger in this entire affair — herself.

Then Tamsin reached the body, and rehearsal became irrelevant.

From across the gorge she watched them work — quick, economical, no wasted motion, the movements of someone who had done this before and wished they hadn't. They freed the legs from the stone shelf. They hauled the man onto the flood debris with a strength that looked impossible for their frame. And then they knelt over him for a long time, head bowed, hands moving gently at his chest, and Ilse realized they were searching the clothing — carefully, respectfully, the way you search the dead when you think they might still have something to say.

When Tamsin straightened, they were holding something small and dark, and even across the roar of the water Ilse saw the way their whole posture changed. The pragmatism cracked. Something else showed through — old, raw, personal.

They looked up. Across the gorge, through the mist, directly at her.

And Ilse understood, with the clarity that arrives only after the point of no return, that whatever he had found against his chest was about to end her cover whether she spoke or not.

* * *

They met again on the ledge below the bend, where the river widened and went briefly quiet, pooling green-gray over stones. Tamsin laid Kwabena Osei's body out with what dignity the terrain allowed — arms at his sides, jacket zipped to the chin, face turned from the water. Then they turned to her, and in their palm lay a notebook bound in clear waterproof film, taped flat against a rigid backing, sealed with the obsessive thoroughness of a man who expected to drown.

"This was against his chest," Tamsin said. "Taped. Under his outer layers. You don't waterproof a notebook like this for weather, Varga. You waterproof it because you believe the water is coming for *you*." Their thumb moved once across the plastic film. "There's a name written inside the cover. And there's a page folded over near the end, corner creased hard, like somebody marked it in a hurry. I haven't opened it past the cover. That part I'm saving for you."

"For me."

"Because you already know who he is. Because you've been lying to me since Halden's Rest with a discipline I almost admire. And because if I open this alone, I'm going to assume the worst about you, and right now I'd rather hear it from your mouth." Tamsin's eyes didn't leave hers. The river roared behind them like an audience. "So. Consultant. Tell me about geology."

The stone in her chest finally moved. It came up rough, and it came up all at once.

"My name is Varga. That part's real. But I don't consult on geology." She made herself stand straight, made herself meet the dark moving water of their gaze. "I'm Directorate. Internal security. Signals and field assessment. I was sent to find this team because they stopped transmitting nine days ago, and because the last transmission they sent was flagged at the highest level and buried within the hour. I was sent alone, unofficially, with a cover story, because whoever buried it did not want it found — only contained."

Tamsin said nothing. The silence had edges.

"Ilona Marsh ran that team," Ilse went on, faster now, the words tumbling like scree. "Twenty-two years in the field. Decorated. Incorruptible — I checked, everyone checks, it's practically a hobby in my line of work. People like Marsh don't stop transmitting. People like Marsh don't walk off warm stoves. So yes, I knew who Osei was. Yes, I read the manifest. Yes, I ordered us downstream at the camp, because I was afraid, and because I thought whatever took six professionals might still be there." She exhaled, and it shook. "I lied about what I am. I have not lied about what we'd find. Because I don't know. And that frightens me more than the lying does."

The wind came down the gorge and pushed between them, cold enough to make her eyes water, which was convenient.

Tamsin looked down at the notebook for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had lost none of its edge but gained something underneath it — a grudging, exhausted recognition, the sound of one survivor identifying another.

"A spy with a conscience," they said. "Those are rarer than honest ministers."

"I'm not sure I have a conscience. I may just be tired."

"Tired I understand." Tamsin peeled back the plastic film with deliberate care, the way you open something that might be wounded. The pages inside were fine-grained survey stock, dense with Osei's neat hand — elevation readings, grid references, instrument calibrations. Professional. Ordinary. Until the folded page.

Tamsin unfolded it and went very still.

"What?" Ilse stepped closer. "What is it?"

Osei's handwriting changed on that page. The neat surveyor's script broke apart into something hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss the next sheet, written by a man whose hands were shaking:

*Vault in the ice above the source. Sealed steel, older than any map. Marsh says the records inside name names — the Kesswil valley, the winter cull, all of it, state-ordered, state-filed. She wants to bring it out. She says the public has a right. Rook argued to report position only. We voted. I voted with Marsh.*

*Someone signaled from the ridge at dusk. Three flashes. Marsh thinks it's the resupply. I don't think resupply teams signal at night.*

*If anyone reads this, the bearing is 041 true from the split boulder. The door is in the glacier's face. Whatever happens to us, the records survive the ice. They were meant to.*

Beneath it, almost an afterthought, in letters gone small and careful, as if written slowly enough to be legible forever:

*Tell my daughter the river was kind to me. It wasn't, but tell her anyway.*

Nobody spoke. The river spoke for both of them, grinding its millstones, keeping its counsel.

Ilse became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking — not from cold. *The winter cull.* She knew the phrase. Everyone in the Directorate knew the phrase, the way you know the load-bearing wall in a house you're not supposed to discuss: *the incident in the western valleys, resolved, closed, no further action required.* Closed files. Sealed decades. A generation of families who had never been told where their grandparents went.

And Marsh had found the file. The actual file. Buried in ice older than the state that wrote it.

"They didn't get lost," Tamsin said quietly. It wasn't a question. They were reading the page again, lips barely moving. "They found something, and then somebody found *them*. Three flashes at dusk." They looked up, and their face had gone the color of the glacier — composed, and underneath the composition, running fast and dark. "Your Directorate buried the transmission within the hour, you said. Who gave that order?"

"I don't know. Above my level. Far above."

"Corrigan?" The name dropped between them like a stone into a well, and Ilse felt it fall all the way down.

She hadn't said the Deputy Minister's name in eleven days. Hadn't let herself think it in connection with this operation, because thinking it made the shape of things too clear. But Tamsin Okonkwo said the name with the ease of an old wound, and suddenly the blacklist, the ruined reputation, the guide too dangerous to hire — all of it reassembled itself into a pattern she should have seen from the first night in Halden's Rest.

"You know him," she said.

"I know what he builds." Tamsin folded the notebook closed with terrible gentleness, the way you close a coffin. "Blacklists. Cover stories. Teams of six that become bodies in rivers." They looked down at Kwabena Osei, at the patient water combing past his boots. "Your desperate missing team isn't lost, Varga. They're silenced. And the man who signed my name out of two countries is the most likely pen."

The gorge narrowed ahead, the river compressing itself toward some harder argument downstream. Above them, unseen beyond the ridgelines, the source waited with its sealed door and its buried dead, and somewhere between here and there, Ilse knew, men with radios were already moving.

"So what now?" she asked. "You walk away. I report a tragic accident. We both survive."

Tamsin slung their pack, adjusted the straps, and looked up-valley — north, toward 041 true, toward the ice.

"Now," they said, "you decide whose side the river keeps."

* * *

They buried what they could of Kwabena Osei above the flood line, under a cairn of river stones that Tamsin built with a mason's patience while Ilse stood watch on the trail, scanning the ridgelines for three flashes that hadn't come yet but would.

As they climbed away from the gorge, Ilse caught herself checking the compass in her mind — 041 true — and hating how naturally the number fit there now, alongside the lies she was no longer telling.

Behind them the river went on saying what it had always said, in the only language it knew, to anyone willing to stay long enough to listen.
