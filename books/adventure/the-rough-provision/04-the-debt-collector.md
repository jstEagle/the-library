## 4. The Debt Collector

Halden's Rest smelled of wet wool and diesel the way it always did in October, when the first snows came down off the Karveth shoulder and settled into the town's bones like a debt no one intended to pay. Tamsin Okonkwo sat in the back booth of the Anvil & Anchor with a cup of tea gone cold between their hands and Ilse Varga's money folded flat inside their jacket, against the ribs, where it felt less like payment than like a splinter.

Three days since they'd come down off the mountain. Three days since the tents standing empty above the treeline, stove still warm, radios singing nothing but static. Three days since Varga had gone the color of old ice and said *downstream, now* in a voice that had no consultant in it at all.

Tamsin had spent those three days doing what blacklisted guides did best: nothing, expensively. Drinking tea they couldn't taste. Watching the door.

The Anvil & Anchor was half full — haulage men off the southern road, two game wardens drying their socks by the stove, a knot of seasonal workers playing cards with the grim concentration of people who had already lost. Nobody looked at Tamsin. That was the arrangement here. Halden's Rest was a town built on the wrong side of too many borders to care about anyone's ledger, which was why the blacklisted came here, and why it never quite felt like refuge. A town that didn't ask questions also didn't offer answers. You could live ten years in that gap.

Tamsin had lived four.

The tea had gone cold because Tamsin kept lifting it and forgetting to drink. The notebook was the reason. The dead surveyor's notebook, wrapped in its waterproof sleeve, sat in the bottom of their pack under a coil of rope, and all day it had been pulling at them the way deep water pulls — not visibly, not loudly, just constantly, a pressure behind the sternum. *You should have left it.* They hadn't. *You should have given it to Varga.* She'd ordered them downstream and then she'd vanished from the trailhead before dawn, and Tamsin had woken alone with a dead man's diary and a client's cash and the growing, sickening understanding that neither of those things had been meant for them to keep.

The card players swore softly. The stove ticked. Outside, sleet ticked against the window in fine dry grains, like something small trying to get in.

That was when the door opened and let the cold walk in wearing a good coat.

Tamsin knew him before the light found his face. Not from photographs — though there had been photographs, years ago, in the inquiry files Tamsin had read so many times the paper had gone soft at the folds. It was the way he entered a room: unhurried, certain of being waited for. Deputy Minister Aldous Corrigan had the build of a man who had once been athletic and had since decided that other people would do his climbing for him. Silver at the temples, precise as frost. He stood in the doorway a moment, letting the room notice him, and the room did — the wardens straightened, the card game faltered — and then he crossed the floor directly to Tamsin's booth, and Tamsin understood that the last three days had not been theirs at all.

"Okonkwo." He said the name correctly. That was the first cruelty of it. Most officials mangled it; Corrigan had learned it, deliberately, the way a man learns the name of a horse he intends to buy or put down. "May I?"

He was already sitting. Across from Tamsin, hands folded on the scarred table, coat still buttoned, as if he did not intend to stay long enough to be comfortable. As if comfort were a currency he dealt in rather than used.

"You're a long way from the capital," Tamsin said.

"I'm where the work is." He lifted one hand and caught the barmaid's eye without looking at her. "Two teas. Fresh." Then, to Tamsin, mildly: "I know you don't drink anything stronger before an ascent. Or after one. You've become quite disciplined since—" He stopped himself with a small, practiced smile. "Forgive me. I'm not here to reopen wounds."

"No," Tamsin said. "You're here to price them."

Something flickered behind Corrigan's eyes — appreciation, maybe, or its close cousin. "They told me you were blunt. The file undersells you."

"What file? I thought my file ended six years ago."

"Files don't end, Okonkwo. They wait." The teas arrived. He paid with notes pulled from a fold of bills that had no business being carried openly in Halden's Rest, and waited until the barmaid had retreated before he went on, voice dropped into a register meant only for this table. "Five surveyors went into the Karveth headwaters in August. Five did not come out. The government's position is that they were lost. Weather. Terrain. The mountains are indifferent, etcetera. You've heard the eulogies."

"I've read better fiction."

"I'm sure you have. You wrote some of it yourself, at the inquiry." The smile again, thinner. "Here is what I want, and I'll be direct, because I'm told directness is the only language you respect anymore. There is equipment belonging to that team scattered through the upper valley. Instruments. Records. Notebooks." His gaze rested, briefly and without pretense, on the pack leaning against the booth's end. "If such materials were recovered and returned — quietly, without copies, without stories — the Ministry would look favorably on certain outstanding matters. Your standing. Your certification. The restrictions that have made your professional life so… narrow."

The word *narrow* landed exactly where he wanted it. Four years of guiding poachers' trails and smugglers' shortcuts because no licensed outfit in two countries would touch the name *Okonkwo*. Four years of watching lesser guides take clients up routes Tamsin had cut first. Four years of the Anvil & Anchor's cold tea and the border towns shrinking around them like a wound closing over a splinter.

Corrigan watched all of that move across Tamsin's face, and Tamsin realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had been watching it for six years. That the blacklist had never been punishment. It had been seasoning. You keep a blade useful and starve it until it's grateful for any hand.

"And if I say no?" Tamsin asked.

"Then the mountains remain indifferent," Corrigan said pleasantly, "and the record remains closed, and your file remains open, and we both go on as we are." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle. "But I think you've already seen enough up there to know that 'lost' isn't what happened. And I think you're intelligent enough to know that whoever holds what that team found will not hold it safely. There are people looking for it, Okonkwo. Serious people. If it surfaces in the wrong hands — or the right ones — there will be consequences that reach further than one disgraced guide. The village communities in that valley, for instance. The ones who aren't on the maps." He sipped his tea. "Governments can be very protective of geography."

The threat arrived dressed as concern, and it was expertly aimed. Not at Tamsin — Tamsin had nothing left to lose and Corrigan surely knew it — but outward, at the hidden places, the unnamed people, everything soft in the world that Tamsin might still be fool enough to stand in front of.

"The surveyor in the river," Tamsin said quietly. "He had a notebook strapped to his chest. Waterproofed. He wasn't protecting survey data. People don't die holding instruments to their hearts."

For the first time, Corrigan's composure moved — a hairline crack, quickly plastered. "Drowning men cling to things. It means nothing."

"It means everything. You know it does. That's why you're sitting in a diesel-smelling tavern in the arse-end of nowhere instead of sending someone." Tamsin leaned forward. "Whoever he was, he thought what he was carrying was worth dying for. And you think it's worth ruining me twice over. So let's stop pretending this is about equipment."

Corrigan set down his cup. When he spoke again, the ministerial warmth had drained out of his voice, leaving something older and colder underneath, and somehow that was almost a relief — at least it was honest.

"Six years ago you led a rescue into the Serac Col that killed three people. Including a girl of nineteen whose family has never once stopped writing to this Ministry asking why the guide responsible was never charged." He said it evenly, factually, a man reading coordinates. "The inquiry found negligence. The blacklist followed. All of that is true, and all of it is yours, and none of it changes. What I am offering is not forgiveness. It is reinstatement. Certification restored, borders opened, the file sealed. You would guide again. Legally. Anywhere." He paused. "All you have to do is bring me what the river gave you, and forget the rest."

*Forget the rest.* Tamsin looked at him and saw, superimposed over the silver-templed official, a younger version of the same face reading findings into a hearing-room microphone six years ago — the same careful cadence, the same talent for making ruin sound like procedure. Corrigan had built the blacklist personally. Tamsin had learned that later, from a clerk with a conscience and a bottle. Every restriction, every closed border, every winter of cold tea: drafted in his office, signed with his initials, *A.C.*, neat as frost.

And now here he was, offering to unbuild it, one brick at a time, in exchange for a dead man's testimony.

The temptation was not subtle. That was the humiliating part. It wasn't some abstract moral fog — it was concrete, physical, warm. Tamsin could feel it the way you feel a fire after a night on a glacier: certification restored. The Karveth routes with Tamsin's name legally attached again. Walking into a permit office in either country and being *recognized*. Being able to send money to Mama without explaining where it came from. Six years of exile, undone with a signature, in exchange for silence about people who were already dead.

*They're dead either way,* whispered the reasonable voice, the tired one, the one that had kept Tamsin alive on bad mountains for twenty years. *The notebook won't raise them. Take the deal.*

But the notebook had been strapped to a living chest. Someone had believed, with their last strength, that it would get downstream. To whom, Tamsin didn't know. Maybe to anyone. Maybe — and this was the thought that would not leave — maybe to whoever was supposed to find it, and the river had chosen Tamsin, and rivers did not choose randomly. Rivers chose according to the shape of the land. And Tamsin's whole life had been shaped toward this valley, toward these headwaters, toward a father who had walked into the Karveth thirty years ago with a survey compass and never walked out, and whose disappearance had been filed, in another ministry, in another era, under *lost*.

*Lost,* Tamsin thought. *The mountains are indifferent, etcetera.*

"No," Tamsin said.

Corrigan didn't sigh. He had been expecting it; Tamsin could see that now, see the whole architecture of the meeting — the directness, the threats dressed as concern, even the insult about the inquiry. This was not an offer. It was a measurement. He needed to know whether Tamsin could be bought, and if not bought, then how much pressure the frame would take before it bent.

"That's a pity," he said, and stood, buttoning his coat with unhurried precision. "For the record, Okonkwo — and there will be a record — you were offered a way back, and you declined it. Whatever happens next, remember that you chose it." At the edge of the booth he paused, and for one moment the mask slipped far enough to show something beneath it that was worse than menace: urgency. Fear, even, worn thin under the authority. "There are things buried in that ice that were buried once already, by men who believed the state was more important than the truth. I am one of those men. I would prefer to be the merciful kind. Don't make me the other kind."

He left money on the table for the teas — exact change, of course — and crossed the room, and the door swallowed him, and the cold came in after and settled.

Tamsin sat for a long time with the untouched tea going cold all over again, listening to the sleet tick against the glass, running the conversation back through their mind the way you re-read a route description before a descent, looking for the trap you know is there.

It took three passes to find it.

Not in the threats. In the courtesy. Corrigan had known about the notebook — had looked, openly, at the pack. Which meant he already knew Tamsin had it. Which meant the meeting had never been an offer at all. It had been a marker: *we know where you are, we know what you carry, and now you know we know.* Everything after that would happen on his schedule, not Tamsin's.

Tamsin finished the cold tea in one swallow, tasting nothing, and stood, and slung the pack, and felt the notebook's weight settle against the spine like a hand.

Outside, the sleet had thickened toward snow. The street lamps of Halden's Rest burned halos into the dusk, and at the far end of the road, under the lamp by the bridge — the only bridge out of town toward the high country — a dark car idled with its lights off, patient as weather.

Tamsin turned the other way, toward the old stock track behind the tannery, the one that climbed out of the valley without touching a single checkpoint, the kind of route only a blacklisted guide would know by heart.

Behind, faint under the sleet, the car door shut.

* * *

The stock track was goat-steep and slick with new snow, and Tamsin climbed it in the dark without a lamp, on memory and balance, breath tearing, heart hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with altitude. Twice they stopped to listen. Twice the valley below gave back nothing but wind and the far-off bark of a dog.

At the saddle above the tannery roofs, Tamsin finally allowed one glance back. Halden's Rest lay below in its bowl of lamplight, small and warm and finished with them. On the bridge, two figures stood in the snowfall, sweeping flashlight beams across the water with the methodical patience of men who had all night and official permission to use it.

They weren't chasing yet. They were closing the obvious doors first, knowing — counting on — the quarry bolting through an unobvious one.

Which meant the unobvious ones were about to stop being unobvious. Which meant Tamsin had perhaps hours, not days, to get up-valley, off every marked route, into country where Corrigan's tidy machinery couldn't follow.

Tamsin turned north, toward the black mass of the Karveth foothills, toward the headwaters and the glacier and whatever the ice was keeping. The snow thickened. The compass — the father's compass, brass-cased, needle trembling in its housing like something alive — rode in the breast pocket where it had ridden for twenty years, and tonight, for the first time, it felt less like an inheritance than an instruction.

Halfway down into the next drainage, Tamsin smelled woodsmoke where no smoke should be, and saw, wedged in the rocks below the ridgeline, the orange flicker of a fire burning low in the mouth of a cave —

— and a figure inside it, sitting up slowly against the stone, watching Tamsin with eyes that caught the firelight and held it, and a voice cracked raw by cold saying:

"You're not one of his. So either help me, or finish what the mountain started."
