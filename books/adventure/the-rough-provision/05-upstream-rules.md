## 5. Upstream Rules

The refusal took less time than Corrigan had spent building his career.

Tamsin heard it leave their own mouth — *no* — and watched it land on the Deputy Minister's face like a stone dropped into still water. He didn't flinch. That was the thing about Corrigan that had frightened them even years ago, back when he'd been only a regional administrator with a clipboard and a talent for ruin: nothing ever landed on him. Everything slid off and was absorbed somewhere beneath the surface, filed away for later use.

"No," he repeated, tasting it. "You know, Okonkwo, most people in your position would at least pretend to consider."

"I did consider." Tamsin stood in the doorway of their rented room above the ferry office, one hand on the pack already half-stuffed with rope and rations, the dead surveyor's notebook zipped into the inner pocket against their spine where no search would find it without undressing them. "I considered for three years every time I applied for a permit and got denied. I considered when my name went on the blacklist ledger with a note about *operational negligence* attached to a rescue where I carried a man's body eleven kilometers through a whiteout because your ministry wouldn't authorize a helicopter after dark. So yes. Considered."

Corrigan's smile was a professional instrument, calibrated to the millimeter. "That rescue is exactly why I'm here. You're the best guide this range has ever produced, and the range knows it. The mountain doesn't read ledgers." He glanced past Tamsin into the small room, at the narrow bed, the single window fogged with river damp, the kettle with its chipped handle. His gaze performed pity the way other men performed arithmetic. "Full reinstatement. Both countries. Your father's permits restored to the family name. All of it, for one quiet retrieval."

"You said data," Tamsin said. "What data?"

"Survey records. Instrument logs. Nothing you'd understand, frankly."

"Then it should be easy to describe."

For the first time something moved behind Corrigan's eyes — not anger, but the cold recalculation of a man deciding how much truth a transaction required. "Glacial hydrology. Seismic readings. The kind of material that causes confusion when it circulates among people who don't have the expertise to interpret it." He straightened his coat. "You have until dawn tomorrow. There's a man at the Karveth trailhead who'll be waiting either way. If you come down with the records, you come down clean. If you come down without them—" He let the sentence die unfinished, which was worse than finishing it. "You've seen what happens to people who are inconvenient in that valley, Okonkwo. You knew one of them personally."

They hadn't mentioned Tamsin's father. They never needed to. It was printed somewhere in the same ledger as everything else.

"Get out of my room," Tamsin said quietly.

Corrigan put a card on the table — heavy stock, embossed, absurd in a room that smelled of kerosene and wet wool — and left. His footsteps descended the stairs at an unhurried pace, a man who had never once in his life been made to hurry by anyone beneath him.

Tamsin waited until the ferry office door banged below, then counted to two hundred, listening to the river. Then they finished packing in the dark, by touch, the way their father had taught them: *if you can't rig it blind, you can't rig it scared.* Rope, stove, bivouac sack, four days of food compressed to the density of a brick. The notebook stayed against their spine.

There was no question of going out the front. Corrigan would have a man on the trailhead road within the hour — probably already did; the offer had been a courtesy, a last chance extended before the net closed, not instead of it. Tamsin went out the window instead, down the drainpipe they'd eyed the first day they arrived in Halden's Rest with nothing but a duffel bag and a reputation like a criminal record, and dropped into the alley behind the fish smokehouse, where the smell covered everything and the dogs knew them well enough not to bark.

Upstream rules. Their father's phrase, from years of summers in the high country: *downstream, everyone can see you coming. Upstream, you make your own weather.* It meant travel at night. It meant cold camps, no fires, no trails. It meant trusting the mountain more than any human being in it.

Tamsin crossed the river on the old footbridge below the weir — rotten planks, unlit, off every official map since the new road went in — and turned north into the black shoulder of the foothills while the town's few lights shrank behind them like a drowning constellation.

By midnight they were above the treeline, moving by starlight across scree that clicked and shifted underfoot like a field of loose teeth. By two they had put the first ridge between themselves and Halden's Rest. They allowed themselves ten minutes in the lee of a boulder, chewing frozen bread, and took out the notebook.

Kessler's notebook — the dead surveyor, the one they'd pulled from the rapids with Ilse Varga standing over him lying about who she was and wasn't. Waterproofed against his chest, wrapped in plastic and bound with surgical tape, the way men carry things they intend to survive them. Tamsin had read it twice on the descent and twice more in their room, and each reading had made the book heavier. Survey bearings. Depth soundings. And in the final pages, in a handwriting gone hurried and pressed-hard, a set of coordinates and a single underlined word: *SOURCE.*

And beneath that, dated three days before the camp went silent: *V. says go down. M. says we owe them the truth. Splitting the party was a mistake.*

Tamsin closed the book. Somewhere out in that valley were the rest of the team — or what remained of them — and somewhere upstream was whatever *source* meant badly enough to kill five government employees, and somewhere behind them was Corrigan's net drawing tight around every exit, and ahead—

Ahead there was a sound.

Faint. Rhythmic. Wrong. Not an animal — too regular. Not wind — too wet. A sound like someone breathing through cloth, deep in the dark below the ridge, where the contour lines on Tamsin's mental map said there was an overhang and, below the overhang, possibly a hollow.

Tamsin sat very still for a long moment, weighing it. Downstream rules said avoid the unknown. But downstream rules had gotten five surveyors killed, and Ilse Varga's orders followed, and Corrigan's card burned in memory like frostbite.

Tamsin got up and went toward the sound.

* * *

The cave was barely a cave — a hollow beneath a slab of granite that had calved off the ridge face some centuries ago and come to rest leaning against its parent rock, leaving a gap a meter high and three deep, floored with gravel and old snowmelt. The breathing came from inside it.

Tamsin crouched at the entrance and let their eyes adjust, and let the smell arrive before the sight: blood, sour clothing, the sweetish reek of a body burning its own fat to stay alive.

A man lay curled in the deepest part of the hollow, wedged against the stone like something the glacier had coughed up. Young — younger than Tamsin expected. Black skin cracked gray with cold and dirt, lips split, a beard maybe a week old. One arm was wrapped in a survey-team thermal blanket, stained dark at the elbow. His eyes were open. That was the worst part. He'd been awake, watching the entrance, watching Tamsin's silhouette block the stars, and he hadn't made a sound except that breathing — because making a sound cost heat, and heat was life, and he had been rationing himself down to the last calorie the way a dying man rations water.

"Rook Adeyemi," Tamsin said.

The man flinched so hard his boot scraped stone. His voice came out ruined, a whisper dragged over gravel. "Who—"

"The fifth member of the Meridian survey team. The one who isn't in the rapids." Tamsin kept their voice flat and low. "Your leader's name was Mensah. Your instruments tech was Kessler. Kessler is dead. I found him in the river with his notebook taped to his chest."

Silence. Then, in that shredded whisper: "Are you police?"

"Do I look like police?"

"You look like death," Rook Adeyemi said, "so I can't rule it out."

Despite everything — despite the smell and the dark and the fact that every instinct Tamsin owned was screaming about traps — something almost like a laugh moved through the hollow. Tamsin crawled in. The man didn't resist when they got a hand under his arm; he was past resisting, past anything but the animal cooperation of the hypothermic. Tamsin got the bivouac sack around him, got the stove lit low in the cave mouth where the flame wouldn't show beyond the overhang, got snow melting in the pot with a tablet of electrolyte powder and half a ration bar crumbled in. Sugar first. Warmth second. Questions later, if there was a later.

Rook drank like a man who had forgotten drinking could hurt and then remembered, gasping between swallows. Color came back into his hands in ugly blotches. For twenty minutes neither of them spoke, and the stove hissed, and outside the stars wheeled slowly over the Karveth Range like witnesses refusing to testify.

It was Rook who broke first.

"They told us it was a routine resurvey," he said. "Hydrology, seismic baseline, six weeks. Mensah had done it twice before. Nobody asked why the ministry suddenly cared about a drainage basin nobody's mapped properly in forty years." His eyes fixed on the stove flame. "Until week three. Until we found it."

"Found what?"

"You haven't read the whole notebook."

"I've read all of it. It doesn't say what it is. Just *source*, underlined."

Rook was quiet long enough that Tamsin thought he'd passed out. Then: "Good. That's — good. Because if it's written down, it can be taken off you." He looked up, and in the stove light his eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "Who sent you? Truthfully. Because I've had four days up here to learn that everybody in this story works for somebody, and I will freeze to death before I get handed to another one of them."

The question hung in the cold air between them, and Tamsin understood that this was the hinge — that whatever happened next depended on whether two liars could recognize each other fast enough to become something else.

"My name is Tamsin Okonkwo," they said. "I guide people in these mountains, when anyone will hire me, which is almost never, because I'm blacklisted in two countries for a rescue your ministry decided to call negligence. Three days ago a woman named Ilse Varga hired me off the books to walk into the foothills as her geological consultant. She's not a geologist. She's intelligence — she admitted it standing over Kessler's body. Yesterday a man named Corrigan offered me full reinstatement if I brought him the survey data quietly. I said no. I stole the notebook and ran. That's the whole of it. Every lie included."

Rook stared at them. Something shifted in his face — not trust, not yet, but the recalibration of a man discovering the terrain was different from his map.

"Okonkwo," he said slowly. "The rescue. Eleven kilometers. The one where—" He stopped himself, but Tamsin saw the recognition land, saw it turn over.

"Yes. That one."

"My mother followed that story," Rook said. "She cried about it. She said the ministry used you as a drain cover — something to throw over the hole so nobody looks down." He exhaled, a long shaking breath. "I led those people up there, Okonkwo. My team. And I'm the only one who walked back down."

"Tell me why."

And here was the thing Tamsin noticed, the thing they would turn over for nights afterward: Rook Adeyemi looked at the stove flame, and his face did something complicated — grief and guilt and calculation all trying to occupy the same features at once — and when he answered, his voice was steady in the way voices are steady when they've been rehearsed.

"We found a vault in the ice. Sealed. Older than the surveys, older than the state. Records inside — testimony, recordings, decades of it. About the Purge. About what the government did in these valleys when the maps were being drawn." His jaw worked. "Mensah wanted to take it public. Varga — she wasn't with us then, but our ministry liaison — ordered us to withdraw and report. Mensah refused. We argued. I—" A pause, half a heartbeat too long. "I went to check the radio relay. When I came back, the camp was torn apart. Tracks everywhere. I ran."

Tamsin said nothing. The stove hissed. Outside, far off and thin, something that might have been a helicopter crossed the sky, and both of them froze and listened until it faded north.

"Tracks going which way?" Tamsin asked finally.

"What?"

"When you came back. The tracks. Which direction did they lead?"

Rook's eyes flickered — down, toward the stove, away. "Downstream. Toward the pass."

Tamsin nodded slowly, and reached into their jacket, and laid Kessler's notebook on the gravel between them, open to the last written page. The stove light fell across the hurried handwriting, the underlined word, and the line beneath it:

*Splitting the party was a mistake.*

"Kessler wrote that the day before he died," Tamsin said softly. "He also wrote that the tracks around your abandoned camp led *upstream*. Toward the source. Every one of them." They looked up and met Rook Adeyemi's fever-bright eyes with their own, level and cold as the scree outside. "So before you tell me one more word, tell me why a man who ran downhill left his camp pitched, his stove warm, and his boots pointing up."

The silence that followed lasted a long time. Somewhere above the cave, wind began to move along the ridge, gathering itself, the first breath of a storm that would seal the high passes for days.

Rook Adeyemi closed his eyes. When he opened them again, the rehearsal was gone, and what was underneath was rawer and far more frightening.

"Because I didn't run from whoever tore the camp apart," he said. "I ran from what Mensah was going to do with it. And I need to show you something before I tell you the rest — because if I'm wrong about you, we're both dead, and if I'm right, you're going to wish you'd taken Corrigan's deal."

He pulled something from inside the thermal blanket, something he'd kept against his skin the way Kessler had kept the notebook against his own chest, and held it out into the stove light.

It was a compass. Old brass, dented, a lanyard of braided leather worn smooth by decades of a particular grip.

Tamsin knew that compass. Had grown up watching their father's thumb rub that exact dent on the bezel every morning before a climb.

Their own compass — the twin to it, the pair their father had split between his two children the summer before the valleys were emptied — was sitting in Tamsin's pocket, and it had begun, faintly and impossibly, to pull toward the ice.
