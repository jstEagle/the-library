## 6. Rook's Version

The cave breathed.

That was Rook Adeyemi's first thought when he came back to himself — not the cold, not the pain in his hands, not the wet wool smell of his own clothes frozen half-stiff against his ribs, but the breathing. The mountain breathed around him, slow and tidal, the way glaciers do when the temperature swings and the ice expands and contracts like the lungs of something enormous and patient. He had been listening to it for — how long? A day. Two. He had stopped counting when counting stopped helping.

Now there was a fire.

Small, disciplined, built in a hollow of stone where its light wouldn't leak out the cave mouth, and above it crouched a broad-shouldered figure feeding it slivers of wood one at a time, as if each piece had to be justified before it was spent. The firelight moved across a face Rook knew from a photograph that had been passed around the survey camp in the last week anyone had trusted each other: Tamsin Okonkwo. The blacklisted guide. The one whose name Corrigan's people had put on the bounty sheets with the word *murderer* printed underneath it in letters so heavy they seemed to have been pressed into the paper with a stamp.

Rook should have run. His body even tried — he felt the flinch of it, the animal arithmetic of *up, out, away* — but his hands wouldn't answer. They were wrapped in strips of his own shirt, and beneath the wrapping they were the color of uncooked dough, and when he flexed them the pain came up his forearms like water finding its level. Frostnip, maybe worse. The survey medic would have known. The survey medic was in the river now, or under the ice, or wherever the Karveth kept the people it took.

"You're awake," Tamsin said, without turning around. "Good. Drink this." A tin cup came into his field of vision, steam rising off it in the cave's cold air. "Slowly. You were somewhere past stupid when I found you. Your body will want to repay the debt all at once."

Rook took the cup in both wrapped hands. The heat went through the cloth into his palms and he made a sound he hadn't meant to make, something small and involuntary, and hated himself for it.

"You know who I am," he said.

"I know what the radio says you are." Tamsin turned then, and in the firelight their face was all planes and patience, a face that had been weathered down to its load-bearing structure. "Same thing it says I am. So either we're both killers, or the government's arithmetic has gotten sloppy. I'm willing to consider the second option. For now."

*For now.* Rook drank. The tea was some alpine scrub that tasted like resin and bitterness, and it was the best thing he had ever tasted, and he hated that too.

* * *

They talked in pieces, the way people talk when they're both deciding how much of themselves to spend.

Tamsin asked the questions a guide asks — where, how long, what did you eat, did you dig a snow cave or shelter in the open — and Rook answered, and the answers were true, and that was the problem. The true answers were easy. He'd had two days to rehearse the false ones and they came out smooth as river stones, but the true ones came out ragged, and ragged is what makes a story believable. He could feel Tamsin cataloguing him the whole time: the way his eyes went to the cave mouth when the wind shifted, the way his wrapped hands curled protectively inward, the way he flinched at nothing whenever the glacier boomed somewhere above them, that deep artillery sound of ice under stress.

"Start at the beginning," Tamsin said eventually. "The survey. Five of you. What were you actually mapping?"

"Water," Rook said. "That's the official brief. Hydrological survey of the headwaters. Flow rates, melt season projections, sediment load. The ministry wants to dam the upper Karveth someday and they need a decade of data first."

"And the unofficial brief?"

Rook looked at the fire. The fire looked back, in its way.

"There wasn't one," he said. "That's what I keep coming back to. There wasn't supposed to be one. We were surveyors, not spies. Osei ran the team — Dr. Osei Mensah, glaciologist, twenty years in the field, the kind of man who apologizes to the mountain when he drives a piton. He got a letter at the last supply drop. Ministry letterhead. He read it three times and burned it, and after that he started taking instrument readings that weren't on any survey schedule I'd ever seen. Bearings into the accumulation zone. Ground-penetrating radar runs over terrain we weren't contracted to touch."

"Where?"

Rook told him. The bearing. The elevation. He watched Tamsin's face as he said the numbers and saw — he was almost sure he saw — a flicker, there and gone, like a fish under ice. Something in those numbers meant something to Tamsin Okonkwo. He filed it away. Filing things away was the only skill he had left.

"We followed the bearing for six days," Rook said. "Up past the seracs, into the cirque below the north col. And there's a glacier there that doesn't behave. It's old — older than the mapping, older than the state, the ice cores Osei pulled said the basal layers predate everything — and it's moving wrong. Too slow. Almost not at all, in a range where everything else creeps downhill like it's late for something. Osei said a glacier that still is a glacier that's *holding* something."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them the mountain exhaled, a long grinding sigh of settling ice.

"We found the vault on the ninth day," Rook said. "Not by digging. The glacier gave it to us. A melt season like the range hasn't seen in a century, and a whole face of the ice calved off in the night — you'd have heard it in Halden's Rest, probably, if you'd been listening — and in the morning there was this..." He searched for the word and hated every one he found. "Door. Steel. Set into the ice like it had grown there. Riveted, seam-welded, no markings except a survey benchmark stamp from before the Republic existed. Osei put his hand on it and he started to cry. I'd never seen him cry. Twenty years of ice and he wept like a man at a graveside."

"What was inside?"

"I don't know." And this, at least, was true, and the truth of it came out of him with real grief attached. "We never got it open. But Osei had the radar data, and the ice behind that door isn't hollow — it's *filled*. Layered. Decades of deposition, man-made deposits, sealed in on purpose. He said — he stood there with his hand on the steel and he said, *they didn't hide a thing here, they hid a witness.*"

Rook stopped. The fire had burned down to a red eye in its stone socket and Tamsin fed it another sliver of wood, unhurried, and Rook understood that the unhurriedness was a technique, that the silence was a tool being applied to him gently, like a chisel.

"Go on," Tamsin said.

"We argued." His voice came out lower. "That night, in the tents. Osei wanted to go public. Radio Halden's Rest, call the capital, bring journalists, the whole circus. He said the door was on ministry-surveyed land and the moment we reported it, it would be sealed behind a hundred layers of classification, and whatever was inside would stay inside forever. He said we had a window of — of *moral custody*. His words. Moral custody. Like we'd been appointed."

"And the others?"

"Vance agreed with him. So did Petra. Marta wanted to report it through channels — official discovery, official credit, let the ministry do the right thing in the light where it could be watched." Rook heard his own voice flatten and hated it, that flatness, the sound of a man reading from a script he'd written in a colder place than this. "And I said we should mark it, log it, and come back with a proper expedition. Equipment. Lawyers. I said we weren't equipped to be heroes."

The silence stretched. Tamsin's face in the firelight gave away nothing, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"And then?"

"And then I left." Rook looked at his ruined hands. "I went down in the night. Alone. I told myself I was going for help, for a satphone that worked, for — I don't know. Reinforcements. I got to the lower camp and the storm came in and I sheltered and when the storm broke I went back up and the camp was empty. Tents still pitched. Stove still warm. No tracks leading out." His throat closed. He made himself finish. "Four people don't vanish off a glacier. I know how it looks. I've had two days of knowing how it looks. It looks like I did something."

"It looks like you did something," Tamsin agreed, mildly. "Or it looks like you ran, and something happened after you ran, and you're the only one left to carry it. Those aren't the same thing. But they're not different enough."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had been banked and the cave had gone blue with the light coming off the ice field outside — Rook lay on his back and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding.

Tamsin Okonkwo slept sitting up, back against the stone, the dead surveyor's notebook in a waterproof bag on their chest, one hand resting on it the way a shepherd rests a hand on a dog. Rook had seen the notebook. Had seen Tamsin's thumb moving over its cover like a man touching a scar. He knew whose notebook it was — Petra's, or Vance's, or Osei's; he hadn't gotten a clear look, and Tamsin hadn't offered, and the not-offering was information.

*His story doesn't hold,* Rook told himself, and it was true, and he was grateful for it, because a story that holds would have been a story he'd have to believe, and believing it would cost him something he wasn't ready to pay.

The seams were there if you looked. He'd looked. He'd spent two days in a snow cave with nothing to do but look at the seams of his own account, and they gaped like the crevasse he'd nearly died in.

The timing was wrong. He'd left the camp at — he checked it again, the way you check a knot you don't trust — at roughly two in the morning. The storm had taken six hours to build. Which meant the camp had been emptied — tents, stove, four people, no tracks — inside a window of perhaps four hours, in the dark, in wind that was already screaming. Possible. Mountains make everything possible the way the sea makes drowning possible. But not easy, and not quiet, and someone would have heard something, and he had heard nothing except the storm arriving like a door slamming.

And the letter. Osei had burned the ministry letter, but Rook had seen the envelope's corner before it went into the stove, and the corner had carried a departmental code he recognized, because you don't work five years in government hydrology without learning which codes mean *water* and which codes mean *watch*. The code on that envelope had not meant water.

And one more thing, the thing he kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it: when he'd fled down the mountain, he hadn't fled blind. He'd had a bearing. He'd had a rendezvous. He'd had a name to call and a number that wasn't any ministry number, and he had stood in the lower camp with the satphone in his hand and the storm coming in and he had — 

He had dialed it.

He had stood in the wind and heard the click of the line connecting and he had said, "It's Rook. I'm calling it in. The vault is real," and the voice on the other end had said, "Hold position. Help is coming," and he had believed it the way you believe things when you're cold and frightened and want to be held, and he had held position, and the help that came was a storm, and the storm was followed by silence, and the silence was followed by two days of hiding from the helicopters that were suddenly, urgently, extensively looking for something in the Karveth high country. Looking for him. Looking for a *murderer*, the radio said, a surveyor named Rook Adeyemi who had killed his own team.

They had flipped him fast. Faster than any bureaucracy he'd ever worked inside. Which meant the machinery had been oiled in advance. Which meant the call he'd made had been expected. Which meant —

Rook lay in the dark and let the thought arrive, because he'd been refusing it for two days and refusing it was a kind of lying too, and he was so tired of lying, even to himself, especially to himself.

*You didn't run from the team. You ran to them. You were the one who told them where to point the radar. The letter that Osei burned — you carried it up the mountain. You were the ministry's instrument, and when the instrument started developing opinions, they scheduled the storm.*

He didn't know if it was true. That was the terrible architecture of it: he had done things he couldn't fully remember doing, in that window between the money and the mountain, and the not-remembering was either mercy or the deepest cut of all. But he knew the shape of his own complicity the way you know the shape of a room in the dark — by the bruises.

Across the dead fire, Tamsin Okonkwo shifted in their sleep and did not quite wake, and the notebook rose and fell on their chest with their breathing, and Rook thought: *they lost everything already. Their name, their work, their country's trust. And they came back up the mountain anyway, carrying a dead man's notebook, looking for the truth of a thing that ruined them once before.* And he thought: *I sold my piece of this for a number in an account I'll never be able to spend, and the people who paid me are using my name as a mask for murder.*

He thought: *if I tell them — if I tell Tamsin the whole of it, the money and the call and the bearing I fed to Osei — they will have every reason to leave me on this mountain to freeze, and not one reason in the world to trust me again.*

He thought: *but they haven't left yet.*

The glacier boomed, long and deep, a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and under it, fainter, there came another sound — a sound Rook knew from the worst hours of the last two days, a sound that meant the search grid was moving, the thrum of helicopter blades beating up the valley from the south, patient as a pulse.

Tamsin's eyes opened. No grogginess in them at all, no sleep in them at all — they had been awake the whole time, Rook realized, awake and listening, and the sleeping had been a performance as rehearsed as his own.

"They're back," Tamsin said quietly. "Your friends. Or Corrigan's. Hard to tell the difference anymore." They rose, unfolding from the stone with the economy of someone who had slept in worse places, and began, unhurried, to kill the last coals of the fire. "You told me a story tonight, surveyor. Most of it even true, I think. But you left out the part where you called someone. I found the satphone in your kit. The call log's been wiped, but the *battery's* warm, and there's one number half-erased in the memory that isn't ministry, isn't survey, and isn't yours."

The coals died. The cave went dark except for the glacier's faint blue breath, and Tamsin's voice came out of it, calm as a man reading elevations off a map.

"So before those blades get here, you're going to tell me who you called — and you're going to tell me what you sold them. Because in about an hour, that valley is going to be full of people who want us both dead, and I need to know whether I'm roped to a victim or to the man who tied the knot."

Rook opened his mouth. Above them, through the stone, through the ice, the helicopters grew louder.
