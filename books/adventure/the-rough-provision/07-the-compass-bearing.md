## 7. The Compass Bearing

The cave held the night's cold like a held breath. Tamsin woke before dawn out of habit older than grief — the body keeping its own ledger of hours even when there was nothing left to be on time for. Rook was still asleep against the far wall, curled around his own injuries like a man apologizing to them, breath fogging in slow, uneven pulses. Outside, the Karveth peaks had gone the color of old bone, and the river below spoke in its one flat voice, saying the same word over and over.

Tamsin sat with the dead man's notebook between their knees.

They had not opened it again since the rapids. There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks like caution: *I'll read it when there's light. I'll read it when we're safe. I'll read it when I'm ready.* Three days of that now, the notebook wrapped in its oilcloth like something surgical, passed from pack to pack, always carried, never touched. Last night, by firelight, Rook had watched them turn it over in their hands and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary.

Now the light came up grey through the cave mouth, and there were no more excuses left that would hold their own weight.

Tamsin unwrapped the cloth.

The notebook was a standard survey issue — waterproof stock, spiral-bound, the kind sold in every outfitter between Halden's Rest and the coast. The cover bore no name, only a team designation stenciled in government block letters: KRS-4, and beneath it a date eleven months gone. The pages inside were dense with a surveyor's tidy hand, columns of elevations, flow rates, ice-core depths, all of it ordinary, all of it the boring grammar of science. Tamsin read fast at first, skimming the way you skim anything written by a stranger, looking for the human seams.

They found the first seam on page nineteen.

*Day 6. V. insists the source readings are wrong. Instruments disagree with each other. Magnetic anomaly upstream — compasses swinging 15–20 degrees off true as we approach the glacier face. D. says it's ore bodies. V. says ore doesn't sing.*

Tamsin stopped.

*Ore doesn't sing.* A strange sentence to find in a survey log, and stranger still because Tamsin knew the handwriting's next line before reading it, the way you know a chord's resolution:

*We will follow the bearing regardless.*

There was a bearing printed there, six digits, declination-corrected, precise as a confession. Tamsin stared at it for a long time without understanding why their chest had gone tight, why the cave suddenly seemed colder than the air justified. Then they reached — slowly, the way you reach toward a stove you already know is hot — for the lanyard around their own neck, and drew out the compass.

Brass case, dented at the bezel where it had once struck stone. The needle settling, settling, pointing its patient north. On the back, worn nearly smooth by forty years of a thumb that wasn't theirs anymore: an engraving too small to read unless you already knew what it said.

*For the truth is rough, and must be provisioned.*

His joke. His only joke, told so many times it had worn into scripture. Tamsin's father had been a hydrologist for a government that later insisted it had never employed him. He had carried this compass up every river in the Karveth Range for nine years, and then he had gone up one more river with four colleagues and come down as a sealed file and a widow's pension paid late and grudgingly, and a seven-year-old daughter who was told he fell.

*Fell,* Tamsin thought. *Into what?*

Their hands were not quite steady. They turned the notebook back to the bearing and did the thing they had done ten thousand times on ten thousand ridgelines without thinking: corrected for declination, translated grid to true, laid the number against the landscape in their head. It was reflex, muscle memory, the guide's arithmetic that ran underneath language.

And the answer came back wrong. Wrong in the specific way that made the hair rise on their arms.

Because the bearing didn't point at some anonymous fold of the upper glacier. It pointed — within the tolerance of any instrument ever made — at the exact heading Tamsin had been following for three days. The heading they'd taken from the survey gear, from the abandoned camp, from the trail of a team walking toward whatever killed them. The heading that led upriver, past the treeline, past the last cairn, to the source.

The same bearing. To the decimal.

Tamsin sat very still and let the cold move through them.

It could be coincidence. They made themselves say it, made the words stand up in the dark of the cave and defend themselves. Rivers drain valleys; valleys funnel travel; anyone going upstream in this range would climb the same corridor. Of course the bearings converged. Geometry, not fate.

But geometry didn't explain page twenty-three.

*Day 9. Found a cairn today that isn't ours. Old. Restacked many times over decades — different hands, different stones. Inside, a tin box, rusted shut. Letters? Records? We couldn't open it. V. photographed everything. She has gone quiet in a way I don't like. D. jokes about ghosts. Nobody laughs.*

Beneath it, a sketch. The surveyor had been good with a pencil — the cairn rendered in careful cross-hatching, a heap of stones on a bench of rock above a river bend. And Tamsin knew the bend. Knew it the way you know your own handwriting, because they had stacked stones on that bench themselves, twice, at fourteen and again at nineteen, restacking a cairn their father had built, because he had asked them to, the summer before he died. *If anything ever happens to me, keep the cairns standing. Cairns are promises, Tam. Someone has to keep saying yes.*

Someone had kept saying yes. For decades, apparently. Different hands, different stones — the village, Tamsin realized, with a certainty that arrived whole and unbidden. The village that wasn't on maps. People had been climbing to that bench for thirty years, leaving things, marking the way, maintaining a promise whose reason had been buried with the men who made it.

Their father hadn't fallen. Their father had found something. And someone had spent thirty years making sure the finding stayed found — and buried.

The notebook's final pages were different in character. The tidy survey hand loosened, crowded, the letters leaning forward like a man walking fast. Dates compressed. Entries came hourly, then in fragments.

*Day 11. The vault is real. Steel door in the ice, older than anything should be up here. V. says the state knows. V. says the state has always known. She won't say how she knows.*

*Day 12. R. argued with V. last night. Voices. I think R. wants to go down. I think V. wants us to go up and stay up. I have copies of everything. If we don't come back—*

The entry ended mid-sentence. The next page held only the bearing again, written larger, underlined twice, and beneath it five words in a hand pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*FOR WHOEVER CLIMBS AFTER US.*

Tamsin closed the notebook.

For a long moment there was only the sound of the river, and Rook's breathing, and the small ringing quiet that follows when something detonates underground — the blast itself unheard, felt only as the ground changing its mind beneath you.

They had taken this job for money. That was the story they'd been telling themselves, and it had the advantage of being mostly true. Eleven months ago a survey team walked up this river carrying instruments and questions, and Tamsin had followed their gear upstream for three days telling themselves it was contract work, salvage, a paycheck with frostbite risk. Even after the body in the rapids. Even after Ilse's gun and Ilse's lies. Even after Corrigan's smile in Halden's Rest, offering reinstatement like a hook dressed in bait.

But the compass around their neck had never been following the survey team.

It had been coming home.

"Tamsin."

Rook was awake. He'd been awake long enough to watch them read, Tamsin realized — long enough to see them take out the compass, see their face do whatever their face had done. He pushed himself up against the wall, wincing, eyes sharp despite the exhaustion pooled under them. "You found it. The last pages."

"You knew." Not a question. The words came out flat and cold, riverbed stones. "You knew what he was. You knew who he was."

"I knew the name Okonkwo was on the original expedition roster," Rook said carefully. "Thirty years back. My leader found it in an archive — an appendix, a list of names the state redacted everywhere else. When I saw your surname in Halden's Rest, I—" He stopped. Started again. "I wondered."

"Wondered." Tamsin laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You watched me carry that compass for three days. You watched me read this. And you *wondered.*"

"What was I supposed to say?" Rook's voice cracked upward, and for once he didn't smooth it down. "That the man whose death made you who you are might have died fifty meters from where my team died? That the same people who erased him signed the order that erased them? You'd have turned around. Or you'd have gone up alone and gotten killed, and I'd have two ghosts instead of four."

"And now?"

"Now you're going anyway." Rook nodded at the notebook. "I can see it. You've got the look. My leader had the look, the last morning." He rubbed his face with both hands. "V. — our V., Dr. Varga — she stood at that door and she said, *they collected all this so it would survive them. So it would outlast the men who buried it. All we have to do is refuse to be the generation that loses it.* And then she looked at me and said, *Rook, somebody climbs after us someday. Make sure they can find the way.*"

Tamsin looked down at the compass. The needle trembled faintly with their pulse, pointing its patient north, pointing upriver, pointing at a steel door in the ice and a voice they had not heard in thirty years but would know anywhere — *the truth is rough, and must be provisioned* — a voice preserved, maybe, on tape or wire or wax behind that door, among decades of testimony from families who had kept cairns standing and promises alive in a valley the maps refused to admit existed.

Their father had climbed here to tell the truth. The state had buried him for it. And the state had then spent thirty years building a second burial — smaller, pettier, quieter — out of a blacklisted guide's reputation, a ruined name, a career drowned in a manufactured disaster, so that the daughter who might one day climb the same river would be too broken, too broke, and too ashamed to be believed if she ever arrived.

Corrigan hadn't ruined Tamsin's life out of malice. He'd done it as maintenance. Groundskeeping.

The fury that rose in them then was unlike anything they had ever felt on a mountain. Mountains taught you that anger kills — anger makes you rush a crossing, skip a anchor, trust the weather to be kind. Tamsin had spent fifteen years drilling it out of themselves, sanding it down to professional calm. This did not sand. This went down to bedrock, and what it found there was not rage exactly. It was purpose. Cold, load-bearing, structural.

"Your leader," Tamsin said. "She wanted to go public. And you ran."

Rook flinched. "Yes."

"Why are you still here, Rook? Why haven't you run again?"

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the light strengthened by degrees, laying a pale blade of sun across the cave floor, across the notebook, across the brass compass in Tamsin's palm.

"Because she was right," he said finally, "and I let her die for it. And because when I saw you in Halden's Rest — broke, blacklisted, taking cash work from a woman you didn't trust, following a bearing you didn't understand yet — I thought, *there. There's the person who climbs after us.* I just didn't have the courage to tell you it was you until you figured it out yourself." He met Tamsin's eyes. "So. Now you know what's up there. Now you know whose voice is waiting behind that door. Do we go?"

Tamsin stood. Shouldered the pack. Wrapped the notebook back in its oilcloth and stowed it against the frame of the pack, next to the water and the rope — provisions, in the only sense that mattered.

"We go," they said. "And Rook — from here on, you don't manage what I know. You don't decide what I can bear. Understood?"

"Understood."

Tamsin stepped out of the cave into the white morning, raised the compass, and took the bearing — their father's bearing, the surveyor's bearing, the same number written in three hands across thirty years — and began to climb.

Behind them, far downstream and far below, faint on the wind off the scree, came a sound they had learned to dread in another life: the distant, rhythmic thrum of helicopter blades, working their way up the valley.

Corrigan had found the trail.

* * *

They climbed fast and high, trading the valley floor for the shoulder of the eastern ridge, putting rock between themselves and the rotor sound. By midday the helicopters had faded to memory, or to patience — with Corrigan it was impossible to know which, and Tamsin suspected patience. A man who builds a blacklist thinks in seasons, not afternoons.

They crossed the bench above the river bend at midafternoon, and Tamsin stopped without deciding to stop.

The cairn stood where the sketch said it would. Restacked many times, by many hands — Tamsin could see it now, the different stones, the different grammars of placement: some piled quick and functional, some fitted with a care that bordered on devotion. Thirty years of yes. They knelt, and cleared the frost from the center stones, and found the seam where the tin box had once sat, a rust-ghost outline pressed into the lichen like a fossil of a promise.

Inside, wedged deep where the old box had been, was a new thing: a plastic sleeve, zip-sealed, containing a single sheet of paper, recent, the ink barely faded.

A list of names. Fourteen of them. Villagers' names, Tamsin guessed — descendants, keepers of the cairns — each with a date beside it, and beside the dates, in a different hand, a single repeated notation:

*Reached the door. Turned back. Still true. Still waiting.*

The last entry was dated four months ago. Two weeks before the survey team came up the river.

Tamsin put the list back exactly as they'd found it, restacked the stones with their father's care, and stood. Somewhere above them, past the icefalls, past the seracs, under thirty meters of living glacier, a steel door older than the state was holding shut against the weight of everything it remembered.

The compass hung at their chest, needle steady, pointing the way home.

They climbed.
