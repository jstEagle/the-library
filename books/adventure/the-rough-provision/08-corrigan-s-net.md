## 8. Corrigan's Net

**MINISTRY OF INTERIOR — DIRECTIVE 44-KARVETH**
*Classification: RESTRICTED / EYES ONLY*
*From: Office of the Deputy Minister, Corrigan, A.*
*To: Regional Command, Northern District*

Effective 0600 hours, all passes, trails, and watercourses draining the Karveth headwaters are to be considered closed. No civilian traffic in or out. The individuals listed in Annex A are to be detained on sight. Lethal force is authorized only in defense of life. This operation does not exist. It was never authorized. If asked, you are conducting a flood-risk assessment.

The river took four people. We are going to find out where it put them.

— C.

* * *

Deputy Minister Aldous Corrigan read the directive twice after his aide printed it, the way he read everything twice: once for content, once for weakness. The paper trembled slightly in the draft from the window, and he pinned it flat with two fingers as though it might try to escape him.

Outside, Halden's Rest was waking up badly. Rain on the tin roofs, the kind of thin persistent rain that made men stupid and roads treacherous. He could hear the market below his borrowed office — the border town's one street doing its daily business of smuggling, haggling, and pretending not to notice government vehicles.

"Annex A," he said.

His aide, a young man named Pell with the unfortunate habit of blinking too often, slid a single sheet across the desk. One name. One photograph, eleven years old, taken from a guide certification file that should have been shredded. Tamsin Okonkwo, standing in front of a rope bridge with the particular half-smile of someone who has not yet learned what mountains cost.

Corrigan studied the face for a long moment. He had ruined this face once already, carefully, through channels — an inquiry here, a leaked report there, a blacklist entered into two countries' registries within the same week. It had been tidy work. It had been necessary work. And now the face had walked back into his operational area like a bad debt, carrying a dead man's notebook, if Varga's last transmission was to be believed.

"The bounty," he said. "Make it substantial but not obscene. Fifty thousand. Enough that every trapper, every muleteer, every cousin with a grudge starts looking at strangers sideways. Not enough that anyone wonders why."

"And the wording, sir?"

"'Wanted for questioning in connection with the deaths of the Karveth survey team.'" Corrigan smiled without warmth. "Questioning. We're very reasonable people."

Pell wrote it down. Corrigan watched him write it, and thought about the vault under the ice, and about forty years of careful forgetting, and about how the worst thing a man could do was start remembering out loud.

"Get me Varga's channel status," he said. "And find out which exits our friend Okonkwo is most likely to try. They'll go high. Blacklisted guides always think altitude will hide them." He turned back to the window, watching rain stitch itself down the glass. "It never does. Altitude only makes them easier to see against the snow."

**RADIO LOG — NORTHERN DISTRICT TRACKER UNITS**
*Frequency 4.7 MHz, encrypted relay, excerpts*

0612 — BASE: All units confirm positions. Kestrel at the west col. Heron at the lower gorge. Magpie holding the lake road. Nothing moves yet. Weather closing in from the north; window is maybe thirty-six hours before the white stuff.

0615 — KESTREL: Copy. West col is a joke, base. One trail, knee-deep scree, you can hear a man breathing at four hundred meters. If they come this way we'll hear them praying first.

0617 — HERON: Gorge is running high. Anybody tries the river route they'll be fishing bodies again. Request permission to string wire across the narrows.

0618 — BASE: Denied. No infrastructure. This is a flood assessment, remember. Say it back.

0619 — HERON: Flood assessment. Copy. My grandmother would laugh herself sick.

0620 — BASE: Your grandmother isn't on this net, Heron.

0623 — MAGPIE: Base, Magpie. Town gossip says the bounty poster went up an hour ago. Fifty thousand for the guide. Half the town's already up on their roofs with binoculars. You want these people motivated, you've done it.

0626 — BASE: Motivated is good. Creative is not. Nobody goes above the treeline alone. Pairs only. The survey team went up there in fours and came down in zeros.

0627 — KESTREL: What did happen to the survey team, base? Officially?

0629 — BASE: Officially? They were lost. Unofficially, Kestrel, stop asking questions you'll wish you hadn't heard the answers to.

* * *

Ilse Varga heard none of that transmission, because her radio was off, wrapped in a wool sock at the bottom of her pack like a sleeping animal she didn't trust. But she knew its heartbeat anyway. She had spent nine years knowing it. There was a weight a government radio had, even silent — a gravitational pull toward the voice on the other end, and hers was pulling west, toward Halden's Rest, toward Corrigan's borrowed office with its rain-streaked window.

She crouched at the mouth of the cave and watched the valley through falling weather. Below, at the tree line, she could just make out figures moving along the lower trail. Not hikers. Hikers argued about pace. These moved in pairs, evenly spaced, the way men move when they're being paid by the result.

"They've sealed it," she said quietly.

Behind her, in the cave's blue dark, Tamsin looked up from the notebook. Rook slept against the wall, or performed sleeping, which with Rook was often the same thing.

"How many?"

"Six that I can count. Probably more in town." Ilse didn't turn around. "They'll have the passes by now. The gorge. The lake road. Every way down is a throat, and they're standing in all of them."

"Then we don't go down."

"You can't stay up here. The storm coming in off the north ridge will kill you faster than Corrigan will."

Tamsin closed the notebook with a soft, deliberate sound, like a door being shut on something valuable. "You know his playbook," Tamsin said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I helped write parts of it." Ilse finally turned. In the grey light her face looked carved rather than lived-in. "He seals the valley, then he lets the weather do the work. Three days of storm and whoever's up here comes down frozen, or desperate, or both. Desperate people make mistakes. Mistakes make corpses. Corpses make closed cases." She paused. "And the bounty means every local eye is his eye now. You can't buy bread. You can't buy rope. Someone will sell you for fifty thousand and sleep well after."

Rook's voice came from the dark, dry as old snow. "I'd sell me for fifty thousand. I'm offended it's that cheap."

"Shut up, Rook," said Tamsin, and Ilse noted — filed away, the way she filed everything — that there was no real edge in it. Two days ago Tamsin had wanted to leave him in the cave. Now the refusal was reflexive, almost tender, and that frightened her more than the trackers did, because loyalty was the one weapon Corrigan always knew how to turn around.

* * *

**NOTICE — POSTED AT HALDEN'S REST MARKET SQUARE, THE FERRY LANDING, AND THE CHURCH DOOR**

*By order of the Northern District Authority:*

REWARD — 50,000 MARKS

For information leading to the apprehension of:

**OKONKWO, TAMSIN**
Mountain guide. Last seen armed with survey equipment. Wanted for questioning in connection with the deaths of four members of the government survey team, Karveth Range.

Do not approach. Report to any District officer.

*The deceased deserve justice. Help us give it to them.*

* * *

Marta Ilic read the notice three times, her basket of onions getting heavier in her hand each time, though the onions hadn't changed.

She had known Tamsin Okonkwo eleven years ago, back when the name still opened doors — back when Tamsin had carried her father's medicine chest over the pass in a whiteout because no one else would go, and had refused payment, and had eaten dinner at their table laughing about the marmots. Then the disaster, whatever it truly was, and the blacklist, and the silence that followed a person through a small country like a smell.

Now the government said Tamsin had killed four people. The government also said, in the same breathless season, that the survey team had simply gotten lost, which Marta's brother-in-law — who packed freight into the high valleys and saw everything — said was impossible, because that team had radios, and satellite phones, and the best equipment money could print, and nobody loses five people who can call home.

Nobody loses them quietly, either. Not for six days. Not without search parties going where the search parties were told not to go.

She looked at the photograph. Eleven years older than the woman who'd carried medicine through a whiteout, but the same set of the jaw.

Fifty thousand marks was two years of everything.

Marta bought her onions, and said nothing to anyone, and hated herself a little for how long she stood there deciding.

* * *

In the cave, the storm arrived early, the way debts do.

It came down the north ridge like something poured, erasing the peaks in white, filling the world to its edges with a hiss so total it became a kind of silence. Ilse lit the small stove and the three of them watched the flame lean in the draft, and nobody spoke for a long time, because the wind outside was saying everything that needed saying: *the exits are closed, the clock is running, decide.*

"We cross the high pass," Tamsin said at last.

Ilse looked up sharply. "In this?"

"In this. Everyone knows the storm closes the low routes. Nobody believes anyone would take the saddle above the glacier in a whiteout." Tamsin's eyes had gone somewhere distant and vertical, reading terrain that wasn't visible anymore, that existed only in memory and muscle. "My father took that crossing in worse. He used to say the mountain doesn't hide you. The weather hides you, if you're willing to be nothing for a while."

"And if the weather decides otherwise?"

"Then the weather collects." Tamsin fed a stick into the stove. "It's owed, either way. It always is."

Rook sat up, and for once there was no performance in his face — just a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "The saddle above the glacier," he said slowly. "That puts us directly over the source."

"It puts us over everything," Tamsin said. "That's rather the point."

Ilse felt the radio's absence against her hip like a missing tooth, and thought of Corrigan's voice on the other end of every frequency in the valley, patient and certain, certain that the mountain would deliver them to him frozen and folded and finished.

She let herself hope, briefly and dangerously, that for once the mountain would keep what it was given.

Outside, the snow thickened until the cave mouth became a grey curtain, and somewhere far below, six pairs of boots waited at the throats of the valley for footsteps that were not coming — because above them, in the white blindness, three people began packing rope.
