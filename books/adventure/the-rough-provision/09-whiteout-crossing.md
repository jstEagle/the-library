## 9. Whiteout Crossing

The storm came down the valley like something with intent.

Tamsin had watched weather build in the Karveth for twenty years — the slow grey bruising over the western peaks, the way the light went flat and metallic before the wind arrived — and they had learned to read it the way other people read faces. This one they read from the mouth of the cave at dawn, and what it said was: *you have four hours, maybe five, and then the pass closes and everything on either side of it belongs to me.*

"We go now or we don't go," Tamsin said.

Rook stood at the cave mouth with his arms crossed against the cold, watching the same sky. He'd slept badly — Tamsin had heard him turning in the dark, muttering fragments of names that weren't his own — and there was a rawness around his eyes that hadn't been there yesterday. "The pass is at three thousand meters," he said. "In this?"

"The pass is at three thousand meters in anything. That's why it's a pass." Tamsin cinched the last strap on their pack and checked the rope coil out of habit, though they both knew neither of them was fool enough to travel unroped above the icefall. "Storm'll hold the trackers down too. Corrigan's men are good, but they're not stupid, and only stupid men climb into that."

"And if we're the stupid ones?"

"Then we're stupid together." Tamsin shouldered the pack. The notebook rode against their chest, inside the waterproof shell, where it had ridden for six days now — the dead surveyor's careful hand, the bearings that matched a compass Tamsin had carried since they were nineteen years old. They did not let themselves think about that. Not yet. Thinking about it felt like standing at the edge of a cornice, testing snow that might hold or might drop you eight hundred feet onto rock.

They climbed out of the cave into air that tasted of iron and coming snow.

* * *

The first two hours were kind, which Tamsin distrusted immediately.

Kindness in mountains was always a loan, never a gift. The sky held its grey weight overhead without releasing it; the wind moved in long low breaths across the glacier's shoulder; the snow underfoot squeaked but did not drift. They made good time along the moraine, boots crunching, breath smoking, and for a while there was nothing in the world but rhythm — step, breathe, plant, step — and the small animal comfort of another person's footprints beside your own.

Rook walked well. Better than he had six days ago, when Tamsin had found him half-frozen in the cave below the tree line, mumbling and frost-nipped and lying through chattering teeth about how he'd gotten separated from his team. He'd recovered the way certain people recover — fast, completely, as though hardship were a coat he could take off and hang by the door. Tamsin kept noticing things like that. Kept setting them aside in the same mental drawer where they kept the inconsistencies of Rook's story: the split in the team that didn't match the camp's layout, the direction he claimed to have run that would have taken him away from shelter instead of toward it, the way he never quite said the word *vault* without looking at the horizon.

"You keep staring at me," Rook said, without turning his head.

"I'm checking your gait."

"My gait is fine."

"Your gait is fine *now*. I've seen strong walkers die at altitude because their gait went fine-to-not-fine between one switchback and the next." Tamsin lengthened their stride to come level with him. Below them the glacier bent away in a long blue curve, and beyond it the valley they'd climbed out of lay folded in shadow, already beginning to fill with cloud. "Talk to me. Count backward from a hundred by sevens."

"A hundred, ninety-three, eighty-six—" Rook glanced sideways. "You're serious."

"Altitude dulls people before it kills them. Keep counting."

He counted. His voice stayed steady. Somewhere around forty-two the wind changed.

Tamsin felt it before they heard it — a pressure shift, a flattening of the air, the particular hush that comes when the mountain inhales. Then the sound arrived: not a roar, exactly, but a vast sliding friction, like the whole sky being dragged across stone. The grey ceiling that had hung overhead all morning began to move, visibly move, pouring eastward off the summit ridge, and the temperature dropped ten degrees in the space of thirty seconds.

"There it is," Tamsin said quietly. "Loan's called in."

They roped up at the base of the pass proper — twelve meters apart, Tamsin leading — and started up into weather that no longer deserved to be called weather. It was a place. It was a white country with its own laws. Snow came horizontally, then vertically, then in spirals that seemed to have no source, and the wind drove ice crystals into every gap between skin and fabric with a patience that felt personal. Visibility collapsed from fifty meters to twenty to ten to nothing — not darkness, which would at least have been honest, but a featureless pearl-grey void in which Tamsin's own outstretched hand was a rumor.

They navigated by compass and count. Two hundred paces bearing 047, then a rising traverse of ninety paces bearing 090, then the crevasse field where the route bent north. Tamsin had crossed this pass eleven times. They knew it the way you know a house in the dark. But houses change their furniture, and glaciers change everything, and somewhere in the third hour Tamsin realized with a cold slide of the stomach that the terrain under their feet did not match the terrain in their head.

They stopped. The wind screamed past. Behind them, twelve meters of rope, Rook was a pale smudge, barely a smudge.

"Belay!" Tamsin shouted into the wind, and drove their axe in, and crouched with their back to the storm, trying to think.

The problem was simple and enormous: they were off-route. Off-route on a glaciated pass in a whiteout, with a storm that had hours yet to spend, with a man on the rope whose story had more holes than the ice beneath them. Every instinct Tamsin owned said *dig in, wait it out* — but waiting meant cold injuries, meant burning fuel they'd need later, meant sitting still while Corrigan's net closed behind them. And going on meant walking blind across ground that might open into a crevasse wide enough to swallow them both.

"Tamsin!" Rook's voice, shredded by wind. "What's happening?"

"Route's drifted. I need to find the crest line." Tamsin raised their head, squinting into the void. There — almost nothing, a shading in the grey, a suggestion of slope falling away to the left. Or was it right? In a whiteout your eyes lied to you constantly, offered you horizons that were hallucinations, dropped phantom cliffs into flat ground. You had to trust instruments and feet and nothing else.

They trusted their feet. Their feet said the snow here was wind-slab, hollow-talking, the kind that bridges things.

"Stay on the rope," Tamsin called back. "I'm going to probe forward. If I say *down*, get down."

They took four steps. On the fifth, the world opened.

There was no dramatic crack, no warning shudder — just a sudden absence where the ground had been, and Tamsin was falling, arms windmilling, the rope snapping taut around their harness with a force that slammed the breath out of them. For one lurching instant they swung free over a blue-black depth full of drifting snow, and the thought that went through their mind was absurdly specific: *the compass — protect the compass* — and then the rope caught, jerked, held, and Tamsin was hanging in space, spinning slowly, alive.

"Rook!" Their voice came out cracked. "ROOK!"

Above, at the lip of the crevasse, a shape against the pearl-grey. Rook had thrown himself flat, had gotten his axe in, had somehow arrested a fall that should have dragged him straight in after them. Now he was working — Tamsin could see it even through the blur of snow — building an anchor, methodical, fast, hands moving with a competence that looked nothing like a man who'd spent the last week stumbling and lying.

"Talk to me!" Rook shouted down.

"I'm hanging! I'm — I'm all right. Rope's good. Don't come to the lip!"

"I'm not an idiot!"

"No," Tamsin gasped, "apparently you're not—"

It took forty minutes. Forty minutes of screaming wind and burning arms, Rook paying out line inch by inch from his anchor while Tamsin climbed the rope with prusiks frozen stiff, boots kicking uselessly against smooth blue ice. Twice Tamsin's grip failed and they dropped, jerking, swinging, tasting blood where their teeth cut their cheek. The cold found the gaps in their clothing and worked at them with fingers of absolute patience. Somewhere in the middle of it Tamsin stopped thinking entirely and became pure effort — pull, breathe, clamp, pull — and when finally they hauled themselves over the lip and collapsed onto the wind-slab, gasping like a landed fish, the storm was still there, indifferent, eternal.

Rook knelt over them. His face was a mask of rime, eyebrows frosted white, eyes the only living thing in it.

"You caught me," Tamsin croaked.

"You told me to stay on the rope."

"I told you not to come to the lip. You anchored like a professional. That was a textbook crevasse rescue in a whiteout, performed alone, in under an hour." Tamsin pushed themselves up on their elbows, and the question came out before they could stop it, stripped bare by exhaustion: "Where did a surveyor learn to do that?"

Something moved behind Rook's eyes. Something closed.

"Get up," he said. "We can't stop here. You know we can't stop here."

* * *

They found shelter at dusk — a shallow melt-cave beneath the lee side of a serac, barely deep enough for two, but out of the worst of the wind. Tamsin got the stove lit with hands that wouldn't quite obey, and they sat shoulder to shoulder in the blue gloom while snow hissed past the entrance and the little flame threw their shadows huge against the ice.

Rook pressed a cup of melted snow into Tamsin's hands. Their fingers were clumsy around it. Two fingertips on the left hand had gone waxy-white during the fall and were only now flushing back to painful life.

"You should have let go of the rope," Tamsin said.

"What?"

"When I fell. Standard procedure says if the load's unarrestable, you protect yourself. You didn't. You threw yourself down and held on, and if that anchor had failed, we'd both be at the bottom of that crevasse." Tamsin turned to look at him. In the stove-light his face was gaunt and young and very tired. "Why?"

Rook stared into the flame for a long time. The wind moaned outside, a low shifting note, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Because everyone else on my team is dead," he said finally. "And most of them are dead because of me."

Tamsin went still. "You've told me the team split. That you ran when Halvorsen talked about going public."

"That's what I told you." Rook's laugh was a dry, terrible sound. "Here's what happened. We found the vault. Halvorsen wanted to broadcast — said the world needed to hear those recordings, said that was the whole point of collecting them. And I told him he was insane. I told him nobody thanks you for digging up bodies, especially bodies the state buried. And then—" His jaw worked. "Corrigan contacted me. Months before, actually. Back when the survey was just a line item. He'd recruited me as a watchman. Report anything unusual. A retainer, paid quiet, into an account my sister set up. When we found the vault, I reported it. Within the hour. I used the emergency beacon — told the team it was a routine position fix."

The stove hissed. Snow ticked against the ice above them.

"They came up the valley two days later," Rook went on, voice flat as the whiteout. "Not soldiers — contractors, deniable ones. Halvorsen sent me downstream to check a cache. Deliberately, I think, afterward — I think he knew what I was by then, and he sent me away so I wouldn't have to watch. Or so I couldn't warn them. I don't know which is worse." He looked at his hands. "I sold them the location, Tamsin. I took the money. And when I understood what was going to happen, I ran — not to help them, not to stop it. Just ran. And I've been running for nine days, and every direction I run, I end up next to you."

The silence that followed was the longest of Tamsin's life. Wind. Flame. The small ticking of cooling metal.

Every thread of the last week rewove itself in Tamsin's mind: the cave, the lies, the competent anchor, the counting backward by sevens. The man who had saved their life six hours ago was the man who had fed their hunters the map. Loyalty and betrayal sat in the same body, breathing the same thin air, and there was no clean way to cut between them.

"You could have let me fall," Tamsin said at last. "Down there. One slack rope. No witnesses."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Rook met their eyes, and there was nothing left in his face to hide behind. "Because I am so tired," he said, "of being the reason people die on this mountain."

Outside, the storm went on screaming. And far below them, faint but unmistakable through a break in the wind, came a sound that turned Tamsin's blood to ice water: the whine of a helicopter, climbing the valley in the dark.
