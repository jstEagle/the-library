## 10. The Village That Isn't on Maps

The storm broke at dawn, and what it left behind was a valley that should not have existed.

Tamsin stood at the lip of the descent with the compass open in their palm, watching cloud peel off the mountainside like wet paper. Below them, wedged between two walls of granite that no map in Halden's Rest had ever acknowledged, lay a fold of green — terraces cut into the slope, stone walls silvered with lichen, smoke rising thin and straight from chimneys that had no business being there. A river ran through it, milky with rock flour, feeding fields of barley that glowed pale gold in the early light.

"That's not possible," Rook said behind them. His voice was raw from three days of coughing. "I've flown this range. I've surveyed this range. There's nothing here."

"There's nothing on the maps," Tamsin corrected. They closed the compass. Their father's voice lived in that instrument somehow — *the map is what they let you see* — and Tamsin had spent thirty years not understanding him until this moment. "Different thing."

They descended through old-growth pine that thickened as they dropped, the air warming, smelling suddenly of soil and growing things after days of ice. The trail they found was not a trail so much as an absence of obstacles — stones moved long ago, roots worn smooth by generations of feet. Someone had maintained this path for longer than the state had existed to forbid it.

Rook stopped twice on the way down, once to cough and once to look back up at the pass they'd crossed, his face gray with exhaustion and something else. Guilt had a geography, Tamsin was learning. It sat differently on different people. On Rook it sat like a pack loaded wrong, pulling him backward every time he tried to move forward.

They were still a quarter mile from the first terrace when the children appeared.

Three of them, barefoot on the cold stone, standing across the path with the unhurried confidence of kids who had never been told to fear strangers because strangers were impossible here. The oldest couldn't have been more than ten. She looked at Tamsin's frostbitten face, at Rook swaying on his feet, and said something in a language Tamsin didn't know — vowels rounded, consonants soft, nothing like the state tongue.

Then she said, in accented but fluent state speech: "You came over the White Shoulder. Nobody comes over the White Shoulder." A pause, her dark eyes moving between them with unnerving gravity. "Grandmother says you'll be wanting soup."

Tamsin laughed before they could stop themselves — a cracked, disused sound. "Your grandmother sounds very wise."

"She says everyone who crosses the White Shoulder thinks they're wise too, right up until the mountain teaches them otherwise." The girl stepped aside. "Come on, then. Before the trackers' drones come back over the ridge. They fly at midmorning when the thermals are good."

The word *trackers* landed like a stone in still water. Tamsin exchanged a glance with Rook, whose face had gone carefully blank — the blankness of a man recalculating everything.

*

The village was called Serevan, though the girl — her name was Mirela — admitted the name meant nothing outside the valley. Forty households, maybe fewer, built of stone and timber, roofs weighted against wind. Chickens scratched in the lanes. An old man was repairing a loom in his doorway and did not look up as they passed, which Tamsin understood immediately as courtesy rather than indifference: he was choosing not to see them, and that choice was a gift.

Mirela led them to a longhouse at the village center, larger than the others, its doorframe carved with spirals worn nearly smooth. Inside, a fire burned in a central hearth, and the smell hit Tamsin like a physical thing — woodsmoke, barley broth, dried herbs hanging from beams, the deep animal warmth of a home that had been continuously warm for centuries.

The grandmother was small and upright, white hair braided tight against her skull, hands like tree roots. She looked at Tamsin for a long moment without greeting, and Tamsin felt the inspection go all the way through them — past the blacklisted name, past the ruined reputation, down to whatever was underneath.

"You are Okonkwo's child," she said at last.

The floor seemed to tilt. "My father never came here."

"No. He wanted to. He wrote to us for eleven years." She gestured to a bench. "Sit. Eat first. Grief keeps; hunger doesn't."

The soup was barley and lamb root and something green Tamsin couldn't name, and it was the best thing Tamsin had ever tasted, or grief and exhaustion made it seem so. Rook ate like a man who had forgotten food existed, then fell asleep sitting up against the wall, mid-chew, his bowl still in his hands. The grandmother watched him with an expression Tamsin couldn't read.

"Your friend sleeps like a man who hasn't slept honestly in years," she observed.

"He hasn't."

"Mm." She refilled Tamsin's bowl herself. "We know why you've come, guide. Not the details. But the direction. People come up the river asking about the source, and they come from downstream, and downstream is where the government lives. So." She folded her root-hard hands. "Tell me what you know about the vault."

"The vault," Tamsin repeated.

"Do not insult us both by pretending ignorance. You carry his compass. You crossed the pass in a whiteout in spring. You are either very lost or very found, and nobody crosses the White Shoulder by accident."

So Tamsin told her. The survey team, the notebook, the bearings, the drilling rig at the glacier's face, the steel door older than the state. And then — because the grandmother's eyes demanded it, because lying to her felt like lying to the mountain itself — the rest. Corrigan. The blacklist. The massacre records they believed were sealed in the ice. The bounty now out naming Tamsin a murderer.

When Tamsin finished, the grandmother was quiet for a long time. Outside, a rooster crowed absurdly, defiantly, in a valley that officially did not exist.

"My name is Baba Yeva," she said finally. "And I am going to tell you what my grandmother told me, and what hers told her, all the way back to the winter of the killing." Her voice didn't waver. It had been sanded flat by repetition, by decades of telling a story no one outside would hear. "You asked what the vault holds. I will tell you: it holds us."

*

Rook woke near dusk, and the whole village gathered in the longhouse — forty-odd faces lit by firelight, farmers and weavers and a stonemason with hands like paving slabs, and every one of them a descendant of the dead.

Baba Yeva spoke first, and the room listened the way stone listens.

"In the year the state calls its founding, soldiers came up this river and cleared these valleys. Every settlement. Eleven thousand people, by our count — theirs was lower, always lower. Our ancestors who survived did not survive by fighting. They survived by walking upriver, into the high country, into places the maps refused to go. And they carried one thing with them above all else." She held up a hand, fingers spread. "Testimony."

An old man rose, the loom-mender, and brought a wooden chest to the fire. Its lid was scarred, patched, older than anything Tamsin had ever touched.

"Every family kept the names," Yeva continued. "The dead, the disappeared, the villages erased. Written on birch bark, on hide, on paper smuggled in over sixty years. Recorded voices, later — when we got machines. Grandmothers telling granddaughters what happened, and the granddaughters recording it, and hiding the recordings where the state would never think to look." Her mouth curved, dry as riverbed. "Where did the state never look? Up. Always up. Into the cold. So seventy years ago, our people began carrying the archive up the glacier and sealing it in the ice, in a place that was already old when the state was young. A vault. Steel and stone under the glacier's heart. We added to it every generation. My own voice is in there. I recorded my testimony when I was nineteen, and again at fifty, and my granddaughter will record hers."

Tamsin's throat had gone tight. "The survey team—"

"Found it in autumn. Four of them, following the water. Good people, we think — they came down to the valley after, shaking, asking questions we hadn't been asked in generations. Their leader, a woman named Dr. Aslan, sat where you're sitting and listened to the whole of it." Yeva's eyes went briefly, unbearably soft. "She said, *This cannot stay buried twice.* She said she would make it public, properly, with evidence, with names, with the weight of the state's own instruments behind it. And then they went back up the river." Her jaw tightened. "And did not come down."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere a baby fussed and was hushed.

"They were killed for it," Tamsin said. Not a question.

"Four of them. The fifth ran." Yeva's gaze slid, deliberate and terrible, to Rook. "Or so we heard."

Every head in the longhouse turned. Rook sat very still, his face the color of ash, and Tamsin watched him understand that there was nowhere in the world this story had not already reached.

"I didn't kill them," he said hoarsely. "I need you to hear that. I ran. Whatever else is true, whatever I've done — I ran, and they died, and I have been running since. But I didn't—"

"We know you didn't kill them," Yeva said, cutting him off with terrifying gentleness. "If you had, you would not have come over the White Shoulder. The guilty go downstream, young man. Only the haunted come up."

Something in Rook broke then — quietly, the way ice breaks, all at once and without sound. He put his face in his hands. No one moved to comfort him. No one moved away from him either, and Tamsin understood that this, too, was a kind of mercy this valley knew how to give.

*

Later, when the fire had burned low and the villagers had drifted home, Yeva led Tamsin out onto the longhouse step. The sky was doing what high-country skies do at night — pouring stars, the Milky Way thick as spilled milk above the black teeth of the ridgeline. Down-valley, faint but unmistakable, a drone's navigation light blinked its patient red.

"They watch the passes," Yeva said, following Tamsin's gaze. "They have always watched. They know the valley exists — how could they not? — but knowing and admitting are different things. To admit we exist is to admit what was done to fill this valley with ghosts. So we are unmapped. Unacknowledged. It has kept us alive and it has kept us buried, both at once." She turned to Tamsin. "But your Deputy Minister has stopped pretending. He has men in the low passes and machines in the sky, and he has named you a killer to make the hunting clean. He will find this valley eventually. Perhaps within days."

"Then we leave tomorrow. Draw them off—"

"No." Yeva's hand closed on Tamsin's wrist, grip like a vise wrapped in skin. "Listen to me, child of Okonkwo. For seventy years we hid the truth because hiding was survival. We sealed our dead in ice because the living would be slaughtered for speaking. Every generation asked the same question: *when?* When will it be safe? When will someone carry our voices down to the world?" Her eyes caught the starlight and held it. "And now the world has sent us a blacklisted guide with a murdered team's notebook and nothing left to lose. Do you understand what you are? You are the answer to a prayer we stopped daring to say aloud."

"Baba Yeva—"

"We are done being a village that isn't on maps." Her voice didn't rise, but it filled the night anyway. "Let them put us on the maps. Let them write what happened here in their histories, in their courts, in their shame. My granddaughter should grow up in a valley with a name that means something other than *hidden*. You will broadcast the testimony. From the source itself, if it must be so. And we will help you — every soul in Serevan, everything we have. Because grief—" she released Tamsin's wrist, and her hand, Tamsin noticed, was trembling now that it had nothing to hold, "—grief is a debt that compounds. Seventy years of interest. It is time it was paid."

Tamsin looked down the valley, toward the glacier they could not see but could feel, the way you feel a wound under bandages. Somewhere up there, in blue ice older than the state, their father's voice waited beside eleven thousand others. *The map is what they let you see.*

"All right," Tamsin said quietly. "But we'll need a route up that no tracker would ever guess. Something off every map."

Yeva smiled then, and it transformed her face — suddenly young, suddenly fierce, a girl of nineteen recording her testimony into the dark.

"Child," she said, "we've had seventy years to dig tunnels. Come inside. There's someone you need to meet — our keeper of the ways. And there's something else you should know." Her smile faded into something graver. "The drone light you saw. It's been circling since sundown. Your Deputy Minister's men aren't searching the passes anymore."

"What do you mean?"

"They've stopped looking for where you went," Yeva said. "Because an hour ago, a signal reached them from somewhere in this valley. Someone down here is transmitting."
