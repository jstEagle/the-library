## 11. Ilse Returns

The radio had been transmitting for eleven hours.

Ilse knew the exact count because she had checked it eleven times, once an hour, in the dark of the lean-to she'd built from spruce boughs and survey tarp above the confluence, and each time the little green light on the set's face had burned steady as a votive candle. Not blinking. Not searching. Transmitting — a continuous carrier wave humming out into the cold like a held breath, telling anyone with a direction-finder exactly where she was.

She could have turned it off. That was the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: she *could have*. Her thumb had rested on the switch a dozen times. The circuit was right there under the rubberized cover, two wires, a child could do it. And each time she had lifted her thumb away and told herself the same lie she'd been telling since Halden's Rest, since before Halden's Rest, since the day she'd first put on a ministry lanyard and learned that the difference between a lie and a report was only who signed it.

*They need to be able to find me,* she told herself. *If I go dark, they'll assume the worst.*

The worst was already assumed. She'd heard it in Corrigan's voice on the last voice contact, three days ago, before she'd switched to carrier-only: *Varga, you will bring them in or you will be brought in with them.* The words had been calm, almost gentle, which was how Corrigan said the most violent things. She had known him nine years. She had watched him ruin Tamsin Okonkwo over a rescue that had failed because the ministry had ordered the helicopter grounded — a fact that existed in one file, in one cabinet, behind one lock, and nowhere else on earth except inside her own memory.

She had done nothing then. She had signed nothing, objected to nothing, gone home and slept badly and said nothing. Nine years of nothing, stacked up like cordwood, and now here she was in the high valley with a transmitter burning her position into the sky every second of every hour, unable to cut the wire.

Not because she was loyal. Because she was afraid. And because some small, rotten, honest part of her understood that the transmitter wasn't a leash Corrigan had put on her.

It was one she'd put on herself.

* * *

She found their fire at dusk, or rather she let them find her, standing at the edge of the alder thicket with her hands visible and empty, the way you approach a frightened animal or a blacklisted guide who has every reason to shoot you.

Tamsin Okonkwo came out of the shadows with a rope coil in one hand and something in their face that was not quite surprise. Behind them, half-lit by the fire, Rook Adeyemi rose slowly from a crouch, and Ilse watched his eyes flick to her hip, to the radio case slung there, and narrow.

"You're a long way from your escort," Tamsin said.

"I don't have an escort anymore." Her breath smoked. "I'm a long way from everything."

"Convenient."

"Yes," she said. "It is. I know how it looks."

"Do you?" Tamsin's voice was flat as lake ice, the kind that holds until it doesn't. "Because the last time I saw you, you were pointing me at a dead man's notebook and telling me a story about geological consulting. Before that you were lying to me about who you worked for. So forgive me if my first instinct isn't to pour you tea."

"I'm not asking for tea." She took one step forward, deliberately slow, and crouched, setting the radio case on the ground between them like a hostage laid down at a border. "I'm asking you to look at this and tell me what you see."

Rook moved closer to the firelight. He looked older than he had in the cave, or maybe just more tired; the whiteout crossing had taken something out of all of them. "A ministry field set," he said. "K-7 series. Encrypted burst capability."

"And?"

"And it's on," Rook said quietly. "You can hear it. There's a hum."

Ilse nodded. She made herself hold Tamsin's gaze while she said it, because looking away would make it worse. "It's been on for eleven hours. Continuous transmission. Anyone with a DF unit within forty kilometers knows precisely where I am, which means they know approximately where you are, because I am standing next to you." She let that settle into the cold air between them. "I'm not going to pretend I forgot to turn it off. I want you to understand what you're deciding about."

Tamsin didn't move for a long moment. Firelight moved across their face instead, orange and shadow trading places, and Ilse thought: *they're doing the arithmetic.* She had watched them do it on the glacier, reading terrain, reading weather, reading people, always the same narrowed attention, the same refusal to accept the obvious answer when the obvious answer might kill you.

"You're bait," Tamsin said finally. Not angry. Almost gentle, which was worse. "Or you're a leash. Either way, you're walking toward us wearing a bell."

"I'm walking toward you because I have nowhere else to walk." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the way nine years of composure tore so easily along a seam she hadn't known was there. "Corrigan gave me an order. Bring you in or be brought in. I've spent my whole career being the person who delivers orders, Okonkwo. Do you know what happens to a person like that when they finally refuse? Nothing graceful. Nothing dramatic. You just stop sleeping, and then one day you're sitting in a spruce shelter listening to your own radio betray you, and you realize you left it on because some part of you wanted to be caught, because being caught would mean the choosing was over."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, snow slid off a branch with a soft whump.

"That's not a reason to trust you," Tamsin said.

"No. It's a reason to believe I'm telling the truth about being afraid. Those aren't the same thing, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending they are." She knelt back on her heels. "Here's what I can give you. Corrigan's plan. What he intends to do with the vault, with the village, with you. I've been in the room. I've carried the paperwork. And I'm the only person who's ever walked out of one of those rooms still able to say it out loud."

* * *

They let her come to the fire, but they made her sit on the far side of it, the flames between her and them, and she understood the geometry and accepted it. Rook fed the fire spruce knots that spat resinous smoke. Tamsin sat with the notebook — the dead surveyor's notebook, waterproofed against his chest, the one object in this whole valley that mattered more than any of their lives — balanced on one knee, not opening it, just resting a hand on it like a man rests a hand on a dog that might bite.

Ilse told them about Camp Four. About the drilling rig Corrigan had authorized through a shell contractor, the thermite charges listed on manifests as "geological sampling equipment," the timetable. About the village — the one that wasn't on maps — and the word that had circulated through the ministry channels, quiet and bureaucratic and obscene: *resettlement contingency*. About the file labeled with a number instead of a name, the one containing the original operation from thirty years ago, the massacre, the one whose existence Corrigan had built his entire career on keeping theoretical.

"He's not coming to destroy the records because he believes in the state," she said. "He doesn't believe in anything. He's coming because those pages are the only proof that he helped build the cover-up, and proof is the one thing he's never allowed to exist. Everything else — the villagers, the survey team, you — that's inventory. Manageable. But paper survives. Paper gets found. He taught me that himself, years ago, when we were shredding the flight logs from your rescue." She saw Tamsin flinch, a small motion, quickly mastered, and made herself keep going. "Yes. I was there. I fed the pages into the machine. I've been waiting nine years to say that sentence to someone who'd understand what it cost."

"Why?" Rook asked. His voice was hoarse. "Why tell us now?"

"Because I shredded those logs and then I went home and ate dinner, and I have thought about that dinner every single day since." She looked into the fire. "There's no version of this where I get clean, Adeyemi. I understand that. I'm not asking for clean. I'm asking for useful."

Silence, except the fire and the wind moving down-valley, carrying the faint mineral smell of the glacier, that smell like cold iron and old stone that Ilse had learned meant they were getting close to the source.

Tamsin spoke without looking up. "Your radio. If we take it with us, they track the radio. If we leave it, they track its absence. If we smash it—"

"They assume capture or death and stop being careful," Ilse finished. "Which is worse. Right now Corrigan thinks I'm stalling, playing a long game, maybe even genuinely hunting you. As long as that signal keeps singing, he thinks he understands me. The moment it stops, he stops predicting and starts assuming." She reached out and touched the case, the little green light glowing through the canvas. "I know. Believe me, I've run it every way. There's no configuration of this radio that makes me safe. Or trustworthy. It's just the truth about me, broadcasting. Take it or leave it."

* * *

Later, when Rook had taken first watch and folded himself into the rocks upslope, Tamsin came and sat beside her. Not close. A meter of cold air between them, the polite distance of two people who had no reason to trust each other and every reason to need each other.

"You could still turn it off," Tamsin said.

"I could."

"But you won't."

Ilse watched the green light. "Do you want to know the worst part? I don't entirely know why. Part of it is fear. Part of it is—" She stopped. Started again. "When I was a girl, my mother kept a canary. And when it died she couldn't bring herself to take the cage down, so for months there was this empty cage hanging in the kitchen, and I'd catch her looking at it. I think the radio is like that. Proof that something used to sing."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. When they spoke, their voice had changed — lower, rougher, the guide's voice rather than the interrogator's. "Nine years ago I stood in a ministry office and begged a man to launch a helicopter. He looked at me the way you look at a problem, not a person. Then he ruined me to cover the decision. I have spent nine years assuming everyone who works in that building is him." They turned and looked at her, and the firelight caught something in their expression that was not forgiveness, not yet, but was perhaps the ground forgiveness grows in. "You're not him. That's the trouble. Him I'd know how to handle."

"You could handle me too," Ilse said. "Cut the wire. Leave me here. Walk faster than I can follow. No one would blame you."

"No," Tamsin said. "No one would. That's what makes it a choice." They stood, brushing snow from their knees, and paused. "Keep the radio on. Keep it honest. If that signal moves somewhere it shouldn't — if you start leading us in circles, if the bearing drifts toward Corrigan's positions — I'll know. I've read terrain my whole life, Varga. Radio has terrain too."

"I know," she said. "That's why I told you about it."

Tamsin nodded once and walked back toward the fire, and Ilse sat alone in the blue dark with the transmitter humming against her hip, and felt — for the first time in nine years — that the signal going out into the night was not a leash but a promise, and that she had finally chosen which of the two it was.

Then the light changed.

Not the steady green. It blinked — twice, three times, a stuttering pulse she recognized from training days she'd hoped to forget: an incoming priority burst, queued and waiting, sent hours ago, decrypting now on the little screen behind the rubber flap.

Her hands were steady. She gave them credit for that. She opened the flap.

Four words glowed in the dark:

**VILLAGE COORDINATES CONFIRMED. PROCEED.**

Below them, in the hidden valley, forty people were asleep in houses that were not on any map. And somewhere north, moving fast, Corrigan's men were already descending.
