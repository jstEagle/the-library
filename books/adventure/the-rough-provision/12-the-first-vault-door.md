## 12. The First Vault Door

The glacier announced itself before we saw it — a sound like a door being dragged open somewhere beneath the world, deep and grinding, patient as a debt.

We had climbed since first light, following the river backward into its own beginning. That was the thing about the Karveth that no map admitted: the river didn't begin so much as *arrive*, spilling from under the ice in a flood of blue-grey meltwater that tasted of stone and something older, something mineral and wrong, like water that had been sitting in a locked room for a very long time. Tamsin Okonkwo's rule number one was never drink from a source you don't understand. I broke it at noon because my throat was a rasp and the water was there, and it tasted like cold iron and old paper. Like archives. Like a library drowned.

Rook watched me swallow and said nothing, which from Rook Adeyemi was a paragraph.

* * *

The notebook had led us true for three days. Davorin Mlakar's handwriting started confident, surveyor-neat, elevations and bearings marching down the pages like soldiers on parade. By the last third it deteriorated — letters slanting, margins crowded with fragments: *not natural. not geological. ask V.* And then, on the final written page, a single line pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

**THE DOOR IS IN THE ICE AND THE ICE IS NOT OLD ENOUGH TO HAVE MADE IT.**

I'd read that sentence maybe forty times since the cave. Each time it did the same thing to me: it reached into my chest and turned something over, the way you'd turn over a stone expecting worms and finding instead a hinge.

Because I knew what lay under this glacier. Not the specifics — those had been scrubbed from every record Corrigan's people could reach, which is why the scrubbing itself was the confession — but the shape of it. My father had been a hydrologist for the northern district before the state decided he was a problem. Before they made him disappear so thoroughly that even his death certificate was a forgery of a forgery. He used to say, when I was small enough to sit on his knee and bored enough to listen: *Rivers keep accounts, Tamsin. Everything the mountain takes, the river writes down.* I'd thought it was poetry. It wasn't. It was a warning.

The compass in my pocket had been his. The bearing in Davorin's final pages matched it to the degree. Which meant either coincidence — and I had stopped believing in that particular god years ago, on a mountainside littered with the consequences of other people's arithmetic — or the dead man had been following my father's work without knowing whose ghost walked beside him.

"Smoke," Rook said.

I smelled it too. Faint, chemical, out of place — drilling fluid and diesel, threaded thin through the glacial wind. Someone had been burning fuel up here recently enough that the wind hadn't finished erasing them.

We came around the shoulder of the moraine single file, low, moving the way you move when you've spent your life learning what kills people in high places: not avalanches, mostly. Other people.

* * *

The survey team's camp sat at the glacier's toe like something that had died mid-sentence.

Three dome tents, two of them collapsed under fresh snow-load, one still taut. A drilling rig — an honest-to-god core drill, the kind that costs more than a house in Halden's Rest — stood anchored to the ice on four screw-jacks, its mast tilted toward the glacier face at the precise angle of a question being asked. Coiled hose. Fuel drums, two full, one split and frozen into the ice like amber holding a fly. A generator, silent now, its fuel line drained by someone who'd intended to come back.

Nobody had come back.

The snow around the camp told the story in the language snow tells it — honestly, and only once. There were boot prints, many, going toward the glacier face. There were drag marks. There was a wide swath where something heavy had been moved, or someone had been carried, and then the snow smoothed out into wind-scour and nothing else. No tracks leaving. Just like the camp above the treeline. Just like everywhere these people had ever stopped being alive.

"Fresh," Rook said. He crouched at the edge of the disturbed snow, gloved fingers hovering. "Two days. Maybe three."

I looked at the rig. Its bit was still warm when I touched the housing — not warm from sun, warm from *work*. Somewhere under all that ice, machinery had been running within days, and the men who ran it were gone, and the only witnesses were me, a liar with frostbitten fingertips, and a glacier that keeps everything and gives back nothing.

Then I saw the blood.

It was at the base of the rig's near leg, half-buried in drift, and if you weren't looking you'd have called it rust or spilled hydraulic fluid. But I have bled on enough mountains to know blood's color at every temperature. This was dark red going brown at the edges, soaked into the snow, frozen into a fan shape where someone had knelt — no, where someone had *been knelt*, forced down, and then—

There was a lot of it. Too much for a cut hand. Not enough for a body. The math of it turned my stomach over: enough blood to mean a wound you don't walk away from, arranged in a pattern that meant somebody had tried to stop the bleeding, and then stopped trying.

Rook had gone very still beside me. When I glanced over, he was looking at the blood with an expression I couldn't read, and his hands were shaking — not cold-shaking, though it was cold enough. Something else.

"You knew them," I said. Not a question.

"I knew Davorin." His voice came out sanded flat. "He taught me how to read ice cores. He hummed when he worked. Badly." He swallowed. "That's his blood pattern. He always— he kept a cloth in his left sleeve, for nosebleeds. Altitude. He got them above five thousand meters." Rook's breath fogged and tore away. "He's not here. There's no body. So either he walked off a fatal wound, or—"

"Or someone took him," I finished.

Downstream, three days' travel below us, we had pulled Davorin Mlakar's body out of a rapids with a waterproofed notebook against his chest. I had held that notebook. I had read that handwriting. And here was his blood, fresh, at a drill site he could not have survived reaching twice.

Unless the body downstream wasn't Davorin.

Unless the notebook had been sent down the river deliberately — a message in a bottle, launched by a dying man or a clever one — while Davorin himself went somewhere else. Somewhere the state couldn't find. Or was taken somewhere the state wanted him.

I crouched by the blood and let the cold climb up through my knees and did the only thing I know how to do with horror: I turned it into logistics. Blood means injury. Injury means time. Time means someone was here long enough to bleed. The drill was aimed at the glacier face forty meters left of where the seracs overhung — a careful, professional placement, chosen by someone who understood ice. Chosen by a guide's training, or a surveyor's caution, or both.

"They drilled here," I said aloud, because saying things makes them real and I needed this real. "They found something under the face and they drilled to reach it. And then Corrigan's people arrived, and—" I gestured at the tents, the drag marks, the absence. "Cleanup crew."

"And the drill found it anyway." Rook was standing again, staring up at the glacier face, and his voice had changed — gone quiet, reverent, frightened, all three braided together. "Tamsin. Look at the face."

* * *

I looked.

You grow up in mountains, you think you've seen ice. You haven't. You've seen weather that froze. The Karveth's main glacier was something else — a river of it, yes, but compressed over centuries into a depth of blue that isn't a color so much as a depth of time, translucent at the edges and utterly opaque at heart, veined with ash layers from fires before anyone living was born. Standing before it felt like standing before a wall built by the planet itself, mortarless, seamless, indifferent.

And set into that wall, forty meters up and slightly left of the drill site, was a rectangle.

My first thought was *trick of light*. My second was *crevasse shadow*. My third thought arrived without words at all — just my father's voice, thirty years old, saying *rivers keep accounts*, and my hand already pulling out his compass, thumbing the bearing from Davorin's notebook, and the needle swinging around to point not at magnetic north but directly, unambiguously, at the rectangle in the ice.

A door.

Not carved. Not installed. *Encased.* The ice had formed around it the way water forms around any obstacle — flowing, closing, forgetting — except the ice here was young, geologically speaking, a few centuries at most, and the door was steel, riveted, rusted at the seams into colors like dried blood and old bronze. Its frame projected from the glacier face perhaps a hand's width, and around that frame the ice had melted back in a perfect ellipse, as if the metal shed warmth, or the ice refused it. Rivet heads the size of plums. A wheel-lock in the center, ship's-hatch style, furred with frost. And stamped into the lintel plate, half-eroded, characters in no alphabet I knew — angular, dense, older-looking than anything human hands should have put this far north and this far underground.

"It's not on any survey," Rook whispered. "I processed the satellite passes myself. There is *nothing* here. The imagery shows clean ice."

"The imagery shows what someone paid for it to show." I was climbing before I'd decided to climb, front-pointing up the lower apron of the glacier toward the drill site, because the alternative was standing still and thinking about what a door older than the state implied about everything the state had ever told me. "Corrigan didn't send a survey team to find this. He sent them to *confirm* it. To measure it. To figure out how to get inside and empty it."

"Empty it of what?"

Of testimony. Of records. Of whatever my father had died trying to carry out of these mountains and failed to carry, because the state had gotten to him first — or gotten to the vault first, sealed it deeper, buried it twice. The village elders had told us, in the hidden valley: *they collected our voices for sixty years, and then the voices went into the ice, and then the ice became a secret.* Every massacre survivor who ever spoke into a recorder, every name written down, every account smuggled out in grain sacks and sewn into coat linings — it had all come here. To be kept safe. To be kept *gone*.

I reached the drill site proper and clipped in, and that was when I saw what the drill had actually done.

They hadn't drilled *through* to the door. They'd drilled alongside it — a borehole angling down behind the frame, chasing the gap between steel and ice, the way water chases a crack. Someone had understood that the door wasn't sealed into the glacier; the glacier was sealed around the door, and the difference mattered. You don't break a vault. You find the seam.

The borehole mouth gaped at my boots, thirty centimeters across, lined with slush refreezing in ripples. And jammed in its throat, snapped off at the collar, was the drill string — sheared clean, the break bright and new.

Something down there had stopped the drill. Not ice. Ice doesn't shear carbide steel.

Rook came up beside me, breathing hard, and followed my gaze down the hole. For a long moment neither of us said anything. The glacier ticked and groaned around us, settling its ancient weight, and somewhere deep underneath — so deep I might have imagined it, so deep I will spend the rest of my life deciding whether I imagined it — something answered the silence with a sound like a struck bell, muffled by fifty meters of ice.

"What was that?" Rook's whisper barely qualified as sound.

"That," I said, "was the vault telling us it knows we're here."

I didn't believe that. I'm a guide; I deal in gradients and load limits and the exact tensile strength of rope. I don't deal in glaciers that ring like bells. But my father's compass was pointing at the door, and the door was pointing at something underneath, and every instinct I owned — the ones that kept me alive when better guides than me died of confidence — was screaming that we were standing on top of the most important room in the country, and that the men who built their careers on keeping it shut were two days behind us with rifles and a budget.

"We can't get in through the face," I said. "Not with hand tools, not before Corrigan's net closes. The ice is fifty meters thick here. We need another way." I looked downstream, toward the hidden valley, toward the crevasse fields where the glacier fractured and folded over itself like a brain. "Old ice moves. New ice fractures. Somewhere in that chaos there'll be a route under the face — a crevasse system that tunnels back toward the bedrock. Blacklisted guides learn routes that don't exist on maps. That's rather the point of us."

Rook stared at me. "You want to go *under* the glacier."

"I want to go where Corrigan's people can't follow without the same map we have. And I want—" I stopped. Made myself say it. "My father's voice is in there. The village says so. Sixty years of recordings, and his among them. I am not leaving this mountain without hearing it."

The wind picked up, streaming spindrift off the seracs like smoke off a fire. Below us, the camp sat gutted and silent, Davorin's blood going brown in the snow, the sheared drill string pointing down into the dark like a finger pressed to lips.

And the door in the ice — older than the state, older than the maps, older than every lie Corrigan had ever told — waited with the patience of something that had already outlasted one empire and fully intended to outlast his.

Rook touched my arm. "Tamsin. The blood. If Davorin's alive—"

"If he's alive, he's down there." I nodded at the borehole, at the glacier, at the bell-sound still fading in my bones. "And if he's down there, then so is everything else. Come on. We're finding the crevasse route before the light goes."

Behind us, the glacier gave a long, tectonic sigh, and the frost on the door's wheel-lock glittered once, like an eye opening.
