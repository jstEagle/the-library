## 13. What Was Buried Twice

Rook had learned, over the years, that the body kept its own ledger. It remembered every debt long after the mind had filed the paperwork away and called it settled. His shoulders carried the weight of a helicopter door he'd pulled shut with his own hands. His knees carried the memory of kneeling on a survey tarp in Halden's Rest while Corrigan's man counted out bills into his palm like a man feeding something he intended to keep hungry. And now, sitting in the blue dark of the ice cave below the glacier's face, his whole skeleton seemed to carry the one debt he'd never once spoken aloud — not to himself, not to God, not to the river.

Tamsin was asleep against the far wall, or close enough to it. Their breathing had gone slow and even, that shallow mountain sleep that never quite lets go of the ear. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the wind worked at the glacier's snout with the patience of a sculptor who had all of eternity and no deadline, and somewhere beneath them, behind steel older than the state itself, the dead waited with their testimony frozen in the dark.

Rook turned the dead man's notebook over in his hands. He hadn't told Tamsin he'd read it. Every page. Twice.

* * *

It started, if you could call it a start, with a phone call in a parking garage in the capital — the kind of call Rook had told himself for eleven years he'd been forced into, until the night he finally admitted it had been a choice, made freely, by a man who wanted money more than he wanted to be clean.

He'd been broke the way only a man with a reputation can be broke: too proud to beg, too compromised to be hired by anyone honest. The survey team had been his redemption arc, or so he'd told himself. Adeyemi, the steady one. Adeyemi, the man who'd once pulled two climbers off a cornice in whiteout and walked down the mountain carrying one of them on his back. He'd spent years building that man back up out of the wreckage of the first version, the one who drank, the one who owed people, the one who would eventually sit across from a deputy minister's fixer and hear the words *glacial anomaly, Karveth headwaters, need a local man who knows the approaches* and understand exactly what was being asked and say yes anyway.

They'd paid him to do reconnaissance. That was the official shape of it. A geological survey, they said, though the questions were wrong from the beginning — not *what's up there* but *who lives up there*, not *how deep is the ice* but *how stable is the approach in summer*. He'd flown the valley twice in a chartered plane with cameras running. He'd marked the village on a map for men whose maps deliberately did not contain it. He'd sat in Corrigan's office — he'd never met Corrigan himself, only the office, the smell of it, lemon polish and cold coffee, the photograph of the minister on the wall — and watched a man with kind eyes trace the route Rook had drawn and say, *you've done your country a service, Mr. Adeyemi,* and feel the words land like coins dropped into a well he couldn't see the bottom of.

The money had been real. That was the obscenity of it. Forty thousand, in used notes, in a canvas bag, and it had paid off his debts and bought six months of being nobody's creditor and he had hated every hour of it because the money didn't buy silence, it bought appetite. Three months later they came back. *Follow-up work.* A second payment. A third. By the fourth he understood he wasn't a contractor anymore; he was an asset, and assets don't retire, they get used up.

Then the survey team formed, and someone — he still didn't know who, and the not-knowing was its own punishment — put his name forward as the fifth member. The experienced one. The local knowledge. He'd told himself it was a chance to get inside, to find out what they actually wanted, maybe even to protect whatever was up there by knowing where it was. He'd told himself a great many things. Self-deception, he'd learned, is just lying with better production values.

* * *

The team had been good. That was the part that ate him. If they'd been fools, if they'd been careless, if any of them had deserved what came down the mountain toward them, the arithmetic might have balanced differently. But Maren Halvorsen, the leader, had been the best field scientist Rook ever worked under — meticulous, generous, the kind of woman who gave you the last of the coffee and wrote down your observations in her own log so your name would be on the record. Dziedzic, the drill specialist, hummed opera badly and could keep a rig running in conditions that should have stripped its gears. Okoro and Lindqvist were young, hungry, funny in the way people are funny when they don't yet know the mountain is keeping score.

And the vault — when Dziedzic's thermal imaging found the void behind forty meters of ice, when they drilled the pilot core and the core came back smelling of paper and machine oil instead of stone — Maren had gone quiet in a way Rook had never seen a scientist go quiet. She'd held the core sample up to the light like a relic. *This isn't geology,* she said. *This is an archive.*

He should have known then. He'd known, actually. Some part of him had known since the parking garage, since the questions about stability and access, since the map with the village inked onto it by his own hand. But knowing is a thing the mind does, and doing is a thing the body must be asked to do, and between those two states lies the whole swamp of a man's character.

Maren decided, on the fourth night, around the stove, that the findings went public. Not through channels. Channels were how things got buried. She'd said it plainly, the way she said everything: *Whatever this is, it belongs to the families it happened to. We transmit from the source or we don't transmit at all.*

And Rook — Rook, who had already sent one coded message down-valley from the satellite phone, a message that said only *target confirmed, archive substantial, recommend assessment team* — Rook had sat there warming his hands and said nothing.

That silence was the murder. Everything after was logistics.

* * *

The assessment team arrived on the ninth day. He'd heard the helicopter before he saw it, a sound wrong for the altitude, wrong for the weather window, and he'd known — the way you know a rockfall is coming, in the teeth, in the gut, before the eye confirms it — that everything after that rotor-sound was going to be aftermath.

They came up the glacier at dusk. Six men, civilian outdoor gear, military carriage. No insignia. They moved the way Rook had seen soldiers move in places where soldiers weren't supposed to be, which is to say they moved like water finding a channel, economical and total. Their leader was a compact man with a voice like gravel settling, and he'd looked at Rook across the camp and said, *Mr. Adeyemi. Your work is appreciated,* and Rook had felt the last door in himself swing shut.

What happened next, he had spent every night since reassembling and disassembling, looking for the version where he could have stopped it, and there wasn't one. There was no version. There was only the sequence.

They separated the team first. That was the method — he understood later that it was always the method, the state's oldest tool, applied here at the top of the world where no one could hear anything. Maren was taken aside to examine the core samples with the assessment officer. Dziedzic was walked down to the rig. Okoro and Lindqvist were sent to check the upper station, and Rook, standing in the middle of it with his hands in his pockets like a bystander at his own execution, let it happen. He opened his mouth. He closed it. He told himself there were six of them and one of him and a rifle he didn't have, and every word was true, and none of it mattered, because the truth underneath the true words was simpler: he was afraid, and he had been paid, and a man who has been paid is a man who has agreed.

Okoro fell from the upper station. That was the report. A cornice, a misstep, the mountain's indifference. Rook saw the body afterward, at the edge, positioned wrong for a fall, arms tucked like a man set down carefully rather than thrown carelessly, and he knew, and he said nothing.

Lindqvist went into the moulin two days later, checking a meltwater channel alone, which no one on that team would ever have done, because Lindqvist was the one who'd invented their rope protocol and lectured them all about solo travel on ice. The moulin took her and the river kept her and the report said *tragic accident during survey operations.*

Dziedzic's rig failed. Hydraulic line, they said. The failure threw a fragment — they were very sorry — and he died before the descent party reached him. Rook helped carry the stretcher. He remembered the weight of it. He remembered thinking: *this is what forty thousand dollars weighs.*

Maren lasted longest. She understood, near the end — he saw it arrive in her face the way weather arrives, all at once and from everywhere — that the accidents had a pattern, that the pattern had an author, and that the author was sitting in her camp eating her rations. On the last night she came to his tent. She didn't accuse him. That was the worst of it. She sat down across from him and said, *Rook. Whatever they have on you, whatever they're holding over you, it isn't heavier than this. Walk down with me. We go public together, we go loud, we make ourselves too visible to disappear.*

And he had looked at her — this woman who had written his observations into her log so his name would be on the record, who had given him the last of the coffee — and he had said, *I can't, Maren.*

She'd nodded. That was the worst part. She nodded like a woman confirming a diagnosis, stood, zipped his tent flap closed against the cold with a small courtesy that undid him more than any curse could have, and walked out onto the ice in the dark without a headlamp.

They found her three days later, downstream, snagged in the shallows above the falls. Face-down. Notebook waterproofed against her chest — no. Against *his* chest. The man Tamsin had pulled from the rapids. Rook had lied even to himself about that detail for weeks, until the notebook surfaced again in the hands of a blacklisted guide with a compass that pointed at the past, and the lie collapsed under its own weight and left him sitting in the wreckage.

Wait. No. He was confusing the dead. That was what guilt did; it shuffled the dead like cards. The notebook man was Dziedzic. Dziedzic had carried the log. Maren had gone into the water unburdened, unrecorded, erased clean — except that nothing was erased, because the vault was still there, and the ice had kept what the state could not burn, and somewhere in that frozen dark Maren's own voice might be waiting, or the voices of the villagers, decades of them, the testimony the massacre's survivors had carried up the mountain piece by piece for fifty years because it was the only place the state's hands couldn't reach.

The state had buried the massacre twice. Once in the ground, once in the lie. And Rook Adeyemi had been the shovel on both counts.

* * *

The stove had gone cold. Tamsin hadn't moved, but Rook knew — the way you know on a mountain when the person pretending to sleep isn't sleeping — that they were awake. Had been awake for some time. Long enough.

He set the notebook down on the crate between them, deliberately, where the dying stove-light caught its waterproof cover.

"You read it," Tamsin said. Not a question. Their voice was rough with the altitude and the hour and something else.

"Every page."

"Then you know what I know." Tamsin sat up slowly. In the half-dark their eyes were the color of the ice outside, and just as unreadable, and just as old. "The bearings match my father's compass. The final entry says the archive goes deeper than the survey found. And the last page—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "The last page is a list of names. Cross-referenced. Someone inside the operation was keeping a tally."

"I was keeping a tally," Rook said.

The words came out of him the way an avalanche comes out of a mountainside — not chosen, not gradual, simply past the point where anything could hold them. He heard his own voice as if from a distance, flat and strange, reciting the inventory of himself: the parking garage, the canvas bag, the maps he'd drawn, the village he'd inked onto paper for men whose maps erased it, the coded message from the satellite phone, *recommend assessment team*, six words that had weighed exactly as much as four human lives.

He told it all. He told it in order, because disorder would have been a mercy and he was done taking mercy. The stove stayed dead. The wind rose and fell. Somewhere above them the glacier groaned — that vast slow tectonic complaint of ice under strain — and Rook thought: *even the mountain is keeping minutes.*

When he reached Maren — the tent, the offer, the nod, the dark walk without a lamp — his voice finally broke, and he let it. He'd earned the breaking. He sat in the ruin of his own telling with his head in his hands and waited for Tamsin to do what any sane person would do: take the notebook, take the rope, take the crevasse route, and leave him for the glacier to keep along with everything else it kept.

Instead, Tamsin was quiet for a long time. Then they said, in a voice scraped down to bedrock:

"My father's voice is in that vault. I've known it since the bearings matched. Fifty years the village carried that testimony up the ice, and the state buried it twice, and you—" They exhaled. "You were the shovel."

"Yes."

"And Corrigan built my blacklist to make sure no guide would ever be believed if they stumbled onto this. Two birds. One stone. My ruin was housekeeping."

"Yes."

Tamsin stood. Pulled on their gloves, unhurried, terrible in their calm. "Then we stop talking about what was buried," they said, "and we go open it."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, wrong for this hour and this altitude — came the sound of rotors.
