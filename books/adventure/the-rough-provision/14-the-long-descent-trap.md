## 14. The Long Descent Trap

The descent from the hidden valley began in fog, which Tamsin took for mercy.

They left before dawn, three of them moving single file down the goat track the villagers called the Shepherd's Stair — a name that made the route sound gentler than it was. In truth it was a rib of broken limestone stitched together with old rope, handrails of braided hide gone stiff as iron, the whole of it clinging to a wall that dropped four hundred meters into the river gorge. Below, the water ran the color of gunmetal. Above, the fog ate the ridgelines whole.

Ilse went first. She'd insisted, and Tamsin had let her, because Ilse moved like someone who had been trained by people who taught movement as a language — weight always centered, hands always knowing where the next hold would be before her eyes found it. Whatever else she was, whatever her radio had been transmitting to whatever ear on whatever frequency, she was not a liability on rock.

Rook came last. He was still limping from the whiteout crossing, the frostbite in his left foot having passed through the ugly purple stage into something the village healer called *alive, barely*. He climbed on it without complaint, which Tamsin had learned was its own kind of information. Men who complained wanted things. Men who climbed silently on dead flesh wanted one thing only, and they were looking at it.

*The vault,* Tamsin thought. *Or the money for it. Or forgiveness, which costs more than either.*

The fog thickened as they dropped. It came up the gorge in slow rivers, pouring over the lip of the wall above them and down across the track in cold sheets, until Tamsin could see perhaps four meters in any direction — the wet gleam of the next hold, the frayed ghost of the next handline, and nothing else. The world contracted to the size of a body. Tamsin's father would have called it *climbing inside your own coat*, and would have said it the way he said everything, as if the mountain had whispered it to him first and he was merely passing it along.

Tamsin's compass rode in their chest pocket, over the heart, where it had ridden since Halden's Rest. Since the notebook. Since the final pages of a dead surveyor's handwriting had matched, degree for degree, the bearing the old brass compass had pointed for thirty years — the bearing that led, Tamsin now knew, to the place where the river was born and the state had buried its dead twice.

They had maybe two hours of track left. Two hours to the river confluence, where the Shepherd's Stair met the only safe descent out of the upper gorge. After that, the choices forked: south to the rail line, east over the shoulder of Mount Vess, or down the gorge itself, which no one had descended and lived to describe.

Tamsin had been counting exits the whole way down, the way they counted them in every valley, in every range, in every year since the rescue that went wrong and the two governments that had made sure the wrongness stuck. It was a habit like breathing. You didn't notice it until the air got thin.

That was why Tamsin noticed, four meters of fog away and forty meters of track below, that the birds had stopped.

Not gone quiet. Stopped. The way a clock stops — mid-phrase, mid-argument, the choughs that had been squabbling along the gorge wall all morning simply ceasing to exist as sound. Tamsin froze with one hand on the hide rope and held up the other fist, the old signal, and felt more than saw Ilse go still below.

The fog breathed. The river muttered its one long syllable far beneath them.

And then, threading up through the gorge like smoke through a keyhole, Tamsin heard it: the tick and hiss of a radio squelch. Someone keying a handset. Someone waiting.

* * *

They did not speak. Speaking on rock was for civilians. Tamsin descended to Ilse's ledge in four controlled moves and put their mouth close to her ear.

"How many?"

Ilse's eyes flicked downward, into the fog, reading it the way she read everything — as a document with a hidden text. "Two on the track, maybe. One above us on the wall. They've let us come this far." Her breath was warm and very controlled. "They wanted the confluence. It's the only place we can be taken without a rope."

"They knew the route."

"They've had the route since before we did." Ilse's jaw tightened. "Since me."

Tamsin looked at her. In the fog, her face was half a face, the rest of it a suggestion — the sharp cheekbone, the chapped lip, the eyes that had gone pale in an abandoned camp three weeks and a lifetime ago and had never quite warmed again. Tamsin had spent the whole journey since deciding, and un-deciding, and deciding again, what Ilse Varga was. Leash. Trap. Ally. Instrument. The radio in her pack had transmitted on a schedule Tamsin had never been able to break, and Tamsin had let her keep it because the alternative was leaving her on the ice, and Tamsin had already buried enough people in enough ice to last the arithmetic of two lifetimes.

Now the arithmetic was due.

"Options," Tamsin breathed.

"Back up. They'll have the top of the Stair by the time we reach it, and Rook can't move fast enough." A pause. "Down the gorge. We die beautifully and anonymously."

"And the third option."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. Below them, the squelch ticked again, closer, patient as a debt.

"The third option," she said, "is that I've been thinking about my radio for a long time, and you've been thinking about it too, and only one of us has been thinking about it correctly."

* * *

Rook reached them ten minutes later, breath sawing, and Tamsin laid it out for him in clipped guide's shorthand: two below, one above, confluence covered, no exit clean. Rook listened with the particular stillness of a man who had already imagined being caught a hundred times and had furnished each imagining in detail.

"They'll want me alive," he said. His voice was flat. "I'm the one who sold them the location. I'm the one who can testify the others were traitors. Corrigan doesn't want me dead. He wants me *useful*." His frostbitten foot turned on the ledge and he didn't flinch. "And they want the guide dead. You're the loose end with a compass. And her—" he looked at Ilse, and there was something in his face that wasn't quite hatred and wasn't quite pity, "—her they'll collect like a tool they left in the field."

"Thank you for the inventory," Ilse said dryly.

"I'm saying we should split. I draw them east along the track, you two go down the gorge—"

"No one is going down the gorge," Tamsin said. "The gorge is a rumor with a waterfall in it."

"Then I draw them east and you two take the track—"

"Rook." Tamsin put a hand on his shoulder, and felt through the wet wool how slight he had become, how the mountain had been eating him since the whiteout the way it ate everyone eventually, and how he was letting it. "Nobody is bait today. We came out of that vault together. We go down together or not at all."

It was, Tamsin would think later, the wrong thing to say — or the right thing said at the wrong time — because it was exactly then that Ilse reached into her pack.

* * *

She did it calmly, the way she did everything, kneeling on the ledge as if tying a boot. The radio came out wrapped in oilcloth, and she unwrapped it with the unhurried hands of someone opening a letter they'd read a hundred times. It was a government set, military grey, the kind that could be heard through a wall of fog by anyone tuned to the right frequency for twenty kilometers.

Tamsin understood one full second before it happened. One second is a long time on a mountain. It is enough time to see the whole shape of a thing — the route, the fall line, the ending — and to be unable to move fast enough to change any of it.

"Ilse—"

"The confluence is covered because they're watching for three people moving quiet," she said, and her voice was steady, and her hands were steady, and her eyes — her eyes were the only part of her that wasn't, and she let Tamsin see that, deliberately, the way she had let Tamsin see nothing else for the entire journey. "So we give them three people moving loud. Two run east. One stays and makes noise on the net. They can't ignore a live transmitter. They have to come collect it, and while they're collecting it, the other two are already on the rail line."

"You're not staying," Tamsin said.

"I'm the only one they *won't* shoot." She thumbed the power dial and the set came up with its small warm crackle, obscene in the silence of the fog. "That's the whole point of a leash, Tamsin. Corrigan built me to be retrieved. Every man he sent up this gorge has orders that end at me, not past me. I am the one thing in this valley he values more than the records." She almost smiled. "He's about to learn what that costs."

"The radio's been transmitting since Halden's Rest." Tamsin heard their own voice come out low and rough. "Every camp. Every bearing. You led them to us the whole way, and now you want me to believe—"

"Yes." She didn't look away. "I led them. I reported everything. And then the village, and the testimony, and your father's voice on those tapes, and I stopped reporting, and I've been transmitting dead air on schedule for nine days hoping you'd never find out, because the schedule was the only thing keeping their search pattern where I wanted it." She exhaled. "I was going to tell you at the rail line. This is earlier than I planned. Most things today are."

Above them, faint through the fog, came the scrape of a boot on limestone. The man on the wall, moving down.

Rook had gone very still. "You're turning it on," he said. "You're actually—"

"I'm turning it on." She raised the handset to her mouth, and before she keyed it she looked at Tamsin one last time, and what was in her face was not bravery — Tamsin had seen bravery on mountains and knew its particular blankness — but something older and less photogenic. It was the look of a person settling a debt they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was a debt and had started calling it a spine.

"East ridge, forty minutes, don't look back," she said. "If you look back, it was for nothing. And Tamsin — the notebook. Page thirty-one. The bearing your compass points. Your father walked it in 'sixty-one, before they sealed it. He was the one who taught the villagers the vault was there. That's why Corrigan destroyed you. Not the rescue. The *name*. He's been afraid of that compass for thirty years." She keyed the handset. "Varga, call-sign LARKSPUR. I have them. Confluence, twenty minutes. Send everyone."

The fog took her voice and carried it down the gorge like a stone dropped into deep water.

* * *

They ran east along the track with the fog tearing past them and the radio's ghost crackling somewhere below, and it was the worst running Tamsin had ever done, because every part of the body wanted to go down toward the sound and every part of the training said *up and away, up and away*. Rook pounded along beside her on his dead foot and did not complain, and Tamsin understood that he was running not from the soldiers but from the sound of a woman selling herself to buy them forty minutes, and that he would carry the sound for the rest of his life, and that Tamsin would carry it too, and that between them they would carry it like a rope between two climbers on a face too steep for either alone.

Behind them, faint and then fainter, came the sounds of the collection: a whistle, a shouted word, the tick of squelch, Ilse's voice — calm, professional, giving them the coordinates of two runners who were no longer there. Playing them. Playing Corrigan the way she had played the schedule for nine days, dead air dressed as loyalty.

*She was the leash,* Tamsin thought, lungs burning, boots hammering the old rope-ladders of the Stair. *And a leash is just a rope. And a rope is just a line between two points. And the whole art of the mountain is knowing which end you're tied to.*

They reached the east ridge as the fog began to lift, and below them the confluence came into view — and Tamsin stopped, because the picture below was not the picture Ilse had promised.

Six men. Not three. Six, fanning out from the riverbank, and two of them already turning back up the track, already moving fast, moving *east*, and Corrigan's voice carrying up the gorge on the thin morning air, furious and precise, ordering the net thrown wider.

And in the middle of the riverbank, kneeling between two soldiers with her hands zip-tied and her head held high, Ilse Varga looked up — directly up, across four hundred meters of fog-thinned air, to the ridge where Tamsin stood — and shook her head once.

*Don't look back,* she'd said. *It was for nothing.*

Tamsin looked back. Then turned east, took Rook's arm, and ran.
