## 15. Interrogation at Camp Four

They took her radio first.

That was the tell, Ilse thought, as the man in the grey parka unscrewed the battery housing with a jeweler's patience and dropped both pieces into a canvas sack. Not her hands — they'd zip-tied those behind her back before she'd finished pretending to struggle. Not her pack, which they'd gone through with the thoroughness of customs officers who already knew what they were looking for. The radio. Because Corrigan understood, better than anyone in the service ever had, that information was not a thing you carried. It was a thing you transmitted. And he wanted to know, before anything else, whether she had been transmitting.

She hadn't. Not since the pass. Not since she'd turned it on deliberately in the whiteout and let them triangulate her, let them come for her, because a captured asset moving toward the vault was worth more than a free one circling outside it. That had been the calculation. She held onto it now like a handrail in fog: *I chose this. I am here because I chose this.*

The calculation did not stop her hands from shaking.

"Agent Varga." Corrigan's voice came from behind her, unhurried, almost warm. She heard him cross the snow floor of the tent, boots crunching on the ground cloth they'd laid over the ice. "Sit her up."

Two men hauled her by the shoulders until she was upright on an equipment crate, spine straight, chin level. She'd been trained for this posture in a room with padded walls and a one-way mirror; it translated to tents surprisingly well. Cold was cold. The Karveth Range at four thousand meters was simply a colder room with worse lighting.

Corrigan came around into her field of view. Up close, without the ministerial overcoat and the press-conference distance, he looked older than his file photograph and younger than his reputation — a narrow face, pale eyes, the kind of neatness that read as discipline or as emptiness depending on how much you feared him. She did not fear him. She made herself not fear him, which amounted to the same discipline practiced daily, like scales on a piano.

"You've been busy," he said. "Rogue transmissions. Unlogged movements. A rendezvous with a blacklisted guide and a deserter." He crouched so their eyes were level, and smiled the way men smile when they are showing you their teeth are clean. "I want to hear your version first. Then we'll compare it against mine, and see where the discrepancies fall."

"My version is simple," Ilse said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. "I went dark because I found out what the survey team found. I went upstream because someone had to confirm it wasn't salvageable. It isn't. The vault is a myth. Ice and rock. There's nothing in that glacier but old water."

It was a good lie. She'd built it on the descent from the pass, testing its joints the way you test a rope before trusting it with weight. But Corrigan tilted his head slightly, the way a listener tilts toward a false note in music, and she knew immediately that the lie had been wasted — not because he disbelieved it, but because disbelief was irrelevant to him. He had not come four hundred kilometers into the range to establish facts. He had come to establish *narrative*, and narratives were built from whatever material surrendered most easily.

"Ilse." He used her first name now, gently, a scalpel wrapped in a glove. "You were my best recruiter. Do you remember the Ostrava file? You turned a colonel in eleven days. Eleven days, and he thanked you at the end. So don't insult us both with the glacier-is-empty speech. Tell me instead about the guide."

"Tamsin Okonkwo."

"Yes."

"What about them?"

"Where are they?"

"Gone," Ilse said. "Down the eastern drainage, probably. They took the notebook and ran the moment your trackers hit the valley. You know how blacklisted guides are. Self-preservation is the only religion they practice."

Something flickered across Corrigan's face — not anger. Interest. He stood, walked to the tent flap, and looked out at the evening, at the light going bronze and then violet on the seracs above the glacier tongue, and when he spoke again it was in the tone of a man resuming a lecture he'd given many times to many rooms.

"Do you know why the survey team died, Ilse? Actually died, not the version in the incident report?"

"Exposure," she said. "Avalanche. The report said—"

"The report says what reports say." He turned back to her. "Five people went up that river. One came down. And the one who came down led me here two years ago, and then lost his nerve, and then ran, and has been running ever since. Rook Adeyemi. You've met him, I assume. Everyone meets Rook eventually. He's the kind of man history keeps stepping on."

Ilse kept her face still. Inside, something cold moved through her chest, colder than the tent air. *He led Corrigan here.* She filed it away — another weight on the other pan of the scale, another thing Tamsin would have to be told, or would have to discover, and God help them all when that discovery landed between them on the rope.

"And the others?" she asked aloud. "The four who didn't come down?"

"They found the source," Corrigan said, as if describing weather. "A crevasse system under the firn line, leading down to a steel door set into bedrock. Pre-state construction. Older than any archive we officially possess. And inside—" he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping something he intended to eat slowly— "inside are recordings. Testimony. Decades of it. Collected by villagers who were never supposed to exist, from families who were never supposed to have survived, about an event that never happened according to every document this government has ever issued."

"The massacre," Ilse said quietly.

"There was no massacre," Corrigan said pleasantly. "There was a security operation in the Karveth foothills, forty-one years ago, which restored order, and whose records were sealed by executive order, and which does not appear in any census, any registry, or any history book. What exists in that ice is not history. It is *rumor*. Rumor with voices attached. And rumor with voices attached, Ilse, is the single most dangerous substance on earth. More dangerous than fissile material. Fissile material just kills you. Rumor kills the state."

He crouched again, closer this time, and she could smell coffee on his breath, absurdly domestic, and beneath it the antiseptic tang of the stimulant tabs these men all chewed on long operations.

"So here is what happens next," he said. "And you're going to help me, because the alternative is a tribunal, and tribunals for intelligence officers who go rogue are not gentle things, and your mother is still alive in Vellmar and I know exactly which clinic she sits in every Tuesday."

There it was. Not even hidden. He laid it out flat between them like a card played face-up, and watched her take it, and she let him watch, let her breath catch audibly, let the mask slip just enough to show the fear underneath — because a compliant prisoner who fears correctly is a prisoner left unguarded, and an unguarded prisoner hears everything.

*Let him think the leash works*, she told herself. *Let him hold it loosely.*

"Tell me what you need," she said.

Corrigan's smile widened by a degree. "Thermite charges," he said. "Sixty kilograms, already on the mules coming up the south route. We drill the firn above the crevasse system, we drop the charges into the melt channels, and by Thursday there is no door, no vault, no recordings. Just a slumped glacier and a very sad story about unstable ice claiming another survey team." He straightened. "And the villagers."

Ilse felt the cold go all the way through her now, past performance, past control, into the place where fury actually lived — a small hot furnace under all her professional ice, banked and fed for years and almost never opened. She kept her breathing even. She kept her eyes wide and frightened and attentive, the eyes of a woman calculating her own survival, because that was what he needed to see.

"The villagers," she repeated.

"They sheltered Okonkwo. They've been feeding that vault for forty years. When the glacier comes down, the story writes itself: a cult of mountain fanatics, hoarding sedition, sabotaging a government survey, murdering five of its members. We'll find evidence in their homes. We always find evidence in homes." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "It's cleaner than the alternative. The alternative is letting the rumor out, and the rumor doesn't stay in the mountains, Ilse. Rumor walks downhill faster than any flood. It reaches the capital in a week and the coast in a month, and then everything my predecessors spent four decades burying comes up out of the ground like bodies after a thaw."

He said *bodies after a thaw* without any awareness of what he'd said, and Ilse thought: *you have no idea, none at all, that you are standing on top of them right now.*

"And my role?" she asked. Her voice was smooth. Compliant. A little broken at the edges, the way he wanted it.

"You'll confirm the site for the drilling team. Your credentials open the survey permits; mine open everything else. You'll stand there in your official capacity while the charges go in, and afterward you'll sign the after-action report, and in exchange—" he touched her bound wrists, almost tenderly, cutting the ties with a small knife— "your mother's Tuesdays continue uninterrupted, and your file acquires a redemption arc instead of a termination notice."

Blood rushed back into her hands. She flexed her fingers, watching them, buying three seconds of silence in which to hate him with her whole body, cleanly and completely, the way you love someone — total, focused, without distraction.

"When do the mules arrive?" she asked.

"Tomorrow night. South route, below the moraine." He paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the last violet light on the ice, and delivered the closing line like a man leaving a tip: "Oh — and if Okonkwo contacts you, and they will, guides always circle back — you'll transmit. The radio will be returned to you tonight. Consider it a test you've already passed once."

He meant the whiteout. He meant the triangulation. He believed he had caught her signaling, and she let him believe it, nodded once, lowered her eyes like a woman accepting terms.

When the flap fell shut behind him, she sat alone in the tent with the cold pressing in and the furnace inside her burning so hot she could taste metal. Sixty kilos of thermite. A village to be erased. Testimony forty years deep, melted into the river that had already swallowed four witnesses.

They would return the radio tonight. They would watch her with it.

Fine. Let them watch. A transmitter, she thought, was only half a machine. Every transmission needed a receiver, and somewhere up-valley, in the dark above the firn line, Tamsin Okonkwo was listening to the same silence she was — and Tamsin had spent a lifetime reading mountains, reading weather, reading the gap between what people said and what they meant.

Ilse closed her eyes and began, methodically, to plan her stalling: the permits that would need re-verifying, the drill sites that would need re-surveying, the slow bureaucratic treacle she could pour into Corrigan's schedule, hour by precious hour, while sixty kilos of thermite crawled up the south route toward a door that must not be reached.

Outside, the wind rose off the glacier with a sound like distant voices, dozens of them, layered and patient, waiting under the ice.

She recognized the sound. She was beginning to understand that it was not the wind at all.

* * *

They brought the radio at dusk, as promised.

A young officer — barely more than a boy, chapped lips, frost-nipped ears, the exhausted obedience of someone three days above the treeline doing a job he didn't ask about — set it on the crate beside her and retreated without meeting her eyes. Good. The young ones never met your eyes; it was the first mercy the service taught them and the first thing it took away.

Ilse turned the radio over in her freed hands. Battery intact. Frequency dial untouched. They'd inspected it, certainly, but inspection only proved they didn't understand the machine. Corrigan's people thought in terms of messages — words sent, words intercepted. They had never learned what the old mountain operators knew: that a carrier wave, hummed at intervals, no words at all, told a listening party everything. *I am alive. I am held. Here is where.*

She waited until the camp sounds settled into their night rhythm — generator drone, sentry footsteps compressing snow at measured intervals, the mutter of men around a stove. Then she keyed the transmit switch in short, deliberate presses, three seconds on, three off, three on, and spoke nothing into the gathering dark but the rhythm itself, over and over, a heartbeat broadcast at the glacier.

If Tamsin was listening, Tamsin would know the interval. Anyone who'd worked high rescue knew the standard survival pulse. Three-and-three meant: *alive, captive, position known to captors, do not attempt extraction.*

But there was a second layer, and this was hers alone, improvised in the space between heartbeats: she varied the fourth cycle. Three-and-three, three-and-three, then a single long press — six seconds — before resuming pattern. Any monitor logging her traffic would read jitter, cold-stiffened fingers, signal noise. But a guide who knew her, who had watched her hands work a radio through a storm, would read the anomaly precisely: *the long tone marks the lie. Everything I say under their eyes is the long tone. Trust nothing spoken. Watch what I do.*

She transmitted the sequence twice more, then stopped, set the radio down, and lay back on the cot they'd allowed her, staring at the orange nylon ceiling glowing with stove-light from the command tent next door.

Through the fabric wall, muffled, she could hear Corrigan's voice giving orders — calm, modular, the voice of a man assembling furniture. "...south route mules confirmed... drill team moves at first light tomorrow... and get me the village coordinates again, the ones from the '83 census exclusion..." A pause. Paper rustling. Then, lightly, almost conversationally, to someone in the tent with him:

"No, she's cooperative. Grief does that. People want to be useful after they break." Another pause, and then a sound Ilse knew well — the dry click of a lighter, though smoking was forbidden at altitude, rules being for the people who could still afford them. "Her mother, though. Vellmar. Keep the Tuesday file current. If she steps wrong, we move on the clinic before we move on the glacier."

Ilse lay perfectly still in the dark and let the words land, each one a small calibrated cruelty, and did not let herself shake until the stove-light dimmed and the camp exhaled into true night.

Then, silently, against the wool of her sleeve, she let the fury finish its work. Not the hot kind — the hot kind got people killed. The cold kind. The kind that kept ledgers.

*Melt the vault. Burn the village. Blame the dead.* She turned each item over like stones in her hand, weighing them, and found them heavier than her mother's Tuesdays, heavier than her file, heavier than the tribunal he dangled. Somewhere in the weighing, without ceremony, without relief, she crossed the line she'd been hovering along since Halden's Rest: she was no longer playing both sides. There was only one side now, and it was made of ice and testimony and a guide with a stolen compass bearing, and she belonged to it completely.

Which meant the stalling began at first light.

She had, she estimated, thirty hours before the mules reached the moraine. Thirty hours to lose — permits misfiled, drill sites contested, weather windows argued into oblivion, her own competence weaponized into delay. She was, Corrigan had said, his best recruiter. He'd forgotten the other half of her résumé: she was also the best obstructionist the service had ever trained, and she had never once been caught at it, because obstruction done properly looks exactly like diligence.

Ilse pulled the blanket to her chin and watched the ceiling glow fade, and listened to the glacier settle around her with its patient, layered voices.

Hold on, she thought into the dark, to no one, to everyone. Hold the door.

I'm coming as fast as I can lie.
