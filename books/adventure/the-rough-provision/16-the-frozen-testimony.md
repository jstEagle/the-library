## 16. The Frozen Testimony

The crevasse field had no name on any map, because the maps stopped pretending at this altitude. The Karveth's source glacier spread below us like a river that had been told to hold its breath — a mile of blue-white ice, cracked and ridged and groaning under its own weight, all of it feeding the water that ran down through the valley, through the village that wasn't on maps, through the town where I'd been ruined, out to a country that drank from it every day without asking what the mountain remembered.

I knew the way in because of the worst day of my life.

That was the thing nobody understood about blacklisting. It didn't just take your permits and your name from the registries. It took everything you'd learned in the learning of it. Eleven years of routes, of hidden lines, of places where the ice thinned or held or opened like a throat — all of it still in my body, all of it worthless now except to someone willing to pay cash and ask no questions. And now, standing at the edge of the Serac Field with Rook Adeyemi shivering beside me, it turned out the knowledge was worth exactly one thing: getting inside a vault my government had spent decades pretending didn't exist.

"You're sure about this line," Rook said. Not a question. A man talking to keep his fear at a manageable volume.

"No," I said. "But I'm sure about the other options, and they're worse."

* * *

We went in at first light, when the ice was still frozen hard enough to trust.

The route was one my father had shown me, back when I was nineteen and furious with him for reasons I couldn't have articulated if you'd held a knife to my throat. He'd called it the Seam — a crevasse system where two tributary glaciers ground against each other, leaving a fissure that never fully closed, running down into the glacier's dark interior like a crack in porcelain. He'd found it during a survey season before I was born, and he'd never reported it, which should have told me something even then. My father, who filed every form in triplicate, who believed in institutions the way other men believed in God — he'd kept one secret route off the books his whole career.

I used to think it was sentiment. A place that was only ours.

Standing over it now, rope snaking down into blue darkness, I understood it differently. Some things you find and know immediately: this is not for them.

"Rope's anchored," I said. "You go second. If I say drop, you drop."

"And if you don't say anything?"

"Then I'm dead and you should probably keep going."

He almost smiled. In the week since the whiteout, since his confession by headlamp — *I led Corrigan here, Tamsin. I sold the location once already* — we had built something fragile and strange between us. Not forgiveness. I wasn't there yet, might never be. But there was a kind of honesty in two people who had both been bought and both been broken by it, and honesty, it turns out, holds weight better than trust. Trust snaps clean. Honesty stretches.

Rook clipped in. "Your father found this place?"

"My father found this crack. What's at the bottom of it—" I looked down into the dark. "That's what we're here to find out."

I went over the edge.

* * *

The Seam swallowed sound. That was the first thing, the thing that made my skin crawl even after eleven years away from this work: the silence. On a glacier you live inside noise — the pop and tick of settling ice, meltwater ticking somewhere below like a clock in the walls. But in the Seam the walls were too close, too smooth, and our breath came back to us flattened, as if the mountain were listening and disapproving.

We descended forty meters, then sixty. My headlamp carved a cone out of the dark and the dark took everything outside it. The ice around us changed character as we dropped — surface ice is white, weathered, full of last summer's grit, but deep ice is old ice, compressed until it's blue as a bruise, clear as glass. Bubbles hung suspended in it like trapped breath. Air sealed in centuries ago. The glacier was a library of atmospheres, and we were descending through its stacks.

At eighty meters the crevasse floor appeared — not a floor, a narrowing, the two walls converging into a slot barely wide enough for a body. And cut into the near wall, at the level where a climber would naturally pause:

A door.

Not the steel door the survey team had drilled toward — that one was deeper in, sealed behind the glacier's heart, older than the state. This was newer. Cruder. A hatch of welded plate, rust bleeding down from its hinges in orange tears, with a wheel-lock gone stiff and a stencil across its face so faded I had to brush frost from it with my glove to read it.

HYDROLOGICAL STATION 9. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

Station Nine. I knew the number. Every guide in the Karveth knew the numbers — Stations One through Twelve, the official monitoring posts strung along the range, each one mapped, maintained, inspected. There had never been a Station Nine. The sequence jumped from Eight to Ten, and when I'd asked about it as an apprentice, some twenty years ago, my instructor had laughed and said the numbering was a clerical error, the kind governments made.

My father had kept the route to it off the books.

"Tamsin." Rook's voice, very quiet. "There are fresh marks on the wheel."

He was right. Under the rust, the steel showed bright scratches — recent, weeks old at most. The survey team had come this way. Had known about the Seam, or found it, or been brought to it. And beyond this hatch, somewhere in the glacier's cold interior, they had found the thing that got them killed.

I put both hands on the wheel and threw my weight against it. It resisted, then gave with a shriek that echoed up the shaft like something waking. Cold air poured out through the opening — not glacier-cold, not the wet chill of ice, but a dry, still cold, the cold of a room that had been closed for a very long time.

Inside: stairs. Steel, bolted to the rock beneath the ice, descending into blackness. And wired along the wall, dead and dusted with frost, a cable — thick, industrial, running down into the dark.

Power cable. Someone had run power down here once.

"Okay," Rook said behind me, and his voice had gone thin. "Okay. I'm ready."

Neither of us was ready. We went anyway.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than any hydrological station needed to go. Two hundred steps. Three hundred. My thighs burned and my breath echoed off stone — we were under the ice now, into the bedrock shoulder of the mountain itself, and the air was dry enough to make my lips crack. The cable followed us down. At intervals, old junction boxes, their paint flaking, stamped with dates going back further than I'd expected. Further than the massacre. Further than the republic that ordered it.

Whoever built this had built it before the state existed. Before there was anyone to hide it from.

At the bottom, the stairs opened into a chamber that my headlamp could not finish describing. I stood on the landing and swept the light across it and felt my understanding of the world tilt, quietly, like a table losing a leg.

It was a vault, yes. But it was also a chapel.

The chamber was maybe thirty meters long, hewn rough from bedrock, and along both walls ran shelving — hundreds of linear meters of it — and on the shelves sat canisters. Reel-to-reel tapes in metal cases. Cassette decks in padded boxes. Later, microcassettes, minidiscs, memory drives in anti-static bags, the technology marching forward decade by decade while the shelves filled and filled and filled. Thousands of units. Tens of thousands. Each one labeled in careful handwriting, different hands across the generations, different scripts, different alphabets in the oldest ones.

And in the center of the chamber, a machine. A playback deck, old but maintained, its heads clean, connected to the power cable that climbed back up through the mountain toward whatever generator fed it. Beside it, a single wooden chair.

People had come here for decades. Had carried their testimony down through the Seam, or however they'd come in the early years, and had spoken their dead into these reels, and had climbed back out into a country that called them liars. And the state had known — must have known, eventually — and instead of destroying it, had sealed it. Buried it twice: once in denial, once in ice. Because destroying it would mean admitting it existed.

"They never destroyed it," Rook whispered. He'd come up beside me, and his light was shaking. "All these years. They just—"

"Froze it," I said. "They froze it."

* * *

We worked for hours. There was no other way to say what happened next: we listened.

I found the index first — a ledger, oilcloth-wrapped, in a waterproof case on the shelf nearest the machine. Columns of dates and canister numbers and names, some real, some obviously assumed, the handwriting changing generation to generation. The most recent entries were in English, neat and small. The last entry was dated nine months ago. Canister 4,417. The name beside it was E. Varga.

Ilse. Ilse had recorded her testimony here. Whatever she'd been when she intercepted us on the glacier — leash, ally, hostage — she had sat in that chair and spoken her truth into the dark, knowing what it would cost her.

I set that canister aside with both hands, carefully, the way you handle something alive.

Rook found the survey team's contribution. Their drilling rig topside, their blood on the ice — they'd gotten this far. Their leader, Osei, the one who'd talked of going public, had recorded a summary: a reel labeled in his handwriting, FOUND AND CONFIRMED. FULL INDEX ATTACHED. His voice, when I played it, was calm in the way people are calm when they've decided something irreversible.

*"If you're hearing this, we didn't make it out. The vault is real. The testimony spans six generations. It concerns the Kessel Valley action of — well, the state calls it the Kessel Valley Resettlement. The people who recorded these call it what it was. Play them. All of them. That's the whole instruction. Play them, and don't let anyone tell you the mountain is empty."*

Rook sat down on the floor when it ended. I let him. I was looking at the ledger, running my finger down the early entries, and my heart had begun doing something strange — beating fast and slow at once, the way it does in the moment before a fall.

Because I knew the valley name. Everyone in the guiding trade knew it as a caution: the Kessel drainage, closed to travel, bad history, don't ask. And I knew another thing, a smaller thing, a thing I had carried like a stone in my boot for thirty years without ever letting myself look at it directly: my father had grown up in the Kessel Valley. He'd been evacuated as a child. He'd never spoken of it. He'd become the most institutional man I ever knew — forms in triplicate, faith in the system, faith like penance — and he'd kept one secret route off the books his entire career, and he'd died on a mountain carrying a compass whose bearings matched, to the degree, the final pages of a murdered surveyor's notebook.

Canister 112. Dated fifty-one years ago. The handwriting in the ledger was old-fashioned, careful, a young person's hand trying to be formal.

The name beside it was Okonkwo, J.

I didn't move for a long time. The chamber hummed faintly, the way deep places hum, and somewhere above us the glacier shifted its enormous weight and the sound came down through the rock like a slow tide.

"Tamsin?" Rook said.

I threaded the reel onto the deck. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I would have expected them to shake. Maybe shock does that. Maybe the body understands, before the mind consents, that some doors only open once.

I pressed play.

First there was tape hiss, a long breath of it, and then a voice — young, thinner than I remembered, but underneath the years and the fear it was unmistakable, it was the voice that had taught me knots and stars and how to read avalanche slope, the voice that had said *be careful* at every trailhead and meant *come home* —

*"My name is Jonah Okonkwo. I am fourteen years old. I am recording this so that someone, someday, knows what happened to the people of Kessel Valley, because everyone says it will be forgotten, and I have decided that I will not forget it, and I am putting it here in the ice where forgetting cannot reach.*

*"They came in the winter. The soldiers said it was relocation for safety, and some people believed them, and those people are the ones I grieve the most, because they packed slowly. My grandmother did not believe them. She made us leave at night, up the glacier, and she told me: the river remembers, boy. Rivers carry everything down. One day the water will tell it.*

*"I saw—"*

The tape ran on. I will not write down what the fourteen-year-old voice described, not here, not in any record I control, because he described it precisely and at length and with the terrible clarity of a child who has decided that accuracy is the only monument available. What I will say is that I sat in the wooden chair in the vault under the source of the river, listening to my father at an age younger than my own grief, and the glacier held us both, and the river above us ran on carrying everything down, exactly as my grandmother had promised, exactly as she'd known.

Rook knelt beside the chair. He didn't touch me. He just stayed there, present, the way you stay with someone on a bad ledge — close enough to grab, far enough to fall free.

When the reel ended I wound it back with great care and returned it to its canister and wrote nothing in the ledger, because there was nothing left to add. The entry had been waiting fifty-one years for someone to read it.

"He knew," I said finally. My voice sounded scraped. "He knew about this place. He recorded here. And then he spent his whole life serving the state that did it, filing forms, believing in the system — because believing was easier than remembering."

"Or because he was buying time," Rook said quietly. "Keeping the route alive. Keeping the door findable. Somebody had to remember where Station Nine was, Tamsin. Somebody had to pass it down." He looked at me. "He passed it to you."

The words went through me like a piton driven home.

And that was when we heard it — faint, distorted, rising up the stairwell from the relay speaker wired near the hatch: a radio transmission, encrypted-burst fragments, the signature rhythm I'd heard once before on Ilse's set and hoped never to hear again.

Corrigan's net. Calling in the clear, no encryption at all, which meant they'd stopped caring who heard:

*— all units converge grid seven-four. Subject Okonkwo confirmed inside. Repeat: subject is at the source. Minister inbound tonight. Nothing leaves the mountain—*

Rook was already on his feet. "How long?"

I looked at the ledger, at four thousand canisters of testimony, at my father's fourteen-year-old voice sealed in its case. Then at the stairwell, and the long climb, and the glacier grinding overhead like a slow thought.

"Less than a night," I said. "So we'd better decide right now what we're broadcasting."
