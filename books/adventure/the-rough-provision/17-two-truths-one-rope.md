## 17. Two Truths, One Rope

They came down out of the vault into the blue hour before dawn, when the glacier gave back no light of its own but held, instead, a kind of remembered brightness — the day's sun stored in the ice, leaking out pale and slow, like a coal banked under ash. Tamsin felt it on their face as they emerged from the crevasse mouth, that cold luminescence, and thought: this is what my father's voice sounded like. Something kept. Something glowing faintly in the dark long after the fire went out.

The recordings were safe. That was the only word for it, and even *safe* felt like a loan the mountain could call in at any moment. Tamsin had them distributed now the way a good guide distributes weight across a rope team — the digital copies split between three devices, the original reel-to-reel tapes wrapped in Rook's spare shirt and lashed inside Tamsin's pack, against the spine, where nothing would crush them. Forty years of testimony. Ninety-one voices. The village of Serevan Hollow, which did not appear on any map, had spoken into a government microphone for four decades believing that someday, someone would listen.

And among them, on the fourth tape, in a young man's accent that Tamsin had spent twenty years trying to forget and could not: *My name is Ekwueme Okonkwo. I was a road engineer on the Karveth project. What I am about to describe happened on the fourteenth of October—*

Tamsin had not listened past that sentence. Not yet. They would. They had to. But not yet.

Rook was waiting at the snow cave they'd cut below the icefall, a shallow scoop in the glacier's shoulder roofed with a tarp and two crossed ski poles, barely a shelter at all. He had a stove going — the little white-gas burner hissing its small stubborn song — and two cups of melted snow with the grey tinge of glacial flour suspended in them, grit you drank because the alternative was dehydration. He'd taken off one boot and was working feeling back into his toes, and he looked up when Tamsin crawled in, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because the vault was still between them, still filling the small space the way the ice filled the valley: completely, and in silence.

"You heard it," Rook said finally. Not a question. He'd been outside the vault door when Tamsin had stopped moving, when the sound they made had not been a word at all.

"I heard the beginning."

"Yeah." Rook turned his cup in his hands. His fingers were clumsy with cold, the knuckles split and scabbed from the drilling work. "Your old man."

"My old man." Tamsin said it flatly, testing the weight of it, the way you'd test a snow bridge before trusting it. It held. Barely. "He's been dead twenty-two years. Officially: crevasse fall, solo ascent, his own fault. They printed that in the alpine journal. They gave him a small obituary and a line in the accident report that read like a reprimand." A breath. The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, deep in the icefall, a serac groaned — the glacier settling its accounts. "And the whole time, there he is. On tape. Telling the truth into a machine, in a room under a glacier, two years before they killed him for it."

"They didn't kill him for it." Rook's voice was careful. "They killed him for what he did with it. He was the one who carried the first copies out. The original courier. The vault's whole existence — the reason it's sealed, the reason the village trusted it — that chain starts with him."

Tamsin closed their eyes. The blue light through the tarp was the color of the inside of a closed eyelid, the color you see when you press your palms to your face, the color of the body looking inward. Twenty-two years of carrying a father's death like a stone in the chest — *he went up alone, he was reckless, he didn't file a route plan, he was warned* — and the whole time the truth had been that he hadn't gone up alone at all. He'd gone up carrying other people's voices. He'd died the way couriers die.

"I need you to understand something," Tamsin said, and opened their eyes, and looked at Rook straight on, the way you look at a client on a bad pitch — total attention, no softness, because softness gets people killed. "I have spent my entire adult life being the person whose name is on a blacklist. Two countries. I can't get a ferry ticket under my own name. I have been *the problem* so long that I don't remember what it feels like to be a person who is allowed to exist. And I am telling you this not because I want your pity but because I need you to hear what I'm about to say next with all of that in mind."

Rook set down his cup. "Okay."

"When this goes out — when we broadcast this — someone is going to have to stand in front of it. Someone's name has to be on it. Corrigan will see to that; it's the first thing men like him do, they find the person and make the person the story, so the story stops being ninety-one dead villagers and starts being one criminal with a grudge." Tamsin's voice was steady. It had cost them something to make it steady, and Rook, who had spent nine days watching them, could see the cost. "So I've been thinking about who that person is. And I want you to hear my plan before you say anything."

Rook's face did something complicated. Tamsin watched it happen — the flicker of relief, the quick arithmetic, the guilt arriving late and trying to look like generosity — and understood, with a sinking clarity, that Rook had been building toward the same conversation from the other direction.

They spoke at the same time.

"I'll take the blame—"

"I want you to—"

They stopped. The stove hissed. Outside, the glacier ticked and settled, and somewhere far down-valley a wind moved through the pass with a sound like a long exhale.

Rook recovered first, because Rook had always been quicker with words than with silence. "Tamsin. Listen to me. You're the one they're hunting. Your face is on the bounty bulletins in every ranger station from here to the coast. *Survey team killer.* If you're the one standing at the transmitter, Corrigan doesn't even have to work. He just lets them shoot you and calls it justice. But me—" he spread his hands, and Tamsin saw they were shaking, and saw him will them still, "—me, I'm nobody. No record. No blacklist. And I'm *guilty.* That's the whole point. I led Corrigan to that glacier. I took his money. I watched my team die because of what I did. If I stand up and say *my name is Rook Adeyemi, I sold the location, and here is what the state did to bury it* — then the confession and the evidence come from the same mouth. It's unanswerable. Nobody trusts a clean accuser. Everybody believes a guilty man. It's the oldest trick there is."

"It's not a trick, it's a sacrifice."

"It's an *exchange rate.*" Rook leaned forward, and the stove light moved on his face, and for a second Tamsin saw the man he must have been before — the surveyor, the one the others trusted with the instruments, the one whose handwriting in the notebook was neat even under fear. "Think about the math. You walk out with the tapes. You get them to the village, to a real newsroom, to whoever's left that Corrigan doesn't own. You're a witness, not a fugitive — a blacklisted guide who found the truth, that's a *story people love*, that's the kind of story that gets blacklists overturned. And I take the fall. A few years, maybe. I've done the reading; what I did is manslaughter-adjacent at worst, and with the recordings out, no court in the country will want to touch me harshly. I come out the other side and I'm the man who helped fix it. You come out the other side and you're *Tamsin Okonkwo* again, with a name, with a passport, with a—" he stopped, and his voice dropped, "—with your father's name cleared too. Both of you. That's what this buys. That's what it's *for.*"

The cave was very quiet. Tamsin became aware of the sounds a body makes when it is holding itself together: breath, the small creak of a jaw kept from clenching, the pulse in the ears like a second, slower stove.

"That's the plan you built," Tamsin said. "While I was in the vault listening to my dead father. You sat out here and built a plan to erase yourself."

"I've been erased before. It doesn't take." Rook almost smiled. "You'd know. You came back."

"No." Tamsin said it quietly, and then, because quiet wasn't enough, said it again with everything behind it: "*No.*"

Rook blinked.

"You want to know what I learned in there, on the fourth tape, in the first thirty seconds?" Tamsin reached into their jacket and drew out the compass — the brass one, the one with the bearing to the source scratched inside the case in a dead man's hand. They held it between them, in the stove light, and the needle swung and settled and pointed, as it always did, at the glacier. "My father didn't die a martyr. He didn't die a hero. He died a *courier* — and the reason the courier matters, the reason any of this matters, is that he understood the one rule that makes the whole thing work: *nobody carries the truth alone.* You split the load. You rope up. That's why there are ninety-one voices on those tapes and not one. That's why the village recorded for forty years instead of sending one brave man to the capital with a briefcase. Because one brave man with a briefcase is a story that ends. A rope team is a story that *continues.*"

"Tamsin—"

"You are offering to cut yourself off the rope so I can move faster." Tamsin's voice cracked, finally, on the last word, and they let it. "And I am telling you that I have spent twenty-two years being the person everyone else cut loose. Corrigan cut me loose to save his report. The association cut me loose to save its reputation. Two countries cut me loose because it was *convenient.* I know exactly what it does to a person, being the one who gets left on the mountain. I know it from the *inside.* And I will not — do you hear me — I will *not* become the thing that was done to me. Not for a passport. Not for my name. Not even for my father's. There is no version of walking free that's worth watching you walk into a cell so I can have it."

"It's not a cell, it's—"

"It's erasure. That's what you said the state does. That's what the massacre *was* — ninety-one people erased so a road could be built and a report could be clean. And your plan is to feed yourself into the same machine because it's tidy." Tamsin put the compass down between them, on the tarp, needle still swinging. "No."

The silence that followed was long. The stove hissed. Rook looked at his hands, at his ruined knuckles, at the boot he still hadn't put back on, and Tamsin watched him do the thing that honest men do when a plan built on self-destruction collapses: he grieved it. You could see the grief move through him like weather.

"You'd rather hang together," he said finally. "Is that it. Both of us burned. Both of us exiles. That's your *better* math."

"It's not math." Tamsin picked the compass back up. Closed the case. The needle stilled against the glass. "That's the whole point. It was never math. Corrigan does math. The ministry does math. *Ninety-one casualties against a national infrastructure project* — that's math. My father carried tapes through a glacier because he'd stopped doing math." They looked up. "We go out together or we don't go out. We broadcast it together, we take whatever comes together, and if they take us, they take *two* people, and two people make a worse story for them than one. That's not sacrifice, Rook. That's just rope technique. You never cut the man below you loose because the pitch would be easier solo."

Rook was silent a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, broken, startled sound, the laugh of a man whose legs give out at the top of a climb, relief and terror in the same breath. "You're insane. You understand that. I hand you a clean exit and you call it *rope technique.*"

"I hand *you* one, too, and you built a martyrdom out of it. We're both insane. It's why we're still alive."

Rook put his boot back on. His fingers were steadier now, or pretending to be, which on a glacier amounts to the same thing. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the salesman's cadence, down to the bare wire of the man underneath. "Then tell me how. Because if we're doing this together, we do it *right*, and I need to know you have a way that doesn't end with both of us in a ditch."

Tamsin looked out through the gap under the tarp. The east was graying now, the true dawn coming, and the glacier's shoulder above them had begun to catch the first light — a thin line of rose along the seracs, beautiful and indifferent, the mountain wearing its morning the way it wore everything: without opinion.

"The vault has a transmitter," Tamsin said. "Cold War vintage, the kind they built to run for years on a trickle of power. The village kept it maintained — it's how they got recordings *in* without carrying them out. Ilse knew about it. Which means Corrigan knows about it, which means he'll come to kill it." A breath. "So we don't wait for him. We go back up. Tonight, tomorrow — before his column clears the pass. We broadcast from the source itself, on the old frequency and the village's satellite phone both, everything, all ninety-one voices, unedited, my father's tape first. And we do it *live*, with both our names, spoken out loud, on the record, so that whatever they do to us afterward happens in front of the whole world."

"They'll come for us while we're transmitting."

"Yes."

"It'll take hours to push it all out on that old rig."

"Yes."

Rook looked at the glacier light climbing the wall of the cave, and then at Tamsin, and something in his face finished settling — the last of the salesman gone, the last of the plan to die alone gone with it, down to something simpler and harder and finally trustworthy. He held out his hand, palm open, the way climbers do.

"Together, then," he said. "Both names. Both voices. Nobody cut loose."

Tamsin took the hand and gripped it, and the grip said the rest.

Then, outside the tarp, down-valley, where the pass opened onto the long descent: a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. The unmistakable cadence of an engine — not one, several — grinding up through the frozen morning, still miles off, still small, but coming.

Rook's head turned. His grip on Tamsin's hand tightened.

"They're early," he said.
