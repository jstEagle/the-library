## 18. Corrigan at the Source

The glacier told them before any radio did.

Tamsin felt it first in the soles of their boots — a tremor too deep to be wind, too rhythmic to be settling ice. Then Rook's head came up from the transmitter like a deer scenting dogs, and a moment later the sound arrived across the ice plain: engines. Two of them, maybe three, grinding up the moraine track that no vehicle was supposed to be able to climb, that Tamsin had told the survey team was impassable, that Corrigan had evidently decided was merely difficult.

"Back door," Rook said, already moving, gathering the testimony canisters into the canvas rucksack they'd carried up from the vault. His hands were steady now. Whatever he'd been in the valley below — the liar, the seller of locations, the man who'd led wolves to his own pack — the ice had burned that out of him. What was left was simple and fast.

"No," Tamsin said. "The back door is a two-day descent. They'd have men on it by now. Corrigan doesn't leave doors open."

Rook stopped. The light coming off the glacier was that terrible late-morning white, the kind that flattened everything, made the whole world look like paper waiting to be written on. Tamsin had hated that light their whole life. It was the light of the day their father's rope came back up empty.

"Then we run for the pass," Rook said.

"And carry forty years of voices over scree at altitude while helicopters—" Tamsin stopped. Listened. The engines had gone quiet. That was worse. Engines meant approach. Silence meant arrival.

They came around the shoulder of the serac field on foot, because Tamsin wanted to see with their own eyes before deciding anything, and seeing had always been the one thing they were good at. Two snowcats, parked in the lee of the moraine with their engines ticking as they cooled. Six men in matching grey, fanning out with the unhurried competence of people who had done this in other valleys, other countries, and never once been named for it.

And between them, hands zip-tied in front, hair full of frost, walking with her chin up like she was leading the party —

Ilse.

* * *

They had talked about this, in the vault, in the blue dark with the testimony playing soft around them. Tamsin had said: if they take Ilse, they'll use her. Rook had said: she knew that when she turned her radio on. Tamsin had said: that doesn't make it easier. Rook had said, with the flat honesty of a man who had no credit left to spend: nothing about you is easy, Okonkwo. That's the only reason I trust you.

Now Tamsin crouched behind a pressure ridge with the wind pulling at their hood and watched Corrigan walk out onto the ice.

He had aged since Halden's Rest. Or perhaps Tamsin had simply never looked at him properly before — had kept him, all these years, as a shape in a doorway, a voice on a phone line, a signature at the bottom of a blacklist. In the flesh he was smaller. A compact man in a government-issue parka with the insignia removed, grey at the temples, moving across the glacier with the careful steps of someone who had been briefed on crevasse danger and did not enjoy it. He stopped thirty meters out, in the open, alone. The six men held back near the snowcats, weapons slung but visible.

"Tamsin Okonkwo." His voice carried strangely on the ice, the way voices do at altitude — clear and close and wrong. "I'd like to talk."

"You brought six rifles to a conversation."

"I brought six rifles so we wouldn't need them." He spread his hands. "I need you to understand something before anything else. Ilse Varga is alive because I want this conversation. She stays alive the same way. I'm not a monster, Okonkwo. I'm an accountant of sorts. I balance things."

Tamsin's father had said something like that once, about the mining company. *They're not evil, they're arithmetic.* It had not comforted him in the end.

"Let her go."

"When we're finished." Corrigan shifted his weight, and Tamsin saw him glance down at the ice, testing it, and something in Tamsin's chest went cold and old and familiar. He was afraid of the glacier. He had come anyway. That was worth knowing about a man.

"I'll make this simple," Corrigan called. "The same offer I made in Halden's Rest, improved. Full reinstatement. Both countries. Your record expunged — not sealed, *expunged*, the Karveth disaster reclassified as what it was, a chain-of-command failure, with you named in the inquiry as the guide who did everything right. Compensation for eight years. And a quiet life afterward, if you want it. A permit. A name people can say without flinching."

The words went into Tamsin like water into a crack, finding every fault line, widening them. Eight years. Eight years of doors closing, of the Halden's Rest job board going quiet when they approached, of other guides crossing the street, of their mother's voice on the phone getting smaller every year as the shame worked its way down the family like frost heave. *Expunged.* He had chosen the word deliberately. He had done his homework. He had always done his homework — that was the horror of Corrigan, that he was not lazy, not cruel in the cheap way, but thorough, the way ice is thorough.

"You killed five people," Tamsin said. "The survey team. You killed them and you wrote my name on the wall so no one would look at yours."

"I balanced a situation." No flinch. He had rehearsed this too. "Those five were going to start a fire that would burn a great many people who had nothing to do with any of it. Villages. Careers. A country's whole idea of itself. I made a decision that the people above me were grateful for and that I have carried alone ever since. You think I don't know what I am, Okonkwo? I know exactly what I am. The question is whether you do."

"And what am I?"

"A person who lost everything because of one bad night on one bad mountain." Corrigan took a step closer. The six men didn't move. The wind moved for all of them. "And I am offering to give it back. Every bit of it. All you have to do is hand over what's in that vault and walk down with me, and in a year this glacier will be a story nobody tells."

Behind the pressure ridge, Rook made a small sound. Not a word. Tamsin knew what it was anyway. It was the sound of a man watching someone else being offered the thing he'd sold his soul for, at a better price.

* * *

Tamsin stood up.

It was not bravery, or not only. It was that there was a version of this conversation that could only be had face to face, and Tamsin had spent eight years having it in absentia — through lawyers' letters, through the blacklist ledger, through the silent phone. Corrigan had built his architecture on the assumption that Tamsin Okonkwo would always negotiate from hiding. So Tamsin walked out into the open, boots crunching on the wind-carved snow, and stopped ten meters from him, close enough to see the frost in his eyebrows, far enough that the six rifles would have to choose their shots.

Corrigan's eyes flicked — just once — to the vault's direction, then back. "You went in."

"I went in."

"Then you know it's not survey data." His voice dropped, and the wind nearly took it, and he had to raise it again, and Tamsin watched him hate that. "You know what's down there. Recordings. Testimony. Old voices. You know that half of it is unverifiable and the other half is grief with a microphone held in front of it."

"It's my father's voice," Tamsin said.

For the first time, something moved behind Corrigan's face. Not guilt. Calculation losing its footing, briefly, on unfamiliar ground. "I know," he said. "I've known since I pulled the first file. Why do you think you were the guide I hired for the original approach, eight years ago? Why do you think the Karveth disaster was allowed to bury you instead of burying me?" He shook his head slowly. "You were never collateral, Okonkwo. You were the contingency. A blacklisted guide with a personal grievance and a compass that points at the source — you were my insurance that no one else would ever get there. You've been working for me for eight years without knowing it. Every job you turned down kept the valley empty. Every bridge you burned kept the maps quiet."

The glacier creaked beneath them, a long groan like a ship changing its mind.

Tamsin had imagined this moment, in the bad years, in the way that people imagine confrontations they'll never have. In the imagination, Corrigan was a wall and Tamsin was weather. In the flesh he was a tired man standing on ice he didn't trust, offering to buy a voice that had already been playing for three days on a transmitter older than his ministry, streaming out through a satellite phone powered by a solar panel two villagers had carried up the mountain on their backs.

The broadcast had started at dawn.

Corrigan didn't know. Or — Tamsin watched his eyes, watched the way they kept ticking toward the snowcats, toward the radio antenna visible there — he suspected. That was why he was here in person, why he'd risked the glacier, why Ilse was still breathing. You didn't fly a deputy minister up a mountain for records. You flew him up a mountain for a leak.

"You're stalling," Tamsin said. "You're waiting to hear whether the signal is still up."

"And you're stalling," Corrigan said, "because she's still standing." He turned his head and raised his voice. "Ilse. Tell them what you told me."

Ilse had been standing in the grey men's custody like a folded tent, patient, waiting to be pitched. Now she lifted her head. Her lips were cracked with cold and there was blood on her lower lip and her eyes found Tamsin's with no apology in them at all — which was, Tamsin understood suddenly, its own kind of respect. She had never apologized. She had never pretended. She had put a radio on her back and walked into Corrigan's net with her eyes open, and whatever she was about to say, she had chosen it.

"He knows about the village," Ilse said. Her voice was hoarse but it carried. "The valley that isn't on the maps. His plan was always to blame the massacre records on them — fabricate a separatist archive, call the testimony propaganda, and clear the valley. Every one of them. That was the operation. The survey team wasn't the mission, Tamsin. The village was. The team just found the proof too early."

"You're lying," Corrigan said, without heat. "She's a compromised officer reciting her captors' narrative—"

"Am I?" Ilse looked at him. "Then melt the vault and leave the village alone. Say it. Say it in front of the guide whose father is down there in the ice."

The wind came down off the col in a long white gust, and in it Tamsin heard — or imagined, or did not imagine — the faint thread of a voice. The transmitter's range was short but the relay the villagers had rigged on the ridge was not, and somewhere down in the world, in kitchens and workshops and a hundred small rooms, people were listening to the dead speak. Tamsin's father had been dead for thirty-one years and had more listeners this morning than he'd had in his whole life.

"Last chance," Corrigan said quietly. "I am not threatening you, Okonkwo. I am describing a future. There is a version of tomorrow where you are Tamsin Okonkwo, licensed guide, and this mountain is closed for seismic reasons, and Ilse Varga goes home to her family, and the ledger is clean. And there is a version where you are the survey killer, formally charged by nightfall, and this glacier comes down on its own schedule, and no one is left to say otherwise. I don't choose between them. You do. That's the whole of my authority here — I choose nothing. I only ever balance."

*You choose nothing.* Tamsin thought of the ledger in Halden's Rest, the neat columns of a life priced out. Thought of Rook in the cave saying *I took the money because I thought the money was the smallest thing I could lose.* Thought of Ilse's radio, transmitting, transmitting, transmitting — the leash she'd turned into a lifeline. Thought of the vault door, older than the state, and the state's whole genius: it did not break things like that door. It buried them. Twice if necessary. It waited for people to grow tired, and tiredness was the only ally it had ever needed.

Eight years ago, on the mountain that broke them, Tamsin had made the decision that defined them: they had gone back up. Everyone said don't. The storm said don't. The arithmetic — one guide, three clients already lost, odds that no ledger would balance — said don't. They went back up anyway, and they failed, and the failure was named after them for the rest of their life. But the going back was never the crime. Tamsin had known that for eight years and had never once been able to say it out loud where it counted.

"You're right about one thing," Tamsin said. "I did work for you for eight years. Every silence. Every door I didn't open. You bought all of it at the price of my name, and I paid, because I thought the name was the biggest thing I had." Tamsin took a breath, and the cold went all the way down, clean as water. "But I've got forty years of voices in a bag on that ridge, and a transmitter that's been on since dawn, and a village full of people you planned to erase, and a compass that doesn't point where your maps say it should. You can't balance this, Corrigan. It's already spent."

Corrigan looked at them for a long moment. And then — this was the thing Tamsin would remember, the thing that would come back on other mountains, other nights — he nodded, slowly, almost respectfully, the way a man nods at a river that has risen past his marks.

"I hoped you'd say that," he said. "Because the second version was always funded better."

He turned and walked back toward the snowcats, and the six grey men began to un-sling their rifles, and behind Tamsin, up on the ridge, the transmitter's carrier wave hummed on — and beneath everyone's feet, patient as a ledger of its own, the glacier began to answer the drilling charges Corrigan's men had set in the night, in the dark, at the vault's foundations.

The ice under Tamsin's boots shuddered once, deep, like a held breath about to break.

"Rook!" Tamsin shouted, already running. "Get to the vault — we're out of time to be careful!"
