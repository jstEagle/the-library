## 19. Broadcast From the Ice

The vault breathed cold.

Not the ordinary cold of altitude — Tamsin had lived inside that cold for years, worn it like a second coat — but something older, a stillness that came off the ice walls in slow waves, as though the glacier had been holding its breath for decades and only now, with the steel door breached and the crevasse route behind them sealed by their own careful hands, had begun to exhale.

Inside, the chamber was smaller than Tamsin had imagined. The survey team's floodlights still stood where they'd left them, tripods planted in blue ice, casting long shadows across shelves of equipment that predated every government Tamsin had ever cursed. A transmitter sat on a table of packed snow and timber — a heavy analog beast, dials gone amber with age, its casing stamped with a serial number from a country that no longer existed on maps. Beside it, coiled like sleeping snakes, cables ran up through a shaft in the ceiling toward the antenna the survey team had never gotten to raise.

And everywhere, everywhere, were the tapes.

Reels of magnetic tape in rusted canisters. Cassettes in plastic bags. Miniature digital recorders, their batteries long dead. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Labeled in handwriting that changed across the decades — different hands, different inks, different alphabets sometimes — each canister marked with a date and a name and, on some, a single word in the old village script that Rook had translated for Tamsin on the climb up: *witness*.

"They recorded everything," Rook said quietly. His voice did strange things in the vault, coming back flattened and close, as if the ice refused to let sound travel far. "Every survivor who could make the walk. Every grandmother who remembered. They carried these up here for sixty years because no one downstream would listen."

Tamsin ran a gloved finger along one canister. The label read *Okonkwo, Adaeze — 1974*. Their father's mother. A woman Tamsin had known only as a photograph on a shelf, a woman who died before Tamsin learned to walk, whose name had been a closed door in their childhood home.

Their hand shook. They let it.

"We have maybe an hour," Tamsin said, and was grateful their voice came out level. "Maybe less. Corrigan's trackers were two ridges back at dawn. Ilse bought us time, but time is all she bought."

Rook was already at the transmitter, pulling panels, tracing wires with the competence of a man who had spent his life making machines lie about what they knew. "The unit's dry-cell powered. If the capacitors haven't leaked—" He stopped. Swore softly. "They haven't. Someone maintained this place. Someone came up here every year, all these years, and kept it ready."

"The village," Tamsin said. "The ones who sheltered us. Old Sefa said her grandfather built this room. She said they always believed someone would come who'd finally send it."

"Then let's not disappoint sixty years of grandfathers."

* * *

Getting the signal out took forty minutes and both of their hands.

The transmitter alone wouldn't reach past the valley walls — Karveth's granite ate radio waves the way the river ate stone, patiently and completely. But Rook had carried the village's satellite phone wrapped against his chest since the hidden valley, and when he spliced its output into the transmitter's antenna feed, muttering apologies to engineering gods he clearly didn't believe in, the phone's signal strength indicator climbed from nothing to a single trembling bar.

"One bar," Rook said. "That's enough for audio. Not video. Not much bandwidth at all."

"Audio is what we have." Tamsin knelt among the canisters, sorting. "We play the tapes. We stream them raw. No commentary, no editing, no framing anyone can dispute. Let them hear the voices."

"And yours?"

Tamsin looked up. In the floodlight, Rook's face was hollowed and frost-burned, his split lip crusted dark, and there was something in his expression Tamsin hadn't seen before — not suspicion, not guilt, but a kind of fierce, frightened hope, like a man watching a rope being tied that would hold or wouldn't.

"Mine too," Tamsin said. "At the end. Names, dates, what we found, what Corrigan tried to buy. Everything I know about how the survey team died and why. If I'm going to be erased for this, I want to be erased loudly."

"You might not be erased. You might just—"

"Rook." Tamsin stood, and the vault seemed to lean in around them. "I spent nine years being quiet. Quiet didn't save me, and it didn't save your team, and it didn't save any of the people on these tapes. We're done being quiet."

He held their gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded once, the way climbers nod before committing to a pitch, and turned back to the machine.

* * *

The first voice came through at 11:47 by Tamsin's watch.

It was an old woman's voice, thin and reedy, warped by decades inside a canister, speaking in the village dialect before shifting into accented state-language so the recording would be understood beyond her own people. She gave her name. Her village. The year — 1963, eleven years before Tamsin's father was born. And then she described, in a voice that never rose and never broke, what soldiers had done at the river crossing in the winter of that year: the village burned, the men taken to the ice fields, the official report that called it an avalanche.

She described how she had walked three days through the pass to bring her testimony here, to the vault under the source, because the glacier kept what men would not.

In Halden's Rest, in the capital, in whatever rooms the stream reached — and Rook had routed it through three relays and a journalist's drop box Ilse had given them the address for in a note sewn into her jacket collar, a note that had finally convinced Tamsin she had meant every word — people were hearing a dead woman speak.

Tamsin fed tape after tape onto the reels. Each one, they announced aloud first: name, date, contents. Their voice steady, their hands not. Rook monitored the signal bar, the upload buffer, the little green light that meant somewhere far away, servers were catching the voices and scattering them beyond any minister's reach.

An hour in, the tapes were still playing and the light was still green, and Tamsin allowed themselves four seconds of believing it might work.

Then the ice above them rang like a struck bell.

* * *

Rook heard it first — of course he did; he'd grown up reading mountains the way other men read letters. He killed the floodlights with a slap of his palm and the vault went dark except for the transmitter's amber dials and the faint blue glow of the satellite phone.

"How many?" Tamsin whispered.

"Hard to say. That was a charge. Small one — breaking trail ice, not bringing anything down. They're not trying to bury us yet." His breath ghosted in the dark. "Yet."

"They'll find the crevasse route."

"It took us six hours. It'll take them longer, and they don't know it exists — unless Ilse told them." A pause, heavy. "She wouldn't."

"She turned her radio on for us," Tamsin said. "I know. But Corrigan has methods I don't want to imagine, and she's been in his hands for two days."

Another detonation, farther off, muffled by distance and depth. Then, faintly, carried down the shaft that had once been meant for an antenna: voices. Boots on ice. The clipped cadence of men following orders they didn't fully understand.

Tamsin made the calculation the way they made all calculations on a mountain — fast, unsentimental, final. "We keep broadcasting. If they come down the shaft, they come down into a live transmission. Every word they shout goes out with the testimony. Corrigan can't shoot us on air."

"He can collapse the entrance."

"He needs the tapes destroyed more than he needs us dead, and the tapes are already out. Every minute we transmit, destruction gets more pointless and murder gets more expensive." Tamsin's eyes had adjusted enough to find the canisters again. "Keep the reel turning. I'll take the door."

"Tamsin—"

"I'm not going to fight anyone, Rook. I'm going to stand in the doorway and be witnessed." They almost smiled. "It's the one thing I've ever been qualified for."

* * *

The next hour passed in fragments.

A man's voice on tape, 1979, describing the mass grave beneath the old ferry landing. A girl, sixteen years old, recording in 1988, her testimony interrupted halfway by what sounded like dogs and running feet, resuming three days later from a different room, her voice harder. Tamsin's great-aunt, 1991, naming officers. Naming Corrigan's predecessor. Naming, in a flat and terrible aside, a young deputy named Corrigan himself, newly posted, who had signed the transport manifests.

Outside, the sounds grew closer. Shouting now, echoing down the shaft. A generator coughing to life somewhere above — they were rigging lights at the original door, the steel one older than the state, the one the survey team had drilled toward and died beside.

Tamsin stood at the breach with an ice axe they hoped not to use and listened to their family's ghosts pour out of the mountain into the ears of the world.

At 13:22, the satellite phone chimed.

Rook snatched it up. Listened. His face went through three expressions in two seconds — disbelief, recognition, and something that broke Tamsin's heart a little, because it looked like relief.

"It's Ilse," he said. "Live. She's — Tamsin, she escaped. She's calling in on the journalist's relay. She says Corrigan's column hit the upper moraine twenty minutes ago. Twelve armed. He's with them personally." A pause. "She says he knows about the crevasse route. She says he made her draw it."

The cold in the vault seemed to deepen. "Did she give him the real route?"

"She says yes. She says she's sorry. She says—" Rook's jaw tightened. "She says he doesn't need the route anymore anyway. Because he's not coming for the tapes."

Tamsin turned that over and felt the floor of the world tilt. "Then what?"

Rook looked up from the phone, and the amber dial-light caught the fear in his eyes. "He's coming for the glacier. She says the drilling charges are already on the moraine. He's going to bring the whole ice field down on this chamber, seal the vault forever, and call it a natural calving event. With us inside it, if we're still inside it." His voice dropped. "She says we have maybe fifteen minutes before they set the first charge."

Above them, faint and rhythmic, came the sound of drills biting into ancient ice.

* * *

What happened next, Tamsin would remember afterward in pieces, the way survivors remember avalanches — not as continuous time but as a sequence of decisions, each one made in a silence louder than the noise around it.

They could run. Six hours back through the crevasse route, which Corrigan now knew, which might already have men in it. Or up the shaft, into twelve guns. Or nowhere, and die under a hundred thousand tons of moving ice alongside sixty years of testimony that was, by then, safely scattered across servers no minister could reach.

Or they could finish.

"There are nineteen tapes left," Tamsin said. "Nineteen voices that waited decades in the dark. We're not leaving any of them in here."

Rook stared at them. Outside, the drills screamed. Somewhere below the world, ministers were reaching for phones, and the story was already loose, already breeding, already beyond anyone's power to smother — and none of that mattered to the two people standing in the vault, because what mattered had never been the outcome. What mattered was that no one who had climbed this mountain to speak would be silenced by the sound of boots.

"Nineteen tapes," Rook said. "About forty minutes."

"Then we'd better not waste breath."

They worked side by side in the failing light, feeding reels, announcing names, and Tamsin spoke their own testimony into the transmitter's microphone while the mountain groaned overhead — their name, their blacklist, the disaster at Serac Col that Corrigan had manufactured to bury them, the offers, the threats, the notebook, the bodies in the river, the compass bearing inherited from a dead father that had led them here like a thread through nine years of wilderness. They said all of it plainly, without poetry, because the truth needed no ornament. It only needed witnesses.

On the last tape, a child's voice. Recorded in 2001. A boy describing his grandfather's stories of the massacre, and ending with words that stopped Tamsin's hands on the reels:

*"My grandfather says the glacier remembers even when everyone else forgets. He says someday the ice will give the memory back. I am putting my voice in the ice so that when it does, I will be part of what comes out."*

The reel ran empty. The transmitter hissed into static.

And then, from the shaft above, a new sound: not drills, not boots. A voice, amplified, bouncing down the ice throat with terrible clarity, familiar as a scar.

"Okonkwo." Corrigan. Even distorted by echo, the voice carried the same reasonable warmth it had in the Halden's Rest hotel room, the same patient menace. "I've heard your broadcast. All of it. Very moving. Very futile — do you imagine a stream changes anything? I've survived worse than a dead woman's tape." A pause, and when he spoke again the warmth was gone entirely. "You have ten minutes to come up that shaft with your hands visible. After that, I stop negotiating with the mountain and start demolishing it, and you can explain your principles to the archaeologists of the next century."

Tamsin looked at Rook. Rook looked at Tamsin. The satellite phone's green light still glowed — still transmitting, still carrying every word of the ultimatum to whoever was listening, and Tamsin understood suddenly that Corrigan knew it too, and didn't care, and that was the most frightening thing of all: a man who had stopped caring who heard him.

They keyed the microphone one last time.

"For the record," Tamsin said, voice carrying up the shaft and out into the world together, "the Deputy Minister of the Interior is currently threatening to destroy a national glacier and murder two witnesses. My name is Tamsin Okonkwo. I am at the source of the Karveth. And I am not coming up."

From above: a sigh, almost regretful. Then, in a tone of brisk administration: "Set the first charge."

The explosion, when it came, was not loud. It was deep — a concussion felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the soles of the boots, as though the mountain itself had been struck below the waterline. Dust sifted from the vault ceiling. The amber dials flickered. Somewhere far above, a fracture opened in the ice field with a sound like the world's largest door beginning, very slowly, to open.

Rook grabbed Tamsin's arm. "The crevasse route. Now. If the whole face lets go—"

But Tamsin was looking at the vault wall, at the shelves of canisters, at sixty years of patience packed in rusted tin — and doing arithmetic no guide should ever have to do. Nineteen tapes had gone out. There were hundreds more. An entire people's memory, sealed in a chamber about to become a tomb.

Unless.

"The lower gallery," Tamsin said. "Sefa told me. There's a second chamber, deeper, under the source pool — her grandfather dug it as a backup. If the upper vault collapses, the lower one holds." They were already moving, hauling canisters by the armful, arms burning, lungs full of ice dust. "Help me carry. We have minutes, not ten of them."

Rook didn't argue. Together they loaded sacks, packs, jackets buttoned around tape canisters, working in the strobing dark while the glacier above them cracked and boomed like artillery, and the last thing Tamsin registered before the second charge went off — closer now, much closer, close enough to feel the ice shift beneath their feet like a living thing deciding to move — was the transmitter's green light, still glowing, still sending, faithful to the end.

Then the ceiling came down, and the world went white.
