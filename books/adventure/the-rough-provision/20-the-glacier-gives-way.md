## 20. The Glacier Gives Way

The broadcast ended at 4:17 in the morning, and the mountain began to die at 4:19.

I remember the order of things because I have spent my whole life learning the order of things — which sound precedes which failure, which tremor in the snow announces which collapse. It is the only education I ever trusted. And so when the first crack came, a deep arterial groan rolling up from beneath the glacier's belly like something ancient turning over in its sleep, I was already moving.

"Rook." I grabbed his arm where he sat slumped against the transmitter housing, wrung out and hollow-eyed from three hours of feeding testimony into the old military rig while the village's satellite phone relayed it to a world that had, until tonight, been allowed to forget. "Rook, we're done. We're done, and we need to be gone."

He looked at me the way men look at you when they've given away everything they were carrying and haven't yet learned how to stand upright without it. His lips were blue-tinged. The vault's interior light — that strange, sourceless blue glow that seeped through the ice itself, refracted through sixty years of frozen strata — made his face look drowned.

"They heard it," he said. Not a question. Wondering.

"The whole valley heard it. Half the country heard it, if Ilse's frequencies held." I hauled him to his feet. "Which means Corrigan is out of time, and desperate men with drills are worse than avalanches."

Because we had heard them too. For the last hour, faint through the ice like a heartbeat in another room: machinery. Generators. The high whine of thermal lances biting into glacier ice two hundred meters above us, where the original door stood — the steel door older than the state, the one the survey team had found and died for finding. Corrigan's men had come down the north col in the dark, and they were not coming to negotiate.

The vault's mouth gaped behind us, that crevasse route I'd threaded blind years ago on a smuggler's run, the one no map carried because no licensed guide would ever admit to knowing it. Inside, the shelves of recorded testimony stretched back into the blue dark — hundreds of reels, cassettes, memory cards wrapped in waxed cloth, decades of voices telling what happened at Karveth in the winter of the massacre. My father's voice among them. I had listened to eleven seconds of it before my knees gave out, and Rook had knelt beside me in the ice and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for me.

We could not carry it all. We had copied what the transmitter could send; the village would hide the rest. That was the bargain we'd struck with Marta and her people, and bargains were the only currency left to us now.

"Up and out," I said. "Same route, reversed. You go first."

"No." He said it flatly, no argument in it, just fact. "You first. If it comes down while I'm climbing, you can still get word to the village. If it comes down while you're climbing—" He shrugged. "I've already sold this mountain once. I'm not going to be the reason it keeps you."

"Rook—"

"Tamsin." My name in his mouth, and underneath it all the weight of everything he'd confessed at Camp Four, kneeling in the snow like a man emptying his pockets before a firing squad. *I led Corrigan here. I took the money. They died because of me.* "Please."

There was no time to fight about it. The glacier groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere above us a section of serac let go with a report like artillery. Fine ice-dust sifted down through the blue light, glittering, almost beautiful, the way disasters always are right up until they aren't.

I went first.

* * *

The crevasse route was a throat of ice, near-vertical in places, the walls close enough that my shoulders brushed both sides. My father's compass hung at my neck inside my jacket, and I could feel it there like a second heartbeat — the same compass he'd carried into the Karveth foothills the winter they killed him for asking questions, the same bearings inked in the dead surveyor's notebook. Everything in my life had been walking toward this ice. Now the ice wanted it back.

Rook climbed below me, breathing hard, calling holds up to me in guide's shorthand — *good edge, rotten lip, trust the left* — and there was something unbearable in it, that the man who had betrayed everyone should climb so honestly, giving me the truth of every hold the way he should have given the survey team the truth of Corrigan. Redemption is not a grand gesture. I understood then that it is calling holds correctly in the dark, one at a time, long after anyone would blame you for stopping.

Two-thirds up, the world turned white and sideways.

Not a storm. A concussion. The thermal lance above must have breached some hollow chamber in the glacier's heart, because the whole mass of the Karveth source shuddered like a struck bell, and the crevasse walls flexed — actually flexed, ice bending the way steel bends before it fails — and the route beneath Rook simply ceased.

I heard it happen more than saw it: the wet, grinding exhalation of a pitch collapsing, the clatter of his axe striking away down into the blue, and then his voice, thin and very far below.

"Tamsin!"

I anchored fast — two screws, hands shaking so badly the first one went in crooked and I had to back it out and start again, and every second cost us both. Below me, the blue glow showed him wedged on a shelf of ice no wider than a coffin lid, one arm hooked through a rope loop he'd clipped to a stub of old piton, rusted, pre-war, holding by faith alone.

"I'm here!" My voice cracked off the walls. "I'm here. Don't move. Rook, look at me — don't move."

"I'm not planning a dance." But his laugh came out broken in the middle.

The glacier was dying around us in stages now, each groan deeper than the last. Meltwater had begun to move somewhere far below — I could hear it, that rising whisper that every mountain guide learns to fear more than rockfall, the sound of the glacier's blood remembering it is water. When the vault's structure failed, everything in this throat would flush downward and out, and anything still inside it would go too.

I rappelled to him. There was no plan beyond that. Sometimes there is no plan; there is only the next meter of rope.

His hands were shaking too badly to work the piton free. I got my knife under the rope loop and cut it rather than unclip it, pulled his arm over my shoulders, and started up with him half-climbing, half-hanging off me, his boots scrabbling for edges his exhausted eyes couldn't find. He was heavier than he looked. Everyone is heavier when they are trusting you completely.

"Leave me," he said once, quietly, against my ear.

"Not a chance," I said. "I've buried enough people in mountains. I'm not burying you in one that owes you nothing."

Above us, the last pitch. Above that, the night sky, shockingly clear, stars scattered over the col like spilled salt. And across the glacier's surface, silhouetted against the burning lights of Corrigan's camp — figures running. Because even from outside, even the fools with the lances could feel the mountain letting go beneath them.

We came out of the crevasse mouth into air that tasted of iron and cold, and I dragged Rook the last body-length onto the surface, and we lay there gasping like landed fish while the Karveth Glacier died around us.

* * *

It did not die quietly.

The first sign was the door. Even at this distance I could see it — or see the lights around it, Corrigan's floodlamps blazing at the glacier face where the steel door stood embedded in ice older than any border, any ministry, any lie. Men were moving equipment toward it. I saw, absurdly, a shaped charge being carried forward on a litter like a wounded comrade.

Corrigan stood apart from them. Even at four hundred meters, in starlight, I knew his silhouette the way you know a scar — the set of the shoulders, the impatient angle of the head. The man who had built my blacklist with a signature and a phone call. The man who had offered me my name back twice, as if a name were a coat he could hand across a table.

"He's going to blow it," Rook rasped. "The recordings are already out, but the originals — the physical archive, the village's proof — he'll take the whole face down rather than leave evidence."

"He doesn't understand," I said. "He thinks the glacier is a building. A wall. Something you put a hole in."

The glacier was not a wall. The glacier was a living thing under mortal stress, its meltwater veins swollen with a summer's warmth and now with the heat of his own machines, and when the charge went, it would not cut a neat doorway. It would ask the ice a question, and the ice would answer with everything it had been holding.

I was already moving, dragging Rook downslope toward the moraine ridge — the high ground, the only ground — shouting at him to get his feet under him, run, don't look, *run*. Behind us, a voice amplified by a megaphone barked orders I couldn't parse. Then Corrigan's voice, cutting through, closer than the others, furious:

"Okonkwo! You're under arrest! Stop where you are!"

I stopped. God help me, some blacklisted reflex in my spine still obeyed that voice after six years. I turned.

He was coming down toward us alone, away from his men and the charge, pistol drawn but pointed at the ice, shouting across the slope. "The broadcast changes nothing! Do you hear me? It's noise! Noise gets corrected! Give me the village's copies and every name on your side of this walks — *everyone*, even the girl with the radio, even Adeyemi—"

"You'll melt it," I shouted back. "Ilse told us. You'll melt the vault and burn the village's testimony and call it an accident with their names on it."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt. Recalculation. And then, over his shoulder, muffled by distance and wind, one of his men called out — *"Minister, the charge is set, do we have your—"* — and Corrigan raised his hand, and hesitated.

That hesitation was the mountain's opening.

The glacier answered the question early.

There was no single crack this time. There was a chord — a vast, harmonized tearing, as if the entire Karveth source had drawn one breath six hundred years long and finally let it out. The floodlamps at the face flickered, steadied, and went out. In the sudden starlight I saw the ice shelf beneath the steel door sag, tilt, and begin to slide, carrying the door and the drilling rig and two of Corrigan's men and forty meters of glacier face down into the darkness where the meltwater waited.

"DOWN!" I screamed — at Rook, at Corrigan, at the world — and threw myself flat as the pressure wave hit, a wall of displaced air and ice-powder that scoured the slope like a sandblaster made of winter.

The world became white roar. I lost Rook's hand. I lost the sky. I lost, for a span I cannot measure, myself — tumbling across sastrugi, breath driven out of me, the compass swinging wild on its cord and cracking against my teeth. Somewhere in the chaos a secondary collapse thundered, and then another, the glacier settling into its new shape with the terrible satisfaction of a thing finally allowed to fall.

Then silence. The ringing, cotton-wrapped silence that follows the biggest sound you have ever heard.

I dug myself out of knee-deep ice-dust, spitting, ears screaming. "Rook!" Nothing. "ROOK!"

A hand broke the surface of the drift ten meters below me, waving. I plunged toward it, dug with bare hands, and hauled him out coughing, snow-packed, alive — alive, alive, I pressed my forehead against his frozen hood and said it aloud like a prayer I didn't believe in. "Alive. You're alive."

"The door," he managed. "Tamsin. The door—"

I looked up at the glacier face.

It was gone. Not damaged — gone. Where the steel door had stood embedded in ice older than the state, there was now a raw amphitheater of blue fracture, weeping meltwater in a dozen silver threads that gathered, quickened, and became a torrent racing down the valley floor — the river reborn, violent, uncontrolled, carrying the vault's outer chambers away in house-sized blocks. The original door itself I caught for one instant, tilting in the flood like a headstone uprooted, starlight sliding across its corroded face — and then the water took it, rolled it, and swallowed it whole, and the river that had kept the secret for sixty years carried the last of its architecture down toward the lowlands and the sea.

Corrigan.

I scanned the debris field with a heart gone strangely quiet. His men — the survivors — were clustering far upslope near their wrecked camp, one of them firing a flare that painted the destruction red, red, fading. Of the Minister himself there was no sign. No silhouette shouting orders. No voice.

Only, at the edge of the flood's new channel, a single glove, black and official, half-buried in slush.

Rook followed my gaze. "Tamsin." His voice was careful. "The water goes down to the gorge. Nothing—"

"I know what the water does." I had spent my career learning what water does. "I know."

And I felt — I will write this plainly, because Vesper taught me that plainness is the hardest honesty — I felt no triumph. Only a vast, cold emptiness where vindication was supposed to go, and the understanding settling into me like sediment: the mountain had not chosen justice. Mountains don't choose. It had simply failed, the way everything fails eventually, and Corrigan had been standing on it when it did. The state that built him would call it a tragedy. The village would call it enough. I didn't yet know what I would call it, and maybe I never would.

What I knew was this: the records were safe. Copied, scattered, spoken aloud into a thousand receivers while the man who wanted them burned stood close enough to touch the silence afterward. My father's voice was out in the world now, beyond any minister's reach, beyond fire and flood. Whatever else the night had taken, it could not take that back.

Rook gripped my shoulder. His fingers were shaking. So were mine. Together we watched the flood rage down the valley, carrying winter itself toward the plains, and above us the survivors' flares rose and faded, rose and faded, small red hearts beating against the stars.

Then, downstream, where the new torrent rounded the moraine — a light. Moving. Deliberate. A headlamp, bobbing along the flood line, climbing toward us through the wreckage with the sure, economical stride of someone who knew this terrain.

Rook saw it too. He went rigid. "Nobody could walk that bank," he whispered. "Nobody who didn't know the way."

The light swung up, found our faces, and held.

"Tamsin," Rook breathed. "Is that—"

I knew that stride. I had watched it walk away from me once, down a corridor in Halden's Rest, handcuffed and composed, buying us minutes with her capture. I had told myself I would never see it again.

"Ilse," I said.

The light stopped ten meters away. Behind it, out of the steam of the dying glacier, a voice — hoarse, water-soaked, unmistakably hers — carried across the ruin:

"You'd better both be worth rescuing. Because we have about twenty minutes before Corrigan's men regroup, and I know where the villagers are hiding the copies — and so does the one man who just walked out of that flood."
