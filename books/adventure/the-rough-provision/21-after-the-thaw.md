## 21. After the Thaw

The recordings went public on a Tuesday, which felt wrong to me. Something that large should have arrived like weather — a front you could see coming for days, pressure dropping in your teeth. Instead it came the way most true things come: sideways, unglamorous, at four in the morning when half the country was asleep and the other half was too tired to care.

I was in Halden's Rest when it broke. Back in the same border town where Tamsin had first taken my money and my silence, sitting in the back booth of the Salt Larder with a cup of tea I'd been nursing for three hours because buying a second one would have meant admitting how little I had left. The owner had stopped looking at me weeks ago, which in Halden's Rest was a kind of hospitality.

The television above the counter was tuned to the state channel, as it always was, and so I saw it happen live — or what passed for live, the footage already three days old by the time the censors gave up trying to bury it. There was no giving up, exactly. There was just a moment where the dam of them decided it was cheaper to look like they'd known all along.

A woman's voice first. Old, cracked, speaking a dialect the anchors had to subtitle. *They came at dusk. My father was still in the field. They did not let him finish the row.* Then another voice, younger, recorded on equipment so primitive it hissed like the river itself. Then another. Forty years of voices, stacked end to end, played over footage of the Karveth Range that some drone pilot had shot before anyone thought to confiscate it — the white shoulders of the peaks, the green throat of the valley, the river running out of it bright as exposed nerve.

The anchor's face did its best. Concerned. Gravely neutral. The face of a person reading words someone else had approved at two in the morning.

*Records recovered from a glacial archive in the upper Karveth watershed appear to document the events of the Sarneth Valley relocation, long characterized by the Ministry of Interior as a security operation resulting in regrettable civilian losses.*

Regrettable civilian losses. Two hundred and eleven people, if you counted the ones the vault counted, and the vault had counted everyone. It had names. It had the sound of a man singing to his daughter to keep her calm while soldiers walked the rows of houses with kerosene. It had a government surveyor — Osei, the team leader, his voice recognizable even through forty years of tape hiss — reading aloud the ministry memo he'd found sealed inside the steel door, the one authorizing the operation, signed by a deputy minister whose name was not yet Corrigan but whose office was, whose desk was, whose chair Corrigan sat in now or had sat in until eleven days ago.

I watched my own confession scroll across the chyron. Not mine by name — the state still preferred its monsters anonymous — but *a former survey team member has admitted to guiding officials to the site in exchange for payment.* They'd gotten that part right, at least. I'd given them everything else myself, on camera, in the village hall, in front of the people whose grandparents' voices I had helped lead men to melt out of the ice. I'd said it plainly because there was no version of it that survived being made pretty. *I took the money. I drew the map. I told them the glacier was stable when I knew it wasn't.* The only thing I'd left out was that I hadn't known about the door until after, and by then knowing wouldn't have changed what I'd sold.

Marta Okonkwo — no relation to Tamsin, though the village had decided otherwise within a day, deciding kinship by deed rather than blood — had stood at the back during my confession with her arms folded, and when I finished she'd said only: *Good. Now sit down and listen.* And I had. For six hours I'd listened to testimony from the vault's duplicate copies, the ones the villagers had kept in a dry cellar beneath the meeting house for three generations, waiting for someone with a transmitter and nothing left to lose.

That someone turned out to be Tamsin. Of course it did.

* * *

The official response unfolded the way ice rots: invisibly, then all at once, then in pieces nobody could reassemble.

First denial. A statement from the Ministry of Interior expressing *grave concern at the circulation of unverified materials.* Then a grudging acknowledgment that the materials were, in fact, verified — authenticated by three independent archivists, two of whom promptly resigned from their universities for reasons never explained. Then arrests. Not of Corrigan, not at first. Of clerks, drivers, a mid-level archivist who had spent twenty years quietly losing files. The small hands of the machine, offered up while the head pretended to be shocked by what its fingers had done.

Corrigan himself was gone by then. That was the part the broadcasts didn't say clearly, the part I knew because Ilse Varga told me, sitting across from me in this same booth five days after the flood, her left hand still bandaged where the ice had taken skin off it in sheets.

*He wasn't in the collapse,* she'd said. *I want you to understand that. Whatever story makes you sleep — he didn't die heroically trying to destroy the records, and he didn't die at all. When the serac let go, he was already moving downhill. He'd sent his men up ahead of him. He was thirty meters lower than the door when the glacier came apart.*

*Then where is he?*

She'd looked at her bandaged hand the way you look at a tool that failed you. *Gone. There are ports on the east coast that don't ask questions, and men like him have had their bags packed for years. He built the blacklist, Rook. Do you think a man like that doesn't keep a list of his own?*

So Corrigan was somewhere south, presumably, drinking something expensive under a name that sounded nothing like his, and the ministry was prosecuting his network with the fervor of people burning evidence of their own proximity to it. Three deputies arrested. One minister resigned citing health. The President's office issued a statement about *a difficult chapter in our nation's history* and announced a commission of inquiry with a mandate broad enough to drive a convoy through and a timeline vague enough to outlast everyone who remembered why it mattered.

But the village got named. That was the thing. That was the thing I couldn't stop turning over.

Sarneth Valley. On the maps again — not the old maps, which had never carried it, but the new ones, the ones drawn in the six weeks since, with a small marker and the words *Memorial Site* in letters smaller than any town of two hundred living residents deserved. Descendants had begun making the pilgrimage up before the passes even cleared. I'd watched them arrive in the village: old women who'd been children on the night of the relocation, walking the last kilometers on legs that shook, carrying photographs of faces the vault had preserved in voice if not in image. Marta met every one of them at the trailhead. She'd appointed herself greeter the way other people appoint themselves mayors, and nobody argued.

And Tamsin Okonkwo remained blacklisted.

* * *

I learned the details from Ilse, because Tamsin wouldn't talk about it, and because Ilse had developed a habit of finding me in the Salt Larder as if checking whether I were still capable of sitting still.

*The commission reviewed the case,* she said. *Both cases. The Karveth survey team deaths, and the original incident — the Fenris rescue, the one that started the ledger.*

*I know how the Fenris rescue ended.*

*You know the rumor of it. So do I, now, officially.* She turned her teacup a quarter turn, a habit she probably thought was invisible. *Tamsin got nine people down off a face in a storm that killed two guides with better reputations. The ninth was a client who panicked at the rappel and clipped into a rotten anchor against direct instruction. The anchor failed. The client fell. Tamsin had hold of the rope. The rope burned through both their gloves and tore the finger tendons — you've seen the scars. The client died. The inquiry found procedural violations: unauthorized route, continued descent in deteriorating conditions. What it declined to note was that the conditions deteriorated because the ministry's own weather service sat on a storm warning for six hours to avoid disrupting a timber contract in the valley below. Corrigan's office signed off on that suppression. Corrigan wrote the recommendation that stripped Tamsin's certification. And now Corrigan's office is dissolved, and the commission has reviewed the file, and do you know what they concluded?*

*Tell me.*

*'Inconclusive.'* She smiled without anything in it. *The storm warning suppression is documented — it's in the vault, actually, one of the ancillary files Osei copied — but the commission ruled it outside their mandate. The mandate covers the Sarneth relocation. Everything else is, quote, 'a matter for separate review.' Separate review that will be convened next fiscal year, by a different body, subject to funding. Tamsin's certification remains revoked pending that review. The blacklist stands. Both countries honor each other's lists, you know. It's one of the few things they cooperate on.*

I sat with that. Outside the window, Halden's Rest went about its morning: a man arguing with a mule, snowmelt running brown in the gutters, the mountains standing over everything with the patience of things that have outlasted every name ever given to them.

*Does Tamsin know all this?*

*Tamsin read the commission report before I did. Someone in the archives owes them a favor from the old days.* Ilse finally drank her tea. *Here's what I can't work out. Tamsin could fight it. Publicly. The recordings made Tamsin famous — the guide who broadcast the truth from the source. There are journalists camped at the village asking for interviews. One word, one press conference, and the blacklist becomes a scandal the ministry can't survive twice.*

*And?*

*And Tamsin said no.* She set the cup down with a small precise click. *Said the dead didn't come back for vindication, so why should the living get to trade on theirs. Said something else, too, that I've been chewing on for a week.* She looked at me directly then, and Ilse Varga looking at you directly was like a searchlight coming around. *Tamsin said: 'Rook understands. Ask him.'*

* * *

Did I understand? I turned it over for days, walking the low trails above town because my body still expected altitude and complained without it, and I think I finally did, or close enough.

It was the same arithmetic as the rope. In chapter seventeen of this disaster — if disasters have chapters, and I've learned they do, each one worse than the last — Rook Adeyemi had stood in the vault with the copies in his arms and offered to be the one erased. *Give me the originals. Let me walk out and take the blame publicly. You're the one they'll listen to; I'm the one they already believe is guilty. You walk free with a clean name and I get to stop being the man who sold the map.* And Tamsin had looked at me the way you look at a crevasse bridge that might hold — assessing, not hopeful — and said: *No. Nobody gets erased for me again. Not you, not the dead, not my father's ghost. We go out together or we don't go out.*

I'd hated it then. I'd wanted the bargain. A clean name was the whole currency I understood; I'd spent a team's lives chasing the reverse of one. But standing on the trail above Halden's Rest with the commission's *inconclusive* hanging over Tamsin like weather that never breaks, I finally saw the shape of it. A name restored by the same machinery that blacklisted you isn't restoration. It's a receipt. It means the machine has decided you're useful again, and usefulness is a leash with better stitching. Tamsin had spent years wanting vindication the way a drowning person wants air — and then held the air in their hands and given it away, because the people who needed it more were under the ice, and the people who could breathe freely were the ones who'd put them there.

Exoneration by Corrigan's successors would have cost the same thing Corrigan's condemnation once had: it would have made the story about Tamsin. And the story was never supposed to be about Tamsin. It was supposed to be about a row of houses that burned, and a man singing to his daughter, and two hundred and eleven names spoken aloud by strangers until the strangers became descendants and the descendants became witnesses.

So Tamsin stayed blacklisted. Unemployable in two countries, famous in neither, living — I learned eventually — in a room above the village meeting house, paying rent in guided walks for the pilgrims who came to see the memorial and underestimated the approach. The tendons in both hands ached in cold weather. The scars caught fire at altitude. Tamsin walked anyway.

* * *

On the last morning of April, Ilse came to the Salt Larder and didn't order tea, which meant she was leaving. She stood at my booth with her pack on and her radio — the infamous transmitting radio, the leash — slung openly over her shoulder, switched off.

*I'm resigning,* she said. *Formally. There's a form. There's always a form.* She dropped an envelope on the table. Inside was a certification document, real, bearing a seal I recognized and a signature I didn't. *The commission's cleanup reached the licensing board. Your record was reviewed as part of the survey team case — posthumous exoneration for Osei and the others required reconstructing who'd assisted them. Tamsin's guiding work was central to the reconstruction. The board reinstated the certification as an administrative correction.* She paused. *Tamsin hasn't collected it. It's been sitting in the Halden's Rest post office for nine days. The clerk asked me to find out if the recipient is alive.*

*Are you going to tell them?*

*I'm telling you.* She shouldered her pack. *Because you're going to carry it up to Sarneth Valley, and you're going to put it in Tamsin's hands, and you're going to watch what happens. Consider it your penance. You're good at carrying things uphill for people you've wronged — it's practically a vocation now.*

At the door she stopped, half-turned. *For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I switched the radio off myself. First time. It felt like taking off a boot that had been on fire for a year.* Then she pushed out into the street and was gone, and I sat with the envelope in front of me feeling its weight, which was almost nothing, which was everything.

* * *

I climbed to the valley over two days, because my lungs were rebuilt wrong and because I wanted the approach to mean something. The memorial marker was new stone, and someone — Marta, I suspected, or one of the schoolchildren she'd drafted — had carved all two hundred and eleven names into it in a spiral, so that reading them meant walking around and around, closer to the center, the way grief actually works.

Tamsin was at the meeting house, mending a coil of rope, hands moving with the economy of someone who has done ten thousand times what they're doing. I put the envelope on the bench between us and explained what it was. Tamsin didn't touch it.

*Ilse sent you.*

*Ilse sent me.*

*A certification.* Tamsin turned the rope over, found the frayed section, began to whip the end with cord. *From the board that took it.*

*From the board that took it,* I agreed. *Reinstated. Legal in both countries. You could guide anywhere. You could take clients up the Karveth proper — commercial expeditions pay absurd rates for that range now, everyone wants to see the source.*

The whipping cord went taut. Tamsin bit it clean and sat back and looked at the envelope for a long time, and then at the window, where the peaks stood white and indifferent above the tree line, and where — higher than any of them, higher than the maps allowed — the glacier held its new scar, the wound where the vault had been, already healing over with fresh snow that would be ancient ice again in a century.

*You know what I keep thinking about,* Tamsin said. *My father's compass. The bearings in Osei's notebook matched it exactly — the man who led the first collection run used the same routes my father mapped, the routes he taught me before the state erased him. That compass pointed at this valley my whole life and I never once followed it here. I followed it everywhere else. Every summit, every paycheck, every desperate job that ended with me on a ledger. It was pointing home the entire time and I was navigating by everything else.*

*Tamsin—*

*Keep it.* Tamsin slid the envelope back across the bench, unhurried, final. *Not thrown away — keep it somewhere safe. Maybe someday the review convenes, maybe someday I care. But I'm not signing anything that says the mountains are open to me now, as if they were ever closed, as if the closure was theirs to give.* Tamsin picked the rope back up. *There's a group of the village kids who've been asking me to take them up past the source line this summer. First ascent party, all of them under nineteen, all of them grandchildren of people in that spiral out there. Marta wants them to see where the voices lived. I told them I'd think about it.*

*And?*

Tamsin smiled — the first one I'd seen in weeks, small and crooked and entirely uninterested in permission. *I'm thinking yes. But I need a second rope. Someone who knows the Karveth approaches, someone with nothing left to lose and a debt to work off, someone who can carry a heavy pack and complain about it in at least two languages.*

The wind came down off the glacier and moved through the pines, and somewhere below us the river ran bright out of the valley, carrying snowmelt and silt and the memory of everything toward the sea.

*Rook,* Tamsin said, tying off the rope's new end and holding it out to me like a hand, like a rope, like the beginning of the only provision that had ever mattered. *How do you feel about teaching children to read ice?*
