## 22. The Rough Provision

The morning of the ascent came in the color of old pewter, and Tamsin Okonkwo woke before the alarm they no longer needed.

In Halden's Rest, waking had been a negotiation — with debt, with dread, with the ledger of names that would not hire them. Here, in the valley that wasn't on maps, waking was simpler. You opened your eyes to the smell of woodsmoke and barley porridge, to the sound of the river running clean off the glacier, and you got up because the mountain was there and because four young people were counting on you to be awake before they were.

That last part still startled them, some mornings. Being counted on. It fit strangely, like a boot resoled with the wrong leather — functional, but you felt every seam.

Tamsin dressed by feel in the cold dark of the guest hut, fingers finding the familiar choreography: base layer, fleece, shell. The compass lived in the chest pocket, where it had lived for twenty years, where their father's compass had lived for thirty before that. Brass casing worn smooth at the edges. The needle still pulled true north with a small, stubborn insistence that Tamsin had come to think of as hereditary.

Outside, the village was already stirring. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin gray threads. Somewhere a dog complained about the hour. And at the edge of the threshing ground, four figures stood in a row with packs too big for them, trying very hard to look like they hadn't been waiting since before dawn.

They were the Oduya grandchildren, more or less — the village didn't hold much with precise genealogy anymore, not after what had been done to its family trees — and they ranged from fifteen to nineteen, all elbows and appetite and barely contained nerves.

"You're late," said Meret, who was fifteen and had already decided she was in charge of something.

"I'm early," Tamsin said. "You're just earlier." They checked each harness with two fingers, tugged each buckle, made each of them recite the day's rules back in order. Rope team spacing. Crevasse protocol. What to do if the weather turned, which it would, because it always did above the ice line, and anyone who told you otherwise was selling something.

"And if we see strangers?" asked Dagny, the youngest, whose voice still cracked on the word *strangers*.

"You don't see strangers," Tamsin said. "Not up there. Up there there's only us and the mountain, and the mountain doesn't care about our paperwork."

This got the laugh Tamsin wanted. Nerves burned off best as laughter; they'd learned that from their father, who had learned it from someone whose name the massacre records had preserved in a voice like dry leaves.

Rook was waiting at the trailhead, leaning against the marker stone with a thermos and an expression he probably thought looked casual.

"You're coming," Tamsin said. Not a question. They'd had this argument twice already this week, and Rook had lost both times, and both times had shown up anyway.

"Someone has to carry the heavy rope," Rook said. "And someone has to be worse at altitude than the children so they feel brave."

"That's your job now? Designated coward?"

"Senior consultant," Rook said. "It's in my contract." There was no contract. There were no contracts anywhere in this valley, which was rather the point of it. But Rook had taken to inventing titles for himself the way other men took to drink, and Tamsin had decided it was harmless. Mostly harmless. He still flinched at loud noises and couldn't sleep under open sky, but the flinching was getting smaller, the way a bruise gets smaller, going through colors on its way to gone.

He'd told them everything, in the end. All of it, in front of the village elders, in the long hall, with his hands flat on the table like a man surrendering weapons he no longer carried. *I led Corrigan here. I took the money. They died because of me.* And the elders had listened the way stone listens, and then old Mama Oduya had said the thing Tamsin still turned over at night sometimes:

"A rope with one knot holds nothing. Sit down, boy. Eat."

That was the whole of the village's justice. No tribunal. No blacklist. Just a bowl of food and the expectation that you would spend the rest of your life being worth the food. Tamsin had watched Rook weep into his barley porridge like a man being pardoned, and had understood, finally, why the vault had been kept here for sixty years by people with no army and no state and nothing but each other.

Some debts could only be repaid in currency the banks didn't recognize.

* * *

They climbed out of the valley as the sun came over the eastern ridge and set the glacier burning white-gold.

Tamsin led, reading the ice the way their father had taught them — not with instruments but with attention, with the whole listening body. The glacier had changed since the collapse. Everyone said so. Where Corrigan's charges and the destabilized seracs had brought down the original face, the ice had reformed into new shapes, new crevasses, a new grammar entirely. The route to the source was different now. The route to the source would always be different now. That was, Tamsin thought, as good a monument as any. A door that could never be found the same way twice.

"Guide!" Meret called from second position. "Tell us about the survey team."

"Meret."

"You always tell us on the glacier. You said."

She was right; Tamsin had said. It had become part of the route, the way cairns are part of a route. On the lower ice: the story of the five surveyors who came up looking for minerals and found testimony instead. At the first rest stop: Ilse Varga, who had worn a wire and then cut it, who had let herself be taken so that two strangers could climb higher, and who had walked out of Corrigan's camp three weeks later with frostbitten fingers and a resignation letter written in a hand that shook, and who now sent postcards from a fishing town on the coast that signed off, always, with *the river remembers.*

At the second rest stop: the broadcast. Ninety-one minutes of recorded voices streamed live from inside the ice while government men moved in below, and the world — or enough of the world, or at least enough of the world's memory — had stopped and listened. Tamsin did not tell that part dramatically. They told it the way it had happened: breathless, terrified, hands shaking on an ancient transmitter while Rook read testimony in a voice that cracked and held and cracked again. The young people deserved the true version. Everything in this valley ran on true versions.

"And then the ice fell on him," Dagny said, at the third rest stop, with the relish children reserve for the villain's death.

"The ice fell on him," Tamsin agreed. "But listen — it almost fell on us too. Rook went into the meltwater. I went in after him. And the reason I could find him, in water cold enough to stop a heart in four minutes, is that I knew this glacier the way you know your own house in the dark. Blacklisted guides know things licensed guides don't. Remember that. Every hard thing you survive becomes a door for somebody else."

Dagny nodded solemnly, absorbing this, and would repeat it wrong at dinner tonight, and it would get corrected, and eventually it would enter the village's stock of sayings, slightly bent, the way all inherited wisdom arrives. Tamsin had stopped minding. Their father's sayings had arrived bent too.

Above the icefall, the climbing turned serious, and talk gave way to breath and rope and the small essential language of the mountain: *tight, slack, step left, hold.* Tamsin moved through it gladly. This was the country where they had never been fluent anywhere else. Two countries had revoked their right to guide; the ice had never gotten the memo. The ice judged only what you did next.

They reached the site of the vault entrance at midday.

There was nothing to see, now. That was the thing visitors never understood — the villagers' cousins from the coast, journalists who still occasionally hiked up with satellite phones and apologies. The collapse had sealed it. Sixty years of testimony, thousands of hours of recorded voices, the steel door older than the state itself: all of it entombed again, deeper than before, in ice that would outlast everyone currently breathing. The copies had gone out into the world on the broadcast night, mirrored across servers by strangers who owed the village nothing and gave everything anyway. The original stayed here, the way bones stay in the ground of the place that made them.

The young people stood in a half circle at the blue wall and did not talk. Even Meret. The wind moved. Somewhere deep in the glacier, ice settled with a sound like a long exhale.

"We used to think," Tamsin said quietly, "that vindication would look like a letter. An official letter, with a seal, saying *Tamsin Okonkwo is cleared.* We climbed toward that letter for years. Carried it around in our head like a compass bearing." They touched the brass case in their chest pocket. "Then the truth came out, and the letter never came. The people who ruined us are mostly dead or in hiding, and the ones who aren't will never say sorry, and officially — on paper, in the registries, in the two countries that matter to bureaucrats — we are still exactly what they said we were."

"That's not fair," Dagny said.

"No," Tamsin agreed. "It isn't. But here's what we learned, standing right where you're standing. Fairness is a thing governments issue. Belonging is a thing people make. One of those showed up for us. Guess which."

Meret frowned, working it. "People."

"People," Tamsin said. "So. Last pitch. The source itself. Who wants to lead?"

Four hands went up at once, and Tamsin laughed, and put Meret on point because she'd earned it and because being fifteen and in charge of something was a feeling worth giving a person early.

* * *

The final pitch was short and steep and honest — good nevé, solid protection, the kind of climbing that asks everything of you and gives it all back with interest. Meret led it carefully, placing gear with the deliberation of someone handling her grandmother's dishes, and topped out onto the broad shoulder of rock below the source basin with a yell that scattered a pair of choughs off the cliff walls.

The basin opened around them like a cupped hand.

The river's forbidden source was not dramatic, in the end. It was a seep — a wide dark stain on the rock where the glacier wept, feeding a pool the size of a kitchen table, so clear it seemed not to exist except where the light bent in it. From this pool, improbably, impossibly, the Karveth River began: the water that ran down through the hidden valley, past the village, through the gorge where a surveyor's body had once snagged with a notebook waterproofed against his chest, all the way to the lowlands and the towns and the sea. Every drop of it passed through this place first. Every drop of it carried something away.

The young people knelt around the pool without being told. That was the ritual, such as it was — the one the elders had given them, the one that consisted of nothing but presence. You came up. You knelt. You listened. The dead of the massacre had poured their testimony into machines for sixty years so that someone, someday, would kneel here and hear it.

Tamsin knelt with them and took out the compass.

It lay in their palm, brass worn bright at the edges, needle swinging once, twice, settling north with its small stubborn insistence. Their father's compass. The one whose bearings matched the dead surveyor's notebook page for page, because their father had walked this route before any of them, before the massacre even had a name, mapping approaches for a government that would later erase him and everyone who sounded like him. Tamsin had spent years thinking the compass was a relic. Then evidence. Then a burden. Now it was just what it had always been: an instrument. It pointed. What you did with the pointing was the whole of the moral life.

"Guide," Dagny whispered, kneeling close. "What do we do now? Now that we're here?"

Tamsin looked out over the basin — the ice, the rock, the four young faces wind-burned and shining, Rook standing a little apart with his hat in his hands like a man at a graveside, which he was. Below them, invisible, the valley that wasn't on maps held its smoke and its dogs and its barley fields and its stubborn, unregistered life. Somewhere far south, in two countries that kept immaculate records, a blacklisted guide's file gathered dust, accurate and meaningless.

"Now," Tamsin said, "we go home. And tomorrow we come back up with the next ones. And the next. As long as there's ice and someone to walk on it." They closed their hand around the compass and stood. "That's the provision. The rough one. Nobody signs it. Everybody keeps it."

Meret was already studying the descent route, because she was fifteen and in charge of something. Dagny was filling a small bottle from the pool, for her grandmother, because the water remembered and the living should too. Rook came and stood beside Tamsin, shoulder to shoulder, and neither of them said anything, because between the two of them everything that needed saying had been said on rope, in storms, in meltwater, and silence had long since stopped meaning distance.

They started down as the afternoon light went long and amber across the ice.

And at the edge of the basin, where the maps ended and had always ended, Tamsin paused once — just once — and looked back at the seeping dark water that fed the whole patient river, and felt, for the first time in twenty years, no pull at all toward any direction but home.
