## 1. What the Tide Gives Back

The wreck lay at thirty-one meters, and Ivo Villalobos had been lying to herself about it for three weeks.

She told herself it was salvage. That was the word on the manifest she'd signed in a navy office that smelled of cold coffee and floor wax — *salvage survey, non-disclosure attached* — and the word she repeated when she dropped through the green dark with a torch and a cutting rig and no one watching but the fish. The *Almirante Reyes* had gone down in '61 with a hold full of machinery nobody would name, and now the navy wanted certain plates removed before some satellite or sonar sweep got curious. They paid by the kilo. Ivo was forty thousand in debt to a man named Ferro who broke fingers alphabetically, and so she dove.

That morning the water was wrong from the first breath.

She noticed it at the surface, treading beside her boat while the regulator hissed: the kelp that usually brushed her fins hung limp and high, its fronds trailing above her head instead of around her waist. The buoy chain on the wreck site ran taut at an angle she'd never seen. She checked her gauge out of habit, then the sky — high, hard blue, no storm swell anywhere on it — and told herself the tide tables were just tables, printed by people who'd never been underwater.

Below, the light came down in long clean columns, unbroken by the usual murk. The *Reyes* sat on sand that should have been dim and blue-lit but instead lay washed in silver, every rivet sharp, every rust bloom vivid as coral. It looked less like a grave than like something being displayed. She worked anyway. Torch, pry bar, the four numbered plates Casals's men wanted, each one heavier than sin and colder than the debt they were buying down. Her hands did the work while her mind kept drifting upward, toward the surface, toward the strangeness pressing down on the sea from above rather than rising from below.

It wasn't until she surfaced, hauling herself over the gunwale with the last plate clanking against the tank rack, that she understood.

The tide was going out. And it was not coming back the way tides do.

She stood on her deck and watched the sea withdraw from the coast like a hand pulling back from a table. Not a tsunami-drawdown — nothing that violent, that apocalyptic. This was slower, stranger, a steady peeling-back of water that had no business obeying anything but the moon. The channel between the harbor rocks narrowed to a brown ribbon. Sandbars she'd only ever seen as darker shadows under the surface emerged glistening, ribbed like the backs of buried animals. Fishing boats listed against mud that hadn't seen air in decades, their hulls weeping weed.

And below the old lighthouse — the dead lighthouse, the one the charts marked *disused* and everyone else just called the old man — the cliff itself was showing something new.

Ivo had grown up on this coast. She knew the cliff below the lighthouse the way she knew the scar on her own knee: a sheer basalt face dropping straight into twelve meters of green water, no ledges, no beaches, nothing for a climber or a swimmer. Except that right now there was no water. There was a shelf of wet black rock, and set into the base of the cliff, exposed for the first time in anyone's memory, a mouth.

A cave mouth. Tall as a door, edged with barnacles that must have spent a century underwater and were now dying in the open air, popping faintly in the sun like distant firecrackers. Dark inside. Breathing out cold.

She should have reported it. That was procedure; the non-disclosure clause covered everything in the survey zone, and the lighthouse cliff was squarely inside it. She should have radioed the naval office, logged the anomaly, let Lieutenant Casals send his own people to look.

Instead she found herself checking her remaining air, swapping tanks, and rowing her dinghy toward a cliff that had never existed before today.

*Curiosity,* her grandfather used to say, *is just greed wearing a better coat.* He'd said it laughing, teaching her knots on the deck of his little sloop, and she'd laughed too, not knowing yet that he'd meant it as a warning about himself.

The shelf was treacherous — kelp-slick, stinking of exposed seabed, littered with stranded crabs clicking their indignation. The cave mouth exhaled air so cold it fogged. She clicked on her torch at the threshold and stepped in.

The floor rose gently, dry past the barnacle line, and the cold deepened with every step until her breath streamed behind her like smoke. The walls were basalt veined with quartz that caught the torchlight and threw it back in white threads. Fifty meters in, the passage turned, and the turn held a draft — moving air, which meant another opening somewhere, higher, maybe far higher. A mountain breathing through stone.

Then the light found the boots.

They were leather, hobnailed, a style sixty years out of date, and they were attached to legs. The legs were attached to a man sitting against the cave wall with the composed posture of someone resting between pitches, hands folded in his lap around a small bright object.

Ivo stopped. The cold walked up her spine and settled at the base of her skull.

He was preserved. Not mummified — *preserved*, frozen and thawed and frozen again across decades into something smooth and waxy, his face calm, his beard frosted with mineral white. He wore vintage climbing gear: a canvas anorak gone amber with age, a hemp-and-steel rope still coiled at his shoulder, wool trousers, a rucksack propped beside him with its buckles done up neat. Everything about him said *expedition*. Everything except the location, because there was no mountain within a hundred kilometers of this cave, and no glacier, and no reason on God's cold earth for a climber to be sitting here in full kit, four hundred meters from the sea.

Her torch shook slightly. She steadied it with both hands, the way you steady a tool, because that was the rule: treat fear like equipment. Check it, maintain it, don't let it fail you mid-job.

She crouched near him without touching. The rucksack first — she couldn't help it — but it held only ruin: a tin cup, a waxed bundle that crumbled when she looked at it, a journal whose pages had fused into a single block of pulp. Whatever words he'd written had drowned in time.

Then the object in his hands.

A compass. Brass-cased, lid hinged, the glass crazed but whole. She knew it before she touched it. She knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

Because there was one exactly like it hanging on a nail above the chart table of her boat. Her grandfather's compass — the one he'd left her, the one she'd worn on every dive since she was nineteen. Same maker's mark stamped into the case: a small anchor over crossed pitons. Same weight, same patina, same hinge-wear where a thumb pushes the lid open a thousand times a year.

And when she finally — gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal — lifted it from the dead man's hands, the engraving on the inside of the lid caught the torchlight.

*V.*

Just the letter. Just the initial. The same single letter engraved inside her grandfather's compass, in the same fine script, cut by the same hand or the same machine on the same day, sixty years ago, for two instruments that had ended up forty years apart in the dark — one on a nail above a chart table, one in the lap of a frozen man beneath a dead lighthouse.

Ivo sat back on her heels in the cold and felt curiosity curdle, slowly and completely, into dread.

Her grandfather had died eleven years ago, an old man in a warm bed, full of stories she'd half-believed — the legendary expedition, the one that vanished on the high range before she was born, the one the newspapers still ran retrospectives about every anniversary. He'd never talked about it directly. He'd talked *around* it, the way you talk around a hole in the ice. And he'd given her a compass and taught her the sea and told her curiosity was greed in a better coat.

The dead man's other hand — the left, the one not holding the compass — was closed around something too. She pried the stiff fingers open with a tenderness that surprised her. Inside: a scrap of oilcloth, folded small, and inside that, a square of heavy paper gone soft as cloth at the edges.

A map. Hand-drawn, ink faded to the color of weak tea, but precise — contour lines, a route, elevation marks climbing page after folded page toward a summit marked with a small careful X and a word in her grandfather's handwriting, because it *was* his handwriting, she'd know it anywhere, she'd addressed birthday cards to him in that exact slanted script:

*CACHE.*

The cave breathed its cold breath over her. Somewhere above, impossibly high, the second opening let in a thread of daylight that fell across the dead man's face like a spotlight finding an actor who has waited forty years for his cue.

Ivo thought about the non-disclosure clause. About Lieutenant Casals and his cold coffee and his eyes that counted things — she'd seen it the first day, the way they flicked over her boat, her gear, her debts, tallying. About the navy sealing a wreck nobody could explain and paying cash by the kilo for plates nobody would name.

She thought about her grandfather, and the hole in the ice he'd talked around all those years.

She could log this. Report it. Let the professionals come with their evidence bags and their classified stamps, and whatever this man knew, whatever that map led to, it would go into a drawer in a building with a flag outside, next to the mystery of the *Almirante Reyes*, and she would go back to diving for men who owned her.

Or.

Her hands moved before the argument finished. Compass into her dive bag, deep beneath the spare mask. Map folded into the inner pocket of her suit, against her skin, where it warmed immediately and stayed there like a second heartbeat. She arranged the dead man's empty hands back in his lap, folded, patient, and heard herself say, quietly, in the empty cold:

"I'll come back for you."

She didn't know if she meant it as a promise or an apology.

Outside, the sea was already turning, sliding back up the shelf, reclaiming the barnacles with a sound like applause. By the time she rowed away, the cave mouth was a dark seam beneath the water again, and the cliff wore its familiar blank face, and any diver passing in a normal tide would swear there had never been anything there at all.

Ivo motored back to her mooring with the sun low and her heart going like a bilge pump. She cleaned the catch plates, logged the weights, ate standing up. She did not radio anyone. She did not write anything down. When the chart lamp flickered on at dusk she took her grandfather's compass down off its nail, opened the lid, and set the dead man's compass beside it — twin brass faces gleaming, twin letters hidden inside — and watched them like a woman watching two ends of a fuse burning toward each other.

In the morning, she told herself, she would decide what to do.

She was still telling herself that at 0400, when the knock came on her hull — three slow blows, deliberate, official — and a voice out of the dark said, "Señora Villalobos. Lieutenant Casals. We need to discuss your survey."
