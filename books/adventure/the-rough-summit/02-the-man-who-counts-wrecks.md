## 2. The Man Who Counts Wrecks

The summons came the way bad weather comes to this coast: not as a surprise, but as a pressure you feel in your teeth before the sky admits anything.

I was elbow-deep in the *Magdalena's* bilge pump when the harbor patrol boat idled alongside, and a boy of maybe nineteen handed me an envelope with a navy crest and my name spelled correctly. That alone told me how much trouble I was in. Most people on this coast still spelled it Villalobos with two l's and no heart.

*Lieutenant D. Casals requests the pleasure—*

Nobody in the navy had requested my pleasure since they'd revoked my wreck permit three years ago. The word *requests* was doing the work that a man with a sidearm usually did.

I went anyway. Debts don't care about pride, and mine had grown teeth. Forty thousand to the bank, twelve to the fuel dock, and a number I didn't say out loud to a man named Ferro who collected with a crowbar instead of invoices. The navy wreck was the only contract left that paid enough to matter, which is why I dove it at dawn, alone, and why I never spoke about what lay down there — not because they'd sworn me to silence, but because some things you keep quiet just to prove they can't take everything from you.

The naval office sat above the customs house, all grey stone and salt-stained windows. A clerk walked me up two flights without meeting my eyes, which is its own kind of message.

Lieutenant Casals stood at the window with his back to me when I entered. He was younger than his reputation — mid-forties, close-cropped silver coming in early at the temples, hands clasped behind him like a man holding himself together at the seams. On the wall behind his desk hung a framed chart of the coast, and someone had pinned a small red flag to the cliff below the old lighthouse.

I knew that cliff. I'd been standing inside it forty hours ago.

"Señora Villalobos." He turned. His face was pleasant the way a frozen lake is pleasant. "Thank you for coming so promptly."

"You made it easy to find you."

"I try." He gestured to a chair. I stayed standing. Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Recalculation. "How is the *Magdalena*? I'm told her hull survey is due."

"My boat is none of the navy's business."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But her debts are public record. Forty-one thousand, six hundred, as of Tuesday. Plus interest accruing at—" he consulted nothing, reciting from memory, "—a rate that will take her from you by spring. It would be a shame. They say her keel was laid by your grandfather."

My grandfather's name again. It kept surfacing lately, like something drowned that refused to stay down.

"What do you want, Lieutenant?"

He crossed to his desk, opened a drawer, and set one object on the blotter. Not a weapon. A photograph.

It showed the cave mouth beneath the lighthouse cliff — taken from the water, at low tide, the black opening exposed like a wound. The timestamp in the corner was eleven years old.

"The tide has been behaving strangely this season," Casals said. "Low records broken twice in October. You'd know; you were there." He let that sit. "You hold a salvage permit for the wreck offshore. The cave is outside your permit. Whatever you found inside it on Thursday morning is therefore, legally speaking, unreported recovery of material from a restricted site. Which makes it mine. Which makes returning it a matter of patriotism rather than theft."

"And if I found nothing?"

"Then this conversation would be shorter." He slid the photograph aside and produced a second sheet — a printed form, half-filled in. My eyes went to the figure at the bottom before I could stop myself. *41,600 — marked PAID IN FULL.*

"I am authorized," he said, "to settle your accounts. All of them. Today. In exchange for the items you removed from the cave."

*Items.* Plural. My pulse ticked up once, and I hated him for hearing it.

"There was a body," I said carefully. "Frozen. Old climbing gear. I reported nothing because it wasn't a wreck and it wasn't mine to report. I touched nothing else."

Casals smiled. It was the first time, and it was worse than everything before it.

"A climber," he repeated softly. "In vintage equipment. Tell me, Señora Villalobos — did the dead man have anything in his hands?"

The room got very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"I don't remember," I said.

"You do. But let's proceed as though you don't." He picked up the form, squared its edges against the desk, and held it out. "You have until tomorrow evening. If the items are in my possession by then, this is signed, the bank is notified, and you walk out of here owing nothing to anyone. If they are not—" he set it back down, unhurried, "—then I regret that the navy will be compelled to pursue charges regarding your unauthorized entry into a sealed site. There are also questions, I understand, about irregularities in your last three salvage declarations. Ferro has been very helpful to this office."

Ferro. My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. The crowbar and the lieutenant, working the same vein.

"How do you know what's in that cave?" I asked. "That photo is eleven years old."

For a moment — half a breath — something moved behind Casals's composure. Not guilt. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own history read back to him.

"Because I have been counting wrecks on this coast for twenty years," he said. "And every wreck has a story, and every story has a grave, and some graves are better left unopened. Your grandfather understood that. Ask yourself why a man like Emil Villalobos never once, in forty years of diving, went near that cliff."

He walked me to the door himself. At the threshold he said, quietly, almost kindly:

"The tide will come back in, Señora. It always does. What matters is what we choose to carry out before it does."

* * *

I didn't go straight home. I took the long way around the harbor, bought diesel I couldn't afford, and watched my reflection in the chandlery window until it stopped looking guilty.

The lie was already costing me. *I don't remember.* As if a woman who could recall the serial numbers of every fitting on her boat wouldn't remember the weight of a brass compass in a dead man's fist — the same make as the one in my cabin, the same engraving on the case back, the same family mark my grandfather put on everything he ever owned. Two compasses. One dead man. One grandfather whose name Casals said like a man handling a live wire.

I could give it to him. Sign the paper, sink the debt, keep the boat, sleep at night — except I'd seen the red flag on his chart, and men don't guard a hole in a cliff for twenty years over nothing. Whatever the dead climber had been carrying, Casals wanted it buried worse than he wanted it found. And things people want buried have a way of being worth more than debts.

Or I could run. Point the *Magdalena* south and disappear into the fishing lanes. Except Ferro had eyes in every port and Casals had a navy, and boats are slow, honest things.

So I did neither. I went back to the *Magdalena*, locked the compass and the folded map from its lid inside the engine-room toolbox where nobody ever looks because nobody wants to crawl in there, and I waited for dark, telling myself I was being careful when really I was being a coward with a schedule.

They came at 2 a.m.

Not Casals — I'd bet the boat on that. This was quieter, sloppier, hungrier. I woke to the sound of drawers hitting the deck and came up the companionway with the flare gun, and the cabin lantern showed me a stranger going through my charts with gloved hands — tall, thirty-ish, expensive jacket ruined by salt, a face that had been handsome before grief got its hands on it.

He heard me and turned, and there was a pistol in his hand, pointed somewhere between my sternum and my regrets.

"Don't," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Don't scream. Don't reach for anything. Just tell me where it is."

"Where what is?" I kept my own voice level, which cost more than the flare gun would have.

"You know what. The compass. From the cave." He stepped over my scattered charts without looking at them. "Tomás Abarca was my uncle. He went up that mountain forty years ago and everyone said he died in the storm, and last week a fisherman saw a woman diving off the lighthouse point, and yesterday a navy lieutenant cleared a debt he had no reason to clear, and today I find out my uncle's body has been sitting in a hole in the rock for four decades while the whole coast pretended he never existed." The gun barrel trembled. So did his jaw. "So yes. I know what. Where is it?"

I looked at him — really looked — and saw what I'd missed in the first second. He wasn't a predator. He was prey that had learned to grow teeth. His hands were shaking not from nerves but from exhaustion; there were days of no sleep written under his eyes. And he'd said *uncle* like a man saying a prayer he'd stopped believing in.

"Heir, are you?" I said. "Come to collect your inheritance?"

"It's mine. It was always supposed to be mine. His letters—" He stopped himself, too late.

"What letters?"

"None of your business." He raised the gun higher, steadier now. "The compass. Last chance."

And there it was, the whole shape of my life laid out in a nine-foot cabin at two in the morning: a lieutenant with a signed debt release on one side, a grieving man with a loaded pistol on the other, and me in the middle holding something neither of them deserved — something that belonged, if it belonged to anyone, to a frozen man who'd climbed toward a summit and never come down.

Casals wanted it erased. This one wanted it cashed. Nobody, it seemed, wanted it *read*.

I thought about my grandfather's engraving on the case back. I thought about Casals saying *ask yourself why*. I thought about the tide going out impossibly far, the sea giving something back after forty years, like it had been waiting for someone too broke and too stubborn to look away.

"I'll tell you where it is," I said.

His shoulders loosened half an inch.

"On one condition. We go together — you, me, and whoever your uncle's letters say we need. Because that compass isn't money and it isn't evidence and it isn't yours or mine or the navy's. It's a map, Teo Abarca. And maps are only worth something to people willing to follow them."

He stared at me. The gun didn't lower.

"How do you know my name?"

"Lucky guess," I said, and reached slowly, slowly past him for the engine-room hatch. "Now put that thing away before I change my mind and let the lieutenant have you. He pays better than I do — but he buries what he finds. And I've had enough of this coast deciding what stays buried."

Outside, far down the harbor, the tide was turning. I could hear it against the pilings — coming back in, patient as a creditor, ready to erase everything the low water had given up.

We had until it did.
