## 3. A Team of Last Resorts

My uncle's compass sat between us on the table like a third negotiator.

"You could have shot me," Ivo Villalobos said. She was drying her hair with a rag that had probably wiped bilge an hour earlier, and she said it the way other people commented on weather. "You didn't. That's either decency or you need me more than I need you."

"Both." I turned the compass over in my hands. The brass was cold even after two days in my pocket. "You've been inside the cave. You know what's there besides him."

"I know what's there is forty years old and none of it pays my debts."

"Then why did you take it?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and I understood then what kind of person she was — the kind who answered questions honestly when lying would be easier, because honesty cost her something and she liked paying it. "Because it has our name engraved on the lid," she said. "Villalobos. Same as mine."

I hadn't known that. The engraving on my uncle Tomás's compass read *T. Abarca*, worn nearly smooth. But the maker's mark inside the case, the little stamped anchor-and-star, matched hers exactly. Two instruments from the same workshop, from the same expedition, carried by two men who had climbed together and come down apart — one of them dead in a sea cave with his boots still laced.

"So we're not strangers," I said.

"We're worse than strangers. We're family of the same crime." She tossed the rag aside. "I don't have money to hire anyone, if that's where this is going. I have a boat with a seized engine and eleven thousand in debt to men who break fingers alphabetically."

"What I have is a map." I opened the compass lid fully, past the needle housing, and pressed the hidden catch under the hinge. The back plate swung out, and there it was: a strip of silk, hand-inked, folded into eighths until it was thin as a knife blade. My uncle's handwriting crawled across it in brown ink — elevations, bearings, a route drawn in a climber's shorthand that only another climber would bother to decode. And at the top, circled twice, a mark I'd seen once before, in his letters to my mother: *the cache*.

Ivo leaned over it. Her breath fogged the table.

"That's the north face approach," she said quietly. "Nobody's done that route since—"

"Since them. Since the six who went up and the five who came down." I watched her face while she read. "The official story says weather. My uncle's letters say otherwise. He wrote to my mother every year for thirty years asking her to help him go back. She thought he was chasing grief. I think he was chasing proof."

"Proof of what?"

"That's what the mountain will tell us. Or the cache will." I folded the silk away. "I can't do this alone. Neither can you. So we find people who are as desperate as we are."

Ivo smiled without any warmth in it at all. "Desperate people are easy to find in this town. Good ones are expensive."

"Then we'll take bad ones."

* * *

Dr. Sunniva Rask lived above a fish smokehouse on the harbor's wrong end, in a room that smelled of creosote and old paper. Every horizontal surface held maps; every vertical surface held rejection letters, pinned up like trophies. I counted nine before she opened the door wide enough to see us.

"No interviews," she said. "No committees. No journalists. If you're any of those things, the stairs are behind you."

"We're climbers," Ivo said. "Sort of. We need someone who can tell us when the calving season opens the high route."

Rask went very still. It was the stillness of a woman who had been asked a question she'd spent ten years answering to empty rooms. "Who told you I worked on calving windows?"

"Your blacklist did. It's public record. 'Insists, against all observational consensus, that thermal anomalies in the upper icefield will open the col route weeks ahead of historical pattern.'" Ivo recited it flatly, from memory. "The consensus called you a crank. The consensus also hasn't been up there in a decade."

"The consensus has funding," Rask said bitterly. "Funding doesn't require being right. It requires agreeing with whoever signs the checks." She looked us over — Ivo in her salt-stiffened jacket, me with my uncle's compass chain showing at my collar — and I watched her decide something. "Why do you want the window? Nobody climbs the high route for fun. There's nothing up there but wind and the bones of the famous."

"There's a cache," I said. "Left by the Villalobos expedition. Forty years ago."

Her expression changed completely, the way ice changes when the sun hits it — same substance, entirely different light. "Six climbers," she said. "Five came down. I wrote my thesis appendix on their route. The glaciology of their disappearance has never made sense to me. The storm they claimed caught them wasn't in any record." She stepped back from the door. "Come in. Don't touch anything."

The room inside was a museum of one obsession. Ice cores photographed and labeled. Temperature series going back decades, hand-plotted. A wall chart of the upper icefield with the calving face marked in red ink, annotated so densely the glacier underneath had almost disappeared.

"If your anomaly holds," Ivo said, studying the chart, "when does the route open?"

"Eleven days. Maybe twelve." Rask said it the way a priest says a number that decides souls. "And it closes fast. Three days of crossing, four if the seracs are shedding. After that the face calves out and the col is a memory until next season." She turned to face us. "I'm not coming for treasure. I'm coming because if I'm right, I want to stand on the evidence. And if I'm wrong—" she shrugged, "—I'm already ruined. What's one more glacier?"

"You'll be wrong about the company, at least," Ivo said. "We're broke."

Rask almost smiled. "So am I. It appears we're forming a club."

* * *

Marisol Quen took more finding and less persuading, which surprised me until I understood her. We found her at the climbing gym on Ferrer Street — not training, but teaching children to tie figure-eights on a subsidy wage, her guide certification revoked, her name still spoken in the valley like a curse. *The guide who let Daniel Ossa fall.*

She saw us watching her from the bleachers and finished the lesson first. That told me something too. Whatever they said about her, she didn't abandon a rope mid-pitch.

Outside, in the alley that smelled of chalk dust and rain, she listened to our offer with her arms crossed and her jaw set like a man waiting for the punchline.

"You want a banned guide on a route that kills certified ones," she said. "Because I'm cheap."

"Because you're good," Ivo said. "I've seen your logbook. Before Ossa. You ran harder routes than anyone in the range, clean, for six years."

"And then a client died, and now I'm a story people tell to scare each other." Quen's voice didn't waver, but her hands, uncrossed now, were white-knuckled around each other. "You know what happened on that pitch?"

"Everyone knows the official version."

"The official version is that I misjudged the anchor." She laughed, short and ugly. "The anchor was fine. I placed it myself. What nobody asks is why Daniel Ossa — a banker, a man who'd never led anything harder than a boardroom — insisted on that route, that week, against my recommendation, and why he kept checking a phone he shouldn't have had on the mountain. Somebody wanted him up there. Somebody paid for him to be up there. And when he fell, somebody needed a guide to blame." She looked at me. "You're Abarca. Teo Abarca. Your uncle was on the Villalobos expedition."

"Yes."

"Then maybe you understand better than most how a dead man gets turned into a lesson." She was quiet a moment. "What's the pay?"

"Nothing," Ivo said. "Possibly ruin. Possibly prison, if a navy lieutenant named Casals catches us first."

Quen stared at her, then at me, and something in her face loosened — not trust, not yet, but the exhaustion of a woman tired of carrying her disgrace alone. "God," she said. "It's the worst offer I've ever had." She stuck out her hand. "When do we leave?"

* * *

We lit the fire on the shingle below the sea wall, where the harbor police didn't bother coming and the tide could take whatever sparks escaped. Four of us now: a debtor, an heir, a crank, and a ghost. Rask had brought a bottle of aquavit she claimed was medicinal. Quen had brought rope ends to practice knots, because her hands couldn't stay idle. Ivo had brought the boat's kettle and a tin of coffee older than the debt collectors hunting her.

I unrolled the silk map across a flat stone and weighted its corners.

The firelight made my uncle's ink lines glow like veins. We bent over it together, four heads, four shadows, and I talked them through it the way he must once have talked it through with his own team — the forest road to treeline, the scree gullies, the piton line the original six had fixed across the headwall, the col, the icefall, the summit shelter where the cache waited under forty years of frost.

"It's a suicide route in normal conditions," Quen said, tracing the icefall with one finger. "Even with your window, Rask, it's marginal."

"Marginal is generous," Rask said. "But the window is real. I'd stake what's left of my career on it."

"Which is nothing," Ivo said.

"Which is nothing," Rask agreed comfortably, and drank her aquavit.

Quen looked up at me across the fire. "What's in the cache, Abarca? Really. Not the story you tell yourself. If we're going to die on this route, I want to know what it's for."

I thought about lying. I thought about how my uncle had lied to my mother for thirty years, softly, protectively, and how it had gotten him nowhere except alone in a cave with his boots still laced.

"I don't know," I said. "Letters. Documents. Something worth killing six men over — or five, depending which version you believe. Something my uncle spent half his life trying to reach and never found the courage to climb for until it was too late." I fed a stick into the fire. "Whatever it is, it belongs to the families of the dead. All of them. Even the ones who came down."

Ivo was quiet for a long time. When I glanced at her, she was looking at the fire, and her hand had gone unconsciously to her pocket, where I knew her own compass sat — the twin of mine, the same maker's mark, the same engraved name. There was something she wasn't saying. I filed it away. On a rope, you learn to notice what your partner isn't telling you; it's usually the thing that kills you both.

"So we agree," Rask said into the silence. "Eleven days. Treeline by the second dawn, the col by the fifth, the crossing when the face opens. After that, no plan survives the mountain anyway."

"Agreed," Quen said.

"Agreed," Ivo said.

They all looked at me. I looked at the map, at my uncle's small brown handwriting circling the summit twice, and I felt the whole weight of the last forty years settle onto my shoulders like a pack I'd been fitted for at birth.

"Agreed," I said.

The tide was coming in. Beyond the sea wall, black water slid up the shingle toward our fire, patient as everything else in this country, and far inland — invisible, impossible, eleven days away — a glacier waited to open its one door.

None of us heard the footsteps on the sea wall above. None of us saw the cigarette ember flare and die in the dark, or the silhouette that watched us fold the map away, and turned, and walked off toward the naval station to make its report.
