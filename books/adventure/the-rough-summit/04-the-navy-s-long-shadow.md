## 4. The Navy's Long Shadow

Marisol Quen had spent eleven years reading weather the way other people read faces, and the sky over the harbor that morning wore the expression of a man deciding whether to lie to you. The storm was still two days out — she could feel it in the pressure behind her eyes, in the way the gulls had gone silent over the fish market — but the navy had already read it too, and they were moving.

She watched them from the deck of Ivo's boat, the *Salacia*, while pretending to check a mooring line she had no intention of touching. Two shore patrol jeeps at the head of the pier. A lieutenant with a clipboard talking to the harbormaster, who kept shrugging in a way that meant he'd been paid not to know anything. And a third man, civilian clothes, wrong shoes for the rain, standing where he could see the gangplank.

"They're not looking for smugglers," Marisol said quietly.

Ivo crouched at the open engine hatch, grease to her elbows, and didn't look up. "No."

"They're looking for us."

"I know." Ivo wiped her hands on a rag that was already blacker than her hands. "Engine's ready. We go tonight, before the front comes down. Casals thinks we'll wait out the storm like sensible people."

"And you're not sensible people."

"We're four people with nothing left to lose and a window that closes whether we're on the mountain or in jail." She stood, joints cracking, and finally looked up at the cliff road where the lighthouse leaned against the gray sky. "Sensible people don't find dead climbers in sea caves."

Teo Abarca came up the gangplank carrying two crates of supplies he'd bought with money Marisol suspected he didn't have, wearing the smile of a man who'd already spent the inheritance. Sunniva Rask followed him, arms full of instruments wrapped in wool socks, muttering about barometric drift.

"Patrols at the checkpoint," Teo said, setting the crates down. "Coastal road north. They're turning back every vehicle with climbing gear in it. My cousin saw them open a fish truck with a crowbar."

"Then we don't take the coastal road," Marisol said.

"The coastal road is the only road," Sunniva said.

Marisol looked at the three of them — the diver with debts, the heir with secrets, the scientist with a blacklist — and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her shoulders, the one she'd carried since the day they took her guide's license. Someone always had to be the one who said the true thing out loud.

"So we walk," she said.

* * *

They slipped out of port at 0300, running dark, no lights, no radio, the *Salacia* threading the breakwater on Ivo's memory alone. The storm caught them an hour early, as storms do when you've insulted them by planning around their schedule, and for six hours Marisol stood at the wheel while the sea tried to explain something urgent and personal about humility.

They put in before dawn at a fishing village whose name wasn't on the charts Ivo owned, a place of drying racks and dogs and one chapel with a leaning cross. From there the plan was simple: forty kilometers up the old shepherd tracks into the foothills, cutting inland ahead of the navy's net, rejoining the route at the high valley where the original expedition had staged its climb.

Simple plans, Marisol knew, were just complicated plans that hadn't met the terrain yet.

The terrain introduced itself on the second morning, when they crested a ridge above the coastal highway and saw the checkpoint.

It shouldn't have been there. It was twenty kilometers from anywhere the navy had jurisdiction to care about, a folding table, two vehicles, a canvas shelter, and six men in uniform stopping every car and truck on the only paved thread between the villages and the interior. Even at this distance Marisol could see the methodical boredom of it, men who had been told to stop everything and would stop everything until told otherwise.

"He knew," Teo said. He'd gone very still, binoculars pressed to his face. "Casals knew we'd come this way. He didn't even try to catch us at sea. He just closed the door behind us."

"He's not trying to stop us," Sunniva said slowly. She'd taken the binoculars and was studying the line of vehicles backed up at the table. "He's trying to slow us. Every hour we spend finding another way through is an hour off the calving window." She lowered the glasses. Her face, which usually looked like a woman arguing with data, looked frightened. "My models gave us nine days of margin. If he takes three of them—"

"Then we don't give him three," Ivo said.

But it took two. Two days of contouring south through country that wasn't meant for people — scree chutes, a river crossing that came up to Marisol's chest and nearly took Sunniva's pack with the instruments, a night huddled under a tarp while sleet hammered it like applause from an enemy. On the second evening they came down into a valley and found the road again, and found it patrolled, a jeep crawling the shoulder with its spotlight sweeping the verges, and they went back up into the dark without speaking, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't become an argument.

It was on the third night, in the lee of a granite wall with a fire small enough to hide under a cupped hand, that the thing Marisol had been watching for finally happened.

* * *

She'd seen it building for days, the way you see a serac lean a little further each morning. Teo had started checking his uncle's letters after every halt, reading them by firelight with his shoulders curved around the pages like a man guarding food. Ivo had started answering questions a half-beat late. And Sunniva had stopped talking about the ice, which for Sunniva was the loudest alarm there was.

"We're being honest with each other now, or we're not?" Marisol said it plainly, because she'd learned that the mountain punishes indirection more than it punishes error. "Because if we're not, I'd rather know tonight, above the snowline, than find out at the icefall."

Nobody answered. The fire ticked. Somewhere below them the jeep's light swept the valley floor, patient as a lighthouse.

"Fine," Marisol said. "I'll start. You all know what I did. Client died on my rope. License revoked, name ruined. What you don't know is that the client didn't die because of me. He died because Dario Fuentes paid a man named Ossian to swap my anchor kit. Ossian confessed to me two years later, drunk, crying, terrified. Fuentes works for Compañía Minera del Norte. The same company your uncle was investigating, Teo. The same company whose crimes are sitting in a cave under a lighthouse." She fed a stick to the fire. "They didn't ruin me because I failed. They ruined me because I succeeded, and the wrong person noticed."

Silence. Then Sunniva, quietly: "I knew about Ossian. I'm sorry. I should have said."

"You knew?"

"I testified at your hearing. You never saw my face. I was the glaciologist they discredited the following year." Sunniva's mouth twisted. "We were both casualties of the same company. I thought you knew. I thought everyone knew."

Marisol stared at her, recalculating eleven years of exile around this new fact, and decided — not painlessly, but decisively — that anger could wait until they were off the mountain.

"Your turn," she said to Ivo.

Ivo was looking at the fire. When she spoke, her voice was flat in the way voices go flat when someone has rehearsed a sentence until it stops hurting.

"There were two compasses in the cave."

Teo's head came up.

"One was in the dead man's hands. One was tucked in the lining of his pack. Same make. Same engraving. Villalobos family engraving." She reached into her jacket and set it on the stone between them: brass, green-crusted, older than any of them. "I told you I found a map. I didn't tell you how. The map is engraved inside the lid of the second compass. And I kept it. I've had it since the first night. Before Teo ever came aboard. Before any of you."

"Why?" Teo's voice cracked on it. Not rage — worse, bewilderment. "It's my uncle. It's my inheritance. You let me chase you across half the coast for a compass you already had."

"Because I needed to know what it opened before I handed it to anyone," Ivo said. "Including you. Especially you." She turned the compass over so the lid faced the firelight. "And because of the engraving, Teo. Look at it. That's my grandfather's mark. He made these. Two of them. Which means either he gave one to your uncle, or—" she stopped, and the sentence sat there unfinished, ugly, obvious.

"Or your grandfather was on that expedition," Sunniva said softly.

"Or my grandfather sent the man who was." Ivo picked the compass back up and closed her fist around it. "I don't know yet. That's why I kept it. Not to steal from you. To find out what my own blood did before it finds me."

The fire popped. Down in the valley the jeep's light swept past and swept back, and Marisol realized they'd all been watching it instead of each other.

"So," Teo said at last, and his laugh had no humor in it at all. "The diver, the disgraced guide, the banned scientist, and the liar. And we're trusting this team with our lives above the icefall."

"No," Marisol said. She stood, brushing frost off her knees, and looked at each of them in turn. "We're not trusting each other. Not yet. We're just the only people left who want the same truth. Trust is what we build on the way up — if the mountain gives us time."

Above them, far up the valley, the clouds tore open for a moment around a peak none of them had named aloud yet, white and indifferent, and Sunniva made a sound low in her throat.

"That's not right," she whispered. "That's not right at all. The cornice on the north ridge — it's already sloughing. The glacier's warming weeks ahead of schedule." She turned to the others, and the firelight showed her face stripped of everything but arithmetic. "Our window isn't shrinking. It's closing. We have maybe five days. And Casals knows it better than we do — that's why he's herding us, not catching us. He doesn't need to arrest us. He just needs us to be late."

Marisol looked up at the mountain, then at the three broken people around a hidden fire, and understood that the race had stopped being metaphorical somewhere in the last hundred meters of altitude.

"Then we don't sleep here," she said, and kicked snow over the fire.
