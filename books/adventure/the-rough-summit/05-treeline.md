## 5. Treeline

The forest ended the way a sentence ends — not all at once, but in fragments, the last stunted pines leaning away from the wind like listeners straining to hear something behind them. Sunniva Rask stopped at the edge of it and let the others pass her, because she wanted the moment whole: the green world closing at her back, the grey world opening ahead.

She had spent eleven years reading ice for a living and three years being told she read it wrong. The trees had never once lied to her. The ice had never once lied either; only people had.

"Scree starts here," Marisol said, twenty meters up the slope, testing a boulder with her boot heel. "We make the moraine by dark or we camp exposed."

"We'll make it," Ivo said. She always said that, in the flat voice of a woman who had decided something was true and would not be taking questions about it.

Sunniva followed, but slowly, because the ground itself had begun to speak.

She had noticed it first two days ago, below the checkpoint they'd slipped past on foot while Casals's patrol swept the coast road: a rusted piton, half-fused into a crack above a stream crossing, its eye corroded to lace. Then another, higher. Then a third with a sling still knotted through it, the webbing gone stiff as bark. Nobody had climbed this line in decades — not officially, not on any map she'd ever seen — and yet someone had climbed it *well*, placing gear with an economy that made her own hands itch with recognition. You didn't leave pitons like that unless you were good, and you didn't leave them behind unless you were in a hurry, or dead, or both.

Now, at treeline, the story got louder.

A cairn stood where the scree steepened, and it was wrong. Not wrong-built — beautifully built, each stone fitted like masonry — but wrong-*placed*, set back from the route as if the builders had stepped off their path to do it. Sunniva circled it while the others drank. It wasn't a trail marker. It was a grave.

Five names were cut into a flat slab wedged at its crown, the letters hand-chiseled, weathered soft. She read them twice, then a third time, mouthing them because the wind kept stealing them: *Halvorsen. Abarca. Duarte. Okonkwo. Lindqvist.* Five names. She looked down.

Six lines of boot tracks were pressed into the old snow patch beside the cairn, preserved under a lens of refrozen meltwater like insects in amber. Six distinct tread patterns. She counted twice, kneeling, breath fogging the ice, one glove off so she could touch it. Six.

"Sunniva." Ivo's voice, behind her. "What is it?"

"A grave," Sunniva said. "With five names." She stood, wiped her bare hand on her thigh. "And six sets of tracks walking away from it."

Nobody spoke. The wind filled the silence with the sound of the mountain breathing — a long, slow exhalation off the icefields above, carrying that mineral coldness that meant glacier, meant mass, meant time.

Teo came last around the cairn. He looked at the names for a long moment, and Sunniva watched his face do something complicated and quick, like a lock turning through tumblers. His eyes stopped on *Abarca* and stayed there.

"You knew," Marisol said. Not cruelly. Just flatly, the way she stated facts about rockfall zones.

"I knew there was a memorial," Teo said. "My family doesn't talk about the expedition. That's rather the point of my family."

"Then talk now," Ivo said. "Because we're standing on it."

Teo crouched, pulled a folded packet of oilcloth from inside his jacket — he'd been carrying it against his chest since the harbor, Sunniva had noticed the way he slept with one arm over it like a child guarding a toy — and unwrapped three letters, browned and brittle, written in a slanted hand. He held them without offering them.

"My uncle wrote these the summer before he left. To my mother. She hid them. I found them when I was nineteen and she was dying." He smoothed one open with exaggerated care. "He says here—" his finger moved along a line, and Sunniva saw his jaw tighten— "he says the sixth member of the team was going to turn them around. Weather window closing, and this person wanted to descend. He says the others voted."

"Voted on what?" Ivo asked.

Teo looked up at her, and for the first time since the night he'd put a gun on her in her own cabin, he looked young. "On whether to send him down alone."

The wind gusted. Somewhere above them, deep in the icefield, something settled with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"That's not what happened," Ivo said quietly. "That's not how the official record reads."

"The official record," Teo said, "was written by the four survivors. Who came home rich enough to never speak of it again." He folded the letters away. "I'm not telling you this to recruit you to my grief. I'm telling you because you keep asking why I'm here, and the answer is that my uncle's body is on that mountain, or his truth is, and I intend to bring one of them home."

Sunniva said nothing. She was watching the snow patch again, the six tracks, and thinking about the way evidence behaves when it's been waiting forty years for someone literate to walk past.

* * *

They made the moraine by dark, barely. Marisol found a wind scoop behind a granite fin and they dug in, four bodies and too little stove fuel, and Sunniva took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep yet. Her mind was doing what it always did at altitude — running models, running numbers, running the mountain forward in time.

From the col above them, she could see it: the calving face of the Sørbreen arm, a wall of blue-black ice two hundred meters high, holding shut the high route like a fist. In every published study — including hers, the one that had gotten her blacklisted — the seasonal opening came in late spring, a window of perhaps nine days when the glacier's margin retreated enough to expose the rock rib beneath. Her disgraced paper had argued the window was moving earlier and shrinking faster than anyone admitted. Her colleagues had called it alarmism dressed as data.

Tonight, through her binoculars, she watched the face shed a serac the size of a chapel. The crack of it reached them a full eight seconds later, rolling down the valley like artillery.

She did the arithmetic anyway. She couldn't help it. Melt degree-days, the warmth in the last week, the face's geometry — and the answer came out uglier than even her paper had claimed. Four days. Maybe five. After that the route wouldn't just close; it would close violently, the whole margin re-advancing in collapse after collapse, and anyone caught between the walls would become part of the stratigraphy.

Below her, Ivo stirred in her bag. Not asleep either, then.

"How bad?" Ivo asked, without opening her eyes. She'd learned Sunniva's watch-face well enough to read it.

"Four days to cross the icefall. Five if we're lucky and the weather holds." Sunniva lowered the binoculars. "It won't hold. There's a front coming off the interior — I can smell it."

"You can smell a storm."

"I can smell a storm, Lieutenant Casals can smell a promotion, and your grandfather's ghost can apparently smell us coming." She hadn't meant to say the last part. It came out anyway, the way things do at altitude, where the air is thin enough that filters fail.

Ivo opened her eyes. In the starlight her face was unreadable, which Sunniva suspected was a skill learned young and paid for dearly. "You think someone's following us."

"I think history is following us. Whether it's wearing navy boots or not, I couldn't tell you." Sunniva nodded back down the slope, toward the black seam of the forest they'd left. "But those pitons we passed today? Fresh traffic on top of old. Someone has climbed that line within the last season. And the grave — someone tended it recently. Cleared the stones. That's not forty years of neglect, Ivo. That's maintenance."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. "Casals knew what was in the cave before I did."

"Yes."

"He's not chasing us to arrest us. He's chasing us to finish something."

"Yes," Sunniva said again, and turned back to the glacier, because looking at Ivo's face while saying those things felt like handling evidence without gloves.

* * *

Dawn came up bruised and windless, which was worse than wind. Wind you could read. Stillness at altitude was the mountain holding its breath.

They broke camp fast, and the scree took them up in long switchbacks, Marisol setting a pace that was brutal and exactly right, and by midmorning they stood below the icefall itself — the Sørbreen's calving margin, up close now, so close Sunniva could hear it working. Not the dramatic collapses. The small sounds underneath: the tick and groan of ice under tension, the trickle of meltwater finding channels, the constant whisper of a structure rearranging itself toward failure.

"It's beautiful," Teo said, and he sounded ashamed of it.

"It's a clock," Sunniva said. "And it's already past noon." She pointed out the route as she'd mapped it in her head a hundred times: the rock rib exposed along the glacier's eastern margin, a corridor maybe thirty meters wide, narrowing at two points where seracs from the upper icecliff leaned over it like rotten teeth. "We go through tomorrow at first light. Coldest hour, most stable ice. Today we rest, eat everything, and check every rope twice."

"And if the window closes early?" Marisol asked.

Sunniva looked at the face of the glacier, at the blue light glowing out of its depths like something alive behind glass, and gave the only honest answer she had.

"Then the mountain keeps what's in that cache, and us with it."

They spent the afternoon in the strict, wordless economy of preparation — ropes coiled, crampons filed, harnesses checked by a second pair of hands. Sunniva checked Teo's knots and found them good, and found, tucked behind the tie-in point, the corner of an oilcloth packet. The same one from the grave. He'd transferred the letters to his harness.

"You climb with your grief," she said. "That's either very brave or very heavy."

"Both," Teo said. Then, after a moment, quieter: "There's something in the letters I haven't told them. Something my uncle promised my mother."

Sunniva waited. On the glacier, a serac the size of a bus leaned, groaned, and held.

"He wrote that if anything happened to him, she should look for the compass — that whoever carried the twin compass would know where the truth was buried." Teo's hands were very still on the rope. "Sunniva. Ivo's grandfather's name isn't on that grave marker. But my uncle's letters say the sixth man — the one who was sent down alone — was named Villalobos."

The wind arrived then, finally, out of the interior — cold, dry, smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet. Above them the icefall answered it with a long, descending note, like a struck bell, like a countdown.

Sunniva looked downslope, to where Ivo stood apart from the others, facing the glacier, her grandfather's compass — *the* compass — presumably riding in her pocket the way Teo's letters rode on his chest.

Two keys to the same lock, climbing the same mountain, neither of them knowing yet what the other half knew.

Tomorrow the ice would open. Sunniva only hoped the truth opened as cleanly — and closed more slowly.
