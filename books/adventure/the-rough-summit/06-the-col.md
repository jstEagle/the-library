## 6. The Col

The storm came down on them like a lid.

One moment Ivo could see the col ahead — a saddle of wind-scoured snow between two black shoulders of rock, the pass Sunniva had promised would carry them to the high glacier. The next, the world went white. Not fog, not cloud: a solid, howling nothing that erased the horizon, the sky, Marisol ten feet ahead with her rope taut between them. Ivo stopped walking because walking had become meaningless; there was no up anymore except what her boots told her.

"Snow cave!" Marisol's voice arrived as if through water. "Now — before we lose the slope!"

They dug into the lee of a cornice where the wind had already carved a hollow, and for two hours the four of them took turns with the shovels and the saw, cutting blocks, tunneling back into the drift until they had a chamber barely tall enough to kneel in, floored with packs and rope coils, sealed behind a wall of snow blocks with a vent hole Marisol kept clearing with a ski pole every twenty minutes.

Then there was nothing left to do but wait.

* * *

The first day was silence and small economies. Stove lit once, briefly, to melt water. Two fuel canisters left. Rations counted out in Marisol's palm like a miser's coins. The air in the cave thickened by degrees — four bodies breathing into a space the size of a grave — and Sunniva made them open the door block twice an hour even though each time the storm reached in and flayed them with cold.

It was Teo who broke the silence, on the second night, when the stove was dead and the dark was total except for the blue ghost-light through the vent.

"My uncle didn't die on the mountain."

Ivo heard it more than saw it — his voice coming from the direction of the wall, flat, rehearsed, like something he'd carried so long it had worn smooth.

"He told my mother he'd found something on the expedition. Something worth more than the summit fee. He wrote letters from the approach — six letters, one posted at each village. The last one said he'd be home rich enough to buy the boatyard back." A pause. The wind filled it. "The official report says he fell crossing the icefall. But the man who found him — a porter, the only witness — swore the rope above him had been cut. Clean cut, not frayed. The navy took the porter's statement and it never appeared again."

Marisol shifted in the dark. "You've known this since you were a boy?"

"I've known it since I learned to read his handwriting." Teo's laugh had no humor in it. "I spent eleven years and my family's money getting here. My mother thinks I came to scatter ashes. I came to find out who held the knife."

Nobody answered. In the compressed dark, the confession hung like breath.

* * *

It was Sunniva, inevitably, who turned the key another notch. She had been quiet too long, running numbers in her head the way she always did — Ivo could almost hear the abacus clicking behind her eyes.

"The calving window," she said. "I need to say this now, while everyone is calm and no one can walk away. The glacier will open early. Three days, maybe four after this storm breaks. And it will close fast. If we are not across when it opens, we winter here or we go home. There is no third option." A beat. "And there is something else. On the moraine yesterday — before the whiteout — I found a cairn that wasn't natural. Stacked stones, fresh-turned. Someone is behind us. Days behind, but moving well."

"Casals," Ivo said.

"Casals, or Casals's money. Either way." Sunniva's voice was level, scientific, terrible. "I'm telling you because whatever we decide in this cave, we decide knowing the clock has two hands. The weather is one. The men with the fresh cairns are the other."

The vent pole scraped. Cold air knifed in and out. Ivo pulled her knees tighter and felt the second compass against her sternum, inside her jacket, where it had ridden since the cave below the lighthouse. Her secret. Her inheritance. The thing she had not told them.

She was tired of it. It was heavy as ballast now.

"There's something you should know about me," she said. "Before anyone else says anything else true tonight."

* * *

She told them about the tide. About the wreck she wasn't allowed to name, the debt that owned her, the impossible low water that had peeled back the sea like a bandage and shown her the cave mouth beneath the lighthouse. The frozen climber in gear forty years out of date. The compass in his fist.

"And in his hand was a compass identical to mine," she said. "Same maker. Same engraving. Villalobos. My grandfather's mark — he engraved every instrument he ever sold with our name, it was his vanity, his signature." Her voice caught on the word *vanity* and she pushed through it. "I took it. I didn't tell Casals. I didn't tell any of you. I've been carrying it up this mountain like a thief carries a stolen candle."

"Why?" Marisol asked quietly. Not accusing. Just asking.

"Because I thought it was mine." Ivo laughed, and hated the sound. "Because my grandfather died when I was nine and left me nothing but stories and a diving mask, and here was a piece of him frozen in the rock, pointing at me. I thought the mountain was giving my family something back." She drew the compass out and held it up, though no one could see it. "Two compasses. His and mine. They were made as a pair — I know that now. He gave one to the expedition leader."

Silence. Then Teo, very carefully: "Who led the original expedition?"

Ivo closed her eyes. The answer had been sitting in her chest since the lighthouse, since the checkpoint, since the icefall, growing heavier with every meter of altitude like a stone swallowed whole.

"Emiliano Villalobos," she said. "My grandfather led them. Six climbers went up. Five names are on the marker at treeline. My grandfather came down."

The cave seemed to contract around the words. She heard Sunniva exhale slowly, the sound of a hypothesis confirming itself. She heard Marisol's rope creak as she sat forward.

"And your uncle," Ivo said to Teo, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, "was the sixth name. The one that isn't carved anywhere. My grandfather's expedition. My grandfather's rope. If Tomás Abarca was pushed off this mountain, then the order came from—" She couldn't finish it. She didn't have to.

* * *

The fracture happened the way fractures do: all at once, along a line that had been there all along.

Teo moved first. She heard him scramble upright in the dark, heard his head crack the ceiling of the cave, heard him swear and keep coming anyway, hands finding her shoulders in the black.

"You knew." His voice was shaking. "You climbed three days beside me and you knew and you said nothing—"

"I found out what it meant last night. I suspected since the checkpoint—"

"You *suspected*. You let me tell you about the cut rope. You let me pour it out like a fool while you sat on the name of the man who signed the order!" His fists were shaking against her jacket, not striking, just holding on, the way a drowning man holds a spar. "Give me the compass. Give me the map. I'll finish this alone."

"You'll die alone," Marisol said, sharp as a piton. "Sunniva just told us there are hired climbers on our trail and a window measured in hours. You want to cross the icefall solo with a vendetta in your pocket, be my guest. The crevasse field will love you."

"This doesn't concern you—"

"The hell it doesn't." Marisol's voice dropped, and in the dark it sounded older, wearier. "You think you're the only one the company touched? Sit down, Abarca. Since we're confessing."

A long, furious silence. Then the sound of him subsiding, packing himself back against the wall like a folded knife.

"Three years ago I guided a client named Elena Brandt up the south ridge," Marisol said. "She fell. Everyone said I anchored wrong. I lost my license, my clients, my name. What nobody printed — what I only proved last year — is that my anchor was cut too. Not frayed. Cut. By a guide named Ferro, who was paid by a land consortium to discredit me, because I was the one guide in the valley who kept refusing their access contracts." A pause. "Guess who chaired the consortium's environmental clearance board. Guess whose signature bought Ferro's silence."

"Villalobos," Sunniva said softly.

"Emiliano Villalobos." Marisol's breath came out ragged. "Your grandfather cost me everything, Ivo. Before either of us was born into this mess, he was already in the business of cutting ropes."

The vent pole scraped. Snow sifted down through the blue light like ash.

"So that's the arithmetic," Teo said at last, and his voice had gone cold and precise, which was worse than his anger. "My uncle's murder. Your ruined career. Whatever Sunniva lost her name over, I'd wager it traces back to the same mine, the same company, the same navy desk. All of it paid for and buried by the Villalobos generation." He turned toward Ivo, and though she couldn't see his face she felt his eyes. "So tell me why I shouldn't take the compass and leave you here with your blood."

"Because I'm not my grandfather," Ivo said. "And neither is the truth. It doesn't belong to your family or mine. It belongs to everyone they buried it from." She pressed the compass into his hands before he could refuse it — both of them, hers and the dead man's, the pair reunited in the dark. "Read the map. Lead us across. But we go together or the mountain takes us one at a time, starting with whoever walks out angry."

For a long moment the only sound was the storm hammering the mountain like a fist on a door.

Then Sunniva, practical as gravity: "The window opens in roughly sixty hours. We spend them resting, eating, and not killing each other. Agreed?"

No one agreed. No one objected. In a snow cave above the col, that was a treaty.

Ivo lay back against the packs and stared into the dark, listening to Teo's breathing slow beside her, the compasses whispering together somewhere in his jacket. Behind them, days down the mountain, men with fresh-cut cairns were climbing toward the same window. Ahead lay the icefall, and whatever her grandfather had ordered buried at the top of the world.

Sleep took her like deep water. Her last thought was of the tide going out below the lighthouse, giving something back — and of what else the sea might still be holding, waiting for its turn to speak.
