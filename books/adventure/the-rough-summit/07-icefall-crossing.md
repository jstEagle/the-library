## 7. Icefall Crossing

Marisol heard the glacier before she saw it open.

It came up through her boots first — a sound like a door being dragged open at the bottom of the world, deep and grinding and patient, as if the ice had been waiting forty years for someone to knock. She stopped on the moraine ridge and held up a fist, and behind her the line went still: Ivo with her eyes already on the ice, Sunniva with her head cocked like a bird listening for worms, Teo breathing hard and pretending he wasn't.

"There," Sunniva said.

The calving face of the glacier — a wall of blue-gray ice two hundred meters high, hanging over the valley like a wave that had forgotten how to fall — shuddered. A chunk the size of a cathedral broke free and dropped into the meltwater lake below with a boom that rolled up the valley and slapped back off the cliffs. The spray caught the morning light and hung there, a brief rainbow over ruin.

And where the seracs had been jammed together for decades, fused into an impassable rampart of leaning towers, the ice *moved*. Blocks the size of houses tilted apart. A gap opened along the old compression zone, dark and clean-edged, running from the toe of the glacier up toward the col in a jagged diagonal — exactly where Sunniva's models said it would be.

"Three days," Sunniva said quietly. "Maybe four. Then it pinches shut again until next season." She looked at Marisol, and there was no apology in her face, only arithmetic. "I told you the window would come early."

"You told us it might come early," Marisol said. "There's a difference between prophecy and weather."

"Everything is weather," Sunniva said. "Including us. We should move now, while the settling is still violent. The seracs will shed their loose blocks in the first hours. After that—" she shrugged, "—after that we're walking under loaded guns that only look quiet."

Marisol swept the route once more, the way she'd been trained to sweep a face before committing a client to it: anchors, runnels, the shadow lines where rockfall funneled, the pale scars of old avalanches fanned across the lower slopes. It was bad. It was very bad. It was also, for the first time since the map had come out of the compass lid, *possible*.

"Rope's short," she said. "We lost thirty meters to the crevasse field yesterday. I want tight spacing — ten meters, no more. Nobody moves unless the person ahead of them is moving. Ivo second, Teo third, Sunniva last with the science gear. If I say run, you run. Don't ask where. Don't ask why. Just run."

"And if you fall?" Teo asked.

Marisol checked the knot at her harness, not looking at him. "Then you'll have learned something about grief."

She started down the moraine before anyone could answer, because answers were a luxury the mountain didn't sell.

* * *

The icefall was worse inside than it had looked from the ridge, which was the one universal truth of mountains Marisol had never managed to unlearn.

They moved through a canyon of leaning blue walls, some of them taller than the lighthouse back in port, tilted at angles that made her inner ear argue with her eyes. Meltwater ran everywhere — underfoot, down the faces, dripping from overhangs with a ticking that sounded like a clock counting something down. The light came through the ice green and submarine, so that they climbed as if inside the wreck Ivo wouldn't talk about, as if the whole glacier were a drowned ship and they were salvaging their way through its ribs.

Twice the mountain spoke. The first time it was a crack like a rifle shot somewhere above them, and everyone froze except Marisol, who kept moving and hissed at them to do the same — *the ones that announce themselves are the ones you survive*. The second time there was no announcement at all. A serac the size of a bus simply let go three hundred meters upslope, slid, tipped, and shattered against another block with a concussion she felt in her teeth. The debris fan stopped forty meters from their line. Snow dust rolled over them, cold and strangely gentle, like the mountain patting them on the head before deciding whether to eat them.

"Faster," Sunniva said. Not panicked. Just factual, the way she said everything, and somehow that was more frightening than screaming would have been.

They were halfway across when Marisol saw the tracks.

Boot prints in the fresh snow dust, coming up their same line from the south side. Two sets. Crampon marks, modern, crisp-edged — cut within the last day, maybe less. Beside them, the twin dotted lines of trekking poles, spaced with the metronome discipline of people who did this for money.

"Casals," Ivo said behind her. She didn't say it loudly. She didn't need to.

"Hired climbers don't come up the icefall," Marisol said. "Too slow, too exposed. They'd take the east flank and drop onto our line at the col." But even as she said it she knew it wasn't quite true, because hired climbers who wanted to catch someone *before* the col would absolutely come this way, and the navy paid well enough that men would walk under falling ice to earn it.

"They're behind us," Teo said. His voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man holding something shut. "How far?"

"Half a day," Marisol said. "Less if they're good." She looked back at him, crouched on the ice with the wind pulling at his hood, and thought: *he knows how far. He's been counting all morning.* There was something in his pack he kept adjusting, something heavy he shifted from hip to hip every hour like a man with a stone in his boot he refused to name. She'd been a guide long enough to know what guilt looked like when it walked. She just didn't yet know whose guilt it was.

"Then we stop being careful," Ivo said, "and start being fast."

"No," Marisol said. "We stop being *dead*, and we stay fast. Those are different things." She turned up the line. "Move."

* * *

The crevasse took Teo an hour later, and it took him without ceremony.

One moment he was crossing a snow bridge Marisol had already tested with her axe — solid, she'd have sworn it was solid — and the next the world opened under him with a soft whumpf like a held breath released, and he was gone, and the rope snapped taut around her waist with a force that lifted her off her feet and dragged her toward the lip on her belly.

She got her axe in. Then her other axe. The snow here was rotten, granular, the consistency of wet sugar, and it gave away under her in handfuls. Ten meters of rope between them, and Teo's weight was not the weight of a small man. She felt herself going — felt the particular horrible patience of the ice, the way it doesn't fight you, just waits for you to tire — and she did the thing she had sworn never to do again since the client died, the thing that had ended her career and very nearly her sleep:

She chose.

"Ivo! Anchor!" she screamed, and let go with her right hand, and fed rope.

Because the choice was this: hold everything and lose everything, or give line, let him fall deeper but slower, buy seconds for Ivo and Sunniva to build a boot-axe anchor behind her. Every instinct screamed to lock the rope. Locking the rope meant both their weights on one rotten lip, and the lip was already crumbling under her chest like a sandcastle at tide's edge.

Ivo was fast. Faster than Marisol expected — no hesitation, no shouted questions, just the flat efficient violence of someone who'd spent years tying knots under water where mistakes killed you quietly. The anchor went in. The load came off Marisol's harness and onto the buried axes, and she lay there gasping into the snow, tasting copper, listening to Teo's voice rising thin and echoing out of the dark below her.

"I'm okay — I'm okay — my leg hit something — Marisol—"

"Don't talk," she called down. "Save your breath for climbing."

The radio was on her belt. She reached for it to call Sunniva forward, and her hand came away with the casing and a spill of dead batteries, and she understood in one cold instant what the drag of the fall had done — the radio had taken the impact against the ice, and the radio was finished. No calling the valley town. No weather updates. No telling anyone where they were if the mountain closed over them.

She looked at the broken radio, then at the hole, then at the rope running down into the blue dark, and she laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh, because the alternative was crying and she hadn't cried on a mountain since the funeral.

"Marisol?" Teo's voice floated up. "What's funny?"

"The bill," she said. "This crossing keeps sending me invoices." She racked her axes. "Prusik up. Now. While the sun's still on the upper wall — the ice moves when it warms, and I'd rather not fish you out twice."

He climbed. Slowly, badly, favoring the leg he'd banged, swearing in two languages, but climbing. And Marisol lay on the lip above him paying out slack and taking it back, hand over hand, watching his helmet lamp rise through the green dark like something surfacing from deep water, and she found herself thinking about the client. About the fixed line that had failed. About the inquiry and the ban and the four years of harbors and cheap boats and clients who'd heard the name Quen and gone elsewhere.

She had spent four years believing she'd chosen wrong on that mountain. Lying here, hauling a stranger's weight out of a hole because the arithmetic of survival demanded it, she understood for the first time that she hadn't chosen wrong. She'd chosen *at all* — which was the one sin the industry never forgave, and the one act that separated a guide from a witness.

Teo's hand came over the lip. She grabbed his wrist, hauled, and they collapsed together on the solid ice, breathing like bellows, close enough that she could see the blood on his temple and the wild animal gratitude in his eyes.

"You cut the rope," he said. "You could have locked it. You'd have held me."

"I'd have held you and the lip would have gone, and we'd both be in the hole arguing about it."

"You didn't know that."

"I gambled," she said. "That's different from knowing." She sat up, checked the rope — frayed badly where it had run over the edge, half its strength gone, another invoice — and began coiling it. "Don't make it mean something. I'd have done it for any of them."

"Liar," Teo said softly.

Marisol looked at him. He was looking back, and there was nothing soft in his face anymore — the gratitude had burned off like morning mist and left something harder underneath, something that had been waiting the whole climb for exactly this moment of debt and blood and honesty.

"Sunniva says the window closes tomorrow night," he said. "Casals's climbers are half a day behind. And there's something in my pack I should have shown you at treeline." He pulled the heavy bundle loose and set it between them on the ice. "My uncle's letters. All of them. Including the ones that aren't about treasure."

Above them, far up the glacier, the ice boomed again — the mountain clearing its throat, the window beginning to think about closing.

Marisol looked at the letters, then at the narrowing sky, and reached for the string.
