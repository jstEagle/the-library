## 8. The Cache

The shelter was smaller than Teo had imagined, a stone hut half-swallowed by the ridge, its roof caved in on one side like a skull crushed by a careful fist. Forty years of storms had scoured it. The door was gone; the doorway framed nothing but sky.

Marisol went in first, the way she always did, her ice axe held low and ready, as if the mountain itself might object to their arrival. Sunniva followed, then Ivo, and Teo came last because he had spent his whole life coming last — after his father's expectations, after his uncle's legend, after everyone who had ever decided what the Abarca name should mean.

Inside, the wind dropped to a whisper. Snow had drifted through the broken roof and lay across the floor in pale dunes. And against the far wall, wedged beneath a slab of granite that someone had rolled there with deliberate, desperate strength, was a metal ammunition box stenciled with a shipping company's logo that no longer existed.

Teo's legs nearly gave out.

Forty years. His whole life he had grown up inside the absence of Tomás Abarca — the empty chair at holidays, the photograph on the wall that no one would explain, the way his grandmother went silent whenever the mountains were mentioned, as if the range itself were a wound she refused to look at. *Your uncle died a hero,* they said. *The mountain took him.* And Teo, at seven, at twelve, at thirty, had kept asking the question no one wanted answered: *what was he carrying?*

He knelt beside the box. The granite slab weighed more than he did; it took all four of them, grunting, boots slipping on ice-glazed stone, to roll it aside. The sound echoed off the ridge and out over the white void beyond, and for a moment the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

Ivo put her hand on the lid. She looked at him — really looked, the way she'd started doing since the col, since the confessions, since everything between them had cracked open and refused to close again.

"Together," she said.

He nodded. He couldn't speak.

The hinges shrieked like something being born or dying. Inside: oilcloth, wrapped tight around bundles. Photographs, their edges curled but their images sharp as yesterday. Ledgers. Letters. And at the bottom, a document of perhaps ten pages, written in a hand that shook only once.

They spread it all out on the stone floor, kneeling in the snow-drifts like children at a grave.

The photographs first. Men in vintage climbing gear standing before a mine head — not the official mine, not the one with the company's smiling facade in the brochures, but a second entrance, up-slope, unmarked. Men loading drums that were not ore. Men shaking hands with naval officers whose faces Teo recognized from history books, from commemorative plaques, from the very uniform Casals wore. One photograph showed a tailings pond behind the ridge, and the creek running from it, and downstream — Teo's throat closed — the village where his grandmother had been born. Where the cancers had come from. Where they'd said it was bad luck, bad water, bad blood, anything but the truth.

"They weren't smuggling treasure out," Sunniva said quietly. She was holding a ledger, her glaciologist's fingers turning pages of numbers. "They were moving waste. Toxic tailings, dumped above the watershed. The company paid the navy to look away, and the expedition—"

"The expedition found out." Marisol's voice was flat, the voice she used on the mountain when the news was too bad for inflection. "That's why six went up and five came down."

Teo lifted the confession. The paper was brittle as moth wings. He read the first line aloud, because some things need air to be real:

*"We, the undersigned, members of the Abarca Expedition of forty years past, do swear what follows to be true..."*

Five signatures. Five men agreeing to carry the evidence down and give it to the world. And below them, in different ink, added later, a sixth account — written by whoever survived longest, by the last man alive on this ridge, describing how the order had come down from the company through a naval liaison, and how the expedition's own leader had been bought before they ever tied in.

Teo read the name of that leader and the world tilted.

*Emiliano Villalobos.*

He read it again. It didn't change. The signature beneath it was elegant, confident, the signature of a man signing away a life the way other men signed for packages. *Authorize termination of the sixth member upon descent. No witnesses. Compensation as discussed.*

"Teo." Ivo's voice, very small. "Teo, let me see."

He didn't want to give it to her. Every part of him that had bled for this — the gunpoint night on his boat, the checkpoint, the crevasse, the rope Marisol had sacrificed, the window closing over the icefall behind them — every part of him screamed to clutch the pages to his chest and never let a Villalobos touch them.

But he handed it over. Because that was the difference between him and them, maybe. Between him and his grandfather Emiliano, who had kept secrets to protect the family name.

Ivo read. He watched her face do what faces do when the ground opens: disbelief, denial, and then the long, sick settling of a person understanding that the blood in her veins was the poison. Her grandfather hadn't just been on the expedition. He had led it. Had sold it. Had ordered Tomás Abarca — Teo's uncle, the sixth member, the one who'd discovered the dumping and insisted on going public — pushed into a crevasse on the descent.

Not the mountain. Not bad luck. A man with a rope and an order.

"My grandfather killed your uncle," Ivo said. She said it plainly, the way you'd state an elevation or a weather report, because the alternative was to soften it and softness would be a lie. "And my family has spent forty years living on whatever they paid him."

Teo heard himself breathing. In the silence of the hut, under the broken roof, with the summit ridge glittering beyond the doorway like a blade, he felt the revenge he had carried since the harbor rise up in him, warm and sweet and terrible. Here it was. The inheritance. Not gold — better than gold. A name he could burn. A family he could ruin. All he had to do was turn to her and say the word, and Marisol and Sunniva would stand with him, and Ivo Villalobos would learn what it cost to be born on the wrong side of a signature.

"Ivo," he began.

And that was when the light changed.

* * *

It came through the doorway as a flicker — sunlight interrupted by movement on the ridge below. Marisol saw it first; she was always seeing things first. She moved without a word, pressing flat against the wall beside the doorway, axe reversed in her grip, and held up four fingers.

Four climbers. Roped in pairs, moving fast, too fast for the altitude unless they'd acclimatized for weeks — which meant they'd been on the mountain longer than the team had known. Which meant Casals had never been chasing them. He'd been herding them.

Teo stuffed the documents back into the box. Too late. The first climber ducked through the doorway, snow crusted in his beard, a navy-issue pistol riding openly on his hip, and behind him came three more filling the hut like a tide. The last one through was Lieutenant Casals himself, breathing hard but composed, his rank insignia catching the light, his eyes going immediately — hungrily — to the ammunition box.

"Well," Casals said. "You found it."

"You knew where it was all along," Ivo said. She rose slowly, placing herself between Casals and Teo. "The cave. The wreck. You've known for years. You just couldn't reach it without someone like us."

"Climbing is a young person's profession, Ms. Villalobos." Casals peeled off a glove and warmed his hands at nothing. "I needed divers, guides, a glaciologist, and a grieving nephew. And now, generously, I need none of you." He nodded to the box. "Here is my final offer, and it is a good one. There are five of you and there is money enough for ten lifetimes. The company's successors still pay for discretion — generously. Burn the papers, keep the photographs if you must have souvenirs, and walk down rich. Or refuse, and walk down as the six names on the next cover-up. I've done this before. I'm rather good at it."

Silence. Wind worried at the broken roof.

Teo watched each of them weigh it. He saw it honestly, even in Sunniva — the flicker of a woman whose career, whose reputation, whose blacklisted name could be restored with a signature. Even Marisol, whose ban could be lifted, whose dead client could be avenged more cheaply with money than with truth. He felt the offer land in his own chest too, because his uncle was still dead either way, and the money would buy the boat, the freedom, the end of debt—

No. That was the lie talking. That was Emiliano Villalobos's voice, dressed in his own.

Casals turned to him last, deliberately last, the way a surgeon saves the crucial cut. "Señor Abarca. Your uncle's killer's granddaughter is standing right there. Everything you climbed for is in that box, and she shares the blood that signed the order. Take the money. Take it *from* her, if it helps. Revenge and a fortune in the same envelope. Where else in this world will you find such a bargain?"

Every eye came to Teo. Ivo didn't flinch. That was the thing about her — she stood there and let him look at her, let him hate her if he needed to, offering herself up as the price of the truth without asking for mercy.

Teo thought of his grandmother going silent. Of the village downstream of the tailings pond. Of the empty chair. And he thought of the col, and the rationed air, and Ivo giving up her own oxygen line to Marisol without a word, and the rope in the icefall, and the way Sunniva had laughed for the first time in years two nights ago, and how none of that — *none of it* — had anything to do with blood.

He picked up the confession. Held it where Casals could see the signature.

"This isn't yours to buy," Teo said. "And she isn't yours to divide me from. My uncle died so this would come down the mountain. So it comes down the mountain."

Casals sighed, genuinely regretful, the actor to the end. "Then we're finished here." He stepped back into the snow and raised his voice to his climbers. "Gentlemen. The documents stay. The witnesses—"

The tallest climber, the bearded one, looked at the photograph in Sunniva's hand — the one showing the tailings pond, the poisoned creek, the village — and something in his face closed like a door. He was from those valleys. Everyone on this mountain was from those valleys.

"Lieutenant," he said quietly, "I signed on to retrieve papers. Nobody said anything about witnesses."

The pistol stayed holstered. The wind rose. And on the ridge outside, Teo saw the clouds boiling up from the west, black and total, the storm Sunniva had promised arriving hours early — sealing the summit, sealing the route down, sealing all nine of them together on top of the world with the truth in a steel box and nowhere left to run but through each other.
