## 9. Rough Summit

The storm found us at the col below the summit ridge, and it did not come gently. It came the way debts come — all at once, after years of pretending they wouldn't.

Wind screamed off the ice cap and funneled through the notch where we'd sheltered, tearing at the tent fabric with fingers of grit. Above us, maybe four hundred vertical meters, the old expedition shelter sat buried to its eaves in rime. Teo's uncle had described it in his letters: *a stone hut like a knuckle of the mountain itself.* Forty years of weather hadn't knocked it down. Nothing up here stayed down easily, except people.

"We go now or not at all," Marisol shouted over the wind. She was coiling rope with hands that didn't shake anymore, not since the icefall, not since she'd hauled Teo out of the blue dark by sheer refusal to let go. "Casals's climbers are two hours behind us at most. Sunniva?"

Sunniva pressed her bare palm flat against the rock, reading it the way other people read letters from home. "The window holds until dusk. After that the storm seals everything for a week." She looked up, snow crusting her eyebrows, her eyes calm as deep water. "Dusk is our whole life, compressed."

"Then we spend it well," I said.

We roped in pairs — Marisol leading, me second, then Teo, then Sunniva — and climbed into the white throat of the storm.

* * *

The ridge was a knife's edge of black rock and wind-polished ice, dropping away on one side into cloud and on the other into nothing we could name. Visibility shrank to the length of an arm. The world became sound: the wind's long howl, the creak of our own breath freezing in our collars, and beneath it all — faint, rhythmic, wrong — the crack of gunfire.

Not at us. Not yet. Somewhere below on the glacier approach, muffled by the storm, someone was shooting at the mountain, or at each other, or at ghosts.

"They're close," Teo said against my ear. His face was a pale smudge inside his hood. "Ivo. If they take the cache—"

"They won't." I said it like a fact because I needed it to be one. "It's ours. We earned every meter of it."

Marisol held up a fist. We stopped. Through the murk ahead, a shape resolved — angular, man-made, furred with forty winters of ice. The shelter. Its door hung open, half its hinges gone, swinging in the wind with a slow iron groan like something breathing.

I went in first.

Inside, out of the worst of the wind, the air was still and cold enough to ache. Our headlamps swept across stone walls scrawled with names and dates in carbon pencil — the original six, signing their presence into the mountain like children carving initials into a desk. *E. Villalobos. 1979.* My grandfather's handwriting, confident and looping, the same hand that had signed my childhood birthday cards.

And there, beneath a collapsed shelf in the corner, wedged behind a rusted tin marked FUEL in peeling paint, a canvas satchel, frozen stiff as a board.

Teo made a sound I'd never heard from him — small, cracked, younger than his years. He knelt and touched the canvas with both hands, the way you touch a coffin lid.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's everything he died for."

That was when the light hit us from the doorway.

* * *

Three headlamps, blinding after the dark. Behind them, silhouettes: broad-shouldered, roped together, rifles slung across their chests with the casualness of men who'd carried them so long the weight had become part of their posture. Hired climbers. Casals's wolves.

And stepping between them, ducking under the lintel, Lieutenant Casals himself — frost on his collar, breath ragged from the climb, but his eyes as level and patient as they'd been across the table in his office, offering to erase my debt.

"Ivo Villalobos." He said my name like a receipt. "You've cost me a great deal of trouble."

"You should have let the tide keep what it took back," I said.

He smiled without warmth and gestured, and his climbers spread along the walls, cutting the shelter into territories. One of them — a big man with a gray-shot beard and the sunburned squint of a lifetime at altitude — let his gaze drift across the carbon-pencil signatures on the wall. He stopped at *E. Villalobos. 1979.* Something moved behind his eyes.

"Sunniva Rask," Casals said pleasantly, nodding to her. "Dr. Quen. Señor Abarca." Then, to me: "The satchel. That's all. You walk down this mountain debt-free — all of you. Every record wiped. Cash, if you want it. There are men who would kill for this offer, and I'm told I've already met several of them."

"The documents are evidence of murder," Sunniva said quietly. "Six people. You'd have us sell six lives for a clean credit line."

"I'd have you sell *paper* for your lives," Casals corrected. "The difference matters. Paper burns. People are inconveniently durable." He extended his gloved hand toward Teo. "Your uncle was a fool, Abarca, but he was a fool with excellent instincts. Don't inherit them."

Teo stood slowly. I could see the war in him — I'd been seeing it since the col, since the confessions in the snow cave, since the night he'd put a gun in my face on the dock and called it inheritance. Revenge lived in him like ballast. And here was the man whose navy had buried his uncle twice: once in ice, once in lies.

But Teo looked at me instead. Just looked. And whatever he saw — the granddaughter of the man who'd signed the death order, standing between him and the satchel with no right to be there except that she'd climbed every meter beside him — settled something in his face.

"No," Teo said. "I'm done inheriting things from dead men. I'm keeping this one alive instead." He lifted the satchel and held it against his chest. "It goes public. All of it."

Casals sighed, and the pleasantness drained out of him like water through scree. "Then you leave me no arithmetic to do but one kind."

His hand dropped to his rifle.

* * *

What happened next happened fast and badly, the way violence always does at altitude, where the thin air makes everyone stupid and slow.

The gray-bearded climber spoke first — one word, low: "*Jefe.*" Not agreement. Question. He was still looking at the wall, at the signatures, at the tin satchel now open in Teo's arms, photographs spilling loose across the stone floor. Faces stared up from the images, lit by our headlamps: six climbers grinning into a camera older than any of us. A ledger page. A letter with a company letterhead. A body half-buried in snow, photographed by whoever killed it, because killers keep receipts too.

The gray-bearded man read them upside down from three meters away. Climbers read terrain; give one a document and he'll read it just as fast.

"These are the six," he said. "The ones from the official report. The ones who *turned back*." He looked at Casals, and his rifle drifted, very slightly, away from us. "Nobody turned back."

"Pick it up," Casals snapped. "That's an order."

"From who?" the climber said. "You're not navy anymore, jefe. You're a man in a shed on top of the world holding a gun over paper. I don't die for paper."

The second climber unslung his rifle and set it down against the wall, deliberate as laying a tool aside at the end of a shift. The third followed. Three rifles leaning against stone like exclamation points at the end of a sentence nobody would finish.

Casals stood alone in the lamplight, and I watched forty years of career collapse through his face — the cover-up that built him, the promotions stacked on silence, the whole architecture of his life balanced on six bodies in the ice. He'd climbed all this way not to destroy the evidence, I understood suddenly. He'd climbed it because he couldn't stand for it to exist anywhere he wasn't holding it. Power hoards even its crimes.

"Give it to me," he said, and his voice cracked on the last word, and that crack was the truest thing he'd ever said to us.

He lunged.

For the satchel — for the photographs sliding across the floor — for the whole buried truth lying loose in the open air. Teo twisted away. Casals grabbed for the papers themselves, snatching at a photograph of the sixth climber, the murdered man, young and laughing, and the photograph skated under his boot on the icy threshold stone.

The door. The open door, the broken hinges, the wind waiting outside like a hand.

He went through it sideways, arms full of nothing, eyes fixed on the single photograph spinning away across the snow beyond the threshold — and the mountain simply declined to hold him. No drama. No scream that lasted. The ridge gave him to the storm the way the tide gives things back, only in reverse, only keeping.

The photograph tumbled after him, white on white, gone.

Silence, except the wind and the iron groan of the door.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then the gray-bearded climber picked up his rifle, slung it, and looked at me. "Señora. Whatever's in that bag — make it loud. Make it so loud they can't bury it again." He jerked his chin at his companions. "We were never here. Neither was he." He glanced once at the empty doorway, and there was no grief in it, only the flat accounting of a man who'd finally seen the books.

They walked out into the storm, roped together, going down.

* * *

We sealed the door as best we could with the broken hinge and a length of piton wire, and while Marisol got the stove coughing and Sunniva chipped rime from the window slit, Teo laid the contents of the satchel out on the stone floor, item by item, like a surgeon laying out instruments.

Ledgers. Company correspondence. A naval memo stamped CLASSIFIED, routing orders through channels that ended at a desk my grandfather had occupied. And last, folded into a oilcloth wrap gone brittle with age, eleven pages in a careful, looping hand I knew better than my own.

My grandfather's confession.

I read it alone, by the stove, while the others slept in shifts. I owed it that much privacy — his and mine.

*We told ourselves the mountain took him,* it began. *Mountains take people. It is the one murder no one investigates.*

Page by page, in his tidy birthday-card handwriting, he laid it out: the mining survey the sixth member had stumbled onto, the tailings poisoning the river valleys, the company's payments, the navy's protection, the order given at base camp in a voice so quiet it might have been a suggestion. *I did not push him,* he wrote. *I signed the paper that pushed him. I have decided, at the end of my life, that this is worse.*

He'd written it before the final ascent. He'd known. He'd climbed up here anyway to hide it where only the desperate would ever find it — and then, I think, hoping someone desperate enough would someday come.

I sat with the pages until the stove burned low, and I felt the shape of the choice he'd left me, the same shape Casals had lunged for off the edge of the world. Inheritance. Silence, handed down like a watch. I could burn these pages and be only a granddaughter, debt-free, unnamed. Or I could carry them down — carry *him*, all of him, the killer and the confessor — and let the world weigh what the family never could.

Outside, the storm began to slacken, as Sunniva promised it would. Through the window slit, the sky was breaking into cold stars above the ridge where Casals had fallen.

I folded the confession along its old creases and put it inside my jacket, against my heart, where shame belongs.

"Dawn in three hours," Sunniva said softly from her bunk, awake after all. "The window closes tomorrow night. Whatever we're going to do with the truth, Ivo — we do it fast."

Below us, far down in the dark, a valley town waited with a signal tower and a working phone line, and forty years of silence waited to end.
