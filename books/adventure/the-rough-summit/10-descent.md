## 10. Descent

### Ivo

The mountain let them go the way a fist opens — slowly, finger by finger, as if reluctant to admit it had ever held anything at all.

They came down through the closing window with the storm chewing at their heels. Sunniva called the timing like a woman reading her own pulse: *two hours, maybe three, before the icefall seals.* They moved without speaking. There was nothing left to say that hadn't been said at eight thousand meters over the bodies of two hired climbers who had looked at the photographs in the cache and simply sat down in the snow and refused to be part of it anymore.

Ivo carried the documents against her chest, inside her jacket, wrapped in the oilskin Teo's uncle had used forty years ago to wrap the same pages for the same descent that never happened. The weight of them was nothing. A kilogram of paper. It felt like carrying a corpse down a mountain, and she supposed in a way she was — six corpses, forty years dead, finally going home.

Casals's body stayed on the ridge. The climbers who had survived — Voss and the quiet one, Adeyemi — helped them lower him into a crevasse rather than leave him for the birds, and they did it without being asked, and when it was done Adeyemi said only, "We were told you'd burn it. He said you'd all burn it for money." He looked at Ivo with something that wasn't forgiveness yet but might become it. "Nobody told us what was *in* it."

"No," Ivo said. "They never do."

Below the icefall the world turned green again, absurdly, insultingly green after three weeks of white. Marisol led them through the treeline by routes she knew from some earlier disgrace, avoiding the checkpoint towns, moving at night along logging cuts until the valley opened and there was light — electric light, the ugly orange glow of a place with cell towers and a post office and a bar where nobody knew their faces.

Sunniva found the signal first. She stood in the middle of the road holding her phone above her head like an offering, and when the bars appeared she made a sound Ivo had never heard from her — a laugh, cracked and rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"Signal," Sunniva said. "God. Actual signal."

They looked at one another in the sodium light outside the closed bakery. Four people who had climbed out of the world together and come back down into it carrying the thing that would unmake it.

"Not here," Marisol said quietly. "Not standing in the street. Somewhere warm. Somewhere we can think for five minutes before we end our lives."

Teo almost smiled. "You make it sound like a funeral."

"It is," Marisol said. "Ours. The people we were last month are dead. We're just the ones who have to keep walking around afterward."

* * *

### Teo

He uploaded everything from the back booth of a bar called the Faro, where the owner was too tired and too drunk to care about four filthy climbers with a laptop and a satellite file.

Forty years of his life had pointed at this moment, and now that it was here, Teo found he couldn't feel it. His hands shook too much to feel anything. He'd imagined vindication as a kind of warmth. It wasn't. It was cold and enormous, like the col itself — a place so high there wasn't enough air to breathe in it.

The confession. The photographs of the tailings pond, the dead fish, the village clinic's records of the poisoned children. The navy correspondence, stamped and signed, ordering the sixth member of the expedition — his uncle Tomás — *"removed from the record."* And beneath that signature, in handwriting Teo recognized because it matched the engraving on a compass he had once held at gunpoint over a stranger: **E. Villalobos, expedition leader.**

His grandfather. Her grandfather. The same man.

He pressed send.

The files went up in pieces — to the national archive, to three newspapers, to a journalist named Ondine Kastel who had spent nine years being laughed out of rooms for asking about the vanished expedition. To the families. All the families, including the ones who had paid him to find the cache quietly and burn it quietly, including his own mother, who had told him since childhood that his uncle died a hero on the mountain and that some questions were a form of disrespect.

The progress bar filled. Sent.

Teo set the phone face-down on the table and discovered that Ivo was watching him across the booth, her hands wrapped around a coffee she hadn't touched.

"You could have kept your name off it," she said. "Redacted yourself. Nobody would have known Villalobos signed the order if you hadn't put my name next to his."

"I know."

"Why?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to her had stopped working somewhere on the icefall, somewhere between the crevasse and the rope going taut and Marisol's arm hauling him out of the dark.

"Because a redaction is just a smaller silence," he said. "And I've been living inside my family's silence my whole life. I'm not building another one. Not even a kind one."

Ivo nodded slowly. Then she reached into her jacket and set both compasses on the table between them — hers and the dead man's, twin instruments, twin engravings, the whole poisoned inheritance in brass and glass.

"Then we do this properly," she said. "Neither of us keeps them."

She slid them toward the center of the table, side by side, the needles swinging and settling, pointing north, pointing nowhere, pointing home.

### Sunniva

The collapse took eleven days, which was faster than any glacier Sunniva had ever modeled, and she watched it happen the way she watched ice calve — with professional admiration and personal grief.

The navy tried to contain it first. Of course they did. There was a statement about *fabricated documents of unknown provenance,* and then Casals's death became a *training accident during a high-altitude exercise,* and then a junior officer in the coastal command did the arithmetic on her own career and decided she'd rather survive than stay silent, and leaked the internal review that showed Casals had diverted salvage assets to seal a sea cave under a decommissioned lighthouse.

After that it was water finding the crack. The mining company's stock lost half its value in a week. Two ministers resigned. The old expedition's logbook — the real one, recovered from the cache — ran serialized across every front page in the country, and Sunniva read each installment in the Faro's back booth with the strange vertigo of a scientist watching her own hypothesis confirmed at scale: the mountain had kept the record perfectly. Cold preserves. That was the whole truth of glaciers, the first thing she'd ever loved about them. Cold remembers.

Her own rehabilitation arrived by email, absurdly, from the university that had blacklisted her. *In light of recent events validating your seasonal projections, the department would like to discuss—*

She deleted it.

"You're not going back?" Marisol asked, reading over her shoulder with the shamelessness of a woman who owned no boundaries.

"They didn't want the science," Sunniva said. "They wanted to be seen agreeing with it. That's not the same job." She turned the phone off. Outside the window, the range stood white and indifferent against a hard blue sky, and she felt the oddest peace. "Besides. I've got fieldwork."

Marisol raised an eyebrow. "What fieldwork?"

"The cave," Sunniva said. "Under the lighthouse. The tide exposed it once; it'll expose it again. There's a preserved microclimate in there — forty years of atmospheric data in the ice formations, the body's tissue, the gear. It's a time capsule. Nobody's studied it." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like a muscle remembered from before. "Somebody should. Somebody who isn't trying to erase it."

* * *

### Marisol

The debts dissolved in scandal rather than payment, exactly as Ivo had predicted, and Marisol discovered that being legally resurrected was its own kind of disorientation.

The guide certification board reinstated her license with a letter full of words like *regrettable circumstances* and *in light of new evidence regarding the conduct of third parties.* The rival guide who had sabotaged her ropes — paid, the evidence showed, by the same company, the same web, the same long gray tentacle reaching up the mountain — was arrested in a city Marisol had fled through ten years ago with a dead client's name on her and nothing else.

She read the news standing at the harbor rail, and she waited to feel free, and what she felt instead was the specific grief of a person whose prison door has been opened and who realizes the walls had become load-bearing.

Ten years she had built her life around that disgrace. Who she talked to. Where she worked. How far from the mountains she allowed herself to want things. And now the reason was gone, and underneath it there was just — her. A woman of fifty-one with bad knees and good hands, standing in a harbor town, unowned.

Ivo found her there. She always seemed to know where people were; Marisol suspected it was the diver in her, the habit of tracking bodies in low visibility.

"So," Ivo said, leaning on the rail beside her. "The board wants you back."

"The board wants a press release," Marisol said. "With legs."

"And?"

Marisol watched a fishing boat come in, low and heavy with the morning's catch, gulls screaming behind it like debt collectors.

"And I'm taking clients again," she said. "But not theirs. Mine. Small teams. People the big outfits won't touch — beginners, old-timers, kids with more nerve than sense." She glanced sideways. "A team of last resorts."

Ivo huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Is that what we were?"

"We were what happens when last resorts stop being last," Marisol said. "It's rarer than you'd think. Most of them stay last." She straightened up. "First climb's in June. Sunniva's already volunteered to route-find. Says the high glacier will be stable till August — she's *very* confident, don't get her started."

"And Teo?"

"Teo's writing a book." Marisol shrugged. "About his uncle. About all of them. He asked me—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes that Marisol chose not to name aloud, "—he asked me to check his mountaineering chapters. For accuracy."

"For accuracy," Ivo repeated, deadpan.

"Don't," Marisol said, and Ivo grinned, and the grin made her look twenty years younger and thirty years lighter, and Marisol thought: *there she is. The one under the debt.*

* * *

### Ivo

She went back to the lighthouse in October, alone, because some doors you have to walk through by yourself or they never really close.

The navy's tape was still strung across the cliff path, yellowed and flapping, though everyone official had long since stopped caring. Below it, the tide was doing what tides do — nothing remarkable. Mid-range, unhurried, the water sliding over the rocks where the cave mouth hid, giving back nothing at all.

That was the thing she'd come to understand, standing there with salt wind in her teeth: the sea had never given her anything. Not the wreck, not the coins, not the compass in the frozen man's hand. The tide only *returned* — and returning is not the same as giving. Everything it brought back belonged to someone else, someone dead, someone drowned, someone pushed. Her whole salvage career had been a career of other people's losses, and she had told herself it was honest work because the debts were honest debts.

The debts were gone now. Dissolved in scandal, wiped clean by the collapse of the men who'd created them — and it turned out that owing nothing felt exactly like she'd always feared having nothing would feel: light, and slightly terrifying, and entirely hers.

Both compasses were gone. She and Teo had buried them together on the ridge below the summit shelter, in a cairn of their own making, beside a second marker they'd cut from driftwood and carved with six names instead of five. Let the mountain hold them. The mountain had always been better at keeping things than any of them.

Her boat was still moored in the harbor, debt-free, and she'd sold the salvage rig — the tanks, the lift bags, the sonar — to a young diver who reminded her uncomfortably of herself, and whom she'd warned, at length, about the difference between returning things and keeping them.

Behind her, boots on the gravel path. She didn't turn. She knew the walk — four sets of footsteps, actually, arriving in loose formation the way they'd learned to move on the mountain, spread out enough to survive a fall, close enough to reach.

Sunniva first, breathless from the climb, already talking about ice cores and sampling permits. Then Marisol, saying something dry that made Sunniva laugh. Then Teo, quieter, carrying a thermos of coffee he'd pour into four cups without asking who wanted any, because that was who he'd decided to be.

Ivo watched the tide slide over the hidden cave mouth, smooth and silver and empty-handed.

"Nothing today," she said, mostly to herself.

"What's that?" Marisol called.

Ivo turned away from the water and faced them — her ragged, disgraced, impossible team, her inheritance, the only fortune she'd ever keep.

"Nothing," she said, and meant it completely, and smiled. "Let's go home."
