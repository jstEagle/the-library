## 1. The Door the River Opens

The Marguerite came downriver on the last of the ebb, riding low and quiet, her paddle wheel turning with the patience of a thing that had learned not to hurry. Sable Brightwater stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the brass rail worn smooth by nine years of other hands' sweat, though only theirs, really — Pip's and theirs, back and forth, season after season — and watched the water do what water always did: carry everything downstream and tell nothing.

Nine years of this. Nine years of mooring shallow where a deep berth would hold better, of casting off before dawn while the dockside lamps still burned, of paying in coin rather than credit because credit left a trail. Stopping let the past catch up. Sable had learned that lesson early and paid for it dearly, and whatever the tuition had been, they no longer remembered it clearly — only that they'd paid.

"Bar's coming up," Pip called from the bow, his voice thin over the water. He was nineteen and all elbows, leaning out past the jackstaff with a lead line swinging. "Six fathoms. Five and a half."

"I see it." The bar showed itself as a long brown seam across the river's skin, the current folding over it like cloth over a knee. Sable eased the throttle down and took them across at an angle, feeling the Marguerite's hull shudder once, gently, as if clearing its throat. Below them lay forty feet of silt and old wrecks and whatever else the river kept and never surrendered.

They made Grayharbor Landing by late afternoon, and Sable did what Sable always did: tied up short, in two feet less water than the boat needed to float comfortable, bows nosed into the shallows where the mud sucked at the hull like a hand reluctant to let go. A shallow mooring meant an easy departure. An easy departure meant leaving was already half done before there was anything to leave from.

"We could stay the night," Pip said, coiling lines. "There's a dance at the pavilion. Marta's cousin's playing the fiddle."

"You can go to the dance."

"You don't ever go anywhere."

Sable looked at him — this boy they'd fished out of a flood six years ago, who had grown up on the deck of a boat that never stayed — and said nothing, which was answer enough. Pip's mouth flattened. He knew the shape of their silences by now, knew which ones were doors and which ones were walls.

* * *

It happened near slack water, when the tide did something it had not done in living memory.

The almanacs had promised an extreme spring tide — the moon full and close, the river swollen with mountain melt pushing against it from upstream and the sea dragging from below — but almanacs promise, and rivers decide. What decided, that evening, was to drain. The water fell and kept falling, past the low-water marks cut into the pilings, past the marks nobody alive had seen exposed, until the riverbed itself stood naked in the failing light: ribbons of gray mud, sunken snags like the ribs of drowned cattle, and the stink of a century of secrets breathing out all at once.

And there, below the bluff at the channel's bend, where the water had always been too deep even for the soundings charts to guess — a door.

Not a door built of planks or hung on hinges. A door carved into the cliff's root, an arch of black stone maybe thirty feet high, its threshold still knee-deep in retreating water, and above it, cut deep enough to have been made before the stone went dark with age, a mark: a circle broken by a single vertical stroke, like an eye that had been closed on purpose.

Pip came running along the deck, lead line forgotten in his fist. "Captain. Captain, look at the bluff —"

"I'm looking."

"That's the Drowned Door." His voice dropped as though the word might cost him. "My gran used to tell it. Cave under the river. Says it admits no one free — you go in, you pay. She wouldn't say pay *what*. Just that folk come out missing pieces."

"Folk say a lot of things."

But Sable was looking at the mark above the arch, and something cold was walking up their spine on small deliberate feet. They knew that mark. Not from stories. From their own body — a scar behind the left ear, thin and curved, that they'd carried as long as they could remember carrying anything, whose origin no doctor had ever satisfactorily explained and whose shape Sable had never been able to see until now, tracing it blind with a fingertip a thousand times, guessing.

A circle broken by a single stroke.

Their hand rose without permission and found the scar. The fit was exact.

* * *

The salvager washed out at full dark.

Sable heard him first — a knocking, rhythmic and wrong, coming from the direction of the Door, where the last of the tide was seeping back in through the arch. Then the shape: a man facedown in the shallows, turning slowly with the inflow, arms wrapped around something white he would not release even unconscious.

They pulled him aboard with boathooks and hauled him onto the deck plates, and he was young, younger than Pip, his lips gray, his hands torn to ribbons as though he'd climbed out of the cave over broken glass. In his death-grip: half a chart, water-ruined, its remaining fragment showing a stretch of river Sable knew intimately and a marked position beneath the bluff — and across the top, in faded iron-gall ink, a name.

*Coronation.*

Everyone knew the Coronation. The steamer that had gone down sixty years ago with a governor's fortune aboard, and more than a fortune — with the deed, the paper that was said to grant its holder every toll on the river, tolls collected ever since by whoever held the paper's successor rights, tolls that bled every boat on this water including the Marguerite. Salvors had hunted her for three generations. The river had never given her up.

Until tonight. Until the tide drained away and the Door stood open and a boy crawled out of it holding directions.

The salvager woke once, briefly, convulsing around the words. "Went in four of us," he rasped, eyes fixed on nothing. "Cave takes its due. Takes a memory apiece, my gran said, and I laughed at her. I can't remember—" His face worked. "I can't remember why I needed the money. I know I needed it. It's gone. It's just — gone, and there's a hole where it was." He seized Sable's wrist with shocking strength. "Don't go in. Whatever you're owed, don't—"

He didn't finish. He didn't die either; he slid back into a sleep that looked too much like drowning, breathing in shallow pulls, and Pip knelt over him with blankets and fear in equal measure.

"Captain." Pip's voice cracked. "Tell me we're not going in there."

Sable stood at the rail. Behind them the Door waited in the dark, the tide creeping back up its threshold, patient as debt. Ahead, downstream, lay the safe routine of another year of shallow moorings and early departures. And inside Sable's chest, the restlessness that had driven them for nine years — the itch that had them jumping sandbars ahead of magistrates and questions — had gone suddenly, terribly still, the way a river goes still above a falls.

Because the scar matched. Because some part of them, some part drowned deeper than memory, had known that mark before tonight. Because the restlessness had never been a wish to run toward anything. It had been a running *from*, and for nine years Sable had told themselves the destination didn't matter.

The tide was rising. By morning the Door would be sealed again, and the chart would be worthless, and the Coronation would keep her secret another sixty years, and Sable would spend the rest of their life wondering what they'd been too afraid to learn.

"Pip," Sable said quietly. "Get up steam."

"Captain —"

"We're going upstream. To the Door. Now, while the water's still off the threshold."

Pip stared at them, at the ruined chart, at the sleeping salvager, and Sable watched the argument build in the boy's face and then collapse under something worse than argument: understanding. "You've got business with that place," he said. "Don't you. You've had it all along."

Sable turned the wheel and brought the Marguerite's nose around, upstream, into the dark, toward the arch and the broken-circle mark and whatever toll waited inside.

"Full ahead," they said, and did not answer him.
