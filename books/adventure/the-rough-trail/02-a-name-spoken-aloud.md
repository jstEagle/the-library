## 2. A Name, Spoken Aloud

The fleet came in on the morning tide like a verdict.

Sable saw them first as smudges against the pale line where river met sky — three hulls, deep-bellied dredges with crane arms folded like sleeping herons, and behind them a steam launch flying no colors at all. They took up station across the channel from the Marguerite's mooring with the unhurried precision of things that had never once been told to move along. Anchors went down. Smoke went up. The river, which had belonged to Sable for nine years of small evasions and smaller profits, suddenly felt surveyed.

Pip stood at the rail with his elbows on the salt-crusted wood and his chin in his hands. "Salvage," he said, tasting the word. "That's the Coronation crowd, then."

"It's nobody's crowd until they say so." Sable coiled a line that didn't need coiling. Their hands wanted work; their hands always wanted work when the other thing started, the restless scratching behind the ribs, the feeling of a door opening somewhere in a house they couldn't remember owning.

The brass token sat in their coat pocket where it had sat since the salvager washed out of the Drowned Door two tides ago, cold as a coin pulled from a well. Half a chart, too, water-ruined, the ink blooming into gray flowers — but legible enough. *Coronation.* A course line drawn straight through the bar and into the cave's throat. And above the door, before the sea had taken him, the dying man had seen the mark carved there, and Sable had seen it too: a spiral, worn shallow, exactly the size and shape of the scar on the back of their own left hand that no doctor in nine years of river towns had ever been able to explain.

"Captain." Pip's voice had gone careful. "Someone's coming over."

The launch crossed the channel without hurry, throwing a long bow-wave that rocked the Marguerite on her mooring like a mother jostling a cradle. A gangway came across. And up it, in boots that had never touched bilgewater in their lives, came a woman in a charcoal coat cut close as a knife sheath, gray at the temples, gray-eyed, carrying herself the way harbor-magnates carry themselves — as though the dock beneath her feet were hers by right of purchase.

She stopped on deck. She looked at Sable for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that was not surprise. It was older than surprise. It was certainty, arriving.

"Hello, Merrin."

The name went through Sable like a harpoon through clean water.

Nine years. Nine years of Brightwater, of Captain, of hey-you and stranger and nothing at all. Nine years since anyone had spoken the name they'd been born with, and Sable had made sure of it — had buried it so deep even they sometimes fished for it and came up empty-handed. Now this woman stood on their deck and said *Merrin* the way you'd say a word you'd kept wrapped in oilcloth all this time, preserved, waiting.

"I don't know what you—" Sable began.

"You do." Odile Vann did not raise her voice. She didn't need to; the river itself seemed to lean in to listen. "Merrin Vann. My brother's child. Who walked off a barge at Corvess Landing nine years ago with a bag and a grudge and left their family name on the dock like an old coat." Her gaze traveled the Marguerite — the patched rail, the honest rust, the wheelhouse glass gone cloudy with age. "You've been hiding well. I'll grant you that. But the tide opened the Door, Merrin. Tides don't ask permission, and neither do I."

* * *

They talked in the wheelhouse because Sable refused to talk below decks, refused to give this woman the intimacy of the cabin, though some traitor part of them noted how naturally Odile found the companionway, how her hand knew the rail, as if she'd stood on a hundred boats just like this one — or on this one, in another life Sable couldn't see.

"The Coronation carried the toll deed," Odile said. "Every levy, every mooring fee, every cent the river collects between the headwaters and the sea — signed, sealed, and bound for the capital when she went down. Salvage law is murky on deeds. Possession isn't. Whoever brings that paper up owns the river's purse. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Not a share of cargo. The *river.*"

"And you want me to pilot your dredges over the bar." Sable kept their voice flat. "Six usable tides before the dry season strands us all, and you want the one captain who knows the channel."

"I want the one captain who's been inside." Odile's eyes were patient and terrible. "Your salvager died with half a chart. I have the other half. What I don't have is someone who can read the water over the bar when the wind turns, and we both know the wind will turn." She leaned forward. "A third share, Merrin. Enough to stop running. Enough to buy a boat that doesn't leak and a berth that doesn't rot. Enough to never again cast off at dawn because someone downstream asked your name."

And there it was — the hook, baited with the exact hunger Sable had fed for nine years. Rest. Permanence. A place to put down weight.

What frightened Sable was not the offer. What frightened them was the feeling underneath the fear, rising through it like warm water through a cracked hull: *relief.* Being recognized. Being *known.* After nine years of being no one to everyone, here was a person who could say *Merrin* and mean a whole history — birthdays, a barge, a father whose face lived somewhere behind a locked door in Sable's own skull. It would be so easy to say yes. To be found. To stop being a ghost.

"No," Sable said.

Odile studied them the way an appraiser studies a stone, looking for the flaw. "No?"

"I run my own boat. I don't run yours. Find another pilot."

"There is no other pilot."

"Then you'll find the bar the hard way." Sable opened the wheelhouse door. "Good luck with your tides, madam."

Odile rose. At the companionway she paused, and did not look back. "I bought your deckhand's loyalty this morning for less than you'd think," she said. "Consider what else I might have bought while you slept. You were always a heavy sleeper, Merrin. Like your father."

Then she was down the gangway, and the launch carried her back across the channel, and Sable stood at the wheel gripping it hard enough to whiten knuckles, listening to the word *father* echo in a room inside them that had no furniture they could remember.

* * *

The reprisal came quietly, which was its own kind of message.

First the anchors. Sable woke on the second morning to find the Marguerite dragging — both bow anchors cut clean at the shackle, not dropped but *cut*, paid-for work done with a sharp edge and no noise, and only Pip's midnight habit of smoking on the foredeck had saved them from drifting onto the oyster stakes downriver. They re-anchored in the gray light, saying little.

Then the charts. Sable kept the good ones — the hand-corrected surveys, nine years of soundings in their own cramped notation — in a oilskin roll under the bunk. The roll was there. The charts were not. In their place, a single sheet of cheap paper: *Third share stands until the fourth tide.*

And then Pip.

Sable found him on the afternoon of the third day, sitting on the pilings with his feet dangling, watching the dredges across the channel with an expression Sable recognized because they'd worn it themselves, once, on a dock at Corvess Landing. Guilt, doing arithmetic.

"They offered me forty crowns," Pip said, not turning around. "Just to tell them your soundings. I didn't take it." A pause. "I haven't taken it yet."

"Pip—"

"Don't." He turned now, and his young face was miserable and stubborn at once. "I've been with you three years, Captain, and you've been decent, but you've never once told me where we're going or why we leave places so fast or why you flinch when folks ask about your people. Forty crowns is a year's wages. And she called you something else. Something real." His jaw set. "I'd like to know who I'm working for. That's all. Is that so much?"

Sable looked at him — at the boy who'd forgotten his mother's face was still ahead of him, though he didn't know it yet — and felt the strange relief rise again, unwelcome as floodwater. Someone wanted to know them. Someone was asking. And every instinct honed over nine years screamed that being known was how you got caught, how you got owned, how you ended up signing papers with a name that wasn't really yours.

"The fourth tide," Sable said quietly. "Keep your forty crowns in your pocket, Pip. By the fifth, you won't want it."

"How do you know?"

Because the token in my pocket matches the scar on my hand, Sable almost said. Because the cave takes payment, and I've already paid once without knowing. Because Odile Vann has the other half of the chart, and she'll come for the Door whether I pilot her or not, and if she raises what's in that hold—

Across the channel, the dredges were raising steam. All three of them. Cranes unfolding like herons waking, anchors coming up in a rush of brown water, the whole fleet swinging its bows toward the bar and the Drowned Door beyond it.

Odile Vann stood on the lead dredge's deck, and even at that distance Sable could feel her looking back — not at the bar, not at the water.

At them.

"She's going without me," Sable said. "Fourth tide. She's going now."
