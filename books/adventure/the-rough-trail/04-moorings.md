## 4. Moorings

The sixth tide came in like a beggar.

Sable stood at the wheel of the Marguerite and watched the river fail to rise. The water climbed the pilings a hand's width, hesitated, and stopped, as if it had thought better of the whole enterprise. Across the channel, where the Drowned Door had yawned open for five tides running, the sea lay flat and gray and unremarkable, hiding its cave the way a miser hides his ledger.

"Too weak," Pip said beside them. His voice was still wrong — thinner than it had been, as though something had been scooped out of him along with his mother's face. He'd spent two days asking Sable to describe her, and Sable had done their best, inventing kindnesses they had no right to invent, until Pip told them gently to stop. "It won't open."

"No." Sable turned the wheel and brought the Marguerite about, slow, so the turn wouldn't argue with the shallow water. "It won't."

Behind them, on the far bank, Odile Vann's salvage fleet sat at anchor like a funeral that refused to end. Her dredges hung idle. Her lanterns burned all night in patterns Sable didn't bother to read anymore. Whatever she'd screamed at the closing water on the fifth tide, whatever curses and bargains she'd shouted into the flood, the river had declined to answer. She would dig at the bar for another season, maybe two, and then the dry season would strand her hulls on the mud like beached whales, and she would learn what Sable had learned nine years ago: some doors only open when the water is generous, and generosity is not a thing you can purchase.

The deed was ash. Sable carried the knowledge of that the way they carried the scar above their wrist — as fact, not feeling. They had fed it to the flooded air of the hold page by page while Odile screamed, and the ink had gone up green and gold, every toll on the river from the Head of Waters to the salt, every name it could have made them, burning in a room that charged admission by the memory.

They should have felt triumphant. They felt moored.

* * *

That evening, in the lamplit cabin, Sable went hunting through their own head for the reason they ran.

It was a strange labor, like dredging a channel by feel. Nine years of habit said: you ran because running is what you do. But habit was not a reason, and Sable had learned, watching Pip grope for a mother's face, exactly what shape a taken thing leaves behind. There was a hollow in them. They had assumed it was grief, or guilt, or the ordinary scar tissue of a bad life. Now they went looking for its edges.

They found them quickly, once they knew how to look.

There had been a father. That much remained — a shape, a weight, a voice like a lock turning. The man had owned things. Tolls, Sable thought. The word arrived without any picture attached, clean and cold as a coin. He had owned the tolls on the river, and he had wanted — this came with a flicker, a lantern-lit office, hands flat on a desk — he had wanted his child to inherit. And there had been a night, a boat, a fire or a flight, Sable couldn't say which, because —

Because it wasn't there.

Sable sat very still in the cabin while the lamp guttered. The memory of leaving wasn't missing the way a thing is lost. It was missing the way a thing is *paid*. Neatly. Completely. With interest. Somewhere between the Door and the daylight, the cave had reached into them and lifted out the one scene they had circled for nine years without ever once examining, and it had left the rest — the fear, the flinch at mooring lines, the itch in their feet whenever a town grew comfortable — like change scattered across a counter.

They laughed. It came out cracked and startled, half sob, and Pip looked up from his mending with alarm.

"Captain?"

"I know why I ran." Sable pressed both palms flat against the table, against the good solid wood, against the boat that had carried them nine years without ever letting them arrive anywhere. "I just don't remember doing it. The cave took the leaving. That was my admission fee. Not a face. Not a name." A breath. "*Him.* The last of him. It took the moment I became someone who runs."

Pip set down his needle. He was sixteen and missing his mother's face and he still managed to look at Sable with more steadiness than Sable had ever managed to look at anything. "Does it hurt?"

Sable considered honestly. They turned the loss over the way they'd turn a piece of salvage, checking it for cracks.

"No," they said finally, and found it true. "It lands like cargo coming off. You know how the Marguerite rides high when we've unloaded? Like that. Like I've been carrying him up every river and down every river for nine years, and somewhere in that flooded dark, I set him down." They shook their head slowly. "I keep waiting for the grief. It's there. But it's — light. It's grief with no freight in it."

"That's not how grief works," Pip said, with the authority of the young.

"No," Sable agreed. "But it's how caves work."

* * *

In the morning they took the Marguerite up past the fish market, past the customs house whose clerks had long since stopped asking questions, to the deep-water moorings under the bluff — the berths Sable had avoided for nine years because deep moorings meant staying, and staying meant being found.

The lines went out heavy and sure. Bow line, stern line, spring line, each one paid out and made fast with the old clean motions of a body that knew its work. When the last hitch was set, Sable stood on the deck of their own ship, tied to a town, tied to a season, tied to the mud that would rise around the hull as the river dropped, and waited for the panic.

It didn't come.

What came instead was the morning: gulls working the fish waste, the smell of tar and frying bread, a bell buoy clanking its idiot music out in the channel. What came was Pip whistling in the galley, off-key, learning a new song because the old ones were tangled up with what he'd lost and he was determined to grow new ones around the gap, the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

"Brightwater," Sable said aloud, to the deck, to the river, to nobody.

The name sat differently in the mouth now. For nine years it had been a mask worn backward — a false face pointed inward, fooling no one, least of all themselves. Now it was just a word, and the word was theirs. Not inherited. Chosen. A thing picked up deliberately, the way you pick up a tool you intend to use rather than one you're fleeing with.

"Brightwater," they said again, testing it, and the river carried it off downstream without comment, which Sable decided was as close to blessing as rivers gave.

* * *

The child found them at the chandlery three days later, when the dry season had begun in earnest and the Marguerite sat settled in her berth like a woman finally asleep. She was perhaps eight, barefoot on the warm planks, one of the dock children who lived in the gaps between warehouses the way barnacles live between tide lines.

"You're the one went in the Door," she said. Not a question. Dock children knew everything and believed most of it.

"I'm one of them."

"What's in there?" She came closer, dropping her voice to the register children use for important business. "Old Man Teale says it's full of drowned men's gold. My gran says it's full of drowned men's *names*, and if it hears yours, it keeps it."

Sable crouched down to her level. Behind them the harbor glittered, hard and bright, and somewhere across the channel Odile Vann's dredges clanked at a bar that would give up nothing this year.

"Both of those are nearly true," Sable said. "There was treasure. There's less of it now. And yes — it takes a price to let you leave. It took something from me. Something I'd been carrying so long I forgot it was heavy."

"Was it worth it?"

Sable thought of a page going up in green flame. Of a voice like a lock turning, gone quiet forever. Of the ride of an unloaded boat. Of the word *Brightwater*, sitting in their mouth like their own key.

"Some things are worth the price of forgetting," they said. "That's the honest answer, and you can tell your gran the legend's paid for."

The child studied them with the frank appraisal of the young, decided something, and nodded once, gravely, as one merchant to another. Then she was gone, bare feet slapping down the planks toward whatever the tide had brought in.

Sable straightened, and stayed standing a moment in the sun, listening to the harbor work around them. The dry season would be long. There was a hull wanted careening, a deckhand learning new songs, a town they had never once let themselves know. There would be other springs, other tides, and the Door would open again for whoever was foolish or desperate enough — but that was next year's water, and Sable Brightwater, moored deep and riding light, had finally stopped counting tides.

They went aboard their ship, and home.
