## 1. Salt in the Wires

The sea off Kestrel Point was the color of old bottle glass that morning, and Tamsin Montrose liked it that way — murky enough to hide her, clear enough to work. Forty feet down, the wreck spread out below her like something that had lain down to sleep and simply never gotten up.

She'd found it the way she found most of her work: by accident, by stubbornness, and by refusing to ask too many questions until the contract was signed. A fisherman had mentioned a snag that kept tearing nets two miles off the point, and had mentioned it in the particular tone fishermen used when they wanted a diver to make the problem go away without anyone official getting involved. Tamsin had quoted him a fair price, borrowed bottom time from a harbormaster who owed her, and here she was.

The wreck was a coaster, maybe sixty years old, sitting on sand in a scatter of her own ribs. Kelp had claimed the deck. A school of silver bream poured through the wheelhouse windows like the ship had sprung a leak of light. It was, Tamsin thought, a perfectly ordinary dead ship.

She was twenty minutes into surveying it when she found the thing that wasn't ordinary.

The hull had been patched — recently, and badly, and not by the ship's original builders. The patch was a plate, maybe three feet square, bolted over a gash near the waterline. Marine growth had been encouraged along its edges, brushed on with what looked suspiciously like a broom, the way a forger ages a painting. Someone had wanted this patch to look as old as the rest of her.

Tamsin ran her glove along the plate's edge. Under the fake fouling, the metal was bright. Under the bright metal, stamped into the steel in a crisp, official-looking hand, was a serial number.

She knew serial numbers. Every diver did, the way farmers know weather. Merchant hulls carried one kind. Navy hulls carried another, and the navy's kind came with a prefix that told you which yard had rolled the steel and which fleet had ordered it. Tamsin had spent enough of her life scraping barnacles off navy contracts — legitimate ones, mostly, the kind that paid on time — to read them the way other people read street signs.

This one was wrong.

The prefix belonged to a yard that had been decommissioned before Tamsin was born. The fleet code belonged to a squadron that, according to every registry Ovid-type archivists kept and every drunk harbormaster repeated, had never operated in these waters at all. And the number itself sat in a sequence that shouldn't exist — a gap, a ghost, a serial stamped on a plate that had never officially been made.

She'd have missed it if she hadn't been bored. Boredom, Tamsin had learned, was the most reliable salvage tool she owned. Bored divers look at things. Curious divers look at the wrong things, but bored ones look at everything.

She took a rubbing. Charcoal stick, waxed paper, thirty seconds of work. The plate went into her chest pocket, and the wreck went back to being an ordinary dead ship, and Tamsin finished her survey with the pleasant, humming diligence of a woman who had absolutely not just found something that was going to ruin her week.

* * *

The harbor at Kestrel Point was less a port than a rumor of one: three jetties, a fuel pump that worked on alternating days, and the Saltwire, a bar whose name was either a reference to telegraph history or to what the owner did to the wiring when he was angry. Tamsin sat at the Saltwire's corner table with her rubbing under a beer glass and her survey notes under a plate of fried fish, and she did the math.

The coaster was unlisted. Unlisted wrecks were common — half the ships that ever sank were never properly recorded, and the other half were recorded wrong. A navy plate on an unlisted coaster was unusual but explainable. Salvage, refits, paperwork lost in some bureaucratic flood. There were a dozen boring answers.

But the serial that shouldn't exist — that wasn't boring. That was the kind of detail that, if you brought it to the right people, made your life either very profitable or very short, and the trouble was that you couldn't tell in advance which people were which.

"Refill?" The barman appeared at her elbow. His name was Duttle, and he had the build of a man assembled from spare barrels.

"Sure." She slid her glass aside and, on impulse, slid the rubbing out with it. "You seen anything like this before?"

Duttle looked. Tamsin watched his face do something interesting: nothing at all. Complete, professional blankness, the expression of a man who had been asked questions by people you didn't ask questions back to.

"Looks like a number," he said.

"It does."

"Navy number."

"It does that too."

Duttle put the beer down and wiped the table where the rubbing had been, though the table was dry. "Off the point, are you? The snag?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Fisherman's business, a snag. Nets tear, boats lose gear. Best thing for a snag is for everyone to forget where it was." He refilled her glass without being asked again and moved off down the bar, wiping as he went, a man erasing his own footsteps.

Tamsin looked at her fish. She looked at her beer. She thought about the way the fake fouling had been brushed on, patiently, by someone with time and orders, and she thought about how the navy did not, as a general rule, send anyone to brush marine growth onto hull plates in the middle of nowhere unless there was something on that hull worth hiding.

She was still doing this math when the navy arrived.

* * *

They came in a gray launch, twin engines, no markings except the numbers that meant nothing to civilians and everything to anyone who'd worked navy contracts. Two officers and four sailors, boots on the jetty at half past four in the afternoon, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had never once been late to anything that mattered.

The bar went quiet in the specific way bars do when the drinks stay full and the conversations stop. Tamsin finished her fish. She had learned a long time ago — from a mother who'd learned it harder — that the worst thing you could do when powerful people walked into a room was look like they'd interrupted something.

The senior officer found her without asking. He was maybe fifty, weathered in a way that suggested he'd started on decks and finished in offices, with a voice like a well-oiled hinge. "Montrose. Tamsin Montrose, salvage diver, currently uncontracted."

"Currently drinking," Tamsin said. "But I'm flexible."

He sat down across from her without being invited, which took a certain kind of confidence in a bar where half the clientele carried knives. "Commander Hale. Coastal Survey Division." He said the division name the way a man says a password he doesn't believe in. "You dove the snag off the point today."

"I surveyed a wreck today. Whether it's the snag depends on who's asking."

"The navy's asking."

"Then it's a snag."

Something that might have been respect, or might have been indigestion, moved across Hale's face. He set a folder on the table — a real folder, paper, the kind of thing that didn't officially exist. "Ms. Montrose. I'm going to save us both a great deal of time. You found something down there. I'm going to tell you what it is, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and then you're going to take the offer, and then we're all going to have a very pleasant evening."

"That's a lot of 'thens.'"

"I'm a thorough man."

He opened the folder and turned it toward her. Inside was a photograph — her own boat at the jetty this morning, taken from the water, from an angle that meant someone had been watching her dive. Beside it, a copy of her harbor clearance, and beneath that, a sheet with a single serial number printed on it.

Her serial. The one from the plate.

"You knew before I surfaced," Tamsin said.

"We knew before you dove. The plate was reported missing eleven days ago. We've been waiting to see who'd find it." Hale folded his hands. "What you stumbled onto, Ms. Montrose, is a decommissioning error. A clerical matter. Certain materials from a retired vessel were disposed of improperly, and the disposal has caused confusion. The navy is tidying up. You found a piece of the mess. Congratulations. Here's the offer."

He slid a second sheet across. Tamsin read it. It was, in essence, a receipt: she would surrender any rubbings, photographs, or notes relating to the wreck; she would certify that the wreck was a hazard to navigation and recommend demolition; and in exchange she would receive a salvage fee that was roughly triple what the fisherman was paying, paid in full, today, in cash, plus a standing preference on future navy contracts that would keep her booked and solvent for two years.

Two years. Tamsin had not been solvent for two consecutive years in her adult life. She looked at the number for a long moment, and she was honest enough with herself to admit that the honest part of her wanted it.

"And if I don't take it?"

"Then the wreck remains a hazard, the navy demolishes it anyway, and your survey notes become, I'm afraid, subject to review. Coastal hazards are a navy matter. Your clearance to dive these waters is a navy matter." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced, almost kind. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing the weather."

Tamsin turned the receipt over. Blank on the back. She thought about the fake fouling, brushed on with a broom. Clerical errors did not get brooms. Clerical errors did not get photographed boats and folders that didn't exist and commanders who showed up within hours of a single dive, in a port so small the navy usually pretended it wasn't there.

"You brushed growth on that plate," she said.

Hale's face did the same thing Duttle's had: nothing, expertly. "Tides do strange things."

"Tides don't own brooms."

For a moment — half a breath — the well-oiled hinge of his voice creaked. "Ms. Montrose. I have a great deal of respect for competent people. Competent people who take the offer have long, comfortable careers. Competent people who ask about brooms have short, eventful ones. I would genuinely prefer you comfortable." He tapped the receipt. "The offer expires when I leave this bar."

Tamsin looked at the number one more time. Two years of solvency, sitting on a table in the Saltwire, smelling faintly of ink and threat.

She thought about her mother, who had once told her, in the tone she reserved for things that were actually true: *The sea keeps everything, Tam. It's the people you have to watch.*

She signed.

Hale watched her sign, collected the paper, collected her rubbing and her survey notes, and stood. "Pleasure doing business. The demolition is scheduled. You'll want to be elsewhere." He paused at the edge of the table, and for a moment the officer fell away and something older and wearier looked out of his eyes. "For what it's worth — you're good down there. Better than your file says. Don't waste it on questions."

He left. The bar exhaled. Duttle materialized with another beer Tamsin hadn't ordered and didn't touch.

"You signed," Duttle said. Not a question.

"I signed."

"Smart." He wiped the table again, the dry table. "My advice, diver — and it's free, so it's worth what it costs — when the navy pays you to forget something, forget it all the way. Don't keep little souvenirs in your head. Heads get searched."

Tamsin turned her glass in a slow circle. "What if the souvenir isn't a thing, Duttle? What if it's a question?"

"Then you're in trouble," Duttle said, with the gentleness of a man who'd been there, "and my advice is to drink your beer and think about something else. Weather, maybe. Fish. Anything with no serial number on it."

She drank the beer. She paid her tab. She walked out into the evening, where the light was going copper on the water and the gray launch was already gone over the horizon, and she stood on the jetty and looked at the place where the wreck sat under forty feet of ordinary sea.

The world, Tamsin reflected, had just gotten bigger and dirtier, the way it always did when you looked too closely at anything. She had spent her whole life keeping her head down and her contracts short and her questions few, and it had worked, mostly, the way a leaky boat works — right up until it didn't.

She should take the preference contracts. She should book two years of honest, boring, navy-approved work and forget the serial number, the broom marks, the photograph of her own boat, and the way Commander Hale's voice had creaked on the word *brooms*.

She was going to do exactly that, she told herself. Starting tomorrow.

But that night, in her bunk on her own small boat, Tamsin took out the waxed paper she'd kept after all — a second rubbing, taken before the first, tucked into her boot where no folder would think to look — and she looked at the impossible serial number for a long time by lamplight.

And in the morning, in a port dive bar three jetties down the coast, a prospector with seawater in his lungs and blood on his shirt was about to sit down next to the only diver in the room who couldn't stop looking at things.

The world was bigger and dirtier than Tamsin had assumed. It was also, she was beginning to suspect, about to get a great deal more so.
