## 2. The Man Who Wouldn't Sink

The bar was called the Drowned Rat, which Tamsin had always thought was a little too honest for a port establishment. Most dive bars at least pretended to be something else — a hotel, a chandlery, a place where respectable people drank respectable drinks. The Drowned Rat didn't pretend anything. It smelled of bilgewater and fried onions, its floorboards had been stained the color of old tea by forty years of spilled beer, and its single window looked out on nothing but the pilings of Jetty Three, where the water sat green and patient as a debt collector.

Tamsin liked it precisely because it made no promises.

She'd taken a corner table with her back to the wall, as she always did, and she was working through her second cup of coffee that was mostly chicory when the door banged open and the man came in.

She noticed him the way you notice weather coming in off the water — not all at once, but in pieces. First the sound: a wet, rattling cough that turned heads along the bar like a bad chord. Then the smell: salt, diesel, and under it something coppery that her nose knew before her brain did. Then the man himself, leaning against the doorframe like the building owed him money.

He was sixty, maybe older, built like a length of driftwood — hard, bleached, and shaped by things that had tried to break him. His shirt was soaked through at the chest, and it wasn't sweat. There was blood on his collar, dried to a brown crust, and fresher blood at the corner of his mouth. He stood there a moment, blinking against the dimness, and then he saw her.

Not glanced at her. *Saw* her. The way you see a lighthouse when you've been swimming in the dark for a long time.

"Oh," he said, in a voice like gravel poured through a sieve. "Oh, thank every god there is."

And he started toward her table.

Tamsin's hand moved without her deciding anything — down to her boot, to the knife she kept there, the one good habit her mother had ever drilled into her. But the man wasn't staggering toward her with menace. He was staggering toward her with something worse: recognition.

"You're Montrose's girl," he said, arriving at her table and gripping its edge with both hands, knuckles white as coral bone. "You're — you have her chin. You have her *chin*, girl, I'd know it in a crowd of ten thousand."

Tamsin went very still.

People didn't talk about her mother. Not to her face, not in this tone of voice, not with this look — this awful, wet-eyed, drowning-man look, like Tamsin was both the answer to a prayer and the last person he wanted to be seeing right now.

"Wrong table," she said. "I don't know you."

"No." He laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough bent him double over her table, and what came up of it was seawater — actual seawater, brackish and gray-green — flecked with red. It splattered across her coffee cup and the waxed paper she'd been using as a coaster. "No, you don't. Nobody does. That's rather the point of me."

The bartender, a vast woman named Odile who had once broken a smuggler's arm over an unpaid tab, called out: "Voss! You're bleeding on my floor again!"

"Again?" Tamsin said.

"He comes in every few months," Odile said, wiping a glass with the air of a woman describing tide patterns. "Buys one drink, coughs on something, leaves before anyone can ask questions. Pays double for the mess. Best customer I've got, honestly."

"I'm a *mystery*," Voss said with some pride, straightening up. He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at the smear of red. His expression flickered — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. Like a man checking a clock against a bell tower. "Though I'll admit the schedule's moved up."

"Sit down," Tamsin said, before she could stop herself.

It was the diver in her. She'd seen drowned men before — pulled two of them off a wreck off Sallow Cay, watched a third die on a jetty while everyone shouted useless advice. She knew the sound of lungs full of ocean. This man's lungs were full of ocean, and he was walking around buying drinks and making jokes about it, and every instinct she had was screaming that this was a man on a very short fuse of a life.

"I'd rather stand," Voss said. "Sitting's a commitment."

"Sit *down*."

He sat. Up close, she could take his measure properly: sun-scoured skin gone gray at the edges, hands scarred from rope and reef, eyes the pale washed-out blue of shallow water over sand. A prospector's eyes. Wreck-hunter's eyes. She'd grown up around men with those eyes; they never quite focused on the room they were standing in. They were always looking at the bottom of it.

"Odile," Tamsin called. "Hot water, clean cloth, and whatever passes for brandy back there."

"That's my good brandy—"

"*Now*, Odile."

Odile muttered something about how nobody appreciated her artistry, but she went. Tamsin spread the cloth across the table when it arrived, took the man's wrist — thin as a gull's leg — and pressed two fingers to his pulse. Fast. Fluttering. The heartbeat of a man whose body was filing formal complaints.

"When did you last surface?" she asked.

"This morning."

"And how long were you down?"

He smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked at one corner by the blood. "Longer than I should've been. Longer than anybody should've been. But I found it, girl. After eleven years, I finally—" The cough took him again, harder, and he gripped her forearm with shocking strength while his whole frame shook. When it passed, he slumped back in the chair, breathing in whistles. "Sorry. Where were we."

"You were about to tell me why you know my mother's name."

Something changed in his face. The showman's gloss — and there had been a showman's gloss, she realized now, a performance laid over the sickness like paint over rot — thinned, and underneath it was just an old man, frightened, and tired, and out of time.

"Ede Voss," he said, offering a hand she didn't shake. "I dived with your mother. Merrow's crew, the *Salt Lark* years. Before you were born, or near enough."

Tamsin's cup was halfway to her mouth. She set it down very carefully, because her hands had developed opinions.

"My mother's crew is dead," she said. "All of them. Or scattered so fine it amounts to the same thing."

"Not all." Voss held up one finger. "One got rich and became a captain. One got smart and disappeared. And one—" he tapped his own sunken chest, "—one got stubborn and kept diving."

"The captain," Tamsin said slowly. "That would be Reyes."

Ilsa Reyes. The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. The woman who had taught her to tie her first bowline, to read a pressure gauge, to equalize her ears on descent — and who had then stood up in front of a naval inquiry and testified, in a calm and reasonable voice, that Mara Montrose had falsified her survey logs and dived beyond safe limits on her own authority. The woman whose testimony had turned the salvage community against her mother years before the sea finished the job.

"She speak of me?" Voss asked.

"Nobody speaks of anything in this business. That's how you stay alive in it."

"Sensible." He nodded, approving. "Your mother wasn't sensible. Your mother believed the sea kept accounts, and that someday somebody ought to settle them." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the hollows of his cheeks, and Tamsin saw that beneath the tan he was the color of candle wax. "I owe her a debt, girl. Eleven years I've owed it. And I've come to pay it to the only person left who can collect."

"I told you. Wrong table."

"Is it?" He gestured, weakly, at her. At her oilskin, her calloused hands, the salt-bleached streaks in her hair. "You dive wrecks nobody lists. You work alone because crews ask questions and contracts want signatures. You keep your boat registered three ports away and pay your moorage in cash. I asked about you before I came in, Montrose's girl. I've been asking about you for six months." He smiled that cracked smile again. "You're your mother's daughter down to the keel. You just haven't found your wreck yet."

The hot water arrived. Tamsin took it without looking at Odile, wrung the cloth, and began cleaning the blood off the man's chin with the brisk, impersonal efficiency of someone doing a job to avoid thinking about anything else.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding still."

"You're talking, which is the opposite."

"Talking's the only thing keeping me upright," Voss said. "So let me talk. Let me tell you about a ship called the *Corvane*."

The name meant nothing to Tamsin. She said so.

"It wouldn't. That's the design of it." Voss's eyes had gone distant now, fixed on the middle distance, on the green water past the window. "Colonial packet ship, forty-two years back. Officially lost with all hands in the Serpent Channel storm. Officially carrying trade goods and government correspondence. Unofficially—" he tapped the table twice, "—carrying the treasury of the Meridian families. Gold, deeds, bonds. Everything five islands' worth of merchant clans had scraped together over a century, seized by a colonial governor who decided their loyalty was worth more than their property. Loaded aboard the *Corvane* in secret, at night, and sent to the bottom of the Serpent Channel with a story attached."

"Shipwrecks happen," Tamsin said. "Storms happen."

"Sure. And sometimes storms happen exactly where a governor needs them to." Voss shrugged. "I'm not saying I have proof. Proof's at the bottom of the channel. What I'm saying is I spent eleven years finding it, and three days ago I finally touched the hull."

Tamsin's hands stopped moving.

Three days ago. Three days ago she had been fifty fathoms down at Kestrel Point, running her fingers over a navy hull plate stamped with a serial number that did not exist, and within four hours the navy had been at her mooring with a checkbook and a threat wearing a checkbook's clothes.

"What hull plate?" she heard herself ask.

Voss blinked. "What?"

"You said you touched the hull. What did you find on it?"

For a long moment the old man just looked at her. And Tamsin watched something pass across his face — the same unease she'd felt at Kestrel Point, the sense of a world tilting to reveal its underside — and she understood, with a cold settling in her stomach, that they had both recently brushed against the same thing. Whatever the navy was doing at Kestrel Point, whatever that impossible serial number meant, it lived in the same waters as the *Corvane*. It swam in the same current.

"Copper sheathing," Voss said carefully. "Newer than it should be. Navy pattern. Stamped with numbers I copied onto a chart, because I'm old, not stupid." He paused. "Why do you ask?"

Because three days ago I held the same mystery in my glove, Tamsin thought. Because the navy bought my silence yesterday morning and I took the money because taking it was cheaper than the alternative, and because I kept a rubbing anyway, because some part of me has apparently inherited my mother's inability to leave accounts unsettled.

"Professional curiosity," she said.

Voss studied her a moment longer, then let it go — or seemed to. Old wreck divers, she suspected, were very good at letting things go on the surface while stowing them below decks.

"Here's what matters," he said. "The *Corvane*'s strongroom is intact. I've been through the outer hold — stripped decades ago, nothing there but crabs and disappointment. But the strongroom sits below the waterline, behind a wheel-seal, and it's never been opened. Eleven years I've mapped that wreck. Eleven years I've come back every season the weather allowed. And now—" he coughed, wetly, horribly, "—now my body's filed its final survey. The doctor at the mission hospital gave me a month. The sea gave me less; I pushed my luck this morning testing a new approach to the compartment, and the channel pushed back."

"So find another diver."

"I found dozens. I hired them, begged them, promised them shares." Voss shook his head. "Every one of them either died, quit, or sold the location to somebody with more guns than patience. The last one sold it to a navy man. That's how the fleet found the channel. That's why the survey ships are crawling all over those waters now, why the charts are being quietly amended, why a prospector can't buy a tank of air in any port within two hundred miles without somebody writing his name down." He looked at her, and his pale eyes were terribly clear. "They're not salvaging the *Corvane*, girl. They're burying it. And they've put a captain on the job who has never once failed to make a thing disappear."

Tamsin thought of Reyes. Of course they had. Who else? Who better?

"Why me?" she asked. It came out rougher than she intended. "You've known me for ten minutes. For all you know I'm exactly the kind of diver who sells locations."

"I've watched you for six months," Voss said. "And I knew your mother for nine years. You sit the same way she sat — back to the wall, watching the door, counting exits. You cleaned my chin like it was a job, because if it's a job you don't have to feel it. And when I said the word *Reyes*, your jaw did a thing." He demonstrated, weakly, with his own jaw. "A small thing. A very controlled thing. Your mother used to do the same thing whenever anyone mentioned the admiralty."

"I barely remember my mother."

"Liar," Voss said, gently. "But that's all right. Grief keeps its own logbooks." He drew a breath — a long, careful, deliberate breath, like a man conserving air on a deep dive — and when he spoke again, his voice had dropped to nearly nothing, pitched under the clatter of the bar, meant for her alone.

"Before the end, your mother found the *Corvane*. Did you know that?"

The bar noise receded. Tamsin was aware of Odile polishing glasses, of a pair of dockhands arguing over dice, of rain starting somewhere outside against the tin roof — and none of it, none of it, over the sound of her own pulse.

"She never told me anything," Tamsin said.

"She couldn't. She found it six weeks before she died. She came to me — I was running survey work out of Sallow Cay then — and she showed me a chart with a position on it, and she said, *Ede, I've found the account book the sea's been keeping.* That's how she talked. She said the governor's confession was sealed inside the strongroom, paper proof of everything, and that raising it would hang a dozen important men. She said she needed one more diver she could trust, because her own crew had gone quiet on her — funny looks, closed doors, conversations stopping when she walked in." Voss's voice frayed. "I told her I'd dive with her. I swear to you, girl, on whatever's left of my soul, I told her I'd dive with her. And then I got greedy. There was a contract — big money, legitimate, the kind I'd chased my whole life — and I told her I'd follow in the spring."

The cloth in Tamsin's hands had stopped moving. She made it move again.

"She dove it alone," Voss said. "Or with whoever was left of her crew. And she didn't come back. And the inquiry said she'd falsified her logs and exceeded her limits, and Captain Reyes testified to that effect, and the salvage boards blacklisted her name, and the sea kept the account." He lifted his pale eyes to Tamsin's. "Eleven years I've known where she died. Eleven years I've dived that wreck and never had the strength, or the courage, or the time enough left to finish what she started. And now I've got maybe a week of breath in me, and a chart, and no one to give it to except the daughter of the only person I ever wronged."

The rain thickened against the roof. Somewhere down the jetty, a ship's bell rang the hour, muffled and slow.

"I don't want your guilt," Tamsin said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "I don't want your chart. I've got a contract finishing in Kestrel Harbor and a boat that needs a new impeller, and I don't do ghosts."

"No," Voss agreed. "You do unfinished business. Same animal, different coat." He fumbled at his shirt pocket, and Tamsin tensed — but what he drew out was only a folded square of oilcloth, soft with handling, tied with a length of waxed twine. He set it on the table between them, in the lamplight, and did not push it toward her. "I'm not giving you anything tonight. Tonight I'm going to drink Odile's terrible brandy and sleep in a chair like a gentleman. Tomorrow, if you still want nothing to do with me, I'll take the chart and my cough to the mission hospital and bother somebody else's conscience."

Tamsin looked at the oilcloth bundle. Small. Light. The kind of weight that doesn't show on a scale.

"If I say no tomorrow," she said, "you'll just find me again."

"Probably," Voss admitted. "I'm very annoying when I'm dying. Ask anyone."

Despite everything — despite the blood and the seawater and the name *Reyes* sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast — Tamsin felt the corner of her mouth twitch. She killed it immediately. It felt disloyal to kill it, somehow, but she killed it anyway.

"Finish your brandy," she said. "And if you're sleeping in that chair, you're sleeping upright. If you lie flat, you'll drown in your own bed, and Odile will bill me for the cleanup."

"Aye, Captain," Voss said, and grinned his cracked grin, and drank.

* * *

She walked him nowhere; he wouldn't allow it. He gathered himself up by degrees, refused her shoulder twice, accepted it on the third offer when his knees staged their mutiny, and made it as far as the rented room above the chandlery next door before his legs resigned entirely. Tamsin propped him in the bed at an angle, built a nest of pillows behind his back, and set a basin on the floor because she'd already learned the rhythm of his coughing and knew it would be needed around dawn.

"You don't have to do this," Voss said, as she checked the lamp wick.

"Do what?"

"Any of it. Sit with a dying stranger. Play nursemaid." His eyes were half-closed, but watching her. "You could walk out that door and I'd be a story you tell in bars. *The mad old prospector who claimed he knew my mother.* Nobody would blame you."

Tamsin stood by the door with her hand on the latch, and found that she didn't have an answer ready, and disliked intensely that she didn't.

"Get some sleep," she said, and went out.

She didn't go back to her boat. She walked the jetty instead, in the rain, all the way to the end, where the harbor water slapped the pilings and the lights of the port smeared themselves across the swell like spilled paint. Out past the breakwater, the sea was black and moving, and somewhere out in that blackness was the Serpent Channel, and the *Corvane*, and — maybe — her mother. Not her mother's ghost; Tamsin had never had much use for ghosts. Her mother's *work*. The last thing Mara Montrose had ever touched, wedged into a bulkhead or resting on a deck forty fathoms down, waiting eleven years for somebody to come back for it.

*Back to the wall, watching the door, counting exits.* Voss had read her in ten minutes. It had taken everyone else years, and most of them had never bothered to try.

She thought about the navy men at her mooring, with their clean boots and their generous check and their polite certainty that she would take it. She thought about the hull plate with its impossible number, tucked into her boot in waxed paper. She thought about a colonial governor sealing a confession inside a strongroom, and a captain named Reyes making things disappear, and an old man coughing seawater into a basin above a chandlery because the sea had gotten tired of waiting for him to sink.

Her mother had believed the sea kept accounts. Tamsin had spent thirty years believing the opposite — that the sea kept nothing, that it erased everything, that the only sane response was to keep moving, keep diving, keep your name off every list. Stay unrecorded. Stay unclaimed. Stay free.

But the sea hadn't erased her mother. It had *kept* her. Kept her knife, kept her rig, kept her last dive unfinished. And now there was a man in a rented room who knew where, and a chart in an oilcloth bundle, and a navy fleet crawling over the channel with orders to bury it all before anyone could read the ledger.

Tamsin stood at the end of the jetty until the rain soaked through her oilskin, and she let herself feel the thing she'd been refusing to feel since Voss had said *Merrow's crew*: that she wanted it. Wanted the chart, the wreck, the truth, the fight. Wanted, with a hunger that embarrassed her, to finish something. To stop running mid-stride for the first time in her adult life and turn around and look at the thing chasing her.

Then she went back up the stairs, because she was her mother's daughter down to the keel, and her mother had never once left a dive unfinished if she could help it.

Voss was asleep — really asleep, for the first time since the bar, his breathing a ragged tide in and out. Tamsin took the chair by the window, set her boots beside it, and settled in to keep watch, back to the wall, facing the door.

Somewhere in the small hours, the coughing woke him, and she held the basin, and afterward he lay gasping and said, into the dark:

"You're still here."

"For now."

"That's how it starts," Ede Voss said, and laughed until it hurt, and slept again.

Dawn came gray and thin through the salt-fogged window. Tamsin had not meant to sleep, but she had — an hour, maybe two, chin on chest — and she woke to find the old man watching her with an expression she couldn't read, in a room gone quiet as the inside of a bell.

"It's time," he said simply. "Fetch the oilcloth, girl. We haven't much tide left — yours or mine."

And outside, far out past the breakwater, where the harbor met the open gray of the channel, a navy patrol cutter was standing in toward port, its wake a long white line drawn straight across the morning — as if the day itself had already been signed for.

Tamsin looked at it for a long moment. Then she reached for the oilcloth bundle on the table, and untied the twine.
