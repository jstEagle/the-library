## 3. A Map for a Promise

The room above the Saltgut was the kind of place Tamsin rented when she wanted to be forgotten: one bed with a mattress that had opinions, a window that looked out on the fish-rendering yard, and a lock that would stop an honest man and nothing else. She put Voss on the bed anyway, because the alternative was the floor, and the floor had a smell to it.

He weighed nothing. That was the thing that frightened her most. A grown man, a prospector who'd spent thirty years hauling himself in and out of water, and he came up her stairs like a bundle of wet rope.

"Sit him up," she told herself, out loud, because talking to herself was cheaper than talking to anyone else. "Sitting up keeps the water out of his lungs."

She'd learned that much from her mother, along with knot-tying, navigation, and how to leave a town before anyone learned your face. The medical knowledge was thinner on the ground than she'd have liked.

Voss coughed — a long, tearing sound, like canvas ripping — and brought up seawater. Actual seawater, hours after he'd last been under. Then blood, pink at first, then not.

"Bad," he said, when he could speak again. He said it almost cheerfully, the way another man might say *rain coming*.

"You're full of observations tonight." Tamsin wrung out his shirt over the basin. It was a good shirt once, oilcloth, the kind divers paid real money for, worn soft as cloth now at the elbows. "How long underwater without gear?"

"Long enough." He let her prop him against the wall. His eyes were still sharp, which seemed unfair given what the rest of him was doing. "You're Mara Montrose's girl."

"I'm nobody's girl."

"You're her girl. I saw you take that hull plate off the wreck at Kestrel Point." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth, and he didn't seem to care. "Clean work. Your mother dove dirtier but she dove prettier."

Tamsin's hands went still on the wet shirt.

"Careful," she said.

"With what?"

"With my mother's name. People who use it usually want something."

"Everyone wants something, girl. I want a glass of whatever you've got that isn't the Saltgut's rum, because the Saltgut's rum is a war crime."

She had tea, cheap black stuff gone stale in its paper, and she made it because it gave her hands something to do that wasn't shaking. He drank it in small sips, wincing, and between sips he watched her with those too-clear eyes, and she got the distinct feeling of being read like a chart.

"Your mother," Voss said, "died looking for something."

"Everyone says that. Everyone has a different something."

"I'm the only one who found it."

The lamp guttered. Downstairs, the Saltgut was getting loud in the way it did around midnight, when the longshoremen finished their second round and started inventing grievances. Tamsin sat down on the one chair, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

"Talk," she said.

* * *

He talked for two hours, and it went like this:

Thirty-one years ago, a colonial governor named Aldous Corvane — no relation to the ship, or rather exactly relation to the ship, having named it after himself the way small men name large things — loaded a treasure vessel in the Meridian islands and sailed it east under navy escort. The official manifest said trade goods: tin, spice, dye. The actual hold held the treasury of six Meridian families, seized under emergency decree, plus a strongroom full of papers the governor never intended to see daylight again.

"The ship went down off Kestrel Point in the autumn gales," Voss said. "Navy escort survived. Funny thing, that. Escort ships are fast. Treasure ships are slow. Somebody chose speed."

"And the families?"

"Ruined. Scattered. Some hanged themselves, some hanged others, the usual arithmetic of a stolen fortune. And the navy wrote the wreck down as a total loss with cargo of negligible value." He coughed, wet and deep. "Negligible. Thirty years of salvage men diving that reef, and every chart says *nothing here*. You know why?"

"Because somebody paid to make it say so."

"Because somebody paid to make it say so." He beamed at her, delighted, a dying man finding a student. "You do listen. Your mother listened too."

"Don't." She stood, took his cup, refilled it mostly to have somewhere to put her anger. "You keep doing that. Reaching for her. What did you want, Voss? Why come find me?"

"Because I'm dying, and dead men can't keep promises, and I've got one outstanding that's thirty years old." He took the cup. His hand shook; the tea trembled in rings. "I was on her crew. Your mother's. Last contract she ever ran."

The room got very quiet. Even the Saltgut downstairs seemed to lower its voice.

"She hired me as a diver," Voss said. "I was younger then. We were looking for the Corvane — she'd gotten a fragment of the story, same as everyone eventually does, except she believed it. She believed it the way other people believe in weather. We spent four months on that reef. Found the outer hull in the third month."

"And then?"

"And then someone sold us to the navy." He said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Reyes's patrol boat came out at dawn. Your mother ordered everyone into the water to scatter. Some of us made shore. She didn't."

"They arrested her."

"They testified against her. Salvage without license, trespass on a naval chart, conspiracy." He counted them off on fingers. "Reyes gave testimony. Clean, professional, devastating. Your mother got five years. She got pneumonia in the second winter and didn't finish them."

"I know how she died." Tamsin's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "I was fourteen. I read the letter."

"Then you know who signed the affidavit."

"I know Captain Ilsa Reyes signed it. I've known since I was fourteen. It's the reason I don't dive navy wrecks." She heard herself and almost laughed. "Except apparently I do, now, because the sea doesn't check my grudges at the door."

"Grudges are ballast," Voss said. "Useful, right up until they drag you under." He set down the cup. "Girl. Tamsin. Look at me."

She looked. Up close, in the lamplight, she could see what the dying was doing to him — the gray creeping in from the edges, the way his chest moved wrong, shallow and quick, like a bellows with a split seam.

"I found the strongroom," he said. "Two years ago. Before the sickness. I got into the antechamber and no further — the inner seal's a wheel mechanism, brass, salted shut, and I didn't have the crew or the time or, frankly, the years left. So I drew it. Everything. Every bearing, every depth, every deck I remember from your mother's survey and everything I added since." He pressed both hands flat against his ribs, as if holding something in. "It's all on paper. One copy. And it's yours."

"For what?" Tamsin asked, though she already knew. Dying men always wanted something, and it was always the thing you couldn't give.

"A promise."

* * *

He made her wait while he caught his breath, which was either cruelty or caution, and knowing prospectors it was probably both.

"Here's the shape of it," he said finally. "The monsoon comes early this year — I've watched the birds, I've watched the pressure, and I've been watching weather longer than you've been alive. When it comes, it comes hard, and it comes straight across Kestrel Point. After that, the Corvane is gone. Not damaged. Gone. The reef will shift, the sand will bury her past any diver's reach, and whatever's in that strongroom stays there until the sun burns out. You have maybe six tides of safe water. Six."

"Five and a half," Tamsin said. "The spring tides muck the visibility."

Voss grinned. "Oh, I like you."

"Everyone says that right before they ask for something impossible."

"The promise." He leaned forward, and the effort cost him visibly, and he kept leaning anyway. "Raise what's in the strongroom. Not the gold — there'll be gold, governor's treasury, more coin than you've ever seen, and you can swim through it singing for all I care. But inside, sealed separate, there are documents. Ledgers. Correspondence. A confession, if the rumors hold. Proof of what was taken and who took it and where it went." His voice dropped, roughened. "You give those documents to the families. The Meridian families. Whatever's left of them. You don't sell them, you don't trade them, you don't use them to buy your way out of trouble when the navy comes asking — and they will come asking. You put them in the hands of the people they were stolen from. That's the promise. That's the whole of it."

Tamsin sat with it.

She was good at sitting with things. It was the one skill her drifting life had perfected — sitting with discomfort, with silence, with the particular weight of a decision she'd already made and hadn't admitted yet. Because here was the truth of her, laid out plain: she did not make promises. Promises were anchors. She'd watched what promises did to her mother — pinned her to a reef, to a cause, to a crew that sold her out — and Tamsin had built her entire life on the opposite principle. Contracts, yes. Contracts ended. Wages, yes. Wages ran out clean. But promises? Promises followed you.

"Say the rest," she said. "There's a rest. There's always a rest."

"No rest." He spread his empty hands. "That's it. That's the whole trade. A map for a promise."

"What do you get?"

"Peace," he said, "or the closest thing I'm going to get to it." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "And maybe she gets some too. Wherever she is. If she's anywhere."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a bottle broke and a man laughed the laugh of a man about to regret the next ten minutes.

"Why me?" Tamsin asked. It was the question underneath all the other questions, the one that had been itching since he said her mother's name on the Saltgut stairs. "You could take this to any salvage captain on the coast. Someone with a real boat, a real crew, someone who isn't—" she gestured at herself, at the rented room, at the whole shape of her life, "—this."

"Because any other captain takes the gold and sells the papers to the highest bidder, and the highest bidder is the navy, and the navy burns them by Friday." He said it without heat, just fact. "But you — you're angry. Angry's not enough, mind, plenty of angry fools drown. But angry and honest, angry and stubborn, angry and *hers* — that's rarer. Your mother chose truth over fortune once. Cost her everything. I need someone stupid enough to think it was worth it." He smiled, and this time the smile was tired all the way down. "Turns out there's exactly one candidate."

"That's not a recommendation, Voss. That's an insult with extra steps."

"Most inheritances are."

"I'm not inheriting anything. She left me a knife and a letter and a lifetime of people saying *you look just like her* at the worst possible moments."

"Then leave it better than she could," he said. "That's all inheritance is."

Tamsin got up. She walked to the window, because she couldn't look at him while she thought, and looked out at the rendering yard, at the dark harbor beyond it, at the far black line where Kestrel Point lay under its forty fathoms with a dead governor's secrets in its belly. Six tides. Maybe five and a half. She had no boat worth the name, no crew, no money past the season's wages, and a navy hull plate stamped with an impossible serial number hidden in her kit that meant somebody official already knew she'd been poking where she shouldn't.

Every sensible cell in her body voted no.

"Fine," she said.

Voss blinked. "Fine?"

"Fine. Yes. Whatever word you want. I'll raise the strongroom before the monsoon, and the documents go to the families. All of them. Unopened, unsold, unspent." She turned around. "But hear the other half, old man, because a promise cuts both ways or it's just a leash. I do it my way. My boat, my crew, my decisions. If it goes wrong, the failure is mine, and you don't get to haunt me about it. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He was crying, she realized. Not sobbing — leaking, quietly, the way old hulls leak, from places that couldn't be patched. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and looked almost embarrassed. "Thirty years," he said. "You'd think a man would run out of tears before he ran out of time."

"Give me the map."

* * *

It was drawn on sailcloth, folded into eighths, soft as skin from handling. He'd done it in ink and pencil and, in places, what she sincerely hoped was rust stain. The Corvane's hull plan occupied the center — a three-masted colonial trader, 140 feet, lying on her port side at thirty-eight fathoms, keel buried to the turn of the bilge. Around the hull, dense as scripture, were his annotations: bearings taken off the reef heads, tide tables corrected in his own hand, current arrows, a sketch of the antechamber with the brass wheel drawn in careful cross-section.

And at the bottom corner, in smaller writing, almost shy:

*M. Montrose surveyed the outer hull, third month. Her marks are the ones in red. Keep them.*

Tamsin traced the red lines with one finger. Her mother's hand. Neater than hers, more patient. Four months of diving, preserved in ink, on a scrap of cloth that had crossed an ocean of years to end up in a rented room smelling of fish guts.

"She marked the strongroom approach," Tamsin said.

"She marked everything. She was thorough." Voss's eyes had begun to close. "Thorough and too trusting. Watch that you're neither."

"I'll aim for a middle course."

"Do." His breathing was going shallow, spaced further apart. "Tamsin. One more thing. The person who sold your mother — I never learned who. I've had thirty years to wonder and it's eaten me worse than the sickness. Could've been any of us. Could've been me, God forgive me, I was drinking in those days and I talked too much even sober. So when you raise those papers—" his eyes opened, fixed on her with sudden terrible clarity, "—watch your own crew. Trust is a luxury. Spend it carefully."

"Noted," Tamsin said. "Now sleep. You look like a drowned rat that lost an argument with a second ocean."

He slept. Or something near it.

She sat in the chair with the map across her knees and did not sleep at all. She read it until she could have redrawn it blind. She thought about contracts and promises and the difference between them, and about a woman in red survey marks who had chosen truth over fortune and been buried for it, and about whether Tamsin Montrose — who had never stayed anywhere longer than a contract, who had never promised anyone anything harder than *I'll be at the dock Tuesday* — was about to become the kind of person who kept faith with the dead.

Around four in the morning, the light in the harbor changed. She noticed it without understanding it at first — a strange green-gray cast over the water, the air going thick and sweet, the pressure settling on the town like a hand on the back of the neck.

Weather coming. Early.

She looked at Voss. He was very still, and his breathing had stretched from shallow to sparse to, somewhere in the last hour, absent. His face in the lamplight was peaceful in a way it hadn't been all night, the way a man looks when a debt's been handed off and the ledger's closed.

Tamsin checked for a pulse because you checked, and found what she'd known she'd find, and sat back down.

"Well," she said to the room, to the map, to the dead man and the dead mother and the whole quiet conspiracy of the sea. "That was easy."

Too easy. That was the thought that followed her to the window as the first fat drops of pre-storm rain began ticking against the glass. Promises made easily were the ones that collected interest. Somewhere out past the harbor bar, under a sky turning the color of a bruise, a wreck waited with her mother's handwriting on its bones, six tides of safe water — fewer now, maybe, if that green light meant what she feared it meant — and a navy that had already shown her, once, how quickly it moved when someone got curious.

She folded the map into eighths, the creases falling into their old habits, and tucked it inside her jacket, against her heart, where inconvenient things belong.

Then she went downstairs to find a boat, a crew, and a miracle, in roughly that order of difficulty.

The barkeep looked up as she came through, took in her face, and reached for the good bottle behind the bad ones. "Trouble?" he asked.

"Worse," Tamsin said. "A deadline."
