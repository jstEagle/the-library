## 4. Six Tides

The tide tables at the Kestrel Point harbormaster's office were pinned to the wall behind a counter so scarred it looked like it had survived its own shipwreck. Tamsin Montrose stood in front of them for the better part of an hour, a pencil behind her ear and Voss's chart folded in her jacket pocket like something alive that might bite her if she moved wrong.

The harbormaster — a woman named Pell with forearms like dock rope and the patient expression of someone who had watched a thousand divers do arithmetic badly — let her stand there without comment for the first forty minutes. On the fifty-first minute, she set down a chipped mug of coffee on the counter beside Tamsin's elbow.

"You've been staring at that wall like it owes you money," Pell said.

"It does, sort of." Tamsin took the coffee because refusing free coffee was against her religion. "I need to know when the monsoon makes the outer shoals undivable."

Pell's eyebrows went up a fraction. The outer shoals were where sensible people did not dive even in fair weather. "You're working out there? This time of year?"

"I'm asking about it."

"Uh-huh." Pell came around the counter, which Tamsin understood to be a significant gesture, the way a cat showing you its belly is a significant gesture. She tapped one thick finger against the tide table. "All right. Storm season runs early this year — the barometers have been sulking for a week, and the gulls went quiet Tuesday, which I don't love. First real blow should make landfall—" she counted days on her fingers, lips moving slightly, "—nineteen days. Maybe twenty-two if we're lucky. We're never lucky."

"Nineteen days," Tamsin repeated. Her stomach did something complicated.

"But," Pell said, holding up the finger that meant *but*, "the diving window closes before the storm does. You get maybe six good tides after day ten. After that the swell starts stacking and the visibility goes to soup, and anything you're doing down there becomes a way to die with extra steps."

Tamsin turned back to the tables and made herself look at them properly, not the way she'd been looking — the way you look at a bill you can't pay. Six tides. She counted them out in her head: slack water at dawn, slack at dusk, the long midday lulls between. Six windows of an hour, maybe ninety minutes each if the weather behaved, which weather did not.

Six chances to find a strongroom on a wreck nobody had located in thirty years, cut into it, and open it.

She laughed. It came out as more of a bark, and Pell gave her the look reserved for customers who had finally done their sums.

"That bad?"

"That bad." Tamsin drained the coffee. It was terrible, burnt and over-salted by the sea air, and it was the best thing she'd tasted all week. "Harbormaster. Hypothetically. If a person wanted to hire a boat and crew on short notice—"

"On this dock?" Pell snorted. "Hypothetically, they'd be hiring from what's left after the navy swept through. Which isn't much. Which might be why you're asking me instead of just doing it."

Tamsin went still. "The navy swept through."

"Two days ago. Requisitioned three hulls, bought every tank of compressed gas on the island at double rate so nobody else could afford any, and posted a salvage exclusion zone over the whole eastern shoal line." Pell shrugged, but her eyes stayed on Tamsin, sharp under the shaggy gray brows. "Officially it's a hazard survey. Unofficially, my nephew works the naval telegraph office, and he says the fleet orders came down signed by a name he wasn't allowed to say out loud because the other clerks got quiet when he read it."

"What name?"

Pell glanced toward the door, then leaned in, lowering her voice as though the name itself might be requisitioned. "Reyes. Captain Ilsa Reyes."

* * *

Tamsin walked out of the harbormaster's office and kept walking, past the fish stalls and the chandlery and the pawnshop where she'd once hocked her mother's spare regulator to buy two weeks of food, until she reached the seawall at the end of the breakwater and sat down on it with her boots dangling over water the color of hammered tin.

Reyes.

There were names you carried like stones in your pocket and names that carried you, dragging you along whether you wanted to go or not. Ilsa Reyes was both.

Tamsin had been nine years old the first time Reyes put a regulator in her hands. Not her mother — her mother was off on a contract, always off on a contract — but Reyes, who was twenty-eight then and already famous, already *the* Captain Reyes of the salvage brig *Merrow's Debt*, who came to dinner at their rented house above the cove and found a skinny kid drawing wrecks on the back of cargo manifests.

"You draw the hull lines wrong," Reyes had said, studying the paper over Tamsin's shoulder. "Here. A ship doesn't sink straight down. She argues with the water all the way." And she'd picked up the pencil and shown her, and Tamsin had thought, with the absolute certainty available only to nine-year-olds, that this was the most important person alive.

Reyes taught her to equalize before her mother did. Reyes taught her to read a current by the way kelp leaned. Reyes taught her the rule that lived in Tamsin's spine to this day: *the sea keeps what it takes unless you're smarter than it, and being smarter means knowing when to quit.* Reyes had held her hand over the side of a dive boat, eleven years old and terrified, and told her that fear was just information, and that a diver who couldn't read her own fear would drown reading everything else fine.

And then, when Tamsin was fourteen, Reyes had stood up in a naval inquiry room with brass buttons and a steady voice and testified that Mara Montrose had been diving outside her licensed depth on a protected wreck, in violation of salvage law, against direct warning — testimony that stripped her mother's license, her boat, and eventually, by slow degrees, everything else. Her mother had never dived again. She'd taken a shore job packing crates for a shipping concern and died four years later of a fever that a woman who spent her life at sea should probably have been immune to, as if her body had simply decided there was no point.

Tamsin had been eighteen. She'd gone to see Reyes once, afterward, at a naval reception in Meridian City, and Reyes had looked at her across the room with an expression Tamsin had spent fifteen years failing to decode — not guilt, exactly, and not coldness either. Something more like recognition. Like looking at a wreck you yourself had scuttled.

Then Tamsin had left. Port to port, contract to contract, never longer than the work required. That was the arrangement she had with the world: nothing stays, so don't stay either.

And now Reyes was coming to the Corvane. With a fleet. With requisitioned gas and a sealed exclusion zone and nineteen days of monsoon breathing down everyone's neck.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to the water. The water didn't answer, which she appreciated. Water was honest that way.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop doing the math on: if the navy knew about the Corvane — if they'd mobilized a fleet, sealed the shoals — then either they'd gotten the location from somewhere, or they were still looking. And if they were still looking, then Voss's chart was worth more than gold. It was worth a head start.

She unfolded the chart right there on the seawall, weighting the corners with two shells and her boot. Voss's hand was shaky but sure — depth soundings in fathoms, a reef drawn in careful hatching, and the wreck marked with a small, stubborn X inside the leeward elbow of the shoal, where the coral grew thick enough to hide a hull from sonar. Notes in the margin in the same trembling script: *strongroom below waterline, aft of the magazine. Wheel seal. Take the papers, girl. Leave the rest.*

Leave the rest. As if there were a *rest*. As if a colonial governor's stolen fortune were an afterthought.

Tamsin stared at the X until the ink seemed to swim, and thought about six tides, and about a woman with brass buttons bearing down on the same patch of ocean, and about a promise made to a dying man that had seemed, at the time, like the easiest thing in the world.

*Finish what I started.*

She'd said yes because he was dying and because saying yes to dying men costs nothing. Now it cost six tides, a boat she didn't have, a crew she didn't have, and a confrontation with the person who had taught her everything she knew about the sea and then helped take her mother away from it.

"Right," she said, folding the chart. "So that's the easy part sorted."

* * *

That evening she went back to the Salt & Cinder, because it was the only bar in Kestrel Point where the regulars wouldn't ask questions, mostly because they were all people questions were dangerous to ask.

It was quieter than the night she'd patched up Ede Voss. The same lamp flickered over the same scarred tables; the same one-eared dog occupied the same corner. But Voss's stool by the stove was empty, and someone had wiped the blood off the floorboards, and the barman — a soft-spoken giant called Dunny who owed Tamsin for pulling his nets out of a propeller two seasons back — slid her a beer unasked and said, quietly, "Heard you took him in that night. Heard he didn't make the morning."

"He made the morning," Tamsin said. "Barely. He died at dawn."

Dunny nodded slowly, wiping a glass that was already clean. "Good of you. Most folk would've rolled him into the street."

"Most folk would've been robbed. He paid." She hadn't mentioned the chart to anyone and wasn't going to start, but grief sat strangely in her chest — she'd known the man eight hours and buried him twelve, and somehow he'd wedged himself in deeper than people she'd known for years. Maybe that was what promises did. They were barnacles. They attached to whatever was closest and made moving expensive.

She was halfway through the beer when the door opened and the room changed.

It didn't go silent — the Salt & Cinder was too well-practiced for silence — but the conversation dropped half a register, the way water drops before a squall. Two men in navy shore uniform came in, scanned the room with the flat professional boredom of men hunting paperwork rather than trouble, and walked to the bar.

Tamsin kept her head down and her glass up and listened.

"—exclusion zone's posted at the harbor office," one was telling Dunny. "Eastern shoals, effective immediately. Any vessel caught inside the boundary will be impounded and the master charged. Salvage rights in the sector are suspended pending naval operations."

"Suspension of rights needs a writ," Dunny said mildly. "This is a free port."

"This is a port with a naval coaling station," the sailor said, "and a fleet arriving Thursday. Writs are coming. Consider this a courtesy." He set a printed notice on the bar. "Post it where your customers can read it."

Dunny looked at the notice, then at the sailor, then — briefly, deliberately, in a way only Tamsin caught — at Tamsin. "I'll pin it up," he said. "Might be some folk can't read. I'll read it to them too."

The sailors left. The room exhaled. Tamsin turned her glass slowly on the sticky wood and did the last of the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the seawall.

A fleet arriving Thursday. Under Reyes. Sealing the shoals before anyone else could get near them — which meant the navy didn't have the location yet, or they'd have gone straight in and saved themselves the theater. They were sweeping. Dredging, probably, methodically, the way Reyes did everything: grid squares, no sentiment, the whole seabird coast combed like a pocket for lost coins.

Which meant the race wasn't abstract anymore. It had a shape, a schedule, and a face.

Tamsin finished her beer and laid coins on the bar, and Dunny leaned across as she stood, voice pitched under the lamplight hum.

"You're going out there, aren't you."

"I'm considering my options."

"You're going out there." He shook his head, but he was smiling a little, the way people smile at weather they can't stop. "You'll need a boat. There's a survey hull rotting at the north moorings — the *Kestrel's Wake*. Owner died in the winter, estate's been arguing about it ever since. She's ugly and she leaks, but she's got a sound keel and an engine bay big enough to work in, if you knew an engineer mad enough."

"I don't know any engineers."

"You know *of* one. Brannoc Hale. Worked the dredge barges till he rebuilt a governor's yacht engine without permission and made it better, and the yard blacklisted him for touching navy-spec parts. Drinks here Thursdays. Sits where Voss used to sit." Dunny straightened up, resumed wiping his clean glass. "Course, that's Thursday. Tomorrow there's a girl comes round the fish market who reads currents for the net boats — Sefa, they call her. Seventeen, maybe. Knows the shoal waters better than the charts do, and the charts know them pretty well. And there's an old man at the records hall, Ovid Marsh, used to be an archivist before he drank half his career. If it's the *Corvane* you're after—" and here Dunny's voice went very casual indeed, "—he's the man who'd know her manifest."

Tamsin stared at him. "How do you know what I'm after?"

"I don't." Dunny smiled properly then, wide and gap-toothed. "But you asked Pell about the outer shoals, you sat on the seawall for two hours looking like a woman doing sums she hates, and you flinched when the navy boys said *Thursday*. I keep a bar. Reading customers is the trade."

Tamsin laughed despite herself, a short, surprised sound. Then she looked at the empty stool by the stove, where a dying man had traded her a map for a promise, and felt the weight of the six tides settle onto her shoulders like a wet coat.

Nineteen days. Six windows. One rotting boat, three strangers, and a captain with brass buttons and a fleet coming to bury the truth in the deepest hole she could dig.

"Thursday," Tamsin said. "What time does Brannoc usually start drinking?"

"Early," said Dunny. "But he slows down around the third pint. That's your window."

Tamsin drained the last inch of her beer, touched two fingers to the empty stool — a salute, or an apology, she wasn't sure to whom — and pushed open the door into the salt wind, where the first far-off thunder of the season was muttering on the horizon like something clearing its throat.

Six tides. She'd better start spending them.
