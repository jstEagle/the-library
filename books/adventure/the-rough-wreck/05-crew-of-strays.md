## 5. Crew of Strays

The harbor at Lowmark was the kind of place that kept its secrets the way a bilge keeps water — reluctantly, and only until someone pumped. Tamsin walked the seawall with Voss's chart folded against her ribs, counting the people she'd need and the people the port actually had, and finding the arithmetic ugly on both sides.

She needed an engineer, a navigator who could read water instead of paper, and someone who knew what the *Corvane* had been carrying besides rumor. What Lowmark offered, at seven in the morning, was a man arguing with a winch, a girl stealing bait, and a drunk asleep in the archive doorway.

It would have to do.

* * *

Brannoc Hale was arguing with the winch because the winch deserved it.

Tamsin found him knee-deep in the guts of a crab boat's deck machinery, sleeves rolled to the shoulder, forearms striped with old burns and new grease. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray coming in at the temples like frost on iron, and he was addressing the winch drum in a low, reasonable tone that suggested this was not the first conversation they'd had today.

"No," he was saying. "You don't get to do that. I know what you did. You know what you did."

"Trouble?" Tamsin asked.

He didn't startle. That was the first thing she noted about him — a man so used to things going wrong around him that a stranger's voice barely registered as a threat. He wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands and looked her over with the flat professional assessment of a man reading a hull for stress fractures.

"You're standing on my foot," he said.

She stepped aside. "I'm told you're the best engineer in Lowmark."

"I'm told that too." He went back to the winch. "I'm also told I'm a drunk, a fraud, and the reason the *Merry Adeline* sits on the bottom of the channel with forty tons of frozen fish. People tell you all sorts of things when you've been disgraced. It's practically a pension."

"The *Merry Adeline* wasn't your fault. I read the inquiry."

That got him. His hands stopped moving on the drum, just for a second, then resumed. "The inquiry was a farce. The owner cut the maintenance budget to buy himself a bigger house, and when she went down he needed someone to blame who couldn't afford lawyers." He snorted. "You read the inquiry. Lovely. And what did the inquiry say about me, exactly?"

"It said your testimony contradicted itself three times."

"Because they asked me three times whether I'd inspected the stern gland, and I'd inspected it three different ways depending on which version of events kept me out of prison." He finally turned to look at her properly. "Who are you?"

"Tamsin Montrose."

Something flickered across his face — recognition, then something more careful behind it. "Montrose. The diver. The one who works alone and leaves before anyone learns her surname."

"That's the one."

"And now you're here learning mine." He sat back on an upturned crate, wincing. "So either you're hiring or you're arresting. I've had both. The hiring paid worse."

"I'm hiring. One contract. Six tides of work, maybe less if the weather turns. Salvage dive support — engine, pumps, winch work, and keeping a rotten survey boat alive through all of it."

"What boat?"

"*Kestrel's Wake.*"

Brannoc laughed — a short, disbelieving bark. "The *Kestrel's Wake*. I know that boat. She's been tied up at the breakwater since before the war, rotting into a public nuisance. Half the harbor master's complaints file is about her." He shook his head. "You want me to keep *that* alive through monsoon-season salvage?"

"I want you to try."

"Why? Why me? There are engineers in this port who haven't been publicly ruined."

"Because the ruined ones are cheap," Tamsin said. "And because the ones who aren't ruined ask fewer questions and answer to other masters. I need someone whose only loyalty is to the work."

Brannoc studied her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the winch, at the crab boat, at the gray morning and the gray water and the rest of his gray life stretching out ahead of him like a becalmed sea.

"Pay?" he said.

"Half now, half on completion. Plus salvage shares if we raise anything worth sharing."

"Shares." He said the word like it tasted of rust. "Last time I took shares instead of wages, I ended up owning a third of a lawsuit." He rubbed his jaw. "One condition. Nobody asks me what happened aboard the *Adeline*, and nobody tells me what we're really after until I've already said yes. I work better ignorant."

Tamsin thought of Voss's chart against her ribs, of the navy hull plate stamped with a serial that shouldn't exist, of Reyes's fleet already mobilizing somewhere south. Ignorance sounded luxurious.

"Done," she said.

They shook on it. His grip was strong and careful, the grip of a man who had learned exactly how much force things could take before they broke — and how much he could take before he did.

"One more thing," Brannoc said as she turned to go. "The *Kestrel's Wake* — her engine is a Marlowe 40, and the last man who touched her was a butcher. When I open her up tomorrow, whatever I find in there, I'm going to swear. A lot. In front of whoever's aboard."

"Noted," Tamsin said. "There'll be swearing."

"There always is," said Brannoc, and turned back to his winch.

* * *

Sefa stole bait because it was there, and because hunger made philosophers of everyone eventually.

She was fifteen, maybe sixteen — even she'd stopped being sure — small and quick and brown as a nut, with hair cropped short against lice and a knife she'd won off a dockhand in a game she'd cheated at. She lived in the spaces between the fish market's legal hours, and she was very good at it, which was why it annoyed her deeply when the tall woman caught her wrist mid-reach without even looking.

"Fast hands," the woman said.

"Let go."

"Or what? You'll bite me?"

"I might."

The woman let go. Sefa backed off two steps and sized her up properly: diver's shoulders, salt-bleached hair, eyes that did the thing Sefa hated most in adults — the thing where they looked at you and saw a problem to be solved rather than a person to be avoided.

"You're the one who reads currents," the woman said.

Sefa's stomach dropped. Being known was dangerous. Being known by strangers was worse. "I don't know what you mean."

"Three weeks ago, the pilot boats lost a skiff off the north point in a cross-current. Everyone said drift east. You told the harbormaster's boy to search west of the marker buoy, and they found the skiff there, crew alive." The woman tilted her head. "Nobody taught you that. You just see it."

Sefa said nothing. Saying nothing had kept her alive this long.

"I'm putting together a crew," the woman went on. "Salvage job. Six tides, maybe less. I need someone who can look at water and tell me where it's going and what it's hiding. Pay is real. Food is included."

"I don't work for people."

"Everybody works for somebody. Even free people. They just pick who."

"I pick nobody."

"Then you'll pick hunger," the woman said, not unkindly, "and hunger picks for you."

Sefa hated her a little for that, mostly because it was true. She looked past the woman's shoulder at the harbor — at the pilot boats and the patrol skiffs and the navy tender sitting fat and smug at the fuel dock, and felt the old cold crawl up her spine. The navy tender meant patrols. Patrols meant questions. Questions were bad, but staying in Lowmark was getting worse by the week; the harbormaster's boy had started asking around about a girl matching her description, and the description was getting closer every time.

A salvage job. Six tides. Out of port, out of sight, paid in food and coin.

"Where's the job?" she asked.

The woman smiled slightly, like a door opening a crack. "North. Past Kestrel Point."

Something in Sefa's chest went tight and strange. North. Toward the fleet anchorage. Toward the waters where the navy ships ran their grids and their searches and their quiet, patient looking.

"Who else is crew?" she asked, buying time.

"An engineer named Brannoc. An archivist named Ovid. Me."

"A washed-up drunk, a book-man, and you." Sefa crossed her arms. "What happens when it goes wrong?"

"Then we go wrong together," the woman said simply. "My name's Montrose. Tamsin Montrose."

Sefa knew that name too — from dock talk, from the way grown divers said it with respect and a little fear. She weighed it against the cold feeling in her chest, against the harbormaster's boy and his questions, against another winter of stolen bait.

"Dawn after next," she said. "If the boat floats. If the food's real. If anybody touches me, I'm gone over the side and you can explain to the coast watch why your diver support drowned."

"Fair terms," Tamsin Montrose said, and held out her hand.

Sefa didn't take it. "I don't shake. I just show up."

"Also fair," said Montrose, and walked away, and Sefa stood there among the fish stalls with her heart going fast, telling herself she'd taken the deal because of the food and the money and definitely, absolutely nothing to do with the fact that for the first time in two years, someone had looked at her quick hands and called them useful instead of criminal.

* * *

Ovid Lasseter was asleep in the archive doorway when Tamsin found him, and woke arguing.

"—entirely out of order, the 1847 shipping registers are stored by quarter, not by year, and if you file them by year I will haunt you personally—"

"Good morning," Tamsin said.

He blinked up at her from under a coat that had once been good wool and was now mostly memory. He was perhaps fifty-five, thin as a filing error, with ink stains on his fingers that no amount of salt water would ever fully lift, and spectacles mended twice with wire. Behind him, through the archive's barred windows, she could see the shelves marching away into the dark like a fleet at anchor.

"You're blocking my office," he said.

"This is a doorstep."

"It has excellent light and I've been assigned to it. The new administration considers me redundant. Redundant men are given doorsteps." He gathered his dignity around him like the coat. "State your business. If you're from the harbormaster, I already apologized for the pamphlets."

"I'm not from the harbormaster. I need to know about a ship. The *Corvane*."

The change in him was immediate and total. The drunken vagueness fell away like a mask slipping, and underneath was something sharp and quick and hungry — a mind that had been waiting years for someone to ask it exactly this question.

"Sit down," Ovid said.

"There's nowhere to sit."

"Then stand comfortably. This will take some time." He pushed the spectacles up his nose. "The *Corvane*. Colonial registry 4471. Built Harwick Yards, 1839, rated at six hundred tons. Officially listed as a merchantman carrying general cargo — textiles, hardware, specie for the colonial treasury. She sailed from Meridian in the autumn of '44 and went down off Kestrel Point in a gale with all hands." He paused. "All of that is what the record says. Would you like to hear what the record doesn't say?"

"Yes."

Ovid's eyes gleamed. "The *Corvane*'s manifest was amended three times in the week before she sailed. Three amendments, each one reducing the declared value of her cargo. By the final version, she was carrying eleven thousand crowns of specie. Eleven thousand. For a colonial treasury run, in a season when the Meridian accounts were moving nearly four hundred thousand." He held up an ink-stained finger. "Someone wanted the official record to show a ship full of almost nothing. Which means either the clerk was incompetent — and I knew the clerk, he was a dullard but honest — or someone very powerful wanted the *Corvane* to sink as a ship carrying nothing worth saving."

Tamsin felt the chart press against her ribs. "And the missing value?"

"Gone. Unrecorded. Or recorded elsewhere, under seal, in a file I spent nine years trying to reach before the administration decided I was redundant." He sighed, and some of the sharpness dimmed back into tiredness. "I catalogued those papers once, you know. Long ago. Before I understood what I was holding. I put them in order, I numbered them, I filed them where I was told. And I have spent every year since wondering what exactly I helped bury."

"You think the *Corvane* was carrying stolen goods."

"I think," Ovid said carefully, "that a governor does not amend a manifest three times unless he is hiding something worth amending for. And I think that if a certain prospector named Ede Voss spent the last decade of his life diving a wreck off Kestrel Point, he may have known something the archives only whisper." He looked at her sharply. "Voss. Is he alive?"

"He died eight days ago."

Ovid closed his eyes briefly. "Ah. Then the map has passed to someone." It wasn't quite a question.

"To me."

"Then you're either a fool or an heir," Ovid said. "In my experience the two are frequently indistinguishable." He struggled upright, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "You'll want me along, I suppose. Manifests, deck plans, compartment layouts, cargo handling records. I can tell you where the strongroom is likely to be, how she was loaded, which bulkheads were strengthened for heavy cargo. I can save you tides of blind cutting."

"I can't pay much."

"I don't want pay." He waved a hand at the doorstep, the street, the gray harbor beyond. "Look at my situation. I have a coat, a doorstep, and thirty years of guilt about files I should never have shelved. You're offering me a chance to unshelve them. That's not employment, Miss Montrose. That's absolution, and I intend to grab it with both ink-stained hands." He stuck out a bony finger. "One condition. Whatever we find — documents, letters, sealed papers — I read them first. Before you, before anyone. If there's truth in that strongroom, I want to meet it face to face. I've earned that much."

Tamsin hesitated. Everything in her that had survived twenty years of solo work said *never give anyone first look at the prize*. But Voss had made her promise documents to families, not gold to herself, and promises had a way of needing witnesses.

"First look," she agreed. "But they don't leave the boat."

"They won't need to," said Ovid, with a small, fierce smile. "Truth travels well in good company."

* * *

The *Kestrel's Wake* smelled of tar, rot, and thirty years of other people's regrets, and Brannoc loved her instantly and completely.

He stood in her engine room the morning after signing on, lantern swinging overhead, and beheld the Marlowe 40 the way a priest beholds a desecrated altar.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, you poor girl."

The engine cover came off with a shriek of seized bolts. What lay beneath made him close his eyes and breathe through his nose for a count of ten.

"Somebody," he announced to the empty engine room, "has packed this bearing with tallow. *Tallow.* This isn't maintenance, this is a crime scene. I want everyone responsible identified and shunned."

"You're talking to an engine," said Sefa, from the companionway, where she had appeared out of nowhere as she seemed to do, watching him with frank curiosity.

"I'm talking *at* an engine. Talking *to* it comes later, when we're negotiating." He pointed a greasy finger at her. "Who are you, then? The current-reader?"

"Sefa."

"You stow food in the bilge access, did you know? Under the floor plate by the chain locker. Bad idea — damp gets in, biscuits go soft, rats find them. Use the void space aft of the fuel tank instead. Cooler, drier, and nobody looks there." He turned back to the engine. "Welcome aboard."

Sefa stared at him. "...How did you know about the food?"

"I refit boats for a living. Hiding places are just badly designed storage." He patted the engine block almost tenderly. "Now make yourself useful. There's a spanner in that toolbox, the long one with the taped handle. Hand it here and don't drop it, and I'll tell you why this engine is going to get us killed and how I'm going to stop it."

Sefa found the spanner. She handed it over. And when Brannoc began to swear — fluently, creatively, at length, exactly as promised — she stayed, and listened, and somewhere in the middle of the third creative blasphemy she realized she was smiling, and that the smile felt strange in her face, like a muscle remembered from long ago.

* * *

By evening of the second day, the whole strange company had assembled aboard the *Kestrel's Wake* for the first time, and Tamsin stood at the rail and watched them fail to cohere.

Brannoc had claimed the engine room and defended it like territory, emerging only to eat and to complain about the state of the deck winch. Sefa had claimed the forepeak and defended it like a fortress, appearing at meals to eat silently and vanish again. Ovid had claimed the chart table, spread it with papers, and fallen asleep on top of them, spectacles askew, one hand still resting protectively on a photocopy of the *Corvane*'s amended manifest.

"We look like a crew," Tamsin said to Ovid, who had woken enough to grunt.

"We look like four people on a boat," Ovid corrected. "A crew is what we become when the sea tries to kill us and fails. Give it time. Or weather."

"Six tides," Tamsin said. "We don't have time or weather."

"Then we shall become a crew very quickly indeed." Ovid removed his spectacles, polished them on his ancient coat, and put them back on. "Miss Montrose. May I offer an observation, unsolicited and probably unwelcome?"

"You will anyway."

"When I agreed to come, I told you I wanted absolution. When Mr. Hale signed on, he told you he worked better ignorant. And our young friend—" he nodded toward the forepeak, where a shadow shifted away from the hatch, "—told you she doesn't shake hands. Do you notice the pattern?"

"Everyone wants something."

"Everyone is hiding something," Ovid said. "Which is different, and more dangerous, and possibly more useful. A crew of strays, all running from separate fires, huddled on one small boat. The sea doesn't care what we're fleeing. But the sea is fair, in its way — it tests us all equally, and it doesn't ask why we came." He settled back against the chart table. "The question is whether we'll ask each other. Eventually. Probably at the worst possible moment."

Tamsin looked out at the darkening harbor, at the navy tender's lights at the fuel dock, at the black line of the horizon where Kestrel Point waited with its forbidden wreck and its buried dead.

"Then we'd better become a crew," she said, "before the worst possible moment finds us still four strangers."

Below decks, Brannoc swore at the engine. Above, the first stars came out over Lowmark, indifferent and bright. And in the forepeak, Sefa lay awake listening to the hull creak, one hand closed around the small brass compass she'd taken from her grandfather's shop the night she ran — the night the navy came for the family, though she'd never told anyone that part — and thought about north, and Kestrel Point, and the scout ship she'd seen once before, years ago, in waters where no scout ship had any business being.

Dawn after next, they sailed.

None of them slept well. All of them showed up anyway.
