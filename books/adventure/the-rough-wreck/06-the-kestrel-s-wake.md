## 6. The Kestrel's Wake

The boat was called the *Kestrel's Wake*, which Brannoc felt was a cruel joke on the part of whoever had named her, because nothing about her suggested flight or speed or even, at this point, buoyancy. She sat in the mud of the Kestrel Point backwater like something the tide had given up on and forgotten to take back. Her hull was green with weed below the waterline and grey with neglect above it. Her wheelhouse windows were fogged from the inside, as though she were breathing shallowly and hoping nobody would notice.

Brannoc stood on the rickety pier with his hands on his hips and delivered his professional assessment.

"She's dead."

"Good," said Tamsin Montrose, beside him. "Dead things don't argue."

He looked at her. She was watching the wreck of the survey boat with the particular flat hunger he'd already learned to recognize — the look of a woman pricing salvage in her head while pretending to have a conversation.

"You want to buy a corpse," Brannoc said.

"I want to buy a hull," Tamsin said. "Hulls can be resurrected. Engines can be resurrected. Everything else is furniture." She turned to him, and there it was again, that thing she did where she made an outrageous request sound like a reasonable one simply by refusing to blink. "You said you could fix anything."

"I said I could fix *most* things. There's a difference, and the difference is usually money."

"We don't have money."

"Then we're fixing most things."

Tamsin almost smiled. It was a small movement, quickly suppressed, like a bird deciding against landing. "Old man Ferrow wants four hundred for her. He'll take two-fifty if you insult the engine loudly enough."

"The engine deserves worse than insults," Brannoc said. "That's a Meridian Marine Series Nine. They stopped making parts for those before I was born. That's not an engine, that's a religious artifact."

"So you know how to fix it."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You've been staring at her funnel for two minutes with your head tilted like she insulted your mother." Tamsin stepped past him onto the pier, which groaned under her boots in a way Brannoc chose to find ominous rather than instructive. "Come on. Let's go hurt Ferrow's feelings."

* * *

They bought the *Kestrel's Wake* for two hundred and forty, after Brannoc spent twenty minutes describing the Series Nine engine in terms that would have gotten him excommunicated from any respectable engineering guild, if he'd still belonged to one. Old Ferrow took the insults with the serenity of a man who had heard them all before and sold the boat anyway, and threw in a crate of mismatched fittings and a bilge pump that worked "on Sundays," by which he meant never.

Brannoc moved aboard that same night, because a boat being refitted needed a body on her to keep thieves honest, and because his room above the chandlery cost more per week than his dignity could bear. He spread his tools across the saloon table — his actual tools, the good ones, the ones he'd carried out of the Meridian yards in a canvas roll when everything else he owned had gone to creditors — and looked at them lying there in the lamplight like a surgeon's kit laid out before a battlefield amputation.

"Well," he told the boat. "Let's see what we're working with."

What they were working with, he discovered over the next three days, was this: a sound keel with rot in two strakes near the stern; a rudder stock worn to a knife edge; a Series Nine engine that had seized so long ago it had achieved a kind of geological permanence; electrical wiring that appeared to have been installed by someone who hated both electricity and boats; and a fuel tank containing, when he drained it, a sludge that smelled strongly of old fish and faintly of arson.

He wrote it all down in a ledger, because ledgers were how he kept the panic at bay. Numbers in columns. Problems in rows. A problem in a row was a task. A task had a solution, or at least a workaround, and a workaround was just a solution with worse manners.

By the fourth day he had the engine apart on the pier, its components arranged in the order he'd removed them, gleaming with degreaser, looking less like a corpse and more like a very complicated jigsaw puzzle someone had spilled.

That was the morning Sefa arrived.

* * *

She came down the pier at low tide with a seabag over one shoulder and an expression of elaborate unconcern, the way teenagers do when they are trying very hard to seem like they belong somewhere and have always belonged there.

"You're the engineer," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the engineer," Brannoc agreed, not looking up from the cylinder head he was lapping with fine paste.

"Tamsin said you needed hands."

"Tamsin says a lot of things." He glanced up then. The girl couldn't have been more than sixteen, all elbows and salt-stiff hair, with eyes that went everywhere at once — the tools, the engine parts, the boat, the water, the far end of the pier — cataloguing everything, committing it all somewhere behind those quick dark irises. "Can you hold a spanner without dropping it in the drink?"

"Yes."

"Can you tell me what size?"

She looked at the bolt he nodded toward, then at the scattered spanners, and picked the right one on the first try. Brannoc raised his eyebrows despite himself.

"Huh," he said. "Where'd you learn engines?"

"Places," Sefa said, and crouched down beside him, and held out her hand for the next tool, and that was apparently the whole of the answer he was going to get.

He hired her on the spot, mostly because his back hurt and partly because anyone who could pick a fourteen-millimetre spanner out of chaos at a glance had either trained in a proper yard or grown up stealing from one, and Brannoc had learned long ago that the second kind often knew more than the first.

He did not ask about the seabag. He would think about that later, in the dark, and wish he had.

* * *

The work settled into a rhythm that Brannoc would remember afterward as the last easy week of all their lives, though it hadn't felt easy at the time. Mornings were for the engine: freeing the seized pistons with penetrating oil and patience, machining new bearing shells on a lathe borrowed from a man named Coss who accepted payment in flattery and small repairs. Afternoons were for the hull: Brannoc below in the bilge with a chisel and a lamp, cutting out rotten wood while Sefa fed him replacement stock and held things and asked questions — sharp ones, good ones, the kind that suggested she wasn't just learning but auditing.

"Why not replace the whole strake instead of sistering it?"

"Because whole strake means pulling the planking off both frames, and we don't have the time or the timber."

"But sistering is a patch."

"Sistering is a *principled* patch," Brannoc said, mouth full of chisel. "There's a difference. A patch lies about what it does. A sister tells the truth: I'm weak here, so here's someone strong standing beside me." He spat out a curl of wet rot. "Boats are honest that way. People aren't."

Sefa considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. "Which are you?"

"A bit of both. Pass the chisel mallet."

Ovid came by on the sixth day, carrying books. This was, Brannoc would learn, simply what Ovid did: he arrived places carrying books the way other men arrived carrying grudges. He stood at the top of the boarding ladder holding a leather satchel against his chest like a life vest and peered down into the engine compartment with an expression of polite horror.

"This is where I'm to live," Ovid said.

"It's where you're to *work*," Brannoc said. "Living happens in the fo'c'sle, which has two bunks and a smell I haven't identified yet."

"There are charts?" Ovid asked. "Shelves? Somewhere for the manifest materials?"

"There's a drawer. You can have the drawer."

Ovid looked at the drawer, which was roughly the size of a hymnal, and then at Brannoc, and Brannoc watched him perform some internal arithmetic involving thirty years of archive dust and the open sea, and arrive at a total he clearly hated.

"The drawer will do," Ovid said, with the air of a man signing away territory in a treaty he expected to repudiate later.

"That's the spirit."

"It is not remotely the spirit. But it is the arrangement." Ovid descended the ladder with tremendous care, set his satchel on the saloon table, opened it, and began unpacking books into the drawer with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. Brannoc caught titles as they went by: *Registry of Colonial Shipping, 1841–1866*. *Hydrographic Notices, Southern Reaches*. Something with *Treasury* in the spine that Ovid slid in face-down, quickly, like a card player hiding an ace.

Brannoc decided not to wonder about it. He had enough mysteries. The engine was providing one free of charge.

* * *

The engine came alive on the ninth day, at dusk, in a manner that nearly ended all their careers at once.

Brannoc had spent the afternoon reassembling her — pistons home, head torqued in sequence, fuel lines blown clear, fresh diesel in the tank that had previously held fish-flavored arson. Sefa was on the starter handle because she was light and young and Brannoc's shoulder made a sound like a knuckle cracking every time he cranked.

"On my mark," Brannoc called from the engine compartment, hands on the compression release. "Fuel on. Choke half. And Sefa — when she catches, she may kick. Don't fight her. Ride her."

"She's a boat engine, not a horse."

"Everything with a temper is a horse. Mark!"

Sefa threw the crank. The Series Nine turned over once, twice, coughed, caught — and exploded into a bellowing, smoking, magnificent roar that shook the whole hull and sent a gull off the pilings into hysterical flight. The compartment filled with blue smoke and the sweet greasy smell of combustion, and the engine thundered, and thundered, and did not stop, and Brannoc stood there in the racket with his hands still hovering uselessly over the valves, laughing.

He hadn't laughed in — he tried to remember. It had been a while. Long enough that it came out sounding strange, rusty, like the engine itself.

Sefa stuck her head through the hatch, hair wild, face black with grease, grinning wider than Brannoc had yet seen her grin. "She runs!"

"She runs!" Brannoc roared back, over the noise, because there was no other volume available.

Tamsin appeared on the pier — drawn, presumably, by the sound, which was audible in three neighboring districts — and stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the smoke pour from the stack into the darkening sky. She didn't smile. But something in her shoulders came down half an inch, some tension Brannoc hadn't consciously registered until it eased, and she said, quietly enough that only he caught it:

"How long will she keep running?"

Brannoc patted the hot iron flank of the Series Nine, feeling it shudder through his palm like a living thing. "As long as nobody asks her that question," he said.

* * *

It was two nights later, unable to sleep in the heat, that Brannoc found the secret.

He'd gone forward through the darkened boat to check a seacock he'd been worrying about — the port one, aft of the chain locker, the kind of small nagging doubt that engineers carry the way sailors carry knives — and on his way back he passed the fo'c'sle, where Sefa slept, and saw that her seabag was open.

He hadn't meant to look. That was the truth of it, and he clung to the truth of it afterward. The bag was open, the lamp was low, and inside, beneath folded clothes, something had caught the light wrong. Not fabric. Metal.

He should have walked on. He was, by his own repeated insistence, a man who fixed things and asked no questions; questions were how crews came apart, and he'd watched a crew come apart once from the inside, and he had sworn off being any part of that machinery ever again.

But he looked.

Inside the bag, wrapped in an oilcloth with the care of something much heavier than it was, lay a brass fitting — a valve housing, naval pattern, stamped with a serial number and an anchor-and-crown mark that Brannoc knew intimately, because he had spent eleven years making parts to that exact specification in the Meridian yards before the yards closed and the world found other uses for men who could machine tolerances to a thousandth of an inch.

It was navy issue. Current navy issue, or close enough. And it did not belong in the seabag of a sixteen-year-old runaway who claimed to have learned engines in "places."

Brannoc stood in the dark passage for a long moment, listening to the water slap the hull, feeling the fragile new thing they were building — the engine running, the hull patched, the crew assembling piece by piece like his components laid out on the pier — and understanding, with the particular gloom of his profession, that somewhere in this assembly was a part he hadn't accounted for. A part whose function he didn't know. And unaccounted parts, in his experience, were never decorative.

He closed the seabag softly, left the fo'c'sle, and stood in the cockpit looking at the stars for a while.

In the morning, Tamsin came aboard with news. Brannoc met her at the rail, handed her coffee, and told her about the engine's fuel consumption figures, and the sistered strake, and the seacock, and everything else that lived safely in the ledger.

He did not tell her about the brass fitting in the seabag.

Not yet, he told himself. Not until he understood what it was for. Because a problem in a row was a task, and this one wasn't in a row yet, and Brannoc Thackeray's father had taught him one rule above all others: *never name a fault until you know whether it's a symptom or the disease.*

Besides — he thought, watching Sefa come down the pier with bread and a newspaper, quick-eyed as ever, nodding to him like nothing in the world was hidden anywhere — whatever she was carrying, she was carrying it for a reason. And reasons, on boats, had a way of surfacing on their own schedule.

He just hoped, looking at the clean new paint drying on the wheelhouse, at the smoke rising straight and steady from the stack, at the fragile hopeful rust-red hull riding at last on her own buoyancy — he just hoped the schedule wasn't set by anyone else.

Tamsin drank her coffee and said, "Ovid's found something in the archive. Says we need to talk tonight. All of us."

Brannoc looked at her. "About the Corvane?"

"About what's actually in her." Tamsin's eyes went out toward the horizon, where the sea lay flat and innocent under the morning sun. "And Brannoc — it's not gold."

He felt the words settle into him like a dropped tool rolling somewhere unreachable under the deck plates.

"Then what is it?" he asked.

"I don't know yet," Tamsin said. "But Voss died for it. My mother died for it. And I'm starting to think the navy will kill for it too."

Behind them, the Series Nine idled in the well, steady and strong and patched together from borrowed parts, and Brannoc thought: *so are we.*
