## 7. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The Colonial Archive of Port Calloway occupied a building that had clearly been designed by someone who hated both colonials and archives in equal measure. It was a squat granite block with the architectural charm of a strongbox and the ventilation of one too, and it sat wedged between a customs house that smelled permanently of tar and a chandlery that smelled permanently of the customs house.

Ovid Lark had spent eleven years of his life inside those walls, and he had left them the way most men leave bad marriages: quietly, with most of his dignity stuffed into a carpetbag, telling himself he'd be back for the rest of his things later.

He had not gone back for the rest of his things later.

Now here he was, three weeks later, standing on the front steps at seven in the morning like a man returning to the scene of a very slow crime, holding a forged reader's pass in his coat pocket and trying to remember whether his old colleague Mrs. Pellworthy still took her tea at half past or whether retirement had finally claimed her.

The truth — and Ovid was, professionally speaking, an excellent liar about everything except the truth when it mattered — was that he hadn't needed to come back at all. He could have written to a friend-of-a-friend. He could have bribed a junior clerk. He could have done any of the ten sensible things available to a man who wanted a shipping manifest without leaving a trail.

But some documents you had to look at yourself. Some documents you had to stand in front of, in the room where they'd been filed away and forgotten, and let them look back at you.

He climbed the steps.

* * *

Mrs. Pellworthy did still take her tea at half past. She took it behind the reception desk, which she still guarded with the territorial ferocity of a lighthouse keeper, and when she saw Ovid standing there with his hat in his hands, she looked at him for a long moment over the rim of her cup.

"You," she said.

"Good morning, Mrs. Pellworthy."

"You were carried out of this building on the shoulders of your colleagues after the Halloway affair, and not in celebration."

"The Halloway affair was a misunderstanding."

"The Halloway affair was you publishing a minister's private correspondence in a pamphlet with a woodcut of the minister as a donkey." She set down her cup. "It was a very good woodcut."

"It was an excellent woodcut. I commissioned it myself."

Something at the corner of her mouth twitched — the ghost of a smile, quickly suppressed, the way old soldiers suppress the memory of good battles. "Your pass is expired."

"I know."

"Your credentials are revoked."

"I know that too."

"Then what," she said, "are you doing on my steps?"

Ovid leaned on the desk, which was against regulations, and lowered his voice, which was also against regulations, and said the thing he'd rehearsed on the walk over and which came out only half as smooth as intended. "I'm doing research for a maritime history. A survey of colonial-era wrecks. Very dry. Very respectable. Nobody will read it, which is rather the point."

Mrs. Pellworthy looked at him the way she'd looked at junior clerks who misfiled things under the wrong decade — a look that contained thirty years of accumulated disappointment and, buried somewhere beneath it, a genuine fondness she would have denied under oath.

"A maritime history," she repeated.

"With charts."

"With charts."

"And possibly a chapter on salvage law."

She sighed, a long, weathered sound, like a ship taking water slowly and with dignity. Then she slid a visitor's slip across the desk and stamped it before either of them could think better of it. "Reading Room Two. The manifest registers are on the east wall. And Ovid—" she fixed him with a look, "—if anyone asks, you are researching salvage law, and I am a woman who has never seen you before in my life."

"Understood."

"And if this ends with another donkey—"

"There will be no donkey."

"There had better not be," she said, and returned to her tea, and that was that.

* * *

Reading Room Two smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular melancholy of paper nobody had touched in decades. Ovid knew the smell intimately. He had helped create it. There was something almost homey about it now, which he suspected said unflattering things about his character.

The Corvane's file should have been simple. Every merchant vessel lost in colonial waters had a loss report, a cargo declaration, and an insurance assessment, filed together in a green folder with a brass number. He'd catalogued hundreds of them himself. The Corvane — lost off the Meridian reefs, autumn of the year the governor's administration collapsed — should have been sitting in sequence between the *Cormorant's Pride* and the *Corvane's sister ships*, neat as teeth.

It wasn't.

He checked the register index first, because procedure was habit and habit was comfort. The index card existed. He'd written part of it himself, years ago, in his own careful hand: *CORVANE, merchant brig, 214 tons, lost w/ all papers, ref. 4471-C.* But when he pulled the drawer for reference 4471, the slot was empty except for a withdrawal slip dated nine years back, signed with initials he didn't recognize and a department stamp he did: **Office of the Admiralty Survey — Internal.**

Internal. In archive language, *internal* meant *you may look at the card, but the card is all you get.*

Ovid stood there holding the empty folder space the way a man holds an empty birdcage, and felt the old itch start up behind his eyes. Thirty years an archivist teaches you one thing above all others: nothing gets withdrawn from a colonial archive unless someone important is afraid of it. Files went missing because of moths, floods, and clerical error. Files got *withdrawn* because of ministers.

"Well," he murmured to the empty slot, "hello, then."

Because here was the thing about Ovid Lark, the thing his colleagues had never quite understood and his enemies had understood too late: he had spent his career learning where bodies were buried, and he had learned it from the paperwork. You didn't need the file itself. You needed the file's *neighbors*. Documents cast shadows. A withdrawal left ripples — duplicate copies requested, cross-references updated, insurance claims processed from secondary records. The navy could pull the Corvane's folder, but they couldn't pull every fingerprint the Corvane had ever left on the bureaucracy.

He settled into a chair, rolled up his sleeves, and began the slow, patient work of following fingerprints.

* * *

By noon he had three threads, none of them promising on their own.

The first was the insurance record. The Corvane had been underwritten jointly by two firms, and the smaller firm — Halloran & Vetch — had paid out its share within four months of the sinking, which was fast, and had done so based on a cargo valuation of "general trade goods, mixed," which was vague, and had attached an inventory addendum that was, blessedly, filed separately in the insurance registry because insurance men trusted no one, least of all each other.

The second thread was a customs entry. The Corvane had loaded at Meridian Harbor, and Meridian Harbor kept its own loading manifests — duplicates, sent to Port Calloway as a matter of routine and filed, Ovid knew, in the harbor returns, which were so boring that even the navy had never bothered withdrawing them. Boredom, Ovid had long maintained, was the best security system in the empire.

The third thread was stranger. In the governor's office correspondence index — the public one, the sanitized one — there was a single line item from the final autumn of the administration: *Re: disposition of effects, personal, priority, ref. G-114.* No description. No follow-up. Just that one entry, sitting in the middle of six hundred lines of road appropriations and grain tariffs like a knife laid on a dinner table.

He ordered the harbor returns first. The clerk who fetched them — a boy of perhaps nineteen with ink on his chin and the posture of a question mark — looked at the request slip, looked at Ovid, and visibly decided that a man researching maritime history was beneath both suspicion and interest. Ovid tipped him a coin anyway. Goodwill was cheap and compounded well.

The harbor returns came up in two bound volumes, and Ovid carried them to his table and opened the relevant month, and there she was.

*CORVANE, brig, 214 tons, master J. Aldous. Cargo per manifest annexed.*

Annexed. His heart gave a small, undignified leap. He turned pages with fingers that wanted to tear, found the annex, and read.

And read it again.

The official loss report — the one in the green folder, the one he'd catalogued himself, the one every historian who'd ever glanced at the Corvane had relied upon — listed her cargo as dyewood, tin ingots, and general merchandise. Standard. Forgettable. Worth maybe eight thousand crowns insured.

The harbor loading manifest listed something else entirely.

*Crate lots 1 through 40: sealed strongboxes, contents declared under bond, consignee: Office of the Governor, Meridian. Crate lots 41 through 88: plate, specie, jewelry cases, sealed under bond. Crate lots 89 through 96: document chests, wax-sealed, contents undeclared, consignee as above.*

Ninety-six crate lots. Ovid did the arithmetic in his head, then did it again on the back of a request slip because his hands weren't steady enough to trust. At the valuations current in that year — and he knew the valuations; knowing valuations was half of what an archivist was *for* — ninety-six crate lots of specie and plate and sealed strongboxes came to something north of four hundred thousand crowns.

Four hundred thousand crowns, sailing out of Meridian Harbor on a merchant brig, in the same season the governor's administration collapsed, in the same season the Meridian families — the old families, the ones whose land grants predated the colony charter, the ones the government had spent a decade squeezing, taxing, litigating, and finally simply ruining — were filing their last desperate petitions.

He sat back. The reading room hummed around him: the lamp, the turning page of the one other scholar present, the sea-gulls outside arguing about something.

"Oh, you foolish, beautiful thieves," he whispered.

Because it made sense now. All of it. The speed of the insurance payout. The vagueness of the cargo declaration. The withdrawal of the file. A fortune that size didn't sink — it *vanished*, and vanishing required paperwork, and paperwork was the one thing the navy could always be relied upon to provide.

But it was the third thread that turned suspicion into certainty, and it was the third thread that cost him the rest of the day and most of his composure.

* * *

Reference G-114 was not in the public correspondence files. Of course it wasn't. But correspondence indexes referenced *registry actions*, and registry actions had their own logbook, and the registry logbook for that year was filed — he knew exactly where, because he had shelved it himself, in a former life, in a former self — in the overflow stacks behind Reading Room Four, where the light was bad and the spiders held seniority.

He waited until the other scholar left. He waited until the duty clerk made his rounds and settled back down with a newspaper. Then he walked, unhurried, a man with nothing but legitimate scholarly purposes, into the overflow stacks, and pulled the registry logbook, and carried it to the worst-lit corner of the worst-lit room in the building, and opened it to the autumn of the collapse.

G-114. Entry logged at the end of the season, in the registrar's tidy hand:

*Ref. G-114: One (1) sealed packet, personal papers of Gov. R. Merriweather, deposited for secure keeping aboard vessel CORVANE, departing on tide. Deposited by order of the Governor's private secretary. NOT TO BE ENTERED IN CARGO SCHEDULES. Instruction of record: packet to be destroyed unread upon safe arrival, receipt to follow.*

*No receipt on file.*

Ovid closed the logbook very gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

A sealed packet of the governor's personal papers, smuggled out on the same ship as the treasury, with instructions that it be destroyed unread. And no receipt — meaning the Corvane had never arrived wherever she was going, or whoever received her had preferred silence to paper.

Either way, the confession — because that was what it had to be; a man does not seal his personal papers and order them burned unless the papers are the kind that hang people — was at the bottom of the sea. Sitting in a document chest among ninety-six crate lots of stolen wealth, in a wreck that a prospector named Ede Voss had found and mapped, and that Tamsin Montrose was already sailing toward with a promise in her pocket and a monsoon at her heels.

Ovid stood in the dark of the overflow stacks for a long time, feeling thirty years settle onto his shoulders like wet canvas.

He had catalogued the Corvane's file. He had written the index card. *Lost w/ all papers,* he had written, in his own careful hand, and he had believed it, because believing it was easier than asking why a merchant brig's entire file would carry the faint institutional smell of a thing deliberately buried. He had been the archive's faithful servant. He had kept the shadows tidy.

And the whole time, the truth had been sitting in the harbor returns, gathering dust, waiting for anyone bored enough to look.

"No receipt on file," he said aloud, softly, to the spiders. "You absolute cowards. All of you. Including me."

* * *

He copied everything. He was an archivist; copying was sacrament. The harbor manifest went into his notebook in full, crate lot by crate lot. The registry entry went in verbatim, with the reference numbers and the dates. The insurance addendum, the index cards, the timeline of the Meridian petitions against the final tax levies — all of it, transcribed in a hand that shook slightly and did not stop.

By the time he finished, the lamps were being lit and Mrs. Pellworthy was making her closing rounds, and Ovid walked out through the front hall feeling like a man carrying live coals in a paper bag.

She looked up from her desk. "Find your salvage law?"

"Every word of it," Ovid said. "Fascinating stuff. Dry as bone."

"Mm." She studied him over her spectacles with those thirty years of disappointment and fondness, and then, quietly, without looking back down at her ledger: "There was a young woman in here yesterday. Montrose. Asking after wreck registries off the Meridian reefs. I told her nothing, because there was nothing to tell — the section's been flagged internal since before my knees went bad." She turned a page. "She had a chart with her. Hand-drawn. Old paper. I've seen old paper in this building for forty years, Mr. Lark, and I know the difference between a forgery and a copy of something real."

Ovid stopped halfway to the door.

"She asked me," Mrs. Pellworthy continued, "who else had come looking for the Corvane. I told her the truth: nobody, in forty years. But that's not quite true either, is it. Because somebody withdrew that file nine years ago, and somebody flagged the reef sections internal, and neither of those somebodies has ever stopped paying attention — the flag renewals come through every year, regular as rent." She finally looked up. "Whoever drew that girl's chart knew where the body is buried. And whoever renewed that flag every year for a decade knows it too. And one of them is going to reach it first."

The hall was quiet. Somewhere outside, the gulls had moved on to a new argument.

"Mrs. Pellworthy," Ovid said, "you are a far more dangerous woman than this building deserves."

"I'm a librarian," she said. "We're all dangerous. We just wait." She stamped his visitor's slip for exit and added, almost as an afterthought: "The flag renewal authorizations come from the Admiralty Survey. Signed personally. The signature hasn't changed in nine years. If you're going to write your maritime history, Mr. Lark, you might want to know whose name is on it."

She slid a small slip of paper across the desk — a scrap torn from a routing memo, bearing a single line of her own handwriting.

Ovid read the name, and the floor of his stomach dropped away like a hatch opening over deep water.

Captain Ilsa Reyes. The renewals were signed, every year, in her own hand.

He thanked Mrs. Pellworthy, who was already pretending their conversation had never happened, and walked out into the evening with his notebook against his ribs and the name burning through the paper bag like the coals it was.

Tamsin needed to know. Not the treasure — the treasure was almost beside the point now, a fortune riding shotgun on something worth infinitely more. The Corvane wasn't a prize. She was a witness. Ninety-six crate lots of evidence, and a sealed confession, and a paper trail leading straight from a drowned hull to a captain's signing hand.

And Reyes's fleet was already at sea.

Ovid hurried along the waterfront toward the harbor, an old man moving faster than dignity allowed, rehearsing how he would say it — *the manifest, the packet, the signatures, the nine years* — and how Tamsin would take it, and whether the news would make her crew stronger or simply give them all one more reason to be afraid.

Behind him, the archive windows went dark, one by one, keeping its secrets the way it always had.

But not this one. Not anymore.
