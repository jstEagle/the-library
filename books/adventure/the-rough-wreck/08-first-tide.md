## 8. First Tide

The Kestrel's Wake smelled of diesel and old tea and, since yesterday, faintly of Brannoc's soldering flux, which he swore was a sign of good work and everyone else swore was a sign the boat might catch fire.

Tamsin stood at the rail in the grey half-light before dawn and watched the water over the Corvane's grave go from black to pewter to something almost kind. The reef here didn't look like much from above — a long dark smear beneath forty fathoms, a shadow with a shape to it if you knew how to squint. She knew how to squint. She'd been squinting at wrecks since she was nine years old, hanging off her mother's hip on a dive platform somewhere warm and badly regulated.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said beside her.

"What thing?"

"The face. The one where you're pretending you're not thinking about anything." Sefa leaned on the rail, chin on her forearms, watching the swell. "Brannoc does it too. Ovid does it constantly but his is more of a grimace."

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is just quieter." Sefa said it without malice, the way she said most things — like she was reading a current chart aloud. "You're thinking about your mum."

Tamsin looked at her. Sixteen years old, runaway, could read water the way other people read weather, and apparently had decided that boundaries were for people with better things to do than notice them.

"I'm thinking about the dive," Tamsin said.

"Right," said Sefa. "The dive."

* * *

They anchored at slack water because Brannoc insisted, and because Brannoc had been right about everything mechanical so far, which was an uncomfortable habit they were all trying not to encourage too openly lest it go to his head and he become insufferable. He'd already become somewhat insufferable about the winch.

"Forty-one fathoms," he announced, tapping the lead line like it owed him money. "Bottom time at that depth, you've got maybe twenty minutes of useful work before you're flirting with trouble. I want you down, along the hull, marks on the chart, and up. No heroics. No poking into holes. No — what did you call it last time — 'just having a quick look.'"

"It was a quick look."

"It was eleven minutes inside a collapsed cargo deck."

"And I found the entry point we needed."

"You found it by luck, which is not a method, it's a coincidence wearing a method's coat." Brannoc pushed his goggles up onto his forehead, where they lived permanently now, leaving a red band across his skin like a second eyebrow. "I'm saying this as your engineer and also as the person who has to pull your body out of the water if this goes wrong, and I'd like to tell you honestly that I'm not strong enough to do that gracefully. I'd drop you back in. Twice, probably."

"That's very moving, Brannoc."

"I practice."

Ovid appeared from below decks holding two mugs of tea and the expression of a man attending a funeral he'd only read about in advance. He handed one mug to Tamsin. It was terrible tea. They'd run out of the good stuff three days ago and nobody had confessed to drinking it, but the evidence pointed collectively at all of them.

"For luck," Ovid said. "Or for warmth. Or possibly for flavor, though I make no promises there."

"Is there a reading?" Tamsin asked. Ovid had spent the last two nights with the manifest pages spread across the galley table, annotating them in a tiny, furious hand, muttering about tonnage figures like a man arguing with a ghost.

"There is always a reading." He adjusted his spectacles, which fogged immediately in the morning damp, and gave up on them. "The Corvane went down stern-first, according to the inquiry records. Which means her bow should be relatively intact, driven into the sand, and her stern should be — well. Less intact. If Voss's chart is honest, the strongroom sits forward of the main hold, low in the hull, port side. You won't reach it today. Today you're just —"

"Mapping the outside. I know."

"Yes. But knowing it and knowing it are different things." Ovid looked at her then, really looked, the way he occasionally did when he forgot to be nervous. "Your mother dove this wreck, Tamsin. Whatever's down there, some part of it already knows your name."

That was the trouble with Ovid. He meant well, and he was right, and both of those things were unbearable before breakfast.

* * *

Sefa read the water while Tamsin suited up.

The girl crouched at the rail with her bare feet hooked under the toe-rail and her whole body angled toward the sea like a compass needle, and she talked in that flat, certain voice of hers: "Surface chop's nothing. There's a cold layer at maybe twenty fathoms — you'll feel it like walking into a cellar. Under that, the current runs north-northeast along the reef face, gentle today, maybe half a knot. Slack tide gives you another hour, no more. When it turns, it'll turn all at once, like someone flipped a table."

"How do you know all that?" Brannoc asked, for perhaps the fourth time since they'd sailed.

"I don't know it. I see it." Sefa shrugged. "The water tells you things if you shut up long enough to listen. Most people never shut up."

"She means you specifically," Tamsin said.

"I assumed."

She went down the anchor line alone, because the first dive on a new wreck was a thing she'd always done alone, and because some part of her — the part she didn't examine, the part that kept her moving from contract to contract, port to port, never staying long enough for anyone to learn her face — wanted to meet the Corvane without witnesses.

The descent was ordinary, which was its own small mercy. Green light thickening around her, the anchor line humming faintly with the boat's motion far above, her breath loud in her ears and her bubbles rising in silver strings. At fifteen fathoms the warm surface layer let go of her and the cold took her, exactly as Sefa had promised — sudden, total, like passing through a curtain into a different sea. Twenty-five fathoms. Thirty. Her torch beam began to matter more than the daylight, cutting a cone through water that had forgotten the sun entirely.

At thirty-eight fathoms, the reef rose to meet her.

And beyond the reef, out of the gloom, came the hull.

She had seen wrecks before. Hundreds of them. She had a professional's catalog in her head: the way wood rotted and iron wept rust in orange tears, the way a ship underwater stopped being a ship and became a landscape, a geography of collapse. She had thought herself past the point where a wreck could do anything to her but present a work order.

Then her light found the Corvane's bow.

It stood up out of the sand like a cathedral wall. Three hundred years of silt had buried her to the second deck, but what remained rose sheer and black-fouled, crusted in coral and barnacle and the slow white lacework of the reef, and the coral had grown in patterns that followed the original ironwork, so that the wreck wore her own skeleton on the outside — ribs rendered in stone, gunports ringed in living color, anemones waving from the scuppers like small drowned flags. A school of silver fish poured through a gash in the plating and reformed on the other side, unbothered, as if ships sank every day just to give them somewhere interesting to live.

Tamsin hung in the water ten feet off the bow and forgot, for a full minute, to check her gauges.

Her mother had come here. That was the thought that arrived, unwelcome and enormous, filling her mask. Not near here. Here. This exact hull, this exact wall of dead iron and living coral. Mara Montrose had hung in this same green dark with a torch in her fist and looked up at this same impossible face, and had found whatever it was she found, and had gone into the deep compartments after it, and had not come back out.

Tamsin had spent her whole adult life telling herself a tidy version of that story: her mother had been reckless, obsessed, chasing treasure the way some people chase storms, and the sea had simply done what the sea does. It was a story that hurt, but it was a clean hurt, a hurt with edges. It fit in a pocket. You could carry it.

But swimming the length of the Corvane's hull, running her glove along plates furred with growth, feeling the vast patient weight of the thing settle against her palm — the story got heavier. Because the wreck wasn't reckless. The wreck wasn't obsessed. The wreck was just a ship that had been carrying something worth killing for, and her mother had understood that, and had chosen it anyway, and Tamsin was beginning to suspect — with the cold water pressing in and her own heartbeat loud as a drum — that her mother had chosen it for reasons that would not fit in any pocket Tamsin owned.

She reached the forward section where Voss's chart marked the first landmark: a hawse hole, a great round mouth in the bow where the anchor chain once ran, now sealed with coral except for a gap at the top wide enough to admit a diver. Above it, carved into the encrustation by some earlier hand — recent, within years, the edges still sharp — were four letters scratched through the coral crust:

M.V.

Not a marker for salvage crews. Salvage crews used paint and numbers. This was initials. This was a signature. This was someone standing on the Corvane's bow in the dark and the cold, thirty-eight fathoms down, taking the time to scratch their name into the reef like a child carving into a school desk — *I was here. Remember me.*

Tamsin pressed her glove flat against the letters.

The cold came through the glove and into her bones, and she stayed there longer than the plan allowed, longer than Brannoc's margins, longer than sense, breathing hard enough that her regulator rasped, staring at those two letters until they blurred behind her mask plate. She thought about her mother's hands making them. She thought about whether she'd been afraid. She thought — and this was the thought that finally made her move, made her kick upward off the hull like something startled — that she hoped her mother hadn't been afraid, and knew even as she hoped it that Mara Montrose had never once in her life been afraid of the sea, only of the people on it.

She checked her gauge. Fourteen minutes. Fine. Plenty. She was fine.

Her hands were shaking so badly she had to grip the anchor line with both of them.

* * *

She came up the line into grey afternoon light and Sefa's hands on her harness, hauling her over the gunwale with a competence that would have alarmed Tamsin if she'd had the energy to be alarmed.

"Told you," Sefa said, pulling off her mask. "Cellar at twenty fathoms."

"You told me."

"You're shaking."

"I'm fine."

"You're shaking and you've got that face again." Sefa wrapped a blanket around her shoulders with brisk, unsentimental efficiency, the way you'd wrap a parcel. "The quiet one."

Tamsin sat on the deck grating with the blanket around her and the terrible tea Ovid pressed into her hands, and told them what she'd found. The bow, intact. The hawse hole, passable. The route forward along the port side, clear except for one fallen spar they'd need the winch for. And the letters.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Brannoc stood with a wrench in one hand, forgetting he was holding it. Ovid removed his spectacles, polished them on his shirt, put them back on, and removed them again, as though the problem might be optical.

"M.V.," he said quietly. "Mara Veyra Montrose." He'd done the research; of course he had; he probably knew her mother's middle name and her grandmother's maiden name and the name of the cat she'd owned at seven. "Thirty-eight fathoms is deep for free-carving. She must have brought tools. She must have planned to leave a mark." He paused. "People leave marks when they believe someone will come looking."

"Well," said Brannoc roughly, "someone came looking. Took her bloody time, but." He turned away and started coiling rope that didn't need coiling, which was how Brannoc handled feelings — by giving them a job to do.

"We go back down tomorrow," Tamsin said. "Slack tide. We take the winch line and the spare lights, and we start working the hawse hole. Voss's chart shows the compartment access is reachable from the bow section if the inner bulkhead's breached. If it's not breached, we breach it."

"On whose authority?" Brannoc asked, and then answered himself, because he always did: "Yours. Right. Yes. Fine." He was getting used to it. That was either progress or a problem; Tamsin hadn't decided.

Ovid sat down across from her, careful as a man sitting near a sleeping animal. "You touched the hull," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Professional assessment requires contact."

"Mm." He looked at her over the spectacles he wasn't wearing. "When my wife died, I went back to our house once, after. Stood in the doorway. Couldn't go in." He turned his mug slowly in his hands. "Touching things is harder than looking at them. Everyone pretends otherwise."

Tamsin drank her terrible tea and said nothing, and Ovid, who was learning her the way she was learning the reef — patiently, in layers — let the silence stand.

* * *

It was Sefa who saw it first, because of course it was.

She was at the rail drying her hair with a rag, squinting north along the reef line, and Tamsin watched the rag stop moving. Watched the girl go absolutely still in the way she went still only when the water was telling her something bad.

"Smoke," Sefa said.

Tamsin was beside her in three steps. North, past the far elbow of the reef, where the sea went hazy with distance — a smudge against the horizon. Not cloud. Cloud moved. This held its shape, a thin grey thread climbing out of a hull too far away to see, and beneath it, when the swell lifted them all at the wrong moment, a pale sliver of superstructure.

One ship. Sitting on the horizon like a held breath.

"Not a trawler," Brannoc said, appearing with a battered telescope he'd produced from somewhere and refused to explain. He braced it on the rail. Long pause. "Too big. Too clean. Navy lines, or dressed to look like them." Another pause, longer. "There's a pennant. Can't make out the number at this distance."

The wind shifted, carrying the smell of salt and coming rain, and Tamsin felt the day change temperature.

Reyes. It had to be. The fleet was mobilizing — Voss had warned her, the harbormaster's gossip had confirmed it — and Reyes's scouts would be thrown out ahead of the main force like fingers feeling for a pulse. Feeling, perhaps, for a transmitter's worth of signal, or a rumor's worth of movement, or simply sweeping the reef line because Captain Ilsa Reyes did not believe in coincidence and did not tolerate being surprised.

"Can she see us?" Ovid asked.

"At this range? Maybe. Maybe not." Brannoc lowered the telescope slowly. "But she'll see the anchorage marks eventually. Wrecks this rich draw divers like—"

"Like us," Sefa said.

"—like us," Brannoc agreed. "Question is whether we run now, while we've got light, or sit tight and look boring."

Tamsin kept her eyes on the smoke thread. Somewhere behind that smudge was the woman who had taught her to tie her first bowline, who had lifted her onto her shoulders to see over a crowded dock, who had testified — calm, precise, devastating — while her mother's name was dragged through an inquiry and her mother's reputation was fed to the tide. Somewhere behind that smudge was the person Tamsin had spent fifteen years sailing away from, arriving now, at last, at the one place Tamsin couldn't sail away from.

"We stay," she heard herself say. "We look boring. We're a survey boat doing a legitimate survey, and we've got the paperwork to prove it, or Ovid can forge it by morning."

"I resent that," Ovid said. "Accurately forged documents are not forgery. They're historical reconstruction."

The smoke thread climbed the sky, patient as a promise.

Below them, thirty-eight fathoms down, her mother's initials waited in the coral. Ahead of them, on the horizon, the past was coming up fast under full steam.

"Someone get me that tea," Tamsin said. "It's going to be a long night."
