## 9. Currents and Lies

Sefa had learned to read water the way other children learned to read prayers — by repetition, by fear, and by the certain knowledge that getting it wrong would be noticed by something vast and unforgiving.

She'd learned it on the Meridian docks, before the name Meridian meant anything to her except a place where the fish came in silver and the harbor master's son threw stones. She'd been six. Her grandmother had put Sefa's small hand flat against a piling and said, *Feel that? The water's arguing with itself. When it argues like that, you stay ashore.* Sefa had felt it — a low, shuddering pulse, like a heartbeat under the boards — and she had never once, in nine years, ignored it.

Which was why, on the morning of the second dive on the Corvane, she was standing at the rail of the *Kestrel's Wake* with her whole body humming like a struck bell, and nobody was listening to her.

"It's wrong," she said, for the fourth time. She said it to Tamsin's back, because Tamsin was checking her rig at the gunwale with the focused calm of a woman who had already decided. "The longshore current. It's supposed to run east to west along the reef shelf. It's running the other way, and it's fast, and it's *layered* — the top's going one way and the bottom's going another. That's not weather. That's something underneath pushing it."

"Something underneath," Ovid repeated, from his nest of charts by the wheel, in the tone of a man cataloguing a new species of superstition.

"The reef breathes," Sefa said. "Big tidal exchange through the channels. Sometimes it inhales wrong." She didn't have better words. Her grandmother hadn't had better words. The water argued, or it didn't, and you listened or you drowned.

Tamsin Montrose looked up. She had a way of looking at people that made you feel like a manifest entry — weighed, noted, filed under *probably useful*. "You're saying call the dive."

"I'm saying *wait*. Two hours. Maybe three. Let the tide turn all the way."

"We have six tides, Sefa. Six. And I just spent the first one looking at a hull." Tamsin's jaw did the thing it did when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Two hours might be fine. It might also be the difference between raising that strongroom and watching Reyes's dredges chew it into gravel. I'm going. Brannoc's on the winch, you're on my line."

"I'm on your line," Sefa said, "and I'm telling you the line's going to be lying to you."

"Then you'll tell me how it's lying." Tamsin settled her mask. "That's the job."

Sefa wanted to say something else — wanted to say *my grandmother read water for forty years and the sea took her anyway* — but Tamsin was already swinging her legs over the rail, and there was a particular quality to the way Tamsin Montrose went over the side of a boat, like a letter being posted, that made argument feel like shouting at a closed door.

She went down into water the color of old bottle glass, and Sefa paid out the line and hated every meter of it.

* * *

The first twenty minutes were fine. Sefa knew this because Brannoc, who had the emotional range of a boiler but a surprising gift for narration, kept up a running commentary from the winch housing.

"Steady pull. She's moving along the hull — that'll be the port side, if her compass is to be believed, and it's not, so let's say generally port-ish." Brannoc had a voice like gravel in a bucket and used it, Sefa suspected, to keep from being asked questions. "Line's slack. She's stopped. Looking at something. Probably a fish. Probably naming the fish."

"Probably a hull plate," Ovid said, without looking up from the manifest copy he'd annotated until it looked like a document that had survived a war.

"Probably a fish with a serial number," Brannoc said.

Sefa didn't laugh. She was watching the line, and the line had begun to do something she didn't like.

It had begun to lean.

Not much. Two degrees, maybe three, off true vertical, drifting and then correcting, drifting and then correcting, like a man walking beside you who kept bumping your shoulder. The surface of the sea around the boat was doing nothing at all — flat, glassy, innocent, the way liars look you straight in the eye.

"Brannoc," she said.

"Mm."

"The line."

He looked. Sefa watched him look, watched the gravel-voice man go quiet, watched his big hands stop moving on the winch. He'd been an engineer long enough to know what a leaning line meant even if he'd never read water in his life.

"That's current," he said. "That's a lot of current, down there, where there's supposed to be none."

"It's going to get worse." Sefa's mouth had gone dry. "It's going to get worse fast. It does that. It builds and builds and then it — " She made a gesture she didn't have a name for, a kind of scooping, taking motion.

On the line, thirty meters down, Tamsin was swimming through a place where the ocean had quietly changed its mind.

* * *

Later — much later, after everything — Sefa would try to explain to Ovid what it was like, being the only one on a boat who could feel a disaster coming through the soles of her feet, and Ovid would say, *that sounds like being an archivist, actually,* and she'd hit him with a rolled-up chart, and they'd both laugh in the way people laugh when the laughing is load-bearing.

But in the moment there was no explaining. There was only the line leaning, and then the line *moving* — a slow, horrible sideways drift, like a pendulum swinging — and Brannoc saying, very calmly, in the voice he used for structural failures, "Sefa. How long do we have."

"Minutes." She was already hauling line, hand over hand, and it was like hauling on a kite in a gale. "Maybe. She'll feel it. She's good, she'll feel it, but feeling it and fighting it are — "

The line went taut.

Not leaning. Taut. Straight as a harp string, humming, and Sefa felt the vibration come up the rope and into her arms and she knew — the way you know a bone is broken — that somewhere down in the dark, the water had picked up Tamsin Montrose and simply *taken her*, the way the tide takes a hat.

"She's off the wreck," Brannoc said. "The line's — she's moving. Fast. Northeast."

"Northeast is the channel," Sefa said, and her voice came out flat and strange, and she heard her grandmother in it, and hated that too. "Northeast is where the water goes when the reef inhales."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that by the time we pull her up she'll be a mile away and out of air." Sefa's hands were moving without her. "Get the tender. Get the tender in the water *now*."

Brannoc moved. For a big man with a bad knee and a worse reputation he moved like a landslide, which was to say: irresistibly. Ovid was on his feet for the first time in Sefa's memory, charts scattering, and there was a moment — one bright, stupid moment — when the three of them stood on the deck of a rotting survey boat and looked at each other like strangers at a bus stop, each waiting for someone more qualified to arrive.

Nobody more qualified was coming.

"Ovid," Sefa said. "Engine. Keep it running, keep it pointed where I say."

"I don't — I've never — "

"You will today."

And then Sefa went over the rail.

* * *

Here is what Sefa knew, and what she had never told anyone on that boat, and what she would carry alone for a long time: she had been in a riptide before. She had been in one at eleven years old, off the Meridian breakwater, in water much warmer and much less deep, and she had survived it by doing the one thing every instinct screams against.

She hadn't fought it.

The water had taken her out through the channel like a hand closing around her ankle, and her grandmother's voice had been in her head — *don't fight the sea, girl, the sea always wins the argument; swim across it, swim across it, wait for the quiet water behind the reef* — and Sefa had swum *across*, sideways, diagonal, spending strength she didn't have on a current that didn't care, until the grip loosened and the water behind the reef went glassy and still, and she'd floated there, eleven years old, a quarter mile from shore, looking back at a place where she had very nearly stopped existing.

She was fourteen now, and the water was cold, and the current was stronger, and Tamsin Montrose was somewhere in it with a finite amount of air.

Sefa dove.

The world below the surface was green murk and noise — the riptide made a sound, a low rushing roar like a river that had gotten ideas above its station — and Sefa swam *across* it, angled hard to the west, away from the channel, into the layered water she'd warned them about, where the surface pushed east and the deep pushed west and the whole sea was a lie told in two directions at once.

She found Tamsin by the bubbles.

A rope of silver, streaming northeast, going fast. Sefa swam to it, and at the end of it was Tamsin — mask on, regulator in, moving with the terrible controlled calm of a diver who has already done the arithmetic and knows she's losing. Her dive line was wrapped once around her forearm, streaming out behind her toward a boat that might as well have been on the moon.

Sefa grabbed her harness.

Tamsin spun, and even underwater, even in the green roar, Sefa saw the exact moment the older woman recognized her — saw the surprise, and then the calculation, and then something that wasn't surprise or calculation at all. Trust, maybe. Or the decision to trust, which in the water is the same thing.

Sefa pointed: *west*. Across the current. Tamsin stared at her for one second — one second in which Sefa could feel the air ticking away in both their tanks — and then she nodded, and turned, and swam with Sefa instead of against the sea.

They swam across the argument. It took everything. Sefa's shoulders burned, her lungs burned, the cold pushed through her borrowed wetsuit like a rumor of the deep ocean's opinion of her plans, and the whole time the current pulled northeast and they pulled west, two small stubborn vectors against a very large one, and slowly — so slowly Sefa didn't believe it was happening until it had happened — the grip loosened.

The water behind the reef, where the channel's outflow curled back on itself and went quiet, was green and still and full of drifting silt, like the inside of a held breath.

They broke the surface together, thirty meters from the *Kestrel's Wake*, and Sefa heard Brannoc's voice crack across the water — actually *crack*, the gravel splitting — and the tender was already coming, Ovid at the tiller with the expression of a man driving a cart he'd only read about, and Tamsin spat out her regulator and hung on the tender's gunwale and breathed, great whooping breaths, and then looked at Sefa over the side.

"You felt it," Tamsin said. "Before I went down. You felt it, and you told me, and I went anyway."

"Yes," Sefa said.

"And then you came in after me. Without a line. Without anyone knowing."

"Yes."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Water ran off her face. Somewhere behind them the reef muttered to itself, arguing, settling.

"Next time," Tamsin said, "when you tell me the water's lying — I'm going to believe you."

Sefa nodded, and hauled herself over the tender's side, and discovered that her hands were shaking so badly she had to sit on them.

* * *

They gave her the good bunk that night. It was the only bunk, technically — the *Kestrel's Wake* had been built by people who assumed survey crews slept standing up — but it was the one that didn't list, and Brannoc had wedged a shim under the frame to stop the list it did anyway, and when Sefa came down off the deck, dried and wrapped in a blanket that smelled of diesel and someone's ancient pipe smoke, the others were sitting around the little chart table like a tribunal that had brought snacks.

"We voted," Brannoc announced.

"There wasn't a vote," Ovid said. "I proposed it and nobody objected, which in maritime law is — "

"It was a vote."

"You're crew," Tamsin said, simply, and then, because she was Tamsin and sincerity seemed to physically pain her, added, "and you're on line duty every dive from now on, and if you ever go into the water after me again without a line, I'll — " She stopped. She didn't seem to have an ending for the sentence. "Just don't do it again."

"Understood," said Sefa, who had every intention of doing exactly that again if it came to it.

Brannoc pushed a tin mug across the table at her. It was tea, probably. It was hot, certainly, and it tasted like it had been brewed with the same enthusiasm Brannoc brought to everything, which was to say too much. "For the record," he said, "I have worked with divers for eleven years. Navy divers, salvage divers, one man who claimed to be a pearl diver and was definitely a smuggler. Not one of them would have done what you did today. Most of them couldn't have. You read that current from the surface like it was written on a chart."

"It's just water," Sefa said, into the mug.

"It is *not* just water," Brannoc said with sudden passion. "Water is the enemy. Water is trying to kill us at all times, in every way, and today you out-argued it. I intend to be extremely nice to you from now on. I've made a list."

Ovid, who had been quiet, looked up from his manifest. He had the kind of face that aged backward when he was interested — the stoop and the grey going away, leaving a younger, sharper man underneath, one who had perhaps once been someone. "Where did you learn that?" he asked. "The reading. The — you said the reef *breathes*. That's not salvage talk. That's not navy talk. That's older."

Sefa looked into her tea.

"Family," she said. "My grandmother. She was a pilot, back on — back home. She read currents for the fishing fleets." All true. All carefully, carefully true, and none of it the truth, and the distance between those two things was exactly the width of the Meridian islands and everything that had happened there.

"Your grandmother," Ovid said, "must have been quite a woman."

"She was," Sefa said, and meant it with her whole chest, and felt the old grief move through her like the current had, patient and enormous, and did not fight it.

* * *

It was near midnight when she came up on deck for air — real air, deck air, the kind you didn't have to share with a regulator — and found the sea flat and silver under a half moon, the reef a black seam to the east, everything calm and innocent again.

That was when she saw the light.

It was far off, north-northeast, past the edge of the reef shelf where the charts went vague and the deep water began. A single white light, steady, riding high — too high for a fishing boat, too steady for a drifter. It sat there on the horizon like a lit window in a house where someone was awake.

Sefa knew the silhouette. She'd know it anywhere. She'd spent three weeks in a port on the other side of the archipelago keeping her back to walls because of that silhouette — the *Vigilant*, navy scout cutter, low and fast and clean, the kind of ship that found people. She'd seen it once from a fish market roof, once from a ferry, once from the water itself, and every time it had been doing the same thing: sitting at the edge of things, patient, watching, the way a cat watches a hole where something small lives.

Reyes's scout. The fleet's eye. It had found them — or it had found the *Kestrel's Wake* and was waiting to see what they'd dig up.

And it had been hunting her, Sefa, specifically, since the port. She was almost sure of it. The man at the harbor office who'd asked too many questions. The launch that had idled past the *Kestrel's Wake* twice on their first night, running dark. Her grandmother's name, which Sefa had not spoken aloud in three years, written on a list she'd glimpsed and never confirmed and never stopped seeing in her head.

She could tell them. Right now. Tamsin was awake up there at the wheel — Sefa could see her shape against the stars, keeping the first watch, keeping it the way she did everything, like a debt being paid. Sefa could walk up and say: *I know that ship. I know why it's here. It's not here for the Corvane. It's here for me, and I didn't tell you, and I'm telling you now.*

The words got as far as her teeth.

But the *Vigilant* was a long way off, and the night was quiet, and if she said it now — tonight, on the day she'd finally, finally been *kept*, been voted into a crew, been handed hot tea and the good bunk and a place at a table — it would all become about her. The questions. The suspicion. The look Tamsin gave people, the manifest look, the *weighed and filed* look, turned on Sefa at last. And worse than the look: the possibility that Tamsin would do the arithmetic, the cold salvage arithmetic, and decide that a runaway with a navy cutter on her trail was cargo too dangerous to carry.

Sefa had been put off one boat already in her life. She was not going to give this one a reason.

She stood at the rail and watched the white light until her eyes ached, and she memorized its bearing, and she said nothing.

Behind her, on the wheel deck, Tamsin Montrose shifted at her watch, and Sefa heard her say, quietly, to the night or to herself: "She's out there somewhere. Aren't you, Ilsa."

Sefa went below without answering, and lay in the good bunk, and listened to the reef breathe.

The light on the horizon stayed where it was all night — and in the morning, it was gone, and there was a boat standing off the reef at dawn, flying a white flag at its masthead, and a voice on the radio, crisp and cultured and horribly familiar from a port office three weeks ago, saying: *Kestrel's Wake, Kestrel's Wake, this is the survey vessel Vigilant. Captain Reyes extends her compliments, and requests the pleasure of your captain's company.*
