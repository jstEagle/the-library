## 10. The Fleet at Anchor

The invitation came at dawn, delivered by a navy launch that sat in the water like a knife laid on a table.

Tamsin was on deck with a mug of tea she'd already forgotten about when the boat rounded the headland of the reef — white hull, grey superstructure, and a young officer in the bow holding up a sealed envelope as if it might detonate. Behind him, anchored in the deep water past the outer shoals, the salvage fleet had grown overnight to four ships: two dredge tenders, a supply vessel whose cranes could lift a locomotive, and the flagship herself, the *Vigilant*, all nine hundred tons of her sitting there with the patient menace of something that had never once needed to hurry.

"Flag of truce," the officer said, once he'd clambered aboard and found his sea legs among the rust and coiled hose of the *Kestrel's Wake*. He looked around at their little survey boat the way a man looks at a dog that's wandered into a cathedral. "Captain Reyes requests the pleasure of your company aboard the *Vigilant* at eleven hundred hours. Unarmed. Alone."

"She requests," Tamsin said.

"The Captain always requests." He handed over the envelope, saluted nobody in particular, and left.

The envelope contained a single card, heavy stock, engraved. *Captain I. Reyes, Salvage Command.* Below it, in handwriting Tamsin hadn't seen in fourteen years but recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror: *Miss Montrose. You were always better at this than I was. Come see what it bought me.*

Brannoc read it over her shoulder, which for Brannoc meant standing so close he was mostly reading the inside of her skull.

"It's a trap," he said.

"It's brandy."

"It's brandy-shaped trap."

Sefa leaned against the rail, arms crossed, watching the fleet with an expression Tamsin couldn't read — something older than a nineteen-year-old should have owned. "You're going."

"I'm going."

"Then I want to know the current tables off the *Vigilant*'s anchorage before you go, and I want Ovid to memorize them, and if you're not back by thirteen hundred we come get you whether there's a flag flying or not."

Tamsin looked at her. The girl had been aboard six days and had already organized them more thoroughly than Tamsin ever had. "Thirteen hundred."

"And wear the good coat," Sefa added. "If you're going to lie to a captain, look like you can afford to."

Ovid, who had emerged from below with the Corvane's manifest notes stuck to his fingers like leaves, said only: "Mind the third glass of brandy. That's where the offers live."

* * *

The launch took her out across water gone suddenly formal and flat, as though the whole bay understood that something official was happening. Up close the *Vigilant* was worse than from shore — not neglected, exactly, but maintained the way a weapon is maintained, everything oiled and nothing loved. Sailors moved along her decks with the economy of people who had long ago stopped seeing the ocean except as a workplace hazard.

Reyes met her at the gangway herself.

Fourteen years had done to Ilsa Reyes what time does to a figurehead: kept the shape, hardened the surface. She'd been lean and quick when Tamsin knew her, all restless hands and laughter that came too easily; now she stood still the way anchors stand still, silver shot through her cropped hair, her uniform pressed to the point of aggression. But her eyes were the same — pale grey, quick, and always, always measuring.

"Tamsin Montrose." She said it like she was checking a manifest entry. Then, softer, almost against her will: "You look like your mother."

"You look like a promotion."

Something flickered at the corner of Reyes's mouth — the ghost of the woman who used to laugh at Tamsin's mother's jokes around a camp stove on Ansel Key. "Come below. The weather topside is about to be political."

* * *

The great cabin of the *Vigilant* was designed to intimidate and succeeded. Polished mahogany, charts under glass, a model of some earlier flagship on a shelf like a saint's relic. Brandy waited on a small table between two chairs arranged with the careful casualness of a negotiation.

Reyes poured without asking. "Kestrel brandy. From the old distillery. Your mother used to smuggle it out by the case and swear it was medicinal."

"I remember."

"I imagine you do." Reyes sat, and for a moment — just a moment — the captaincy slipped and Tamsin saw the woman underneath, tired in a way that no sleep would fix. "You've been diving the Corvane."

It wasn't a question, so Tamsin didn't insult them both by denying it. She drank instead. The brandy was excellent, which felt like a personal attack.

"Three tides' work on the outer hull," Reyes went on. "My hydrographers are very thorough. You cut through the cargo hatch seal on the second tide. You'll find the hold stripped, by the way. Has been for years. Whoever told you there was treasure down there sold you a story."

"Maybe I'm not after treasure."

"Everyone's after treasure. The ones who claim otherwise are just worse at pricing things." Reyes turned her glass slowly on the table, one full rotation, eyes never leaving Tamsin's face. It was an old habit, that rotation. Her mother used to say Ilsa counted her thoughts like coins. "Here's what happens now, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from a boarding party. The Corvane is a navy matter. Wreck number C-117 in the restricted register. Salvage rights suspended by ministerial order. Any vessel attempting recovery operations will be seized, its crew detained, and its master charged under the colonial maritime act. That's the version for the record."

"And the version off the record?"

Reyes smiled, and it was the smile that frightened Tamsin most, because it was warm. It was the smile that had taught a sixteen-year-old orphan how to read a depth gauge and tie a bowline blindfolded and believe, briefly, that someone in the world was on her side.

"The version off the record is that I'm offering you partnership."

She let it sit. Outside, gulls argued over something dead.

"A quarter share," Reyes continued. "Of whatever's raised. Which, since the hold is empty, means the strongroom — and yes, before you ask, I know about the strongroom. Voss's chart has been in navy hands for two years. He tried to sell it to us first. We declined." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "He then tried to sell it to you. Which tells me either he trusted you, or he wanted you dead, and having met the man I genuinely couldn't say which."

"He's dead," Tamsin said. "Since before I left port."

"Yes. So I heard." Reyes drank, unhurried. "Partnership, Tamsin. My ships, my equipment, my legal cover. Your skill, your crew, and whatever your mother left behind down there that I suspect you want more than gold." Her voice gentled further, which was how Tamsin knew the blade was coming. "I know what she was doing on that wreck. I was there when the search was called off. I signed the form."

The room got very quiet, or Tamsin did.

"You signed the form," Tamsin repeated.

"Somebody had to. Better me than a stranger." Reyes set down her glass. "I testified at the inquiry because the alternative was losing my command and her getting nothing anyway. I've paid for that testimony every day since, and I don't expect forgiveness, and I'm not here to ask for it. I'm here because you're the best diver I've ever seen — and yes, better than she was, she'd have agreed, she'd have been insufferable about it — and because in approximately five days a monsoon is going to arrive and fold that reef in half, and I would rather neither of us ends up folded into it."

"And the documents."

"What documents?"

"The ones in the strongroom. The governor's confession. The Meridian treasury ledger. The proof that the fortune the Corvane carried was looted from families your ministry still sends Christmas cards to."

For the first time, Reyes went still in a way that wasn't controlled. It lasted perhaps two seconds. Then the stillness closed over, smooth as water over a sinking thing.

"That," she said, "is precisely the kind of talk that gets crews detained."

"So there are documents."

"There is a strongroom. Its contents are classified. If they exist, they will be handled by the ministry, through proper channels, over a period of years, with appropriate redactions—"

"—buried."

"—preserved," Reyes said, "in the national interest." And there it was. Tamsin watched it happen across the polished table: the woman she'd loved like a second mother, the one who'd taught her that the sea doesn't care who's right, only who's prepared — watched ambition finish its slow work and harden into something that wore loyalty's face. Calcified. Her mother had used that word once about coral. *It looks like rock, love, but it started alive, and that's why it cuts you.*

"I taught you to dive," Reyes said quietly. "Your mother taught you why. Between us we made you the finest salvage master on this coast, and you're wasting it on a rotting survey boat with a disgraced engineer and a runaway and an archivist who smells of mildew and guilt. Take the quarter share. Take a real ship. Take—" and here her voice did something almost human, almost pleading, "—take the chance I never gave myself to stop moving."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment.

"You think I move because I'm running," Tamsin said. "You offered me a fortune and a fleet and you still don't understand. I'm not running from anything, Ilsa. I just never had anything worth staying for." She set down the glass untasted, the third glass, the one where the offers lived. "Until maybe now."

Reyes's face didn't change, but her hand, flat on the table, went very slightly white at the knuckles.

"The strongroom is a navy matter," she said.

"Then the navy should dive it themselves."

"The navy intends to."

"With charges?" Tamsin asked, and watched the flicker again — faster this time, badly hidden. "I've seen your dredges biting the reef a quarter mile east. Dredging doesn't need that pattern unless you're planning to bring the outer wall down. Seal the wreck. Bury the evidence. Call it storm damage." She stood. "You taught me to read a work site, Ilsa. Before you taught me anything else."

Reyes rose too, and for a heartbeat they stood across the table from each other the way they'd once stood across a camp stove, and Tamsin thought — absurdly, painfully — *say it isn't true, tell me I'm wrong, be her again.*

"Eleven hundred hours was generous," Reyes said at last, cold and correct. "The truce holds until you're back aboard your vessel. After that, the Corvane is closed water. Anything in it belongs to the Crown."

"Everything in it," Tamsin said, "belongs to the families it was stolen from. Even your own law says so, buried somewhere under the redactions. You'd know. You sign forms."

She walked out. At the gangway, unable to help herself, she looked back. Reyes stood at the cabin door with the untouched brandy in her hand, and her expression wasn't anger. It was grief wearing a uniform, and it was the worst thing Tamsin had seen in fourteen years.

"Your mother chose this too," Reyes called after her, not unkindly. "Look how that ended."

* * *

The launch ride back was long and grey. Tamsin spent it doing sums she didn't like: four tides left, maybe five if Sefa's currents held; a fleet with cranes and charges and legal impunity; a monsoon coming like a debt collector. Against that: one rotten boat, three strays, and a promise made to a dying man she'd known for one night.

Bad odds. Her mother would have called them *interesting*.

Sefa met her at the rail before the launch was even tied off, reading her face the way she read water. "Well?"

"We've got until the next low tide before they make their move. Maybe less." Tamsin swung herself aboard. "Get Brannoc. Wake Ovid. Whatever's in that hold, we dive it today — and Sefa?"

"Yeah?"

"If I'm wrong about the timing, we lose everything. If I'm right, we lose it tomorrow instead. Either way—" she glanced back at the *Vigilant*, gleaming and patient at anchor, "—we're diving in an hour."

Below, already, she could hear Brannoc swearing at the winch with the particular affection he reserved for machines he intended to save. The *Kestrel's Wake* smelled of rust and tea and other people's dreams. Tamsin went down the hatch toward the sound of her strange, stubborn, temporary family — and tried very hard not to wonder which of them, someday soon, might turn out to be permanent in the way a knife in the back is permanent.
