## 11. Second Tide

Brannoc had a theory about rust, which he shared with anyone who would listen and several who wouldn't: rust was honest. Steel lied about its intentions right up until the moment it failed, but rust told you everything — where it had been hurt, how long it had been hurting, and exactly how much longer it intended to hold on out of spite.

The Corvane was the most honest wreck he'd ever met. She was telling him, in no uncertain terms, that she wanted to fall over.

"Talk to me, Brannoc," Tamsin's voice said through the comm line, tinny and patient in his earpiece. She was forty feet down at the hull's seaward flank, running her glove along the plating where he'd marked the cut line an hour before. "You've gone quiet, and quiet from you is never good news."

"I'm composing my remarks."

"You're muttering."

"I'm *composing*," Brannoc said, "my remarks." He sat cross-legged on the Kestrel's Wake's aft deck with the cutting rig spread around him like a surgeon's tray, feeding cable and checking gauges while Sefa leaned over the rail beside him, watching the water with the particular intensity of a girl reading something written there. Above them the sky did that thing it had been doing all morning — gathering itself, going bruise-colored at the edges, then pretending to be innocent again.

"The hold access is under the port cargo hatch," he said finally. "Voss's chart marks it plain as a church door. Problem is the door's been shut for sixty years, and the hinges are one with the frame now, and the frame is one with the deck, and the deck—" he patted the schematic pinned under his knee, "—is one with the general concept of collapse. So we don't open the door. We cut a new one."

Tamsin's exhale crackled in his ear. "How big?"

"Big enough for you. Small enough that she doesn't notice."

"She'll notice."

"Of course she'll notice. Everything notices. That's not the point. The point is whether she objects before we're done." He thumbed the igniter test and watched the arc jump blue-white across the electrode gap. "Ovid! Where's Ovid?"

"Here." Ovid appeared at his elbow the way he always did, silently, holding a clipboard he'd owned for approximately two days and already treated like a family heirloom. He'd wrapped it in oilcloth against the spray. The man waterproofed his paperwork more carefully than he waterproofed himself; Brannoc had seen him stand in a foot of bilge without complaint but cover a page of notes with his own body during a squall.

"Read me the manifest entry again. Cargo hold, midships section."

Ovid cleared his throat, which he did before every recitation, as though addressing a committee. "*Corvane*, official manifest, third revision, sealed by the colonial customs office at Meridian. Hold Two: crated goods, miscellaneous, declared value four hundred pounds sterling." A pause. "Declared value," he repeated, with the dry relish of a man who had spent thirty years in archives learning what the word *declared* was worth. "Which we now know covered roughly one-tenth of what actually went into those crates."

"And the crates were iron-banded, oak-faced, lead-lined?"

"According to the customs surveyor's marginalia, yes. The surveyor also wrote 'excessive weight' in the margin and then, presumably after a conversation with someone important, crossed it out."

Brannoc grunted. Lead-lined crates meant the cutting plan held. Oak and iron gave nicely under the exothermic lance; lead sulked but surrendered eventually. It was the *wreck* he didn't trust. Sixty years on a reef shelf, rolled by a hundred storms, picked at by every current in the strait — the Corvane wasn't a ship anymore so much as a rumor of a ship, held together by sediment, habit, and whatever the sea hadn't gotten around to finishing.

"Right," he said. "Lower the rig. Slowly. If you drop my lance I will come down there after it, and I swim like a church."

* * *

The first cut went beautifully, which should have warned him.

Brannoc ran the torch work himself — Tamsin had argued for it, then looked at his hands, then stopped arguing, which he appreciated even if he'd have rathered she'd kept arguing. His hands weren't what they'd been. Ten years grinding valves in a prison machine shop and five more pretending he hadn't been an engineer at all had left them steady enough for wrenches and unsteady enough for anything finer. But the lance didn't need fine. It needed patience, and patience was the one thing prison had given him in industrial quantities.

He went down on the second tide's slack water with Tamsin on his shoulder and Sefa's voice in both their ears, calling the current the way other people called weather. The girl had a gift he couldn't explain and didn't try to. She'd lie flat on the foredeck with her eyes half-shut and say things like *"it's turning early, give me ten minutes"* and be right to the minute, and when Brannoc asked her how she knew she'd shrug and say *"you can hear it if you listen,"* which was either mysticism or a joke at his expense, and with Sefa it was genuinely difficult to tell.

The Corvane lay on her starboard side at fifty feet, canted into the reef like a drunk leaning on a wall, her masts long gone, her superstructure furred white with coral until she looked less like a wreck than a reef that had dreamed briefly of being a ship. Fish moved through her in silver rivers. A grouper the size of a wheelbarrow regarded them from the shadow of the collapsed bridge wing with the flat disapproval of a landlord.

"There," Tamsin said, pointing. The cargo hatch was a dark rectangle under six inches of marine growth, and Voss's chart had marked it true. Brannoc set his feet on the sloping deck, felt the wreck shift a half-inch beneath him — a settling, a sigh, the whole drowned structure deciding whether to tolerate his presence — and lit the lance.

Cutting underwater was a religion with one sacrament: bubbles. You watched the bubbles, you read the bubbles, and the bubbles told you whether the metal was taking the heat or hoarding it for a surprise. These bubbles rose fat and lazy, and the iron parted along his line like bread, and for twenty minutes Brannoc was happier than he had been in fifteen years. There was nothing in the world like making a hole where a hole ought to be. Give a man a lance and a bad structure and he'd forgive God everything else.

Then the plate fell inward, exactly as planned, and the light from their lamps went into the hold and came back wrong.

Not black. Not empty. *Pale.*

"Brannoc," Tamsin said quietly. "Look."

He looked. The cargo hold of the *Corvane* — the hold that according to Ovid's documents contained the looted treasury of the Meridian families, lead-lined crates stacked to the overhead, a fortune in stolen gold sitting in the dark for sixty years — was clean.

Not damaged. Not scattered. *Clean.* The deck plating gleamed faintly where their lamps swept it, scoured of silt. The tie-down rings stood bare. Where crates should have been there were only pale rectangles in the growth on the bulkheads — shadows of shadows, outlines left behind like the ghosts of furniture in an abandoned house — and a scatter of debris too modern for the wreck: shreds of synthetic line, a corroded dive weight stamped with a logo neither of them recognized, and, snagged on a stanchion, a scrap of rubberized fabric that might once have been a lift bag.

Somebody had been here. Somebody had emptied it.

"No," Tamsin said. It wasn't a word aimed at Brannoc or at anything in particular. It was aimed at the hold, at the sea, at sixty years of history that had just rearranged itself out from under her. She swam forward through the hatch opening, lamp sweeping, and the pale rectangles marched past her beam — dozens of them, crate-shaped, all empty, all gone.

"Tamsin." Brannoc caught her arm. "Air check. And look at the silt lines before you touch anything. When did this happen?"

She stopped. He could see her make herself stop, make herself go back to being the diver instead of the daughter, and he respected the hell out of it and hated watching it cost her. Her lamp tracked slowly along the deck. "Silt's thin everywhere inside. Thinner than outside. Whatever washed through here got flushed recently-ish — decade? Less?" She lifted the scrap of lift bag off the stanchion and turned it over. "This fabric hasn't been made in twenty years. Maybe thirty."

"So someone beat us here."

"Someone beat my mother here." Her voice stayed level, which was how he knew it was bad. Tamsin Montrose loud was fine. Tamsin Montrose quiet was a woman standing very still on a ledge. "Voss said she reached the strongroom. If the hold's been stripped since before she dove it, then she knew. She came anyway. She was looking for something the gold was just *covering* for."

Brannoc let that sit. Down the length of the hold, their lamps found the far bulkhead and the low service hatch that led aft toward the engine spaces and, somewhere beyond it, below the waterline, wherever Voss's inked lines meant they should look — the strongroom. The hatch was shut. Of course it was shut. Nothing on this ship was ever conveniently open; the Corvane had died stubborn and intended to stay that way.

"We should go back up," he said. "Report. Plan. Ovid will want to recheck the manifest against the layout, and Sefa says the tide turns in twenty minutes, and I personally would like to not be inside a hollow steel drum when the current starts playing it like a flute."

Tamsin didn't move. Her lamp rested on the far hatch, and Brannoc watched her do arithmetic with her face — air, distance, time, the numbers every diver runs without knowing they're running them — and watched the numbers lose to something that wasn't a number.

"One look," she said. "Down the passage. Just to see if the hatch is even passable."

"That's how it starts," Brannoc said. "One look. Then one more look. Then I'm diving in after your corpse and Sefa has to write a sad song about it, and she'll rhyme badly on purpose because she thinks grief rhyming well is dishonest."

That got a breath of a laugh out of her, which was the point. She turned back toward the hatch they'd cut. And that was when the wreck objected.

It started as a sound — a groan from deep in the keel, long and structural, the voice of sixty years of compromise reaching a decision. Then the deck tilted. Not much. An inch. Two. Enough. Sediment bloomed around them in a brown cloud, and somewhere above, along the old cargo hatch coaming, a seam let go with a crack like a rifle, and the grouper that had been supervising from the bridge wing departed at speed with the air of a creature who had warned them.

"Out," Brannoc said, and shoved her toward the hole they'd cut, and for about nine seconds the two of them performed the least dignified emergency exit in maritime history — fins catching on tie-down rings, lamp beams cartwheeling, Brannoc swearing with a mouthpiece in his teeth, which produced sounds like a kettle being murdered. They shot up the guide line hand over hand while behind and below them the Corvane settled another inch and thought better of it, sulking back into the reef, having made her point.

They broke surface thirty yards from the boat, both coughing, both laughing in the shaky way people laugh when the alternative is screaming.

"Well," Tamsin gasped. "She noticed."

"She noticed," Brannoc agreed.

* * *

The debrief happened in the galley, because the Kestrel's Wake's galley was the only compartment big enough to hold an argument, and everyone aboard understood on some instinctive level that an argument was coming.

Brannoc sat on the counter because the chairs were broken, drinking tea that Sefa had made, which tasted like hot salt with ambitions. Ovid had the manifest spread out and was annotating it in pencil with the grim energy of a man whose life's scholarship had just been mugged in an alley. Tamsin stood at the little porthole with her arms crossed, watching the horizon, where the weather was doing its bruise trick again, and — farther off, a smudge Brannoc pretended not to have seen twice already today — something else.

"Stripped," Ovid said for the fourth time. "Completely. Every crate. Which means—" he flipped pages, "—either the manifest is false, which is possible, manifests lie the way fish swim, naturally and constantly, or the treasure was removed *after* sinking, which is impossible, or—"

"Or it was removed years ago by divers who knew exactly where it was," Tamsin said, not turning from the window. "And left the strongroom sealed."

"Why would they leave the strongroom?"

"Because the strongroom isn't where the gold was." She turned around. "Think about it. Voss's chart doesn't mark the hold. It marks the compartment below the waterline, aft of the engine room. The hold was the decoy — the declared cargo, the story for customs. The real shipment rode low, in the strongroom, where nobody would look. Whoever stripped the hold either didn't know about the strongroom, or couldn't get into it, or—" she hesitated, "—was looking for something else."

"Documents," Ovid said softly. All the pencil-scratching stopped. "If the governor's confession was aboard, and if anyone in the colonial administration suspected it... they wouldn't send salvors for gold. Gold can be explained away. A confession cannot." He took off his glasses, which he did when thinking hard, and polished them on a rag that was dirtier than the glasses. "They may have come for paper and taken the gold merely because it was there."

"Convenient thieves," Sefa said from the corner, where she was wedged into the space between the icebox and the bulkhead, the spot she'd claimed the first day and defended since with the territorial ferocity of a cat. "Rich ones, though. Thirty years ago, gear like that? That's navy-grade kit or money-grade kit. Same thing, mostly."

"Thank you, Sefa, for that bracing dose of cynicism," Brannoc said.

"You're welcome."

Tamsin pulled Voss's chart from its oilcloth sleeve and spread it on the table, weighting the corners with tea mugs. Everyone crowded in. The ink had faded to the color of weak coffee, but the lines were sure — a draftsman's lines, drawn by someone who'd studied the Corvane's plans closely enough to draw her guts from memory. The hold was marked with a small X and the word *declared*. Below the waterline, aft, tucked behind the engine room bulkhead, a second chamber was drawn in heavier strokes, hatched like a fortress, with a single word beside it in Voss's cramped hand:

*Strongroom. Wheel seal. Below load line.*

"Wheel seal," Brannoc read aloud. "Meaning a screw-type door. Threaded spindle, handwheel, dogged all around. Pre-war bank-vault pattern." He traced the passage from the hold to the compartment — through the engine room, through a flooded machinery space, through a watertight door that had been shut since the ship went down. "It's reachable. Barely. It's a bastard of a dive — narrow, dark, silt traps everywhere, and if that wreck decides to finish falling over while we're in the guts of her, no one finds us until the monsoon sorts through the wreckage and files a report."

"Naturally," Ovid said.

"But it's reachable," Brannoc repeated, and heard his own voice do the thing it did when his hands started wanting work. He hated that about himself. Fifteen years sober of engineering and one look at an impossible door and he was planning tooling again, lying awake designing winches. "A screw seal doesn't rot like a hinge. It's built to hold pressure — it's built to hold *sixty years of pressure*. If the threads aren't fused, we can turn it. Slowly. From inside the antechamber, with a bar and a lot of profanity."

Silence, except for the wind finding something loose up on deck to worry at.

"It's there," Tamsin said. Not a question. She was looking at the chart the way she'd looked at the hold — that stillness again — but this time the stillness had a floor under it. "Under everything. Under the decoy, under the stripped hold, under whoever came before us. My mother got to the wheel, Brannoc. Voss swore it. Which means it can be reached, and it can be opened, and whatever's behind it is still there because nobody in sixty years has managed to take it."

"Nobody we know of," Ovid murmured.

"Nobody we know of," she agreed.

Brannoc looked around the galley. The disgraced engineer, the runaway, the archivist, the woman with a dead mother and a promise to a dead prospector, all of them leaning over a coffee-colored map of a dead ship, planning to break into a vault at the bottom of the sea ahead of a monsoon and a navy fleet. In prison he'd known men who'd risked years of their sentences for a cigarette. He'd judged them, once, from the comfortable height of a man who assumed he'd never want anything that much.

He wanted this that much. That was the alarming part. Not the gold — though he wouldn't pretend the gold didn't glint appealingly at the edge of the thought, debts being what they were and his being considerable. It was the door. The impossible, threaded, dogged, sixty-years-sealed door, and the fact that for the first time since the yard closed down and his name went on a list, somebody was asking him to open something instead of explaining why it couldn't be done.

"All right," he said. "I'll need three days to build the winch and the wheel rig. I'll need Brannoc's First Law obeyed at all times."

"What's the First Law?" Sefa asked.

"Nothing falls faster than hope, so tie everything down."

Ovid wrote it on his clipboard. Brannoc chose to find that endearing rather than alarming.

Tamsin rolled up the chart, and her eyes went past all of them to the porthole again, to the horizon, and Brannoc followed her gaze and saw it clearly this time and knew she'd seen it too: a smudge off the southern headland, gray against gray, stationary in a way ships at work are stationary and ships hunting are also stationary, and there was no way at this distance to tell which.

"Sefa," Tamsin said lightly. "What's our tide window tomorrow?"

Sefa glanced at the water, at the sky, at some private ledger she kept behind her eyes. "Slack's at dawn. Good water till mid-morning." A pause, shorter than a heartbeat but Brannoc caught it. "Maybe less."

"Then we dive at dawn," Tamsin said. "Third tide. Straight through the engine room to the wheel."

Nobody mentioned the smudge on the horizon. Nobody had to. It sat there in the middle of the galley with them, quieter than any of them, and waited.
