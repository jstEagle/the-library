## 12. Ovid's Confession

Ovid Pell had spent thirty years perfecting the art of being overlooked. It was a craft, like any other. He had cultivated a stoop that made him seem smaller than he was, a cough that arrived precisely when conversations turned toward his past, and a pair of spectacles so thick they functioned less as lenses than as a kind of curtain. People saw an old archivist, half-blind, half-useless, and they looked away. He had built a life in that looking-away.

But there were limits to any craft, and Ovid had just discovered his.

They were gathered in the Kestrel's Wake's cramped galley — Tamsin at the head of the table with her arms crossed, Brannoc looming by the stove with a wrench he was holding for no mechanical reason whatsoever, Sefa perched on the counter where she wasn't supposed to sit and sat anyway — and all of them were looking directly at him.

"So," Tamsin said. "The hold."

"The hold," Ovid agreed.

"Stripped clean. Decades ago, by the look of the tool marks." She leaned forward. "And you knew."

"I suspected," Ovid said, because even now, cornered, some ancient librarian reflex reached for precision. "There is a difference between knowing and suspecting, and I'd ask the court to—"

"Ovid."

He set down his tea. His hands, he noticed with distant academic interest, were not quite steady. Thirty years of silence, and it turned out silence had weight, and it had been sitting on his chest the whole voyage like a sea chest full of ledgers.

"You're right," he said. "I knew."

* * *

It had started, as most ruinous things do, with paperwork.

Ovid had been twenty-six years old and freshly certified when the Corvane's manifest crossed his desk at the Colonial Archive in Port Meridian. It was flagged for routine verification — a treasure shipment, which meant it needed three signatures instead of one, which meant it needed his. He remembered the afternoon vividly: rain against the archive windows, the smell of dust and sealing wax, the particular boredom of a young man who believed documents were documents.

Then he read the manifest.

The official manifest listed trade goods. Iron ingots, dyed cotton, salted fish, ceramic ware. Perfectly ordinary. But attached to it, in the governor's own hand, was a supplementary schedule — sealed, marked *privy*, meant never to see daylight. And Ovid, because he was thorough before he was wise, broke the seal.

Gold coin. Plate. Jewels. Deeds to land. And beneath the inventory, in the same hand, a list of names. Meridian families — shipping families, farming families, families whose grand houses still stood along the harbor front — each name followed by a figure. What had been taken from them. Not taxed. Taken. The governor had looted them under color of law, loaded their wealth aboard the Corvane, and intended to deliver it into navy hands as the price of his own advancement.

The ship sank. The governor wrote a confession, sealed it, and died comfortable. The families spent a generation telling themselves they'd been ruined by bad luck and worse weather.

And Ovid Pell catalogued the supplementary schedule, entered it into the restricted index, and said nothing.

"Why?" Sefa asked. She wasn't accusing. That was somehow worse. She just wanted to know, the way she wanted to know everything, with that fierce unguarded curiosity of hers.

"Because I was told to," Ovid said. "Because my superior looked at the schedule and went very pale and told me it was above my station. Because the man who signed off on the suppression later became Undersecretary of the Admiralty, and I watched what happened to the last clerk who asked questions about Meridian accounts. He was reassigned to a lighthouse. A lighthouse, Brannoc. The man could barely swim." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, though they didn't need polishing; it was something to do with his hands. "I told myself I was being prudent. Then I told myself I was being patient. Then thirty years passed, and I realized I had simply been afraid for three decades and called it discretion."

Brannoc set the wrench down on the table with a clang that made everyone flinch. "You knew what was in that strongroom. You've known since we left port. And you let us—" He stopped, jaw working. "Tamsin nearly drowned on second tide. We cut into that hold expecting cargo. You knew it might be empty. You knew someone might have gotten there first, and you said nothing, because your precious index told you there'd been a salvage claim filed in—" he squinted at Ovid, "—what year?"

"Forty-one years ago," Ovid admitted. "A partial recovery. Unregistered vessel. The claim was withdrawn after two dives."

"So you knew the hold was probably stripped," Tamsin said quietly, "and you let us burn four days of our six-tide window chasing it."

The galley went very still. Outside, the reef muttered against the hull, and somewhere below them lay the Corvane, and somewhere inside the Corvane lay the strongroom, and somewhere inside Ovid lay thirty years of cowardice that had finally, finally gotten too heavy to carry.

"Yes," he said. "I did. And I am sorry. But before you put me ashore — and you'd be within your rights — I'd ask you to hear why I'm here at all."

* * *

He told them about the night he'd decided to stop being afraid, and it hadn't been noble. That was the thing he needed them to understand. It hadn't been some dawn-of-conviction moment. It had been a Tuesday.

"I was sixty-one years old," he said. "I was reorganizing the restricted stacks — again, because reorganizing is what cowards do instead of acting — and I came across the schedule a second time. My own handwriting on the intake card. Younger handwriting. Neater." He smiled, and it hurt. "And next to it, a newer card. A request slip. Someone had requested the file eleven years prior and been denied. The requesting party: Ede Voss."

Sefa frowned. "The prospector? The one who gave Tamsin the chart?"

"The same. Voss had spent years trying to reach the truth through proper channels. Denied at every turn, and he couldn't understand why, because he didn't know what I knew — that the denial came from the very top, from men whose fortunes rested on that schedule staying buried." Ovid folded his hands. "So I did the only brave thing available to a coward of my particular skills. I misfiled it."

Brannoc blinked. "You what?"

"I misfiled it. Spectacularly. Into a section of the archive so disorganized that no retrieval system on earth could find it — a section, I'll have you know, that I personally created over the course of several years, filled with deliberately contradictory cross-references. The pride of my career, gentlemen and ladies. Forty thousand documents that cannot be found unless you already know exactly where they are." He tapped his temple. "Which I do."

"And then?" Tamsin asked.

"And then I resigned, and I waited, because a document in a vault helps no one, and I had no idea how to get it out. And then, three weeks ago, a dying prospector walked into a dive bar, recognized a diver's name, and traded away the location of the Corvane." Ovid looked at her. "Voss found me, Miss Montrose. Or rather — I found him. I'd been watching the harbors for years, waiting for someone mad enough to go. When I heard he was asking after you — Montrose, of the *Sea Lark*, the woman who died on that reef — I bought him dinner and listened through the wall."

Tamsin had gone very pale. "You were there. In the bar."

"I was in the booth behind you. I ordered a fish stew I did not eat, because I was listening to a dying man make a promise out of you, and I thought: there. There is my vehicle. There is the mad diver who will go where I could not." He spread his hands. "So I presented myself to you as a washed-up archivist who happened to know the manifest. Which was true. I simply neglected the part where I was the washed-up archivist."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Ovid counted the galley clock's ticks. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

It was Brannoc who broke it, and he broke it loudly, which was Brannoc's way with everything.

"You used us." He jabbed a finger at Ovid. "You used all of us. This girl—" another finger, at Sefa, who looked delighted to be included in an accusation, "is seventeen. Tamsin has been diving a wreck that killed her mother, and you sat in the fo'c'sle doing crossword puzzles while we bled for a secret you'd been sitting on since before she was born!"

"In fairness," Ovid said, "the crosswords help me think."

"Not the point, Ovid."

"No," Ovid said. "It isn't. And you're right, Mr. Brannoc, every word. I used you. I chose you the way one chooses a crowbar — for your usefulness in opening something too heavy for an old man's hands. I told myself the ends justified it. I told myself the families deserved the truth more than you deserved my honesty." He took a breath. "I have discovered, at my age, that the ends justify nothing. They merely decorate the means. And the means were contemptible, and I am sorry, and I will spend whatever remains of my life making it right if you'll allow me to stay aboard and try."

Tamsin hadn't spoken. She sat with her arms crossed, staring at the table, and Ovid — who had spent thirty years reading people the way he read documents, for marginalia and erasures — watched her work through it. Anger first. Then something colder: recalculation. Every conversation recalibrated. Every piece of his helpfulness reweighed.

"What else," she said finally. It wasn't a question so much as a door held open.

"The strongroom compartment below the waterline," Ovid said. "Voss's map shows it. So does the governor's confession — he describes the construction himself, in a postscript. A secondary vault, sealed from inside, built to survive the ship. The gold in the main hold was bait, if you like. Flash. The real shipment — the schedule, the confession, the ledger of names — went below." He paused. "And there is one more thing. The partial salvage forty-one years ago. Two dives, then withdrawn. I pulled the withdrawal order myself, before I left the archive. It was signed by a junior officer named Reyes."

The name landed in the galley like a dropped anchor.

"Ilsa Reyes," Tamsin said.

"The same. She dove the Corvane once, decades ago. She knows what's down there better than anyone alive — including, I suspect, herself. And she withdrew the operation the moment her divers got close to the lower compartment." Ovid met her eyes. "Miss Montrose, I don't believe Captain Reyes is hunting this wreck for the navy's honor. I believe she has been keeping it buried for a very long time, and I believe she will do anything necessary to keep doing so."

Sefa slid off the counter. "Then we're not racing her to treasure," she said slowly. "We're racing her to evidence."

"We are racing her," Ovid said, "to the truth. Which is heavier than treasure, harder to carry, and worth considerably more. Though I grant you," he added, with the ghost of his old dry humor, "it does not fence as well."

No one laughed. Well — Sefa almost did, and then caught Tamsin's eye and didn't.

* * *

They argued for another hour. Brannoc wanted him off the boat at the next anchorage, or confined to the fo'c'sle, or — in one memorable flourish — "made to swim home, see how he likes the lighthouse posting." Sefa defended him with the fierce logic of someone who had secrets of her own and knew how easily they could be dragged into the light. And Tamsin sat through all of it, silent, letting the crew show her its seams.

In the end it came down to her, and everyone knew it, and the galley went quiet in a way that made the reef outside sound enormous.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," Tamsin said at last. "My mother died diving for this. Voss died carrying its map. And you—" she pointed at Ovid, and her voice cracked slightly, which she covered badly, "you sat on it for thirty years and then picked my boat to die on, if it comes to that. I should throw you overboard. Brannoc's right. By every rule I've ever sailed by, you're done."

She stood. Walked to the porthole. Looked out at the dark water where the Corvane lay.

"But my mother trusted her crew," she said, without turning around. "And somebody on her crew sold her. So maybe rules are the problem." She turned back, and her eyes were red-rimmed and hard as barnacles. "You stay, Ovid. But you're done deciding things for us. From now on, you tell us everything, the day you learn it, even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly. Are we clear?"

"Perfectly," Ovid said. "And thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank Sefa, who talked me out of feeding you to the reef." Tamsin headed for the companionway, then stopped. "One more thing. The confession — the governor's. Is it readable? After forty years underwater?"

Ovid allowed himself a small smile. "It was written in iron-gall ink on rag paper and sealed in waxed canvas, according to the governor's own instructions. Rag paper survives immersion remarkably well. The governor, whatever else he was, was a meticulous criminal." He tapped the table. "It will read like it was written yesterday. That, Miss Montrose, is rather the point."

She nodded once and went up the ladder. Brannoc lingered, wrench in hand, glaring at Ovid with the expression of a man who has lost an argument but intends to win the war.

"For the record," Brannoc growled, "if you hold out on us again, I don't care how good your crosswords are."

"Duly noted," Ovid said. "And Mr. Brannoc — for what it's worth, I'm glad it was your boat. All of you. Even the shouting." He hesitated, then added, quieter: "Thirty years is a long time to be the only one who knows a thing. I'd forgotten what it was like to say it out loud."

Brannoc grunted — which, Ovid was learning, was the man's equivalent of a warm embrace — and clanked off toward the engine room.

Sefa stayed. She hopped back onto the counter, which was against regulations, and studied Ovid with those quick current-reading eyes.

"You knew Voss," she said. "Before the bar. Didn't you? You said you found him. But the way you talk about him — that's older."

Ovid polished his spectacles again. The curtain trick worked better on adults.

"Everyone has their thirty years, Miss Sefa," he said gently. "Mine are spoken for. Ask me again when we've raised the strongroom."

She narrowed her eyes but, mercifully, let it go — and it was only later, much later, that Ovid would remember the way she'd looked at him just then, and wonder whether she'd been thinking the exact same thing about him.

Above them, the wind changed. It came around to the northeast with a low moan through the rigging, and Sefa lifted her head like a dog scenting rain, and said, mostly to herself:

"That's early. That's way too early."

Ovid looked up from his tea. Outside, the first clouds of the monsoon were stacking on the horizon like unread files.
