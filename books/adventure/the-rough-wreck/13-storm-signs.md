## 13. Storm Signs

Sefa felt the change before anyone else did.

She was on the foredeck of the *Kestrel's Wake* at dawn, barefoot on planks still cold from the night, eating the last of the ship's biscuits and pretending they were food. The sea had a smell to it that morning — or rather, it had stopped smelling like itself. For six days the water over the reef had been clean and green-glassed, the kind of sea that let you see your own face in it if you leaned far enough out. Now there was something else under the salt: a green rot-smell, sweetish, like weeds torn up from somewhere deep and left in the sun.

The birds had gone quiet too. That was the part that made her stomach tighten. Gulls were rude creatures; they never shut up when there was a wreck to squabble over. This morning they sat hunched along the reef spit with their backs to the wind, silent as old men at a funeral.

Sefa set down her biscuit, closed her eyes, and listened to the water slap the hull.

Three beats, then a hitch. Three beats, then a hitch.

"Brannoc," she said, without opening her eyes. "Your engine's missing on the third stroke again."

From below came a clatter, a curse, and then Brannoc's head emerging from the engine hatch like a bad-tempered turtle. He had grease on his forehead, his chin, and — somehow — one ear. "It is not *missing*, it is *contemplating*."

"It's stuttering."

"It's developing character." But he was already wiping his hands and looking past her at the horizon, because Brannoc pretended to be lazy but he never actually was. "What's got you up at this hour? You sleep like a cat. I've seen Ovid try to move you off the chart table and get bitten."

"The swell's wrong," Sefa said. She pointed south, where the horizon had gone the color of wet slate. "Feel the boat. We're rolling long, but the wind's still coming from the east. Wind and swell don't agree, the swell wins. Swell always wins. That's coming from the monsoon trough."

Brannoc squinted south for a long moment. Then he said, quietly, all the humor gone out of him: "How early?"

"I don't know yet. Two days. Maybe three." She hugged her knees. "Maybe less. The birds know. Ask the birds."

"I'm not asking the birds. Last time I asked the birds about anything, Ovid wrote four pages about gull migration patterns and read them aloud at dinner."

"That was educational," said Ovid's voice from the companionway, followed by Ovid himself, clutching a mug of tea and a book he was almost certainly not reading. His hair did its usual morning impression of a startled octopus. "I heard my name being taken in vain. Sefa, you look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," she said. "Weather."

He followed her pointing finger, and she watched the color leave his face by degrees, like tea going cold. Ovid was many things — pedantic, absent-minded, prone to lecturing about colonial trade tariffs at moments of crisis — but he was not stupid, and he had grown up on these waters same as her. He knew what an early monsoon meant for a dive window measured in tides.

"I'll wake Tamsin," he said.

"She's already up," Sefa said. "She's been up since before me. She's on the stern rail watching the south. She knows too."

* * *

Tamsin called them together in the cabin an hour later, and Sefa watched her do the arithmetic on the back of Voss's chart, because Tamsin Montrose did her grieving and her planning with the same pencil.

"Six tides," Tamsin said. "That was the number. Six safe windows before the monsoon shuts us out and turns this whole reef into a washing machine. We've used two." Her pencil tapped the chart. "If the season holds true, we have four left. If it doesn't—"

"If it comes early," Ovid said, "we have fewer."

"If it comes early we have fewer," Tamsin agreed. "So we work every tide like it's the last one. No more careful pacing. Brannoc, I need that winch finished yesterday."

"The winch is a work of art and art takes time."

"Art can take time after we're rich or dead. Preferably rich."

Sefa raised her hand, the way she always did even though nobody had ever told her she needed to, and Tamsin nodded at her. "The swell's going to complicate the descent line," Sefa said. "Long-period swell from the south means the surface gets lazy but the surge down deep gets mean. It'll push and pull everything on the wreck twice as hard as the waves up top suggest. Whoever's working the strongroom wheel is going to be fighting the wreck breathing."

"The wreck *breathing*," Brannoc repeated. "I hate that phrase. I hate that you say it like it's normal."

"It is normal. Wrecks breathe. Water goes in through the hull openings on the surge and comes back out on the slack, and if you're inside when it exhales—" Sefa shrugged. "You go with it. Through whatever hole is smallest."

There was a silence in which everyone privately resolved never to think about that again.

"Then we schedule around the slack," Tamsin said. "Sefa calls the windows. If she says dive, we dive. If she says wait, we wait, and nobody argues, including me." She looked around the cabin, and Sefa saw her hesitate over the next words, weighing them. "And if the window shrinks again — if we lose tides — we may have to make choices about what we go home with."

Nobody said what everyone was thinking: that the choice between documents and gold was supposed to be a problem for a crew that survived long enough to have it.

Ovid cleared his throat. "There is also," he said delicately, "the matter of Captain Reyes."

"What about her?" Brannoc asked. "Besides the obvious, which is everything."

Ovid passed his spyglass across the table. "See for yourself. Southeast anchorage. They moved in during the night."

Sefa took the glass and found them almost immediately: three ships lying quiet behind the outer shoal, hulls dark against the dawn water. And beside them, low and ugly, a barge she hadn't seen before — flat-decked, crowded with machinery, a crane arm folded against its spine like a sleeping insect.

"Dredge," she said.

"A dredge," Ovid confirmed, with the grim satisfaction of a man who has done his reading. "A suction dredge, unless I miss my guess. They won't dive the wreck themselves — too slow, too risky, and Reyes doesn't have divers she trusts down there. So they'll dig. Pump the sand and silt off the reef shelf, undermine the hull, and let the Corvane come apart on its own. Everything breaks open. Everything floats up or scatters. Then their salvage crews pick the bones."

"They'd destroy the evidence," Brannoc said slowly. "They'd destroy the *wreck*. The governor's confession, the manifest, all of it — shredded into silt and scattered across a hundred miles of ocean. Nobody could ever prove anything."

"That," said Ovid, "is rather the point."

Sefa lowered the glass and looked at Tamsin. Tamsin was very still, the way she went still when she was angry — not the hot anger that shouted, but the cold kind that planned. Sefa had only known her a few weeks, but she'd learned that much. Stillness in Tamsin Montrose was the most dangerous weather of all.

"How fast can a dredge eat a wreck?" Tamsin asked.

Ovid spread his hands. "Days. Three, perhaps four, working around the clock. Less if the reef gives way early — and it will give way early, because the Corvane broke her back when she sank. She's been balanced on that shelf for sixty years like a plate on an edge. All they need to do is sweep away the crumbs holding her."

"Four days," Tamsin said. "Same as our tide count. It's a race now. A real one."

"And they have a dredge," Brannoc said, "and we have a winch that is still, technically, art."

* * *

The first thunder came that afternoon, far off to the south, a sound so low Sefa felt it in her teeth before she heard it in her ears. She was aloft on the jury-rigged mast platform — a perch Brannoc had built her out of salvage pipe and pure stubbornness, because he'd decided within a day of meeting her that a girl who could read currents deserved a proper place to read them from — and from up there she could see the whole board at once.

To the south: the storm line, no longer a suggestion but a fact, a bruise-colored wall sitting on the horizon with lightning walking inside it like something pacing a cage.

To the southeast: Reyes's fleet, and the dredge barge moving.

That was the thing that turned her blood cold. Not the storm. Storms were honest; storms announced themselves; storms gave you the courtesy of terror. But the dredge was moving *now*, in daylight, in water that was already starting to misbehave — and it was moving toward the reef channel nearest the Corvane, which meant Reyes wasn't waiting for fair conditions either. Which meant Reyes's clock had shrunk too. Which meant somebody above Reyes — somebody in a uniform with more brass than sense — had told her to make the wreck disappear before the weather made the decision for her.

Sefa slid down the shrouds and found Tamsin at the rail.

"Storm's maybe thirty hours out," she reported. "But that's not the problem." She handed over the glass and pointed. "Look at the dredge. She's positioning now. She'll start pumping on the next slack water — tonight."

Tamsin studied the barge for a long moment. "Tonight," she repeated. "While we're between tides. Clever. She digs while we sleep, and by tomorrow's first safe window half the reef shelf is soup."

"She's not even trying to hide it."

"Why would she? As far as the world knows, that's an empty stretch of reef and she's conducting a hydrographic survey." Tamsin's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely wasn't. "The navy's favorite kind of truth is the kind with paperwork attached."

Down the deck, Brannoc emerged from below wiping his hands, and Sefa saw that he'd heard. Everyone always heard things on the *Kestrel's Wake*; the boat was too small for privacy and too leaky for secrets, which Ovid claimed was why democracies should be built like old survey boats, though nobody ever understood what he meant by that.

"So what's the play?" Brannoc asked. "We can't stop a dredge. We've got a rusted survey boat, a homemade winch, and a crew of — forgive me — strays. They've got a naval squadron."

"We can't stop the dredge," Tamsin said. "But we might be able to feed it."

Sefa frowned. "Feed it?"

"Dredges are stupid machines. They pump what's in front of the intake. If the intake finds hard material — rock, dense wreckage, a few tons of chain — it chokes. Clogs. Shuts down until they clear it." Tamsin was looking at the reef now, reading it the way Sefa read currents, feature by feature. "And we happen to be sitting on a boat full of ballast scrap and dead weight we don't need."

"You want to sink junk in front of their intake," Brannoc said. "You want to *feed rocks to the navy*."

"I want to waste their hours. Every hour that dredge is clogged is an hour the Corvane holds together, and an hour closer to the next tide." Tamsin finally turned, and there it was — the cold anger, banked down to a coal, giving off light. "Reyes wants to bury the truth in silt. Fine. Let's bury her machinery in scrap and see which of us runs out of patience first."

There was a pause. Then Ovid, who had appeared with his tea and his book and his expression of a man attending a lecture he suspected would end badly, said mildly: "For the record, I believe this plan is reckless, probably illegal, and almost certainly doomed."

"Thank you, Ovid."

"You're welcome. I'll get the chain."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the building heat, stripping the *Kestrel's Wake* of everything that weighed her down and didn't keep her alive. Old anchor chain. Ballast pigs. A crate of engine parts so corroded Brannoc pronounced them "decorative." Two of the original survey drums, which nobody had ever figured out how to use, and which Ovid insisted on delivering a short eulogy over before they went over the side.

"We load it into the skiff," Tamsin directed, "run it in close on the flood, and drop it right on the shelf edge upstream of their intake line. Sefa picks the spot."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only one aboard who knows where the current will carry it. I know wrecks. You know water. Drop it wrong and it lands in forty fathoms and does nothing but annoy some fish."

Sefa felt the weight of that settle onto her shoulders — pleasant weight, mostly, the good kind of being needed, with a thin wire of fear running through the middle of it. Because getting close to that dredge meant getting close to Reyes's picket ships, and one of those ships was the *Vigilant*, the scout, the sleek dark-hulled vessel that had been haunting the edges of Sefa's vision since the port.

She hadn't told them. She still hadn't told them. Every day she didn't tell them, the telling got heavier, and every day the *Vigilant* sat on the horizon wearing its navy gray like a mask, Sefa felt the wire pull tighter.

*Later,* she told herself, the way she'd been telling herself for weeks. *After the strongroom. When it can't hurt the mission.*

But the storm was coming early, and she was starting to understand that things which came early didn't wait politely for later.

* * *

They ran the skiff out at dusk, riding the last of the flood tide, with the scrap stacked and lashed and the sky behind them going the color of a healing bruise. Sefa piloted. Tamsin rode in the bow like a figurehead with opinions. Neither of them spoke much; the water spoke enough for both of them.

"There." Sefa pointed to a patch of sea that looked identical to every other patch of sea, because to eyes it was. "Drop here. The ebb will drag it west along the shelf edge, right across their intake lane. By midnight they'll be pumping gravel and chain."

Tamsin cut the lashing. The scrap went down with a series of soft, final splashes, and the sea closed over it without comment, accepting the offering.

For a moment they just drifted, engines idling, and Sefa looked back at the fleet. The dredge barge had lights on now, work lights, harsh white circles bleeding into the darkening water. Figures moved on her deck. Somewhere over there, Captain Ilsa Reyes was watching the same storm line they were, doing the same arithmetic, and Sefa thought — with a small, cold clarity that surprised her — *she's scared too.* Not of the storm. Of what the storm might wash ashore if she failed.

"Tamsin," Sefa said, before she could stop herself. "What happens to us if we lose? If the wreck collapses and Reyes sails home and that's the end of it?"

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, the first real drops of the leading rain began to speckle the swells, fat and warm, like the sky clearing its throat.

"Then we're five people on a rotten boat with nothing to show for it," Tamsin said at last. "Same as we started." She turned, and in the failing light her face was hard to read, but her voice wasn't. "Except we'd know each other. That's not nothing, Sefa. I spent twenty years making sure I didn't know anybody. Turns out it's the only cargo worth carrying."

Sefa swallowed something that had risen unexpectedly in her throat, and put the skiff about, and headed for home.

Behind them, faint across the water, came a new sound — a deep mechanical groan, rhythmic and hungry, as the dredge began to pump. And beneath it, closer now, the thunder answered.

The clock was running. Four tides. Maybe fewer.

And Sefa still hadn't told them about the *Vigilant* — or about the name painted on her stern registry, the one Sefa had recognized in the port the first day, the one that connected her own family's ruin to the woman hunting them across the water.

Two more days, she promised herself. Two more tides, and then the truth.

If the storm left any of them time to tell it.
