## 14. Third Tide

The third tide came in grey and mean.

Tamsin felt it before she saw it — a change in the way the Kestrel's Wake rolled at anchor, a deeper, more reluctant sway, as though the sea itself had decided to make things difficult. She stood at the rail with her coffee going cold in her hands and watched the water over the Corvane's grave churn with a brownish silt that hadn't been there yesterday. Somewhere under all that murk lay the flooded lower decks, and somewhere in those decks, if Voss's chart could be believed, was the sealed compartment below the waterline.

And somewhere in those decks, if her own stubborn heart could be believed, was an answer to a question she'd been carrying for eleven years.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said, appearing at her elbow with the particular silent grace of someone who had grown up reading water instead of books. "The staring thing."

"I'm assessing conditions."

"You're staring at the wreck like it owes you money."

"It might," Tamsin said. "Everything out here does."

Sefa grinned, then sobered, squinting at the churn. "Current's wrong today. It's coming off the reef shelf instead of along it. Means it'll push you into the hull if you're not careful." She held up a hand before Tamsin could object. "I know you're going anyway. I'm not telling you not to go. I'm telling you where it'll try to kill you."

"That's the most useful thing anyone's said to me all week."

"That's because Brannoc only talks about winches."

* * *

Brannoc, in fact, was talking about winches. He had spread himself across the aft deck like a man who owned it, surrounded by a jury-rigged array of lift bags, spare line, and a compressor he'd coaxed back to life with what looked suspiciously like prayer and a bent spoon.

"Third dive in two days on those margins," he said without looking up when Tamsin came aft. "You know what your blood's doing down there? I know what your blood's doing down there. It's filing complaints."

"Noted."

"Noted, she says. Last time somebody noted my concerns, we lost a bilge pump and half my eyebrows." He finally looked up, and his gruffness softened around the edges, the way it always did when he was actually worried and trying to disguise it as engineering. "Bottom time's forty minutes, hard stop. I don't care if you've got your hand on the strongroom wheel itself. Forty minutes, and I'm reeling you up myself if I have to."

"Forty minutes," Tamsin agreed.

"And Ovid's topside with the deck plans. You get turned around down there, you follow his markings. He painted them himself. In glow paint. He was very proud."

From the wheelhouse, Ovid's voice floated out, wounded: "I heard that. The lines are *precisely* measured. I used a plumb bob."

"He used a plumb bob," Brannoc repeated solemnly. "So you know they're good."

Tamsin almost smiled. Almost. It was strange, she thought, how quickly these three had gotten under her skin — three weeks ago they'd been strangers she'd scraped out of a port gutter, and now they were standing around her at dawn worrying about her blood like it was communal property. She didn't know what to do with that. She'd never had anything that was communal property. Everything she'd ever owned had fit in one duffel bag, and she'd liked it that way, or told herself she had.

She checked her rig. Checked it again. Checked Sefa's current readings a third time, mostly to have something to do with her hands.

"Hey," Sefa said quietly, catching her wrist as she reached for her mask. Her grip was light but certain, a pilot's grip. "Whatever's down there — whatever you find — you come back up. That's the job today. Not the compartment. Coming back."

Tamsin looked at her. Sixteen years old, runaway, carried secrets like ballast, and still managed to say the true thing flat-out when everyone else was dancing around it.

"Coming back," Tamsin said.

She went over the side.

* * *

The water swallowed the grey morning and gave her green.

Below ten fathoms the light went from green to bruise, and by twenty she was running her lamp through a world of suspended sediment, visibility down to perhaps four meters, the Corvane materializing out of the murk in pieces — a rail here, a davit there, the great slumped shoulder of the hull rising out of the dark like something sleeping badly. Two days of diving had taught her the outer body of the ship, but today she wasn't going over the side into the cargo hold. Today she was going inside, down through the flooded lower decks, following Voss's penciled route toward the antechamber above the strongroom.

The entry point was a cargo hatch on the port side, warped half-open by the sinking. She slipped through sideways, lamp beam cutting a cone through dead water, and the ship closed around her.

Diving a wreck was never quiet, no matter how still the water. The Corvane creaked. That was the thing nobody warned you about — that drowned ships groaned, shifted, sighed, settling by millimeters year after year, and every sound came to you through the water with no direction to it, so that the whole structure seemed to be murmuring just behind your ear. Tamsin had dove wrecks for fifteen years and had made her peace with the voices. But this was the first time she'd ever been inside a wreck that meant something, and every groan sounded personal.

Voss's route took her down the main companionway. The stairwell was choked with silt and collapsed decking; she pulled herself along the handrail, finning carefully, disturbing as little as possible, because disturbed silt in an enclosed space was blindness, and blindness in a wreck was how divers became part of the wreck. Her gauges glowed steady. Thirty-eight minutes of bottom time. Plenty.

The second deck had been officers' country once. She could tell by the doors — brass fittings gone green, nameplates corroded illegible. A dining saloon opened off to starboard, its long table still bolted to the deck, chairs drifted against the far bulkhead like the whole room had flinched. A china cabinet lay on its face, and through the broken doors she could see plates still stacked in their racks, waiting for a meal that had never been served. One hundred and sixty years of waiting.

Her mother had come through here.

The thought arrived without permission, the way it always did. Eleven years ago, Mara Montrose had come down through some version of this darkness — maybe this very companionway, maybe this very saloon — with a torch and a chart and a crew Tamsin had never met, hunting the same sealed compartment. And she had found it. Tamsin knew that now with a certainty that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, because Voss's map only marked what Voss had seen, and Voss had seen the compartment's antechamber. Someone had reached it. Someone had touched the strongroom wheel.

And then the Meridian had lost its best diver, and Tamsin had lost her mother, and the sea had kept both facts to itself for a decade.

Focus. She shook it off and swam on.

The route dropped again — a maintenance ladder well, rungs furred with soft white growth — and then the third deck, where the flooding had done its worst. This deep in the hull the destruction was total: bulkheads buckled, passages crushed shut, the ship's bones bent by the violence of her going down. Voss's pencil line threaded a maze here, annotated in his cramped hand — *squeeze, mind the plate, DO NOT take the port passage, it goes nowhere.* Tamsin followed the line through a gap between a collapsed frame and a fallen pipe, a squeeze barely wide enough for her shoulders, and came out into a long corridor that the chart labeled, in letters that had faded almost to nothing: LOWER DECKS — CREW COUNTRY.

It was quieter here. Or it felt quieter. The silt lay thick and undisturbed, a grey snowfall over everything, and her lamp showed her a narrow lane of bunks, lockers, a mess table with a checkerboard still set mid-game, the pieces frozen in a position nobody would ever resolve. She hung in the middle of it, breathing hard through her regulator, listening to the ship murmur, and felt suddenly, horribly like an intruder. This had been people's home. Sailors had slept in these bunks, argued over that checkerboard, scratched their names into that locker door — and she could see the scratches now, names and dates and one crude drawing of a pig wearing a hat, preserved under a century of silt like a message in a bottle.

*Forty minutes,* Brannoc's voice said in her head. She checked her gauge. Twenty-two elapsed. Fine. She was fine.

The antechamber access was at the corridor's end, behind what Voss's map called the *strongroom trunk* — a vertical shaft housing the wheel mechanism that operated the compartment's seal from above. She found the hatch to the shaft space exactly where the map promised, and her heart began to beat faster, a stupid animal response she couldn't control, because this was it, this was the place, this was the room her mother had died in or near or for—

The hatch was jammed. Of course it was. She worked it with her knife tip, prying at the corrosion, taking care, taking time, and the whole while the ship talked to her, low and continuous, and she talked back to it in her head the way she always did: *Easy, old girl. Easy. I'm not here to take anything from you.*

Except that was a lie, and the ship probably knew it. She was here to take everything.

The hatch gave with a crack that echoed down the corridor, and she slipped through into the shaft space.

It was a small chamber, maybe three meters square, built around the great iron spindle of the strongroom wheel, which descended through the deck plates below her like the axle of the world. Silt had drifted in through a hundred small wounds in the hull. Her lamp swept the space once, twice —

— and caught on something wedged in the bulkhead seam at head height, half-buried in the grey snow.

Not structural. Not salvage. Too regular. Too deliberate.

She moved closer, and the silt slid off it as her wake stirred the water, and the lamp light found steel, and a handle, and a lanyard hole worn bright by years of a thumb rubbing it absent-mindedly during long surface intervals, and Tamsin stopped breathing.

She knew that knife.

Every diver's knife is individual the way handwriting is individual — the grind of the blade, the wear pattern, the repairs. She had grown up watching this one get sharpened on the rail of a hundred boats, watching it cut line and open oysters and, once, memorably, perform emergency surgery on a winch belt. She had learned to dive holding it, her mother's big hand wrapped around her small one, both of them gripping the handle together on her first descent in the shallows off Meridian harbor.

Mara Montrose's dive knife. Wedged in the bulkhead seam of the Corvane's strongroom trunk, driven in hard — driven in the way you drive a knife when you need a purchase point, when you're prying at something, when you mean to come back and retrieve it in a minute.

In a minute. Eleven years ago.

Tamsin's hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking with a kind of distant curiosity, as though they belonged to someone else, and then the rest of her caught up, and the shaking was everywhere, and her vision blurred behind the mask, and the regulator's rhythm — *inhale, exhale, inhale* — was suddenly the only thing keeping her assembled.

She'd known. She'd known since Voss's bar-side confession, known in the abstract, the way you know a storm is coming when the barometer falls. But knowing and finding were different animals. Finding was this: her mother's knife, right here, in her mother's last working position, left mid-task because Mara Montrose had fully intended to finish the job. Left the way a careful, methodical, infuriatingly competent woman leaves a tool — within reach, ready for the next step.

There was no next step. There had never been a next step. Something had happened in this little iron room, and her mother had gone into the water, and the sea had closed over it all, and Tamsin had spent eleven years telling herself a story about it — that it had been quick, that it had been an accident, that her mother wouldn't have suffered — and now the story was lying on the seabed in pieces, and the truth was a knife in a bulkhead, and she couldn't stop looking at it.

Her gauge said fourteen minutes remaining. It took her a moment to understand the number. Time had gone strange, thick, underwater-slow.

*Come back,* Sefa had said. *That's the job today.*

Okay. Okay. She wiped her eyes inside the mask — useless gesture, tears in saltwater, but she did it anyway — and made herself work. She worked the knife free of the seam with the same care she'd give any artifact, wrapping it in the mesh bag at her hip, and her fingers wanted to clutch it and she let them, just for a moment, just long enough to feel the handle fit her palm the way it had fit her mother's. Same hands. Everyone had always said it. Same hands, same stubborn chin, same refusal to leave a job unfinished.

"Well," she said aloud, and her voice came out as a burst of bubbles and nothing else, "here I am, Mum. Finishing it."

The ship groaned overhead, long and low, and Tamsin chose — deliberately, superstitiously — to hear it as an answer.

She turned for the exit, and that was when her lamp swept the base of the spindle and stopped.

Scratches. In the corrosion layer on the iron, low down, near the deck plates where the silt had only recently — geologically recently, decades recently — been disturbed. Letters. Cut deep, cut fast, cut by someone with a knife blade and very little time and an iron will to be understood.

She knelt in the silt and brushed it away with the edge of her glove, and the letters came clear, and the world went narrow and ringing:

**T.M. — IT WAS REYES**

Three words and a name. Her mother's initials. And beneath them, smaller, hurried, almost gouged: **TELL MY GIRL.**

The gauge said nine minutes. Tamsin stared at the scratch marks until they blurred, until the lamp halo wavered, until her own breath sounded like surf in her ears. Reyes. *Reyes.* Ilsa Reyes, who had taught her to read a depth gauge before she could read a book. Who had stood at her mother's memorial with dry eyes and a folded flag of protocol. Who had testified — testified, in that flat naval cadence — that Mara Montrose's final dive plan had been reckless, unauthorized, unsanctioned. Who was, at this very moment, anchored a quarter-mile east with a salvage fleet and a minister's orders, dredging toward this same wreck with the patience of a glacier.

*Tell my girl.*

"I'm here," Tamsin whispered to the water, to the iron, to the woman who had scratched her last message into a dying ship and gone out into the dark. "I'm here, Mum. I'm telling myself."

Seven minutes. She photographed the scratches twice with the wrist camera, committed them to memory a third time, and forced herself to turn away, finning back through the crew country, past the checkerboard and the pig in the hat, up through the squeeze, up the ladder well, out through the officers' country and the cargo hatch and into the open green.

The ascent was a blur she would later reconstruct in pieces: the decompression hang at five meters, counting seconds; the grey sky resolving overhead; the hull of the Kestrel's Wake swimming into view above her like a blessing. Hands grabbed her harness — Brannoc's hands, huge and gentle — and hauled her over the gunwale, and she came up the ladder on legs that didn't entirely work.

They were all there. Brannoc peeling her mask back, Sefa pressing a blanket around her shoulders, Ovid hovering with a thermos and the expression of a man rehearsing several speeches at once.

"Forty-one minutes," Brannoc growled. "Forty-*one*. I said forty."

"I found something," Tamsin said.

Her voice came out wrong — cracked, seawater-rough — and all three of them went still. She reached into the mesh bag with hands that had finally, mercifully, stopped shaking, and drew out the knife, and laid it on the deck between them, and watched them understand it one face at a time: Sefa first, sharp as always, her eyes going wide and then wet; Ovid removing his hat, which for Ovid was the equivalent of a keening; Brannoc staring at the blade and then at Tamsin with something terrible and tender in his weathered face.

"Mara's?" he said quietly. He'd never met her mother. He knew the story the way the whole crew knew it now, the way a crew knows anything — in fragments, over dinners, in the spaces between work.

"She reached the compartment," Tamsin said. "She was working the seal when—" She stopped. Started again. "And she left a message. Scratched into the spindle housing." She made herself say it plainly, because saying it plainly was the only way to keep it from eating her alive. "Two words. *It was Reyes.*"

The silence that followed lasted three full swells of the swell.

It was Sefa who broke it, in a voice very small and very steady: "Then she didn't drown diving. She was *put* in the water."

"No," Tamsin said. She picked up the knife and closed her fist around the handle her mother's hand had shaped, and looked east, toward the horizon, toward the masts and cranes of a fleet at anchor, toward the flagship flying the truce flag that had meant nothing for eleven years. "No. I don't think she was. And I think Ilsa Reyes has known it since the day it happened."

Ovid cleared his throat, and when he spoke, the archivist in him had taken over, precise and grim: "Then everything she told the inquiry was perjury. And everything she's doing here—" He stopped, because he didn't need to finish. They all finished it together, in the same heartbeat.

Everything she's doing here is finishing the job.

Above them, far to the east, a dredge barge coughed to life, and its engines carried across the water like a countdown.
