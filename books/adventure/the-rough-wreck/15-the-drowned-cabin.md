## 15. The Drowned Cabin

Tamsin told herself it wasn't disobedience. It was reconnaissance.

The distinction mattered to her, the way small distinctions always matter to people who are about to do something they've already decided is wrong. Brannoc had said — loudly, with diagrams, with a grease pencil squeaking against the chart table — that nobody went past the antechamber bulkhead until he'd rigged the winch and they'd run the wheel in relay teams with full surface support. Sefa had backed him up with current readings that made the lower decks sound like a washing machine with grudges. Ovid had backed up Sefa, which Tamsin suspected was less about hydrodynamics and more about the fact that Ovid backed up whoever had spoken most recently.

So the crew's position was unanimous. And Tamsin had nodded, and agreed, and meant it, right up until the moment she didn't.

* * *

The trouble was the knife.

She'd found it on the third tide, wedged in a bulkhead frame forty feet below the strongroom passage — a dive knife with a cracked bone handle and a lanyard she'd last seen tied to a wrist in a galley on the *Salt Meridian*, fifteen years and one lifetime ago. Her mother's knife. Proof that Mara Montrose had come this far, through this same black water, and had kept going.

Tamsin had surfaced with it and sat on the deck of the *Kestrel's Wake* holding it like something that might bite, and Brannoc had said, gently, "We'll get you there, lass. Properly. Four more tides."

Four more tides. And the monsoon had already moved its schedule up once without asking anyone's permission. And Reyes's dredge was chewing the reef a quarter-mile off, patient as a tax collector. Tamsin had looked at the knife, and at the water, and had done the arithmetic that people who live alone do automatically: *if not now, possibly never.*

So here she was, at slack before the fourth tide, kitting up on the dive step while the rest of the crew pretended to sleep.

"You're a terrible liar," Sefa said from the wheelhouse doorway, arms crossed, hair a wet black rope over one shoulder. She'd been on watch. Of course she'd been on watch. Sefa was always on watch; Tamsin had begun to suspect the girl simply didn't sleep, but instead stood somewhere in the dark listening to the ocean like a cat listening to a wall.

"I'm going down to check Brannoc's cut lines," Tamsin said. "Reconnaissance."

"Reconnaissance," Sefa repeated, in the flat tone of someone tasting a word and finding it off.

"Standard practice."

"You're wearing your deep kit. You've got the good torch. You've got the spare torch *in your other pocket*, which is a thing only people who plan to stay down a long time do." Sefa came down the steps and stood in front of her, and for a moment Tamsin saw her mother in the set of the girl's jaw — which was absurd, and unfair, and exactly as effective as if it had been true. "Brannoc will kill you. Then he'll fix the boat, then he'll kill you again."

"Then don't tell him."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. The sea breathed against the hull. Somewhere out in the dark, Reyes's dredge thumped and ground, a heartbeat that wasn't theirs.

"I'll read the current for you," Sefa said finally. "The slack holds for maybe fifty minutes, not sixty like the almanac says. The moment it turns, you come up, no arguments, no heroics. And—" she jabbed a finger at Tamsin's chest, "—you come up *yourself*. Not dragged. Not winched. Yourself. I'm not explaining to Brannoc why his captain is a limp rag."

"Deal."

"And Tamsin." Sefa's voice dropped, and all the sharpness went out of it, which was somehow worse. "Whatever you're looking for down there. It's been down there fifteen years. It can wait fifty minutes more, but it can't wait for you to die. Understood?"

Tamsin nodded, because her throat had closed up around anything more articulate than that.

She went over the side at 0340, into water the color of strong tea and old grief.

* * *

The descent was muscle memory and nothing else. Down the shot line, past the thermocline where the warmth peeled away like a coat being taken off her shoulders, into the green-black cathedral of the *Corvane*'s hull. Her torch beam swept the outer plating, the weed-softened gunports, the great scar where the reef had taken a bite out of her the night she went down — and Tamsin felt the old vertigo, the sense of standing in a doorway between the world of the living and the world of things the sea had decided to keep.

The passage to the lower decks was exactly as Brannoc had described it: a collapsed section of cargo hold, a crawl-through at the frame line, then the flooded corridor running aft toward the compartment Voss's chart marked with a single word — *strongroom* — and a little inked arrow, the handwriting cramped and shaking.

She'd been through it once already, on the third tide, when she'd found the knife. She knew the route the way you know a route through a house you grew up in, which is to say: her body knew it and her mind had the decency to stay quiet about it.

The corridor swallowed her torch beam. Fish scattered like thrown coins. Her own bubbles went up ahead of her in silver strings, and she watched them go with the envy of someone who could not follow.

*Fifty minutes,* Sefa had said. She checked her watch: nine gone.

The antechamber opened out of the corridor's end like a held breath. It was a small space — a checkpoint between the ship and whatever the governor had considered worth guarding, with a hatchway on the far wall and the great iron wheel of the strongroom door set into it, crusted with coral but unmistakable. Voss's chart had been right about everything so far. The wheel was here. The door was here.

And the door was still shut.

Tamsin hung in the middle of the antechamber and let her torch do the slow, careful sweep that Brannoc had drilled into her a hundred times — *look with your light, not with your hands* — and that was when the beam caught the corner of the room and her heart stopped like an engine hitting a rock.

A rig.

A full diving rig, old pattern, brass and canvas, the kind they'd stopped making before Tamsin was born. It sat against the bulkhead in a slumped, settled way, and for one merciful second she thought: *wreckage. Salvage. Part of the ship's stores.* And then the torch moved and showed her the rest, and the second ended.

Bones. Inside the rig. A skeleton in the seated posture of someone who had knelt at the wheel and simply — stayed. One arm extended, the bones of the hand resting against the strongroom wheel's spokes as if the fingers had been wrapped around them when everything else had gone. The other hand was drawn up near the chest, and Tamsin's torch found what it held: a brass cylinder, small, sealed, green with age but whole.

The water was very cold. Tamsin noticed this from a great distance, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

She should have turned around. Every rule she'd ever taught herself, every margin Brannoc had begged her to keep, every promise she'd made to a teenage girl on a dive step three hours ago — all of it said *turn around, surface, come back with the crew, do this properly.* This was exactly the kind of discovery that required witnesses and winches and a grown-up in the room.

Instead she swam closer.

* * *

The rig told the story the way rigs do, to anyone who knew how to read them, and Tamsin had been reading them since she was small enough to drown in a rain barrel. The helmet was an old free-flow pattern, but modified — the exhaust valve had been reworked with a shim, a hack, the kind of improvisation that kept you alive when your equipment was wrong and your money was gone. Her mother had done that. Tamsin knew the hand. She'd watched those hands rework a regulator on the *Salt Meridian*'s galley table with a butter knife and total contempt for the manufacturer.

The lanyard at the wrist was gone — no, not gone. Cut. There were fresh-looking nicks in the old canvas cuff, deliberate, the kind of cuts you make when you're trapped and need to get free of something. Her mother had cut herself loose. Had fought. Had gotten this far, to this room, to this wheel.

And then had stayed.

Tamsin hung there in the green dark, six inches from her mother's bones, and the fifteen years between them collapsed to nothing. She was nine years old again, waiting on a dock. She was nineteen, at the inquiry, listening to Ilsa Reyes say *the sea takes the careless* in a voice like a closing ledger. She was thirty-one, holding a cracked-handled knife in a wheelhouse, doing arithmetic about tides.

*You got here,* she thought. *You actually got here. You found it, you reached the wheel, and you died with your hand on the door of the truth.*

The anger came first — it always came first, anger was the fastest thing she had — anger at her mother for coming out here alone, for diving a wreck like this on old gear with no support, for leaving a daughter on a dock. Then the anger turned, the way it always did, and became something worse: understanding. Because Tamsin was here too, wasn't she? Alone, against orders, on borrowed minutes, at the door of the same truth. She was her mother's daughter down to the bone, and the bone knew it.

Her watch said twenty-two minutes of slack left.

She made herself do the work. That was the discipline, the only discipline she'd ever trusted: when the feeling gets too big, make your hands busy. She checked the wheel — seized, but the spokes were intact, and Brannoc's winch could get purchase on it if they rigged a bridle. She checked the door frame for the seal condition Voss's notes had described. She photographed everything with the little camera strapped to her wrist, flash after flash, the skeleton lit up white and then falling back into the dark, lit and dark, lit and dark, like a memory being taken out and put away.

Then she looked at the brass cylinder in the bone hand.

It came free with a gentleness that undid her more than force would have. The fingers didn't resist; fifteen years of current had done the resisting already. Tamsin held the cylinder in her palm — small, cold, lighter than anything in this whole drowned ship had any right to be — and felt the weight of it anyway. Whatever was in there, her mother had carried it out of the strongroom, or tried to. Had held onto it while the water came in. Had held onto it past the point where holding onto anything made sense.

*Some things you keep because they're true,* Voss had said, on the last night of his life, coughing seawater into a rag. *Everything else you keep because you're afraid.*

Tamsin tucked the cylinder into the pouch on her harness, cinched it down, and patted it once, the way you'd pat a pocket with a sleeping animal in it.

She was turning to go — she had the photos, she had the cylinder, she had eleven minutes and a promise to a girl on a dive step — when her torch swept the bulkhead beside the wheel one last time and stopped.

Scratches. In the coral crust over the steel, low down, at the height of a kneeling person's hand. Not random. Deliberate. Letters, cut deep, by someone with a knife and very little time and, she suspected, very little air.

She scraped the coral away with her mother's own knife, the one hanging now on her own harness, and read:

**TAM — GO HOME.**

Two words. Fourteen years of silence, and her mother had spent her last minutes of light and air scratching two words into a bulkhead at the bottom of the sea, and the words weren't *I found it* or *I was right* or *avenge me.*

They were *go home.*

Tamsin laughed. It came out as a burst of bubbles, a ragged silver string rising through the antechamber, and if anyone could have heard her through the water they'd have thought she was sobbing, and they'd have been right, and they'd also have been wrong. It was both. It was the whole ridiculous, unbearable joke of it: her mother had died reaching for the truth, and her last message was telling her daughter not to follow.

*Sorry, Mum,* Tamsin said into the water, knowing the water would keep it the way it kept everything. *You married a Montrose. We've never once gone home when told.*

She took one photograph of the scratches — for Ovid, who would want the documentation, and for herself, who would want the wound — and then she did the hardest thing she'd done all day, harder than the dive, harder than the bones: she turned her back on the skeleton in her mother's rig, and she swam out.

* * *

She broke the surface at 0431, fifty-one minutes after going down, one minute past Sefa's deadline, which she knew because Sefa was already in the water up to her waist off the dive step with a rescue line coiled in her fist and an expression that could have stripped barnacles.

"You said fifty," Sefa said.

"Current held."

"You said fifty *minutes*."

"Current held for fifty-one." Tamsin got a hand on the ladder and found, to her mild surprise, that her arms were shaking. "It was very cooperative."

Sefa hauled the line in, muttering in at least two languages, one of which Tamsin was fairly sure she'd invented. Then, as Tamsin came up the ladder, helmet off, hair plastered to her skull, Sefa stopped muttering and went very still.

"Tamsin. Your face."

"What about my face?"

"It's—" Sefa struggled. "You look like you found something. And like it found you back."

Tamsin sat down heavily on the dive step. The eastern sky was going the color of a bruise healing, and out on the horizon Reyes's lights were still burning, patient and organized and wrong. Inside her harness pouch, the brass cylinder knocked softly against her ribs with each breath, like a small heart.

"Is Brannoc up?" she asked.

"Brannoc is *asleep*, which is the only reason you're not already dead. Ovid's up. Ovid's been up all night reading about hull corrosion, which he does for fun, which is its own kind of cry for help." Sefa crouched in front of her, searching her face. "What did you find down there?"

Tamsin looked at her — at this fierce, half-feral kid who'd pulled her out of a riptide and kept her secrets and stood watch over her lies — and made a decision that would have been unthinkable a month ago. She'd spent her whole life telling people nothing. It turned out the sea was right about one thing: everything you keep, you keep because you're afraid.

"I found my mother," she said.

Sefa didn't gasp or clutch or do any of the things people did in stories. She just sat down on the step beside Tamsin, shoulder to shoulder, and watched the sky get lighter, and after a while she said, "Was she where you left her?"

"Fifteen years in the same spot." Tamsin worked the brass cylinder out of the pouch and held it up between them, green-crusted, sealed, impossibly light. "But she was holding this."

Sefa looked at it the way she looked at currents — reading it, weighing it, finding the pull in it. "What is it?"

"I don't know." Tamsin turned it over in her palm. "But Voss's chart says the strongroom holds the documents. And my mother died with her hand on the wheel of that door and this in her fist. Which means—" She stopped, because the thought arrived all at once, whole and cold, the way the bad ones always did.

"Means what?"

"Means she got inside." Tamsin heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for bad weather. "The wheel turns, Sefa. It turned fifteen years ago. She opened that door, she took something out — and then she never surfaced. And the door is *shut again.* Somebody shut it after her."

The eastern sky split with the first grey light. Out on the water, faint through the dawn hush, came the sound of Reyes's dredge starting up its morning's work — closer, Tamsin thought, than it had been yesterday.

Sefa stood, and pulled her up, and said the thing that Tamsin would carry into the next hour like a stone in her boot:

"Then somebody else knows how to open it. And I don't think it was us."
