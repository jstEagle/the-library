## 16. Mutiny Weather

Brannoc Vail had been thrown out of better engine rooms than this one, and he had learned long ago that the trick to surviving a bad situation was to keep your hands busy and your opinions in a locked box. For three days aboard the *Kestrel's Wake* he had kept both rules. He'd rebuilt the bilge pump twice, re-caulked the port scupper, and said nothing at all about the fact that their captain was diving herself to death.

On the fourth morning, he opened the box.

It started, as most disasters do, with arithmetic. Brannoc was an engineer, and engineers are people who believe the universe can be talked down if you show it your figures. He sat in the engine room at dawn with a grease pencil and a scrap of hull plating, and he did the sums on Tamsin Montrose's last dive.

Depth to the antechamber: forty-one meters. Bottom time on her last run: nineteen minutes. Decompression required for that profile: eleven minutes at six meters, minimum, and that was assuming she came up slow, which he knew from the way she'd surfaced — white-lipped, gasping, hauling herself over the gunwale like something the sea had spat back out — that she had not. Add the cold at depth, add the silt, add the fact that she'd been down there alone, tethered to nothing but a guideline she'd rigged herself, and the numbers stopped being numbers. They became a eulogy with decimals.

He wrote it all out anyway. Neat columns. He even underlined the total twice, because if you were going to start a mutiny, you might as well be legible about it.

Then he went up on deck, where Tamsin was checking her rig for the fifth tide, and he laid the scrap of plating on the hatch cover between them like a man laying down a card he couldn't take back.

"No," Brannoc said.

Tamsin looked at the plate, then at him. She had salt dried white in her hairline and shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose things in, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked less like a captain than like a woman who hadn't slept since she'd seen what was waiting for her at the bottom of the Corvane.

"Excuse me?" she said.

"You heard me." Brannoc tapped the plate. "Those are your dive profiles. Last four runs. I've been keeping track." Which was true, though he neglected to mention he'd also written her name and the words *bloody idiot* next to two of them. "You're running past every margin we've got. No decompression stops worth the name, no standby diver, no chamber within a hundred miles. You go down again on those numbers, Captain, and you're not coming up. Not alive, anyway. I've done the math."

"Then do it again," Tamsin said, "because I'm going down."

The deck of the *Kestrel's Wake* was not large. It took perhaps four strides to cross, and all five of them were standing on it, which meant that by the time Brannoc said "No" a second time, they had an audience.

Sefa was coiling line by the rail and had gone very still, the way she did when she was reading water or reading people — Brannoc had never worked out which, and suspected it was both. Ovid stood in the companionway with a folio of deck plans clutched to his chest like a breastplate. And Fen, their deckhand of nine days' tenure and questionable provenance, paused halfway through scrubbing the deck with an expression of pure delight, because Fen would have paid money for this.

"Tamsin," Brannoc said, and made himself use her name instead of her title, because titles were how this had gotten out of hand. "You found what you were looking for down there. You found—" He stopped. Everyone on the boat knew what she'd found. A skeleton in her mother's rig, one hand still on the strongroom wheel. Nobody said it out loud. It sat in the middle of the deck like ballast nobody could shift. "You found her. The documents are behind one more door. One door, and it needs a winch and a relay team, not a suicide run. We do it properly or we don't do it."

"We don't have time to do it properly." Her voice cracked on *properly*, and he saw her hate that she'd let it. "The window's shrinking. Reyes is dredging the reef a quarter mile off. Every tide we wait, that wreck gets closer to being rubble, and everything in it — everything my mother died for — goes down with it. So no, Brannoc. We don't do it properly. We do it now."

"And when you convulse at thirty feet?" Brannoc heard his own voice rising and didn't care. "When you come up frothing and your heart stops on my deck? Who raises the door then? Sefa? Ovid? Ovid can't swim!"

"I can swim," Ovid said with dignity, "in principle."

"It's not the same thing!"

"It's rarely the same thing," Ovid agreed, "with anything."

"Fen," Brannoc said desperately, "back me up."

Fen considered this, scrub brush dripping. "I mostly back whoever's winning," Fen said apologetically. "It's a policy."

* * *

Here is what Brannoc would not say, then or ever, out loud: that he had watched a captain kill herself before. That he had served eleven years on the survey vessel *Halcyon Sound* under a master who treated depth ratings as suggestions and crew as consumables, and that Brannoc had kept his head down and his hands busy and his opinions boxed until the day the *Sound*'s number three compartment flooded with four men inside it, and the inquiry asked him — polite, tired men with clipboards — why he hadn't spoken sooner.

*I assumed someone else would,* he had told them. It was the truest sentence of his life and the one he was least proud of.

So no. He was not going to stand on another deck and assume someone else would speak.

"I'm telling you as your engineer," he said now, quieter, which somehow made it worse, "that boat—this boat—cannot afford to lose you. And I'm telling you as—" He faltered. They had known each other nine days. Nine days, one refit, one riptide rescue, one night of bailing in a squall shoulder to shoulder while Sefa sang something obscene about a customs officer. It should not have been enough time to feel what he felt. It was. "As your friend. Don't make me do the math on your funeral too. I've got enough figures."

Something moved across Tamsin's face — quickly, gone before he could name it. Then it hardened into the other thing, the thing he liked far less.

"That's an order, Mr. Vail," she said. "Stand down. Prep the rig."

And that — that word, *order*, dropped onto the deck of a boat where nine days ago they'd all voted on dinner — was the mistake. Brannoc watched it land. Watched Sefa's coil of line stop moving. Watched Ovid's eyebrows climb toward his hairline with scholarly interest, a man witnessing a historical event he'd only read about.

Because here was the trouble with Tamsin Montrose asserting command for the first time in her life: she did it exactly the way a person does who has spent twenty years refusing to be commanded by anyone. Badly. Loudly. With her chin up and her fists half-curled, like a woman daring the ocean itself to give her orders back.

"No," Brannoc said for the third time, and now it wasn't engineering anymore. Now it was mutiny, plain and simple, and some traitorous part of him noted with interest that it felt considerably better than expected.

* * *

They fought for an hour.

Not physically — although Fen later swore to anyone who'd listen that Brannoc had squared up, which was a slander Brannoc chose not to dignify, mostly because he couldn't entirely remember whether he had. They fought with words, in the wheelhouse first and then on deck, and the crew split along fault lines nobody had mapped.

Ovid sided with survival, in the manner of a man siding with survival: eloquently, at length, citing three maritime disasters and one Icelandic saga. "The saga of Grettir," he explained, holding up a finger, "contains a passage most apposite—"

"Not now, Ovid."

"—in which the hero insists upon a task beyond his strength, and the poet's judgment is unambiguous—"

"*Not now.*"

Sefa, to everyone's surprise including her own, sided with Tamsin. She did it without raising her voice, which in Sefa was more alarming than shouting. "I've read that current," she said. "The one through the lower decks. It's mean, but it's readable. If she goes at slack water, with a line, with me calling the intervals—" She caught Brannoc's look and lifted her chin. "I know what the margins are, Brannoc. I grew up on margins. Margins are where my family lived until the navy took even those away. Sometimes the safe thing and the right thing aren't the same thing, and you have to pick."

"The safe thing keeps us alive to pick again tomorrow," Brannoc shot back.

"The tomorrow isn't guaranteed either!" Sefa's voice finally cracked upward. "Reyes's dredge is eating that reef! You think she'll leave us a wreck to dive? You think she'll leave us a *boat*?"

That landed harder than Brannoc wanted to admit. He looked at Tamsin, expecting triumph, and found instead something worse: she wasn't triumphant at all. She looked like a woman holding a rope that was pulling her apart, one end toward a grave on the seafloor and the other toward a crew she could not bear to bury.

"I'm not asking you to agree," Tamsin said, and her voice had gone low and rough. "I'm telling you how it is. I gave Voss a promise. I gave *her*—" the pronoun snagged, and everyone heard what she didn't say, *my mother, her bones, her hand on the wheel* — "a promise. I'm finishing this. Anybody who wants off, the Wake puts in at Halfway Rock tonight and you can walk. No hard feelings. I'll even write you references. Ovid, yours will be glowing."

"My references," Ovid murmured, "are the reason I needed a boat in the first place."

"Anybody?" Tamsin said again, and here was the ugly birth of it, the thing Brannoc would remember years later with a wince and, eventually, an odd tenderness: she asked the question like a challenge, not like a captain. Like a woman so used to leaving that she'd turned departure into a weapon. *Go on, then. Everybody always does.*

Nobody moved.

Fen kept scrubbing. Sefa re-coiled a line that did not need it. Ovid adjusted his spectacles and observed, to the horizon, "In the Icelandic sagas, the crew typically stays. It's the heroes who get everyone killed, and the poets who survive to complain about it."

Brannoc stood at the rail with his grease pencil in his pocket and his figures in his fist, and understood, with the particular misery of the engineer, that he had lost the argument and won the point, and that neither of those facts changed the numbers one decimal.

"Slack water's at fourteen hundred," Sefa said quietly, into the silence. "I'll call your intervals."

"Sefa—"

"You said relay team," Sefa said to Tamsin, ignoring him entirely. "You said line. Fine. But you don't go alone, and you don't go past eighteen minutes, and if I call you up, you *come up*. Those are my terms. I'm not crew if I get to watch you die politely."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Then at Brannoc. And Brannoc, who had built engines out of scrap and hope, recognized the expression on her face because he wore it himself most days: the look of a person accepting terms they hated because the alternative was worse.

"Eighteen minutes," Tamsin said. "Relay. Line. Your terms, all of them." She held out her hand to Sefa, and Sefa shook it, and that was that — command asserted, command immediately negotiated down, which Brannoc supposed was how command worked on any boat worth sailing.

"And me?" he asked.

Tamsin turned to him fully. Up close, in the flat grey light before the fifth tide, he could see how much the last three days had cost her, and how hard she was working to spend it invisibly.

"You," she said, "will build me that winch. Out of salvage, out of stubbornness, out of whatever you've got. Because if we're doing this, we're doing it with machinery, not heroics, and you're the only one aboard who knows the difference." A pause. The ghost of something that was almost humor crossed her face. "And Brannoc — thank you. For the math. Keep doing the math. Somebody on this boat has to."

"Somebody," Brannoc grumbled, already turning toward the engine room, because if he stayed on deck one more minute he was going to say something unforgivably warm, "has to. God help this crew, apparently it's me."

* * *

He was elbow-deep in the winch housing by mid-morning — a beautiful ugly thing, salvaged drum, borrowed brake band, a gear ratio that would have made his old instructors weep into their slide rules — when Fen appeared in the engine room doorway, scrubbing rag over one shoulder, wearing the expression of a person delivering a telegram they'd already read.

"Skipper wants you," Fen said. "Oh — and Ovid says to tell you the dredge changed course."

Brannoc's hands went still inside the housing. "Changed course how?"

"Towards us," Fen said cheerfully. "Or towards the reef. Hard to tell. Ovid's up top with the glass having some kind of crisis about it. Says the dredge isn't dredging anymore. Says it's *positioning*."

Brannoc wiped his hands slowly on his rag, and thought about figures. About a quarter-mile of reef, a wreck full of documents somebody powerful wanted dead, a fleet at anchor, and a dredge that had stopped digging and started waiting.

Outside, the light had begun to change. The wind had backed a point or two, and the swell had picked up that long, organized rhythm that Sefa called *storm weather learning its lines*.

The monsoon was coming early. And somewhere out there, so was Reyes.

"Well," Brannoc said to the winch, to the engine room, to the whole rusting, stubborn, impossible boat around him. "Better finish this, then."

He picked up his wrench.
