## 17. Reyes's Gambit

The flagship *Vigilant* had a wardroom table long enough to serve forty officers, and Captain Ilsa Reyes had spent twenty-two years learning every way that table could be used against her.

Today it was being used by a man named Undersecretary Corvel, who sat at the far end of it as though the distance were a formality he'd earned rather than an accident of seating, and who was eating her ship's biscuits with the delicate disgust of a man who believed the sea owed him better bread.

"You'll forgive me for coming out in person," Corvel said, brushing crumbs from his dove-grey coat. "Minister Halloway felt the matter required... discretion."

"Discretion," Reyes repeated. She poured coffee for both of them from the battered silver pot that had survived three commands and one fire. It was the only honest thing on this ship. "That's a word we use differently, you and I."

"Do we?" Corvel smiled. He had a good smile. Reyes had spent enough years around politicians to know a good smile was like a fair-weather sky — pleasant right up until it wasn't. "I should think we use it identically. It means *don't write anything down*."

Reyes drank her coffee and said nothing, which was itself a kind of writing-down. Corvel's smile thinned by a degree. Good. Let him do the arithmetic of who on this table could outlast whom.

"The Corvane," he said, "is a rumor."

"It's a wreck. I've seen the survey soundings myself."

"It's a *rumor*, Captain, wearing the costume of a wreck. Rumors are best handled before they learn to walk." He produced a leather folder — navy issue, unmarked — and slid it down the length of the table with two fingers, the way a card sharp deals a hand he already knows. "You'll want to read this alone. But I'll summarize, since you strike me as a woman who prefers summaries. The manifest your archivist friend has been so industriously copying does not exist. Officially. The treasury of the Meridian families was lawfully transferred to colonial administration following the insurrection. Anything suggesting otherwise is sedition dressed as history."

"Anything suggesting otherwise," Reyes said slowly, "is sitting under four hundred feet of water with a governor's confession sealed inside it."

"Which is why the sea is such a convenient filing cabinet." Corvel spread his hands, all reasonableness. "Files get lost at sea all the time. Ships burn. Hulls fail. Monsoons are terribly thorough. All the Minister requires is that you be holding the pen when the paperwork writes itself."

Reyes set down her cup. The click of porcelain on oak was very loud in the empty wardroom.

"You're asking me to demolish a wreck."

"I'm asking you to *manage* a hazard. There's a difference, and the difference is your pension." Corvel leaned forward, and for one moment the smile dropped away entirely, and Reyes saw the thing underneath, which was not stupidity — she'd have preferred stupidity; stupidity could be managed. This was conviction. The worst kind: the tidy kind. "Captain Reyes. Thirty years ago a handful of families were ruined because someone had to pay for the insurrection, and they were chosen because they were rich enough to be worth ruining and too few to fight back. That decision kept the colonies stable for a generation. Stable means no civil war. No civil war means your sailors come home. You may call that theft. I call it load-bearing masonry. You don't pull out a stone just because it turns out to be ugly."

"And if the stone is sitting on top of people?"

Corvel considered her with something almost like respect. "Then you build over them quietly, and you build well, and in fifty years nobody remembers there was ever anyone underneath. That, Captain, is what government *is*. Now." He stood, buttoned his coat. "The monsoon gives you perhaps five working tides. I've taken the liberty of having demolition stores transferred to your tender this morning. Charges, timers, the usual. If any part of the Corvane above the silt line still exists when the storm passes — if so much as a hull plate washes up on a beach with a serial number on it — then the woman who failed to make it disappear will be explaining herself to a board of inquiry, and boards of inquiry are where careers go to be quietly drowned. Whereas—" he paused at the wardroom door, and gave her back the smile, its fair-weather best, "—whereas the captain who solves the problem will find the Minister remembers his friends. Flag rank, Captain. Your own command. A shore posting, even, if the sea has lost its charm."

It hadn't. That was the whole trouble. It never had.

"Good day, Captain," said Corvel, and left her alone with forty chairs, one cold cup of coffee, and a folder she found she did not want to open, and opened anyway.

* * *

She read it standing at the stern rail that evening, because there were some documents you shouldn't read sitting down.

It was everything Ovid Marsh would have given his ink-stained eyeteeth to see: the original transfer orders, the governor's private correspondence, the itemized looting of the Meridian treasuries written out in a clerk's beautiful copperplate, sums and names and dates, thirty years of theft with a ribbon tied around it. And at the bottom, in the Minister's own hand, a single instruction that had nothing to do with any of it:

*Ensure the diver Montrose is not permitted to surface with materials.*

Tamsin Montrose.

Reyes folded the folder closed and held it against the rail and watched the water go by underneath, black-green and patient, and let the name sit in her chest the way a swallowed fishhook sits.

There were perhaps six people alive who knew that Ilsa Reyes had once been called Ilse by a laughing woman with salt-bright hair who dove wrecks off Kestrel Point like she'd been born with gills. Mara Montrose had called her that exactly twice, both times underwater, both times through a regulator, which meant the name had arrived in bubbles, which was somehow the only way it had ever sounded right.

Mara Montrose had been the finest diver Reyes ever knew, including herself, including everyone, and Reyes had testified against her in a courtroom that smelled of varnish and lies, and Mara had gone to prison for salvage fraud she had not committed, and died there of a fever the prison doctor had treated with indifference and prayer, and Reyes had received the letter a month late and read it standing at this exact rail on a different ship, and had not cried, because captains don't, and had instead been promoted within the year, because the service rewards precisely the thing it breaks in you.

And now here was the daughter. Diving the same wreck. Chasing the same strongroom. With Voss's chart, if the port gossip was half true, and port gossip usually was.

*Ilsie,* Mara had said once, hanging off the Corvane's anchor chain at sixty feet, hair streaming like weed, *if they ever send you to bury this place, promise me you'll at least feel bad about it.*

Reyes had laughed. Sixty feet down, through a regulator, laughing so hard her mask fogged. *I don't bury things, Mara. I raise them. It's the whole point of me.*

* * *

The demolition chief was a broad, cheerful man named Tuppan who loved explosives the way other men loved dogs — unreservedly, and with a complete absence of judgment about what they were used for. He laid out his kit on the tender's deck for her inspection with the pride of a gardener showing seedlings.

"Shaped charges, Captain, twelve of 'em, plus the seismic pots for the reef face. Placed proper, the whole outer section comes down in on itself and the silt does the rest inside a season. Come spring, sonar won't find a shape down there so much as a suggestion." He beamed. "Cleanest job I've rigged since the harbor clearance at Port Anselm. You remember that? Whole pier went down like a lady taking a curtsy."

"I remember," Reyes said. "Twelve charges."

"Twelve, Captain. Redundancy built in. Any six do the work; twelve makes certain."

*Makes certain.* Everything in the navy came down to that phrase eventually. Make certain. Certainty was the commodity Corvel traded in, and Reyes had spent thirty years supplying it, and the supply had never once run short, and she had told herself for thirty years that this was competence rather than complicity, and the telling had gotten easier every year, which was how you knew it was a lie.

"Tuppan," she said. "If I gave you eleven charges and told you to sink the twelfth overboard, could you account for it?"

His cheerfulness flickered — not at the ethics, she noted; at the paperwork. "Be a discrepancy in the stores ledger, Captain. Has to be signed for."

"Yes," Reyes said. "It always does." She looked at the crates, stencilled and neat, each one a small sealed argument that the past was negotiable. "Rig all twelve. We'll discuss placement after the next survey pass."

"Aye, Captain." He hesitated, and then, because Tuppan was fundamentally incapable of leaving a technical question unasked: "Only — placement matters, see. If you want it buried, we drop the reef face on it. If you want it *gone* — shattered, unrecoverable, nothing for a future diver with a hard hat and a grudge — that's a different pattern. More charges inside the hull. Closer to whatever it is you're making certain of." His eyes were mild and incurious. It was the kindest thing about him. "Which is it?"

Reyes looked south, toward the low grey smudge on the horizon where the reef lay and the Corvane lay inside the reef and a strongroom lay inside the Corvane, and somewhere in the deep water between here and there, a girl with her mother's stubbornness was running out of tides.

Inside the hull, then. Close. Nothing for a future diver.

"Both patterns," she heard herself say. "Prepare both. Placement after survey."

Because she was not ready. That was the truth of it, the small shabby human truth under the braid and the braid under the coat. Thirty years of certainty, and she could not yet bring herself to fix the placement, because fixing the placement made it real, and once it was real there was only one order left to give, and the last person who had ever asked her to feel bad about an order was dead, and the daughter was diving toward the same water, and Ilsa Reyes — decorated, promoted, certain — stood on her own tender unable to sign a piece of paper.

"Captain?" Tuppan said.

"Carry on," she said, and went back to the *Vigilant*, and did not look at the crates again.

* * *

Her first officer found her at dusk on the bridge wing, where she'd been standing long enough that the gulls had begun to regard her as furniture.

"Scout report, Captain." Lieutenant Aswan was young, competent, and possessed of the fatal honesty of junior officers everywhere, which was that he reported facts without yet knowing which facts wanted softening. "The survey boat — the one the divers are using, the *Kestrel's Wake* — she's moved to the eastern anchorage inside the reef maze. And there's more traffic than expected. A second vessel came alongside her yesterday. Small, fast, unregistered."

"Smuggler resupplying them, most likely."

"Perhaps. But the scout got a look at the crew through the glass. Young woman at the helm, dark hair, maybe sixteen. Aswan paused. "The description matches the girl from the port incident, Captain. The one the liaison flagged. Granddaughter of the Aldemar family."

The wind came around the headland and pushed at them both, indifferent as arithmetic.

Aldemar. Reyes turned the name over. It was in the folder. Page nine, copperplate clerk's hand: *House Aldemar, treasury seized in full, principal parties dispersed.* Dispersed meaning scattered, meaning ruined, meaning a family tree chopped off at the trunk — except trees are notorious for sprouting, and here was a sprout, sixteen years old, helming a dive boat straight at the navy's best-kept grave.

"They're not treasure hunters," Aswan offered carefully. "Are they. Not really."

"No," Reyes said. "They're not."

Treasure hunters could be bought. Treasure hunters could be outrun, outbid, out-waited. But a crew that thought it was retrieving justice — that was a different animal entirely. That animal didn't want the gold. It wanted the truth, and truth was the one cargo in the world that multiplied when you tried to sink it.

"Captain," Aswan said, "the demolition schedule. Tuppan's asking for final placement authority. Low tide tomorrow puts us inside the window."

Tomorrow. One tide sooner than Corvel's arithmetic allowed, because the monsoon was shifting early, because the sea did not care about ministers or mentors or promises made in bubbles at sixty feet.

Reyes took out the placement order — she'd drafted it three times that afternoon and burned none of them, which was unlike her; drafts were for indecision and she prided herself on never keeping any — and signed it at the rail, using the chart case as a desk, her handwriting as steady as it had been at every court-martial, every funeral, every promotion.

*All twelve charges. Interior pattern. Execute at next low tide.*

"There," she said, and handed it to Aswan, and watched him carry her signature away across the deck, and felt the particular lightness that follows a terrible decision, which is not relief but merely the absence of weight, the way a healed bone feels lighter than the one that broke.

Below, far off, at the edge of the darkening water, a single lamp burned on a small boat anchored inside the reef. Someone on it was probably laughing. Crews did that, early on, before they understood what they were sailing toward. She remembered laughing on a survey boat once, a lifetime ago, with salt-bright hair blowing into her face and a regulator in her hand and the whole ocean believing in her.

*Promise me you'll at least feel bad about it.*

"I do," Reyes said quietly, to no one, to the gulls, to the lamp on the little boat. "For what it's worth, Mara. I do."

The lamp winked once behind a swell and vanished.

At dawn, the charges would go down. And unless a stubborn girl and her strays did something reckless in the next twelve hours — and every instinct Reyes owned, honed on thirty years of watching Montroses, said they would — the Corvane would keep its secrets until the sea itself forgot them.

She went below to write the signal to Corvel, and found, annoyingly, that her hand was not quite steady, and blamed the coffee.
