## 18. Sefa's Secret

The storm had been sitting on the horizon all day like a guest who wouldn't take the hint, and Sefa had been watching it the way you watch a debt collector cross the street toward your door.

She was up in the bow of the Kestrel's Wake with her feet hooked under the rail and her chin on her knees, reading the water the way other people read letters from home. The swell had come around two points since dawn. The birds had gone quiet over the eastern reef. And the wind had that taste in it — not salt, exactly, but the memory of salt, the taste the sea got right before it decided to take something back.

Four tides. Maybe three and a half, if the monsoon was in a hurry, and monsoons were always in a hurry. That was the thing nobody understood about them until they'd lived through one: they weren't weather, they were a personality. A big, impatient, destructive personality that showed up at your house uninvited and rearranged the furniture, the roof, and occasionally the coastline.

"Sefa." Brannoc's voice from the deckhouse, muffled by the bulkhead and by the fact that Brannoc had the vocal range of a landslide. "Galley. Now. Crew meeting."

"I'm watching the weather."

"You can watch the weather from the galley. It's bigger there."

It was not, in fact, bigger in the galley, but Sefa went anyway, because crew meetings on the Kestrel's Wake had become the closest thing she had to a religion: badly attended, frequently argumentative, and held in a room that smelled permanently of diesel and Brannoc's terrible tea.

* * *

The galley was barely big enough for four people and a stove with opinions. Tamsin sat at the little bolted-down table with the chart of the reef spread over it and her hands flat on either side, which was her thinking posture, which Sefa had learned meant *don't talk yet*. Ovid was wedged into the corner with three of his notebooks, because Ovid took notes at meetings the way other men took breaths. Brannoc filled the doorway, which was the only place he fit, holding a mug of tea that could have stripped barnacles.

"Sit," Tamsin said.

"I'm standing."

"Sefa."

"I'm sitting." She folded herself down onto the bench across from Tamsin, knees up, arms around them. Small spaces, small posture. Old habit. Old as the port, old as the night she'd run.

Tamsin looked at her a moment too long. That was the thing about Tamsin — she didn't miss much, and what she missed, she circled back for later, patient as a shark with a legal education.

"Reyes's fleet is a quarter-mile off the reef," Tamsin said. "The dredge started this morning. Brannoc heard it on the hydrophone — says it sounds like the sea clearing its throat."

"It sounds like the sea being murdered," Brannoc corrected.

"And the window's shrinking. Sefa, you've been reading the swell better than any of us. How long do we actually have?"

Sefa looked at the chart without seeing it. She'd been asking herself a different question for six days now, and the question had teeth, and it was chewing.

"Three tides," she said. "Maybe four if we're lucky, and the sea doesn't believe in luck. The monsoon's leading edge is moving faster than the almanacs say it should. It's going to come down on that reef like a door slamming."

"Three tides," Tamsin repeated. She said it the way you'd say a diagnosis. Then: "There's something else."

Sefa's stomach dropped through the hull, through the water, through the reef, all the way down to the Corvane, where it could drown in peace.

"The scout ship," Tamsin said. "The one that's been sitting on our horizon since the first tide. Brannoc pulled its silhouette off the hydrophone returns and ran it against the fleet registry." She slid a page across the chart. A ship's profile, drawn in Brannoc's blunt pencil strokes, and beneath it a name.

Sefa didn't look at it. She looked at her own hands, wrapped around her knees, and felt six years collapse into this galley like a deckhouse in a storm.

*The Vireo.* She'd known. She'd known since the second day out, when she'd seen that particular rake to the mast and that particular lazy figure-eight it made on patrol, and she'd said nothing, and every day since, the saying-nothing had gotten heavier, until now Tamsin was holding the name out on a piece of paper and Sefa's silence was the heaviest thing aboard.

"The *Vireo*," Ovid read aloud, and then, because Ovid's mind was a filing cabinet with a conscience problem, "Scout class, navy auxiliary. Attached to Reyes's command. Why would Reyes's own scout be shadowing *us* rather than surveying the wreck for her? She knows where the wreck is."

"Good question," Tamsin said, and her eyes came back to Sefa, and stayed there.

The galley got very quiet. Even the stove seemed to hold its opinion.

"I can tell you why," Sefa said.

Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She cleared her throat and started over.

"I can tell you why, and I should have told you six days ago, and I'm going to tell you now, and then you can all decide what to do with me."

"Sefa—" Brannoc started.

"Let me get through it," she said, "because if I stop I won't start again, and I've been starting and stopping for a week and I'm tired."

* * *

She told it in order, because that was the only way she could hold it.

"My name isn't Sefa. Well — it is, but it's half of one. Sefa Meridian. My family were the Meridians. The islands, the trading houses, all of it. Before the war with the northern syndicates, my grandfather's family ran more shipping than the colonial office did. And then the governor — Calloway, the one whose confession is sitting in that strongroom — decided the war needed funding, and the funding needed a source, and the source was us."

Ovid had gone very still. Ovid, who had confessed in this same galley not two weeks ago that he'd catalogued the loot with his own hands and said nothing for thirty years — Ovid looked at her like a man watching a door he'd nailed shut swing open.

"The navy didn't just take the treasury," Sefa said. "That's the part nobody says. They took the treasury, and then they needed the theft to look legal, so they needed *us* to look guilty. Smuggling, they said. Collaboration with the syndicates, they said. There were trials. My grandfather stood trial in a courtroom where half the judges owned navy pensions. They took the islands, the ships, the houses. My mother's family got scattered across four ports. My father—" she stopped, and made herself go on, because stopping was the old habit and she was done with old habits, "—my father didn't survive the first year after. People like him, people who lost everything at once, sometimes they just... stop floating."

Nobody spoke. Brannoc set his terrible tea down with the gentleness of a man handling explosives.

"I was nine when my mother died. I went into the port system, which is a polite word for a machine that turns children into dockworkers. I ran at fourteen. I kept the name Sefa because it was true, and I dropped Meridian because it was a target. You don't walk around a navy port with a name the navy spent thirty years teaching everyone to hate."

"The *Vireo*," Tamsin said quietly.

"The *Vireo*," Sefa said. "There's an officer aboard her. Lieutenant Corvane-Hale — yes, that Corvane, the governor's line, they kept the name and the money and lost the guilt somewhere along the way. He's been hunting stray Meridians for years. Not arresting them. Just... finding them. Watching them. Every time one of us gets near anything to do with the old case, the *Vireo* turns up. I saw her in the port the week we signed on. I told myself it was coincidence. I told myself a lot of things that week." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm very good at telling myself things. It's practically a trade skill."

"Why didn't you tell us?" Tamsin asked. Not cruel. Just the question, laid flat on the table like a knife she hoped she wouldn't have to pick up.

Sefa made herself meet her eyes.

"Because I watched what Ovid's confession did to this crew. Because I watched you all look at him like the floor had opened up, and I thought — if I say it now, after they've learned to trust me, after the riptide, after everything — I'll lose this. And I have never had a *this*." She spread her hands at the galley, the boat, the whole rusting improbable enterprise of them. "I've had berths. I've had contracts. I've had four months here, a season there. I have never had a crew that would come looking for me when I went over the side. And I was a coward about it. There. That's the whole confession. I traded the truth for a place at the table, which is exactly what the navy did to my family, just with smaller numbers."

The stove hissed. Somewhere aft, the hull ticked as the swell changed.

It was Ovid who moved first. He closed his notebook — actually closed it, which Sefa had never seen him do while anyone was talking — and he looked at her with his wet, tired archivist's eyes, and he said:

"I catalogued your family's ruin. Shelf by shelf. Deed by deed. I put labels on your grandfather's ships and filed your mother's exile under *miscellaneous assets*, and I went home to my tea." His voice cracked in the middle and he let it. "You have nothing to confess to this crew that I haven't already confessed worse. The question was never whether we'd forgive each other. The question is whether we'd use each other's sins as weapons. I refuse to. I'm done with it. I spent thirty years being a weapon."

Brannoc cleared his throat, which for Brannoc was an aria. "For what it's worth," he said, "I knew."

Every head turned.

"You *knew*?" Tamsin said.

"I knew there was something. Not what." Brannoc rubbed the back of his neck, embarrassed, a landslide trying to look sheepish. "Girl reads water like it's her own handwriting, sleeps with her boots on, flinches every time a navy hull goes by, and lies about her last name with a little pause in front of it — the pause is the tell, always the pause. I figured it out around the third tide and I figured it wasn't mine to say." He shrugged, enormous. "Figured she'd say it when the boat was strong enough to hold it."

"And is it?" Tamsin asked. Not of Brannoc. Of the boat. Of all of them. Of herself, maybe, though Tamsin would have bitten through her own tongue before admitting she had a self that needed holding.

"It held Ovid," Brannoc said. "It'll hold her."

* * *

Afterward — after the shouting, which was briefer than Sefa had braced for, and the long silence, which was longer, and Tamsin finally saying, "You read currents for this crew from now on and you tell this crew the truth from now on, and those are the same job" — Sefa went back up to the bow, because that was where she thought, and found she wasn't alone.

Ovid had wedged himself against the windlass with a notebook, because of course he had.

"You're going to write it down," she said. "The true history. Us. Her."

"Every word I can verify." He patted the page. "Which, thanks to you, is now a great deal more. The Meridian case was the first file I ever catalogued. I was twenty-three. I remember thinking the labels didn't fit — *miscellaneous assets* — and I remember deciding the feeling was above my pay grade." He looked out at the blackening horizon. "Thirty years, and the file comes back to me wearing a teenager's face and reading my hydrophone better than Brannoc does."

"She's not a file," Sefa said, and then, because the truth was loose in her now and apparently intended to keep coming, "and neither was my family. That's the thing nobody wrote down. The trials, the seizures, the *miscellaneous assets* — it's all paper. But there were people. My grandfather used to sing while he coiled line. Terribly. My mother could name every fish in the shallows in three languages. Nobody writes that down in a seizure order. The navy took the islands and got paper, and thought the paper was the thing." She hugged her knees. "It was never the thing."

Ovid was quiet for a while. The swell came around another point. Behind them, faint through the water and the wind, came the sound Brannoc had named: the dredge, chewing the reef a quarter-mile away, patient as a debt collector.

"The strongroom," Ovid said at last. "The governor's confession. If it names the seizures as theft — if it names the judges, the officers, the Corvane line — then it isn't treasure, Sefa. It's a key. Everything they locked away comes open at once. The islands. The name. Your family's standing." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were bright and terrible with an archivist's fever. "Do you understand what's in that strongroom? It's not the governor's guilt. It's your grandfather's *name*, waiting thirty years at the bottom of the sea for someone to carry it home."

Sefa looked at the horizon, where the monsoon had begun to assemble itself out of cloud and intention, and past it, where the *Vireo* sat its lazy figure-eight, and she felt something in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet — something that wasn't fear, for the first time in six years.

"Three tides," she said.

"Three tides," Ovid agreed, and opened his notebook, and began to write.

She was still watching the *Vireo* when she saw it change — the lazy figure-eight tightening, the bow coming around, the little ship putting on speed and pointing, not at the reef, not at Reyes's fleet, but straight at them.

"Ovid," she said. "Get Tamsin. Now."
