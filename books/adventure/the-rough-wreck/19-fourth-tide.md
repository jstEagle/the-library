## 19. Fourth Tide

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and I knew it before the instruments told me. You always do, after enough years on the water — a change in the way the hull breathes against its mooring, a shift in the slap of water under the transom, something older than clocks. The Kestrel's Wake groaned at her lines like an old man getting out of bed, and I lay in my bunk for exactly four seconds of self-pity before Sefa's voice came down through the deck plates.

"Tide's turning. Brannoc says if you're not up in five minutes he's starting without you."

"Brannoc," I called back, "is afraid of the winch."

"I heard that," said Brannoc's voice, from considerably closer than his engine room should have allowed.

I climbed out into grey pre-dawn light and found him crouched over the aft deck like a surgeon over an open chest, surrounded by salvage that had no business being part of the same machine. A hand-cranked capstan he'd pulled off a trawler wreck two seasons ago. Chain from our own anchor locker. A length of steel cable Ovid had identified, with genuine scholarly excitement, as "almost certainly not rotten." And at the center of it all, bolted to a plate Brannoc had welded through the night with the boat's last good electrodes, a gear assembly he'd machined by hand out of a propeller shaft.

"It's beautiful," Sefa said, deadpan, chewing on the end of a rope.

"It's functional," Brannoc corrected. "Beauty is for people with spare electrodes." He sat back on his heels and surveyed his creation the way other men survey their children. "Four hundred meters of cable running down to the strongroom wheel through the guide sheave. Crank ratio gives us roughly eleven turns of the capstan for every turn of the wheel. Which means—" he paused, because Brannoc loved this part, "—we don't need divers strong enough to turn it. We need divers patient enough to connect it."

"And how long will that take?"

"Connecting? Twenty minutes a diver, if the current behaves." He looked out at the water, which was doing that flat, innocent thing it does when it's planning murder. "The current will not behave."

* * *

The Corvane lay in thirty-one fathoms, which sounds manageable until you remember that thirty-one fathoms is where the light starts lying to you. By the time we'd anchored over her — third try; the reef has opinions about where boats belong — the sun was up enough to make the water go from black to a deep bruised green, and I went down first to run the line.

The descent never got easier. People think you get used to sinking toward a grave, but what actually happens is you get better at pretending. The Corvane rose up out of the murk the way she always did: all at once, a wall of encrusted iron and coral, listing hard to port, her masts long since gone to rubble, her name still legible on the stern in letters the sea had been trying to erase for forty years and only half succeeded. C-O-R-V-A-N-E, each letter wearing a small garden.

I'd touched her hull on the first tide like a pilgrim. Now I touched her like a burglar, which felt more honest.

The guide line ran from the capstan above, down past the broken cargo hold — stripped bare years ago, we'd learned that much already — along the flooded corridor where I'd found my mother's knife wedged in a bulkhead like a signature, and ended at the antechamber door. Beyond that door, the compartment Voss's chart showed. Beyond the compartment, the strongroom wheel itself: a brass ring the size of a dinner plate, fused shut by four decades of salt and silence.

My mother had died with one hand on it.

I clipped the cable to the eye Brannoc had specified, checked it twice, checked it a third time because checking things is free and dying isn't, and gave the signal line three sharp tugs. Above me, faint as a heartbeat through thirty-one fathoms of water, the capstan began to turn.

Nothing happened to the wheel. Of course nothing happened to the wheel. But the cable came taut with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, and when I put my hands on the brass ring I could feel it — a tremor, a shiver of tension, the whole apparatus above holding its breath. The seal hadn't moved. But for the first time in forty years, something was asking it to.

I stayed until my bottom time forced me up, feeling that tremor like a held note, and then I ascended into a world that smelled like diesel and burnt coffee, both of which were Ovid's fault.

* * *

"We are drinking the strategic reserve," Ovid announced, pouring coffee from the dented pot into four mismatched cups. "I want that understood. This is the coffee we were saving for an emergency, and I have decided that hope is an emergency."

"You decided that yesterday," Sefa said, taking a cup anyway.

"Yes, well. Yesterday I was less certain the emergency would be survivable." Ovid settled onto the coil of spare rope he used as a chair and produced his notebook, because Ovid produced his notebook the way other people breathed. "For the record: relay schedule, fourth tide. Diver one connects and holds tension. Diver two relieves at twenty-minute intervals. Estimated total crank time to shear the outer seal — assuming Brannoc's ratios are correct, which they always are, and which he will tell you himself in a moment — six hours of continuous turning."

"Five and a half," said Brannoc, emerging from below with grease to his elbows. "If the sheave doesn't jump its track."

"If the sheave jumps its track," Ovid said, "we will all die, the Corvane will remain unopened forever, and history will record that the expedition failed due to a component salvaged from a fishing trawler. I've drafted the epitaph already. It's very dignified."

"Dignified," Sefa repeated. She was coiling dive line with quick, economical hands, and she didn't look up, but there was a smile hiding somewhere around her mouth. "What's it say?"

"'They nearly had it.'"

We laughed. It surprised me, that laugh — short and rough, but real. Four days ago we'd been strangers arguing about ballast. Now we had jokes with load-bearing walls.

Then I looked east, toward the quarter-mile of open water between us and the reef's outer shoulder, and the laugh went out of me like air from a punctured lung.

The dredge was there.

Reyes's fleet worked in shifts, and the day shift had brought up the big one — a converted landing barge with a crane arm like a gallows and a suction head that hung in the water off her flank, chewing. Even at this distance I could see the sediment plume spreading from her position, a brown stain blooming across the reef shelf, slow and patient as ink in water. Every hour, that plume grew. Every hour, the reef that held the Corvane in her cradle lost a little more of itself.

"She started at first light," Sefa said quietly, following my gaze. "She's been working the same seam since. Consistent. Deliberate."

"She's not dredging for salvage," Brannoc said. His voice had gone flat, the way it did when the engineer in him saw something structural he didn't like. "You don't dredge a wreck site in a pattern like that unless you're trying to undermine it. She's eating the reef out from under the hull. Give her three more days and the Corvane rolls into deep water, and nobody raises anything ever again."

"Two days," I said. "Monsoon comes tomorrow night or the night after. After that, nobody works this reef, including her."

"So she wins either way," Sefa said. "Storm takes the wreck, or she takes it first."

"No." I picked up my cup and drank Ovid's emergency coffee, which tasted like it had been brewed during an actual war. "She wins if we stop. That's the whole arithmetic. Everything else is just weather."

Nobody argued. That was the strange, fragile miracle of this crew — not that they agreed with me, but that they'd stopped needing to before they'd obey. Brannoc grumbled his way back to the winch. Sefa checked the tide tables twice. Ovid wrote something in his notebook that I chose to believe was inspiring.

* * *

The relay began at slack water, and slack water lasted ninety minutes, and we spent every one of them.

I dove first with Brannoc — his first look at the wheel, and I watched his face behind the mask go through contempt, calculation, and finally a kind of fierce tenderness, the way he looked at any machine that had been asked to do too much for too long. He ran his gloved fingers around the brass ring, tapped it, listened to it through his bones, and then spent fifteen minutes rigging the cable's lower attachment so the pull came across the wheel's face at the angle he wanted.

"She's seized," he signaled, when we were face to face. "Not just corroded. Seized. Something's jammed behind the door — or the door's jammed behind something."

*Something*, plural, was the word he used, and I thought of the skeleton in the antechamber, my mother's rig, her hand on the wheel, and I made myself signal back: *Can we turn it?*

Brannoc considered. Then he gave me the single most Brannoc answer possible: a shrug, followed by a thumbs-up, followed by a gesture meaning *ask me again in six hours.*

We turned it for six hours.

The rhythm of it became its own world. One diver down at the wheel, guiding the cable, keeping the sheave honest. Two on the capstan above, cranking in shifts while the third rested and Ovid called encouragement that was technically accurate and emotionally useless. Eleven turns of the capstan for every turn of the wheel, and the wheel needed perhaps forty turns to come full circle, and the seal fought us for every degree like it had been paid to.

By midday my shoulders were on fire. By early afternoon Sefa surfaced from her rotation with her hands shaking so badly she couldn't work the clip on her harness, and Brannoc wrapped them in a towel and put the coffee cup between them and said, "Drink. You're done for today," in a voice that permitted no appeal, and she drank, glaring at him over the rim, and he glared back, and something passed between them that I pretended not to see because it wasn't mine to see yet.

At the three-hour mark the wheel moved.

It was Sefa who felt it first — she was down, on her second rotation, stubborn beyond all sense — and her signal line sang four sharp tugs, the emergency code, and my heart dropped through the deck before her voice crackled up the comm line, breathless and laughing: "*It moved! Half a degree! It MOVED!*"

Above, Brannoc threw his wrench in the air. Ovid stood up so fast he knocked over the strategic reserve. And I stood at the rail looking down at thirty-one fathoms of green water, at the brown plume creeping closer from the east, at the whole impossible machine of rust and cable and stubbornness we'd built, and I felt something I hadn't let myself feel since Voss died with my promise in his cold hand.

Hope. Ugly, exhausted, unreasonable hope.

* * *

We cracked the outer seal at 6:42 in the evening, with the light going amber and the barometer falling and everyone aboard knowing exactly what the falling barometer meant.

It didn't give way dramatically. There was no bang, no triumphant groan of ancient metal. What happened was smaller and stranger: after hours of resistance measured in fractions of degrees, the wheel simply went soft — the cable pulled and the wheel turned and kept turning, easy as a well-oiled thing, and Sefa, who was down again because none of us could make her stop, reported in a voice gone quiet with awe:

"There's a gap. Light's coming through the door edge. It's — Tamsin, it's open. The outer door is open."

I was in the water before anyone could stop me, which nobody tried, because they'd known I would be.

The descent took everything I had left. My arms were finished; my legs were rumors of legs; I hauled myself down the guide line hand over hand like the line owed me money. The corridor swallowed me. The bulkhead with the knife scar slid past. And then the antechamber, and the door — the outer door of the compartment, standing open a hand's width, black gap edged in disturbed silt, breathing cold water out at me like a held exhale finally released.

Beyond it, darkness, and the shape of the inner strongroom door beyond, and the wheel on that door, and whatever waited behind it.

I didn't go in. Every safety margin I had left said no, and for once — thinking of Brannoc's face, of Sefa's shaking hands, of the fact that these people had become, without my permission or my notice, the reason I wanted to surface — I listened.

I put my palm flat against the opened door instead. Cold iron, furred with forty years of growth, trembling faintly with the vibration of the capstan far above, still turning, still asking.

*Tomorrow,* I told it. *One more tide.*

And then, from a quarter-mile away, through the water itself, I felt it — a deep, wrong shudder, a concussion that had nothing to do with our winch. The dredge. Closer than she'd been at dawn. Her suction head biting into the reef shoulder, and the whole seabed flinching around us, and a fine rain of disturbed sediment beginning to drift down through the corridor like dirty snow.

When I surfaced, the sky to the southeast had gone the color of a bruise, and Reyes's flagship had moved, and she was no longer a quarter-mile away.

She was anchoring.
