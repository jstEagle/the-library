## 20. The Minister's Man

Ovid Quill had spent thirty years learning how governments lie, and he had catalogued every method the way other men collected stamps. There was the lie of omission, which was merely tidiness. There was the lie of reclassification, which was ambition. And there was his personal favorite — the lie of hospitality — in which a powerful institution invited you to lunch, fed you rather good fish, and waited for gratitude to do what interrogation never could.

So when the man in the immaculate grey uniform bought him a second brandy at the Meridian Yacht Club and said, "Mr. Quill, the Ministry has been reviewing your case," Ovid felt something very much like homecoming.

"Have they," he said. "How flattering. I'd assumed my file was gathering dust in a drawer marked *Irrelevant*."

"The drawer is marked *Pending*." The man smiled. He was perhaps fifty, with the kind of face that had been sanded smooth by decades of saying nothing important. His name, as offered, was Commander Pell. His hands, Ovid noticed, were wrong for a sailor — soft, uncalloused, the hands of a clerk who had never hauled a line in his life. "Your pension was suspended after the Corvane matter. Thirty years ago."

"After I signed off on a manifest that described forty crates of colonial trade goods," Ovid said mildly, "when any fool with eyes could see the crates were stamped with the Meridian family crests. Yes. I remember it fondly."

Pell's smile did not flicker, which told Ovid everything. A man who could hear that and not blink had been sent to give, not to take. Which meant someone above him wanted something badly enough to pay for it.

"We're prepared to reinstate the pension. Retroactively."

Ovid set down his glass with great care, because his hand wanted to shake and he refused to let it. Thirty years of retroactive pension. He did the arithmetic out of old habit — the archive salary he'd lived on, the rooms he hadn't rented, the winters he'd spent choosing between coal and books. It came to a number that would have bought him a small house with a view of the water and enough paper to write the true history of the Corvane twice over.

"And in exchange for this generosity," he said, "the Ministry asks...?"

"Nothing so crude as an exchange." Pell produced a leather folder and laid it open between them like a man dealing cards he already knew the value of. Inside was a photograph — grainy, aerial, taken from altitude — of a survey boat at anchor among the reef channels. He knew her lines the way he knew his own handwriting. *Kestrel's Wake*. "We ask you to help us understand. You are, after all, the foremost living authority on the Corvane's cargo. The Ministry finds itself confused. Rumors reach us of salvage activity in restricted waters. We would hate for an elderly gentleman of letters to be caught up in something unfortunate."

"How unfortunate?"

"Piracy charges can be so tedious to defend," Pell said pleasantly. "Especially for a man with no family to advocate for him."

And there it was — not the offer but the shape beneath it, the way a hull shows beneath the waterline before you ever see the ship. Ovid looked at the photograph for a long moment. Tamsin would be below decks or asleep; Sefa would be somewhere aloft reading the wind; Brannoc would be swearing at the engine with the tenderness of a man scolding a child he loved. His crew. His strange, improbable, hard-won crew.

He thought about Voss, too — dead Voss, who had traded a map for a promise and died owing a debt thirty years old. Ovid had joined this venture to pay a debt of his own. He had catalogued the looted treasury of the Meridian families with his own neat, careful hand, and then he had signed the false manifest, and then he had gone home and slept, more or less, for three decades.

"Well," Ovid said, and heard his own voice settle into the register he had once used to soothe suspicious ministers — the register of a tired, harmless, slightly vain old man. "You've caught me at a weak moment, Commander. My knees ache. The boat leaks. The cook burns everything."

"Brannoc," Pell said, consulting a card. "The engineer. Disgraced from the Harbormaster's Office, yes, we know."

"You've been thorough."

"The Ministry is always thorough." Pell slid a pen across the table. It was a good pen, heavy, gold-nibbed — the kind of pen that made a signature feel official even when it was a lie. "You needn't decide anything today. But if you were inclined to be helpful — if, say, you could tell us where the strongroom lies on the wreck, or what documents might be found within — well. A man who helps the Ministry finds that doors reopen. Pensions restore themselves. Histories get written with the cooperation of the archives, rather than against them."

Ovid picked up the pen. It balanced beautifully in his hand. He thought: *this is the exact weight of thirty years of silence*, and found, to his surprise, that the weight no longer frightened him. He had carried heavier.

"I'm an archivist, Commander," he said. "I deal in locations. Give me a chart and I'll show you where the strongroom sits as precisely as I can — though I'll warn you, my memory of a thirty-year-old deck plan may have gaps."

"Gaps we can forgive," Pell said warmly.

They spread a chart across the table, and Ovid bent over it, and lied with the fluency of a lifetime's practice. Not entirely — that was the art of it. He gave them true things wrapped around a false center. The Corvane lay on her starboard side, true. The strongroom sat aft of the main hold, true. Its access ran through the flooded lower decks, true. But he shifted the compartment two frame-sections forward, into a section of the hull that Brannoc had already surveyed and condemned as collapsed — a honeycomb of buckled plating that no diver could enter and no charge would profitably crack. Let them dig there. Let them spend their tides on rubble.

Pell watched him draw the little cross with an expression of such benign satisfaction that Ovid nearly pitied him.

"There," Ovid said. "That's worth a pension, surely."

"That's worth a great deal, Mr. Quill." Pell folded the chart away and, as an afterthought that was clearly not an afterthought, added: "One more thing. The Ministry takes its obligations seriously. Once we act on this information — and we will act soon, before the season turns — it would be best if your association with that boat ended cleanly. Be ashore when it happens. There will be an inquiry afterward, and inquiries are so much simpler when witnesses are scattered."

"When *what* happens?" Ovid asked, keeping his voice bland as porridge.

Pell smiled the smile of a man who believed he had already won. "Salvage operations can be disruptive. Explosives especially. The reef is unstable. Accidents occur." He stood, buttoned his coat, and shook Ovid's hand with both of his soft ones, as if greeting a long-lost uncle. "Next low tide, Mr. Quill. Be ashore. Enjoy your retirement."

He left. Ovid sat alone with two empty glasses and one full brandy and let his hands shake now, properly, because there was no one to see and because the shaking was honest and he had decided, some months ago aboard a rotting survey boat, to stop being dishonest in small ways if he could manage it.

*Next low tide.* Charges. They were going to bury the Corvane — the documents, the confession, the proof, the whole drowned truth of it — under a hundred tons of collapsed reef, and call it an accident, and Ovid Quill was meant to be safely ashore collecting his pension when the sea closed over it forever.

He finished the brandy. It really was very good fish they served here, he thought absurdly, and then corrected himself, because the fish had been at lunch. The brandy was also very good. Both facts seemed suddenly, enormously important, the way trivial things do when a man is holding himself together with trivia.

Then he paid for his own drink — a point of principle; he would not owe them so much as a brandy — and walked out into the evening with the gait of a much older man, and began, in his head, composing the fastest report of his career.

* * *

The Kestrel's Wake smelled, as always, of diesel, salt, and Brannoc's particular genius, which expressed itself chiefly as organized chaos. Ovid came up the ladder breathing hard and found the whole crew gathered in the cramped cabin around the chart table — Tamsin with her arms crossed, Sefa perched on the locker swinging her heels, Brannoc holding a wrench he was using as a back-scratcher, which explained the smell of machine oil in the galley.

"You look," Tamsin said, "like a man who's seen a ghost."

"Worse. I've seen a bureaucrat." Ovid lowered himself onto the bench with a groan he did not entirely perform. "Commander Pell, liaison to the Minister himself. He bought me lunch and offered me thirty years of back pension."

Sefa stopped swinging her heels. "For what?"

"For the location of the strongroom." He met Tamsin's eyes. "Which I gave them. Two frame-sections forward of the truth, inside the collapsed section Brannoc surveyed. They'll waste their divers on rubble."

Brannoc lowered the wrench. "You *lied* to the navy."

"I misled the navy. Lying implies I expected them to believe me. Misleading is a craft." He allowed himself a thin smile, and then let it go, because the rest of it wouldn't wait. "But here's the price of the performance, and it isn't small. They intend to seal the wreck with demolition charges. The reef is 'unstable.' Accidents 'occur.'" He heard his own voice go dry and precise, the archive voice, the voice that read death warrants in triplicate. "At the next low tide. Tomorrow morning. They told me to be ashore when it happened, and to consider my association with this vessel concluded."

The cabin went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the rigging ticked against the mast, counting seconds nobody wanted counted.

Tamsin spoke first, and her voice was flat and cold in a way Ovid had learned to fear more than shouting. "They told you the time."

"They told me the time," Ovid confirmed. "Because they believe I've sold you out. That belief, ladies and gentlemen, is the only asset I have left, and I intend to spend every coin of it." He leaned forward over the chart table, and his hand — the traitorous, shaking hand — steadied itself flat against the paper. "We have until tomorrow's low tide. One tide. Reyes's dredge is quarter-mile off the starboard reef and she thinks we don't know her schedule. We know her charges are coming, and she doesn't know we know. In thirty years of archival work, I can tell you that is the single most valuable piece of intelligence I have ever handled."

Sefa was already at the porthole, reading the sky with that unnerving stillness of hers. "Low tide's at oh-six-forty. Wind's backing. If we move tonight—"

"If we move tonight," Tamsin said slowly, and Ovid watched the calculation turn behind her eyes like a wheel beginning to turn, "we can work the strongroom during the last hours of darkness, before their demolition team ever reaches the wreck. Beat them to our own grave."

"Or," Brannoc said, with the gravity of a man setting down a wrench he'd very much like to throw, "we run. Right now. Take the documents we have, the manifest copies, Ovid's testimony, and let the navy blow up their own dirty secret. Live to publish another day."

"And leave the letter?" Sefa said quietly. "Leave whatever else is down there? Leave the gold for Reyes to raise and vanish with?"

Nobody answered that. The lamp swung gently overhead, and the shadows swung with it, and Ovid thought: *here it is again, the same fork in the same road, the one my whole generation took the wrong way thirty years ago.*

Tamsin looked at each of them in turn — the engineer, the runaway, the old liar — and Ovid saw her arrive at the decision the way a diver arrives at the surface: all at once, gasping, committed.

"We don't run," she said. "We dive tonight. All hands. Brannoc, I need that winch ready by midnight. Sefa, I need every minute of slack water you can find me in the dark. Ovid—" and here something almost like a smile touched her mouth, tired and fierce, "—I need you to keep being the Ministry's most valuable traitor. If Pell comes looking for confirmation, feed him rubble and smiles."

"Madam," Ovid said, rising with as much dignity as his knees permitted, "misleading the government is the only honest work I've done in thirty years. I should hate to stop now."

As they broke to their stations, he paused at the foot of the ladder and looked back at the chart table, at the little false cross he had drawn for Commander Pell still ghosting in his memory two sections forward of the truth. Somewhere out in the darkening harbor, a flagship rode at anchor, and a minister's man was writing a report that would end with the words *the archivist cooperated fully.*

Let him write it, Ovid thought. Let the whole rotten ministry believe the old man had finally been bought.

By dawn, one way or another, they would all learn what his signature was worth.

He climbed up into the wind and began, at last, to hurry.
