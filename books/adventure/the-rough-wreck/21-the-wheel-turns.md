## 21. The Wheel Turns

The fifth tide came in grey and mean, the kind of morning that made the sea look like it was holding a grudge. Tamsin stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Wake* with a mug of Brannoc's coffee — which tasted like engine degreaser that had once met a coffee bean in passing — and watched the reef break white a quarter-mile to starboard.

Somewhere under all that churn, behind a wheel her mother had died touching, was the strongroom.

"Water's at slack in forty minutes," Sefa said, appearing at her elbow with the tide tables folded into her back pocket like they were part of her body. She'd taken to carrying them everywhere, even to sleep. "Current's going to turn east-southeast. You'll have maybe fifty minutes of workable bottom time before it starts dragging you toward the cut."

"Fifty minutes," Tamsin said. "Generous."

"It's not generous. It's what you get." Sefa squinted at the horizon, where the masts of Reyes's fleet sat on the water like needles waiting for a vein. "The dredge stopped an hour ago."

Tamsin went still. "Stopped?"

"Stopped. Not left. It's just sitting there, engines idling." Sefa's voice dropped. "Brannoc thinks they're repositioning. Ovid thinks they're waiting for something. I think neither of them slept last night and they're both guessing."

"And what do you think?"

Sefa looked out at the fleet for a long moment. "I think we open that door today or we never open it."

***

They'd spent two tides learning the wheel.

It was a brute of a mechanism, a brass-spoked valve the size of a cartwheel set into the strongroom's outer seal, designed to be turned by four men standing shoulder to shoulder on dry deck. Under forty feet of cold seawater, crusted with nineteen years of coral, it turned like the whole ocean had been poured into the mechanism and left to set.

The first relay dive, Tamsin and Brannoc had managed a quarter-turn between them before their air margins screamed them back up. The second tide, they'd brought the winch.

The winch was pure Brannoc — a salvaged capstan from a fishing trawler, married to a hydraulic pump that had no business working and worked anyway because Brannoc had spent three nights swearing at it in two languages. They'd bolted a chain to the wheel's spokes, run the chain up through a guide block lashed to the wreck's superstructure, and let the capstan do what human shoulders couldn't.

"You know what I love about this plan?" Brannoc had said, spitting into his mask to defog it. "If the seal blows outward instead of inward, the pressure differential turns us both into a red mist, and we won't even have to write the incident report."

"Your bedside manner is a gift," Tamsin said.

"I'm an engineer. We don't do comfort. We do tolerances." He'd grinned at her through the mask, and there had been something in the grin she hadn't seen since the first day — something that wasn't fear dressed up as a joke. Something almost like joy.

Now, on the fifth tide, the wheel stood at three and a half turns. The seal groaned beneath them like a sleeping thing being woken by degrees.

***

Tamsin went down first.

She always went down first now. It wasn't bravery; she'd stopped pretending it was bravery around the third tide, when she'd found her mother's knife and understood that some part of her had been diving this wreck since before Voss ever put the chart in her hands. Going down first was just honesty. The Corvane was hers to face.

The descent was quick and blue-black, her torch beam cutting a cone through water thick with the monsoon's early sediment. The wreck rose up to meet her — the great slumped hull, the cargo hold gaping where they'd cut it, the superstructure furred with coral like frost. And below the waterline, half-buried in the sand the dredging had shifted, the antechamber: a low iron mouth with the wheel set in its throat.

Her mother's knife was still wedged in the bulkhead beside it. Tamsin touched it each dive now, the way you'd touch a doorframe leaving home. A ritual. A hello.

She clipped her line, checked the chain runs, and gave the signal line two sharp pulls. Above, the capstan engaged with a shudder she felt through the wreck itself, through her teeth, through the soles of her boots.

The wheel turned.

Three and three-quarters. Four. The coral crust cracked and sloughed off in sheets, drifting past her torch like snow falling upward. At four and a quarter the mechanism made a sound she felt more than heard — a deep, structural complaint, iron remembering what it was built for.

Then, somewhere inside the seal, something *let go*.

Not the catastrophic release Brannoc had joked about. A softer sound. A lock, old and stubborn, finally conceding. The wheel spun free a full half-turn on its own, and the chain went slack, and Tamsin hung in the black water with her heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a door.

The seal had opened a hand's width. Dark water breathed out of the gap — colder than the sea around it, dead-still, the water of a room that hadn't exchanged a breath with the world in nineteen years.

Tamsin pulled the signal line twice more. *Hold.* Then she took out the pry bar, fitted it to the gap, and leaned her whole weight against the door of everything.

It swung.

***

By the time the others reached the bottom, Tamsin had her torch inside the strongroom and had already stopped breathing properly.

The room was small — maybe ten feet by twelve, an iron box built into the ship's spine. And it was *empty*.

Not stripped. Not looted. Empty in a way that meant someone had come here long ago and carried the gold out — or, she realized as her beam swept the walls, someone had never put gold here at all.

Along one wall ran a row of lead-lined document chests, naval pattern, their lids sealed with wax and wire. Along another, shelving held oilcloth-wrapped bundles stacked with a care that looked deliberate, almost reverent. In the center of the floor, weighted down with a piece of ballast, lay a single leather satchel, its flap tied shut.

And on top of the satchel, a letter.

Tamsin's torch shook. She hated that it shook. Nineteen years of diving, and her hands chose this room, this moment, to remember they were human.

The letter was wrapped in oilcloth, triple-folded, and the wrapping had been done well — done to survive. Someone had wanted it found. Someone had believed it would be found, eventually, by whoever opened this door, and had written accordingly.

Through the oilcloth, in ink gone brown but legible, in a hand Tamsin knew better than her own —

*M. MONTROSE.*

Her mother's handwriting. Her mother's name. Her mother's letter, sitting on a satchel in a strongroom at the bottom of the sea, addressed to no one and therefore to everyone, which meant —

Which meant her mother had known. Had gotten here. Had opened this room, read whatever was in those chests, and then sat down in the dark and the cold and written a letter, and sealed it, and left it where the truth would find it if the truth ever came looking.

Tamsin didn't remember deciding to surface. One moment she was staring at the oilcloth packet; the next, Sefa's hands were on her harness, guiding her up the line, and Brannoc's voice was in her ears asking questions she couldn't answer because answering would have required words, and words required breath, and all her breath was being used for something else.

***

They laid everything out on the deck plates of the *Kestrel's Wake* like surgeons laying out instruments: six document chests, eleven oilcloth bundles, the satchel, the letter.

Nobody touched the letter.

Ovid knelt over the nearest chest with cotton gloves he'd produced from somewhere, his hands trembling in a way Tamsin had never seen from him. He broke the wire seal, lifted the lid, and went very quiet.

"Well?" Brannoc demanded. "Is it the governor's confession? Is it the—"

"It's the Meridian treasury records." Ovid's voice came out strange, flattened, like a man reading his own autopsy. "Itemized. Dated. Signed." He turned a page with exaggerated gentleness. "Every seizure order. Every 'customs forfeiture.' Every family named, every estate catalogued, every shipment routed through the Corvane with falsified manifests — my falsified manifests, the ones I—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's all here. Thirty years of theft, documented in the navy's own hand."

"The bundles?" Sefa asked.

Ovid unwrapped one with the care of a man defusing something. Inside were letters — personal correspondence, dozens of pieces, tied in families. He read the topmost one and his face changed. "These are the letters the families sent begging for their property back. And the replies." He looked up. "The replies are signed by the governor. Refusing them. In writing. With the colonial seal."

"So it's real," Brannoc said. "All of it. Voss wasn't selling us a story."

"It was never gold," Tamsin said.

They all looked at her.

She was crouched by the satchel, not touching it, the way you don't touch a sleeping animal. "Voss said raise what's in the strongroom. Everyone assumed treasure — I assumed treasure, Reyes assumes treasure, the minister sending dredges assumes treasure. But there's no gold here. There's paper. Proof. Enough proof to hang a ministry." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "We've been racing a war fleet across a reef to steal a filing cabinet."

"A filing cabinet worth more than the gold would've been," Ovid said quietly. "Gold spends. This *convicts*."

"That's why Reyes wants the wreck buried," Sefa said. "Not recovered. Buried. You can't buy back paper once it's read."

The wind picked up across the deck, carrying the smell of rain from the southwest. Nobody commented on it. Everybody thought about it.

"Tamsin." Brannoc's voice was careful. "The letter."

She looked at it. The oilcloth, triple-wrapped. Her mother's name in her mother's hand.

*Addressed to no one,* she'd thought. But that wasn't true, was it? It was addressed to whoever opened the door. And who opened the door? A salvage diver with a borrowed chart and a dying man's promise. A diver whose mother had died in the antechamber with her hand on the wheel.

Her mother couldn't have known that. Couldn't have known it would be Tamsin.

And yet.

"Tamsin," Sefa said softly. "You don't have to read it here. You don't have to read it today."

"Yes, I do." Tamsin picked up the packet. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "I promised a dying man I'd finish what he started. Whatever's in this letter is the rest of what he started. And if I wait — if I sleep on it, if I let it sit in a dry bag overnight — I'll talk myself into opening it tomorrow, and the next day, and one day the monsoon takes the boat and it goes to the bottom a second time." She managed something that was almost a smile. "I'm not letting the sea have her twice."

She unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers that wouldn't stay steady. Inside was a single sheet, folded once, sealed with a drop of candle wax pressed with a thumbprint — her mother's thumbprint, she realized, a signature for a woman who trusted prints more than names.

She broke the seal.

The paper was dry. After nineteen years in a sealed room behind a waxed wrap, impossibly, perfectly dry. Her mother had known how to preserve things. Her mother had planned for decades.

Tamsin read.

She read it once fast, the way you take a blow. Then again slowly, the way you take a wound. The crew watched her face do things none of them had seen it do — not in the riptide, not at the skeleton in the antechamber, not once in all the weeks since Kestrel Point.

When she finished, she lowered the page and looked at her crew — her ragged, impossible, borrowed crew — and the look on her face made Brannoc take a half-step back.

"What is it?" Sefa whispered. "What does it say?"

Tamsin read it aloud. Her voice only broke twice.

---

*Tamsin —*

*If you're reading this, then either the sea finally gave up its grudge, or someone finished what I started, and either way I hope they were kinder to you than the sea was to me.*

*I got here. I want you to know that first, because I know how you'll worry. I opened the room. I read everything. And Tamsin — there is no fortune. There never was. The Corvane carried stolen lives in boxes, and the men who stole them are still eating dinner off the proceeds. I sat in this room for six hours deciding what a person does with a truth this heavy, and here is what I decided: you don't sell it. You don't trade it. You carry it to the people it belongs to, even if carrying it costs you everything, because the moment you sell it, you become one of the men who stole it.*

*I was going to bring it up myself. But someone on my crew told Captain Reyes where we were diving, and by the time I understood that, the fleet was on the horizon and my air was half gone and I made the choice any diver makes: the truth goes somewhere it can survive, and I go wherever I go.*

*So I'm leaving this where it can be found by whoever comes next. If it's a stranger: finish it. Give it to the families. Don't sell it, don't trade it, don't let anyone in a uniform tell you it's a navy matter.*

*And if it's you, Tamsin — my girl, my stubborn, brilliant girl — then I'm sorry. Sorry you had to find me this way. Sorry about your father, who I now believe was right about everything. And sorry most of all for this: trust your crew with your life, because you'll have to, but check everything twice. Somebody sold me. Somebody always sells somebody. Love them anyway. Just count the silverware.*

*Come find me when you're old. I'll be in the good water.*

*— Your mother, who stayed*

---

The deck was silent except for the wind and the creak of the hull.

Tamsin folded the letter along its original creases, exactly, precisely, and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it sat like a coal.

"Somebody sold her," Brannoc said at last, hoarse. "On her own crew."

"I know."

"Somebody on *our* boat could—" He stopped himself, but too late; the thought was loose among them now, moving from face to face like something contagious.

"There's no transmitter," Sefa said quickly. "We checked. Brannoc tore the engine apart."

"We checked the *engine*," Brannoc said. "We didn't check the people."

"Nobody on this boat sold anything," Ovid said, rising, and for the first time the washed-up archivist sounded like a man with a spine. "I've kept worse secrets than anyone here for longer than anyone here, and I gave mine up freely. Sefa gave up hers. Brannoc—"

"Brannoc is standing right here," Brannoc said, "and Brannoc would like to point out that we are all very tired, very scared, and holding the single most dangerous piece of paper in the colony while a war fleet sits a quarter-mile away. Maybe nobody accuses anybody until everybody's slept."

Tamsin looked at each of them in turn. Sefa, jaw tight, hands balled. Ovid, pale and resolute. Brannoc, arms crossed, engineering the argument like he engineered everything — load-bearing, reluctant to fail.

Her mother's letter burned against her ribs. *Love them anyway. Just count the silverware.*

"Nobody's accusing anybody," Tamsin said. "Tonight. But tomorrow, before the last tide, we go through this boat rivet by rivet, and then we go through the manifest of our own lives — who knew what, who talked to whom in port, all of it. Because Reyes hasn't moved that dredge in six hours, and a woman like Ilsa Reyes doesn't stop unless she's already sure of something." She looked southwest, where the rain-smell was strengthening into a promise. "And I intend to find out what she's sure of before the sea decides this for all of us."

Below their keel, in the dark and the cold, the strongroom stood open and empty — emptied of everything except the one thing no fleet could dredge up and no charge could bury, which was currently riding over the swell in the pocket of a woman who had just learned that the treasure was never gold.

It was a letter. And it was a warning.

And somewhere out in the darkening water, Ilsa Reyes was already counting on the second one arriving too late.
