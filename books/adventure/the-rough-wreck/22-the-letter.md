## 22. The Letter

The strongroom smelled of iron and old salt, the way a grave smells when the sea has been keeping it.

Tamsin Montrose hung in the doorway of the compartment she had spent five tides and most of her blood trying to open, and did not move. Behind her, the guide line swayed with the swell a hundred feet above. Ahead of her, on a shelf that some careful hand had cleared of silt thirty years ago, sat a brass tube, a bundle of oilcloth-wrapped documents bound in waxed cord, and an envelope. The envelope was soft with age, its corners rounded by decades of cold water, and across its face, in handwriting Tamsin would have known anywhere — in handwriting she had last seen on a grocery list pinned to a galley door when she was nine years old — was written:

*Tamsin.*

Not *To whom it may concern.* Not *For the families.* Just her name, the M looped the way her mother always looped it, like a wave deciding whether to break.

Her regulator hissed. Her gauges said she had eleven minutes of bottom time and no business spending any of them standing still. She took the letter anyway.

It went into her suit's inner pocket, against her chest, where her heartbeat could argue with it all the way up.

* * *

She didn't read it on deck. She meant to — Brannoc was there, and Sefa, and Ovid, all three of them crowding the rail as she came over the side, all three of them wearing the particular expression of people who have spent six hours cranking a winch made of salvage and prayer and want very badly for it to have been worth something.

"Well?" Brannoc demanded. His hands were shredded from the wheel bar; he'd wrapped them in tape that was more tape than hand at this point. "Is it gold? Tell me it's gold. I've been imagining gold for two days. There's a specific kind of gold I've been imagining, with little—"

"It's not gold," Tamsin said.

The three faces fell in perfect unison, which under other circumstances would have been funny.

"It's paper," she said. "Documents. A confession, maybe — Ovid, you'll want to look. And—" Her hand went to her pocket without her permission. "And something else."

"Something else," Sefa repeated. At sixteen, she had already mastered the art of making three words sound like an interrogation.

"A letter." Tamsin climbed onto the deck proper, legs unsteady, the Kestrel's Wake rolling beneath her in that familiar rotten-wood way. "From my mother."

Nobody spoke. Out past the reef, a quarter mile off, Reyes's dredge chewed at the waterline with a sound like a giant grinding its teeth, but nobody spoke about that either.

Ovid recovered first, because Ovid had spent thirty years recovering first in archive rooms full of terrible news. He took off his spectacles, polished them on a shirt that needed it more than the spectacles did, and put them back on. "Then you should read it," he said gently. "Alone, if you need."

"I don't need—"

"Tamsin." Brannoc, quieter now. "Go read your mother's letter. We'll guard the paper. That's the whole job today. Guarding paper." He managed half a smile through his exhaustion. "We're very good at guarding paper. It's the only thing we've successfully done all week."

She wanted to argue. She was, she realized distantly, always wanting to argue lately — with Brannoc about margins, with Sefa about currents, with Ovid about everything, because Ovid's confession was still a splinter in the crew's palm that every grip pressed deeper. But her chest hurt where the envelope sat, and so she went below, into the little cabin that smelled of diesel and Brannoc's engine grease, and she shut the door, and she opened the letter with hands that shook worse than they had at ninety feet.

* * *

*Tamsin,*

*If you're reading this, then either you found the Corvane or you found my things in a pawnshop, and I'm sorry for both, because one means I'm dead and the other means I got desperate, and I promised myself I wouldn't get desperate again after the thing with the horse.*

Tamsin laughed. It came out wrong, wet and cracked, and she pressed her fist to her mouth. The horse. God. The horse had been before Tamsin was born — a racehorse her mother had briefly owned, briefly, catastrophically, in partnership with a man who turned out not to own it either. Her mother had told the story a hundred times, always ending the same way: *Never trust anything that eats better than you do.*

*I'll be quick, because air is air even on paper. I found the strongroom. I found what Voss sent me to find. It isn't treasure, love. Or rather — it is, there's enough coin down here to buy a fleet, and I sat with it for a long time and thought about your school fees and a house with a door that locks, and then I read the documents underneath the coin, and I stopped thinking about doors.*

*The governor stole it. All of it. The Meridian treasury, the pension funds, the widows' allotments — he loaded it aboard the Corvane and wrote a confession saying so, sealed it, and planned to trade it later for his neck. The navy knows. Some part of the navy has always known. That's why the wreck is unlisted. That's why the charts lie.*

*I'm taking the documents up. Not the gold — I know how that sounds, coming from me, but the gold is bait, Tamsin. Anyone who surfaces with it becomes a thief, and thieves can be silenced cheaply. Documents are different. Documents have to be answered. I'm going to give them to the families, and then we're going home, and you're going to stop asking me why we never stay anywhere, because I finally understand it. We were never running from places. We were running toward this.*

*One more thing, and I hate to write it, but if these papers reach you instead of Meridian, then something went wrong, and I want you to know what: someone on my crew sold me to Captain Reyes. I don't know who. I found the transmitter yesterday — navy issue, hidden in the ballast — and I haven't said a word to anyone, because I don't know who to say it to. Six people on this boat and I can't trust one of them with the truth, and that is the loneliest sentence I have ever written, and I once wrote a eulogy for the horse.*

*So listen to me. If you ever have a crew — and you will, because you'll pretend you don't need one and then you'll drown without one — watch what they do when the plan costs them something. Greed shows itself early. Fear shows itself late. And the ones who volunteer too eagerly to be trusted are the ones who've already decided they need forgiving.*

*Come find me down here someday, if you must. But live first. Live a lot first.*

*All my love, always,*
*Mum*

*P.S. The combination to the strongroom is your birthday. I know that's sentimental. I don't care. Sentiment is the only cargo that never spoils.*

* * *

Tamsin read it four times.

The first time she read it as a daughter. The second time she read it as a diver, mapping her mother's last hours into the wreck she now knew room by room — the flooded lower decks, the knife in the bulkhead, the skeleton at the wheel with its hand still resting where the letter said it would be. The third time she read it as a captain, and that was when the cold started.

Because the fourth time, she wasn't reading her mother's words anymore. She was hearing them in her own voice.

*Someone on my crew sold me to Captain Reyes.*

Brannoc, who had built their winch out of parts whose provenance he'd waved away with a joke. Sefa, who had appeared out of nowhere with a secret she still hadn't fully told, who had recognized Reyes's scout ship from her own past and said nothing until cornered. Ovid, who had catalogued the looted treasury with his own hands and stayed silent for thirty years, who had joined the crew — by his own admission — to atone, and who had been approached by a navy liaison in port, everyone knew it, everyone had pretended not to know it.

*Watch what they do when the plan costs them something.*

Ovid had cost them something. He'd nearly broken the crew apart with his confession, and Tamsin had forgiven him because forgiveness was easier than suspicion, and suspicion was a poison she'd sworn off somewhere around the time she'd decided never to belong to anything.

But her mother had sworn things off too. Her mother had written *I can't trust one of them with the truth* in a steel room at the bottom of the sea, alone, surrounded by gold she refused to touch, betrayed by a face she'd shared meals with.

And Ede Voss. Voss, who had known her name from her mother's old crew. Who had traded a map for a promise with his dying breath. Whose effects still sat in a sea chest in the hold because nobody had gotten around to going through them properly.

Tamsin folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, the way you handle something that has already survived one catastrophe and shouldn't be asked to survive carelessness. She sat with it in her lap for a long moment while the Kestrel's Wake creaked around her, and above her head, muffled through the deck, she could hear her crew — Brannoc's laugh, big and unguarded; Sefa's voice rising in some argument about dinner; Ovid's dry counterpoint underneath.

They sounded like a family. That was the thing. They sounded exactly like a family.

So had her mother's crew, presumably. Right up until the transmitter in the ballast.

* * *

She came up on deck at dusk with the letter in a waterproof sleeve inside her jacket and her face arranged into something neutral, and discovered that arranging your face is a skill that decays without practice, because Sefa looked at her for approximately one and a half seconds before saying, "You read it."

"I read it."

"And?"

"And it confirms the documents are real. The governor's confession is down there. The whole thing." Tamsin leaned against the rail, facing the water rather than the crew, which she told herself was tactical and knew was cowardice. "Reyes can't let it surface. That's why the dredge. That's why the charges we heard today. She's not salvaging the Corvane. She's burying it."

"Charges?" Brannoc straightened. "What charges? Nobody said charges."

"The dredging pattern," Ovid murmured. He'd gone pale in the way he did when an archival instinct connected to a present-tense horror. "If you place demolition charges along the reef shelf at low tide, the collapse would take the stern section down into the scoured channel. Irrecoverable. Even for us." He removed his spectacles. "Even for the navy, afterward. She'd be destroying any chance of recovery permanently."

"She's destroying evidence," Tamsin said. "Permanently is the point."

Silence, except the dredge, far off, grinding.

"So we go down tomorrow," Sefa said. "Fifth tide. Get the documents before—"

"We already have the documents," Tamsin said, and felt three pairs of eyes snap to her, and understood immediately that she had made a mistake, because a captain who says *we* and means *I* only gets away with it until someone notices the seam.

"You—" Brannoc frowned. "You brought them up? When? You were down ten minutes past your margin as it was—"

"The letter was with them. The documents are safe." She kept her voice level. "That's what matters."

"That's not what matters," Sefa said slowly, "that's not — Tamsin, you went into the strongroom, cracked a seal Brannoc nearly lost fingers on, wrapped up thirty years of evidence, and swam it back up a guide line past your margin, and you're telling us about it now, standing at the rail, facing the water?" The girl's voice wasn't angry. It was worse than angry. It was hurt, and sharp with it. "Why are you facing the water?"

"I'm not facing the—"

"You are. You've been facing the water since you came up. You only turn around when Brannoc talks, and you answer him sideways." Sefa crossed her arms. "I read currents, remember? People are just slow ones."

Brannoc laughed — reflexively, uncomfortably — and stopped when nobody joined him. "Tamsin. Love. What's in the letter?"

And there it was. The question, hanging in the dusk like a hook.

She could tell them. It was the obvious move, the warm move, the move the woman she was trying to become would make: sit the crew down, share her mother's words, let the grief be communal, let the warning be communal. *Watch what they do when the plan costs them something.* Trust them with the truth and see.

But her mother had trusted, and her mother was at the bottom of the sea with her hand on a wheel.

And one of the three people currently looking at her with worry, with hurt, with patient scholarly concern, might — might — be the reason her mother had died alone. Might be reporting their position right now, tonight, to a flagship whose searchlights she could almost see through the darkening haze. Might be volunteering, eagerly, to be trusted.

*The ones who volunteer too eagerly to be trusted are the ones who've already decided they need forgiving.*

Ovid had volunteered his confession. Sefa had volunteered her secret, eventually, under pressure. Brannoc —

Brannoc was watching her now with those big ruined hands wrapped in tape, and saying, in a voice gone quiet and careful, "Tamsin. What did she say?"

"Nothing I can share yet," Tamsin heard herself say.

The word *yet* was supposed to soften it. It didn't. It landed on the deck between them like something dropped, and rolled, and everybody watched it roll.

Sefa's chin came up. Ovid's mouth thinned behind his beard. And Brannoc — Brannoc just nodded, slowly, twice, the way a man nods when a load he's been carrying shifts in a way he doesn't like, and said, "Right. Well. I'll check the winch," and walked aft, and the sound of his boots on the deck was louder than it needed to be, the way sounds are when everyone is listening to them.

Tamsin stood at the rail and faced the water, because facing the water was easier, and hated herself with a thoroughness that would have impressed her mother.

Out in the dark, the dredge had stopped.

She noticed it the way Sefa would have — not as a fact but as a change in pressure, a missing tooth in the night's rhythm. The grinding that had been the constant bass note of the last two days was simply gone, and in its absence the sea sounded enormous, and close, and full of things moving quietly.

She turned around. Ovid was at the chart table with a lamp, pretending to study reef soundings. Sefa was in the bow, small and rigid, staring back at the flagship's distant lights. And aft, in the engine housing's open throat, Brannoc's lamp moved — checking the winch, he'd said, except the winch was forward, and the engine housing was where a transmitter would fit, if a person were the sort of person who thought about transmitters.

*Someone on my crew sold me to Captain Reyes.*

Or maybe the dredge had simply finished for the night. Maybe charges took time to place, and Reyes was being methodical. Maybe the silence out there meant nothing at all.

Maybe.

Tamsin Montrose, who had spent her whole life believing that the only safe number of people to trust was zero, stood on the deck of the boat that had taught her otherwise, holding her dead mother's letter against her ribs, and understood with sick clarity that the promise she'd made to a dying stranger had just handed her a second, harder test:

Find the truth about the Corvane.

And figure out which of the people she loved was lying about everything else.

Below decks, faintly, she heard Brannoc's lamp click off — and then, a moment later, click back on.
