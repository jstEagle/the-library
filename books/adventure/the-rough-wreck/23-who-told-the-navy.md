## 23. Who Told the Navy

The letter sat in the middle of the chart table like a dead thing they'd all agreed not to look at.

Tamsin had read it four times since dawn. Her mother's handwriting hadn't changed in twenty years — the same impatient slant, the same way she crossed her sevens as if daring them to be mistaken for ones. *I know now who sold us. I will not say it here, in case this letter travels where I cannot.* And then nothing. No name. Just that sentence, hanging, and Tamsin's own imagination filling the blank with every face aboard.

She'd slept maybe two hours. She could hear the others moving around above her — Brannoc's boots on the engine housing, the particular clank of a man working too loudly on purpose, and Sefa's voice running through current tables in a low murmur, the way she did when she was nervous. Ovid was silent, which from Ovid meant either deep thought or deep guilt, and lately there was no telling which.

The monsoon was two days out, maybe three. The strongroom stood open behind them, its documents sealed in brass, its single letter opened and read. They should have been celebrating. Instead Tamsin climbed into the wheelhouse, set the letter down where everyone could see it, and said the thing she'd been chewing on all night.

"Somebody talk to me about Reyes."

* * *

Brannoc came up first, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last month. He looked at the letter, then at Tamsin, and his jaw worked like he was grinding coffee.

"You think one of us told her."

"I think somebody knew where we were diving before we ever left port," Tamsin said. "I think her scout ship found us on the first tide, in open water, with no broadcast and no lights. I think her dredge went straight for the reef channel like it had our heading written on it." She kept her voice level. It took effort. "I'm not accusing anyone. I'm asking."

"Then ask better," Brannoc said, "because what you're doing is standing there looking at us like a customs officer."

Ovid emerged from below with a mug of tea he would not drink, only hold, for warmth and something to do with his hands. "There is a third possibility," he offered carefully, "which is that Captain Reyes is simply very good at her work."

"She's good," Tamsin said. "She's not psychic."

Sefa came last, folding herself onto the bench by the window with her knees drawn up, saying nothing, watching everything. She'd been quiet since the letter. Quieter than usual, which for Sefa was saying something, because usual was already a kind of silence — the silence of a girl who'd learned early that the safest place in any room was the corner where nobody remembered you were standing.

"So let's be logical," Ovid said, settling into the tone he used for cataloguing, which under the circumstances was either comforting or infuriating. "Who could have known? Voss gave you the chart alone. The crew assembled after. The dive plan was posted—"

"On the bulkhead," Brannoc said. "In pencil. Behind a door."

"The refit involved eleven borrowed parts from six different yards," Ovid went on, counting on his fingers. "Any of those yards could have talked. The harbor master saw our stores manifest. The chandler saw our air bottles. Half the port saw us load a decompression chamber through the front of a fish market, which, I'll remind everyone, was *your* idea, Brannoc, and drew rather a crowd."

"It was efficient."

"It was a parade."

"And yet," Brannoc said, turning to face them all, arms crossed, "here we are, dancing around the obvious. One of us has a past. All of us have pasts. Hers—" he jerked his chin at Sefa, and Tamsin felt the whole room tilt toward the girl like iron filings finding north.

Sefa didn't flinch. That was the thing about her; Tamsin had seen her take worse than this and not flinch. But something moved behind her eyes, some quick recalculation, like a diver checking her gauge.

"Say it," Sefa said quietly.

"You turned up out of nowhere with hands that know navy signals," Brannoc said. "You read that scout ship's pennant flags before I even saw the mast. You knew the Meridian channels no chart shows. You've got more training than half my old engineering crews and you're *seventeen*, and every time Reyes comes up you go quiet as a bilge pump."

The wheelhouse was very still. Somewhere below, the engine ticked as it cooled, patient as a clock.

"Sefa," Tamsin said, and hated how her own voice sounded — flat, official, her mother's voice, or worse, Reyes's. "Is there something you want to tell us?"

For a long moment Sefa looked at the letter on the table. At her mother's slanted sevens. Then she looked up, and her chin came up with it, and Tamsin thought: *there she is.*

"Yeah," Sefa said. "There's plenty I want to tell you. Starting with the fact that I've been on this boat since before the Corvane was anything but a story, and I've bailed seawater out of your bilge with my bare hands at three in the morning, and I dove relay on the strongroom wheel until my fingers split. So if you're going to put me off this boat over a feeling, Captain, you'd better be sure of the feeling."

"I'm not putting you off the boat."

"You're thinking about it." Sefa's voice didn't rise. Somehow that was worse. "Everyone in this room is thinking about it. Ovid's thinking it politely. Brannoc's thinking it loudly. You're thinking it in your mother's handwriting." She unfolded from the bench and stood, small and straight-backed in the gray morning light. "So here's what I'll give you for free. Yes, I recognized that scout ship. Yes, I know things I shouldn't. And no, I am not ready to tell you why, because the last time I told someone why, it ended badly for people I loved, and I don't yet trust this boat with it. Ask me again when you've decided whether I'm crew or cargo."

She walked out. The hatch banged behind her, and the sound of it sat in the wheelhouse like a fourth person.

"Well," said Ovid into the silence. "That went poorly."

* * *

Brannoc found her an hour later in the engine room, wedged into the space beside the fuel lines where she liked to sit because it was warm and because nobody could see her face.

He didn't say anything at first. He just handed her a wrench — the wrong size, deliberately, their oldest joke, from the first week when she'd fixed his coolant pump and he'd pretended to be offended — and sat down across from her with his back against the hull.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

"For what?"

"For pointing at you because pointing at you was easier than pointing at myself." He turned the rag over in his hands. "I've been sitting with it all morning. Who told the navy. And you know what keeps coming up? Nobody on this boat had anything to gain. We're not treasure hunters, any of us. We're strays. What would any of us sell them — the promise?"

"Reyes doesn't need gain," Tamsin said from the hatchway. She'd come down without either of them hearing, which was unlike her, which meant she'd wanted to hear. "She needs leverage. She needs to know where we are so she can be somewhere first." She slid down the bulkhead to sit with them, three of them crammed in the warm dark among the pipes, which was ridiculous and also exactly right. "And I owe both of you an apology too. I handled it like a naval inquiry. My mother used to say suspicion is a leak — you can't plug it from outside, you have to find where it's actually coming in."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have made a decent engineer," Brannoc said.

"She'd have made a terrible one. She never met a system she didn't want to argue with." Tamsin looked at the two of them, at the grease and the worry and the stubbornness, and felt the fracture line in the day begin, just barely, to knit. "We find Sefa. We apologize properly. And then we stop hunting each other, because whatever's feeding Reyes information, it isn't loyalty we lack. It's time."

They were still sitting there, none of them moving, when the engine coughed.

It wasn't a dramatic cough. Just a stutter, a half-second hiccup in the idle, the kind of thing Brannoc heard the way other people heard their own name. His head came up. "That's new."

"That's bad-new or interesting-new?" Tamsin asked.

"There's no such thing as interesting-new." He hauled himself up, cracked the main housing panel, and leaned in with a work lamp, muttering the private litany of syllables he reserved for machinery that disappointed him. Tamsin watched his shoulders go stiff, then stiffer, then absolutely rigid.

"Brannoc?"

He didn't answer. He reached deep into the housing, past the fuel governor, into the bracketed void behind the flywheel where no part of any engine had any business being, and came out holding something small and dark and caked in grease.

He held it up to the lamp. A cylinder, palm-length, with a stub antenna and a casing stamped with a serial number that thirty years in navy yards had taught him to read the way sailors read weather.

"A transmitter," he said. His voice had gone very quiet, which from Brannoc was louder than shouting. "Hardwired into my ignition circuit. Drawing off my battery. Sitting *there* since—" He stopped. Tamsin watched him do the arithmetic, watched it land. "Since the refit. Since before the refit. This housing was stripped to bare metal in Kestrel Point. There was nowhere to hide a hairpin in it, let alone this."

The three of them looked at the thing dangling from his fingers, dripping grease onto the deck plates.

"It wasn't one of us," Tamsin said slowly. The relief came first, huge and shameful, and then the horror arrived right behind it and settled in. "It was in the boat before we ever left port."

"Which means," Brannoc said, turning the transmitter over, reading the stamp again as if hoping it would say something different, "she's known our position every hour since the first tide. Every dive. Every channel. Every night at anchor." He looked up at Tamsin, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man whose engine — his engine, his one clean pride in a ruined life — had been used against the only people he had left.

"Tamsin," he said. "This is navy issue. Old pattern, but navy issue. Somebody put this here who had access to the yard, access to the hull, and a reason to want us followed from the minute we cleared the breakwater."

Above them, faint through the deck, they could hear Sefa's footsteps crossing the wheelhouse, and beyond her, far out on the graying water, the low shape of a scout ship holding station at the edge of visibility — patient, unhurried, exactly where it had always been.

Tamsin took the transmitter from Brannoc's hand. It was lighter than it should have been, somehow, for the weight of what it meant.

"Get Sefa," she said. "Both of you. We need to talk about who's been watching us — and how long they've known we'd find the Corvane before we did."
