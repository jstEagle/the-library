## 24. The Transmitter

Brannoc had spent thirty years learning to listen to engines, and in that time he had developed a theory that every machine was honest in the way that drunk uncles are honest: loudly, at the worst possible moment, and about things you'd rather they kept to themselves.

The Kestrel's Wake's engine had been muttering all morning.

Not the good mutter — the deep, contented churn of a well-fed diesel — but a thin, irregular tick buried under the rhythm, like a man tapping a spoon against a cup in another room. Brannoc had heard it twice yesterday and blamed it on the fuel pump. He had blamed it on the fuel pump the way you blame a creaky stair on the house: because the alternative explanations were all worse.

He lay in the engine bay with a rag under his chest and a mirror on a stick, in the posture that had defined most of his adult life — flat on his belly in a hot, greasy space, arguing with machinery that did not respect him.

"Talk to me," he murmured. "Come on, girl. What's your trouble."

The tick came again. Faint. Metallic. Wrong.

He worked the mirror deeper into the housing gap behind the flywheel cover, a space no engineer should ever need to reach and which the original designers had clearly sealed out of spite, and the mirror caught the lamplight and threw it back at him in a shape he did not like at all.

A shape with edges. A shape with intent.

Brannoc lay still for a long moment, breathing engine-smell and dread, and then he said, very quietly, the way a man says a thing so he can pretend afterward that he said it to himself:

"Oh, you have got to be joking."

* * *

He did not go straight to Tamsin. That was the discipline of it, the part he was quietly proud of. A lesser engineer would have gone topside shouting. Brannoc instead did what he always did when he found something in an engine that should not be there: he got a second opinion from the only colleague he trusted, which was himself, at greater length.

He pulled the inspection cover properly. He drained the bilge sump to get room to work. He made Sefa — who had appeared at the hatch with two mugs of tea and the unerring instinct of teenagers for finding the exact wrong moment to be helpful — hold the lamp.

"What is it?" Sefa asked.

"It's a tick," Brannoc said.

"Engines tick."

"Engines tick *in rhythm*. This is ticking *off* rhythm, which means it's not the engine. Which means it's something that doesn't belong." He reached in with two fingers, careful as a surgeon, and his fingertips found a casing that was warm — not engine-warm, not friction-warm, but the specific, modest warmth of a thing that had been running on its own power for days. "Sefa. Put the lamp closer and don't touch anything."

"Is it a bomb?"

"Why would it be a bomb?"

"Because everything on this boat is either a bomb or a betrayal. Ovid told me. He said it's called foreshadowing."

"Ovid needs to stop narrating our lives." Brannoc worked the casing loose with a flat screwdriver and a quarter-turn of courage, and it came free into his palm, and he held it up to the light.

It was a transmitter. He knew it the way he knew a face from an old warrant notice — squat, olive-grey, a stub antenna folded flat against its spine, a pressure-sealed case with the serial number stencilled along the flank in that particular navy stencil, the one where the sevens were crossed and the letters leaned like tired sailors.

He knew the model. That was the worst part. He'd serviced them, years ago, back when he still had a rating and a future. Navy issue. Fleet-standard hull-tracking beacon, the kind you bolted to a vessel you wanted to find again — a tender you'd lent out, a smuggler you were letting run, a ship you intended to follow home to its friends.

The kind you bolted to a ship *before* it left port.

"Brannoc?" Sefa said. "Your face has gone interesting."

"Has it."

"You've gone the colour of the bilge water."

He set the transmitter on the rag with the gentleness of a man handling a sleeping snake, and he sat back on his heels, and he looked at it, and the two halves of the truth arrived in his chest at the same moment and refused to share the space.

The relief came first, and it was enormous. *It wasn't one of them.* It wasn't Sefa with her hidden past and her too-good reading of currents. It wasn't Ovid, who had already confessed to one silence and carried his guilt around like a trunk he'd forgotten how to set down. It wasn't Tamsin, or the ghost of Tamsin's mother, or any of the small suspicions that had been gnawing through the crew since the letter had come up out of the strongroom like a curse. Nobody on the Kestrel's Wake had sold them out. Nobody had slipped ashore in the night. The family — and he had begun, with great private embarrassment, to think of them as a family — was intact.

And then the horror arrived, and it was worse than the relief was good, because the horror had arithmetic in it.

*Before they ever left port.* Before Voss died. Before the promise. Before the first tide. Which meant Reyes — or whoever served her — had marked the Kestrel's Wake as a ship worth following before Tamsin had even finished recruiting a crew. Before Brannoc had finished welding. Before the paint was dry, someone had crawled into this engine bay in some dark Kestrel Point boathouse and bolted a beacon to the frame of a rotten survey boat that no sane fleet would bother to track.

Which meant they had never been ahead. Not for one single tide. Every channel Sefa had threaded, every night run, every smug hour of separation they'd bought off the reef — all of it had been theatre, performed for an audience that already knew the ending.

"Brannoc," Sefa said. "You've gone from bilge-water colour to no colour at all. Should I get Tamsin?"

"Yes," Brannoc said. "Get everyone. And Sefa —" he caught her arm at the ladder, "— nobody touches this. Nobody says the word *transmitter* above decks. We do this quiet."

"Why quiet?"

"Because if I'm right about what this is, then the only thing we still own is the fact that *they don't know we know*." He looked at the little olive-grey thing on its rag, ticking its small, faithful, treacherous tick. "And I'd like to keep owning it for a few more hours."

* * *

They gathered in the galley, which was the only room on the boat where an argument could be held without one participant standing in the rain. Tamsin sat with the transmitter on the table between them, and nobody touched it, the way nobody touches the centre of a table when the centre of the table is the problem.

Ovid had put on his spectacles. He did this for examinations of evidence, and also, Brannoc suspected, for examinations of conscience. He leaned close, read the stencil, and went pale in the specific way of a man whose memory has just handed him a bill.

"Type Forty-One hull beacon," Ovid said. "Fleet standard for — I catalogued them, years ago, in the logistics annex. Issued for long-range tracking of vessels of interest. Battery life is rated at —" he closed his eyes, retrieving it, "— ninety days. Sealed unit. You can't switch it off from the receiving end; you have to find it and kill it."

"Ninety days," Tamsin said.

"From installation."

Brannoc did the arithmetic out loud, because someone had to, and because saying numbers made them smaller. "We refitted over eleven days in Kestrel Point. We've been at sea — what, nineteen? Thirty days, give or take, since someone put this in the housing. It's got sixty left in it. Maybe more. It's been reporting our position the whole time."

"Every tide," Sefa said quietly.

"Every tide," Brannoc agreed. "Every reef run. The night we thought we'd lost them in the maze —" he had to stop, because his voice did something embarrassing. "We didn't lose them. We *showed* them. We gave them the channels, Sefa. Your channels. The ones no chart shows."

Sefa had gone very still, in the way she went still when the past reached up and grabbed her ankle. Brannoc saw it and gentled his voice, which for Brannoc meant lowering it from a bark to a growl.

"Nobody knew," he said. "That's the point. That's the *only* point. It wasn't you. It wasn't Ovid. It wasn't any of us."

"It was the boat," Tamsin said.

They all looked at her. She hadn't touched the transmitter. She was looking at it the way she looked at a wreck through green water — measuring it, hating it, understanding it.

"It was the boat," she said again. "They tagged the *boat*, not the crew. Which means they didn't suspect any of us. They suspected *the promise*. Somebody at Kestrel Point knew Voss had passed something on, and they knew the Kestrel's Wake was the ship it left on, and they tagged her before we ever cleared the harbour." Her jaw tightened. "Reyes has had our position since before I recruited Brannoc. Before I recruited any of you. She's watched the whole thing. Every dive. Every mistake. She's been *letting us work*."

"Letting us find the strongroom for her," Ovid said. "Letting us open what she couldn't open without explaining why she wanted it opened."

"Letting us bleed for it," Sefa said.

The galley was quiet. Rain ticked on the deck above, in rhythm, which Brannoc noted sourly was more than could be said for his engine.

"So what do we do?" Sefa asked. "Smash it?"

"No," said Brannoc and Tamsin, at the same moment, and then looked at each other, and Tamsin nodded him on, because it was his engine bay and his find.

"Smashing it tells them," Brannoc said. "The moment that signal dies, whoever's listening knows we found it, and they know we know, and every hour of advantage we've got left evaporates. Right now, Reyes thinks we're blind. She thinks her little beetle in my engine bay is still whispering to her. That's worth more than the boat." He rubbed his face with a hand that left a grease stripe across one eyebrow, which nobody mentioned, because the crew had learned that mentioning Brannoc's grease was a privilege you earned. "We don't kill it. We *lie* to it."

Ovid looked up. "Lie to it how?"

"We move it." Brannoc was already talking with his hands, sketching in the air, and the others leaned in because watching Brannoc explain an idea was like watching a man juggle with his elbows — ungainly, improbable, and weirdly compelling. "The beacon reports a position. Fine. Let it report a position — a *wrong* one. I can't stop it transmitting, but I can change what it's transmitting *about*. I wrap the antenna, I put the unit in a pressure case, and I stow it somewhere that isn't the Kestrel's Wake. A buoy. A salvage float. Something that drifts on a current heading somewhere we are not."

"You'd make it a ghost," Sefa said, and something lit up in her face — the same light she got reading water. "We let it sail on without us. It keeps ticking, Reyes keeps following it, and it leads her fleet chasing a float through open water while we —"

"While we go the other way," Tamsin said. "Quiet. Cold engine when we can. No lights."

"There's a catch," Brannoc said, because there was always a catch, and he'd rather say it himself than let the sea say it first. "The moment we separate from the beacon, we're dark. No signal, no tracking, nothing. If we get into trouble out there, nobody finds us. Nobody's *looking* for us — that's the whole idea. And if Reyes figures out the trick — if she sends a boat to check on the signal and finds a float bobbing in the shipping lanes —" he shrugged. "Then she knows exactly what we did, and exactly which direction we *didn't* go, and she stops being patient."

"How long do we have before she checks?" Tamsin asked.

Ovid took his spectacles off and cleaned them on his shirt, which was what he did instead of sighing. "She's a careful commander running an illegal demolition under ministerial orders. She checks her instruments. I'd say — a day. Perhaps two. She'll want to verify before she commits the fleet to a bearing."

"Then we have a day," Tamsin said, "to get somewhere she isn't."

"Which is where?" Sefa asked. "Because every port in the archipelago has a harbourmaster who reads navy signals."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment, looking at the transmitter, and Brannoc watched her and understood — not for the first time, but with a new weight — that the woman was carrying the whole map in her head, and the whole promise, and the whole dead weight of a mother and a mentor and a man named Voss, and that she did it without ever setting any of it down on a table.

"Meridian," she said at last. "We were always going to end at Meridian. The families. The documents. That was the promise, and the promise doesn't care what the navy's doing." She looked around the galley, at each of them, and Brannoc felt the look land on him like a hand on the shoulder. "We stop running from her and start running *at* the thing she's afraid of. She can't bury the truth if it's already been delivered."

"She can sink us before it's delivered," Brannoc said.

"Yes," Tamsin said. "She can. So we don't give her the chance." She pushed the transmitter an inch across the table toward him. "How fast can you build your ghost?"

Brannoc picked up the little olive-grey traitor and turned it over in his hands, and found, to his own surprise, that he wasn't angry at it anymore. It was just a machine doing what it was built to do, faithfully and without malice — the same as him, once, back when he wore the uniform. The difference between a beacon and a man, he thought, was that a man could learn to lie.

"Give me until the tide turns," he said. "And somebody find me a float that floats."

* * *

He worked through the afternoon in the engine bay with the hatch half-open and the rain coming in sideways, and the crew came by in relays, the way people do when a thing has frightened them and they want to stand near the person fixing it.

Ovid brought him tea and stayed to hold the lamp, and asked, in the careful voice he used for archives, "You're certain it was planted before we left? Not at the last port?"

"Certain." Brannoc tapped the housing bracket. "See the corrosion on the bolt heads? That's thirty days of salt at minimum. And the mounting position — whoever set it knew this hull. Knew the one blind spot behind the flywheel cover that the original survey team never opened. That's not a dockside opportunist, Ovid. That's someone who'd been aboard. Or someone who'd read her plans."

"The navy built her," Ovid said. "She was a survey vessel before she was a wreck. Her plans will be in every fleet annex from here to the capital."

"Which means it could have been anyone with a filing cabinet and a grudge."

"Or a captain with orders." Ovid said it softly, and then, softer still: "Brannoc — do you understand what this means? About the letter? About Tamsin's mother's crew?"

Brannoc's hands went still on the casing. He had been keeping that thought in a sealed compartment of his own, down behind the flywheel of his mind, and Ovid had just gone and opened the cover.

"I understand," he said, "that thirty years ago somebody on that crew sold her out, and we spent two days tearing each other apart wondering if it was one of us. And it turns out the rot wasn't in the crew at all. It never is. It's in the *system* — it's a beacon bolted to your bones before you even know you're being followed." He tightened the last bracket, harder than he needed to. "Tamsin's mother probably had one of these in her hull too. Or something like it. Something that told Reyes where she was every tide, right up until the tide she didn't come back from."

Ovid was quiet for a long moment. Rain drummed. The engine, freed of its passenger, ticked in honest rhythm at last.

"Then we'd better deliver those documents," Ovid said. "Before anyone bolts anything to us again."

"That," said Brannoc, "is the first sensible thing anyone's said on this boat all week."

* * *

At dusk, in a grey and heaving sea, they launched their ghost.

Sefa chose the float — an old salvage buoy from the forward locker, barnacled and honest, riding a current that ran southeast toward the open lanes. Brannoc sealed the transmitter in a pressure case with the antenna fed through a gasket he was not entirely proud of, and lashed it inside the buoy's frame where the sea could rock it and the fleet could chase it, and Tamsin herself leaned over the rail and set it adrift with both hands, the way you set down something heavy you've carried too far.

They watched it fall astern, small and orange and faithful, ticking its lie into the water.

"It's going to be fine," Sefa said, "right up until it isn't."

"That," Brannoc said, "is the most accurate navigation report I've heard all voyage."

They came about, cold and dark, and ran north into the rain with no beacon and no signal and no one in the world knowing where they were — not the fleet, not the navy, not God Himself, though Ovid muttered that God probably kept better charts.

And it might have worked. It might have worked beautifully.

It was Sefa, at the wheel in the small hours, who saw it first — a light far off the starboard quarter, where no light should be, where their ghost was *not*. She called Tamsin up. Tamsin called Brannoc. And the four of them stood in the rain at the stern rail, watching a white running light grow steadily out of the black, riding the exact bearing of the current, the exact line of the drift, the exact wake of their little orange lie — moving too fast for a buoy, too steady for the sea, and coming on like an answer to a question they had hoped nobody would think to ask.

"She checked," Tamsin said quietly.

Brannoc's stomach dropped through the bilge and out the keel. "She checked *already*."

The light held its bearing. Held it. Held it.

And then it flashed — three long, deliberate pulses, white against the rain, in a pattern that belonged to no lighthouse, no buoy, and no honest ship.

Ovid said, in a voice gone thin: "That's a fleet recognition signal. That's a *challenge*."

"She's not chasing the buoy," Sefa whispered. "She never was. She's been ahead of us the whole time — and she just turned on her lights to let us know."

Ahead of them, out of the rain, the running lights of a second vessel appeared to starboard. Then a third, off the port bow. Closing slow. Closing patient. The way you close on a ship you have already decided to take.

Tamsin put her hand on the wheel beside Sefa's, and Brannoc heard her say, calm as a woman reading a tide table: "Brannoc. How much of that engine have you got left?"

"Everything," he said, and went below to find out if it was true.
