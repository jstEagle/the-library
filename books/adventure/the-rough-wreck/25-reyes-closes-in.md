## 25. Reyes Closes In

The *Vigilant* did not so much sail through the building seas as argue with them, and Captain Ilsa Reyes had long ago decided that arguing with the sea was a young officer's game. You did not win it. You simply outlasted it, the way you outlasted everything else.

She stood on the flag bridge with her hands clasped behind her back and let the spray come. The glass showed a sky the color of wet slate, stacked low to the east where the monsoon was assembling itself like something patient and enormous that had all the time in the world. She did not have that kind of time. That was the whole trouble.

"Reef station reports the dredge is holding position," said Lieutenant Okonkwo, appearing at her elbow with the particular carefulness of a man who had learned that his captain's moods arrived like weather fronts — visible from a distance, best respected early. "Quarter-mile off the Corvane site. They're asking whether to begin the second cut."

"Tell them no."

"Captain?"

"Tell them they'll begin when I say, and not one bucket sooner." She kept her eyes on the horizon. "If they chew that reef apart before we know exactly where the strongroom sits, we may as well drop the whole history of the colonial treasury into a drain and flush it. I want the wreck located precisely. Then I want it gone."

Okonkwo wrote it down, because that was what lieutenants were for. "And the survey boat, ma'am? The Kestrel's Wake. She's still on station. Our last fix puts her—"

"I know where she is." Closer than he thought. Closer than the transmitter suggested, because Tamsin Montrose had never once in her life done the predictable thing, and Reyes had counted on that the way other captains counted on wind. "Signal the scout to shadow only. No contact. No warnings. If she runs, we follow. If she dives—" Reyes paused, and found that the pause had teeth in it. "If she dives, she's racing us, and I intend to win."

"Aye, Captain." He hesitated, which was unlike him. "Begging pardon, but the men are saying things. About the wreck. About why the navy wants a hundred-year-old hull plate buried instead of raised. Rumor's a funny cargo, ma'am. It leaks."

"Then plug the leak or get promoted off my ship," Reyes said mildly, and Okonkwo went, and she was alone with the sea and the slate-colored sky and the thing she had been carrying for twenty years, which weighed nothing and everything, depending on the hour.

* * *

In her cabin, the sea got louder. It always did. Reyes poured two fingers of the minister's brandy — the navy paid for the ship; the ministry paid for the brandy, and there was a lesson about the world in that if you cared to dig for it — and sat at her desk without drinking, because drinking alone before an operation was a habit of dead captains, and she intended to keep living out of spite.

On the desk, under a brass paperweight shaped like an anchor that had never anchored anything, lay a photograph. It was old. The edges had gone soft and yellow, the way photographs do when they are handled too much and then hidden too long. Two women stood on the deck of a survey launch, younger than anyone alive now would believe. One of them was Reyes herself, thirty-one and already gray at the temples, laughing at something outside the frame. The other was Maren Montrose, who was not laughing, who was never quite laughing, who was looking directly into the camera with the expression of a woman who knew exactly how the picture would end and had decided to be in it anyway.

Maren Montrose. The one person who had ever chosen truth over her.

That was the sentence Reyes used, privately, in the locked room of herself where she kept the things that could not survive daylight. Other people had regrets. She had a sentence. It was efficient. It saved time.

Because here was the shape of it, the shape she turned over at night like a stone with something underneath: Maren Montrose had been the finest diver Reyes ever trained, and the truest friend she ever had, and when the inquiry came — when the *Corvane* file cracked open the first time, twenty years ago, quietly, the way rotten things crack open — Maren had been given a choice. Everyone was given a choice. That was how institutions worked; they didn't crush people, they offered them doors, and then crushed the ones who wouldn't walk through.

Take the settlement. Take the silence. Take your share of the story that keeps everyone comfortable and your daughter fed and your name clean. That had been the door. Reyes had stood beside her while it was offered, and she had wanted — God help her, she had *wanted*, with a nakedness that still embarrassed her — for Maren to take it. Because if Maren took it, they would both be safe. They would run salvage contracts together until their hands gave out, and they would grow old in the same harbor, and none of it — the looted treasury, the governor's confession, the families ruined to build a fleet — any of it would ever have to be looked at straight on.

Maren had looked at the door. Then she had looked at Reyes, with that terrible clear gaze, and said, "Ilsa. You know what's right. Come with me."

Come with me. As if it were simple. As if truth were a boat and all you had to do was step aboard.

Reyes had testified instead. Not against Maren, not exactly — against the dive, against the unauthorized survey, against the whole reckless enterprise, in language so precise and procedural that the board had no choice but to strip Maren's certification and blacken her name, and the file had closed again, quiet as a drawer sliding shut, and the fleet had gone on being built on money that belonged to someone else.

And Maren had gone out anyway. Of course she had. She had gone out with a borrowed rig and a crew of ghosts and she had found the strongroom, and she had died there with her hand on the wheel, and Reyes had spent twenty years telling herself that this proved something. That it proved truth was a reef that sank ships. That the woman who chose it drowned, and the woman who chose the institution was promoted, and therefore the institution was correct, QED, case closed, file shut.

It was a good argument. It had held up for twenty years under daily use.

It was starting to creak now, in the way old hulls creak, and Reyes hated that she could hear it.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, which meant it was important, because everyone aboard knew better than to knock at dusk unless the alternative was worse.

"Enter."

It was Commander Hale, the liaison — the minister's man, all pressed uniform and reasonable tones, the kind of officer who had never been cold in his life and considered this a professional qualification. He set a folder on her desk with the delicacy of a man laying down a winning card.

"The ministry's position has firmed up, Captain."

"The ministry's position firms up every six hours. It's very athletic."

Hale smiled the smile of a man who had decided not to hear insubordination. "The charges are approved. Demolition team embarks tonight. We seal the site at the next low tide." He tapped the folder. "There's also this. A signal intercept. Your survey boat has been transmitting."

Something moved behind Reyes's ribs, and she was too old and too honest with herself to pretend it was surprise. "Transmitting what?"

"Position fixes. Regular as a clock." Hale paused, savoring it. "Navy-issue beacon. Old series, but functional. Someone aboard that boat has been reporting to us since before they left port."

Reyes looked at the intercept sheet. The fixes marched down the page in neat columns, and each one was a small betrayal, and each one told her something she had already suspected, which was that the world was dirtier than the idealists believed and cleaner than the cynics hoped — dirtier, because even a boat full of strays carried a rat; cleaner, because the rat wasn't one of hers, wasn't one of *Tamsin's*, and some small treacherous part of her was relieved on behalf of a woman she was preparing to bury along with a wreck.

"Interesting," she said.

"You don't seem surprised."

"I'm rarely surprised by equipment working as designed." She closed the folder. "Commander, tell the demolition team they embark tonight, and tell them the window is the next low tide, and tell them that if the Kestrel's Wake interferes, they are to proceed regardless. Is that understood? Regardless. I will not have weather, sentiment, or salvage pirates stand between this ministry and its housekeeping."

"And the girl?" Hale asked, too casually. "Our other loose thread. The Voss girl — Sefa. Meridian family stock. The scout reported she was seen aboard."

The cabin seemed to get smaller. Reyes kept her face the way she kept her desk: ordered, weighted, nothing adrift. "I'm aware of who Sefa is."

"Her grandmother signed the original claim. If the documents surface and the families get hold of them—"

"Then the documents will not surface." Reyes stood, and Hale, to his credit, understood that the meeting had ended several sentences ago. At the door he paused.

"For what it's worth, Captain — the minister thinks highly of you. There's talk of a commodore's board after this."

"There usually is," Reyes said, "after."

He left. She stood a long moment with the intercept sheet in her hand, and then she did a thing she had not done in twenty years: she took the photograph out from under the anchor paperweight and looked at it properly, in the lamplight, letting herself see it.

Maren's face. That clear, unarmored look. *You know what's right. Come with me.*

Everyone assumed Reyes had chosen ambition. Even Reyes had mostly assumed it. Ambition was the respectable word for it, the word that fit in fitness reports and dinner conversation. But standing there with the sea hammering the hull and the monsoon stacking itself black over the eastern water, Reyes admitted the thing she had spent two decades not admitting: she hadn't chosen the fleet over Maren. She had chosen fear. Fear dressed in a uniform, fear with a pension, fear that called itself duty because duty had better tailoring. Maren had walked toward the truth knowing it would cost her everything, and Reyes had watched her go and called it recklessness, because the alternative was calling it courage, and courage was contagious, and she had not dared catch it.

And now here came Maren's daughter, sailing the same course with the same clear eyes, dragging a crew of strays and a dead prospector's map toward the same reef, and the navy had handed Reyes a beacon intercept and a demolition order and a commodore's board, and expected her to feel triumphant.

She felt like a woman watching a photograph develop in reverse.

* * *

At midnight she went up to the flag bridge, because sleep had become a negotiation she was losing. The sea had built through the evening — long, heavy swells out of the southeast, the leading breath of the monsoon, the water moving in that deep muscular way that meant the storm was no longer coming but arriving. The *Vigilant* drove through it with her bow light swinging wide arcs, and ahead, faint on the radar repeater, a single small echo held its course toward the reef: the Kestrel's Wake, stubborn as a splinter, exactly where a Montrose would be.

"She's making for the site, Captain," the watch officer said. "Best speed, looks like. In this sea."

Of course she was. Reyes almost smiled. Tamsin Montrose dove the way her mother had — against the margins, against the clock, against every sensible voice — and Reyes had taught her that. That was the joke at the bottom of everything, the one nobody laughed at: every skill the girl was using to undo the navy, Reyes had put there personally. Knot by knot. Dive by dive. She had built the instrument of her own unmaking and called it mentorship, and she would do it again, because teaching Maren's daughter had been the closest she ever came to apologizing.

"Signal the fleet," she said. "All units close on the reef. Full operational tempo. And signal the scout — if the survey boat attempts a dive, report immediately. Nothing more."

"Shall we warn her off, Captain? Weather's turning ugly. There are regulations about civilian vessels in a declared storm zone."

Reyes watched the small echo crawl across the repeater, and behind it, invisible, the wreck that held a fortune, a confession, a skeleton wearing a rig she recognized, and the last unburied piece of the only person who had ever asked her to be brave.

"No warnings," she said. "Maren Montrose never needed one, and neither does her daughter. Set the demolition team to work the moment we make the site. Whatever that boat finds — whatever surfaces — the wreck goes under before the monsoon does. That is the order. Log it."

The watch officer logged it. The fleet turned as one, five sets of running lights converging through the dark on a patch of ocean that had already swallowed one Montrose, and Reyes stood at the glass with the photograph's image burned behind her eyes and made herself one final promise, the way a woman makes a promise to a mirror:

This ends here. The file closes. The past goes down with the ship.

But beneath the promise, in the locked room, the sentence shifted its weight, and for the first time in twenty years it sounded less like a verdict and more like a question: *the one person who ever chose truth over her — and what, exactly, had that cost the rest of them?*

Ahead, through the rain that had begun to fall in long grey ropes, the Kestrel's Wake ran for the reef — and Reyes gave the order to come about and follow her down into the dark.
