## 26. Running the Reef

The first thing Sefa felt was the engine change — a stutter in the deck plates under her bare feet, a half-second hesitation between one heartbeat of vibration and the next. She'd been asleep on the chart table in the wheelhouse, cheek on Ovid's spare coat, and she woke the way she always woke: all at once, listening.

The second thing she felt was Brannoc shouting from below, his voice muffled by two decks and pure fury: "She's losing oil pressure! I told you — I *told* you —"

The third thing was the searchlight.

It swept across the water astern like a white blade laid flat on the sea, and Sefa's stomach dropped through the hull and into the channel below. She knew that light. She'd been seeing it in her sleep since the port, since the scout ship with the navy-grey superstructure had sat at anchor three berths down, its crew pretending to care about fishing while their cameras cared about her.

"Tamsin!" she called, already scrambling into the pilot's chair. "They're coming. They're coming *now*."

Tamsin Montrose came up the companionway ladder at a speed that should have been impossible for a woman who'd spent four hours underwater that day. Her hair was still wet. Her eyes went to the searchlight, then to the compass, then to Sefa — and Sefa watched her make the same calculation Sefa had made a hundred times alone in her head since they'd left port: *we cannot outrun them.*

The Kestrel's Wake did eleven knots on a good day with a following wind and Brannoc in an optimistic mood. The flagship behind them did eighteen, and it wasn't even trying yet.

"Brannoc!" Tamsin shouted down the hatch. "How long can you keep her above ten?"

A clatter, a curse, then: "Define 'keep.'"

"Brannoc."

"Twenty minutes! Maybe thirty if the pump holds and the pump is held together with prayer and solder!"

Tamsin turned to Sefa. And here was the thing — the thing Sefa would think about later, lying awake in her bunk in Meridian harbor, the thing that had cracked something open in her chest and never quite sealed again: Tamsin didn't say *can we make it.* She didn't say *what are our options.* She looked at Sefa the way you look at someone you've decided to trust, which was a look nobody had ever given Sefa before, not once, not ever, and said:

"You said there were channels out here no chart shows."

Sefa's mouth went dry. "I said I *thought*—"

"Sefa." Tamsin's voice was quiet under the engine roar. Behind them, the searchlight swung again, closer now, and somewhere on the flagship a whistle shrilled three times — the sound a navy vessel makes when it stops asking politely. "You read water better than anyone I've ever dived with. Better than me. Where do we go?"

Sefa looked out the wheelhouse windows. Black water. Blacker sky. The reef ahead was a rumor — a faint paleness where swell broke over coral heads, a smell of churned calcium on the wind, the particular hush the sea made when it was hiding teeth. No lights. No markers. No chart, because the hydrographic office had surveyed these waters eighty years ago from a ship too big to come in close, and had written *unsurveyed — dangerous* across the whole maze in letters that kept everyone out.

Everyone except the people who lived out here. Everyone except smugglers and wreckers and girls who'd grown up running messages through the shallows because it was faster than the roads, and who'd learned the reef the way other children learned their prayers: by repetition, by fear, and by watching what happened to those who got it wrong.

Her grandfather's voice, in her memory, steady as a tide table: *The reef doesn't hate you, little fish. It just doesn't know you're there. Your job is to be known.*

"I can take us through," Sefa said. "But you have to let me steer. All the way. Nobody touches the wheel, nobody second-guesses, and when I say slow down, we slow down even if they're right behind us. Especially then."

Tamsin didn't hesitate. That was the other thing. "She's yours."

Sefa put her hands on the wheel. They were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice weather happening to somebody else, far away, unimportant.

* * *

The reef maze began where the deep water ended, a sudden ragged edge of white noise where the swell met coral. On the flagship's plotter — Sefa knew, because Ovid had described the standard navy fit to her once, in his fussy archivist way, complete with dates of manufacture — that edge was a blank space with a warning printed in it. A captain with a minister breathing down his neck had exactly two choices: go around, adding six hours to the chase, or follow straight in and trust his draft and his luck.

Reyes wouldn't go around. Sefa had never met the woman, but she'd spent enough nights watching the scout ship to understand the type. Reyes followed straight in. Which meant the next hour would decide everything, and it would decide it in water shallow enough to see the coral's colors by moonlight, if you knew how to look.

"Ovid," Sefa said. "I need your eyes aft. Tell me everything about that light. When it sweeps, when it steadies, how far behind."

Ovid appeared in the doorway clutching a lantern he hadn't lit, which was very Ovid — prepared for a darkness he'd forgotten to actually address. "You want me to — ah. Yes. Observation. I observe." He wedged himself by the aft window and peered out with the intensity he usually reserved for marginalia. "The searchlight is sweeping in a pattern. Twelve seconds left, twelve right, pause. They're searching, not tracking. They haven't got us precisely."

"They will," Sefa said. "The moment we cross open water inside the maze, we're a lit match. So we don't cross open water."

She took the Kestrel's Wake toward a gap in the white line that did not exist. That was the only way to describe it. There was no gap — not to any instrument aboard, not to any eye trained on charts. But Sefa could hear it. Under the engine noise and the wind there was a difference in the breakers' voices: the coral heads roared, and between two of them, a stretch maybe forty meters wide, the sea spoke in a lower, rounder tone. Deep enough. Clear enough. A channel her grandfather had called the Needle's Eye, because you threaded it or you didn't, and the wrecks on either side of it were the ones who hadn't.

"Slow to five knots," she said.

"We're being chased," Brannoc yelled up the hatch, appearing in the companionway with grease on his face and genuine anguish in his eyes. "Slowly?"

"Slowly." Sefa kept her eyes forward. "Speed kills us down here, not them. The reef doesn't care who's winning."

The Needle's Eye came up fast. In the sweep of the distant searchlight she caught one glimpse of it: black walls of coral rising on either side, close enough to touch, the passage between them barely wider than the boat, the water inside it moving with a slow, muscular current that wanted to shove them sideways onto stone. She felt the current take the stern and corrected before her conscious mind finished the thought, hands doing arithmetic her grandfather had taught them twenty years ago.

The coral slid past so close that a fan of dead white coral scraped the hull with a sound like a fingernail down the world's longest chalkboard. Brannoc made a noise like a dying kettle. Ovid gripped the doorframe and recited, apparently involuntarily, the tonnage regulations of 1871.

And then they were through, into a lagoon of glassy black water dotted with coral heads like sleeping animals, and behind them the searchlight swept across the outer reef and found nothing but the white line of breakers, and Sefa realized she had stopped breathing somewhere back at the entrance.

"Breathe," Tamsin said quietly. She was standing at Sefa's shoulder. Sefa hadn't heard her move. "That was — I've dived wrecks with more room than that."

"There's worse coming," Sefa said. "That was the easy one."

Tamsin laughed. Actually laughed — a short, disbelieving bark, half terror, entirely real. "Of course it is."

* * *

The maze proper took three hours, and Sefa remembered perhaps half of them afterward, the rest worn away by concentration like soft stone under current. She worked by sound and starlight and the pale ghost-glow the reef gave off where the moon caught breaking water, calling depths and headings in a low voice, feeling the boat answer her now instead of fighting her — Brannoc had done something heroic and probably illegal to the throttle linkage, and the old survey boat moved through the narrow water with a grace she'd never shown in open sea, as if she'd been built for this and had merely been waiting decades for someone to ask.

Twice the searchlight found them.

The first time, they were crossing a wide sandy basin between channel systems — unavoidable, the only way east — and the light snapped onto them like an accusation. The flagship's bow wave appeared beyond the outer reef a moment later, a smear of phosphorescence, and Sefa heard the engines change pitch as Reyes committed to the maze.

"She's coming in after us," Tamsin said.

"She'll lose steel," Sefa said. "Or she'll lose us. She can't do both."

The second time was worse, because it wasn't the light — it was the sound of tearing metal rolling across the water from the northeast, a long grinding shriek, followed by silence, followed by a whistle pattern Sefa didn't know but Ovid did.

"That's a man-overboard-and-hull-breach signal," he said softly. "They've hit the outer barrier reef. The one marked on the charts."

Nobody cheered. Sefa noticed that. Brannoc, hauling himself up the hatch with a wrench still in his hand, opened his mouth, closed it, and just nodded slowly, looking out at the dark where men were currently pumping water and maybe bleeding. Tamsin stood at the window with her arms crossed, and her face in the binnacle light was complicated — satisfaction and pity tangled together like fishing line.

"They chose to follow," Tamsin said finally. "That's on her. Get them clear, Sefa."

Sefa got them clear.

* * *

Dawn found them anchored in a pool no chart had ever named, ringed by coral and mangrove islets, in water so still and green that the Kestrel's Wake seemed to float on glass. Sefa dropped the hook with hands that had finally stopped shaking about an hour ago, and then discovered that her whole body had been holding a tension she hadn't known about, because it all let go at once and she nearly fell down the wheelhouse steps.

Brannoc caught her elbow. "Easy, little pilot. Adrenaline bill comes due."

"I'm fine."

"You're grey," he said. "Sit. I'll make the terrible tea. It's medicinal. Everything about my cooking is medicinal, which is why nobody asks for seconds."

They gathered in the cockpit as the sky went pink — Tamsin, Ovid, Brannoc, Sefa — and Sefa looked around at them and saw the night written on all of them. Ovid had a bruise blooming on his forehead where he'd hit the doorframe during the Needle's Eye. Brannoc's hands were bandaged in three places and he couldn't remember why, which he announced cheerfully. Tamsin had salt dried white in her hair and a cut on her palm she was ignoring. And Sefa herself, she realized, was wearing Ovid's spare coat still, and had been for the entire chase, and nobody had mentioned it, and somehow that undid her more than anything.

Ovid poured the tea. It was terrible. He raised his tin mug anyway.

"To the unsurveyed waters," he said, "and to the young woman who has read them better than the Hydrographic Office has managed in eighty years. I intend to cite you extensively, Miss Sefa, though I warn you my footnote style is punishing."

"I don't know what that means," Sefa said, "but thank you."

"It means you're in a book now," Brannoc said. "Forever. Congratulations and condolences."

And then — Sefa couldn't have said who started it, and later each of them blamed a different person — Ovid snorted into his tea, and Brannoc began describing, with escalating precision, the exact noise he'd made when the coral scraped the hull ("a professional assessment of structural damage, rendered vocally"), and Tamsin said, deadpan, "It sounded like a seagull being audited," and Ovid wheezed, and Brannoc clutched his bandaged chest, and then they were all laughing — really laughing, the helpless kind, the kind that hurts your ribs and makes your eyes run, the kind Sefa had heard other families have through other people's windows her whole life and had privately concluded was mostly a story people told.

It went on too long and stopped too soon and left them all wiping their faces, and in the quiet afterward Tamsin looked around the cockpit at her battered, ridiculous crew and said, "We bought a tide. Maybe two. Reyes has to pull her people off the barrier reef and find another way around, and that costs her the morning. So we spend it well. We eat, we patch, we sleep in shifts, and then—"

"And then the strongroom," Sefa said.

Tamsin's smile faded into something steadier. "And then the strongroom."

* * *

Sefa slept four hours in the bow netting and dreamed of nothing, which was itself a gift. When she woke, midday sun was filtering through the mangroves and the boat hummed with small industry: Brannoc below, swearing musically at the oil pump; Ovid on deck with the Corvane's deck plans spread across the hatch cover, annotating; Tamsin checking dive gear with the methodical tenderness of someone handling something fragile.

Sefa climbed down and helped without being asked, and it was while they were coiling the safety line together that Tamsin said, casually, in the tone of someone mentioning weather:

"The scout ship. You knew her silhouette before you ever told us about her. Back in port, the first morning — you went stiff when you looked at her berth. I've been waiting for you to tell me why."

Sefa's hands stopped on the rope. The mangrove water lapped. Somewhere below, Brannoc dropped a wrench and apologized to it.

"My family," Sefa said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "My mother's family. We're Meridian. You know that much — I told you about my grandfather, the pilot. What I didn't tell you is what happened to the rest of them. After the Corvane. After the treasury." She swallowed. "The navy didn't just take the money, Tamsin. They took the houses, the shipping contracts, the names. My grandmother's family lost everything in a single season, and when her father wrote letters asking why, the letters came back opened, and then they stopped coming back at all. My grandmother raised my mother on stories about a ship called the Corvane and a governor who stole from his own people and a navy that helped him do it and then burned the proof." She looked up. "That scout ship has been hunting me since the port. I recognized her number. I know who sent her. And I didn't say anything because — because every time I've trusted anyone with that name, it's cost them."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. Then she sat back on her heels, took the coil of line from Sefa's frozen hands, and finished coiling it herself, unhurried, giving Sefa's hands something else to do by handing them the next rope.

"Last night," Tamsin said, "you took this boat through a hole in the reef that I would have sworn didn't exist, on a promise and a sound. You saved all of us. Whatever name you're carrying — whatever it's cost you — it bought you the right to tell us in your own time. But Sefa? Your time might be shorter than you think. If Reyes is hunting you specifically, that changes what she does next. I need to know what she knows."

"She knows my face," Sefa said. "And she knows my family name. And if she takes this boat—" She stopped.

"If she takes this boat," Tamsin finished quietly, "she finds a Meridian girl aboard a salvage vessel bound for the Corvane, and she connects it to the manifest Ovid pulled, and she understands exactly what we're raising out of that strongroom." She tied off the coil, set it down, and met Sefa's eyes. "Which means we're not just racing her to the wreck anymore. We're racing her to the truth. And she's had a head start her whole career."

From below, Brannoc's voice floated up, cheerful and oblivious: "Pump's holding! We have oil pressure and approximately no dignity!"

Ovid looked up from his plans, pen paused, sensing the weather change the way he sensed everything on paper. "Ladies? Is there news?"

Sefa took a breath. The mangrove pool lay green and calm around them, and beyond the reef the sea was already building — long, heavy swells marching in from the southeast, the monsoon's outriders, arriving early, exactly as the clouds had promised.

"Yes," Sefa said. "There's news. And you'd both better sit down."

Above the eastern islets, thin and far away, a searchlight blinked once in the daylight — a signal, passed ship to ship, hunting along the horizon for a boat that had vanished into unmapped water.

It was getting closer.
