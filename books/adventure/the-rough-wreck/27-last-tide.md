## 27. Last Tide

The monsoon did not arrive the way storms do in stories — no distant grumble, no slow darkening you could pretend not to notice. It arrived as a wall.

Tamsin stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Wake* and watched it come across the water like a decision that had already been made about her. One horizon was still blue-grey and ordinary, gulls wheeling over the reef as if it were any other morning. The other half of the world was black, a standing cliff of cloud maybe eight miles out, rain falling from it in grey curtains so dense they looked solid, like the sea had grown a second, worse sea above itself.

"Right," said Brannoc beside her, chewing on the unlit pipe he'd given up smoking three years ago and never quite given up carrying. "That's not weather. That's an opinion."

"It's a deadline," Tamsin said.

Behind them, a quarter-mile off, Reyes's dredge bit into the reef again with a sound like a giant grinding its teeth. They'd been at it since before dawn, hauling coral and hull plating up in great rusted mouthfuls, working their way toward the Corvane with the patience of something that didn't need to breathe. Somewhere under all that churning water lay the strongroom they'd cracked open two tides ago — the documents in their oilskin wrapping, her mother's letter still sealed, and forty years of stolen gold that Tamsin had looked at for exactly one minute before turning away from it.

Sefa came up from the pilothouse two steps at a time, hair tied back, eyes bright with the particular terror she wore like a uniform. "Barometer's dropped another six points since breakfast. Ovid says that's bad. Ovid says a lot of things are bad."

"Ovid is rarely wrong about bad things," Brannoc said. "It's his one gift."

"He also says," Sefa went on, "that the leading edge will give us maybe ninety minutes of surface calm before the wind comes through ahead of the rain. Ninety minutes, then everything goes sideways for eleven hours minimum, and after that there is no wreck. There's a reef with a rumor in it."

Tamsin did the arithmetic without wanting to. Dive down, thirty meters through water already stirred to soup by the dredging. Find the antechamber in visibility that would be measured in hand-widths. Cut the documents free, get them into the brass tube, get herself back up the line. Forty minutes down if everything went right. Twenty minutes of margin for everything going wrong.

"And the charges?" she asked.

Ovid appeared at the companionway, moving with the careful dignity of a man who had decided long ago that running was undignified and had regretted it every emergency since. He held a mug of tea he would never drink. "The liaison's man confirmed it to me last night, before I stopped playing along. Demolition team goes in at low tide. Which is—" he checked his watch, an affectation he'd kept from thirty years in the archive where time was measured in filing deadlines, "—now. Essentially now. Their boat launched four minutes ago."

"So we have the monsoon coming from one side," Tamsin said, "and men with explosives coming from the other, and the thing we came here for sitting in the middle like a coin on a railway track."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless," Brannoc said. "Say it slower and it sounds suicidal. Either way I'm against it."

"You're against diving on those margins, you said. Two days ago. Loudly."

"I stand by loudly." He took the pipe out of his mouth and pointed it at her, which was as close to a formal protest as Brannoc ever filed. "But I've been watching you look at that water since dawn, and I know better than to argue with a woman doing arithmetic she's already finished. So instead I'm going to say the thing nobody's said yet." He paused, and gentled, which on Brannoc was more alarming than shouting. "You don't have to be the one who goes down. We could lose the letter. We could lose all of it, and still be a crew at the end of the day. That's not nothing, Tamsin. Some days it's everything."

She looked at him. At Sefa, who had gone very still in the way she did when she was reading a current or a room. At Ovid, whose tea trembled very slightly, whose whole life had been one long education in what happened when good people stayed quiet.

"No," Tamsin said. "It has to be me. It was always going to be me. My mother went down there alone because she trusted the wrong person to watch her back. I'm going down there alone because I finally trust the right ones to watch mine. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point."

Nobody argued. That was the strange, warm, terrifying thing about this crew of strays she'd assembled out of the port's leftovers: when she said a thing plainly enough, they carried it for her.

"All right," she said, and heard her own voice go flat and workmanlike, the voice she used for contracts. "Sefa, you're my surface reader. If the current shifts even a whisper, you pull the line twice and I come up whether I want to or not. Ovid — deck plans. If I signal confusion, you talk me through it by line-taps, same code we drilled. Brannoc—"

"I know." He was already moving toward the winch they'd built out of salvage and spite. "I keep the line clean, I keep the winch honest, and I don't let anyone from that fleet touch my rail. Go get your mother's letter, girl. And Tamsin?" He almost smiled. "Leave the gold. Let Reyes's lot dig for treasure while the sky buries them. There's a poetry in it I intend to enjoy from a safe distance."

* * *

The water took her the way it always did — a cold hand closing over her, the world going green and then brown and then nothing much at all.

Visibility was worse than she'd budgeted. The dredging had chewed the reef into a permanent storm of silt, and the monsoon's pressure was already pushing swells down through the water column in long slow pulses she felt against her chest like something breathing. She followed the descent line by feel, counting, letting her hands do the navigating her eyes couldn't.

At twenty meters the Corvane rose out of the murk.

Even now, even after five dives, it stopped her. Three hundred feet of colonial ambition lying on its beam ends in the sand, masts snapped, hull furred with a decade of soft grey growth, portholes staring like the eyes of something that had died mid-thought. Her mother had touched this hull. Her mother had died inside it, one hand on the strongroom wheel, and Tamsin had spent most of her life pretending that fact was a story about someone else.

Not anymore. She pulled herself along the rail toward the breach they'd cut, and the wreck received her the way it seemed to receive everyone who came for its truth: reluctantly, and completely.

The flooded lower decks were a maze she knew by heart now and hated anyway. Companionsways choked with silt. A galley where copper pots hung suspended in the gloom like a school of dull fish. Through the second-class saloon, where a piano sat on its side with its keys furred white, and Tamsin thought, not for the first time, that somewhere in this ship was the last song anyone ever played on it, and no one would ever know what it was.

She reached the antechamber. The compartment beyond it was dark as a held breath.

Her lamp found the skeleton first, as it always did — her mother's rig still on the bones, faded canvas and rotted rubber, the dive knife wedged in the bulkhead where she'd left it or lost it or driven it in as the water came for her. Tamsin had taken the knife two tides ago. It rode in a sheath on her own leg now, and she felt its weight like a hand on her ankle.

The strongroom door stood open. Beyond it, the shelves. The gold, glinting dully where her lamp touched it — coins, ingots, plate, a governor's whole stolen fortune sitting patient as a saint. And on the top shelf, wrapped in oilskin and wired shut, the bundle that mattered: the manifest, the confession, the truth of forty families looted so a colony could shine.

And the letter. Her mother's letter, sealed in wax, addressed in a hand Tamsin knew better than her own: *For Tam, if she's braver than I was.*

She'd read it once, alone, two nights ago, and hadn't told the crew she'd read it. She wasn't ready for what it asked of her. Maybe she'd never be ready. But she'd promised Voss, and she'd promised a dead woman, and promises were the only currency she'd ever respected.

Tamsin cut the wire, stuffed the document bundle into the brass tube, screwed the cap down hard, and clipped it to her harness. Then she knelt in the silt of the strongroom floor, in the cold and the dark, and let herself have ten seconds she hadn't allowed anyone to see.

Ten seconds of looking at the gold and understanding, finally, what it was. Not wealth. A test. Forty years sitting in the dark asking every diver who found it the same question: *what are you?*

Voss had failed it. Her mother had passed it and paid for passing. Reyes had answered it so long ago she'd probably forgotten there'd been a question.

Tamsin stood, left the gold gleaming in the dark for the navy to bury, and turned for the door.

The wreck shuddered.

It wasn't an explosion — not yet. It was the dredge, a quarter-mile off, its cable snagging and hauling, and the whole hull groaned around her like a ship remembering it was dying. Silt rained from overhead in soft grey sheets. Somewhere aft, something large let go and fell.

Then, faint but unmistakable, through the water, through the silt, through everything: a second sound. A sharp metallic crack, followed by a slow bell-tone hum through the hull.

Brannoc's line tapped against her arm — three sharp pulls. His signal for *get out now*, reserved for emergencies, used once before, never lightly.

She kicked for the breach, and behind her, deep in the Corvane's drowned heart, she heard the small precise sounds of men who had come to make sure no one ever found anything here again.

* * *

She broke the surface into a world that had begun to end.

The wall of cloud was closer — two miles, maybe less — and the wind had arrived ahead of it, dragging white streaks across the swell. Rain hit the water in a hiss like static. The *Kestrel's Wake* pitched hard at her mooring, and Brannoc hauled her over the rail with hands that shook, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"The tube," he managed. "Tell me—"

"Got it." She patted the brass cylinder strapped to her chest. "Documents, letter, everything. Gold's still down there keeping the fishes rich."

"Good lads, the fishes." He was laughing, or something near it, the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming. "You're bleeding."

She looked down. A scrape along her forearm she had no memory of earning, welling pink in the rain. "Occupational."

"Tamsin." Sefa's voice from the pilothouse, tight as wire. "Their demolition boat — it's pulling back. Fast. They're clearing the blast radius."

Ovid emerged clutching his eternal tea, now entirely rainwater, and said, with the mildness of a man narrating his own funeral, "I believe that means the charges are set."

The wind hit them then, properly, the monsoon's vanguard arriving like a shove between the shoulders. The *Kestrel's Wake* swung on her moorings. Rain went from hiss to roar. Out across the blackening water, Tamsin could see Reyes's flagship holding position beyond the reef, lights burning through the murk, waiting for her fleet to finish the job the sea had started forty years ago.

"We cut loose," Tamsin said. "Now. Before the first charge."

"And go where?" Brannoc shouted over the wind. "There's no harbor inside sixty miles that'll have us in this!"

"There's the reef channels!" Sefa was already at the wheel, alive in a way Tamsin had only seen when the girl was doing the impossible. "The ones that aren't on any chart! I can run us in behind the coral — the fleet can't follow, their draft's too deep — but I need time, I need them busy looking at us, and I need—" she swallowed, "—I need someone to buy me ten minutes."

Everyone looked at Brannoc.

Brannoc looked at the water, where the Corvane's outline still showed as a darker darkness beneath the swell, and at the demolition boat's wake receding toward the flagship, and Tamsin watched him arrive at the same conclusion she had, and watched him decide not to say it out loud, because saying it would make it real, and Brannoc had always believed problems were like cats — ignore them firmly enough and they might go away.

"How many charges?" he asked quietly.

"Ovid?"

"The liaison said a pattern. Six, I believe. Placed along the old boiler line." Ovid's face had gone the color of the sky. "Brannoc, no."

"Somebody's got to pull the firing leads," Brannoc said, already reaching for his dive rig, moving with the terrible calm of a man who had finally found a problem worth fixing with his own hands. "Or jam the detonators, if I can get to them before their man wires the box. It's salvage work, really. Just salvage work with a shorter warranty." He grinned at Tamsin, and it was the grin of the best engineer she'd ever known, and the worst liar. "Keep my line clean, Captain."

The first charge went off while he was still buckling his weight belt.

It came up through the hull of the world — a deep, bone-shaking boom, and the sea itself flinched, a ring of white water rising a quarter-mile off where the Corvane slept. The shockwave slapped the *Kestrel's Wake* sideways. Sefa fought the wheel. Ovid dropped his tea and, for the first time in recorded history, ran.

Tamsin caught Brannoc's arm at the rail. Below them, the water was already boiling with silt, and somewhere in that churning grey, five more charges waited on a timer or a wire, and the wreck that held her mother, and the truth, and the gold nobody wanted, was beginning — slowly, hugely, irreversibly — to come apart.

"Brannoc," she said. "Wait for my signal. And if you're not back on this rail in twenty minutes, I'm coming down after you, margins be damned."

"Aye," he said. "Knew you'd say that." And he stepped off the rail into the ruin of the world.
