## 28. The Charge

Brannoc had always believed that a man could tell the quality of an engineer by the way they treated a fuse. A careless one crimped it fast and walked away whistling. A good one took their time, checked the bridge wire twice, and said a small apology to the charge before they left it — because a charge was a promise, and promises deserved respect.

He was thinking about this because the navy's demolition team, visible through the Kestrel's Wake's salt-smeared glass as a cluster of lights a quarter mile off, were clearly *very* good engineers. He could tell by the rhythm of their work boats, the tidy geometry of their marker buoys, the unhurried confidence of divers who knew exactly what they were placing and where.

They were going to do a beautiful job of it.

And then there would be no Corvane at all.

"Stop staring at them like they're going to feel bad about it," Sefa said from the helm. She'd been holding station for six hours on three hours of sleep, and her voice had gone flat and hard as ballast iron. "They're not going to feel bad."

"I'm not staring. I'm assessing." Brannoc adjusted the binoculars. "Their placement pattern is textbook. Charges along the port hull seams, staggered depth intervals, probably a shaped main charge at the keel line to drop her into the trench. It's elegant work. It makes me sick."

"It should," Ovid said quietly. He sat at the chart table with the Corvane's deck plans spread out under his palms, as if he could hold the ship down by weight of paper alone. "Thirty years of history, and they're going to solve it with high explosives. There's a word for that."

"There are several words for that," Brannoc said. "Most of them I learned from Tamsin."

Tamsin wasn't aboard. That was the whole problem. She'd taken the dinghy and Sefa's borrowed dive rig out to the reef's far shoulder before dawn, working the strongroom wheel relay alone because the fourth tide had been spent getting Brannoc's salvage winch rigged and the fifth tide was burning away while they argued about margins. She'd kissed his forehead on the way out — which she only did when she thought she might be about to do something stupid, a habit he'd catalogued with growing alarm over the past week — and told him to keep the engine warm.

Keep the engine warm. As if the engine were the thing that needed him right now.

The engine, frankly, was fine. The engine was the one member of this crew who never lied, never sulked, and never volunteered to swim into a collapsing wreck. Brannoc loved it dearly.

What he did not love was what he was looking at through the binoculars: a second work boat detaching from Reyes's flagship, riding low in the water, carrying long gray cases that any engineer worth his union card could identify at two hundred yards.

Demolition charges. Plural. Enough of them to fold a hundred-and-forty-year-old hull like wet paper.

"They're doing it now," he said. "Ovid. They're laying them now. The liaison told you next low tide—"

"The liaison told me what he wanted me to believe," Ovid said, but his hands had gone still on the plans. "Or what he believed himself. Ministers change schedules. Weather changes schedules." He looked up, and behind his cracked spectacles his eyes were wet and furious, which for Ovid was roughly equivalent to another man screaming. "Brannoc, if those charges go, everything inside goes with her. The documents. The letter. Everything Tamsin is down there risking her neck for right now, and she doesn't know."

Sefa turned from the helm. For once she didn't have a sarcastic remark loaded and ready. She just looked at him, seventeen years old and tired past her years, waiting to see what he'd do.

Which was unfair. He was the engineer. Engineers fixed things that were broken; they did not volunteer to become the thing that broke.

But he'd spent eleven days with these people. Eleven days of Sefa stealing his tea and Ovid narrating the Corvane's cargo manifest like a bedtime story and Tamsin pretending she wasn't learning their names by heart. Eleven days of the first family he'd had since the admiralty took his warrant and his reputation and handed him back to the world with a stain on him that wouldn't wash.

You didn't let people blow up your family. Even if you were scared. Especially if you were scared.

"Sefa," he said, already pulling his dive bag from under the bench, "how much current between here and the port seam?"

She blinked. Then the navigator came up in her face, quick and precise. "Slack in twenty minutes. You'll have maybe forty before it turns against you. After that you're swimming backward through a river."

"And the seam where they're placing?"

"Port side, midships, below the third gunport. I can put you within ten meters of it if we run the Wake in close — closer than I'd like, closer than is smart."

"Smart left when the navy showed up with explosives," Brannoc said. He started checking his kit aloud, because talking kept the fear in its cage. "Cutting gear. Two spools of line. The epoxy — no, both epoxies. The bridgewire spool. And my good pliers, not the ones Tamsin bent."

"You're going to *jam* naval demolition charges," Ovid said. It wasn't quite a question. It was the question of a man who had read enough engineering reports to know what the answer would be.

"I'm going to disconnect their firing circuits and pack the detonators in epoxy so they can't be re-armed quickly. If I get to all of them." He zipped the bag. "If I get to most of them, maybe the collapse is partial and slow instead of total and immediate. If I get to none of them—" He shrugged, and found the shrug wouldn't come off casual, so he abandoned it. "Well. Then I'll have had a very educational morning."

"That's not funny," Sefa said.

"No," Brannoc agreed. "It's really not. But if I don't laugh, I'll think too clearly, and clear thinking is how a man talks himself out of the right thing."

* * *

The water swallowed him at slack tide, and the world went green and quiet.

Brannoc had been diving for thirty years, mostly for repair work, and he'd never loved it the way Tamsin did. To her the sea was a language. To him it was a workshop with terrible lighting and a persistent attempt on his life. He descended hand over hand down his own line, ears popping, the reef falling away beneath him in terraces of dead coral and old rope, until the Corvane rose up out of the blue-gray murk like the spine of some drowned cathedral.

Even now — even knowing what was coming — his breath caught. She was huge. Her timbers had gone soft and dark, furred with weed, but her bones were still bones. A ship built by proud men in a prouder century, carrying stolen gold and a governor's confession, waiting a hundred and forty years for someone to ask her what happened.

And moored along her flank, neat as churchgoers, the navy's charges.

He saw the first one before he was even level with the rail: a gray cylinder lashed to a bracket welded — *welded*, the barbarians — to a hull plate, with a firing cable running up to a float-mounted initiator buoy. Textbook. Elegant. He hated it personally.

He got to work.

The theory was simple. Every demolition circuit needs continuity — a closed loop from initiator to detonator. Break the loop and the charge is a very expensive paperweight. The practice was less simple, because the navy used waterproofed connectors with locking collars, and his fingers were going stupid with cold, and somewhere above him men in a work boat were watching gauges and counting down to a schedule he hadn't been invited to.

First charge: collar seized. He cut the cable instead, low, near the detonator housing, then packed the severed ends and the housing itself in epoxy — a generous, vindictive amount, the epoxy equivalent of gluing every lock on a building shut. Two minutes. His hands were shaking. Not from cold. Well. Partly from cold.

Second charge, fifteen meters aft: same treatment, faster. He was getting a rhythm. Cut, strip, pack, move. Somewhere in his head a voice that sounded suspiciously like his old instructor said, *Good technique, Mr. Brannoc. Pity about the court-martial.* He told the voice to mind its own business.

Third charge was at the keel line, the big one, the shaped charge meant to drop her into the trench. It was also, he realized as he came around the curve of the hull, already connected to a main firing line that ran to the surface buoy — and the main line was live. He could see the indicator window on the junction box glowing a faint, patient amber.

Armed. They'd armed the network. Which meant the countdown wasn't a schedule anymore. It was whenever somebody topside decided the divers were clear and turned a key.

His chest went tight around the regulator. Okay. Okay. Think like an engineer. A network has nodes. Kill the node, kill the branch. The junction box was bolted to a plate near the main charge, and if he could open it and pull the master bus bar—

He got the cover half off when the current shifted.

Sefa had said forty minutes. The sea, as usual, had its own opinion. It came up the reef face like a hand turning him on a lathe, dragging his safety line taut, shoving him sideways across the hull. He grabbed the junction box and held on while the water tried to peel him off like a label, and through the fog of bubbles and noise he heard it —

a sound he felt more than heard, deep in his sternum, a flat wooden *crack* rolling up through the Corvane's bones.

Not a charge. Not yet. Just the old ship settling, or the dredge biting the reef a quarter mile off, or the hull finally giving up some joint it had held for a century and a half. But it traveled through the wreck and into his hands and up his arms, and it sounded horribly like a warning.

*Hurry,* said the ship, in the only language she had left.

He wrenched the bus bar free. The amber light died. One branch down — the main charge, the killer, the one that would have dropped her into the trench. He sagged against the hull with something like laughter bubbling up through his reg, mad and relieved, and reached for the epoxy.

That was when the first detonation went.

* * *

Later — much later, in the hospital light of memory — Brannoc would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. Memory is a bad archivist. What he kept were fragments:

The shockwave arriving not as sound but as a fist, closing around his torso, squeezing the air out of his lungs in one great silver burst of bubbles.

The Corvane *screaming* — steel and timber and a hundred and forty years of patience letting go all at once, a groan so vast it was almost musical, a ship-sized chord of ruin.

The junction box tearing out of his grip. The plate it was bolted to peeling away like foil. Himself, tumbling, line snapping somewhere above him — his beautiful line, his lifeline, gone — tumbling down the hull's flank into the rising storm of silt.

And through it all, absurdly, one clear engineering thought, calm as a Sunday: *they fired the secondary net early, they must have had a manual initiator on the work boat, which means the remaining charges are on independent timers or manual fire, which means I have no idea how many are left, which means—*

The ceiling came down.

Not the whole ceiling. A section of the upper deck, folded down by the blast, collapsing across the midships hold in a slow-motion avalanche of black timber. It missed him by the width of a prayer. The pressure wave of its landing shoved him through a shattered gunport and into the Corvane's belly, into darkness and drifting silt and the smell — yes, even underwater, somehow the *smell* — of a grave being dug.

He fetched up against something solid and soft. A rotted cargo bale, maybe. Or a coil of ancient rope. His left arm wasn't answering his messages. His regulator was breathing short and raspy, the diaphragm fouled with silt. His light was gone, or his eyes were, because the world was brown murk in every direction.

Somewhere above him, muffled by fathoms and chaos, the sea was roaring. Somewhere beyond that, a boat held a girl who steered like the water was her native tongue, and an old man who remembered every deck plan he'd ever read, and a woman who had kissed his forehead that morning because she was about to do something stupid.

They'd come for him. He knew it the way he knew load tolerances — absolutely, without proof. Tamsin would come down into this collapsing throat of a wreck without a second's hesitation, and Sefa would read her the currents, and Ovid would recite the corridors like scripture, and they would come *in here*, into this.

Which meant his job wasn't done after all.

Brannoc felt along the bale, found a rib of the old ship, and began, one-handed, arm screaming, to haul himself deeper into the wreck — toward the interior, toward the open hold, toward the one place big enough that a rescue diver might reach him before the rest of the ceiling came down.

"Stay together, old girl," he told the Corvane, mouth pressed useless against his regulator. "Just a few minutes longer. We're both owed better than this."

Above him, faint through the churn, he heard a new sound cutting down through the water — a diver's exhaust bubbles, descending fast, and a shape moving in the murk with the sure, angry grace of someone who had absolutely forbidden him to do exactly this.

*Tamsin.*

The second detonation, deeper in the wreck, rolled through the dark like thunder through a house.
