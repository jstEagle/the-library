## 29. Into the Collapse

The water turned to smoke.

That was the only way Tamsin could describe it — one moment the Corvane's stern rose out of the blue like a cathedral wall, and the next the whole reef exhaled, a brown-and-grey thunderhead of silt rolling up from the seabed and swallowing everything below forty feet. The shockwave came through her chest before her ears caught it: a deep, wet *whump*, the sea itself flinching.

Brannoc was down there.

She'd watched him go over the side eleven minutes ago with a cutting torch and a coil of jamming clamps, moving with the deliberate calm of a man who had spent thirty years arguing with machinery and usually winning. She'd watched Reyes's demolition skiff pull away from the wreck's shadow, its crew laughing, job done, charges set on timers because no one in the navy wanted to be aboard when they went.

And then the first charge had gone off early.

"Brannoc!" she shouted into her mask, knowing the water would eat it, knowing he couldn't hear her any better than she could hear him. "Brannoc, answer your line!"

The lifeline hung slack off the Kestrel's Wake's rail, running down into the murk at an angle that made her stomach drop. Slack meant either he'd stopped moving or the line had parted. She hauled it hand over hand, three pulls, four, and felt nothing but dead weight and current.

"Tamsin." Sefa's voice behind her, tight as wire. "The collapse is still going. Look."

Tamsin looked. Below them, where the wreck's midsection had been, the silt cloud pulsed — a slow heartbeat of settling debris, and every pulse sent another shudder through the hull plates she knew were down there folding like paper. The Corvane wasn't dying all at once. It was dying in sections, deck by deck, and somewhere inside that grinding dark was a man with a bad knee and a torch and the stubbornest hands she'd ever known.

"He went in through the cargo hatch," Ovid said. He had the deck plans spread across the cockpit chart table, weighted down with a coffee mug and Sefa's spare knife, his finger tracing lines he'd memorized decades ago and never expected to matter. "If he followed my route — and he did, I drew it for him myself — he'd be here. Frame ninety. The old strongroom access corridor. It's the strongest structure left in the ship. Everything around it can come down and that corridor will hold, at least for a while—"

"How long is a while?" Tamsin asked.

Ovid looked up at her. His face, usually so carefully arranged into the expression of a man who had opinions about everything, was simply frightened. "I don't know. Minutes. Perhaps less. The charges were placed along the keel line — Brannoc said so himself when he volunteered. The whole point was to get under them before they fired."

"They fired early," Sefa said. "Or he tripped one."

Nobody said the third possibility. Nobody had to.

Tamsin pulled her fins on. Her hands were shaking, which annoyed her, so she looked at them until they stopped, the way her mother had taught her — *fear is just information, Tammy, read it and put it down* — and clipped her tank harness closed with two sharp clicks.

"You are not diving into that," Brannoc would have said, if Brannoc were here to say it. He'd said exactly that to her two days ago, standing in the rain on the aft deck with his arms crossed, refusing to let her dive on margins that would have embarrassed a suicide. *You don't get to be a martyr, Montrose. Martyrs are useless. We need you annoying and alive.*

Well. He wasn't here. And she was captain, and being captain, she had discovered over these last terrible weeks, mostly meant doing the things you'd forbid anyone else from doing and hating yourself cleanly afterward.

"Sefa," she said. "I need currents."

Sefa was already at the rail, one bare foot hooked over the toe-rail, watching the water the way other people watched fire — with total attention and no sentiment. "Surface layer's pushing east, half a knot, maybe more now the storm swell's coming in. But underneath—" she squinted, tilted her head as if listening to something below her hearing, "—the collapse punched a hole in the reef shelf. Water's pouring into the wreck through the breach and out through the cargo hatch. It'll suck anything loose straight through the ship and spit it out the far side."

"Then I go in against the flow."

"Then you go in against the flow," Sefa agreed. "Frame ninety, you said?"

"Frame ninety," Ovid confirmed. "Down the cargo trunk, portside passage, second bulkhead door. There's a junction — if the corridor's flooded with debris, take the overhead crawl space instead. It runs parallel. Brannoc knows it. I showed him."

"Brannoc knows everything," Tamsin said. "It's his worst quality."

She swung over the rail. The sea came up to meet her, warm and filthy, and the world above — the boat, Sefa's outstretched hand, Ovid's white knuckles on the chart table — shrank to a bright smudge and vanished.

* * *

Inside the silt cloud there was no up and no down, only a grey-brown nothing that pressed against her mask light and gave back eight feet of visibility, then six, then four. She swam by feel and by faith: feel for the lifeline she'd paid out behind her, faith that Ovid's memory of a ship he'd catalogued thirty years ago was worth her life.

Her mother had died in a place like this. The thought arrived uninvited, calm as a fish drifting past her faceplate. Somewhere in the dark, in the cold, in the crushing quiet after something went wrong — that was where Marian Montrose had spent her last minutes. Tamsin had spent twenty years not thinking about it. Now she was swimming directly into the shape of it, and the shape fit her like a glove.

*Read it and put it down.*

The cargo trunk loomed out of the murk — or rather, its edges did, a black rectangle of deeper black, exhaling a steady current that tugged her toward it like a polite invitation. Sefa had been right. The wreck was breathing. Every collapse somewhere below sent a fresh gulp of water through the hull, and the hull answered with a groan she felt in her teeth.

She went in against the breath.

The portside passage was worse than she'd feared. Debris hung everywhere in suspension — splinters of decking, a rain of rust flakes, what might have been a chair turning slowly like a thing asleep. Her light found the second bulkhead door half-crushed, its frame bowed inward, a gap at the top barely wide enough for a diver with a tank. Beyond it, darkness, and beneath the darkness, faintly, impossibly, a sound.

A tapping. Metal on metal. Three fast, one slow.

Brannoc's signal. They'd invented it the first week, bored on watch — three taps for *all well*, one slow for *come find me*. He'd laughed when he taught it to her, said it was the only code a man needed, everything else was decoration.

He was alive. He was alive and he was tapping, and the corridor between her and him was a throat full of broken teeth.

She squeezed through the gap, felt her tank scrape stone-hard rust, and pushed into the junction beyond. Her light swept across collapsed plating, a tangle of cable, and then — pinned under a fallen section of overhead framing, one leg bent under him at an angle that made her own leg ache in sympathy — Brannoc.

His mask was cracked. Blood threaded from his hairline, dissolving into the water in a lazy red ribbon. But his eyes found her light and crinkled, and even through the regulator mouthpiece she could see him trying to smile, because Brannoc would try to smile at his own funeral and probably heckle the eulogy.

"Took you long enough," he mumbled around the mouthpiece, slurred and bubbling. "Thought I'd have to rescue myself. Again."

"Shut up and save your air." She was already working, hands moving on their own — checking his tank, half gone; checking the pinning debris, a beam resting across his calf with the whole descending weight of the corridor behind it; checking the frame around them, which shuddered as if in answer. "Can you move the leg?"

"No." No drama in it. Just engineering. "Beam's load-bearing now. Move the beam, the rest comes down. Leave me the clamp cutter, I'll cut myself loose and follow at my own pace, which will be dignified and slow."

"You're not staying in a collapsing wreck to be dignified."

"Somebody has to model good behavior."

The frame groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a whale grieving. Dust sifted down through her light beam. Above them — she could feel it more than see it — the decks were continuing their slow argument with gravity, and gravity was winning.

*Tammy.* Her mother's voice, unhelpfully, in her memory. *When you're trapped, stop pulling. Start thinking.*

Fine. Thinking. What did she have? A jammed man, a failing air supply, a corridor that wanted to become a grave. What did she need? Leverage. A fulcrum. Something to lift the beam without lifting the sky.

The clamp cutter. Brannoc had carried it down to jam the demolition charges — a heavy salvage tool, part pry bar, part hydraulic jaw. It was lying three feet away in the rubble, and it was, she realized, the exact shape of a lever.

"Brannoc. The cutter. If I wedge it under the beam and jack it — how much clearance do you need?"

He considered, eyes half-focused, blood and bubbles. "Four inches. Five to be safe."

"And if the beam shifts while I'm jacking?"

"Then we'll both learn something new about structural failure."

She wedged the cutter's jaw under the beam, braced her shoulder against the frame, and pushed. Nothing. Pushed harder, boots finding purchase on a pipe, the whole strength of her back and legs funneled into one point of contact. The beam creaked. Gave an inch. Two.

"Three," Brannoc reported helpfully, hauling himself up the slope on his elbows, dragging the bad leg. "Four. God, Montrose, what do they feed you—"

The beam snapped free of its seating.

Later, she would remember it as a single continuous motion, though it couldn't have been: the beam swinging up, the cutter flying loose, Brannoc dragging himself clear, and the entire overhead coming down into the space where he'd been pinned — a roar she felt rather than heard, a brown avalanche, her own body slamming flat against the deck plates as debris hammered past like hail. Then silence, thick and total, and her light showing nothing but settled ruin and one boot protruding from behind a fallen plate.

"Brannoc!"

The boot moved. A hand appeared next to it, waving slowly, and then his head, gray with silt, mask fogged, one eye visible and twinkling with what she could only describe as professional appreciation.

"Leverage," he said reverently. "Every time. It's always leverage."

"Air check. Now."

He checked. She watched his shoulders drop. "Eight minutes."

"I've got twelve. Can you swim?"

"On one leg? I can swim beautifully. Like a wounded seal. A proud wounded seal."

* * *

Getting him out was the hardest work of her life, and she'd once spent nine hours cutting a fouled anchor chain in a surge. He couldn't kick, so she took his weight across her shoulders — his tank harness locked to hers, his arm over her neck, his bad leg trailing — and swam the corridor against the current with a grown man's worth of drag and a dwindling gauge needle. Twice the wreck shuddered around them and shed more debris, and twice she felt him tense against her back and then deliberately relax, trusting her, trusting the plan, trusting Ovid's thirty-year-old memory of a crawl space that appeared exactly where the archivist swore it would be.

At the junction, the current tried to keep them. It poured through the crushed bulkhead door in a steady river, and for one horrible moment Tamsin and Brannoc hung in it like flags, going nowhere, her air needle touching five minutes and her arms burning and the exit a black rectangle that might as well have been on the moon.

Then the rectangle filled with light.

Sefa. The girl came through the gap like she'd been born in it — no tank, just a snorkel and a lungful of borrowed air and the absolute certainty of someone who had grown up reading water the way other children learned to read faces. She grabbed Brannoc's harness, pointed emphatically at the current, then at herself, then made a gesture Tamsin understood instantly: *let the water carry him, steer, don't fight.*

Of course. The wreck was breathing out. Ride the exhale.

They went through the bulkhead gap in a chain — Sefa first with Brannoc's harness in both fists, Tamsin behind with a hand on his tank valve — and the current took them, shot them down the cargo trunk in a rushing brown blur, past twisted frames and hanging cables, and spat all three of them out through the cargo hatch into open blue water so suddenly that Tamsin's ears popped and the sun hit her mask like a blessing.

Above, distorted and wavering, the hull of the Kestrel's Wake. Hands reaching down. Ovid's face, peering over the rail with an expression of such naked relief that it aged him ten years backward.

They broke the surface together, gasping, and Sefa was laughing — actually laughing, high and wild, the first time Tamsin had ever heard her do it — and Brannoc was coughing seawater and swearing with tremendous creativity, and Tamsin found that she was holding onto the ladder with both hands and shaking, and that the shaking was fine, was permitted, was just information being read and slowly put down.

* * *

Brannoc sat wrapped in blankets in the cockpit, leg splinted with a boat hook and duct tape — "temporary," he kept saying, "purely temporary" — drinking the medicinal rum Ovid had produced from some hidden reserve with the solemnity of a man administering last rites. His scalp wound had stopped bleeding. His color was coming back. He was, Tamsin realized with a strange lurch, going to be fine, and the realization hit her harder than the collapse had.

"That's twice," she said, sitting down across from him. "Twice you've nearly died on this wreck."

"Third time's traditional." He raised the rum. "Besides, I got the charges jammed before the second one fired. That's why it went off early, incidentally — my clamp shorted the timer. So technically the navy's demolition failed, the wreck's still half-standing, and I am a hero who requires absolutely no further diving, ever, per doctor's orders." He gestured at himself with the bottle. "I'm the doctor."

"The documents," Ovid said quietly. He hadn't touched the rum. He was sitting very straight, hands folded, looking at Brannoc with the careful stillness of a man afraid of the answer. "The strongroom. Did you reach it?"

Brannoc's grin faded into something gentler. He fumbled in the pouch at his belt — the little canvas roll he carried everywhere, the one that held his tools and, apparently, other people's hopes — and drew out a brass tube, sealed at both ends, dented but intact.

"Wasn't time to open the wheel all the way. But the antechamber seal was already cracked from the first charge. I reached in blind." He turned the tube over in his hands, and for a moment his voice went rough. "There's a letter in there too, Tamsin. Sealed. Addressed in ink, not print." He held it out to her. "It's got your name on it."

The cockpit went very quiet. Outside, the first true gusts of the monsoon's leading edge began to sing through the rigging, and the Kestrel's Wake leaned into the wind like a horse scenting home.

Tamsin took the tube. It was heavier than brass had any right to be — heavy with paper, with truth, with twenty years of a question she'd stopped asking. Her mother's handwriting, she thought. In there. Waiting.

"Not yet," Sefa said softly, watching her face. "Not in the storm. Wait till we're through."

Tamsin looked around the cockpit — Brannoc swaddled and smug, Ovid pale and reverent, Sefa steady as a compass needle — at the ragged, impossible family the sea had handed her one stray at a time.

"After," she agreed, and closed her hand around the tube. "After we run the storm."

Brannoc raised the rum bottle again, wincing as his splinted leg knocked the table. "To running storms," he declared. "Badly, but together."

The monsoon swallowed the horizon, and the Kestrel's Wake ran to meet it.
