## 30. Surface

The first thing I felt when I broke the surface was the rain — fat, warm, hammering drops that hit like small apologies. The second thing was Sefa's hands on my harness, hauling me toward the Wake's boarding ladder, and the third thing was the sound Brannoc made when they laid him on the deck: a wet, rattling groan that was, thank every god who'd ever bothered with the sea, alive.

"Breathing," Sefa shouted over the wind. "He's breathing!"

"Of course he is," Ovid said, and I heard the crack in his voice even through the storm. "The man argued with a compressor for six hours yesterday. Death doesn't stand a chance against that kind of stubbornness."

I clawed off my mask and knelt beside him. Brannoc's face was gray as old rope, his lips blue, a gash above his eyebrow weeping pink into the rainwater pooling on the deck. But his chest rose. It fell. It rose again. His hand — that enormous, scarred, impossible hand — twitched once, found my wrist, and gripped it with the last of his considerable strength.

"Charges," he rasped.

"They went off. I know. I was there."

"No." His eyes opened, unfocused, swimming. "The *third* one. Didn't go off. I — I pulled the wire. Jammed the firing box. There's a charge still sitting on the strongroom hull and it's *live*, Tamsin, and nobody knows where—"

"Shh." I pressed my forehead against his soaked, salt-crusted hair, which was not a thing I had ever done to another human being and which I found, to my surprise, I could not stop doing. "You jammed the firing box. You beautiful, impossible man. You jammed the firing box."

"Did I get the documents?"

I held up the brass tube, still lashed to my harness, dented on one side where the reef had kissed me on the way up. "Sealed. Dry. All of it."

Brannoc looked at the tube. He looked at me. And then he did something I had never seen him do in all the weeks of rust and fury and borrowed parts: he smiled, a real one, lopsided and exhausted, and passed out cold in the rain.

* * *

We got him below into the galley, which was the warmest place on the boat because the stove's flue had never worked properly and Brannoc had never fixed it, on the grounds that a cold galley kept the crew honest and a warm one kept them lazy. Ovid stripped off his diving gloves with shaking hands and started wrapping Brannoc's ribs while Sefa bailed — because of course the Kestrel's Wake was taking water, because of course she was, because she was the Kestrel's Wake — and I stood at the wheel with the brass tube strapped across my back like a second spine and steered us out of the killing ground.

Behind us, the Corvane was dying.

I could see it even through the rain, even through the gray chaos of the monsoon's leading edge rolling in off the open sea like a second, darker ocean standing on end. The demolition charges Reyes's team had laid before Brannoc jammed the firing box had done their work on the outer hull. The old wreck had held together for two hundred years under sand and current and the patient weight of the sea, and it took the navy eleven minutes to break her back. I watched the stern section — the part that held the strongroom, the part that held my mother — sag, shudder, and fold into itself, and then the silt came up. A brown bloom the size of a village, rising through the green water, swallowing the broken hull the way the sea swallows everything eventually, just faster.

Somewhere under that cloud was the rest of the gold. Somewhere under that cloud was the charge Brannoc had jammed, sitting live on the strongroom hull, which no demolition team would ever find again, and which would sit there, I supposed, until the sea itself decided what to do about it. Somewhere under that cloud was a skeleton in a dive rig with one hand on a wheel, and I was not going to be able to come back for her, ever, and I knew it, and the knowing went through me like a harpoon line paying out.

"Tamsin." Sefa's voice, close. She'd come up soaked and shivering, hair plastered to her skull, and she put her hand on my arm. "You have to breathe too. That's the whole trick of it."

"I'm steering."

"You're white-knuckling the wheel and staring at a cloud of mud. Ovid can steer. Ovid steers like a drunk librarian, but he can steer." She glanced back at the silt bloom, and I saw her jaw tighten. "It's gone, isn't it. For them, I mean. Reyes will never find it now."

"Nobody will. That's the point of a monsoon." I made myself let go of the wheel one finger at a time. "Sefa. The tube."

"I see it."

"It's heavier than it looks."

"Everything true is." She said it simply, the way she read currents, and then she shoved a mug of something hot and terrible into my hands — Brannoc's emergency tea, the kind he brewed from compressed bricks that tasted like a ship's bilge had aspirations — and went back to bailing.

I drank it. It was awful. I drank all of it.

* * *

The monsoon hit us properly an hour later, and I will not pretend we handled it gracefully. The Kestrel's Wake took a wave over the bow that filled the foredeck like a bathtub, tore loose the spare jerry can we'd lashed by the rail, and knocked Ovid flat while he was trying to take a bearing, so that the bearing he took was of the inside of the cabin roof, which he reported as "overcast, with a chance of ceiling."

We ran east-northeast, away from the reef, away from Reyes, away from the brown bloom on the water behind us, and the Wake — bless her rotten, stubborn, borrowed-parts heart — ran. She groaned like a dying whale and leaked like a politician's promise and she *ran*, because Brannoc had rebuilt her engine housing around a core of pure spite, and even unconscious in the galley, the man's spite held.

I went below once, when Sefa forced me, and found Brannoc propped against the bulkhead with Ovid's coat over his shoulders, awake, gray but awake, watching the deckhead with an engineer's expression.

"You should be sleeping."

"I slept. For a whole hour. I feel reborn." He coughed, winced, held his ribs. "The tube. Let me see it."

I unslung it and set it in his lap. He turned it over with his big clumsy hands, gentle as a man handling a bird's egg, checking the seals, the dents, the threads. Then he looked up at me.

"You left the gold."

"I left the gold."

"There was — Tamsin, there was enough down there to buy this boat a hundred times over. To buy us all out. Pensions. Dry land. You could have—"

"I know what was down there." I sat down across from him, and the boat rolled, and the lamp swung, and for a moment his shadow and mine swung together across the bulkhead like two people dancing badly. "Voss made me promise. Raise what's in the strongroom, and give the documents to the families. He never said a word about gold. I used to think that was because he was dying and careless with his words." I turned the tube over in my hands. "He wasn't careless. He knew exactly what was in there. He knew the gold was bait and the papers were the prize, and he wanted someone who'd take the prize and leave the bait for the sea."

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. Rain drummed on the deck above us like a thousand small impatient fingers.

"Your mother," he said. "She chose the same way. Didn't she."

"Yeah."

"And you hate that you're glad."

I laughed — a short, cracked, surprised sound, because he'd put his finger exactly on it, the way engineers do, finding the flaw under the paint. "I hate that I'm glad," I agreed. "Now sleep, or I'll put Ovid on watching you, and he'll narrate."

"That *is* a threat." Brannoc closed his eyes. Then, half-gone already: "Tamsin. The charge I jammed. If the sea ever moves it wrong—"

"Then the sea will have to argue with you about it. And we've established how that goes for the other party."

He smiled again, that lopsided exhausted smile, and slept, and this time I let him.

* * *

We cleared the worst of the leading edge near dawn, riding the storm's shoulder the way Sefa seemed to feel in her bones rather than calculate. She stood beside me at the wheel for the last dark hours, calling the waves before they came — "big one, left of the bow, brace" — and she was never wrong, not once, and I stopped being surprised by it somewhere around the fourth hour and just started trusting it, which is, I think, what trust actually is. Not a decision. A running out of surprises.

The sky went from black to bruise to a pale, washed, rain-scoured gray, and the sea behind us settled into long rolling hills, and there was nothing there. No reef. No wreck. No fleet.

I swept the horizon with the glasses anyway. Old habit. And for one cold second I caught a shape — a smudge of smoke, a gray suggestion of a hull, far off to the south, running before the same weather we were. Reyes, or one of hers, limping home with a job half done and a wreck that no longer existed to finish it. I watched the smudge until the haze took it, and I felt — and I want to be honest here, because the logbook deserves honesty even if I rarely do — I felt nothing like victory.

I felt like a woman who had dug a thorn out of her own hand. It hurts, it bleeds, you're glad, and you don't call it triumph. You call it *finally*.

"Reyes?" Sefa asked. She'd seen me watching.

"Maybe. Doesn't matter. She's lost it. The wreck, the gold, the confession — all of it's under forty feet of new silt and a monsoon that won't quit for a month. By the time anyone could dive there again, the charts will be wrong and the coordinates will be a rumor." I lowered the glasses. "She spent a career making things disappear. The sea just did it for free."

"She'll be furious."

"She'll be *relieved*, somewhere under the fury. That's the worst part about Ilsa Reyes. She never wanted the treasure. She wanted the past to stop existing. And now it has." I handed Sefa the glasses. "Keep an eye out anyway. Relieved people make mistakes."

* * *

Ovid emerged from below near mid-morning holding the brass tube like a relic, having apparently appointed himself its custodian the moment Brannoc fell asleep, and made me a cup of the terrible tea without being asked, which was his way of saying things he couldn't say. We sat on the aft deck in the washed light, three of us — Sefa at the wheel, Ovid and me and the tube between us — and the Kestrel's Wake rolled on toward Meridian through a sea that looked, for the first time in weeks, like ordinary water.

"Thirty years," Ovid said. "I catalogued that manifest. I shelved the governor's confession in a sealed drawer and I wrote the finding aid myself and I went home and slept, and the next day, and the day after that, for thirty years." He turned the tube in his hands. "And now it's in a dented brass tube on a leaking survey boat, and in three days it'll be in the hands of the families it was stolen from, and I keep waiting to feel different. I don't. I just feel — lighter. Is that enough? Is lighter enough?"

I thought about my mother's skeleton with its hand on the wheel. I thought about Voss coughing seawater and blood into a port dive bar, trading a map for a promise. I thought about Brannoc's smile, and Sefa's hand on my arm, and the brown bloom swallowing the Corvane behind us.

"It's everything," I said. "Lighter is everything. The rest is just weight."

Ovid nodded slowly, and then, because he was Ovid, he ruined it: "That was almost eloquent. Are you feeling quite well?"

"Throw him overboard, Sefa."

"I can't hear you, I'm steering."

But she was smiling, and so was he, and so, I realized with some alarm, was I — a real one, cracked and salt-burned and exhausted, and it didn't fix anything, and it didn't need to.

Behind us the monsoon stood on the horizon like a wall, and somewhere under its gray skirts the Corvane slept at last, unrobbed, unburied by men, kept by the sea along with my mother and her knife and one live charge and a dead man's promise. Ahead of us lay three days of open water, a leaking boat, a broken engineer, a tube full of truth, and a port full of families who were about to learn what had been done to them and by whom.

I went below and got the logbook, and I wrote the position, the weather, and the state of the crew, and then I sat with the pen hovering over the page for a long moment before writing one more line:

*Wreck lost to the sea. Documents saved. Brannoc breathing. Nobody left behind.*

It wasn't poetry. It didn't need to be.

Above me, Sefa called down that she could smell land on the wind, which was impossible at this distance, and Ovid called back that the wind was from the east and there was no land east, and Sefa called back that she knew what she smelled, and I sat in the galley between my sleeping engineer and my dented brass tube and listened to my crew argue about the wind, and I thought: *this. This is what she was trying to buy with her life. Not the gold. This.*

Then the engine made a noise no engine should make, Brannoc sat bolt upright in his sleep and said "the fuel line, the *fuel line*," and I went to find out which of us the sea was going to try to kill next.
