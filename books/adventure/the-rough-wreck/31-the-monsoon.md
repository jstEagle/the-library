## 31. The Monsoon

*Tamsin*

The wall of water came out of the southeast like a decision already made.

Tamsin stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Wake* and watched it come — not a squall line, not a front, but a single unbroken slab of black cloud sitting on the sea from horizon to horizon, and beneath it a curtain of rain so dense it looked solid, as if someone had stood the ocean on end and leaned it against the sky.

"Brannoc," she said into the voice tube, "tell me something encouraging."

The tube crackled. "The engine's running."

"That's not encouraging, that's a fact."

"The engine's running *well*, then. And I've stuffed the worst of the leaks with what I'm fairly sure is Ovid's spare shirt."

There was a muffled protest from somewhere below that the voice tube faithfully delivered: "That was my *good* shirt—"

"It's doing more good plugging a seam than it ever did on your back," Brannoc called over him. "Now stop talking and start bailing."

Tamsin allowed herself half a smile, which was all the storm left room for. Behind her, the Corvane was gone — swallowed whole by silt and surge, taking Reyes's fleet's prize with it. Ahead of her, the monsoon. Between the two, forty feet of rotten survey boat, four exhausted people, one brass tube sealed with documents worth more than any gold, and a wounded engineer who had nearly died in the wreck an hour ago and was now, against all medical advice including his own, wedged in the engine compartment with a wrench in each hand.

"Sefa!" she shouted forward. "How does she look?"

Sefa stood braced at the bow rail, hair plastered flat, reading the water the way other people read books. She turned, cupped her hands, and yelled back across the rising wind: "Bad! Then worse! Then there's a channel!"

"Which part do we aim for?"

"The channel!"

That was Sefa for you. Tamsin had known her three weeks and could count her complete sentences on one hand, but every one of them had been load-bearing.

She put the wheel over and ran for the black wall.

* * *

*Sefa*

The rain hit like a thrown bucket, then like a river, then like the sky simply giving up and coming down all at once. Sefa lashed herself to the bow rail with two turns of line and a knot her grandmother had taught her, the kind you could tie blind and drunk and freezing, which was fortunate, because she was two of those three.

Underneath the roar, she listened to the sea.

People thought the monsoon was chaos. It wasn't. Chaos was random. The monsoon was enormous and angry, but it was still the sea, and the sea always told you where it was going if you shut up long enough to hear. There — under the wind's howl, a lower sound, a grinding rush: current squeezing through the reef shelf to starboard, fast and shallow. And ahead-left, a deeper silence in the noise, a hole in the sound where the water went suddenly deep and calm between two coral fingers.

"Port!" she screamed, and felt the boat answer before Tamsin even finished turning the wheel. They'd stopped being captain and crew somewhere in the last hour. Now they were just hands on the same rope.

A wave came aboard amidships — a whole wave, a ton of green water with opinions — and swept aft, and through the sheeting rain Sefa saw Ovid go down under it, flailing, and grab the bail-line coil, and hold on with both arms like a man embracing a relative he didn't like but refused to let drown.

"You're meant to bail *with* the line, not marry it!" she yelled.

"I am establishing," Ovid bellowed back, dignity intact despite being soaked to the skin and wearing no shirt, "a *system*!"

He was. That was the thing about Ovid. Thirty years in an archive had taught him that everything — everything — could be organized, and he had organized their bailing into shifts and stations and a rotation he'd drawn on the back of Voss's chart wrapping paper, and it was ridiculous, and it worked. The boat was half-flooded and staying exactly half-flooded, which in Sefa's professional opinion was the most anyone could hope for tonight.

Another wave. Another. The world was white noise and green water and the small stubborn shape of the boat climbing each hill and sliding down the far side, and Sefa laughed — actually laughed, alone at the bow in the teeth of it — because her grandmother used to say you could tell a real sailor by whether they cried or grinned when the sky fell, and Sefa had spent sixteen years crying, and this, *this*, with salt in her throat and her family's stolen truth sealed in a tube ten feet behind her, was the first time in her life she'd ever grinned.

* * *

*Brannoc*

Down in the engine compartment, Brannoc had achieved a state of communion with the machinery that he would later describe, when drunk, as religious.

The old paraffin beast had been dying when they bought the *Wake*, and he had performed what he liked to call triage and everyone else called crimes. Parts from three different engines lived in her now, none of them designed to meet, all of them held together by bolts he'd machined himself and a quantity of sealant that was technically food-grade because it was all he had left. The engine did not run well. The engine ran *personally* — as though it understood, in some dim mechanical way, that tonight was not a night to be temperamental.

Which was lucky, because seawater had found the bilge again, and the bilge pump had given up its career, and Brannoc had rigged a replacement from a salvaged winch motor and a length of fire hose in about four minutes while lying half-submerged in his own workshop.

His ribs ached where the collapse had caught him. Every jolt of the boat drove a small hot knife between them. He breathed shallow and kept working, because that was the deal he'd made with himself down there in the dark under the Corvane, pinned under a deck beam with the water rising: *you get out, you get to be useful.* He hadn't expected to collect on it so soon.

The voice tube whistled. Tamsin's voice, shredded by wind: "Engine report!"

"She's giving me everything she has and a bit she doesn't have!" he shouted back. "Ask me again in an hour and I'll either lie better or we'll be dead!"

A pause. Then, faintly, her laugh — ragged, real, carried down the tube like a gift. Brannoc found himself grinning into the dark, alone with his beautiful illegal engine, and thought: *well. If this is how it ends, at least it ends in good company.*

He'd been alone a long time before the *Wake*. Disgraced engineers usually were. He'd told himself he preferred it — no one to see your failures, no one to disappoint twice. But there is a particular warmth in hearing people move around above you in a storm — boots on deck plates, voices shouting coordinates and curses, someone — Ovid, unmistakably — reciting the rotation schedule aloud to himself like scripture. It was the sound of a ship that was alive, and he was part of why it stayed that way.

Brannoc tightened a bolt, patted the engine block the way you'd pat a tired horse, and said, "Not tonight, old girl. Tonight we're *magnificent*."

The engine, being a machine and not a horse, ignored him. But it kept running.

* * *

*Ovid*

Ovid had been in perhaps four hundred storms in his life, and by this he meant storms of paperwork, scandal, committee meetings, and once, memorably, a riot in the colonial archive over a misfiled deed. He had assumed these experiences had prepared him for weather.

They had not. Nothing prepared a man for bailing seawater with a cooking pot while wearing no shirt, in the dark, on a boat that pitched like a metronome built by a madman.

And yet.

He bailed. He counted strokes — two hundred per shift, then pass the pot — and between counts he recited the deck plan of the *Corvane* from memory, quietly, the way other men prayed. It steadied him. Somewhere below the waves behind them lay the wreck he had catalogued the looting of thirty years ago and never once spoken of; somewhere in the cabin behind him lay the documents that would undo it. His thirty years of silence were in that brass tube. His one chance at a different thirty years was in this boat, in this storm, in this pot of seawater.

"Two hundred," he announced, and passed the pot to Sefa, who had come aft dripping and grinning like a feral cat.

"You're smiling," she accused.

"I am *bailing*."

"You're smiling *while* bailing. That's worse."

Ovid considered this. The boat climbed a wave, hung at the top for a heart-stopping moment with the whole black world visible — rain, reef, the distant smudge of Reyes's fleet lights lost astern, swallowed — and then plunged down the far side with a shudder that rattled his teeth.

"I believe," he said, "that I am happy. It's extremely inconvenient. I'll look into having it treated."

Sefa snorted — an undignified, delighted sound — and took the pot.

Later, much later, when the wind had gone from murderous to merely furious and the crew gathered in the flooded cabin to spell each other off, Ovid looked around at them and understood something he should have understood weeks ago. Tamsin, asleep sitting upright with her hand still on the wheel until Brannoc physically pried her loose. Brannoc, gray-faced with pain, refusing to stop checking the engine "one more time." Sefa, who had lost everything twice over, teaching Ovid a knot with the patience of someone handing down an inheritance.

They were not strangers who had hired onto the same desperate scheme. They had not been strangers since the third tide, possibly since the first. Somewhere between the lies and the wrecks and the transmitter and the charges, without anyone deciding it, they had become the thing none of them had ever managed to be on dry land.

A family. A loud, damp, argumentative family, currently possessed of one boat, no money, and a great deal of seawater.

Ovid wrote it down in his head, in the voice he used for history: *On the night of the monsoon, the crew of the Kestrel's Wake had nothing left except one another, and discovered this was sufficient.*

Then Sefa threw a pot of bilgewater at him for falling asleep mid-shift, and he revised the entry to add: *Barely sufficient. Barely.*

* * *

*Tamsin*

Dawn came the way dawn comes inside a storm: not as light but as a rumor of light, a graying of the black, enough to see the waves that had been trying to kill them all night.

They had made it. Tamsin checked the position in her head three times because she didn't believe it, then a fourth time aloud, and Sefa confirmed it with a nod that contained, for Sefa, roughly the emotion of a stranger dancing in the street.

They had outrun the worst of it. The fleet was behind them, blind, and the Meridian islands lay a day ahead, and the documents were dry in their tube, and everyone aboard was alive — bruised, half-drowned, shirtless in Ovid's case, but alive.

Tamsin stood at the wheel and let the rain wash her face and felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since Kestrel Point, since Voss, since the promise. Maybe since her mother. The boat rolled under her, battered and ugly and floating, and she loved it with a fierce, embarrassed completeness.

"All right," she said, and her voice came out rough as gravel. "Shift change. Somebody wake Ovid gently."

Brannoc, wrapped in a blanket and looking like a drowned poet, raised a hand. "Define gently."

"However you like. He bailed on my shift twice."

From below, with tremendous dignity: "I heard that. And for the record, the second time was *strategic consolidation of effort*."

"Strategic consolidation," Tamsin repeated. "Is that what we're calling sleeping?"

But she was laughing as she said it — really laughing, the kind that hurt the ribs she hadn't even injured — and Sefa was laughing too, and even Brannoc, wincing, laughing, and the sound went out over the gray water and got torn away by the wind, and it didn't matter, because it hadn't been for the wind. It had been for them.

The *Kestrel's Wake* ran on through the fading storm, half-flooded, patched with shirts and stubbornness, carrying nothing worth stealing and everything worth keeping.

It was Sefa, at the bow, who saw it first and went very still — the particular stillness she got when the sea told her something important. She pointed south, off the bow, toward the thin gray line where the Meridian islands would be.

Smoke. A column of it, straight and deliberate, rising off the water near the outer passage.

Not a chimney. Not a cookfire.

A navy picket boat, anchored square in the only deep-water channel into the archipelago — waiting, patient as a spider, right on the route every chart said they had to take.

Tamsin's laughter died in her throat. She looked at Sefa. Sefa looked back, and said the truest thing anyone had said all night:

"There's another way in. But it isn't on any chart."
