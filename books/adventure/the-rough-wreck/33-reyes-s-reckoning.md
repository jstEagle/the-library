## 33. Reyes's Reckoning

The recall order arrived at 0400, which told Reyes everything she needed to know about how it had been written.

Ministers who wanted to congratulate you sent their messages at a civilized hour, over tea, with adjectives. Ministers who wanted to feed you to an inquiry sent them before dawn, when the harbor was gray and the typists were still asleep and there was no one around to see the courier's face. She read it standing on the flagship's quarterdeck in her dressing gown, the paper damp from the sea air, and felt something in her chest that was not quite relief and not quite dread but occupied the same address as both.

*Captain Ilsa Reyes, commanding officer, Salvage Fleet Three, is hereby recalled to Meridian Station to answer questions pertaining to the loss of the wreck designated CV-114 and the disposition of materials recovered therefrom.*

*Disposition of materials recovered.* As if the Corvane were a crate of mislabeled boots.

She read it twice, folded it along its original creases, and put it in her breast pocket, where she kept things she intended to look at again when she was older. Then she stood at the rail and watched the monsoon's aftermath rake the water where the wreck used to be, and thought: *well. There it is.*

Thirty-one years of service. Four commendations, one reprimand, two ships lost under her command and neither of them her fault, though only one of those facts had ever been officially established. And now this — a dawn telegram and a long ride north to sit in a room with men who had never been wet in their lives and explain why a treasure ship full of inconvenient paper no longer existed.

They would not ask about the charges. That was the first thing she understood, watching the light come up over the reef. They would not ask about the charges because they had signed off on the charges. The minister's man had been very clear, that night in the flagship's cabin, brandy untouched between them like a border marker. *Make it vanish, Captain. Before the season turns.* The inquiry would ask instead why she had failed — why a survey boat crewed by a disgraced engineer, a runaway girl, and a librarian had slipped her fleet through a reef maze in a rising storm, and why the documents were now sitting in the hands of the Meridian families like a lit fuse.

They would ask why she hadn't caught a woman named Tamsin Montrose.

And she would have to answer honestly, which was the worst of it, because Reyes had built her whole career on never having to lie — only on choosing, carefully, what was true.

* * *

The Kestrel's Wake was anchored in the lee roadstead, three cables off the breakwater, looking — Reyes had to admit — considerably less doomed than the last time she'd seen her. New paint on the hull where the rust had been cut out. A proper crane arm welded to the deckhouse, ugly as a broken finger but obviously functional. Smoke from the galley stack, carrying the smell of frying onions, which struck her as an almost obscene thing for a salvage boat to smell like. Boats in her world smelled of diesel and fear.

She went over alone, in a shore boat, wearing civilian clothes for the first time in eleven years. The jacket didn't fit right anymore. She'd bought it in a hurry from a shop whose owner had looked at her the way shopkeepers look at women who stand like they're reviewing troops, and she'd paid too much for it on principle.

Tamsin Montrose met her at the gangway.

Reyes had prepared herself for anger. Anger she could work with; anger had structure, angles you could brace against. What she got instead was something worse — a kind of tired courtesy, the look of a woman who had already fought this fight in her head and won it, and was now just curious whether the losing side knew it had lost.

"Captain." Tamsin stepped aside to let her aboard. "You're a long way from your fleet."

"My fleet is being inventoried." Reyes came up the gangway slowly. Her knee — the left one, the one the sea had been collecting rent on since '09 — objected to the ladder, and she let it object without letting it show. "I'm being recalled. Inquiry at Meridian Station."

"I heard." Tamsin's face gave away nothing, which was another thing she'd learned from Reyes, and Reyes found she resented that more than she'd expected to. "Tea? We've got tea. It's terrible tea."

"Terrible tea," Reyes said, "is the only honest beverage left in the service. Yes."

* * *

The galley of the Kestrel's Wake was small, warm, and cluttered in a way that navy regulations existed specifically to prevent. Charts pinned over charts. A rack of diving knives that had been sharpened recently enough to catch the lamplight. A cat — an actual cat, orange and enormous, asleep in the bread drawer — opened one eye, assessed her, and closed it again with the verdict of a creature who had seen worse.

"The engineer will be down in a minute," Tamsin said, pouring. "He smells company. He can't help it. Sefa's aloft somewhere pretending not to watch us through the rigging, so if you say anything interesting, assume it gets repeated."

"That's efficient."

"It saves on meetings."

Reyes took the tea. It was, as promised, terrible — stewed past all decency, brewed in a pot that had seen things. She drank it anyway, because refusing hospitality on this boat would have been a kind of retreat, and she had not come here to retreat.

"You know," she said, "when I taught you to dive, you were afraid of the dark under the hull plates. You wouldn't say so. You'd just go quiet, and then you'd go anyway, every time, and I used to think — that one's going to drown herself someday out of sheer stubbornness."

"I remember." Tamsin sat across from her, arms folded. "You also told me the dark under the plates is where the truth lives. Your words."

"My words." Reyes turned the cup in her hands. "I said a lot of things in those years. Most of them were true. That's the trouble with truths — they don't stay where you put them."

The hatch banged open and a mountain of a man came through it sideways, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its original purpose sometime last year. Brannoc. She knew the file: dismissed from the naval yard for insubordination, which in her experience usually meant he'd been right loudly. He stopped dead when he saw her, and his whole face did something complicated — part hostility, part professional appraisal of her jacket's poor tailoring.

"You," he said.

"Me," Reyes agreed.

"You cost me my pension appeal. Your office sat on my paperwork for two years."

"Your paperwork wasn't sat on, Mr. Brannoc. It was buried deliberately, by a lieutenant commander named Hask, because you'd embarrassed him at the yard review. I found out about it four months ago and unburied it. You should have a letter waiting at the port office. With back pay."

The silence that followed had texture. Brannoc looked at Tamsin, who looked at the ceiling with the expression of a woman who had decided not to manage this moment. The cat, sensing drama, emerged from the bread drawer to supervise.

"Huh," Brannoc said finally. He shoved the rag in his pocket. "Huh. Well." He pointed a thick finger at her. "That doesn't make us square. But it makes the tea marginally less poisoned." And he poured himself a cup and sat down at the end of the bench, as far from her as the little room allowed, which was perhaps four feet, and drank it glaring at the middle distance.

From above, faintly, came the creak of someone shifting their weight on the ratlines. Listening. Reyes inclined her head toward the deckhouse roof by a degree, and Tamsin almost smiled.

* * *

They ended up on deck, because the galley had gotten crowded and because some conversations need weather in them. The afternoon was doing its best impression of forgiveness — high broken cloud, a wind out of the southeast with the monsoon's teeth finally pulled, the harbor water going green-gold where the sun got through. Somewhere aft, Ovid was reading aloud to nobody in particular, his voice drifting over the rail in the cadence of a man who had spent thirty years talking to archives.

Reyes stood at the stern rail and looked north, toward Meridian Station, four days' steam. Behind her she heard Tamsin come up and stop, not close, not far. Waiting. The girl had always known how to wait. It was the first thing Reyes had ever admired about her, back when she'd been a skinny dock-rat with her mother's eyes and her mother's refusal to fill a silence just because silence was uncomfortable.

"Say it," Tamsin said. "Whatever you came to say. You didn't row over here in a borrowed jacket to compliment our paintwork."

"No." Reyes watched a fishing boat working the channel markers, small and patient. "I came to tell you the truth. Once. Unofficially, off any record, because once I'm in that inquiry room everything I say belongs to other people."

"All right."

"Your mother testified at the board hearing. Did you ever read the transcript?"

"Some of it."

"Then you read her statement. She'd found the Corvane. She'd raised documents out of the strongroom — not gold, documents — and she brought them to the station, and she asked the board to open an investigation into the governor's estate. And the board asked me, as her fleet commander and her former student, whether I believed her claims were credible." Reyes kept her eyes on the fishing boat. It was easier than the alternative. "And I said no."

The wind moved through the rigging overhead. On the roof of the deckhouse, very quietly, the stowaway listener went still.

"She was credible," Reyes continued. "Her evidence was sound. Her dive logs were meticulous — better than mine, and mine were good. I knew within the first ten minutes of that hearing that she was telling the truth. And I said no, because the minister's office had already spoken to me. Because the fleet I commanded was funded by contracts that would have evaporated if the Corvane became a scandal. Because forty people drew wages under me, and I chose them over her." She paused. "Because it was easier. All of those reasons are true, and none of them are excuses, and I am not going to insult you by picking which one sounds best."

Tamsin said nothing for a long time. When Reyes finally glanced over, the younger woman was looking at the water, jaw tight, hands gripping the rail — and Reyes saw, with a clarity that felt like cold water, not the salvage captain she'd become but the nineteen-year-old she'd been, standing in the same posture at her mother's memorial, refusing to cry in front of the navy delegation.

"You took her career," Tamsin said quietly. "They took her livelihood, and then the sea took her, and you — you were the hand that held the door open for all of it."

"Yes."

"And you waited until now to say it. Until it costs you nothing."

"It doesn't cost me nothing." Reyes heard her own voice go flat, which was what happened when it wanted to do something else. "It costs me the only version of myself I've managed to live with. I've carried that hearing for twenty-two years, Montrose. I have re-run it in my head more times than I've run any drill. Every year I tell myself I'd say yes this time. Every year I know I'm lying." She turned to face her fully. "So understand what I'm offering, because it isn't an apology. An apology asks you to make it smaller. This isn't that. I did it. I'd probably do it again, and that is the ugliest sentence I own, and I'm giving it to you because you're the only person alive with the right to hear it."

The harbor lapped at the hull. Forward, Ovid had stopped reading; even the cat had gone quiet, though the cat's attention was mostly on a gull.

"Why?" Tamsin asked at last. Not angry. Worse — genuinely asking, the way she'd once asked Reyes why the tide tables mattered, hungry for the machinery underneath. "Why tell me now?"

"Because you finished it." Reyes looked north again, toward the inquiry, toward the minister's office, toward thirty-one years arriving at their destination. "Your mother couldn't. Voss couldn't. I made sure of both, in my ways. And then you — with a rotten boat and a runaway and a librarian and an engineer with a grudge — you did the thing that three governments and my entire fleet couldn't stop. So the debt's called in. That's all. The account's settled, and I go north tomorrow and say whatever they'll let me say, and some of it will be true, and I expect it will be enough to end me." She shrugged, a small, dry motion. "There are worse fates than ending. I've read about most of them."

* * *

Tamsin walked her to the gangway. At the top of it, Reyes paused, one hand on the rail, and allowed herself one last look at the deck of the Kestrel's Wake — the ugly crane, the coiled lines, the galley smoke, the whole improbable arrangement of people who should never have worked together and somehow had.

"They renamed her yet?" she asked.

"Not yet. We're arguing about it. Brannoc wants something with guns in the name. Sefa wants something with a bird. Ovid keeps suggesting titles from books nobody's read."

"Let the girl have the bird." Reyes stepped down onto the first plate. "Boats named after birds come home. Boats named after weapons get remembered, which is different and worse." She hesitated, then added, gruffly, because it was the last chance she'd ever have to say it in an official capacity or otherwise: "Your mother dove the dark under the plates because she couldn't help it. You dive it because you choose to. Of the two, hers was braver. Yours is better. Don't lose the difference."

Then she was down the ladder, into the shore boat, and the Kestrel's Wake fell away behind her with its terrible tea and its listening teenager and its orange cat, and Reyes sat in the stern with her bad knee aching and her breast pocket holding a telegram, and watched Meridian Station rise out of the haze ahead — white buildings, flag at half-mast for some admiral or other, and at the quay, unmistakable even at this distance, a staff car with the minister's pennant, waiting.

The driver saw her boat and started the engine.

"Well," Reyes said to the empty water, and almost smiled. "Early, aren't we."

The car door opened before the shore boat touched the pilings, and a young aide descended with a folder under his arm and an expression of practiced regret, and behind him, at the top of the quay steps, stood a figure Reyes had hoped — with the modest, realistic hopes of a career cynic — not to see for another week: the Minister himself, in civilian gray, watching her come ashore like a man watching weather he'd ordered and no longer controlled.

And beside him, half in shadow under the customs-house awning, someone else. Small. Hooded against the wind. A face Reyes had last seen across a truce table, and before that in a portside crowd, and before that — she realized, with a slow drop in her stomach — in a photograph dated twenty-two years ago, filed under the name *Montrose*, in a folder marked *crew manifest, vessel unknown*.

The aide cleared his throat. "Captain Reyes. The Minister would like a word before your transport sails."

Reyes looked at the hooded figure, and the hooded figure looked back, and did not blink.

"Of course he would," she said.
