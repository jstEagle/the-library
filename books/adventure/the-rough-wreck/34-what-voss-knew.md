## 34. What Voss Knew

The chest came up from the Kestrel's Wake's forward hold on the third day after Meridian, when the political fireworks ashore had faded to a dull glow and the harbor had stopped ringing with news criers shouting "the minister has fallen" at anyone who'd stand still long enough to listen.

It was a small chest, salt-stained, its brass corners gone green as old bottle glass. Ede Voss had kept it aboard our boat for six weeks without any of us knowing it was there — wedged behind the freshwater tank where Brannoc swore he'd looked twice during the refit and found nothing but rust and a dead cockroach he'd named Admiral.

"He was dying," Sefa said, turning the chest over in her hands like it might bite. "Dying men don't hide things well."

"Dying men hide things best of all," Ovid said. He had his spectacles down his nose, which meant he was about to be insufferable in an academic way rather than a personal one, and I'd learned to tell the difference. "A man with years left hoards. A man with days arranges. Everything he did in that last week — the bar, the chart, the promise — it was arrangement. This is part of the arrangement too. We just weren't meant to open it until he was good and dead."

"He's been good and dead for two months," Brannoc said.

"Yes," Ovid said, "and we've been busy being alive. There's a difference in urgency."

We opened it on deck, because it seemed wrong to open anything of Voss's below decks, in the dark, where secrets liked to live. The morning was clean and gold, gulls arguing over something in the fish market refuse, and the harbor of Port Meridian lay around us full of boats that had no idea what we'd done or what we'd carried out of the sea to do it.

Inside: a diver's slate worn smooth at the edges. A coil of good line gone stiff with salt. A tin of tobacco so old it had turned to something like peat. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with a knot I recognized — my mother's knot, the one she used for anything she wanted to survive weather — a logbook.

I knew before I touched it. That's the thing nobody tells you about grief. It doesn't ambush you. It waits, patient as tide, and then it arrives exactly on schedule, and you stand there in the sunshine thinking: *of course. Of course it's this.*

The cover was stamped with the name of a ship I hadn't heard spoken aloud in twenty years. *The Lark's Mercy.* My mother's boat. My mother's crew. My mother's handwriting on the first three pages of entries, brisk and slantwise, complaining about the price of diesel in a port whose name I could still smell if I let myself.

And then, partway through, another hand took over. Tighter. Careful. A hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next page.

Ede Voss's hand.

* * *

I read the first of his entries standing up. By the fourth I was sitting on the hatch coaming without noticing I'd sat. By the tenth, Sefa had quietly put a mug of tea in my hands, and Brannoc had quietly stopped hammering whatever he was hammering, and Ovid had quietly positioned himself where he could read over my shoulder, which should have annoyed me and instead felt like being held up by the elbows.

The entries began ordinary enough. Soundings. Weather. A crew list that included my mother's name — Mara Montrose, written by Voss with a care that made the M's look almost reverent — and a note about a broken bilge pump and a card game in which someone named Pell had cheated and been forgiven, mostly.

Then the Corvane entered the record, and the handwriting changed.

*Found the outer hull today. She's bigger than the manifest says. Mara cried in the water. I have never seen her cry. She says we are going to be rich and also that we are going to be right, and I didn't understand what she meant by the second part until tonight.*

I turned pages. The dates crawled forward. The diving got deeper, the notes got shorter, and somewhere in the middle of the book, between an entry about a snapped lift bag and an entry about a storm holding off longer than it should, Voss wrote a single line that I read four times before it would go into me:

*Navy cutter offshore. Mara says we dive anyway. I say nothing.*

"She knew," Ovid said softly. "She knew they were watching."

"Keep reading," I said. My voice came out flat as a becalmed sea.

The next entry was dated three days later, and it was the shortest in the book, and it was the reason my hands were shaking by the time I finished it:

*I told them where the strongroom was. For a share. For my life, they said, though they never said what they'd do if I refused, which is how you know what they'd do. Mara went down that night anyway. I watched her light go into the dark and I told myself she'd come back up. Everyone comes back up. That is the lie I will die inside of.*

*They paid me. I took it. I am writing this so that someday, if I ever find the courage the sea gave her and never gave me, someone will know what I did, and know that I knew it, every day after, for every day there were.*

There were more entries after that. Years of them. Scattered, drunken, sobering, drunk again. Voss chasing other wrecks, other fortunes, always finding them and always leaving them, because — and here his handwriting deteriorated into something barely legible — *nothing down there is worth having anymore.* And then, near the end, the entries shortened and steadied, and became the ones I knew already: the search for the Corvane's true position. The chart drawn and redrawn. The decision, made alone in some rented room with his lungs filling slowly with the sea he'd helped drown her in, to find Mara Montrose's daughter and give her the one thing he had left.

Not the treasure. The errand.

I closed the book.

Nobody said anything. The gulls went on arguing. Somewhere across the harbor a church bell rang the hour with the cheerful indifference of institutions.

"So," Brannoc said finally, into the silence, with the delicacy of a man defusing ordnance, "he was the one."

"He was the one," I said.

"The one who—"

"Yes, Brannoc."

"Right." He picked up his hammer, considered it, and set it back down. "Right."

Sefa was looking at me the way she looked at water she didn't trust — sideways, reading the current under the surface. "You knew," she said. "Before you opened it. You knew what it was going to say."

I thought about lying to her and found I didn't have the energy, or the desire, or really the practice anymore. Six months aboard this boat had sanded most of my habits down to the wood. "I suspected. When he grabbed my wrist in that bar and said my mother's name, he flinched. People flinch when they say the names of people they've wronged. I told myself it was just grief." I turned the logbook over in my hands. "I've gotten very good at telling myself things."

Ovid lowered himself onto the coaming beside me, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "May I?"

"It's your history too," I said. "More than mine, maybe. You catalogued the treasury. He sold the strongroom. Between the two of you—" I stopped, because that wasn't fair, and fairness had become something I cared about lately, God help me. "Between the two of you, the truth survived. That counts. I'm deciding it counts."

"That's generous," Ovid said.

"I'm told it's a phase."

* * *

I took the logbook below that night, to the little cabin that had been Voss's during the passage and was now, unofficially, the place where we kept the things that didn't fit anywhere else — Ovid's duplicate charts, Sefa's growing shelf of borrowed books, the brass tube with the documents, and my mother's knife, which I still hadn't decided what to do with.

I read the rest of the log by lamplight. All of it. Every shameful, halting, human word.

Here is what I found in those later pages, the ones I hadn't reached on deck: Voss had tried to undo it. Not dramatically — there was no midnight confrontation with Reyes, no heroic confession, none of the theater that Ovid's histories would have given him. He had simply spent thirty years pulling threads. Quiet letters to archivists. Small payments to keep certain records from being "lost." A decade of saving money he couldn't spend, earmarked for a salvage run he was too sick and too frightened to make himself. The chart in my possession wasn't a gift. It was a debt instrument. He'd drawn it hoping someone with better lungs and less guilt would collect.

And there was one more thing. A loose page, folded into the back cover, undated, in his tight embossing hand:

*If she reads this — the daughter, I mean — tell her the promise was never for me. Tell her the promise was for Mara, who asked me once, laughing, at anchor, drinking rum out of the bottle because the cups were all broken: if anything happens to me, finish it. Finish it right. I said yes. I said yes and then I did the opposite, and I have been saying yes ever since, to everyone, about everything, because it is the only sentence I have left of hers.*

I sat with that for a long time.

Because I remembered that night. Not clearly — I'd been small, and it lived in me now more as warmth than as image — but I remembered my mother coming home from a season on the Lark's Mercy with salt in her hair and a story she told at the kitchen table while I was supposed to be asleep: about a crew member who'd promised her something foolish and important, and how she'd laughed and said *that's the trouble with promises, Ede, they're heavier than gold and worth less at market,* and how he'd laughed too, and how she'd said, quieter, *no, I mean it. If it ever matters — finish it right.*

He'd carried that sentence for thirty years like a stone in his boot. And when he was dying, he'd handed it to me, along with the map, and I'd taken it the way you take a package at the door — politely, without weighing it.

Except I hadn't dropped it. That was the thing. I'd carried it through six tides and a monsoon and a fleet and a collapsing wreck, and I'd finished it right, or as right as anything gets done in this world, and somewhere in the doing, the promise had stopped being his penance and become ours — mine and Brannoc's and Sefa's and Ovid's, a thing passed hand to hand like a line in a heavy sea, each of us holding on so the others wouldn't have to hold alone.

Voss had betrayed my mother. That was true, and no amount of warm feeling would make it untrue, and I wasn't going to insult either of them by pretending otherwise. But he had also, in the end, in the only currency he had left, paid everything he owed — not to the navy, not to the families, but to a dead woman's laughing voice at anchor, asking him to finish it right.

And I owed him. That was the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the chest came up on deck. Without his treachery, my mother might have lived — and without his penance, the Corvane would have stayed buried, the documents rotted, the Meridian families forgotten, Reyes's fleet still dredging truth out of the sea and calling it salvage. Both things were true. The sea is big enough to hold both. I was learning, slowly, painfully, to be big enough too.

I didn't forgive him the way they do in stories, all at once, with music. I forgave him the way you forgive the weather — acknowledging that it happened, that it shaped the coastline of everything after, and that hating it forever would only keep me standing in the rain.

I said it out loud anyway, to the empty cabin, because some things need air to be real.

"You're forgiven, you old coward," I told Ede Voss. "Now stop making it complicated."

The lamp flickered, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

In the morning I brought the logbook up on deck and read the final entry aloud to the crew, because it belonged to them now as much as to me. They listened the way sailors listen — still, eyes on the horizon, letting the words come in on their own schedule.

When I finished, Sefa said, "So the whole thing — the bar, the chart, the promise — it was him trying to give back what he took."

"And failing," Brannoc said, "because you can't give back a person."

"And succeeding," Ovid said, "in the only way left. He couldn't return her. He returned her purpose." He tapped the logbook's cover. "That's why the letter was in the strongroom, Tamsin. Your mother reached it first. She chose the documents over the gold, same as you did. Voss must have known — or hoped — that if anyone ever opened that room, they'd find her choice waiting, and understand it. He built his penance on top of her foundation. It's terrible architecture and it held."

Brannoc snorted. "Worst-built thing I've ever heard of that actually worked."

"Like this boat," Sefa said.

"Hey."

"Affectionately," she added, patting the rail, and the rail, being Brannoc's refit work, did not fall off, which we all agreed counted as a triumph.

I stood at the wheel with the logbook under my arm and looked out at the harbor — at the fishing boats going out fat and easy, at the customs launch puttering past with a new flag flying since the minister fell, at the shore where, somewhere, the Meridian families were learning to live inside a history that finally had them in it.

Two months ago I'd been a woman who never stayed anywhere longer than a contract. Now I owned a debt to a dead man, a knife I hadn't buried yet, a boat full of strays who argued about dinner, and a story that half the coast wanted to buy and the other half wanted to burn.

Which was when Ovid cleared his throat in the specific register he reserved for bad news delivered gently.

"There is," he said, "a small complication. A syndicate representative arrived on the morning packet. He's been asking at every berth on the waterfront for the crew of the Kestrel's Wake." He produced a calling card from his coat with the weary air of a man who had hoped never to need his coat pockets again. "He wishes to discuss, quote, 'the exclusive narrative rights to the Corvane affair,' unquote. His offer, I'm told, is substantial."

"How substantial?" Brannoc asked, in the tone of a man who intended to be outraged by the number and needed the number first.

"Substantial enough," Ovid said, "to refit this boat twice over and retire us all."

The deck went quiet. Four people, one story, and a stranger ashore with a checkbook and patience.

I looked at the logbook in my hands — Voss's shame, my mother's laughter, thirty years of a promise carried like a stone — and I felt the weight of the next choice settle onto my shoulders, familiar as a dive harness.

"Well," I said. "Let's go hear what our story costs."
