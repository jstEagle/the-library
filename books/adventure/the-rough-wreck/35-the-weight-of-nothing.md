## 35. The Weight of Nothing

Brannoc had rebuilt engines with fewer parts than the Kestrel's Wake now had scars.

He sat in the engine room with his left arm strapped to his chest — the doctor at Meridian had used the word *fracture* and Brannoc had used a word back that made Sefa cover her ears and Ovid pretend not to hear — and he listened to the boat breathe around him. That was the thing about a refit that never stopped: you learned her voice. The hull ticked as it cooled against the pilings. The bilge pump cleared its throat every forty seconds like an old man disagreeing with the weather. Somewhere above, Tamsin's boots crossed the deck in the particular rhythm she used when she was thinking about something she didn't want to say out loud.

His arm hurt. His pride hurt worse, if he was honest, which he tried to be only in the privacy of his own skull. He'd gone down into a collapsing wreck to jam demolition charges with a wrench and a prayer, and the wrench had done its job and the prayer hadn't, and now the navy's finest demolition work lay buried under forty fathoms of silt along with everything else, and Brannoc lay in a hammock he'd rigged between two bulkhead ribs because bunks were for people who planned ahead.

The knock came at the engine room hatch. Three raps, polite, spaced out. Ovid.

"Visitor," Ovid said, without opening it. "Wearing very good shoes."

"Bad sign," Brannoc said. "Good shoes on a pier mean money. Money on a pier means trouble."

"I said much the same. She's waiting in the wheelhouse. Tamsin's with her." A pause, and then Ovid added, in the tone of a man cataloguing a specimen: "She brought a folder. Leather. Brass corners. I've seen folders like that before. They usually contain either a pardon or a bill."

Brannoc hauled himself up with his good arm, which took longer than he liked and hurt more than he'd admit, and followed Ovid up through the boat. The Meridian harbor smelled of tar and frying fish and, this close to the governor's confession going off like a depth charge in the colonial papers, something almost like hope. Half the island had read Ovid's documents by now — the true manifest, the treasury looted from their own great-grandparents' families, the sealed confession naming names all the way up to a minister whose portrait still hung, rather awkwardly, in the customs house.

The woman in the wheelhouse wore grey silk and shoes that had never known a deck. She introduced herself as Vess Andrade, representing the Meridian Courier Syndicate, and she laid the leather folder on the chart table with the care of someone laying down a winning card.

"The exclusive account," she said. "Your story. All of it. The wreck, the fleet, Captain Reyes, the collapse, the rescue. First-person testimony from each member of the crew, verified by our own archivists, published across the colonies." She smiled. "We're prepared to offer forty thousand crowns. Split however you like. Plus residuals."

Nobody spoke. Forty thousand crowns was more money than Brannoc had earned in the last eleven years combined, and he'd once been paid handsomely to ruin a perfectly good ferry engine, so that was saying something.

"And the catch," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question. She was leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed, and Brannoc recognized the set of her shoulders: coiled, waiting for the trap to show its teeth.

"No catch," Andrade said pleasantly. "Only editorial control. We'd want the account to focus on the adventure. The treasure, the storm, the heroics. The syndicate feels the political material is — well, it's being handled elsewhere, isn't it? The inquiry, the minister's fall. The public has an appetite for shipwrecks and gold. Appetites for ledgers are harder to sustain." She tapped the folder. "Omit the manifest. Omit the confession. Omit the families. Keep the romance."

"There it is," Brannoc said.

Andrade turned to him with the patient smile of a woman who had bought many stubborn men. "You'd be rich men. Rich enough never to dive again, if you chose. Rich enough to fix that arm properly, engineer, instead of strapping it to your chest and calling it medicine."

"It *is* medicine," Brannoc said. "It's just also a splint."

Tamsin didn't touch the folder. "We'll talk it over."

"Of course." Andrade slid the folder to the middle of the table and left it there, gleaming, like bait that knew exactly what it was. "I'm at the Grand Meridian until the week's end. I do hope you'll be sensible. Sensibility pays so much better than principle."

Her heels went down the gangway like a metronome counting out the price of everything they'd done.

* * *

They didn't talk about it that night, which was itself a kind of talking about it. Tamsin cooked — which meant she stood over the galley stove burning rice while Sefa rescued it, again, with the resigned competence of a girl who had long since accepted that feeding the crew was her actual job regardless of what anyone's title said. Ovid read aloud from a battered novel he claimed was research into how ordinary people talked, though everyone suspected he simply liked reading aloud. Brannoc sat with his arm strapped up and let himself look around the table at these people who had started as strangers and a contract and had somehow become the only address he had.

Forty thousand crowns. He did the arithmetic the way he couldn't stop doing it, the way he'd been doing it since Andrade's heels faded: new engine block, proper winch, copper sheathing on the hull, a berth big enough that he'd never have to sleep in the engine room again unless he wanted to, which, admittedly, he sometimes did.

"You're doing sums," Sefa said.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're doing sums. You get a line right here—" she tapped the space between her own eyebrows— "when you're adding."

"Everyone gets a line when they're adding."

"Not like you. Yours goes sideways. Like the numbers are trying to escape."

Ovid lowered his novel. "Might I observe," he said, "that no one is eating, and that the silence has the specific texture of a decision being avoided?"

"Observation noted," Tamsin said. "Overruled."

"On what grounds?"

"On the grounds that I haven't decided yet."

But Brannoc watched her, and she wasn't looking at the folder, which still sat on the chart table where Andrade had left it. She was looking at the porthole, out toward the harbor, toward the customs house where the minister's portrait had come down that morning and been replaced — temporarily, the harbormaster promised — by a notice of inquiry. Toward the quarter where the old Meridian families lived, the ones whose names were in the manifest, some of whom had come down to the pier the day the Kestrel's Wake arrived and stood there holding papers three generations old, asking Ovid, in voices that shook, whether it was really true.

Tamsin was looking at them the way she looked at a current she intended to swim through anyway.

* * *

It was Brannoc who cracked first, which surprised no one, least of all Brannoc.

He went up to the wheelhouse alone the next morning, ostensibly to check the compass deviation, actually because he hadn't slept, and found Tamsin already there with two cups of coffee and the expression of a woman who had been awake longer.

"Say your piece," she said.

"That's my line. I'm the grumpy one."

"You've been demoted. I've been grumpier lately."

That was true and neither of them pretended otherwise. He took the coffee. For a while they just listened to the harbor wake up — gulls, fish carts, a bell buoy with a crack in it that rang flat and had rung flat for thirty years and would ring flat for thirty more, and there was something in that thought that steadied him.

"My whole life," Brannoc said, "every time I got a little money together, I spent it getting away from somewhere. New port, new contract, new engine to ruin. Money was just fuel for leaving." He turned the cup in his hand. "And I keep catching myself doing sums about what forty thousand buys, and every single thing on the list is a way of making this boat into the place I'd stop running from. Which means the money isn't offering me anything. It's offering me a faster version of what I've already got, minus the reason we've got it."

Tamsin said nothing, which from Tamsin was a full paragraph.

"And there's the other thing," he went on, because he was committed now. "My father worked the Meridian docks. Before the confiscations. My family lost the ferry license, the house, all of it, to the same ledger Ovid pulled out of that strongroom. I never knew why we were poor. Now I know it was policy." He laughed, short and without humor. "Andrade wants me to sell the explanation of my own life back to me, with the explanation cut out. Forty thousand crowns to help bury the truth a second time. I've helped bury things before. I didn't like the weight."

"So that's your answer."

"That's my answer. I'm telling you first because you're the captain and because if I told the others first, Sefa would make that face, and I can't argue with the face."

Tamsin almost smiled. "What face?"

"This one." He made it. It was apparently accurate, because she looked away quickly, out at the water, and her voice came back rougher than before.

"Voss traded me a map for a promise," she said. "Raise what's in the strongroom. Give the documents to the families. I thought the promise ended at the delivery. Papers handed over, debt paid, sail away." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "But it doesn't end, does it. The truth doesn't stay delivered. It has to be *kept*. Every time somebody offers money to fold it away, somebody has to refuse. Otherwise it was all just — salvage. Another cargo."

"Aye," Brannoc said. "And I've stripped enough wrecks to know the difference between raising something and keeping it."

They drank their coffee while the flat-ringing buoy counted the morning.

* * *

Sefa refused next, and she did it in front of everyone, which was very Sefa.

Andrade came back on the third day — "sensible people reconsider," she said, and Brannoc privately noted that sensible people also carried sidearms in some ports, but the woman was all silk and patience — and this time she made her pitch to the whole crew gathered in the wheelhouse, because she'd understood that the decision wasn't Tamsin's alone. Smart woman. Wrong assumption, but smart.

"Eight thousand crowns apiece," Andrade said. "Think of it as wages for the most remarkable voyage of your lives."

Sefa, seventeen years old and owner of one knife, one borrowed coat, and a seabag containing mostly other people's things she'd fixed, put her hand flat on the folder before anyone else could speak.

"Can I ask a question?" she said. "When you publish it — the version without the manifest — will it say what we found? In the strongroom?"

"The gold, yes. The discovery, the drama—"

"Because there wasn't any gold." Sefa's voice was steady, the way it went steady when she was reading water. "There were papers. Letters. One letter written by a dead woman to a daughter she never got to warn. That's what we nearly died for. That's what Brannoc nearly died *for*, his arm, the charges, all of it. If you print a story about treasure, you're printing a lie about the only true thing I've ever been part of." She pushed the folder back across the table. "My grandmother's name is in that manifest. Anaya Sefek. They wrote her family down as *assets recovered*. Eight thousand crowns doesn't un-write that. Nothing does. But the truth at least makes it *count*."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Andrade, to her credit, didn't smile this time. She looked at Sefa for a long moment the way a person looks at weather they'd underestimated.

"You're throwing away security," she said finally.

"We're keeping something better than security," Sefa said. "We're keeping each other's respect. Try selling *that* with residuals."

After Andrade left, Ovid said, mildly, "That last line needs work," and Sefa threw a rolled chart at his head, and the tension broke the way it always broke aboard the Kestrel's Wake: into laughter that hurt a little, because it was standing in for all the things none of them could say yet.

* * *

Ovid was the hard one.

Not because he wanted the money — Brannoc would have bet the boat he didn't, or rather, would have bet the parts of the boat he could bear to lose. It was because Ovid had spent thirty years being paid, in a sense, to keep quiet, and he knew the taste of that wage better than anyone alive, and part of him — the honest part, the part that had joined this mad crew to atone — kept turning the offer over like a stone looking for what lived under it.

He came down to the engine room that evening, which he never did, because the engine room was Brannoc's country and Ovid respected borders. He stood among the tools with his hands clasped behind his back like a man visiting a museum.

"I've drafted my refusal four times," Ovid said.

"Drafted it?"

"In my head. Refusal is a genre, Brannoc. It deserves craft." He picked up a spanner, examined it, put it down precisely where it had been. "The first draft was eloquent. The second was principled. The third was short. The fourth was just the word *no*, which I believe is the final form." He paused. "But here is what troubles me. Thirty years ago, a man offered me a pension to misfile a manifest, and I took it, and I told myself it was survival, and it was, and it was also cowardice, and those two things fit inside each other like a piston in a bore. And now a woman offers me a fortune to omit the same manifest, and I find the old machinery turning over. *Take it. You're owed. You're old. Let the young ones be pure.*"

"What stopped it?" Brannoc asked.

Ovid smiled, and it was the saddest and warmest thing Brannoc had seen him do. "A teenage girl quoted her grandmother's name at a syndicate agent and meant it more than anything I have ever meant in my life. I thought: I stayed silent for thirty years so that a child would have to do that alone someday. Never again. Not even profitably." He straightened. "So. No. Final form. Though I may polish the syntax later, purely for my own satisfaction."

"Send me the drafts sometime," Brannoc said. "I'll frame the fourth one over the workbench."

"The fourth one is one word long."

"Best things usually are."

* * *

Which left Tamsin, and Tamsin, being Tamsin, did it her own way: alone, at dawn, at the Grand Meridian's pier, with the folder under her arm.

Brannoc only heard about it afterward, from Sefa, who had watched from the rigging with the field glasses and narrated it that evening like a race commentary, complete with sound effects.

"She walks up," Sefa said, "all casual, like she's delivering a parcel. Hands Andrade the folder. Andrade opens it, all pleased with herself — and it's empty except for one page. Ovid's fourth draft. Just the word *no*. Signed by all four of us, because Tamsin made us sign it the night before, which, rude, by the way, deciding for us—"

"She didn't decide," Brannoc said. "She collected. There's a difference. Captains collect."

"—and then Andrade says something, and Tamsin says something back, and I couldn't lip-read it but I know that jaw-set, that's the jaw-set from the flagship, from Reyes's brandy, that's the *no further discussion* jaw. And then she turns around and walks back down the pier and doesn't look at the boat even once, which is how you know she wanted to."

Forty thousand crowns, declined. The syndicate would find another angle eventually — syndicates always did — but for now the story belonged to nobody, which meant it belonged to everybody, which meant Ovid could write it true.

* * *

They felt poor for about six days, and Brannoc would be lying if he said he didn't feel it. Poor was a familiar coat; he'd worn it so long it had shaped itself to his shoulders. There was a morning when the winch cable frayed and he stood looking at the price of replacement cable and felt the sideways line creep between his eyebrows, and Sefa appeared beside him and said, "Stop summing. We'll splice it," and they spliced it, badly, and it held, the way most things on the Kestrel's Wake held: through stubbornness and mutual disbelief.

But something else happened too, in the weeks after the folder went back empty. Word traveled, the way it does in harbors, faster than any syndicate could print it. The families came. Not all at once — first old Mistress Corvane-Hale, whose great-grandfather's signature was on the original theft order, arriving in a carriage too fine for the pier and leaving in it an hour later having cried on Ovid's shoulder, which Brannoc paid good money in imagination to have witnessed. Then dockworkers whose grandfathers had lost licenses. Then a schoolteacher with a class of children who wanted to see "the truth boat," and Sefa showing them the strongroom wheel they'd salvaged — the one piece of the wreck the sea had let them keep — and explaining currents to eight-year-olds with a patience Brannoc hadn't known she had.

Nobody paid them. Nobody gave them anything but thanks, and fish, occasionally, and once an entire crate of limes from a grocer whose family name was in the manifest, and Brannoc discovered that gratitude, unlike crowns, didn't run out when you spent it. It compounded.

One evening he sat on the foredeck with his arm out of the sling at last, flexing it, testing the range, watching the flat-ringing buoy mark the dark. Tamsin came and sat beside him with the logbook under her arm, as she did most evenings now, logging their position in a harbor they had no contract to be in.

"No departure filed," Brannoc observed.

"No."

"Contract's done. Promise is kept. By every law you've ever lived by, we should be gone by Thursday."

"Mm," Tamsin said.

"Sefa's been eyeing the maritime school brochure for a fortnight. Won't touch it. Says it's not her decision to make if the boat's leaving." He glanced sideways. "Ovid's filled two notebooks. Says a history wants a stable desk. Whatever that means."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"And you're going to log our position tomorrow morning, and the morning after, and one day you're going to write something in that book that makes it official, and I'd like to be present when you do, because I intend to complain about it extensively."

Tamsin looked out at the water for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was quiet and completely certain, the voice she used at the bottom of the sea.

"Thursday," she said, "we're taking a legitimate salvage contract. Little coaster aground on the outer shoal. Honest work, fair pay, home by supper." She closed the logbook. "And after that, Brannoc — I don't know. I honestly don't know. And for the first time in my life, that doesn't scare me."

Brannoc flexed his healing hand and found, to his surprise, that the weight of nothing — no fortune, no cargo, no next horizon demanded of them — sat lighter on the deck than any treasure ever had.

Above them, the cracked buoy rang flat into the dark, faithful as ever, going nowhere.
