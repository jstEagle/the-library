## 36. Anchorage

The first thing Sefa noticed about staying put was how loud it was.

Not the harbor itself — Meridian's anchorage was no louder than any other, all halyards and gulls and the endless small argument between water and pilings. It was the *noticing* that was loud. When you were always leaving, you never heard a place settle in around you. You caught the first hour of somewhere and then the sea took it back. But they'd been tied up at the Meridian public quay for eleven days now, and Sefa had started to know the sounds the way you knew a person's breathing in the dark: the fish-market bell at dawn, two notes, always flat; the customs skiff that puttered past at half-past seven with an engine knock like a polite cough; old Ferro on the neighboring ketch, who sang to his anchor chain every evening for reasons he had never explained and nobody had ever been brave enough to request.

Eleven days. She kept counting them the way you'd count money you didn't trust.

"You're doing it again," Brannoc said, without looking up from the winch he was rebuilding on the quay stones. He'd spread its guts across a canvas tarp like a surgeon proud of his patient. "Counting."

"Counting what?"

"Days." He tightened something that clacked. "You get a look on your face. Like the numbers might bite."

Sefa sat down on the bollard beside him and let her legs dangle over the black water. "Don't you?"

Brannoc considered this with the seriousness he gave everything mechanical. "I count bolts," he said finally. "Days are less reliable."

That was Brannoc for you. He'd been out of the hospital three weeks and still moved like a man negotiating with his own ribs, but he'd claimed a corner of the quay as his workshop within two days of arriving, and the harbormaster — a round, suspicious woman named Pell who had initially tried to charge them double for mooring on the grounds that their boat looked like a salvage claim — had already forgiven him three code violations because he'd fixed her pump and her radio antenna and, once, her office door, which had been sticking since before Sefa was born.

Below them, the Kestrel's Wake rode at her lines looking almost respectable. Almost. Brannoc had replaced the worst of the planking, and Tamsin had spent a whole week on the hull with scraper and pitch until she came up smelling of tar and looking vaguely smug about it, but there was only so much respectability a boat could absorb before it started to feel like a costume. Sefa liked her better this way anyway — patched, honest, a little lopsided. A boat that had been through things.

A boat that had stayed.

"School starts Monday," she said, because she'd been circling it for two days and Brannoc was the safest place to land.

He looked up. His eyebrows did the thing they did — one up, one level, a face built entirely of moving parts. "You've decided, then."

"I filled out the forms." She patted her jacket pocket, where the folded papers lived. She'd carried them around for four days like contraband. "There were a lot of forms. Ovid helped. He said the entrance essay section was 'charmingly brief,' which I think means I wrote too little, but he made me fix it."

"What'd you write?"

Sefa shrugged, embarrassed. "About currents. About reading water. About — " She stopped, tried again. "About how my grandmother taught me that the sea doesn't lie, people do, and if you learn to read the water honest, you'll always know which way is true. Something like that. Ovid cried a little. He says it was the wind."

"The wind," Brannoc agreed solemnly. "Very windy in that kitchen."

"It was indoors."

"Extremely windy."

She laughed, and the laugh felt strange in her chest, unpracticed, like a muscle she'd forgotten she owned. That was another thing about staying: she laughed more. She'd noticed it the way you notice a new callus forming. Back when she'd been running — port to port, berth to berth, always one step ahead of the scout ship with the grey hull — laughing had been a luxury, something you did quickly and quietly and never where anyone could mark your face. Now she laughed on the quay in full daylight and nothing happened. Nobody came. The world, it turned out, had other business.

Mostly.

* * *

Ovid had taken over the aft cabin as a study, which meant the Kestrel's Wake now smelled of ink and lamp oil instead of mildew and despair, an improvement everyone appreciated. He wrote in the mornings, when the light came through the stern windows, and in the afternoons he walked to the archive and came back looking like a man who had won an argument nobody else had witnessed.

It was on one of those afternoons that Sefa found him on deck, papers weighted down with a spanner and a teacup, reading aloud to himself in the voice he probably thought was silent.

"'The Corvane sailed from Meridian on the fourteenth of Stormmonth,'" he intoned, "'carrying, by the official manifest, ninety-one crates of machine parts and dyewood.'" He paused, quill hovering. "Machine parts. Ninety-one crates. Of *machine parts*. The gall of it. The sheer administrative gall."

"You could put that in the book," Sefa said, dropping into the cockpit across from him. "'And the gall was sheer.'"

"I intend to. Chapter nine is going to be extremely rude about manifests." He peered at her over his spectacles, which he'd begun wearing indoors and out regardless of need, in the manner of a man who had decided spectacles were part of his authorial identity. "How is the great scholar of Meridian Maritime? Have they expelled you yet?"

"Not yet. Instructor Halloway says I have 'unorthodox chart instincts.' I think that's a compliment. It might be a warning."

"With Halloway those are the same thing. He told me my dissertation was 'unorthodox' and then stole half of it for his own monograph." Ovid dipped his quill. "Go on, then. Tell me about your day. I'm collecting ordinary days. For research."

Sefa rolled her eyes, but she told him anyway, because that was the other thing about Ovid — he asked questions like the answers mattered, and after a lifetime of keeping her mouth shut around strangers, being asked and answering had turned out to be a pleasure she hadn't known she was starving for.

So she told him about the navigation exam, and how she'd finished early and then sat there panicking that she'd finished early, and re-checked everything twice and gotten the same answer both times, which either meant she was right or consistently wrong in a way that would be almost impressive. She told him about the other students, most of them younger than her by a year or two, sons and daughters of Meridian merchants and fishermen, who had been baffled by her at first — the girl off the salvage boat with salt-cured hands and a way of looking at tide tables like they were gossip — and who had, sometime in the third week, simply absorbed her, the way a crew absorbs a new hand, without ceremony. There was a girl named Pemmy who wanted to be a lighthouse keeper and talked about it the way other people talked about love affairs. There was a boy who cheated at cards so badly it was practically a confession.

"And how does it feel," Ovid asked, not looking up, in the deceptively casual voice he used when a question actually mattered, "being a student? Being someone with a desk?"

Sefa thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and because she'd been turning the question over herself for weeks.

"Weird," she said. "Good-weird. Mostly. Sometimes I sit in class and I think — somebody knows where I am. Every day. At this time. And for years that would have been the worst thing I could imagine, being findable. And now it just feels like..." She hunted for it. "Like being on a heading. You know? Like somebody drew the line and I'm on it."

Ovid's quill had gone still. "Your grandmother would have liked that sentence."

"My grandmother would have said I was getting soft."

"Both things can be true." He smiled, and went back to chapter nine, and the gall of manifests.

* * *

Tamsin took the news about the school quietly, which from Tamsin was a kind of ceremony.

She was coiling line on the foredeck when Sefa told her, and she finished the coil first — she always finished the task first; it was how she bought time to feel things — and then she stood there holding the flemished rope like it was a small animal she'd rescued.

"Meridian Maritime," she said. "That's a real school. They have exams. And buildings."

"And a bell. The bell rings a lot. You get used to it."

"Huh." Tamsin set the coil down. Sefa braced for something — a joke, maybe, or worse, sincerity at full volume — but what Tamsin said was: "You'll want your own oilskins. School ones. Not the ones with the burn hole."

"That's — yes. That's the plan."

"There's a chandlery on Cordwainer Street. Tell them Montrose sent you. They'll pretend that means something and give you a discount." And then, gruffly, before Sefa could respond: "Good. It's good. You should — it's good."

Which, translated from Tamsin, was roughly a parade.

The truth was, Sefa had watched Tamsin change too, these eleven days, the way you watch a season turn without being able to name the exact morning it happened. Tamsin still rose before dawn — some things were load-bearing and would never move — but she rose and then she *stayed*, drank tea on deck, watched the fishing fleet go out, and didn't check the horizon like it owed her money. She'd taken a contract, one contract, a straightforward wreck survey for the harbormaster, six days' work, and when it was done she'd come home — home, the word sat differently now — and hadn't started looking for the next one. Sefa had seen her at the quay table one evening, chart open, pencil in hand, and braced for the old restlessness, the itch that always ended with them casting off. Instead Tamsin had been drawing the reef line around Meridian harbor. Not for a job. Just because it was there, and she was here, and for once those facts belonged to each other.

"She's happy," Sefa said to Brannoc one night, watching Tamsin argue cheerfully with Ferro about anchor chain songs. "Actually happy. It's terrifying."

"Why terrifying?"

"Because I keep waiting for it to end. That's the trick of it, isn't it? You grow up running, and even when you stop, part of you keeps a bag packed in your head." She pulled her knees up on the hatch cover. "I keep thinking the school will fall through, or the navy will come back, or Reyes will — "

"Reyes is in Meridian City answering to an inquiry board," Brannoc said. "The navy's busy eating its own tail. And the school won't fall through, because you're the best thing to happen to their navigation program since the compass." He said it plainly, without ceremony, the way he said true things about engines. "You're allowed to unpack, Sefa. All the way. Not just the top layer."

She didn't answer. Out on the water, Ferro had reached the part of the song where he addressed the chain directly, by name, and Tamsin was losing a battle against laughter, and the harbor bell rang its two flat notes for no reason Sefa could determine except that bells don't need reasons when they live somewhere.

Unpack all the way. She went below that night and took her seabag off the shelf where it had lived, packed and ready, since the day they'd anchored — the bag she'd told herself was just habit — and emptied it into the little locker by her bunk. Line, flares, the emergency cash sewn into the lining, the forged papers under a false name she'd kept for three years and never needed again. She kept the photograph of her grandmother. Everything else went into drawers.

It took ten minutes. It cost her more than ten minutes' worth of something, and gained her back more than that. The arithmetic, for once, came out right.

* * *

On Sunday, Ovid read them the first chapter.

He'd cleared the aft cabin and set out chairs that didn't match, and Tamsin brought the good tea, and Brannoc arrived smelling of grease and sat down with the expression of a man attending a funeral for a machine he respected. Sefa took the seat by the window because it was hers now; she'd discovered she had a seat that was hers, plural times, and it still surprised her every time like a gift that kept re-arriving.

Ovid stood, cleared his throat, lost his nerve, sat down, stood again, and finally began.

"'This is a history of the ship Corvane, and of the fortune she carried, and of the thirty years during which everyone who might have told you about them chose not to,'" he read. "'I was one of the people who chose not to. This book is my signature on a confession I should have signed long ago.'"

He read for twenty minutes. It wasn't gentle — Ovid's prose had teeth, and he aimed them at governors and ministers and, most of all, at his own younger self — but underneath the teeth was something careful and fierce, the voice of a man setting down every fact in its proper place because facts, properly placed, were the only monument the drowned ever got.

When he finished, nobody spoke for a moment. Then Brannoc said, "You spelled my name wrong."

"I did not spell your name wrong, I stylized it—"

"You spelled it B-r-a-n-n-o-k. There's a C on the end. There has always been a C on the end."

"There has absolutely not—"

"There's a C. Ask anyone."

"Ask *anyone*," Ovid muttered, already writing a note. "I'm consulting primary sources on your name now. This is what my life has become."

Tamsin was smiling into her tea, and Sefa caught her eye across the mismatched chairs, and something passed between them that didn't need words — the same current Sefa had learned to read in water, the one that runs steady beneath weather, the one that tells you which way is true.

* * *

Monday morning, Sefa put on her new oilskins — Cordwainer Street, ten percent off, "Montrose discount," the shopkeeper had said with a wink that suggested Tamsin's reputation here was complicated and favorable — and walked to the maritime school with her forms and her satchel and her heart going like a drum in a parade.

At the gate she stopped. Just for a second. Old habit, checking sightlines, checking exits, checking the street behind her.

The street behind her held a fruit cart, two arguing fishermen, and a cat with strong opinions. No grey hulls. No scouts. Just Meridian, opening up its ordinary morning, and beyond the rooftops the harbor, and beyond the harbor the Kestrel's Wake riding at her lines, staying.

Sefa turned and walked through the gate.

* * *

They were all at the quay that evening — Brannoc with his winch, Ovid with his chapters, Tamsin with tea and the chart she pretended she wasn't drawing on for pleasure — when the launch came up the fairway.

It was navy. Clean-lined, white ensign, the particular polished silence of official business, and it came straight toward their berth without hesitation, the way boats do when they already know exactly where you are.

Sefa felt it before she understood it: the old cold sliding up her spine, the bag repacking itself in her head, drawer by drawer. Across the cockpit she saw Tamsin set down her cup with exaggerated care, the way she did when her hands wanted to do something else.

The launch bumped their fenders. A lieutenant stepped ashore, immaculate, carrying a sealed folio, and looked at the four of them — the engineer, the archivist, the girl in school oilskins, the captain who had beaten the fleet — with the expression of a man delivering a message he'd been told was good news.

"Captain Montrose?" he said. "I have correspondence from the Admiralty. Regarding the Corvane."

Nobody moved. Somewhere across the anchorage, Ferro began, tenderly, to sing to his chain.

Tamsin rose, unhurried, and took the folio. "Well," she said, to no one in particular, to all of them. "Let's see what they think we've forgotten."
