## 37. The Navy's Silence

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which Tamsin would later decide was the navy's first mistake. Tuesdays were for engine maintenance, and Brannoc was elbow-deep in the Kestrel's Wake's port injector when the launch puttered into the harbor flying the white-and-gold pennant of the Meridian Station. He looked up from his work with grease to his elbows and said, "If that's another survey team wanting to measure our keel, I'm going to throw a wrench at it."

"It's not a survey team," Sefa said from the rail, where she'd been sitting cross-legged since dawn watching the harbor the way she watched everything — like she was reading a book only she could see. "It's one man. One very nervous man."

"One man can still carry a wrench-sized problem," Brannoc muttered.

Ovid appeared from below decks with an ink stain on his thumb and three pages of manuscript clutched against his chest, as though he could sense — the way he always seemed to sense anything involving paper and officialdom — that history was about to be made or unmade at their dockside. "A navy courier," he said. "Here. At *our* dock. In daylight." He adjusted his spectacles. "Either they've come to arrest us, or they've come to apologize. I find both equally unlikely."

"They've come to buy something," Tamsin said.

She felt it before the envelope was even handed over — that particular weight in her chest she'd learned to recognize over thirty years of diving, the pressure change that meant something below was not what it appeared to be on the surface. The man who stepped off the launch was a lieutenant commander with a pressed uniform and a folder under his arm and the expression of someone who had rehearsed a speech in front of a mirror and hated every rehearsal of it.

"Captain Montrose," he said. "Lieutenant Commander Pell. I'm authorized to extend certain... considerations."

"Considerations," Tamsin repeated.

"Considerations," Pell agreed, and held out the envelope.

She didn't take it right away. She looked past him at the launch, at the two sailors who stayed seated in it and didn't meet anyone's eyes, at the harbor beyond where fishing boats rocked on a gentle swell and children were racing paper boats down the drainage channel and the world was going about its ordinary business as if the navy hadn't spent a year trying to pretend the Corvane had never existed.

Then she took the envelope.

Inside was a single sheet of heavy cream paper, the kind of paper that cost more per page than the Kestrel's Wake had cost them all together, even refitted. The letterhead bore the seal of the Admiralty itself. Tamsin read it twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misread it.

*In consideration of services rendered to the Crown during the recent monsoon season, and in recognition of the sensitive nature of certain materials recovered from the wreck designated CV-114:*

*First — full salvage rights to the Corvane site, including all recoverable bullion, assessed at current market value.*

*Second — a full pardon, retroactive, covering all matters pertaining to the vessel Kestrel's Wake and its crew, including but not limited to questions of registry, licensing, and prior conduct.*

*Third — a commission. Captain Montrose is offered a warrant as Salvage Master, First Class, with station at Meridian Harbor, salary and pension attached.*

*In exchange: the historical account currently in preparation by Mr. Ovid Marsh, provisionally titled* A True and Full Account of the Loss of the Corvane, *shall be withdrawn from publication in perpetuity. All notes, drafts, manifests, and documentary evidence shall be surrendered to the Admiralty archive, where they will be preserved with appropriate discretion.*

*The Admiralty acknowledges that this offer may be declined. It further acknowledges that declining carries no penalty whatsoever.*

Tamsin read the last line three times. *Declining carries no penalty whatsoever.* It was the politest threat she had ever read. It was written in the same voice as the letter Voss had left her, almost — that careful, formal language that meant the person writing it knew exactly what they were doing and wanted you to know they knew.

"Well?" Brannoc said. "What is it? Are we rich? Tell me we're rich. I have a list."

"We're being bought," Tamsin said.

She read it aloud instead, because it seemed wrong to keep it, whatever it was. When she got to the part about the commission — *Salvage Master, First Class* — she heard Sefa make a small sound, half laugh, half something else. When she got to the part about Ovid's history, she heard Ovid go very quiet, which for Ovid was louder than shouting.

"The bullion assessment alone," Brannoc said faintly, when she finished. "Even a fraction of what went down with her — Tamsin, that's — we could refit three boats. We could build a *yard*. I could finally replace the number-two injector with one that doesn't sound like a dying walrus."

"You have a list," Sefa said.

"I have a *list*, Sefa."

"And a pardon," Ovid said slowly. "Retroactive. Which would mean—" He stopped. He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which he did when he was thinking hard or frightened, and Tamsin had known him long enough now that she couldn't always tell which. "Which would mean my name comes off the colonial register. Thirty years of being a footnote in other people's files, erased. Legally, formally, *erased*. I could walk into the archive again. I could sit in the reading room. I could be a citizen."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"And all it costs," Ovid said, "is the truth."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Down the dock, the paper boats reached the harbor mouth and scattered. Pell stood waiting with the patience of a man being paid by the hour, and Tamsin noticed he wasn't looking at any of them directly — he was looking at the boat, at the patched hull, at the rust stains and the new planking and the winch Brannoc had built from salvage that was frankly better than anything the navy yard produced. He was looking at what they'd made out of nothing, and Tamsin understood suddenly that he hadn't come to threaten them. He'd come because somewhere above him, someone had done the arithmetic and decided that the cheapest way to bury the Corvane was to pay the people who'd raised it.

That was how power worked, she thought. Not the charges, not the dredges, not Reyes closing in through building seas. That had been force, and force was honest — you could see it coming and you could run from it. This was quieter. This was a cream-colored envelope on a Tuesday morning, offering you everything you'd ever wanted, itemized, with the price written small at the bottom.

"Give us until tomorrow," Tamsin said.

Pell inclined his head. "Of course. There's no urgency." And then, because he was either more honest than his superiors or simply bad at his job, he added: "There is some urgency. The inquiry report goes to print in six weeks. After that, the offer expires." He hesitated. "For what it's worth, Captain — and I know it isn't much — I read the manifest. The real one. Before they sealed it. My grandfather was a Meridian man." He looked at the boat again, at all of them, and Tamsin saw that he was younger than his uniform. "I'm sorry. That's not in my orders either."

He went back down the dock to his launch. Nobody threw a wrench at him, though Brannoc visibly considered it.

* * *

They didn't talk about it through the afternoon, which was its own kind of conversation. Brannoc disappeared into the engine room and attacked the number-two injector with a violence that suggested he was working through feelings. Sefa sat on the rail and watched the water. Ovid shut himself in the cabin with his manuscript, and through the porthole Tamsin could see him laying out his pages — months of work, the true manifest copied in his careful archivist's hand, the governor's confession transcribed word for word, the testimony of the families, the whole drowned history of the Corvane spread out on his bunk like a patient he was trying to keep alive.

Tamsin walked the beach.

It was a habit she'd developed without noticing, these last months in Meridian Harbor — walking the tideline at dusk while the light went copper and the fishing fleet came home. She'd spent her whole life measuring harbors by how quickly she could leave them, and this one had quietly become the first place she'd ever stayed, and she still didn't entirely trust it, and she still walked the beach every evening like a woman checking that a door was really locked.

Voss's effects were still in her sea chest. She'd gone through them a dozen times since the islands — the rotted logbook, the hand-drawn chart soft as cloth from handling, the little brass compass with the cracked face. And the letter. Her mother's letter, addressed to her in handwriting she'd have known anywhere, found in the strongroom beside the documents her mother had died protecting. Tamsin had read it perhaps forty times. She knew it the way she knew the reef channels now: *I chose the truth over the fortune. If you're reading this, finish it differently than I did. Don't do it alone.*

She sat down on the sand above the tideline with the letter in her hands and the navy's offer folded in her pocket, and she tried to weigh them against each other, and discovered she couldn't, because they weren't the same kind of thing at all.

Her mother had chosen truth and died for it, betrayed by the man whose map had started all of this. Tamsin had finished it — surfaced with the documents, delivered them, watched the minister fall. And the truth had won, mostly, publicly, in the way truths win: loudly for a season, then quietly, then not at all, buried under newer scandals and older habits. The families had gotten their names back. Ovid's history would fix the rest of it in ink, permanent, undeniable — unless.

Unless a cream-colored envelope arrived on a Tuesday.

The pardon would erase Ovid's thirty years in the wilderness. The salvage rights would give Brannoc his yard. The commission would put Tamsin's name in navy ledgers as Salvage Master, First Class — her mother's daughter, legitimized, pensioned, respectable. Everything she'd never let herself want, itemized. And underneath it, in letters so small you'd need to dive to read them: *never publish, surrender the evidence, let the Corvane stay a rumor forever.*

Her mother had been offered versions of this too. Tamsin was sure of it now. Somewhere in those last months, someone had sat across from Mara Montrose with a folder of considerations, and Mara had said no, and someone on her own crew had sold her for it, and she had died holding a strongroom wheel in the dark.

*Don't do it alone,* the letter said.

Tamsin looked up. The beach behind her wasn't empty anymore. Sefa had come down the tideline without her noticing — the girl moved like that, like current — and stood a few feet away with her arms crossed, watching the water rather than Tamsin, because Sefa never watched people directly when what they were carrying was heavy.

"My grandmother used to say," Sefa said, "that the tide doesn't bargain. It just comes. You can stand there and negotiate with it all day and it just keeps coming." She paused. "Pell's boat is still anchored out there. I've been counting how long he waits. He's been out there four hours. They told him to wait as long as it takes."

"That's a lot of hourly pay."

"That's a lot of *wanting*," Sefa corrected. "Somebody upstairs wants this very badly, and badly enough to spend a lieutenant commander's whole week on our beach." She finally looked at Tamsin. "You already know what you're going to do."

"Do I?"

"You've had the same face since you read the letter. It's your mother's face. I've seen the portrait Ovid has. It's the same face you make before you do something expensive."

Tamsin laughed despite herself — a short, rough sound the wind took. "Expensive."

"Brannoc's list," Sefa said. "The yard. The pardon. All of it. Whatever you choose, it's going to cost somebody something, and you're deciding whose bill it is." She shrugged, fourteen-going-forty, insufferable, indispensable. "I know which one I'd pick. But it's not mine to pick. That's the thing about captains."

She wandered off down the beach toward the harbor lights, leaving Tamsin alone with the letter and the tide, which continued, unbargained-with, to come in.

* * *

Night had settled fully by the time she climbed back aboard. The lamp was lit in the main cabin, and everyone was there — Brannoc with clean hands for once, Ovid with his manuscript stacked and squared, Sefa curled in the corner seat like a cat who owned the place. They'd been waiting for her. They'd made tea, which was how this crew held meetings: nothing so formal as a table, just mugs and lamplight and the rock of the hull.

"I've done the sums," Brannoc said, before she'd even sat. "All of them. The bullion, the yard, the pension, twenty years of it. Do you want to know the figure? It's obscene. It's a figure with a comma in a place commas shouldn't go." He turned his mug in his hands. "And then I did the other sum. What Ovid's book is worth. And I couldn't get the numbers into the same column. They don't fit. It's like asking what a reef weighs."

"It weighs what it weighs," Sefa said. "The question is whether you sail over it or around it."

"I spent thirty years being a footnote," Ovid said. His voice was steady, which surprised Tamsin; she'd expected anger, or worse, the terrible dignity he wore when he was most wounded. "Thirty years cataloguing other men's loot and calling it scholarship. That pardon would hand me back my life. My *name*." He removed his spectacles. "And I find that I don't want it back at that price. Isn't that absurd? I have wanted nothing else for three decades, and now that it's on the table I wouldn't take it. Because the book isn't mine, Tamsin. It's theirs." He touched the manuscript. "The Voss family. The Meridian families. Your mother. If I sell it, I'm just the archivist again — the man who catalogues the truth and files it where powerful people can find it and lose it."

"So we refuse," Brannoc said. He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "We refuse, and I keep my dying-walrus injector, and I'll love it, I'll cherish it, I'll write it poetry." He looked around the lamplight at each of them. "I want the yard. God, I want the yard. But I helped raise a dead woman's truth out of sixty fathoms, and I'm not handing it to the same navy that sent dredges after it. Refuse. All in favor."

"Aye," said Sefa.

"Aye," said Ovid.

They all looked at Tamsin.

She reached into her pocket and brought out the cream-colored letter, and set it on the table between the mugs, and for a long moment nobody touched it. Then she picked it up again, and left the cabin, and they followed her — because they followed her now, that was the strange alchemy of the last year — down the gangway and along the dark beach to the tideline, where the fire ring from some fisherman's earlier evening still held warm coals.

Tamsin knelt and fed the coals a handful of dry driftwood until they flared. Then she held the Admiralty's offer over the flame and watched the cream paper catch — the seal going first, gold leaf burning black, then the itemized fortunes curling away one by one: the salvage rights, the pardon, the commission, *Salvage Master, First Class*, her mother's daughter made respectable at last, all of it rising off the beach as sparks and ash while the tide kept coming in below, unbargained-with, indifferent, free.

"Twenty years of pension," Brannoc said mournfully, watching it burn.

"Write it a poem," Sefa said.

Ovid said nothing at all. He was watching the ash lift, and there were tears on his face, and he was smiling, and Tamsin thought: *there it is. That's what it looks like when a man gets his name back without selling it.*

When the last corner crumbled, Tamsin brushed her hands and stood. Behind them, out in the harbor, Pell's launch still rode at anchor, a single riding light burning — a man waiting as long as it takes for an answer that was now literally smoke.

"He'll wait till dawn," Sefa said. "Should we tell him?"

"No need," Tamsin said. "By morning he'll have smelled the smoke."

They walked back up the beach together, four shadows in the lamplight spilling from the Kestrel's Wake, and it was Ovid who stopped at the gangway and said, quietly, the archivist's habit of precision surfacing even now:

"You should know — the inquiry report prints in six weeks. And when it does, every question they buried will surface again. Including the ones about Reyes." He paused. "Including the ones about Voss. Someone will come asking what else the strongroom held. Someone always does."

Tamsin looked out at the dark water, where the Corvane slept under silt and storm, keeping what was left to keep.

"Let them ask," she said. "We've got a boat that answers honestly."

But even as she said it, she was thinking of the knife in her sea chest — her mother's dive knife, recovered from the bulkhead, cleaned and oiled and wrapped in oilcloth — and of the reef where the Corvane lay, and of something her mother's letter had said near the end, the line Tamsin had never managed to say aloud to anyone:

*Some things belong to the water. Bring me home to it, if you ever can.*

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would check the charts for the reef passage, and check the weather, and tell no one yet — because some errands a diver makes alone.

The tide came in, all night, asking nothing.
