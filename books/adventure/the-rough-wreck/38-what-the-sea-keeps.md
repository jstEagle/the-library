## 38. What the Sea Keeps

The morning of the dive, Tamsin woke before the gulls did.

That was saying something, because the Meridian harbor gulls were notorious even among sailors — Brannoc had once described them as "flying lawyers with an appetite for anything not nailed down and half the things that are." They started their racket at first light, wheeling over the fish market, screaming at each other about pilchards. But Tamsin was on deck before them, sitting in the grey hush with a mug of tea going cold in her hands, watching the water breathe.

It had been three weeks since they'd burned the navy's offer. Three weeks since she'd stood on the beach with Ovid's history finally set to print — free, unbuyable — and fed the minister's letter to the flames one corner at a time while Sefa kept lookout for witnesses and Brannoc kept making dry remarks about how if she was going to commit arson she might as well have burned something useful, like the survey boat's bilge pump, which had been trying to kill them all season.

Three weeks of quiet. And still, every night, she dreamed of the wreck.

Not the collapse. Not Brannoc's blood in the water, or the silt wall rolling over the reef like a grey avalanche. She dreamed of the compartment — the drowned cabin, the skeleton in her mother's rig, one hand resting on the strongroom wheel as if it had simply paused there, waiting. Waiting thirty years for someone to finish the sentence her mother had started.

The documents were delivered. The truth was out. The governor's confession had done its work in the world, loud and irreversible. So why, Tamsin asked herself over that cold tea, did it feel like there was still something down there with her name on it?

She knew the answer. She'd known it since the night she'd found the knife.

Her mother's dive knife. Wedged in a bulkhead on the third tide, crusted with thirty years of coral, the handle worn smooth in the exact places a hand would grip it. Tamsin had carried it ever since — through the mutiny weather, through the chase, through the monsoon, through everything. It lived in a canvas roll under her bunk, wrapped like something surgical, and she took it out most nights when the boat was quiet and held it without quite knowing what she was holding. A tool? A relic? An accusation?

A knife was a diver's most personal thing. You chose it the way you chose a partner. Her mother had chosen this one, used it on a hundred wrecks, and then left it jammed in a bulkhead of the Corvane because — Tamsin understood now — she'd needed both hands for the wheel. Because she'd thought she was coming back for it.

Because she hadn't come back.

"You're up early." Sefa's voice, soft on the companionway ladder. The girl came up rubbing sleep from her eyes, hair a wild black halo, and dropped onto the rail beside her without asking. She never asked anymore. That was what family meant, Tamsin had decided: nobody asking.

"Couldn't sleep."

Sefa looked at the water, then at her. "You're diving today."

It wasn't a question. Sefa read currents the way other people read faces, and apparently she'd learned to read Tamsin too.

"The reef," Tamsin said. "Where the Corvane went down. One last dive."

"To salvage?"

"No." Tamsin turned the mug in her hands. "To put something back."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she did when a channel checked out against her instincts — no argument, just confirmation. "I'll read the water for you. Slack tide's near noon. After that the current turns ugly around the outer shelf."

"I know."

"You know *now*," Sefa said, with the ghost of a grin. "In six months you'll forget again and I'll have to shout at you. That's the arrangement."

"That's the arrangement," Tamsin agreed, and found, to her mild surprise, that she was smiling.

* * *

Brannoc heard about it over breakfast and made a production of disapproval, because that was how he loved people.

"A ceremonial dive," he said, gesturing with a forkful of eggs. "To a collapsed wreck site. In post-monsoon water. Alone. Have I got the shape of it?"

"Not alone. Sefa's topside."

"One teenager in a dinghy. Splendid. When I drown of worry, note it in the log."

"You don't dive, Brannoc. Your worry has nowhere to drown."

He pointed the fork at her. "I was caught in a demolition charge, Montrose. I've earned the right to be unreasonable about water."

He had, too. The scars along his ribs had healed into pale ridges, and he still moved carefully some mornings, and he still refused — absolutely refused — to talk about the minutes between the detonation and Tamsin reaching him. But he'd also spent those three weeks rebuilding the Kestrel's Wake's engine with his own hands until it purred like something contented, so his relationship with the sea was complicated, the way family relationships usually were.

By midmorning he'd stopped complaining and started helping, which was his second way of loving people. He checked her rig twice, tested both regulators himself, swore at the spare mask strap, and finally pressed something into her hand: a small brass shackle, polished bright.

"What's this?"

"So the knife doesn't drift off into the blue and become someone else's problem in forty years," he said gruffly. "Shackle it to something with weight. Give it an anchor. Things you give to the sea should stay where you put them. That's basic engineering."

It was, Tamsin thought, the most sentimental thing Brannoc had ever said, disguised as basic engineering. She didn't call it that. She just closed her hand around the shackle and said, "Thanks."

"Don't mention it. Ever. To anyone."

* * *

Ovid came up on deck as they loaded the dinghy, moving slowly, a book under his arm as always. He was walking better these days; the sea air and the purpose had done what thirty years of archive dust hadn't. He watched them rig the tanks with the expression of a man attending a funeral he approved of.

"You're taking the knife," he said.

"Yes."

"And you're leaving it."

"Yes."

Ovid nodded slowly. "In my profession we'd call that returning an item to its proper collection." He adjusted his spectacles. "Your mother, from everything I've pieced together — and I've pieced together a great deal, it's rather my condition — your mother was the finest kind of person the sea produces. The kind who finds the truth and refuses to sell it. There aren't many collections that deserve her. I'd say the Corvane does."

Tamsin looked at him. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. I was previously also drunk. The order of operations confused everyone." He handed her the book — it was his manuscript, the true history, still in galley proofs. "Page two hundred and four. Read it when you come back. I wrote your mother into it properly. She'd have hated the attention and loved the accuracy."

* * *

The reef where the Corvane slept was a half-day's sail, and they made it by late morning under a sky scrubbed clean by the retreating monsoon. The water out here was the deep honest blue of open ocean, and the reef rose toward it in terraces of coral, and somewhere beneath the sand and the new growth lay the wreck — flattened now, buried, sealed by the storm and by Reyes's charges and by time itself. No salvage fleet would find her again. No minister could make her vanish more thoroughly than the sea already had.

The sea keeps what it wants, Tamsin thought, kitting up on the rocking dinghy. And gives back what it's ready to.

Sefa anchored the dinghy and ran through the checks with her, brisk and serious, and then — unexpectedly — gripped her forearm, diver to diver, hard.

"She'd be proud of you," Sefa said. "Whoever she was. Anyone who leaves a knife behind is planning to come back, and you came back for her. That counts."

Tamsin's throat did something complicated. "Down in twenty minutes. If I'm not up in forty—"

"I know the drill. I wrote half of it." Sefa grinned, then sobered. "Go on, then. Say what you need to say."

Tamsin rolled backward into the blue.

* * *

The descent was familiar in the way a childhood street is familiar — changed, but mapped into the body. Light fell in long columns through clear water. Fish scattered like thrown coins. She followed the terrace of the reef down, past coral heads the color of old fire, and the deeper she went the quieter the world became, until there was only the sound of her own breathing and the small mechanical music of her regulator, and the vast patient silence of the place where the Corvane lay.

She found the site by the debris field — the scatter of hull plating and concreted iron that marked where the ship had come apart. New coral was already claiming it. Soft fingers of it reached over a rib of the keel. A school of small silver fish had made a home of what had been a cargo hold, pouring through the broken frames like liquid light.

Here. This was the place. Her mother had died within sight of this spot, thirty years ago, alone, with the truth in her hands and no one to carry it up.

Tamsin hung in the water above the wreck and let herself feel all of it at once — the grief, huge and unhurried, and the strange gratitude underneath it, because the same obsession that had killed her mother had given Tamsin a map, a promise, a crew, a family, a reason to stop moving. She'd spent her whole life running from contracts and harbors and people, and it had taken a dead woman's chart to teach her how to stay.

She took out the knife.

Thirty years underwater and three weeks in her bunk had left it stripped clean of coral but marked forever — the blade pitted, the handle worn smooth. She'd sharpened it one last time the night before, though it would never cut anything again. Some tools retire. Some are laid to rest.

She threaded Brannoc's brass shackle through the lanyard hole and shackled the knife to a length of chain, and the chain to a dead-eye of the Corvane's standing rigging — a fitting that had held fast through the collapse, half-buried in coral, solid as the day it was forged. Basic engineering. An anchor. The knife wouldn't drift. It would stay where she put it, in the open water her mother had loved, on the ship her mother had died to find.

Then, because the water was private and the sea keeps confidences, Tamsin spoke — a blur of bubbles and sound that no one would ever transcribe. She told her mother about Voss, and the promise, and the letter. About the crew of strays who'd become something else. About the girl who read currents and the engineer who loved through complaint and the archivist who'd traded thirty years of guilt for one act of courage. About the choice she'd made on the beach, burning the navy's offer, choosing the truth over the fortune, the same choice her mother had made at the bottom of this same sea.

*I finished it,* she told her. *We finished it. You can put the knife down now.*

And it seemed to Tamsin — though she would never swear to it, never claim it as fact, only as the feeling a diver gets sometimes in deep quiet water — that the current shifted, very slightly, warm against her cheek, like an answer. Or like a hand letting go.

She hung there a while longer, breathing, letting the sea hold the weight she'd been carrying since a port bar full of seawater and blood. Then she checked her gauge, found it honest, and began the slow ascent, lighter than she'd been in years.

* * *

Sefa pulled her aboard and didn't ask what it was like, which was its own kindness. Brannoc pretended not to check her twice. Ovid was waiting on deck with page two hundred and four already marked, and Tamsin sat dripping in the cockpit and read what he'd written:

*"Maren Montrose dove the Corvane alone and found the truth, and the truth cost her everything except the future — because the future arrived thirty years late, in the shape of her daughter, and it kept her promise."*

Tamsin read it twice. Then she handed it back and said, "She'd have hated the attention."

"And loved the accuracy," Ovid said.

They raised sail for home. Behind them the reef sank back into its blue silence, keeping what it kept — a knife on a chain, a ship full of nothing but memory, and at last, at rest, a diver's daughter who no longer needed to dive away from anything.

Ahead, the horizon was clear all the way to Meridian harbor, where — Tamsin could see it even from here — something unfamiliar waited at their usual mooring: a small crowd on the quay, and among them, unmistakable even at this distance, a figure in a navy greatcoat, standing very straight, waiting for them to come in.

Sefa saw it too. "Trouble?"

Tamsin watched the figure grow as they closed the harbor, and felt the old instinct to run rise and fall, rise and fall, like a tide going out for good.

"No," she said slowly. "I think it's an invitation."

Brannoc snorted. "Those are rarely different things."

But the figure on the quay, as they came alongside, resolved into a face Tamsin knew — older, harder, but hers — and the woman in the navy greatcoat said quietly, across the widening gap of water, "Hello, Tamsin. We should talk. About your mother — and about me."

Reyes had come ashore.
