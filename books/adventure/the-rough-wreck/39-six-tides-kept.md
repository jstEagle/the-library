## 39. Six Tides, Kept

### Sefa

The bell at the Meridian Maritime School rang at seven, and Sefa was already in her seat, which was a small miracle she intended to keep quiet about forever.

She sat third row, window side, where the morning light came off the harbor and made slow gold shapes on her desk. Her textbook was open to tidal harmonics. Her actual notes were open underneath it, because the textbook was fine but the harbor was better. You could read about amphidromic points all day, but you couldn't *feel* one through a page, and Sefa had spent two years of her life feeling them through the soles of her feet on a deck that wanted to kill her.

"Instructor Vane says the exam will cover lunar semidiurnal cycles," whispered the boy next to her, a round-faced islander named Pell who had decided in September that they were friends and had never once been contradicted.

"I know," Sefa said.

"You could just tell me the answers."

"That's cheating."

"You survived a monsoon chase through an uncharted reef maze. I feel like your relationship with rules is flexible."

Sefa considered this. "Rules are like currents," she said. "You respect the big ones, and the little ones you use."

Pell wrote that down as if it were going to be on the exam. It would not be. It was, however, going to be on his gravestone if he ever tried to pilot anything.

When the bell rang out, she gathered her books and ran — actually ran, bag bouncing, down the hill toward the harbor — because Thursdays were contract days, and contract days meant the crew argued about dinner, and arguing about dinner was, in Sefa's professional opinion, the finest sport yet invented by humankind.

The boat sat at berth six. She'd been re-planked twice, re-engined once, and repainted a color that Brannoc insisted was "harbor gray" and everyone else insisted was "the color of old dishwater," and her name had changed with everything else.

They'd argued about that too, for three weeks, the whole crew shouting suggestions across the galley table until Ovid, of all people, ended it by standing up with the gravity of a man delivering a eulogy and saying, "We were given six tides. We kept every one of them. Call her *Kept Tide* and let me eat my soup."

So: *Kept Tide*, berth six, dishwater-gray, home.

Sefa came down the gangway at a run and stopped dead at the hatch, because the smell coming up out of the galley was a war crime.

"What," she said, "is that."

"Dinner," said Brannoc's voice, from below, defensive already. "It's a stew."

"It's a *threat*, Brannoc."

"It's fish, potatoes, and a spice blend of my own devising."

"The spice blend is the crime part."

She dropped down the ladder into the galley, where Brannoc stood over a pot wearing an apron that said KISS THE ENGINEER, a gift from Sefa herself, chosen specifically because it made him scowl. Ovid sat at the table with his manuscript stacked beside his bowl like a barricade, and Tamsin leaned against the bulkhead with her arms crossed, watching the pot the way she used to watch weather.

"It needs time," Brannoc said.

"It needs a priest," said Sefa.

Ovid turned a page without looking up. "In my archive years I catalogued a naval incident in which a cook's experimental curry caused three men to abandon ship in a storm rather than eat it. The board of inquiry called it 'a failure of leadership.' I have always felt that was unfair to the leadership."

"I'm getting a second opinion." Sefa turned to Tamsin. "Captain. Your ruling."

Tamsin looked at the pot for a long moment. Steam rose off it. Somewhere in that steam there might once have been fish.

"Tamsin," Brannoc warned.

"I've eaten worse," Tamsin said at last, "off the floor of a flooded cargo hold."

"That's not a ruling, that's a trauma response!"

"It's a *vote*. Two to one. Sit down, engineer."

Brannoc muttered something about mutiny being legal now apparently, and ladled out four bowls, and it was — Sefa admitted this only internally, and never aloud, and would deny it under oath — actually not terrible. The fish was a little overdone and the spice blend tasted like someone had described spices to Brannoc over a bad radio connection, but it was hot, and there was enough of it, and nobody was bleeding.

That was the thing about dinner aboard the *Kept Tide* these days. That was the whole miracle of it, the thing Sefa still caught herself noticing, a year on, like a scar you keep touching to check it's real: dinner was just dinner. Nobody ate fast, scanning the horizon. Nobody saved half their portion in case tomorrow didn't come. Brannoc complained about the rice budget. Ovid complained about interruptions. Tamsin complained about both of them complaining.

And Sefa sat there with her bowl and her family and thought about her grandmother's house on Meridian, burned in the requisitions, the family scattered across four islands — and thought about how her grandmother used to say that the sea takes and takes, but sometimes, if you're stubborn, it gives back something it took from somebody else.

She'd gotten more than her share.

"So," she said, around a mouthful of threatening stew, "exam's in three weeks. If I pass top of cohort, the school puts your name on the commendation letter, Captain. Officially. 'Tamsin Montrose, guardian of record.'"

Tamsin went very still, the way she did when something landed somewhere she hadn't armored.

"Don't make it weird," Sefa added quickly.

"I wasn't going to make it weird."

"Your face is making it weird."

"My face is eating stew."

Ovid smiled down at his manuscript. Brannoc pretended to be fascinated by the bottom of his bowl. And outside, the harbor water slapped gently against the hull of a boat that wasn't going anywhere tonight, or tomorrow, or for as long as any of them wanted it to stay.

### Brannoc

Brannoc woke before dawn, as he always did, because a man who has drowned once develops opinions about being awake when things can go wrong.

He climbed into the engine room with his tea and his lamp and ran his hands over the new main like a physician taking a pulse. She was a good engine. He'd built her out of a decommissioned tug plant, a fishing boat's gearbox, and one component he refused to discuss the provenance of, and in fourteen months she had not missed a beat. He talked to her sometimes. He would deny that too, under oath, though his oaths were developing a generally unreliable character lately, what with the apron and the stew and the rest of it.

Fourteen months ago he'd lain on the seabed inside a collapsing wreck with his leg pinned and his air running out, and he had thought, with perfect clarity: *I never finished the refit. The injector timing is half a degree off and no one will ever know.*

Then a hand had come out of the black water and found his collar, and Tamsin Montrose had hauled him out of hell by sheer fury, and Sefa's voice had come down the line reading currents like scripture, and Ovid — Ovid, who had never swum farther than a bath — had stood on the pitching deck shouting deck plans from memory while the world fell in.

He'd woken up in Meridian with a rebuilt leg, a bill he couldn't pay, and a crew that hadn't left.

He'd spent a year trying to figure out what to do with that.

The answer, it turned out, was: fix things. Legitimate things, for legitimate clients, at legitimate rates. A ferry's cooling system. A lighthouse generator. Last month, absurdly, a church organ, which had not been wet but had certainly sounded like it had been. The *Kept Tide*'s hold carried tools now instead of dive gear, mostly, though Brannoc had kept the salvage rig maintained anyway. Not for use. Just because a man sleeps better knowing his ropes are good.

Footsteps on the ladder. Tamsin, hair still damp from a morning swim, dropping onto the workbench beside him with the ease of someone who had long since stopped asking permission to enter his engine room.

"She's running sweet," she said.

"She's always running sweet. I built her."

"You built her out of a stolen—"

"*Salvaged* component whose provenance we agreed never to discuss."

"We did. I just enjoy watching your eye twitch."

He grunted and marked the log. Outside the portlight, the harbor was turning pink, and the fishing fleet was heading out in a loose scatter, and somewhere uphill a school bell was going to ring in forty minutes and a girl who read currents with her feet was going to be in her seat early again and pretend she hadn't rushed.

"Contract papers came," Tamsin said. "Channel survey, north passage. Legitimate. Boring, even."

"Boring pays."

"Boring pays," she agreed, and neither of them moved for a moment, and the engine ticked as it cooled, and Brannoc thought: *this is it, then. This is the thing I got pulled out of the wreck to have.* Not fortune. Not fame. A cold morning, a good engine, a captain sitting on his workbench stealing warmth off his tea.

"Your stew," he said, "was an abomination."

"It won the vote."

"It won because Ovid votes strategically and Sefa votes against me on principle."

"Yes," Tamsin said comfortably. "That's how families work."

He didn't say anything to that. His eye twitched, but for an entirely different reason, and he let her think it was the provenance comment.

### Ovid

The history was finished. That was the strange part. Thirty years of silence, and then one year of writing, and now it was done: *The Corvane Papers: An Account of the Meridian Requisitions, with Documents.* Four hundred pages. The governor's confession reproduced in full. Every family named, every theft dated, every signature witnessed.

The minister had fallen in the spring. The inquiry was still grinding through the admiralty like a slow tide, pulling names up out of the mud one by one. Captain Ilsa Reyes had been recalled in the autumn; the papers said her hearing was scheduled for the new year. Ovid followed those reports the way he followed all reports now — carefully, completely, and without gloating, because he had spent thirty years learning exactly what silence cost, and he had no appetite for watching another person pay it in public.

He sat in the stern with the finished manuscript boxed for the printer and let the harbor move beneath him.

A year ago he had been a drunk archivist with a ruined reputation and a secret the size of a shipwreck. Now he was a sober historian with a modest reputation and no secrets at all, and he could report, with scholarly authority, that the second condition was infinitely preferable.

Sefa had read the draft. All of it, including the chapter about him — the cataloguing, the silence, the thirty years. She'd read it and handed it back and said, "You put it in the book where everyone can see it. That's the bravest dumb thing I ever heard of." Then she'd punched his arm hard enough to bruise, which he understood, in the grammar of teenagers, to mean forgiveness.

Brannoc had not read it. Brannoc had said, "I know what you did. I know what you did after. Second part's the one I keep." Which was, Ovid reflected, the most eloquent review he would ever receive.

And Tamsin — Tamsin had read the chapter about Ede Voss, the informant, the betrayal, the map bought with a dying man's guilt. She had closed the box on that chapter herself, and sealed it, and said only: "He paid what he owed. Print it true."

Print it true. That was the whole creed now, the one they'd all bled for, the one that had cost them a fortune lying on the seabed where the sea could keep it. Ovid had been offered money for the story — syndicates, publishers, one memorably persistent man from a gazette who'd chased him around the fish market for a week. So had they all. And one by one, in galleys and engine rooms and on night watches, each of them had said no, in their own way, until the truth belonged to everybody and therefore to no one's purse.

His only regret was that the book could not include the best chapter: the one about dinner arguments and stolen engine parts and a girl who was going to be the finest pilot these islands had ever produced. Some histories, he had decided, were not for printing. They were for keeping.

He heard the others laughing in the galley — Brannoc's bark, Sefa's cackle, Tamsin's rare, rusty, wonderful laugh — and Ovid settled his hat over his eyes and let the sound be the last thing he heard before his nap, as befitted a man who had finally earned his rest.

### Tamsin

Evening came down over Meridian harbor the way it always did, gold going to violet, the masts going dark against it, and Tamsin stood her watch at the rail of the *Kept Tide* and did the thing she had done every evening for a year without quite admitting it.

She checked the weather. She checked the tide tables. She checked, out of fourteen years of habit, the horizon for sails that might be hunting them.

And then she checked the mooring lines — snug — and the harbor master's flag — calm — and realized, as she did every evening, that there was nothing to run from, nowhere to run to, and no part of her that wanted either.

That was the strange part. Not the safety. The *wanting to stay*.

She had spent her whole life keeping moving, because moving meant you never had to watch a place learn your name and then use it against you. Ports were contracts. Crews were temporary. Family was a word other people used about people who left. Her mother had taught her the sea, and the sea had taken her mother, and Tamsin had decided somewhere in her grief-drunk twenties that love was just ballast you'd eventually have to jettison.

Then a dying prospector had pressed a chart into her hands and asked for one promise, and she'd made it too easily, the way you promise things when you don't expect to survive keeping them.

Six tides, he'd said. She'd gotten five and a half, and a collapsed wreck, and a nearly-dead engineer, and a letter from a woman ten years drowned, and a truth worth more than the gold they'd left lying in the dark for the sea to guard.

And then the sixth tide had come in, and gone out, and instead of sailing away from all of it she had stayed, and enrolled a runaway in school, and funded a historian, and rebuilt an engineer, and somehow — without noticing the exact day it happened — acquired a family.

"Ede," she said quietly, to the water, to the wreck that slept under different water now, far to the south, "we kept them. All six."

The harbor didn't answer. Harbors never did. But the tide turned right on schedule, gentle as a kept promise, and the *Kept Tide* shifted against her lines and settled, unhurried, unafraid, going nowhere.

From the galley below came the sound of the crew arguing about breakfast now, having exhausted dinner — Sefa advocating for fried dough on the grounds that exams deserved celebration, Brannoc insisting that fried dough was not food but "a delivery system for regret," Ovid declaring neutrality while visibly planning to eat both.

Tamsin smiled, opened the logbook, and wrote the evening entry in a steady hand:

*Position: berth six, Meridian harbor. Weather fair. Crew accounted for. No course set.*

She looked at that last line for a long moment. For fourteen years she had written *no course set* as a confession, a small shame between the lines.

Tonight she wrote it as an answer.

Then, because the light was almost gone and the first star was out and nobody was watching, she added one more line, small, in the corner of the page, where only she would ever find it:

*Home.*

Below, Sefa shouted something about doubling the sugar, Brannoc roared in protest, and Tamsin Montrose — diver, salvager, drifter, daughter, captain — shut the logbook, and stayed.
