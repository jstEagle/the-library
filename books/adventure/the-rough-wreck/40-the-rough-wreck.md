## 40. The Rough Wreck

The last night of the monsoon had a sound to it that Tamsin had never quite managed to describe to anyone who hadn't stood a watch through one. It wasn't the violence of the storm's arrival — that had been a wall of black water and screaming wind, the kind of noise that made you feel very small and very temporary. No, the ending was quieter than that. The rain thinned from sheets to threads to a fine drifting mist, and the wind dropped from a howl to a mutter to something almost conversational, and by three in the morning the Kestrel's Wake was riding swells so gentle they might have been breathing.

Tamsin stood the dawn watch alone, which was how she preferred it, though she would never have admitted that out loud anymore. There had been a time — not so long ago, if she was honest, though it felt like another life entirely — when being alone on a boat at dawn had been the whole point of her existence. She'd built her career out of it. Contracts in, contracts out, harbors behind her, horizons ahead, and nobody aboard whose absence she'd have to notice.

She noticed things now. That was the trouble with letting people in: you started noticing them.

She could hear Brannoc snoring through the deck plates, a rhythmic industrial clatter like an engine idling slightly out of true. He'd deny it to his dying day, insist he slept like a man at peace, but the man sounded like a foundry with opinions. Sefa's hammock was slung in the old chart room because she said the cabin felt like a coffin, and every so often she talked in her sleep — fragments, mostly about currents, once, memorably, about a fish she claimed owed her money. Ovid slept properly only when the sea was calm; in rough weather he sat up reading by lamplight, as if the history he was writing needed him awake to defend it.

And below all of it, under the snoring and the sleep-talk and the creak of the hull, there was the other sound: the sound of a boat that had been through hell and come out the far side still floating. Brannoc had rebuilt half the engine housing after the reef run. The patch over the starboard porthole was new glass in an old frame. The scorch mark where the transmitter had been ripped out of the wiring — Brannoc had left that, actually, refused to paint over it. "So we remember," he'd said, and then he'd gone red to the ears, because Brannoc said sentimental things the way other men stepped on rakes.

Tamsin leaned on the rail and watched the east.

The sky over Meridian was doing that thing it did at the end of the wet season — burning low and gold along the horizon while the clouds above it went soft and grey-pink, like the whole sky was a bruise healing. The sea beneath it was the color of hammered pewter, running in long slow swells, and for the first time in two months there was nothing on it. No navy hulls. No dredge barges. No scout ships hanging on the edge of visibility like bad news waiting to be delivered. Just water, and light coming up over it, and the Wake riding both.

She let herself have the moment. That was another thing she was learning, slowly, the way you learn a language late in life — awkwardly, with mistakes, sometimes needing to be corrected. Letting yourself have a moment. Standing still inside it instead of already planning the exit.

* * *

At half past five she went into the wheelhouse and opened the logbook.

It was Ovid's habit to keep the formal log — his handwriting was beautiful, a flowing archivist's script that made even fuel complaints look like poetry — but the dawn watch entries had always been hers, going back to their first legitimate contract out of Port Calloway. She'd started them as a way of giving the watch something to do besides drink coffee and count the minutes. Now they were just part of the shape of the day, like sunrise itself.

She wrote the position first, latitude and longitude in careful figures, the course, the speed — anchored, riding to a single hook in twelve fathoms off the eastern shoulder of Meridian Harbor, wind southeast freshening, sea moderate, weather clearing. All the ordinary words. Then she sat with the pen hovering, because some mornings the log wanted more than weather, and this morning it wanted more whether she liked it or not.

A year ago today — well, near enough; Ovid would know the exact date, Ovid knew everything, it was his curse and his gift — a year ago she had been diving an unlisted wreck off Kestrel Point, pulling a hull plate stamped with a serial number that shouldn't exist, and the world had been exactly as big as she'd assumed it was, which is to say: no bigger than the next contract. She hadn't known Ede Voss yet. She hadn't known what her mother had died reaching for. She hadn't known Reyes's face could go soft and hard in the same breath, or that a teenage runaway could read a riptide like scripture, or that a disgraced engineer's stubbornness could hold a boat together through anything short of the actual end of the world.

She hadn't known that a promise made too easily to a dying man could turn out to be the truest thing she'd ever done.

Tamsin Montrose had spent thirty years perfecting the art of leaving. Ports, crews, harbors, relationships — she'd left them all with a professionalism that people mistook for coldness until they saw her do it, and then they understood it was worse than coldness. It was practice. She'd had so much practice at leaving that she genuinely believed, for most of her life, that staying was something other people did, people with roots and patience and a tolerance for being known.

And now here she was. Anchored. In the same harbor for — she did the math and winced — eleven months. Eleven months in one port, longer than she'd stayed anywhere since she was nineteen years old, and the remarkable thing, the thing that kept catching her off guard like a step that wasn't where her foot expected, was that it didn't itch. She kept waiting for it to itch. She checked, some mornings, the way you check an old wound. Nothing. Just the harbor, and the boat, and the sounds of her ridiculous crew sleeping off yesterday's dinner argument.

Because yes, they argued about dinner now. That was the state of affairs. Last night's dispute — Sefa wanted to try the new cookhouse on the north quay, Ovid held that the noodle stall by the customs house was objectively superior and had, horrifyingly, brought *evidence*, a hand-drawn comparative chart of flavor profiles across six vendors — had lasted forty minutes and ended in a draw and a promise to conduct further field research tonight. Tamsin had sat there listening to them and thought, with a clarity that still startled her: *this is what I ran from my whole life.* Not the danger. Not the sea. This. Being known well enough to argue with. Being expected somewhere at dinnertime.

She put the pen to the paper.

Below the weather entry, in her plain diver's handwriting — no flourishes; Brannoc teased her that her logs read like cargo manifests — she wrote:

*Some wrecks are meant to be raised; some are meant to be remembered.*

She looked at it for a long moment. It wasn't much. Sixteen words. But it was the whole year folded down small, the Corvane and the strongroom and the letter in her mother's hand, Voss's chart and Voss's sin and Voss's penance, the documents that had toppled a minister, the gold they'd left lying in the dark because some things were worth more than what they weighed. Her mother's knife, resting now on open sand where the wreck used to be, before the charges and the silt and the storm took it down deeper. Some wrecks raised. Some remembered. And one — the roughest wreck of all, the one she'd been carrying in her own chest since she was a child watching her mother's boat come back empty — finally, finally laid to rest.

She blotted the page, closed the book, and went back out into the growing light.

* * *

The sun cleared the headland at ten past six, and Meridian Harbor came alive around them the way it did every morning — fishing boats chugging out through the cut, the smell of charcoal and frying dough drifting off the north quay, gulls conducting their eternal shakedown operations over the fish market. The monsoon season was officially over. The harbormaster had posted it on the board by the customs house with the solemnity of a royal proclamation: SEASON OPEN. And all around the anchorage, boats were stirring like the whole harbor was waking from a long sleep, shaking off canvas covers, testing engines, pointing prows toward a season of honest work.

"Captain."

Sefa appeared at the top of the companionway with her hair still damp from a bucket wash and a mug of coffee in each hand, which was her version of a formal address. She'd taken to calling her *Captain* about four months ago, deadpan, after Tamsin had successfully asserted command during a minor crisis involving a fouled anchor and a very indignant harbor seal. Before that it had been *Tamsin*, and before *that*, for the first few weeks, it had been nothing at all — just a look, the guarded flat stare of a kid who'd learned that names were things adults used right before they broke promises.

Tamsin took the coffee. "You're up early."

"Couldn't sleep." Sefa leaned on the rail beside her, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation these days. Sefa had gotten taller. That seemed impossible given the amount she ate, but the evidence was undeniable — she'd grown two inches since the reef run, and had begun wearing her grandmother's silver ring on a cord around her neck instead of hidden in a sock drawer, and had stopped flinching, mostly, when doors slammed. The maritime school had accepted her for the autumn intake. She'd pretended to shrug about it. She'd also read the acceptance letter eleven times, which Ovid had counted, because Ovid counted everything.

"The horizon's clear," Sefa said eventually. "First time I've seen it clear since we got here."

"It'll take some getting used to."

"My grandmother used to say the monsoon doesn't end. It just goes away to think about what it's done." A ghost of a grin. "I used to think that was funny. Now I think it's just true."

They drank their coffee. Down the quay, Brannoc's voice rose over the fish market noise — he was arguing with a chandler about the price of brass fittings, an argument he lost weekly and re-fought anyway, on principle, the principle being that Brannoc had never met a fight he didn't at least want to attend. Ovid emerged shortly after, blinking, clutching his manuscript against the spray, and made his way down the gangway toward the archive with the air of a man going to battle, which, in Ovid's case, meant footnotes. The true history of the Corvane was in its third revision. The families of Meridian had insisted it be published properly, with their names in it, all of them, including the ones the navy had erased. Ovid had cried about it twice and denied both occasions.

"You know what today is," Sefa said.

"I know what today is."

"One year." She turned the word over. "Since Kestrel Point."

"Near enough. Ask Ovid for the exact date if you want to lose an afternoon."

Sefa laughed — easily, unguardedly, a sound that had taken months to arrive and now showed up daily, like a bird that had decided the feeder was reliable. Then she grew serious in the way she did, abruptly, like weather. "Do you ever think about going? Still?"

Tamsin considered it honestly, because Sefa deserved honesty; they all traded in it now, having learned the hard way what its absence cost. Did she think about leaving? She thought about the sea the way you thought about a first love — constantly, tenderly, without any intention of acting on it. The horizon still pulled. It always would. But the pull had changed its terms. It wasn't *go* anymore. It was more like *remember me*, and she could do that from an anchorage, from a deck at dawn, from a harbor where people knew her name and expected her at dinner.

"Every day," she said. "And every day it gets easier to stay. Turns out those aren't opposites. Nobody tells you that."

"No," Sefa agreed. "Nobody tells you anything. You have to nearly die in a collapsing wreck to find out." She drained her coffee. "Breakfast in twenty. Brannoc promised pancakes and then immediately regretted it, so if we don't get up there he'll renegotiate into porridge."

"He wouldn't dare."

"He absolutely would. He's already looking up porridge recipes. I heard him."

She went below, and Tamsin stayed at the rail a moment longer.

* * *

The last thing she did, before breakfast, before the day properly began, was walk aft.

They'd built something small on the stern quarter — Sefa's idea, Brannoc's carpentry, Ovid's wording. A brass plate, bolted to the rail mount where the old salvage crane used to sit, engraved with four lines. Three names: Mara Montrose. Ede Voss. And the third line just said *the crew of the Corvane, wherever they lie*. The fourth line was Ovid's contribution, and it read: *Raised, and remembered.*

Tamsin touched the plate with two fingers, the way she had every morning for a year, and looked out at the water.

Somewhere out past the reef, under sixty feet of green silence and a season's worth of new silt, the Corvane slept at last — truly slept, collapsed into the reef's embrace, her strongroom sealed forever with the gold nobody would ever spend. Reyes was gone, recalled to the inquiry, her command stripped down to questions she couldn't answer. The minister had fallen. The families had their truth. And Tamsin had a boat, a crew, a contract signed for the spring — legitimate survey work, boring as ballast, exactly the kind of job the old Tamsin would have fled inside a week.

The horizon was clear from one edge of the world to the other. The monsoon was over. The season was open.

Tamsin Montrose, who had spent her whole life becoming excellent at leaving, stood on the deck of the only place she'd ever stayed, and waited, gladly, for breakfast.
