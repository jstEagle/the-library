## 1. Six Tides

The sea off Point Adaeze turned the color of hammered pewter an hour before dawn, and Tamsin Okafor was already in the water.

She liked this hour best—the hour when the ocean hadn't decided yet what it wanted to be, when the surface lay flat as poured glass and the light came up from below instead of down from above. She hung at fifteen meters, weightless in the blue-gray murk, and let her body go quiet the way her father had taught her before she was old enough to understand why it mattered. *The water reads you first,* he used to say, tapping two fingers against his own chest. *It knows a frightened heart. It knows a greedy one. Give it nothing to read, and it will give you everything.*

Below her, the wreck waited.

She'd found it eleven days ago by accident—no, she corrected herself, kicking gently to hold her station, not by accident. Nothing on this coast was ever by accident. The trawler nets had been coming up torn for weeks, and every fisherman from here to Lomé had a theory: rocks, old cable, a whale's grave. Tamsin had run her sidescan over the complaints like a doctor listening to a chest, and there it was on the return—a hard shadow in forty-two meters of water, lying in the trough between two sand ridges, too long for a trawler, too square for a ship that had died naturally. A hull with geometry. A hull with *intent*.

And stamped into the navy chart she'd bought secondhand in Cotonou, right across those coordinates, in faded bureaucratic red: RESTRICTED WATERS — SURVEY IN PROGRESS — NO ENTRY.

Which meant, in the grammar of the West African coast, that whatever lay down there belonged to someone who had never once asked the sea's permission, and who would be very angry to learn the sea had already given it away.

Tamsin checked her gauges. Fifty-one bar. Bottom time twelve minutes. She exhaled slowly, watching her bubbles rise silver through the last of the dark, and descended.

* * *

The wreck resolved out of the gloom the way wrecks always did—not all at once but in pieces, as if the sea were reluctant to show the whole of what it kept. First the rail, furred white with coral so thick it looked like the ship had grown a second skeleton. Then the deck guns, or what she took for deck guns, crusted beyond recognition. Then the name, and her heart did the thing it always did when she read a dead ship's name, that small involuntary clench, because a name meant people, and people meant stories, and stories were the one cargo no salvage law could touch.

She couldn't read it. Too much growth. But the letterforms were tall and old-fashioned, serifed, colonial—the kind of lettering cut into ships when ships carried things worth cutting names into.

She swam the length of the hull, fins barely moving, reading the damage the way other people read newspapers. Stern blown out—explosive, internal, deliberate. Cargo hatch sprung but not shattered. Someone had scuttled this ship in a hurry and someone else had tried to open it afterward and failed, and both someones were long dead, and the sea had folded its hands over the whole affair and waited.

Forty years? Sixty? Hard to say. Long enough.

Her torch found the gap at frame fourteen where the hull plates had parted, a black seam just wide enough for a slim woman with good breath control and nothing left to lose. Which described her exactly.

She went in.

Inside, the corridor was a cathedral of rust. Her torch beam swung across collapsed bulkheads, a rain of silt hanging motionless in the still water like the ship's held breath. She moved the way her father had drilled into her—slow, symmetric, always knowing where her exit was, always leaving the room the way she'd found it. A school of cardinalfish scattered and regathered around her light like sparks around a magnet.

The strongbox sat where the scan had suggested it might, wedged under a fallen beam near what had been the purser's office, half-buried in sediment, iron gone the orange-brown of old blood. It was the size of a breadbox, banded with steel, locked with a mechanism that had surrendered to corrosion decades ago. Not gold-weight. Not coin-heavy. Document-weight, maybe. Or something stranger.

Tamsin worked the lift bag under it with the patience of someone threading a needle underwater, which is precisely what she was doing. Fill, check, fill again. The box groaned, shed a century of silt in a slow brown bloom, and rose.

That was when her radio crackled.

* * *

The sound came through her full-face mask comms, thin and distorted, and for one irrational moment she thought it was the wreck speaking. Then the voice resolved, and her stomach performed the small cold drop it always performed at that voice, no matter how many times she heard it.

*"Okafor."*

Silas Drame. Boateng's man. The debt collector with the voice of a man reading a menu.

She didn't answer. She watched the strongbox climb toward the pale ceiling of the surface, tethered to her line, and she told herself the crackle was just the radio, just interference, just the morning.

*"I know you can hear me. Your boat's transponder is pinging off my receiver right now, sweetheart. You're sitting on restricted water and I'm sitting on your paper. Fourteen months past due. Eze says come home or send something back worth talking about."*

Tamsin closed her eyes inside the mask. Above her, faintly, the lift bag broke the surface.

Her boat, the *Adaeze's Daughter*, rode at anchor two hundred meters north, and her ancient VHF was set to channel 16 because channel 16 was free and every other channel cost something, and Silas Drame had apparently decided that made it his personal telephone line to her conscience.

*"You've got six tides,"* he said. *"Eze's feeling generous. After that, he sells the paper to the Liberians, and they don't do tides. They do weeks."*

She keyed the mic with a gloved thumb. "Tell Eze Boateng," she said, and her voice sounded calm even to herself, which surprised her, "that the tide comes twice a day whether he likes it or not, and so will I."

A pause. Then, softly, almost kindly, which was worse: *"Your father said things like that."*

He killed the transmission.

Tamsin hung in the ruined corridor of a dead ship and breathed, four counts in, six counts out, until her heart gave the water nothing to read. Then she followed her strongbox up.

* * *

The engine died at 7:40 in the morning, which was, Tamsin would reflect later, exactly the sort of thing engines did when you needed them most and could least afford it.

She'd gotten the strongbox aboard—eighty kilos of iron and mystery, hauled over the gunwale with a block and tackle and a vocabulary her mother would have washed her mouth out for—and she'd been hauling up the anchor when the diesel coughed twice, shuddered through the whole hull like a dying animal, and quit.

She stood in the cockpit with the starter rope in her hand and the sun coming up gold behind her and said, conversationally, to the engine, to the sea, to God, to whoever was taking notes: "No."

She pulled the rope. Nothing.

"No. Not today. Today of all days."

Nothing. Just the smell of unburned diesel and the flat slap of water against the transom.

She popped the cowling and looked at the engine the way a woman looks at a husband who has disappointed her publicly. Fuel line, probably. Or the injector pump she'd been nursing along since March with prayers and electrical tape. The fuel filter was due; the fuel filter had been due since the fuel filter was invented. Everything on the *Adaeze's Daughter* was due. The boat was twenty-six years old, older than she was, inherited along with the debts and the brass compass and the shack on the dock at Ada Foah, and Tamsin loved it with the fierce, exhausted love you feel for the only thing standing between you and the water.

Because the water, in the end, was all she had. And the water did not care.

She got the sail up—she'd rigged a junk rig two seasons ago out of tarpaulin and stubbornness, and every fisherman in the anchorage had laughed, and she had laughed last during the March squall when three boats with fine engines rode at anchor and hers had sailed home—and she brought the *Daughter* around on a close reach, the strongbox lashed amidships like a coffin, and began the long, slow, humiliating crawl back toward the dock.

Halfway there, Silas Drame's voice came over the radio again.

*"Six tides, Okafor."*

"I heard you the first time."

*"Then hear this. There's a survey team camped on the cay north of your site. Government men. They're diving your wreck right now, in broad daylight, with permits."* A pause, and she could hear him smiling, which should not have been possible over radio and yet was. *"Whatever you pulled up this morning—you might want to think about who you'd rather have find it. Them, or us. Because if it's them, you get nothing. If it's us, you get clear. That's not a threat, girl. That's arithmetic."*

She keyed off the radio and did not answer, and the silence felt like the strongest thing she'd done all day.

But her hands, when she noticed them, were shaking on the tiller.

* * *

The dock at Ada Foah smelled of tar, drying fish, and diesel—the smell of her whole life, the smell she'd hated as a child and now found, in exile from everything else, almost like comfort. She brought the *Daughter* in under sail alone, ghosting the last fifty meters while the fishermen watched from their mended nets with the particular pleasure men take in watching a woman do things the hard way, and she tied off without asking anyone's help, because asking was the first payment and she'd learned never to make it.

The strongbox went into her wheelbarrow. The wheelbarrow went up the beach path. The shack—corrugated roof, salt-eaten walls, one room and a workbench that had been her father's and his father's before him—took the box in like it had been expecting it.

She cleaned it the way she cleaned everything: fresh water first, always fresh water, salt is patient and will wait you out. Scrub brush. Vinegar for the worst of the scale. Three hours of work in the heat of the day while the monsoon clouds stacked themselves up over the horizon like furniture being moved in upstairs rooms.

The lock was long dead. A cold chisel and one strike opened it.

Inside: water, of course. Silt. A leather pouch rotted to lace. And wrapped in oilcloth that had somehow, impossibly, held—

Tamsin stopped breathing.

A compass. Brass, green with age, the glass crazed but whole. She lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and turned it in the light, and the world went narrow and ringing.

She knew this compass. She knew its weight, its dents, the way the bezel clicked when you turned it. She had one exactly like it in a tin box under her bed, her father's, the thing he'd been carrying the night the sea took him, the thing the police had returned to her in a plastic evidence bag when she was nineteen, along with his boots and a receipt for fuel.

Two compasses. Identical. Twin instruments, made in the same hand, in the same year, by the look of the serial stamps worn nearly smooth.

Except.

She held her father's compass in her left hand and this one in her right, side by side on the workbench, and her father's needle swung lazily, faithfully, north. This one did not. This needle strained seaward, quivering against the card like a dog at the end of a chain, pointing not north but out—out past the bar, out past the anchorage, out toward the deep water where a dead ship with a half-erased name lay growing coral in the dark.

*The Meridian,* her father had whispered, the last time she'd seen him alive, drunk for once, holding her wrist too hard on the dock in the rain. *If you ever hear that word, Tamsin—if it ever comes out of anyone's mouth—you run. You swim away from it. Promise me.* And she had promised, because he was her father and he was frightened, and she had spent nine years telling herself the drink had put the fear in him.

The needle pointed seaward. Steady. Certain. Like it had been waiting.

Tamsin sat down slowly on her father's stool, in her father's shack, with her father's debts humming in her ears like flies, and looked at the two brass compasses lying side by side in the lamplight, and understood—with the weary, defiant clarity of a woman who owns almost nothing except the sea's attention—that the past had not drowned with him after all.

It had been waiting underwater. And now it had her address.

Outside, the first thunder of the season rolled in off the ocean, long and low, counting.
