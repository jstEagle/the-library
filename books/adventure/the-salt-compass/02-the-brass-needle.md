## 2. The Brass Needle

The strongbox came apart the way old things do—reluctantly, and only for someone who knew where to ask.

Tamsin worked on the splintered table outside her shack with the last of the afternoon light going amber across the water. The box sat in a shallow basin of diesel and saltwater, its lock a fused knot of green bronze. She had already tried the keyway twice with picks ground from bicycle spokes. Now she set the point of her dive knife against the hinge pins and tapped, gently, the way her father had taught her to tap a conch loose from rock—not forcing, persuading.

*Patience, Tami. The sea keeps everything. It just wants to know you'll be careful with it.*

She was eleven again for half a second, standing knee-deep in the shallows at Takoradi with a conch too big for her hands. Then she was thirty-one, alone, and the pin slid free with a sound like a sigh.

The first hinge gave. The second fought her, crusted in forty years of coral cement, and she worked it with vinegar from a chipped enamel cup, watching the crust fizz and lift. A fishing boat puttered past the point, its wake slapping the pilings beneath her shack in slow, patient strokes. Somewhere behind her, the debt collector's voice still lived in the radio's memory—*Okafor, three days*—but she had turned the volume to nothing, and silence was a currency she could still afford.

The second pin surrendered at dusk.

She lifted the lid.

Inside: silt, fine as flour, and the ghost-smell of iron gone to rust. A leather pouch rotted to lace. And something round, wrapped in oilcloth that had hardened into a shell around it like a cocoon.

She almost didn't unwrap it. That was the strange part, later—the hesitation, the small animal instinct that said *leave it closed*. But her hands were her father's hands, and they did what they were made for.

The oilcloth peeled away in flakes.

A compass.

Brass cased, two inches across, the glass clouded but whole, the bezel worn smooth in the way metal gets when a thumb worries it over decades. Her breath went out of her slowly. She knew this object. She had known it since before she could read, hanging around her father's neck on a braided cord, swinging as he bent over his tanks, tapping it against his palm when he thought, pressing it into her own hand once—only once—with a grip that hurt.

*"If I don't come up, take it and never let anyone see it."*

She had thought he meant it the way fishermen mean things—superstition, ballast for the soul. She had been nine.

Now she held a twin.

Her fingers shook as she reached inside the shack, under the mattress, into the tin that held everything she owned that mattered: her mother's ring, her certification cards, and the compass she'd pulled from the water the day they buried an empty coffin. She brought it out into the failing light and set the two instruments side by side on the table.

Identical. Not similar—identical. Same maker's stamp worn to a shadow on the reverse. Same hairline scratch across the glass, lower left quadrant, as if both had been dropped on the same stone. Same brass, same age, same dark patina in the engraving around the rim, letters so fine she needed the lantern to read them:

*MERIDIAN — 1 — MERIDIAN — 2.*

Two of a pair. Two halves of something.

And then she saw the needles.

Her father's compass pointed north. It always had; she'd checked it against the harbor beacon a hundred times, a child's game, needle and lighthouse agreeing like old friends. But now, lying on the table beside its twin, it had stopped agreeing. Its needle swung lazily, unhurried, ignoring north entirely—and settled pointing seaward, southwest, out past the point, out toward the deep water where the navy's charts went blank and the restricted buoys bobbed their red warnings.

The new one—the one from the strongbox—pointed the same way. Seaward. Both needles aligned like two fingers indicating the same door.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud made it smaller.

Compasses didn't do this. Magnetism was magnetism; a needle found north or it was broken. Unless—

Unless there was something out there big enough, magnetic enough, to argue with the planet itself. A hull. An engine. A shipload of iron sitting on the bottom since before her grandmother was born.

Or unless these weren't compasses at all, but something wearing a compass's face.

She sat back on the crate that served as her chair and looked at the water going dark beyond the point, and the unease moved through her like cold water through a wetsuit—slow at first, then all at once.

* * *

Her father drowned on a Tuesday in the rains, fourteen years ago, and they never found him.

That was the thing people didn't understand about drowning, Tamsin had learned. The sea didn't give you back. The sea kept. Fishermen spoke of it plainly, without drama: *the water took him*, the way you'd say a bank took a house. Emmanuel Okafor went down off the old wreck grounds with two other divers, and both of them came up, and he didn't, and after three days the search became a memory service and after seven it became a story, and stories are the only coffins the drowned get.

She remembered the last time she saw him alive more clearly than she wanted to. He had been restless that week—she understood restlessness now, recognized it in herself every time the radio crackled with Boateng's name. He'd sold his good regulator to cover a debt—that word again, that inheritance—and was diving with borrowed gear, which was how divers died, everyone knew it, everyone dove anyway.

But it wasn't the gear he talked about that last morning. It was the Meridian.

She had been mending net on the dock, thirteen years old and impatient, and he'd crouched next to her and lowered his voice as though the water itself might overhear.

"You know why they call it the Meridian, Tami?"

"Because of the line on the map."

"Because of the line on the map." He'd smiled, but it hadn't reached anywhere near his eyes. "A meridian is a promise the world makes—everything east of it is one day, everything west is another. That ship carried a fortune across that line and the line took payment." He'd turned the brass compass over in his fingers, thumb worrying the bezel, that same worn motion she would know forever. "There are men who've spent their whole lives looking for her. Rich men. Patient men. Men who would take this—" he'd touched her chin with one finger, lifting her face to his, "—take anything, from anyone, to find her. You remember that. If anyone ever asks you about the Meridian, you say you never heard the word."

"Papa, that's silly—"

"Tamsin." Just her name, once, in a voice she'd never heard him use before and would never hear again. "Promise me."

She'd promised. Children promise things; it costs them nothing, and they think the promise is the same as understanding.

Three days later the water took him.

There had been a man asking questions afterward—a soft-spoken foreigner with good shoes who came to the dock and bought fish he didn't seem to want and asked, casually, whether Emmanuel Okafor had ever spoken of old wrecks, of salvage, of anything he might have left behind. Her mother had sent her inside. When she came back out, the man was gone, and her mother's face was the color of paper, and within the year they had left the coast altogether, and her mother had stopped speaking of the sea as though it were a relative and started speaking of it as though it were a creditor.

Which, Tamsin thought now, in the dark of her shack with two impossible compasses on the table, it was. It always had been. The family had been paying it for generations—she just hadn't known in what coin.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She told herself she was cleaning the compasses, and in fairness she was—freshwater baths, soft cloth, the glass polished until the maker's mark emerged like a face surfacing. But her hands were working while her mind drifted down through fourteen years of sediment, dredging up everything she'd buried with the empty coffin.

The night before his last dive, her father had burned papers in a coffee tin behind the shack. She'd watched from the doorway, thinking it was debts, thinking it was shame. Now she remembered the edges of what he fed to the flames: heavy paper, folded charts, and one sheet that didn't burn clean—a corner of it, curling, with a printed heading she hadn't been able to read then and could read now in memory as clearly as if it lay on the table before her.

*Manifest — S.S. Meridian —*

He had been destroying evidence. Or protecting it. With her father those had always been the same act.

And the whispering—God, the whispering. In the last month of his life he'd taken to talking with men at night, out on the water, engines idling low beyond the point. Once she'd crept close enough to hear fragments: *the survey*, *the navy won't touch it*, *the Englishman says the hold is intact*. And once, a voice she didn't know, cold and amused: *"Half now, half on recovery, Okafor. That was the arrangement."*

Her father had been diving for the Meridian before he died. Not near it. For it. And whatever arrangement he'd made, he'd died inside it—or outside it, or fleeing it—and the sea had obligingly closed the account.

She looked at the compasses. Both needles still pointed seaward, southwest, unwavering, patient as the dead.

"What are you pointing at?" she whispered.

The lantern flame leaned in a draft off the water, and for one irrational moment she felt certain the room had answered.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the sea flat as poured metal. Tamsin stood at the rail of her skiff with both compasses in her cupped palms, holding them level, letting them settle. She had barely slept and her eyes felt scoured, but her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady—it was the one inheritance she'd chosen.

Southwest. Out past the point, past the red restricted buoys, past the edge of the charted world, into the square of ocean where a government survey team had pitched its tents three weeks ago and where, yesterday, she had heard on the harbor radio—half listening, always half listening—the first strange report: contact lost. Team unaccounted for. Vessels advised to avoid the area.

Avoid the area. As if the sea had neighborhoods you could simply decline to visit.

Her father's voice rose in her memory one more time, not the warning this time but something older, gentler, from a morning long before the Meridian ever entered their lives. She couldn't have been six. He'd held her up above the gunwale to look at the water, impossibly blue, impossibly deep, and said: *Everything the world has ever lost is down there, Tami. Everything. That's why it's heavy.*

She had thought he meant treasure.

She turned the compasses over one final time. On the back of the strongbox instrument, beneath the maker's stamp, she had missed something in the bad light—a second engraving, finer than the first, scratched by hand rather than machine, hurried, the letters uneven. Someone had written this in desperation or defiance, maybe both, maybe decades ago, maybe on the night her father burned his papers.

Four words, cut deep into the brass:

*TWO KEYS. ONE HOLD.*

Tamsin stood very still while the tide breathed against the pilings and the sun cleared the headland and lit the needles' shared direction like a fuse.

Then she began loading her gear.
