## 3. Gear With No Tracks

The cay had no name on any chart Tamsin owned. The fishermen called it *Ewu Zọ*—the goat that wandered—and left it alone because nothing grew there but salt-burned scrub and a single crooked palm that leaned seaward like a man listening for a voice under the water. She had anchored off its leeward side a dozen times to wait out weather or eat lunch in peace, and in all those visits she had never seen so much as a footprint in its pale crushed-coral sand.

So when she came around the headland at first light with her engine coughing and her tanks half-full from the morning's dive, and saw smoke rising thin and blue from behind the dune line, she cut the throttle and simply drifted.

Smoke meant people. People on Ewu Zọ meant something had changed.

She let the boat glide, one hand on the tiller, the other shading her eyes. The smoke was too clean for driftwood—too steady, too white. Generator exhaust. And beneath it, faint across the water, came a sound she knew from years of working harbors: the whine of a battery charger, the tick of cooling metal, the small domestic noises of a camp that believed itself permanent.

Tamsin sat with that for a long moment. Then she did what she always did when the world offered her something strange: she got closer, quietly.

* * *

She beached the skiff on the far side of the cay and walked the tide line, boots sinking into sand so fine it hissed. The light was still low and gold, laying long shadows from every piece of wrack—the ribs of a dead pirogue, a tangle of ghost net, the bleached keel of some vessel older than memory. Her father had taught her to read a beach the way other men read newspapers. *Everything that washes up is telling you where it's been,* he'd say, crouching over a length of rope, turning it in his scarred fingers. *Your job is to listen.*

There was nothing washed up here telling her anything. That was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was the path.

It wasn't much of a path—a trampled lane through the scrub, wide enough for two men abreast, beaten flat by repeated passage. It ran from the beach straight inland toward the smoke, and it was swept clean. Not raked—swept by feet, dozens of passages, the way game trails form. But when Tamsin knelt at its edge and looked along it against the low sun, she saw no prints leaving it. None going back to the water. The trail simply began at the beach as if the men who made it had materialized out of the surf.

She stood, wiped her palms on her thighs, and told herself there was an explanation. There was always an explanation. The tide took prints. Wind took prints. Men walked the hardpan. She was a woman who made her living finding things underwater; she knew better than most how easily evidence dissolved.

But her hands were cold, and it wasn't the morning air.

* * *

The camp sat in a shallow bowl behind the dunes, sheltered from the sea breeze, and it was wrong in every particular.

Two green military-style tents, guyed and staked with care. A folding table under a tarp awning, set with instruments—she recognized a theodolite case, a laptop in a padded shell, a stack of waterproof charts held down with stones. Two portable generators, one running, one silent, their fuel cans lined up neat as soldiers. A radio mast lashed to the crooked palm, cable running down into the tent. And food—this was what stopped her, what made the skin crawl up her arms—food laid out on a second table as if the meal had been interrupted mid-bite.

Three mess tins. One still held rice, the grains swollen and gummy, gone cold hours ago. A tin of sardines stood open, oil skinned over. A mug of tea had dried to a dark ring at its bottom. Someone had been eating, and then had not been eating, and had not come back to clear it away, and the gulls—which would have stripped an unguarded camp on this cay within minutes of abandonment, she knew, because the gulls here were bold as tax collectors—had not touched it.

No gull droppings on the tables. No gulls anywhere.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the bowl for a full minute, letting her eyes do the work her breath couldn't. Slowly. Systematically. Left to right, near to far. It was how she surveyed a wreck: don't look for the thing you fear, look for everything else, and the thing you fear will show itself by being absent.

What was absent here was people. What was absent was departure. What was absent was time.

She checked the generator nearest her before she could stop herself—laid her palm flat on its housing, the way she'd check an engine block. Warm. Not warm-from-yesterday warm. Warm like it had been shut down within the hour, maybe two. The fuel cap was finger-tight. A coil of orange extension cord ran from it into the lead tent, and when she ducked inside, following the cord, she found the battery charger ticking softly, three marine batteries hooked in series, all of them holding charge.

Inside the tent: four sleep mats, four kit bags, four sets of boots lined up by the door flap. Government issue, she guessed, though the stencil on the bags had been taped over with plain olive tape. A clothesline strung between tent poles held shirts and coveralls drying, still damp at the seams. On a crate serving as a nightstand lay a paperback novel, spine cracked, a pair of reading glasses folded on top of it, waiting for their owner to come back and pick them up.

Four men. Living here comfortably. Eating, reading, charging batteries, drying their laundry. And then—nothing. No struggle. No note. No tracks leading out past the swept trail. Just four empty sets of boots pointing at the door.

Tamsin backed out of the tent into the light and made herself breathe.

* * *

Her father used to say that invisibility was a skill, same as diving. *Nobody looks at a woman in a patched wetsuit hauling scrap,* he told her once, mending her mask strap with fishing line, his fingers black with tar. *That's your armor, Tamsin. Be nobody, and nobody hunts you.* He had lived by it—dived restricted wrecks for twenty years under everyone's nose, paid his debts in cash, kept his name off every manifest he could. And it hadn't saved him. The sea had taken him anyway, or something wearing the sea's face, and all his careful smallness had amounted to nothing but a daughter who also knew how to disappear.

She had built her whole life in the space where people didn't look. It was cramped, but it was hers. Standing in this abandoned camp, she felt that space contracting—felt the gaze of institutions swinging toward these waters, toward the wreck she'd been quietly working, toward the coastline she thought of as hers in the only way a person with nothing can own anything. Somebody had sent four men here with generators and government tape over their name tags. Somebody would send more.

And whoever they were, they had vanished from a guarded camp without leaving a print, and the gulls wouldn't even land.

She heard the boat before she saw it—an outboard, big engine, coming fast from the northeast, from the direction of the naval station at Port Harcourt-side. No fisherman ran an engine like that at this hour, and no fisherman ran one straight at an uninhabited cay.

Tamsin moved.

* * *

The main tent was the survey tent, and the survey tent was where the work lived. She gave herself ninety seconds. She counted them the way she counted bottom time, because panic and nitrogen were cousins and both killed you when you forgot the clock.

Charts on the folding table, weighted with stones. Bathymetric surveys of the shelf waters—she read depths the way she read faces, and these were good charts, recent, dense with soundings that didn't match anything on her own battered collection. Someone had been mapping this stretch of coast in detail. Why, she couldn't say; there was no pipeline route marked, no channel dredge planned that she'd heard of. The soundings clustered thick around a region ten miles offshore, southeast of the navy's restricted zone, in water deep enough to make her recalibrate what she'd assumed about the area.

A grid overlay lay across the largest chart, drawn in grease pencil, and at its center someone had circled a set of coordinates twice, pressing hard enough to tear the paper slightly. She memorized the numbers the way her father had taught her—chunked into threes, spoken silently, anchored to an image. She tied them to the crooked palm leaning seaward, and they set like wet cement.

Beside the charts, a logbook. Hardbound, khaki cover, water-swollen at one corner as if it had been dropped in a bilge and fished out again. The elastic band that should have held it closed had snapped. It lay open to a page of entries in a small, disciplined hand:

*Day 9. Magnetometer run complete, grid sector 4. Anomaly consistent with ferrous mass, displacement est. 400–600 tons. Position confirmed against historical bearing. Cdr. notified by radio. Response: stand fast, await inspection team.*

Below that, fresher ink, hurried:

*Day 11. Inspection team overdue 36 hrs. Radio checks unanswered on all bands. Okafor says—*

The entry stopped. Not trailed off—stopped, mid-sentence, the pen stroke dragging a short tail across the page as if the writer's hand had been pulled away.

Tamsin read the name again. *Okafor.* Her own name, sitting in a stranger's handwriting on a government logbook on an empty cay, and the world tilted a degree sideways before she steadied it. Coincidence. A common enough name on this coast. A cousin she didn't know. The sea was full of Okafors; her family had worked these waters since before anyone drew a chart of them.

Still, her heart was doing something ugly and slow against her ribs.

The outboard was close now, maybe two minutes out, maybe less. She could hear voices—two, three, official cadence, the clipped boredom of men who expected to find paperwork and not mystery.

Ninety seconds were up. She took the logbook.

It was theft, and she knew it, and she did it anyway, sliding the swollen book inside her dive jacket where it sat against her sternum like a second heart. She was a scavenger; salvage law was murky and she'd operated in the murk her whole life. But this felt different, and she let herself feel it for exactly one breath before she moved—different, heavier, the weight of something that had been waiting for her specifically. Her father's voice again, from somewhere far down: *Everything that washes up is telling you where it's been.*

This hadn't washed up. This had been left. And things left behind on purpose are messages, and messages have addresses.

* * *

She was through the scrub and down onto the tide line as the first boat rounded the headland—a gray-hulled rigid inflatable, navy markings, three men in civilian clothes that fooled no one. She didn't run. Running draws the eye. She walked, unhurried, a diver heading back to her skiff, a woman in a patched wetsuit with a mesh bag of scrap over one shoulder, the most invisible creature on this coast. She could feel their binoculars sweep past her and dismiss her, one beat, two, and she almost laughed at how well the armor still worked—

Then the inflatable's driver stood up in the bow, raised a pair of glasses, and swung them back to her.

Held them there.

Tamsin kept walking. Ten steps to the skiff. Nine. She could hear the engine note change as the boat altered course, lazy, casual, the way a hawk adjusts. Eight. Seven. Her mind ran the options with terrible clarity: they could board her, question her, detain her skiff, and she had a stolen government logbook pressed against her chest and coordinates in her head that she could not unknow.

Six. Five.

The man with the glasses said something to the driver. The boat slowed further, wallowing, its wake rolling toward the beach in long gray lines.

Four.

And then the radio on the inflatable crackled—loud enough to carry across the water, a voice tight with static and urgency—and whatever it said made all three men turn toward each other at once, heads together, the glasses lowering, the world's attention sliding off her like water off wax.

Three. Two. One.

Tamsin stepped into the skiff, pushed off the sand with one boot, and started the engine on the first try, which the old thing almost never did, as if it understood the moment. She ran east, low and fast, until the cay sank behind the haze and her hands stopped shaking enough to steer properly.

Only then did she pull out the logbook and open it on her knee, the wind tearing at the pages, and read the last line again—the one that stopped mid-word, the one with her family's name in it.

*Okafor says—*

Says what? To whom? About what?

The compass in her gear bag, the brass one her father had drowned holding, seemed suddenly to have weight in a way it never had before—as if somewhere out past the horizon, another instrument just like it was waiting, and both of them were pointing at the same silence where four men used to be.
