## 4. The Ledger's Shadow

Tamsin did not sleep. She sat in the shack with the logbook spread open on her table beneath the hiss of a kerosene lamp, and she let the waterlogged pages dry one by one, peeling them apart like the leaves of some drowned plant, each sheet translucent as fish skin, the ink ghosted through it in blue-gray shadows.

The survey team had kept good records. That was the first thing she noticed—the handwriting was disciplined, unhurried, a hydrographer's hand or a navigator's, every entry dated and timed to the minute. *Station 14, 0640 hours, bottom temp 27.1. Station 15, 0715, silt layer thickening.* Whoever wrote it had loved the work. You could tell from the margins, where small sketches bloomed beside the numbers: a profile of the seafloor, a cross-section of an anomaly, a little fish drawn with surprising tenderness next to a note about parrotfish disturbing the sediment.

She read by lamplight while the sea worked at the pilings below her floorboards, patient as a debt collector, and somewhere past midnight she reached the page that stopped her breath.

Coordinates.

Six figures, latitude and longitude, written twice—once crossed out, once corrected—and beneath them, in that same careful hand: *Anomaly confirmed. Iron mass consistent with hull displacement. Depth 61m. Recommend re-survey at slack tide.*

Her hands were not steady. She set the lamp closer anyway.

She knew those numbers. Not the way you know a stranger's face, but the way you know your own name written in someone else's hand. For three nights now she had balanced her father's compass on the sill of the shack window and watched its needle swing off north, stubborn and seaward, pointing at nothing any chart could name. And two nights after that, the second compass—the twin from the strongbox—had done the same thing, but swung *differently*, canted a few degrees east of her father's, as if the two needles were two fingers reaching for the same object from opposite sides of a dark room.

She had laid them side by side on the table, brass to brass, and extended their bearings in pencil across her father's old chart until the lines crossed.

The crossing point had meant nothing to her then. A patch of open water forty miles down-coast, unremarkable, unmarked, deeper than her usual grounds. She had stared at it a long time and told herself it was coincidence, that compasses fail, that her father's instruments were old and lying the way old things lie.

But here it was again. The same water. Written down by a government survey team days before they vanished into air.

*Recommend re-survey at slack tide.*

There was no re-survey. There was no entry after this one. The logbook simply continued for another page—routine soundings, weather notes, a grocery list half-finished in different ink—and then stopped, mid-sentence, as if the pen had been set down and never picked up again.

*Half-finished.* Tamsin sat back. Her tea had gone cold in its tin mug and she drank it anyway, tasting salt and tannin and something like iron. On the cay, the meals had been half-eaten too. The generators warm. No footprints leading out. Everything about that camp had been interrupted rather than ended, as if the men themselves had been lifted off the sand like objects from a table.

And now their logbook sat drying on her table, and it pointed at the same empty square of ocean her father's compasses pointed at, and the navy had sealed the whole stretch of coast within a day of the disappearance.

Coincidence was a luxury. She'd never been able to afford it.

Outside, dawn came up gray-green over the water, and with it came the sound she'd been waiting for without admitting she was waiting: an engine, low and official, not the ragged stutter of a fishing boat but the smooth diesel purr of something that cost more than her whole life stacked end to end.

A gray hull rounded the breakwater. Navy white above the waterline. On her stern, letters she could read even at this distance: *NS Sagbama.*

They had come for her. Of course they had come for her. She had been the first on that cay; the fishermen would have talked; invisibility had always been her armor and it was failing exactly when she needed it most.

Tamsin closed the logbook, wrapped it in oilcloth, and pushed it deep under the floorboard where her father used to hide his dive money. Then she put the kettle on, because there was no sense meeting power thirsty.

* * *

Commander Reyes came ashore at eight o'clock sharp, which told her he was the kind of man who believed time belonged to him personally. He was younger than she expected—forty maybe, close-cropped, with the neatness of a man who ironed his own shirts—and he climbed the ladder from the launch with a competence that surprised her. Sailors who can't climb look wrong coming up ladders. He looked right.

Two enlisted men stayed with the boat. He came alone to her shack, ducked under the lintel without being asked, and stood a moment taking inventory with his eyes. The dive gear racked by the door. The strongbox, still open, still holding nothing but rust scale and the smell of the deep. The two compasses on the windowsill, which she had not thought to hide, because hiding them would have been worse than showing them.

"You're Okafor," he said. "Tamsin Okafor."

"Everyone out here is somebody," she said. "You'll have to be more specific."

"The salvage diver." He said it without contempt, which was almost worse. Contempt she could fight. "You found the camp on Ilashe Cay."

"I found a camp on Ilashe Cay. Whether anyone was camped in it is another question."

He took that without blinking. "May I sit?"

"It's my only chair."

He sat anyway, perching on it like a man visiting a museum exhibit of poverty, and accepted the tea she poured because refusing would have been its own kind of statement. He held the mug without drinking. Steam curled between them.

"When did you last see the survey team?" he asked.

"I didn't. I saw their camp."

"Describe it."

"Generators warm. Kettle warm. Food half-eaten—rice, still soft, flies just starting. Six bedrolls, six kits, boots left behind like they'd gone swimming." She paused. "No footprints leaving. Sand holds a print out there for days; I've read beaches my whole life. Whatever went off that cay didn't walk."

"And you reported this immediately."

"I reported it when the harbormaster's radio finally answered, which was four hours later, which is not the same thing."

Reyes set the mug down. "Where were you in that four hours?"

"Diving."

"Where?"

"Out here, the sea doesn't file flight plans, Commander."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, she thought, but recalculation. He was revising her. She could feel herself being moved from one column of his ledger to another, and she understood suddenly that this was what power looked like up close: not violence, not yet, but bookkeeping. Men sorted into assets and liabilities, and the sorting done politely, over tea.

"You were diving in restricted waters," he said.

"Was I? My charts don't show restrictions. My charts are older than your navy's interest in this coastline."

"Your charts are older than a lot of things." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the officer fell away and she saw the man underneath, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Six of my country's best hydrographers are missing, Ms. Okafor. I've spent nine days dragging the water for them. So I'm going to ask you plainly, and I'd take it as a courtesy if you answered plainly. Did you take anything from that camp?"

The floorboard under the table seemed to warm against her awareness, the way a bruise warms. She thought of the logbook wrapped in oilcloth eighteen inches beneath his boot heel.

"No," she said.

It was the first lie she had ever told a uniformed man, and it came out smooth as silt, and she hated how easily it came, the way you hate discovering a door in yourself you didn't know was there.

Reyes studied her for a long moment. Then he did something unexpected: he laughed, softly, without humor, and rubbed his jaw.

"You're good," he said. "Better than the fishermen. They fold in ten seconds. But I'm going to tell you something, Ms. Okafor, because you're going to hear it eventually and I'd rather you heard it from me." He turned the mug a slow quarter-turn on the table, watching the ring of damp it left. "My team wasn't mapping channels. The channel survey was cover. They were looking for something specific, and they found it, and three days later they were gone."

Tamsin kept her face still. It was the hardest work she'd ever done, harder than any dive.

"What kind of something?"

"Documents." He said the word carefully, the way you handle something with edges. "Paper. From a wreck. Old paper." His eyes came up to hers. "The kind of documents better left drowned."

The room was very quiet. Below the floorboards the sea kept up its patient arithmetic against the pilings.

"There's no wreck out there," she said. "That water's been fished and dived for sixty years."

"Not at sixty-one meters it hasn't. Not by anyone with reason to keep quiet about what they found." He stood, smoothing his trousers, and the officer came back over him like a tide. "Here's what happens now. This coast stays closed. Your boat stays moored. If you go out—if anyone goes out—they'll be boarded, and boarded boats get confiscated, and confiscated divers get charged under emergency maritime law, which is not a pleasant document. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly."

"For what it's worth—" he paused at the door, ducking his head under the lintel, and the morning light caught him silver-edged, "—I lost friends on that cay. Whatever's down there, whatever paper it is, whoever wants it buried—I'd burn the whole thing myself if they'd let me. Some inheritances are better refused."

Then he was gone, down the ladder, and the launch pulled away in a long white seam across the green water, and Tamsin stood alone in her shack with her heart going hard and slow, like a diver's heart at depth.

*Some inheritances are better refused.*

He knew. Not everything—but enough. He knew there was a wreck, and he knew it carried paper, and he knew the navy wanted that paper silent, and he had come all this way to tell her so while officially telling her nothing at all. Which meant Commander Reyes was not simply the navy's hand. He was a man at war inside his own uniform, and men like that were dangerous in ways she hadn't learned to calculate yet.

She knelt and drew up the floorboard. The oilcloth bundle was cool and damp against her palms, and when she unwrapped it, the logbook fell open to the corrected coordinates, the careful hand, the words *anomaly confirmed*.

Sixty-one meters. Beyond recreational depth. Beyond most salvage. Deep enough that only someone desperate—or someone with nothing left to lose—would go down.

She laid her father's compass on the open page, and the second compass beside it, and watched the two needles tremble on their bearings like fingers pointing at the same dark room.

The navy wanted it drowned. Boateng would want it sold. And somewhere between those two hung a square of empty ocean holding a ship, a fortune, and a list of names—one of which, though she couldn't yet know it, was her own.

Tamsin wrapped the logbook back in its oilcloth, and began, quietly, to pack her dive gear.

* * *

By noon the harbor radio was crackling with the navy's closure order, read in that flat official voice that turns the sea into somebody's property with one sentence. Tamsin listened from the cockpit of her boat with the engine cover off and her hands in the guts of the thing, and she heard the fishermen grumbling on adjacent decks, and she heard, threaded beneath it all, a voice she knew too well.

Eze Boateng, on the fleet channel, smooth as bilge oil: *"Restrictions are for small boats, brothers. Big water belongs to whoever can hold it. Stand by for instructions."*

Tamsin wiped her hands on a rag and looked south, down the coast, toward the empty square of ocean where two compass needles crossed.

Three days, she estimated. Maybe four before the navy's patrol pattern thinned, before Boateng's patience ran out, before the monsoon's first outriders bent the palms along the shore. Three days to find a wreck at sixty-one meters that six trained men had died finding.

She opened the throttle and her dead engine clicked its dry, useless answer, and she remembered the debt collector's voice on the radio, and she understood that the sea was not the only thing that answered to her anymore.

Everything else wanted a price.
