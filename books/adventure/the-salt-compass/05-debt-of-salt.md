## 5. Debt of Salt

The message came at slack tide, when the water between the islands went glass-still and honest, and nothing in Tamsin's world was either.

*Boateng wants to see you. Tomorrow. His office.* The harbormaster's boy delivered it on a scrap of paper he wouldn't meet her eyes to hand over, which told her everything about how the invitation had been received down at the fuel dock. Eze Boateng did not send invitations. He sent invoices with pleasantries attached.

She spent the evening in her shack doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for three months. The loan: two hundred and forty thousand naira principal, rolled twice, each roll adding interest like silt settling on a hull. The compressor she'd pawned against it. The *Ifeoma*, her boat, technically his until the last payment cleared—collateral, that word written into the agreement in Boateng's own careful hand, because he believed a name on paper was an anchor and anchors were for holding things down.

What she had against all of it: one corroded strongbox, its lock split open by salt and time; a brass compass whose needle swung seaward no matter how she turned it; and a secret sitting four fathoms deep inside her chest where even she was afraid to look at it directly.

She set the compasses side by side on the table again, the way she had every night since the strongbox. Her father's, worn smooth at the bezel from thirty years of a thumb worrying it. The new one, crusted green at the hinge but sound beneath, its crystal unscratched after decades underwater as if the sea had been keeping it rather than drowning it. Both needles pointed the same direction—not north, never north—but not quite the same bearing. A few degrees apart. Two lines drawn from two points, converging somewhere out past the navy's charts.

Two lines make a crossing. She'd learned that before she'd learned long division, hunched over her father's charts while other girls were learning braids.

She wrapped both instruments in oilcloth and put them in the bottom of her tool bag, under the wrenches, because tomorrow she would walk into Eze Boateng's office and the first rule of diving applied to conversation too: never show what you're carrying until you know the depth of the water.

* * *

Boateng's office sat above his salvage yard like a bridge above an engine room, all glass and observation. From his desk he could watch his cranes, his barges, his divers queuing at the air station—and, this morning, a woman walking up his pier with a tool bag and a spine like a keel.

Tamsin had been here before, but never invited. Debtors came up the outside stairs; she noted that the railings were freshly painted, that someone had swept the salt off the landings. He wanted this meeting clean. That frightened her more than dirt would have.

He rose when she entered, which he never did, and poured coffee from a pot that had clearly been waiting, which meant he'd timed her arrival off the tide tables or off some private clock in his head that ran on other people's needs.

"Okafor." He said her surname the way he said everything—like he was appraising it. "Sit. You look like you slept on your boat."

"I slept on my boat."

"Good. Honest people sleep on their boats. Liars sleep in hotels." He smiled, showing teeth that had closed many deals. "Coffee?"

"Black."

"Of course." He poured, sat, folded his hands. For a while he let the silence do his work for him. Through the window behind him, a crane swung a recovered engine block across the yard, dripping seawater in bright ropes. Tamsin watched it instead of watching him. Watching the watcher was a losing trade.

"You've been diving the eastern grounds," he said at last.

"I dive where I dive."

"The eastern grounds are restricted."

"So is the whole coast, depending on who's asking and what day it is."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger. Interest. She'd seen that look on men at auction when a lot came up mislabeled. "You know," he said, leaning back, "when your father used to sit in that chair, he'd do the same thing. Look out the window. Like the answer was going to swim past."

Tamsin kept her face still, but something moved in her chest, old and cold. "You knew my father?"

"Everyone knew Sunday Okafor. Best diver on this coast and the worst businessman God ever made an enemy of." He sipped his coffee. "He borrowed from me too, near the end. Never repaid. Died owing me eleven thousand naira and a favor."

"He died owing nobody," Tamsin said. "He died owing the sea, and the sea collected."

For the first time, Boateng laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of him. "There it is. That mouth. He had it too." He set down his cup and the humor drained out of his face like water through sand, leaving business. "Here's why you're here, Okafor. Your debt stands at three hundred and twelve thousand. I could call it today. Take the boat, take the compressor, take whatever's left of your gear. You'd be walking the beach within the week, selling dives to tourists who tip in cedis."

"And instead?"

"Instead, I'm offering you a partnership." He let the word sit there, gilded. "You've been working waters nobody works. Restricted waters. And last week—" he opened a drawer, unhurried, theatrical, "—my fuel man says you came in with a strongbox strapped to your deck. Corroded iron. Old. Navy-pattern."

Tamsin's blood went quiet and slow, the way it did at depth. *The fuel man.* Of course. Nothing moved on this coast without passing through one of Boateng's hands eventually. She'd known that. She'd been careless anyway, tired anyway, and tired was just another word for careless with better excuses.

"A box," she said. "Empty. Salt-eaten. I sold it to the scrap dealer for the iron."

"Mm." He studied her the way he studied weather. "And the navy's new survey team, the one that vanished off the maps last month—you weren't anywhere near their camp, I suppose."

"I heard they went missing."

"People go missing around you lately, Okafor. Wrecks appear that aren't on charts. Boxes surface." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost gentle. "I'm not accusing. I'm *investing*. That's the difference between me and Commander Reyes and his gunboats. He wants to own what you find. I only want a share."

"What kind of share?"

"Fifty-fifty. Anything you recover from the restricted zone comes to my yard first. We assess together, sell together, split together. In exchange—" he slid a single sheet of paper across the desk, already typed, already signed by him, waiting only for her name, "—the debt dies. Today. Notarized. Your boat becomes yours free and clear, and you get my fleet behind you: compressors, lift bags, a tender when you need one. You'd be diving with the best equipment on this coast instead of praying to a forty-year-old regulator."

Tamsin looked at the paper without touching it. Fifty-fifty sounded generous. It was meant to. But she'd grown up reading contracts the way other children read adventure stories, and she knew that fifty-fifty was a number men offered when they already knew the total would be enormous—or when they didn't know yet and wanted first refusal on finding out. Either way, the paper wasn't the trap. The paper was the bait. The trap was the word *anything*, and the zone it covered, and the fact that once her name was on it, every dive she ever made belonged half to him, forever, including the ones she hadn't planned yet.

Including, possibly, a wreck called the Meridian.

"If I sign," she said carefully, "it covers what I've found. Not what I might find."

"It covers the zone. Everything in the zone."

"The zone is a line some bureaucrat drew on a chart. Water doesn't recognize it."

"Water doesn't pay debts either." He said it mildly, without threat, which made it worse. Then he leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost confiding, almost kind—the register he saved for the moment before closing. "Tamsin. I'm going to tell you something I don't tell many people. I started with one canoe and a borrowed mask. Forty years. Every naira I have, I pulled up out of that water with these hands." He held them out—scarred, square-nailed, a diver's hands gone soft with money. "I respect what you are. There aren't ten people on this coast who can do what you do, and half of them work for me already. I don't want to break you. Breaking costs money. I want to *build* you. But building also costs money, and I've already spent a great deal on you, and a man can only spend so long before he starts expecting returns."

The crane outside swung another load across the sky. Somewhere below, a diver's compressor coughed to life, rhythmic as a heart.

"I'll think about it," Tamsin said.

"Think fast. The monsoon's coming early this year—harbormaster says three weeks, maybe less. After landfall, nobody dives those grounds till October." He picked up the contract, squared its edges, and placed it back in the drawer with the tenderness of a man putting away a weapon he expected to use soon. "But take this with you." He wrote something on a card and held it out. Not the contract. An account number. "Whatever you bring up next—box, brass, paper, gold—I'll pay fair weight, cash, no questions, no paperwork. Consider it a trial run. See how honest we can be with each other before we complicate things with signatures."

A trial run. Cash, no paperwork—which meant no record, no protection, no proof she'd ever brought anything in at all. Which meant that anything she handed over would simply become his, laundered through generosity. She understood now. The contract was for fools. The card was for her.

She took the card, because refusing it would tell him there was something worth hiding, and took her bag, and stood.

"At the door," Boateng said, and she stopped. "Your father, near the end—he came to me asking for money for one last expedition. Wouldn't say where. Wouldn't say what for. Just kept saying the same strange thing." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "He said, *Eze, there's a ship down there that carries the names of everyone who ever got rich off this coast, and none of them want it found.*" He looked up at her, and his eyes were patient and bottomless. "Funny thing to say, isn't it? Unless it's true."

Tamsin held his gaze the way she held position in current—expending exactly enough effort, no more, revealing nothing. "My father talked a lot when he drank."

"Yes," Boateng agreed pleasantly. "So do I. Good morning, Okafor. Mind the stairs—they're freshly painted."

* * *

Eze Boateng watched her go from the window, the way he watched everything he intended to own.

She walked his pier like it was neutral ground, which amused him. Nothing on this coast was neutral. The water itself belonged to whoever owned the boats on it, and the boats belonged to whoever financed them, and the financing belonged to him, and above him there were only ministers and admirals, and those were just men who hadn't yet needed him enough to know it.

He returned to his desk and opened the ledger he kept in his head, the real one, the one no auditor would ever see. *Okafor, Tamsin. Asset class: skill, scarce. Liability: debt, 312k, secured against vessel. Temperament: hostile to ownership. Father: Sunday Okafor, deceased, diver, debtor, dreamer.* He had liked Sunday, in the way he liked all raw material. The man had been the finest diver on the coast and had died with nothing because he'd believed the sea was a neighbor instead of a warehouse. Sentiment. The most expensive substance on earth.

But the daughter had come back from restricted water with a navy-pattern strongbox, and the navy had lost a survey team in the same waters, and Sunday Okafor had spent his last sober year muttering about a ship full of names. Boateng had spent forty years converting the world into columns, and he knew a high-value entry when one surfaced.

He pressed the intercom. "Get me Adjei."

His operations man appeared in ninety seconds, still damp from the yard. "Boss."

"The Okafor woman. I want her followed—not obviously. A fisherman's skiff, something she won't clock. Wherever she goes, whatever cay she stops at, whatever water she drops into. Coordinates, times, depth if you can get it."

"And if she leads us somewhere interesting?"

"Then we'll already know about it before she knows we know." He looked back out at the harbor, at the grey-green water lying flat and innocent over everything it held. "One more thing. The navy's survey team. Find out what happened to them. Quietly. If any of them are alive—if any of them *talked*—I want to know what they said and to whom."

Adjei nodded and went. Boateng sat a while longer, turning his cup on its saucer, thinking about names.

*A ship that carries the names of everyone who ever got rich off this coast.*

If it was true, then the ship carried his name too, or would have, had his grandfather been worth recording. Boateng had built himself from nothing precisely because his family had never been rich enough to be guilty of anything. There was a freedom in that. There was also, he understood now, a fortune in the opposite.

Gold was heavy and traceable and loud. Names were lighter than water and cut deeper than any blade. A ledger naming the founding families of this nation would be worth more than the gold it lay beside—worth more to the families who wanted it drowned, worth more to the ones who wanted it burned, and worth a king's ransom to anyone clever enough to hold it without opening it.

He smiled at the window, at the flat sea, at the small figure of Tamsin Okafor receding down the quay with her tool bag and her unbought soul.

She thought she'd left his office without giving him anything. She'd given him everything. The set of her shoulders when he mentioned the strongbox. The half-second pause before *empty*. The way she'd looked at the account card like it was a hook she could see the barb of. Greed he could manage; fear he could buy; but a woman protecting something specific—that was a treasure map drawn in flesh.

Three weeks until the monsoon, the harbormaster said. Three weeks of divable water.

Plenty of time, Boateng thought, for a debt to come due.
