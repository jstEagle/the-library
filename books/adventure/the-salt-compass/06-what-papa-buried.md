## 6. What Papa Buried

The bus from the harbor left Tamsin with salt in her hair and diesel on her hands, and Lagos received her the way it received everything—loudly, without apology, as if the whole city were a market that never closed. She pressed herself against the window of a danfo crammed with passengers and produce, watching the water disappear behind warehouses and billboards, feeling the particular loneliness of a diver too long out of it. Her lungs still held the memory of depth. Her ears popped at nothing.

Aunt Ifeoma lived in Surulere, in a house that had once been yellow and was now the color of old teeth, behind a wall of bougainvillea gone feral. Tamsin had not been there in four years—not since the funeral, not since she'd stood in this same courtyard while men she didn't know said words about her father that made him sound like a saint or a fool, depending on who was speaking. She had sworn she wouldn't come back until she had something to show for herself. She had nothing now either, except two compasses and a strongbox full of questions.

She knocked because the gate bell hadn't worked since before Papa died.

Ifeoma opened the door already dressed for church even though it was Tuesday, her wrapper the deep indigo of evening water, her face arranged into the expression of a woman who had expected this visit for years and would not give Tamsin the satisfaction of admitting it.

"You look like your father," she said. "Come in before the neighbors see you."

* * *

The house smelled the way it always had: camphor and dried fish and the ghost of incense, the smell of a woman who kept things against time's advice. The parlor was dim and crowded with photographs—Tamsin's grandmother in a headwrap like a crown, uncles whose names she had to guess at, and there, above the cabinet, the largest frame: Papa at twenty-five, standing on the deck of a survey vessel, young and lean and grinning like the sea belonged to him.

Tamsin had looked at that photograph a hundred times as a child. She looked at it differently now.

"Who took this?" she asked.

Ifeoma came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. For a long moment neither of them spoke. A radio somewhere down the street played highlife, tinny and bright, music for people whose fathers had not drowned.

"The government ship," Ifeoma said finally. "Nigeria's own survey vessel, back when we first had one. Nineteen sixty-three. Your father was nineteen years old and the proudest diver on that coast." She touched the frame's edge with one finger, careful, the way you touch something that might still be alive. "He used to say he could hold his breath longer than any man aboard, and the white engineers laughed at him until they timed him."

"Nineteen sixty-three," Tamsin repeated. The year sat strangely in her mouth. "What were they surveying?"

Ifeoma turned away from the photograph. She went to the cabinet, poured two glasses of palm wine from the jug she kept despite the doctor's warnings, and handed one to Tamsin without asking if she wanted it. In this house you drank what was given.

"Sit down, Tamsin."

"I'm sitting."

"On the chair. Not on the edge of it."

Tamsin sat. The wine was sweet and slightly sharp, tasting of fermentation and patience. Outside, a generator coughed to life three houses down, and the light flickered.

* * *

"They were looking for the Meridian," Ifeoma said.

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Tamsin set down her glass so her aunt wouldn't see her hand tremble.

"Papa talked about it," she said carefully. "At the end. When he was drinking. He'd say the name like it was a person who owed him money. I thought it was a story. A fever dream."

"It was no dream." Ifeoma lowered herself into the chair opposite, arranging her wrapper with the deliberation of a woman buying time. "It was a slave ship, child. British registry, though she sailed under whatever flag suited her route. She went down off our coast in seventeen ninety-something with a cargo of gold—the profits of a season's trading—and papers. Ledgers. Lists of names. Every merchant house that bought and sold along this coast, every family that grew fat on the trade, all written down in ink by men who never imagined the sea would keep their receipts."

"And the government wanted it."

"The government wanted the gold." Ifeoma's voice flattened. "The papers were an embarrassment. Do you understand? It was 1963. We had just taken the flag from the British, and here was a document proving that some of the very families now writing our new constitution had built their houses on the trade. Names going back generations. Names that were in the government itself." She sipped her wine. "The search was supposed to be secret. It was not secret. Nothing in this country is secret; it is only expensive."

Tamsin felt the compasses in her bag like two heartbeats. "Papa was on the dive team."

"He was the best of them. He found the wreck first—or near enough. The sonar was crude then, the charts worse. They searched for two years, and it was your father who went down through the thermocline where the other divers refused, who came up with coral in his gloves and a fix on the hull." She paused. "And it was your father who understood what the mission really was."

"Which was?"

"To raise the gold quietly and lose the papers loudly." Ifeoma met her eyes. "There was a man on the expedition—a minister's man, a fixer—who had instructions. The gold would come up in crates marked as salvage for the museum. The documents would be 'damaged beyond recovery' in the process. Accidents happen underwater, Tamsin. Everyone knows this. Paper does not survive salt."

The room seemed smaller. Tamsin thought of Commander Reyes saying *documents better left drowned*, and cold walked up her spine. Sixty years, and the sentence hadn't changed. Only the uniforms had.

"What did Papa do?"

"He stole one of the compasses."

* * *

Ifeoma rose and went to the cabinet, the same one beneath Papa's photograph, and opened the bottom drawer with a small brass key she wore on a cord around her neck. From among folded cloths she brought out a bundle of letters tied with string, and a single photograph, creased nearly in half.

In it, five men stood around a table on which lay instruments—sextants, charts, and two compasses side by side, their cases catching the flashbulb's light. Papa stood at the end, youngest, his hand resting on the table not far from the instruments. His grin in the photograph was the grin of a man who has already decided something no one else knows.

"There were two compasses," Ifeoma said. "Not ordinary ones. German-made, commissioned special for the expedition by the ministry. Each needle balanced on a jeweled pivot, each case engraved with the same star. Individually, they pointed north like any compass, more or less. But together—" she drew a line in the air with one finger, crossing it with another— "together they made a key. You take a bearing from one position with the first, a bearing from another position with the second, and where the lines cross, there is the hold. Not the wreck—the hold. The one compartment the designers sealed with iron and lead, the one place on that ship built to keep its secrets even from the sea."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "The strongbox. The one I pulled up last week—it had a compass inside. Identical to Papa's. Same engraving."

Ifeoma closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer or exhaustion. "Then both keys have come home," she said softly. "After sixty years."

"How did the second one get in a strongbox on a navy-restricted wreck?"

"That," said her aunt, "is the part I know least, and what I know I don't fully believe. Sit. Drink. This is a long story and you will want the wine."

* * *

"In sixty-five, the expedition found her," Ifeoma began. "Or believed they had. The fixer—the minister's man—came aboard the survey vessel personally to oversee the recovery. There was a plan, and the plan required the location to be recorded in a way that could be controlled. So they split the key. One compass stayed with the expedition records. The second was placed in a sealed strongbox, and the strongbox was hidden—dropped at a decoy site, a false wreck, miles from the true one. Insurance, they called it. If anyone ever found the Meridian without authorization, they would find nothing but an empty hull, because the true coordinates existed only where the two compasses could meet."

"And Papa?"

"Your father was chosen to place the box. He was the only diver who could work that deep without trembling." Ifeoma's voice dropped. "But your father had read the manifest. He knew what was in the hold besides gold. And he had watched the fixer burn the first copies of the survey notes in a steel drum on deck, telling everyone it was contaminated material. Your father understood that when the gold came up, the truth would stay down forever, and the families in that ledger would sleep soundly for another century."

"So he took the compass from the records."

"He took the compass from the records and he lied. He told them he'd lost it overboard during the placement dive. There was shouting. There was an inquiry. Nothing could be proven, and a nineteen-year-old diver from the creeks was not worth pursuing when the gold itself was waiting. They re-ran the bearings with what instruments remained—but the fixer's method needed both keys, and the numbers they reconstructed were wrong. Or your father let them be wrong; he was clever with figures even then. The recovery failed. The monsoon came early that year, and then politics came, and then war came, and the Meridian sank back into being a rumor." She spread her hands. "Your father kept the compass. He kept the secret. And it ate him, Tamsin. All those years, it ate him. He was waiting—for the country to be ready for the truth, for someone honest to come looking, for courage he wasn't sure he had. Meanwhile he raised children and repaired boats and drank, and sometimes at night he would sit on the dock and hold that compass and talk to the water."

Tamsin remembered. She had been small; she'd thought he was praying.

"When he drowned," she said. Her voice came out strange, flat, like a sounding line hitting bottom. "Was it an accident?"

Ifeoma was quiet for a long time. The generator down the street died, and in the new silence the house creaked like a hull.

"I don't know," she said at last. "I have asked myself that question for eleven years, and I will tell you honestly: I don't know. He was the finest diver on this coast. Men don't drown doing what they were born to. But he was also a man carrying sixty years of someone else's sin, and he had begun, near the end, to talk about finishing what he started. Going down himself. Bringing up the ledger." She set down her glass. "Three weeks after he told me that, he was dead. Draw what conclusion you can live with. I have drawn mine and redrawn it a thousand times."

* * *

Tamsin stood and walked to the window. The courtyard blazed with bougainvillea; a lizard did push-ups on the wall; the world continued being ordinary, which felt like an insult.

All her life she had carried her father's death like ballast—the debt, the drowning, the compass he left her that pointed stubbornly, uselessly seaward, the whisper of *Meridian* in his drunk and dying voice. She had thought she inherited his failures. His bad luck. His debts, literally, in Boateng's ledger of interest and threats.

But that wasn't the inheritance at all.

He hadn't left her a keepsake. He'd left her a post. A watch that had stood unmanned for eleven years, waiting for someone with his lungs and his stubbornness to pick it up. He had stolen the truth from powerful men and hidden it in the only vault he trusted—the sea—and then he had trusted her with the key without ever telling her, because telling her would have made her a target, and keeping silent had made him a lonely man, and he had chosen her safety over his own peace, and drowned anyway, maybe, for it.

Grief turned over inside her like a hull righting itself.

"Auntie," she said. "The compasses. Show me how they work together."

* * *

Ifeoma brought out paper, a ruler, a pencil worn to a stub. On the kitchen table, under the bare bulb, she drew what her brother had shown her once, decades ago, when he was drunk and weeping and she was the only one who listened.

"Each compass is cut with a second scale, hidden under the bezel ring. See—here, lift the ring gently, it hinges." Tamsin lifted it on her father's compass and found the fine inner graduations, numbers that matched no navigation she knew. "You stand at a known point—one of the old survey benchmarks, they're still marked on the coastal charts—and you take the bearing the needle gives you on the hidden scale. Then you go to the second benchmark, far away, and do the same with the other compass. Two lines. Where they cross—" Ifeoma tapped the pencil twice on the paper— "the hold. Not the charts' wreck site. The charts are wrong, deliberately wrong; the navy has been guarding a lie for sixty years. That is why your survey team vanished, if vanish is truly the word. They were looking in the wrong place, or they were looking in the right place and found something the wrong people feared."

Tamsin stared at the two compasses lying side by side on the table, twin stars engraved on their cases, needles trembling toward each other like the hands of lovers across a table.

Two keys. One lock. Sixty years of silence.

"Papa," she whispered. "You stubborn, frightened, magnificent man."

Ifeoma put her hand over Tamsin's, and her grip was stronger than her age suggested.

"Listen to me now, and listen well. The men who wanted those papers drowned in 1965 are grandfathers now, and their children run this country. If they learn both keys exist, they will not send polite inquiries. Your father spent his whole life paying for one act of courage. Don't spend yours the same way—go carefully, go quietly, and come back to me."

Tamsin gathered the compasses, wrapped them in cloth, and tucked them against her body where they beat faintly against her ribs like a second pulse.

Outside, the afternoon had turned the color of hammered brass, and far to the southwest, over the water, a smudge of cloud was building itself into a tower. The harbormaster's radio, if she'd been listening, would have said what old divers always said at this hour: the barometer is falling, the season is turning, the sea is about to ask its question.

Tamsin flagged a danfo at the corner. As it pulled away she looked back once at the yellow-brown house, at her aunt standing in the doorway with her hand raised—blessing or warning, impossible to say.

By nightfall she was at the docks, charting benchmark positions by lamplight, two lines converging in her mind toward a point miles from anything the navy guarded.

Miles from where the fleet was looking.

Miles from where, in three days, Eze Boateng's armada would weigh anchor chasing a lie—unless she reached the truth first.
