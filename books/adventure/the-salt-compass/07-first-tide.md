## 7. First Tide

The barometer had been falling since midnight, and Tamsin Okafor read it the way other people read faces—looking for the lie in it.

She stood at the rail of her boat, the *Small Mercy*, a twenty-two-foot workhorse with a patched hull and an engine that had died once already this week, and she watched the eastern horizon assemble itself out of cloud. The sky over the Gulf was the color of old pewter, bruised at the seams, and the water beneath her lay flat as poured lead, holding its breath. Fishermen called this kind of calm *the sea clearing its throat*. Her father had called it something else. He'd said, the last season he worked these waters: *When the sea goes quiet like that, girl, it's not resting. It's deciding.*

Tamsin decided too.

The two compasses sat on the chart table below, side by side in the weak green light of her cabin lamp, and she had spent half the night watching them the way you watch a scorpion you've cornered in a room—with respect, and with the understanding that one wrong move would cost you.

Her father's compass she knew by heart. The brass case worn smooth at the hinge where his thumb had worried it for thirty years, the glass scratched by salt grit, the needle that pointed north the way any honest needle should. She'd carried it since she was nineteen, since the morning they pulled what the sea left of him out of the shallows off Cape Three Points and handed her his effects in a plastic bag: a knife, a rosary he never used, and the compass. She'd assumed it was sentiment. A dead man's keepsake passed to a daughter who needed something to hold.

Then the strongbox from the restricted wreck had come up in her hands three days ago, and inside its corroded heart, wrapped in oilcloth older than her grandmother, lay the second compass. Identical brass. Identical wear pattern along the left edge, as if the same hand had held them both. But its needle did not point north.

It pointed seaward. Always seaward. She had turned it through every cardinal direction on her chart table and the needle swung back, patient as a dog returning to its master's grave, until it faced open water to the southwest—and held there, trembling faintly, like a finger pressed against glass from the other side.

And when she set her father's compass beside it, something stranger happened. The old needle, faithful north-pointer all her life, began to drift. Not much. Two degrees, then four, then a slow settling toward the same bearing, as if the second compass were teaching the first a language it had always known but been forbidden to speak.

*Two keys,* her aunt had said in Lagos, gripping Tamsin's wrist hard enough to leave marks. *Your father stole one so they could never be sold together. Alone, each is nothing. Together—* She hadn't finished. She hadn't needed to.

Now Tamsin laid the navy's chart of the restricted zone flat beside them, the official survey coordinates inked in confident blue: a wreck site designated MV-114, sitting in forty-one meters of water, twelve nautical miles off the shelf edge. The navy had marked it, buoyed it, sealed it, and lost a survey team on it, in that order. Commander Reyes wanted her to believe the Meridian—if it was the Meridian—lay where the blue ink said it lay.

But when she took bearings from both compasses at dawn light, standing on deck with the instruments level in her palms, their crossed lines fell nowhere near the blue ink. They fell nine miles south-southwest, into a square of ocean the charts dismissed as featureless sand. Deep water. Deeper than salvage divers liked. Deeper, she suspected, than the navy had ever bothered to look, because the navy had been looking exactly where someone a hundred and sixty years ago had wanted them to look.

A false site. A decoy wreck, planted or misreported, and every chart since built on the lie.

Her father had died within sight of these waters. She let herself feel that for one long breath—the grief with its familiar teeth—and then put it away where she kept it, ballast in the hold, keeping her steady.

The radio crackled at 0540.

"Okafor." Eze Boateng's voice came through the static smooth as palm oil, unhurried, a man who had never in his life needed to raise it. "My harbormaster tells me your *Small Mercy* took on fuel yesterday. Forty liters. That's a long trip for a woman with no destination."

"I dive where I like."

"You dive where I permit," Boateng said, and there was no cruelty in it, which made it worse. "I told you my offer stands. Anything you recover from the restricted zone comes to me, and your father's debt dies with the tide. You know how many people would say yes to that, Tamsin? Everyone. Everyone says yes. That's why I'm rich and they're not."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll be sad," he said, and she could hear him smiling. "And my fleet will still sail at first tide, because the government survey team stopped answering their radios, and men like me can smell an unlocked door across an ocean. Nine boats, girl. Sonar, mixed-gas rigs, a decompression chamber on the flagship. Whatever's down there, I'll have it up before the monsoon closes the window." A pause, filled with the tick of the radio. "You could be part of that. Or you could be a small boat in a big race. Your choice. It's always been your choice. That's what makes losing fair."

He signed off before she could answer, which was his way—he asked questions he didn't want answered.

Tamsin stood a moment with the radio's dying hiss in her ears, then went below and stowed the compasses in the oilskin pouch against her chest, where they rode warm as a second heartbeat. She cast off at 0555, ten minutes ahead of the gray light, and pointed the *Small Mercy*'s bow southwest, toward the blank water, while behind her in the harbor the first running lights of Boateng's fleet were coming on like a string of greedy lanterns.

Nine miles. At her best speed, with the sea still lying flat and treacherous, ninety minutes. She meant to make it in seventy.

* * *

The weather turned at mile six.

It came without wind at first—a swell rising out of the southwest in long, glassy ridges that lifted the *Small Mercy* and slid away beneath her keel, each one taller than the last, the sea rearranging itself in silence. Then the wind arrived all at once, a hot gusting breath off the storm line, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and the pewter sky tore open along the horizon to show a wall of deeper gray stacked to the heavens.

Tamsin had grown up reading water the way literate people read print, and what she read now frightened her in the specific, professional way that kept divers alive. This wasn't a squall. This was the leading edge of the monsoon system, arriving days early, and the window everyone in the harbor had been counting on—six tides, they said, six tides of working weather—was closing faster than any of them knew. Boateng's sonar would tell him that soon enough. His answer would be haste, and haste underwater was how men got bent, or drowned, or left.

She ran with the swell quartering her stern, throttle wide, the old engine hammering like a heart with something to prove, and she navigated not by the chart but by the pouch against her chest. Twice she stopped, killed the engine, and took bearings with both compasses cradled level in her cupped hands while the boat rolled beneath her. Both times the needles agreed with each other and disagreed with everything else, and both times she adjusted course and trusted them, because they were the only things on this ocean older than the lies.

At 0730, nine miles out, she found the place.

There was nothing to see. That was the first awe of it. No shoal broke the surface, no discoloration marked the bottom, no wreckage of any kind announced itself. Just deep open water rolling under the storm light, featureless as the charts promised. Any other diver would have turned back. But Tamsin dropped a weighted line and counted, and the lead touched bottom at fifty-eight meters—deeper than the navy's forty-one, deeper than air divers were supposed to go, deep enough that only a fool or a woman with nothing left to lose would follow it down.

She was, she reflected, tying off the anchor line and checking her tanks, both.

The surge hit as she descended.

It came down through the water column in slow pulses, whole-body pressure waves that squeezed her ears and shoved her sideways along the shot line, and the light around her dimmed from silver to olive to a green so dark it felt like descending through the eye of the storm itself. At thirty meters the temperature fell off a cliff. At forty-five her torch beam found the first thing that shouldn't exist: a chain, iron, furred with coral, running away into the dark below her.

Then the hull.

It rose out of the gloom the way cathedrals rise out of cities—suddenly, impossibly, too large for the space it occupied. A merchant vessel, masted, near two hundred feet of her, lying on her starboard side in the sand at fifty-eight meters, and she was *intact*. Not scattered, not broken, not the debris field of a ship that had died violently. She had gone down whole, settled gently, and the sea had spent a century and a half tucking her in—coral sheeting her rails, gorgonians waving from her deck like slow green flames, a forest of black coral growing from her sterncastle where a nameplate should have been.

Tamsin hung in the water above her and forgot, for a long moment, to check her gauges.

She had dived wrecks all her life. She had run her hands over drowned trawlers and wartime freighters and the sad modern hulks that littered the shipping lanes, and she had thought she understood what wrecks were: endings. Ruins. The sea's paperwork on human ambition. But this was not an ending. This was a preservation. The *Meridian*—and she knew, with a certainty that lived below thought, that this was the *Meridian*—had not been allowed to die. She had been *kept*. Laid down here in deep quiet water, miles from the false site on the navy's charts, hidden by the very empire that loaded her, as carefully as a man hides a ledger from his wife.

Because that was what she was. Not just gold. A record. And records, Tamsin thought, watching a school of barracuda wheel slowly through the rigging like a thought circling a mind, are more dangerous than treasure. Gold can be divided. Truth has to be buried whole.

She descended along the rail, following the coral-furred iron down toward the main deck, her torch cutting a cone through the drifting marine snow. The surge pushed at her in long breaths now, and somewhere far above, thunder arrived as a soft pressure against her chest. Her depth gauge read fifty-four meters. Bottom time burning. She had perhaps fifteen minutes of sane diving left, and she spent the first five of them simply moving along the rail with her glove trailing the iron, learning the ship by touch the way her father had taught her to learn everything—*eyes lie, girl, hands don't*—reading the coral growth patterns, the silt drift, the way the sand had piled against the windward rail.

Which was how she saw the marker.

It snagged on a stanchion twenty meters ahead: a small stainless-steel buoy, maybe the size of a cooking pot, tethered by a thin line to the rail, its surface furred with only a few weeks' growth of algae. Modern. Recent. Stenciled on its flank, half obscured by the algae, were letters she recognized from the news reports and the harbor gossip and Commander Reyes's careful face:

**GOVT SURVEY — GSS-3.**

The missing team's marker. Their datum buoy, planted on the true wreck, snagged and abandoned—or left.

Tamsin's awe curdled in her chest and went cold.

She moved closer, slow, controlled, every instinct she owned rising like hackles. The buoy's tether line led not up but *down*, over the rail and into the shadow between the hull and the seabed. She followed it, torch beam sweeping, pulse loud in her own ears, and found where it ended: a dive reel, clipped to the rail, its line spooled out neatly into the darkness of the ship's fallen deck. Someone had anchored that line deliberately. Someone had swum this exact path—along this rail, past this stanchion—and gone *inside*.

And on the rail beside the reel, wedged into a coral crevice where it could not wash away, sat a slate. Diver's slate, the cheap kind survey teams used, its grease-pencil writing protected under a film of algae that had only begun to grow.

She scraped it clean with her thumb.

Three words, in a hurried, slanting hand:

**THEY KNOW. GO DEEP.**

Tamsin hung there in the green dark, fifty-four meters down on a drowned ship that officially did not exist, holding a dead—or hiding—man's handwriting, while the storm breathed its long pulses through the hull above her and her bottom time bled away second by second.

The barracuda wheeled once more through the rigging and were gone.

Somewhere inside the *Meridian*, in the black between decks where a hundred and sixty years of held secrets waited, something shifted—not the surge, not the ship, but a sound, faint and rhythmic, that her hands knew before her ears did.

Exhaled air. Bubbles. Someone breathing.

Someone was down there still.
