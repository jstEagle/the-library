## 9. Names in the Hold

The Meridian's hull opened to them like a mouth that had been waiting two centuries to speak.

Tamsin went in first, her torch beam cutting a cone through water gone green-gold with silt, and for three full breaths she forgot how to move. She had dived wrecks all her working life—trawlers split like fruit, freighters folded in half, a gunboat once with its guns still trained on an enemy long dissolved—but nothing had prepared her for this: a ship so whole it seemed not sunk but sleeping, masts fallen but standing in memory, the great ribs of her arched overhead like the vaulting of a drowned cathedral.

*She's big,* Kwesi signed against his chest, the old survey-team shorthand. *Bigger than the charts said.*

Charts. Tamsin almost laughed into her regulator, bubbles streaming silver past her chin. The navy's charts put the wreck eleven miles northeast of here, in a trench of cold mud where nothing lived but starfish and patience. The compasses had brought them here instead—to a shelf of basalt off the old slaving road, where the current ran warm and slow as breath—and the Meridian had been waiting exactly where Papa's needle said she would be, all along, under everyone's nose.

Under the fleet's sonar shadow. Under the government's silence. Under her own father's grief.

They swam the length of the main deck, their lights playing over coral-encrusted capstans, a ship's bell fused to its frame in a shroud of orange and violet, the wheel spokes grown through with fan coral delicate as lace. Fish scattered before them in nervous silver schools. A moray eel watched from a cannon port with flat golden eyes, unbothered, proprietary.

At the forward hatch, the deck had rotted through, and the hold below gaped black and patient. Kwesi checked his gauges, then hers, then gave her the survey team's signal—*proceed, I follow*—and Tamsin dropped through into the dark.

* * *

The gold was everywhere.

Not stacked, not stored—*grown*. The bars lay in drifts across the hold floor like ingots of some impossible harvest, and the sea had claimed them slowly, patiently, wrapping each one in coral and calcite until metal and reef were no longer distinguishable. Her light moved across them and they answered with a dull, buried gleam, the way embers answer when you blow on them. Two hundred years of weight. Fortunes. Kingdoms. Wars fought over less than what lay in one armful of this.

She understood suddenly why men died for it. Not because it was beautiful—it wasn't, not anymore; it was ugly with age and burden, crusted like something diseased—but because it was *here*, and because men had always believed that anything the sea held could be taken back.

Kwesi drifted beside her, and even through the mask she could see he was not looking at the gold. He was looking at the far corner of the hold, where a shape sat apart from the treasure: a chest, iron-banded, lead-sheathed, its lid furred with a fine white crust of mineral bloom. He'd described it to her in the cave—the survey team's find, the thing that had started the dying—and now she saw it with her own eyes and felt the water around her go cold in a way the depth couldn't explain.

He touched her arm. Pointed. *There.*

She nodded, and together they descended the last few feet to the floor of the hold, and knelt in the sediment of two centuries, before the chest that held the names.

* * *

The lead lining had done its work. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, the documents lay dry. Dry. After two hundred years underwater, dry as paper in a Lagos archive, and Tamsin felt something superstitious shiver up her spine—the sense of a thing *kept*, deliberately, by hands that had wanted it preserved.

Kwesi took the top bundle with the reverence of a man handling a reliquary. His underwater slate was already out; he wrote in quick strokes, angling it toward her light:

*Manifest first. Then letters. Then the ledger. Read the manifest only if your heart is strong.*

She almost smiled. Almost. Instead she took the manifest and read.

*Ship Meridian, out of London by way of Bonny, cargo manifest as follows:* and then the list, itemized with the tidy horror of commerce. Palm oil. Ivory. And beneath those, in columns ruled straight and true:

*Gold specie, 412 bars.*
*Bills of exchange drawn upon the houses of:* —and here the names began.

Ashcombe. Van Deren. Cole. Whitlock. Adeyemi. Okafor.

She read her own name in the third column, in faded brown ink, in a dead clerk's careful hand, and the ocean went silent around her. Not the good silence of deep water, which is really a kind of fullness—a pressure, a presence. This was the other silence, the empty kind, the kind that opens under a diver when the bottom gives way.

*Okafor & Sons, Factors, Bonny.* Bills drawn and honored. Shares in the venture. A signature at the bottom of a page, elegant, assured, belonging to a man whose blood was in her veins, whose face might have been her father's face, buying and selling alongside the Ashcombes and the Van Derens as though human beings were barrels of oil.

Her father had known. That was the thought that rose through the cold like a body rising through dark water, arms spread, hair drifting. Papa had known. Had dived this same hold or one like it in the sixties, had seen these same names, had stolen a compass rather than let the location be sold—and had come home to a daughter he never told, carrying a secret that ate him alive in whispers about *the Meridian* and warnings she'd taken for an old man's superstition. He hadn't been protecting her from a wreck. He'd been protecting her from *this page*. From finding out what their name had bought and cost.

*Tamsin.* Kwesi's glove on her shoulder. She realized her breathing had gone ragged, loud in her own ears, bubbles hammering upward. He turned his slate: *Breathe. Slow. We're at forty meters and you're burning air.*

She made herself breathe. In for four. Out for four. The way she taught the young divers, the way she calmed herself before every descent. The ocean did not care about her crisis; the ocean would take her as easily as it took the Meridian, and grief was a luxury at this depth. She locked it down the way she locked everything down, in the box behind her sternum where she kept her father's drowning and her mother's leaving and every other thing too heavy to carry in open water.

Then she took the ledger itself.

* * *

It was a book the size of a ship's Bible, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its pages edged in gilt that still caught her torchlight and threw it back in tiny, mocking sparks. The clerk who had kept it had been meticulous. Every voyage. Every cargo. Every name.

And not just the merchants' names. The ledger recorded everything, because commerce records everything: the factors' agents along the coast, the middlemen, the canoe-men who ferried captives out to the ships, the coastal families who grew rich provisioning the trade, the inland raiders paid in iron bars and cowries. Page after page after page. It did not distinguish between the London houses and the delta houses; greed, it turned out, had kept excellent books on both sides of the water, and the ink did not care whose ancestors it indicted.

She found her family again. And again. Entry after entry, decade after decade, the Okafor name recurring like a tide mark on a wall—factors, provisioners, signatories—until the name stopped being a word and became a wall of evidence, and she was reading the architecture of her own inheritance built out of other people's chains.

Kwesi watched her read. When she finally looked up, he turned his slate with the gentleness of a man approaching a wounded animal:

*Now you understand why they started dying.*

She did. Oh, she did. The survey team had found this chest, had photographed these pages, had carried copies up into the sunlight—and somewhere between the wreck and the ministry, someone had understood what the ledger meant. Not the gold. Gold was simple; gold could be divided. But the *names*—the names reached up out of the water and into boardrooms and ministries and old family trusts, into the founding families of the nation itself, and there was no dividing that. There was only burying it. And men had died for the burying.

Her own government wanted this chest the way Boateng wanted the gold: to make it disappear. Reyes's quiet interest, the vanished survey team, the restricted charts—all of it assembled itself in her mind into a single clear picture, and the picture was this: everyone who reached for the truth meant to own it, and everyone who owned it meant to drown it.

Except her. Except maybe Kwesi, kneeling in the silt beside her with his slate and his guilt and his stubborn, surviving life.

She looked at the ledger a long moment. Then she looked at the gold.

Here was the arithmetic of her whole life laid out on the floor of a drowned ship: the debt collectors, the dying engine, the shack at the dock, the radio threats crackling through the pre-dawn dark. Four hundred bars of gold within reach of her two hands, enough to buy back her father's boat ten times over, enough to never dive for salvage again, enough to make the Okafor name mean wealth instead of want. All she had to do was become what her ancestor had been. Take from the dead. Profit from the drowned.

Her hand hovered over a bar crusted white with coral, and she thought she could feel, through the glove, the warmth of all the hands that had never gotten to hold anything.

She pulled back.

Kwesi was watching. He wrote slowly, deliberately: *We take the ledger. Only the ledger. The gold stays with the ship.*

*Yes,* she wrote back, and her hand shook writing the single word, and she hated that it shook, and she let it shake.

* * *

Rigging the chest for lift took twenty minutes and all of Kwesi's remaining line. They wrapped it in lift-bag fabric, clipped the rigging, tested the valves. Forty meters to the surface with a lead-lined chest of paper—the ascent would be slow, controlled, a decompression stop at fifteen feet while the barometer fell and the first breath of the monsoon stirred the water above them into long grey rollers.

But Tamsin lingered.

One last pass down the hold, her light sweeping the coral-fused gold, and she found herself speaking aloud into her mask, though no one could hear her—not Kwesi, not the fish, not the dead. Words without sound, just the shape of them moving through her chest.

"I know what we were," she said to the hold, to the ship, to the hundreds of souls who had passed through this hull in chains and never come back. "I know what our name bought. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you somebody finally read the book."

A barracuda hung motionless at the edge of her light, silver and ancient-eyed, listening or not listening.

"And I'm deciding," she finished, "what we're going to be."

She kicked for the hatch, up toward the grey-green shimmer of the surface world, where a storm was gathering and a salvage fleet was hunting and a naval commander waited with classified archives open like a grave. The chest rose behind her on its lift bags, slow and steady as a soul ascending, carrying four hundred years of names into the light.

Behind her, in the dark of the hold, the gold stayed where it belonged—crusted, silent, and free.

Above her, the first rain of the monsoon struck the sea like a thousand small drums, and Tamsin Okafor, heir to nothing she wanted, broke the surface holding the only inheritance she had ever chosen.
