## 10. Second Tide

The tide turned an hour before dawn, and with it came the sound.

Tamsin heard it first through the hull of the skiff where she and Kwesi lay flat among the fishing nets, a low diesel thrum rolling across the water like a herd animal moving in its sleep. Not one engine. Many. She pressed her ear to the deck planking the way her father had taught her to read weather through wood, and counted: four, maybe five vessels, running without lights, coming fast from the southwest.

"They're early," Kwesi breathed. He had his binoculars up, though there was nothing yet to see but the last stars dissolving over the water. "Boateng's lead ship wasn't due until the third tide. The harbormaster must have sold him more than our coordinates. He sold him the weather window."

"Or Boateng bought it." Tamsin slid down beside him, her back against the gunwale, and did the arithmetic she hated. Three tides of safe diving left before the monsoon made the site a graveyard for anyone below forty meters. Boateng knew it too. Every hour he stole now was an hour the sea would later charge him for, with interest paid in bodies.

The first vessel resolved out of the grey as a blunt-nosed salvage tender, its crane arm folded like a mantis at prayer. Behind it came a flat-deck barge stacked with air compressors and lift bags, and behind that, two smaller boats riding low with the weight of men and gear. She recognized them all. She had drunk palm wine on those decks. She had patched a compressor hose for the *Adom's* engineer with her own hands, taken payment in diesel and silence.

Now they were swarming toward the Meridian like flies toward a wound.

"Under," she said. "We can't run—their sonar will paint us against nothing out here. We go down and we stay down."

Kwesi was already checking his gauge, his movements quick and precise, the hydrographer's calm that had kept him alive while better men drowned. "How long can we hold?"

"As long as we have to." She pulled her mask down over her face and felt the familiar click of the world narrowing to glass and breath. "The wrecks will cover us. There's a scatter field north of the site—three hulls, maybe four, old trawlers sunk on the shelf. We tuck into their shadows and we watch."

They went over the side together, a soft double splash swallowed by the growing engine noise, and the sea closed over them like a door.

* * *

Beneath the surface, the light came down in long grey columns, and the wreck field spread below them like a battlefield after the armies had gone home. Trawlers lay keeled over in the sand, their nets rotted to lace, their holds open mouths filled with coral and small darting fish. Beyond them, at the edge of visibility, the Meridian's dark mass rose from the trench rim—her masts gone, her rail furred with a century of growth, gold sleeping somewhere inside her ribs.

Tamsin guided Kwesi along the seabed by touch and habit, moving from shadow to shadow, until they reached the hollow beneath the second trawler's stern where the sand had scoured out a pocket just large enough for two. From there they could see the whole site, and above it, arriving now, the fleet.

The sound reached them first—a deepening of pressure, a shudder in the water as anchors bit sand overhead. Then the lights came down, great sodium-white cones spearing through the murk, turning the wreck site into something lit and violated, a cathedral flooded with interrogation lamps.

Divers began to descend.

Tamsin watched them come down the anchor lines hand over hand, six of them, then eight, then twelve, their bubbles rising in silver strings. She knew some of their silhouettes before she knew their faces. That wide-shouldered shape hauling a sledgehammer was Musa, who had once carried her father's tanks up a beach at Bonny when the old man's heart stuttered mid-dive. The small diver trailing the group, kicking too hard, burning air with fear or excitement—that was Efua's boy, Sam, seventeen years old and diving for Boateng because Boateng paid cash and nobody else on this coast paid anything at all.

She watched Sam kick straight past the safety line the lead diver strung, deeper than his training, deeper than his tables allowed, drawn by the white glow of the hold opening like a promise.

"No," she said into her regulator, though no sound came. "No, boy, slow down."

He didn't slow down. None of them slowed down. Above them, on the surface, men were shouting into radios, and the shouts came down through the water as vibration, urgency transmitted directly into the bones. Boateng's voice, she was almost certain—one of the shapes on the tender's deck had his build, his restless way of standing at a rail as if the sea owed him money. Which, Tamsin thought bitterly, it did. And he meant to collect before the monsoon foreclosed on everyone.

The divers reached the Meridian's rail and swarmed over it like ants over a fallen fruit. Their lights converged on the hold, and even from fifty meters away, even through the drifting sediment their fins kicked up, Tamsin saw the flash of it: yellow gleaming in the coral matrix, gold bars fused into the wreck's flesh like honeycomb, visible wherever their chisels broke the crust.

The water filled with hammering. She felt it in her teeth.

* * *

An hour passed, measured in tank pressure and the slow drift of the current. Tamsin held Kwesi's wrist to keep them anchored to each other in the scour pocket, and they watched the salvage operation strip the Meridian with the efficiency of locusts.

It was going wrong. She could see it going wrong, the way you can see a fire spreading behind a wall before the smoke comes through.

The haste showed first in the small things. A diver dropped a chisel and let it fall rather than chase it. Two men worked the same coral block from opposite sides and cracked it carelessly, sending shards spinning through the lights. The lift bags went up half-filled, badly rigged, because nobody wanted to spend bottom time on rigging when there was gold showing. And all the while the depth told on them—the cold seeping in, the narcosis blurring judgment, the nitrogen whispering its sweet lies into ears already full of greed.

Musa was the first to bend.

She saw it happen: he found a bar loose in the coral, wrestled it free, and clipped it to his harness instead of waiting for the lift bag—which was the smart move, the only move, except that Musa had been down forty minutes at depth and every minute he lingered was a debt accruing against his blood. When he finally released his grip and kicked for the line, he came up too fast. Everyone comes up too fast when their hands are full of gold. She watched his exhaust bubbles stream upward in a rush and knew, with a diver's sick certainty, what was happening inside his body before his body knew it itself.

He cleared the surface. And then, faintly, carried down through the water, she heard the shouting change key—from urgency to alarm.

They brought him back down within minutes, jammed into the tender's decompression stage, hung at six meters like meat in a smokehouse. Tamsin watched the recompression attempt through the grey water, watched the stage sway on its cable, and thought about the chamber aboard the barge, the old steel cylinder Boateng had bought secondhand from a defunct oil company, the one whose door seal the engineers complained about and Boateng refused to replace because replacement cost money and men were cheap.

Men were cheap. Gold was not. That was the whole of Eze Boateng's arithmetic, and it was written now in Musa's bent body hanging in the water column.

Sam came up next, or partway up. He panicked at fifteen meters—she saw it plainly, saw his fins stop, saw him clutch at the anchor line, saw his mask flood—and when he bolted for the surface she could do nothing but watch, her fingers digging into the sand, her whole body straining against the water that held her helpless as glass. The boy surfaced streaming bubbles, was hauled aboard, and did not reappear. The dive flag stayed up. The compressors kept roaring. Nothing stopped.

Nothing ever stopped. That was the lesson of the morning, written in sodium light and hammering: the work continued around the broken men the way water continues around stones. There were always more divers. There were always more bodies. The coast was poor and Boateng was rich and the gap between those facts was a machine that converted young men into bottom time.

Tamsin became aware that Kwesi was gripping her arm. She turned. His face behind his mask was tight, and he pointed—not at the wreck, not at the divers, but at the water between them and the trawler field.

A swimmer was coming toward them. Alone, no line, no tank—free-diving, gliding down in long easy sweeps with a torch in his fist, sweeping the beam across the wreck shadows. Searching.

Looking for stowaways.

* * *

They moved. There was no decision in it, only the body's older wisdom: Tamsin hauled Kwesi out of the scour pocket and into the black throat of the trawler's collapsed stern, guiding him by touch through a gap in the plating no wider than a coffin lid. Inside was rot-silt and blind shrimp and the smellless dark of a space that had been underwater longer than either of them had been alive. They wedged themselves against the hull plates and killed their lights and listened.

The searcher's beam played across the gap in the plating. Once. Twice. Held.

Tamsin pressed herself flat against the corroded steel and made herself into a thing that had never been born. This was her oldest armor—invisibility, the talent for being overlooked that poverty had drilled into her since childhood—but here, in a lit and crawling wreck site, with a torch beam inches from her mask, the armor felt thin as paper. She could hear the free-diver breathing through his snorkel above the plating, a wet rhythmic sound, patient as a fisherman checking traps.

The beam moved on.

They waited anyway. They waited through the searcher's departure, through another hour of hammering, through the moment when the compressor noise overhead changed pitch and the first lift bag broke the surface carrying its pale cargo of coral-crusted gold. They waited until the tide itself shifted, until the current swung and the sediment clouds rolled through the wreck field like weather, erasing everything.

Only then did Tamsin allow herself to think, and thinking, to burn.

Not the clean anger of being wronged—she had carried that kind all her life and knew its weight like ballast. This was hotter. This was watching Musa hang in the water column with his blood fizzing like soda, watching a seventeen-year-old boy get hauled aboard half-drowned for a payday he'd never see, watching men she had broken bread with be spent like fuel by a man who calculated everything in cedis and nothing in scars—and knowing that she could do nothing, that hiding was the only mercy the morning offered her, that her fury had nowhere to go except inward, where it sat like a swallowed coal.

Power spends other people's bodies. Her father had said something like it once, drunk on cheap gin, staring at the harbor cranes. *The sea doesn't take the strong ones first, Tamsin. It takes the ones somebody else sent down.*

She looked at Kwesi in the dark. His eyes behind his mask were steady on hers, and in them she saw the same recognition: they had both spent their lives being the ones somebody else sent down. The survey team he'd fled, the debt she owed, the ledger sleeping in the hold—all of it was the same story wearing different clothes. Names on paper deciding what flesh was worth.

And the ledger was still down there. In the hold. In the lead-lined chest, under the coral, under the swarm of lights and hammers and grasping hands.

Her stomach dropped as the realization landed: Boateng's divers were working the hold. Working *toward* it. Sooner or later a chisel would ring against lead, and someone would haul that chest into the light, and whatever was written inside would pass into the hands of the one man on this coast who understood exactly what it was worth and would bury it deepest.

"We can't let them reach the chest," she said, when they finally surfaced in the sheltered lee of the trawler's upturned hull, voices low beneath the engine drone. Water streamed off her hood. "Gold they can take. Let them break their backs on gold. But the chest—"

"I know." Kwesi wiped his mask with a shaking hand. She realized he was not shaking from cold. "Tamsin. Look at the fleet."

She looked. The tender, the barge, the two small boats—and beyond them, at the edge of the anchorage, a fifth vessel she hadn't marked before, grey-painted, unlit, sitting at anchor with the stillness of something that was watching rather than working.

Through her binoculars, brought up dripping, she found the figure at its rail. Naval whites. Binoculars raised. Watching the salvage operation with the patience of a man counting other people's mistakes.

Commander Reyes had come to watch Boateng dig.

And Reyes, she remembered with a cold slide of understanding, had seen her face at the bargain. Knew she'd traded false coordinates. Knew she was holding back the true ones. Whatever happened in that hold today—whatever chest came up, whatever names came to light—he would be standing right there when it did, ready to make it all disappear into classified silence.

Below them, through the hull, the hammering went on. Closer now. She could feel it in her spine.

A chisel rang against something that was not coral, not steel, not stone.

Lead.

Tamsin closed her eyes. Then she reached for her tanks.
