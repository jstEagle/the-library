## 11. Bargain at Gunpoint

They came for her on the falling tide, when the channel between the wrecks ran shallow and silver and there was nowhere left to run.

Tamsin heard the engines first—two of them, running rough, the way engines run when their owners don't care how long they last. She was crouched in the lee of a half-sunk barge with Kwesi, both of them soaked to the bone from the second dive, the ledger chest's weight still ghosting across her shoulders though they'd left it hidden in the cave. The sound came around the headland like a rumor she'd been dreading for days.

"Boateng's," Kwesi said. Not a question. He'd worked the survey boats long enough to know every hull note on this coast, and these were workboats pushed past their manners.

Tamsin counted seconds between engine beats. Two boats. Maybe eight men. The skiff they'd stolen was beached in plain view behind them, its borrowed radio still warm from her mistake—the coordinates she'd keyed in under pressure, broadcast to any harbor ear that cared to listen. She had told herself the numbers were scrambled enough. She had told herself a lot of things since dawn.

"We swim," Kwesi said.

"They'll see us. Water's clear as glass out there." She looked at the channel, at the light lying on it like spilled oil. "And we'd be leaving everything."

"Leaving is living."

"Not always." She said it before she could weigh it, and heard in her own voice something that surprised her—not fear, but a kind of cold arithmetic. Her father's voice, almost. *The sea answers to the one who does the sums.*

The first boat rounded the barge before she finished the thought.

* * *

They were not gentle men, but they were not cruel ones either, which was worse, because it meant they were following orders rather than appetites, and men following orders can be reasoned with. Mostly. The big one who pulled her over the gunwale by her life vest checked her hands first—diver's habit, looking for a knife—and found the old scar across her palm where coral had opened her at fifteen. He grunted something that might have been sympathy.

Kwesi came aboard more slowly, hands raised, and Tamsin watched him do his own arithmetic: three men on this boat, five on the other, rifles slung but present, the way a fact is present even when no one mentions it.

"Eze wants a conversation," the big man said. His name, she would learn, was Fola, and he spoke with the flat courtesy of someone who has delivered bad news so often it has worn smooth as sea glass. "He's on the flagship. You know the one."

She knew it. Everyone on this coast knew Boateng's flagship—the converted trawler with the crane arm like a gallows, riding at anchor now two miles east, exactly where the navy's charts said the Meridian should be. Exactly where it wasn't.

"He's diving your false bottom right now," she said, and couldn't keep the edge out of it.

Fola smiled without warmth. "Then you'll have good news for him. Or interesting news. With Eze, those are sometimes the same thing."

* * *

The flagship smelled of diesel and ambition. Its deck had been cleared for salvage operations—winches, cable drums, a decompression chamber bolted down like an afterthought—and its cabin, where they took her, had been cleared for something else entirely. Charts everywhere. Sonar printouts. A whiteboard with tide tables written in Boateng's own hand, the numerals cramped and greedy even on paper.

Eze Boateng sat behind a desk that had clearly been installed for the purpose of sitting behind. He was smaller than his reputation, which she should have expected; reputations add water weight. What he kept instead of size was stillness—a jeweler's stillness, the kind that made you aware of your own fidgeting. His eyes went to her first, then to Kwesi, then back, weighing them the way he weighed everything: against what they might fetch.

"Tamsin Okafor." He said her name like a price he'd already negotiated down. "Your father owed me eleven thousand when he drowned. I forgave it. Did you know that?"

"I knew you sent collectors to my mother's funeral wake."

"I sent an inquiry." He folded his hands. "Grief makes people forget what they owe. I try to be patient with grief." He gestured at the charts. "You've been busy. My divers found your marker line off the northern shoal. Navy coordinates. Very official. Also very empty—we've been dragging that grid for two days and all we've raised is rust and a boot."

"Sounds about right for the navy."

"It sounds wrong for you." He leaned forward, and the stillness sharpened into something with edges. "You don't waste dives, Tamsin. Your father didn't either—that was his whole tragedy, he never wasted anything except himself. So either you're diving those navy numbers because you believe them, which makes you a fool, or you're diving them because you want people like me diving them, which makes you something else entirely."

Behind her, Kwesi shifted his weight. One of the enforcers by the door moved his hand toward his rifle sling, casually, the way a man adjusts a strap.

"What do you want, Eze?"

"The same thing everyone wants. The truth." He smiled, and it was almost charming, which was the most frightening thing in the room. "Or failing that, the location of it. You're holding back. I can smell it. It smells like lead-lined chests and colonial gold, and I will tell you honestly—" he spread his small hands, "—I have spent thirty years converting things into money. Boats. Wrecks. Debts. Men. I am very good at it. And you are the most convertible object to come into my waters in a decade."

"I'm not an object."

"No one is, at first." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the trap's soft lining. "Here is my offer. Give me the true coordinates. Your debt—your father's debt, with interest compounded through years I generously ignored—vanishes. Not reduced. Vanished. And I put a boat under you. A real one. New engine, new gear, your name on the registration. You'd never dive another man's restricted wreck at dawn just to eat."

It was a good offer. That was the obscenity of it—it was a genuinely good offer, built on real numbers, and some tired animal part of her, the part that had eaten instant noodles for nine months and watched the repo men circle her shack like gulls, wanted to say yes so badly her teeth ached.

And underneath that wanting, quieter, her father's compass lay hidden in the cave with the ledger chest, and she thought: *this is how they got the names in the ledger. Not with chains. With offers.*

"You want the truth," she said. "Fine. Here it is."

* * *

She lied the way she dove: breathing slow, watching her gauges, committing fully to the descent.

"The navy's charts are wrong, but not stupid-wrong. They're shifted." She took the chart Boateng slid across the desk and marked it with a grease pencil, her hand steady, her heart doing something ugly and private in her chest. "Whoever surveyed that wreck originally misread the current set. Everything's displaced northeast by about a mile and a half, down the sand spine. There's a scoured trench there—I found debris fielding out of it. Hull plating, brass fittings, coral-fused metal. Too deep for your divers on air, but you've got mixed gas on this tub."

All of it true—except the trench she named was a natural scour hole a mile from the real site, one she'd noted months ago while hunting lobster, empty of everything but old anchor chain and her own disappointment. The navy's actual charts, the ones Reyes's people carried, placed the wreck near that trench anyway. She was simply feeding Boateng the navy's error, polished up with details only a real diver could provide. The best lies are salvage work: true parts, reassembled.

Boateng studied the mark for a long time. Then he studied her.

"You understand I'll verify this."

"Of course. Send your best diver down the sand spine. He'll find the debris field by tomorrow's low tide." She let exhaustion into her voice now, calculated, the truth of it bent slightly to serve the lie. "Eze, I'm tired. I've been diving double tides on a dying engine to pay interest to men like you. Take the coordinates. Clear the debt. Leave me the boat you promised and leave me alone."

"And the documents?" His eyes flicked to Kwesi. "Your government friend here lost some colleagues over documents, I hear. Documents better left drowned, the navy says. Where are they?"

"There are no documents. There's a dead survey team and a navy that wants this coast quiet, and whatever spooked them is still down there with the fish." She shrugged, and hated how easily the shrug came. "If I find paper, you'll be the second to know. After me."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the crane arm creaked against its mooring, a sound like a gallows testing its rope.

Boateng looked at Kwesi. "And you? Nothing to sell?"

"I sold enough when I stayed silent," Kwesi said quietly. "I'm out of inventory."

Something moved behind Boateng's eyes—respect, maybe, or the recognition of a fellow appraiser finding an item with no market. He turned back to Tamsin.

"Debt's cleared," he said. "As of this minute. Fola will log it." He tapped the chart once, twice, a man closing a ledger in his head. "The boat comes when I raise gold from those coordinates. If there's no gold—" he smiled again, and this time there was nothing charming in it at all, "—then I'll know what else you lied about, and our conversation resumes. Conversations with me don't end, Tamsin. They just go quiet."

He waved a hand. Dismissal. And then, as they reached the door:

"One more thing. The officer. Reyes. He watched you come aboard—he's been shadowing you since the cay, did you know? Navy doesn't spend fuel on nobody." He said it lightly, a gift wrapped around a hook. "Whatever game you're playing, you're playing it against two tables now. Mind your chips."

* * *

The transfer boat took them back through the dying light, and Tamsin sat with her hands in her lap and felt the lie sitting in her stomach like swallowed ballast.

It had worked. That was the worst part. Fola had logged the debt cancellation with genuine ceremony, stamping a paper she signed with a hand that didn't shake, and somewhere in the machinery of Eze Boateng's empire, eleven thousand dollars of her father's drowning had been erased with a keystroke. She was free. She had bought freedom with a false coordinate and a steady voice, and it had cost less than she'd feared, and that was the thing that shamed her—not the danger, not the guns, but how *easy* it had been. How naturally the deception had come, smooth as breath, as if some ancestor from the ledger had reached up through sixty years and lent her the family talent.

Kwesi watched the water go by. "You did what you had to."

"I enjoyed it," she said. "A little. That's the problem."

"That's just being good at something bad."

"No." She shook her head. "Being good at something bad is how it starts. Somebody in that ledger started exactly like this—one lie about cargo, one smooth transaction, one time telling themselves it was survival. And then it was a business. And then it was a family." She looked at her palms, at the diver's scar. "I just signed my name next to theirs."

"You signed a debt release."

"Same pen."

They fell silent. Ahead, the navy patrol boat sat black against the last light, exactly where she knew it would be, exactly where Boateng had promised it would be. Commander Reyes stood on its deck with binoculars lowered, waiting, and even at this distance Tamsin could read his posture: not triumphant, not angry. Patient. The patience of a man who has just learned the single most valuable thing about an opponent—that she is capable of lying well, which means she is capable of lying at all, which means nothing she says can ever again be taken at face value, including, especially, her denials.

Reyes raised one hand as their boat slowed. Not a wave. An invitation.

"She knows," Tamsin murmured. "Boateng told me to watch my chips, and Reyes just watched me cash them. He knows I gave away fake numbers. Which means he knows I know the real ones."

"Then he also knows you didn't give them to Boateng." Kwesi's voice was careful. "That makes you the only person on this coast holding the truth and refusing to sell it. To him, that's not a liability, Tamsin. That's an asset he needs secured."

The patrol boat's searchlight snapped on, pinning them in white, and a voice over a loudspeaker told them, politely, to come alongside.

Tamsin looked at the light, at the water burning silver beyond it, at the invisible trench a mile away where a lead-lined chest waited in the dark with all the names inside it—including hers, including the lie she'd just told, which felt suddenly like the newest entry in the oldest book.

"Protection," she said bitterly. "That's what they'll call it."

"Everything they take," Kwesi said, "they call protection."

She turned the skiff toward the searchlight, toward the ship, toward the third table in the game, and beneath the keel the tide turned and began, patiently, to run against them.
