## 12. The Navy's Quiet Interest

The patrol boat's name was *NNS Vigilant*, and Tamsin thought it the funniest thing she'd heard in weeks.

They brought her and Kwesi aboard at dusk, under floodlights that turned the sea to hammered tin. Two sailors took her arms at the ladder—not rough, not gentle, just firm in the way of men following orders they'd been given twice—and walked her down a corridor that smelled of diesel, disinfectant, and old coffee. Her wet suit had been peeled to her waist on the skiff; someone had draped a grey navy blanket over her shoulders with a courtesy that felt like a leash.

Kwesi walked beside her, hands still zip-tied until a lieutenant with soft eyes cut them at the foot of the ladder. He flexed his fingers against his palms as if reminding himself they were his.

"They're not arresting us," he murmured.

"No," Tamsin said. "Arresting is what they do when they want you counted."

* * *

The wardroom was small and clean and lit like an operating theatre. A table bolted to the deck. A pitcher of water sweating in the air conditioning's cold breath. Commander Reyes sat at the far end with two mugs of tea already poured, steam rising off them in thin ribbons, and Tamsin understood immediately that this was theater too—that he'd ordered the tea before their skiff was even alongside, because hospitality was cheaper than handcuffs and worked better.

"Miss Okafor." He gestured to the chair opposite him. "Mr. Mensah. Sit, please. You've both had a long day."

It was not a question of whether they would sit. Tamsin sat. The tea was sweetened—someone had learned how she took it, or guessed, or simply made it the way everyone along this coast drank it—and she hated how much she wanted it. She wrapped her hands around the mug anyway and let the warmth soak into fingers gone numb from the dive.

Reyes was younger than his rank suggested. Forty, maybe forty-two, with a creased face that looked like it had spent more years squinting into horizons than into paperwork. His uniform was immaculate but his boots were salt-stained, and Tamsin filed that away: a man who went out on the water himself. That made him either more honest than most officers or better at pretending.

"You found Mr. Mensah," Reyes said. "That's the good news. The official version writes itself: hydrographer separated from his team during a survey operation, sheltering from weather, recovered by a local diver. Everyone goes home."

"And the unofficial version?" Kwesi asked.

Reyes turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table, aligning its handle with some private meridian. "The unofficial version is that three members of my survey team are dead, Mr. Mensah. Okafor-Adeyemi drowned in ten meters of flat water with a full tank. Boateng—" he caught himself, saw the flicker pass between Tamsin and Kwesi, and continued carefully, "—the other one, Sergeant Danjuma, fell from the crow's nest of a vessel at anchor. And Dr. Amadi was found in her bunk with no mark on her and no explanation anyone will put in writing. So the unofficial version is that I have four missing persons if I count you, and I am currently sitting across from the only two people alive who know why."

The air conditioning hummed. Somewhere below them, a pump cycled on and off, on and off, like something breathing in its sleep.

"You read the ledger," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question either.

"I never saw it." Reyes's voice stayed level, but something behind his eyes shifted—the look of a man stepping around a hole he knew was there. "I was told it existed. I was told what it contained. I was told that its recovery was now a matter of national interest, and that national interest had a very specific address." He paused. "You understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you take orders from people who don't sign anything."

"I take orders from people who own everything," Reyes said quietly. "There's a difference, and it matters less every year."

Kwesi leaned forward. "Commander, your team didn't die of accidents. They died because someone decided dead men can't testify about dead men's money. If you hand us over to whoever gave those orders—"

"I'm not handing you over." For the first time, something raw moved through Reyes's composure, quick as a fish under a boat. "I'm offering you protection. Real protection. Quarters aboard this ship, legal status as material witnesses, immunity from Mr. Eze Boateng's considerable reach, and—" he spread his hands, "—my personal word that nothing happens to you while you're under my flag."

Tamsin set down her tea. "And all you want is the ledger."

"The documents," Reyes said. "Yes."

"For the archive."

"For safekeeping."

"Those aren't the same word, Commander."

He looked at her for a long moment, and Tamsin watched him decide something—watched the decision move through his face like weather. Then he reached into the breast pocket of his uniform and laid a photograph on the table between them, face up.

It was old. Sepia going brown at the edges. A group of men in colonial dress standing on the steps of a government building, and beneath the image, in faded ink, a date from the 1920s and a list of names. Tamsin recognized none of them, and recognized all of them—the way you recognize the architecture of your own house without ever having read the architect's name.

"Do you know who built the harbor at Port Harcourt?" Reyes asked. "Who owned the shipping concessions? Whose signatures are on the land grants along the entire littoral?" He tapped the photograph once. "I do. Not because I'm a historian. Because when this assignment came down, I spent three sleepless nights reading files I was not cleared to read, and I discovered that the families who founded this country's commerce—the names on our currency, our streets, our school crests—are the same names your Mr. Mensah has been sleeping in a cave to protect."

"The founding families," Kwesi said slowly.

"In the ledger." Reyes nodded. "Every one of them. The merchant houses, the factors, the middlemen who sold their neighbors' neighbors. The men whose great-grandchildren sit in the Senate and sign the salvage permits and decide which wrecks get protected and which get plundered." He leaned back. "Now tell me, Miss Okafor—if you were one of those families, and a document surfaced after a hundred and sixty years naming your fortune's first coin—what would you want done with it?"

Tamsin said nothing. The pump breathed below them. On the bulkhead behind Reyes hung a framed chart of the coast, and her eyes kept snagging on it, on the neat printed depths, the tidy fathom lines, all of it wrong, all of it a map drawn by people who wanted the sea to be simple.

"I'd want it drowned," she said finally.

"Yes," Reyes agreed. "So would they. And here is my problem, Miss Okafor: I have taken an oath to a republic, and I have just described to you the people who own it. If I put that ledger into the classified archive—as I've been ordered to—it will be catalogued, sealed, and within five years it will be lost. Misfiled. Destroyed in an unfortunate fire. I have seen how these things go. I am not naive." He folded his hands. "But if I *don't* put it in the archive, then it stays out there—with you—with Mr. Boateng circling you—with every ambitious man on this coast who smells gold and leverage. And people die. My people died. So you'll forgive me if the archive doesn't sound like the worst option available."

"It's not the worst option," Tamsin said. "It's just the quietest one. There's a difference too."

* * *

They were given a cabin—given, not locked into, though Tamsin noted the sentry outside the door and the fact that the porthole had been painted over from the inside, a grey circle where the sea should have been. Bunks with clean blankets. A basin. A carafe of water that tasted faintly of the ship's tanks, of iron and distance.

Kwesi sat on the lower bunk with his elbows on his knees, turning his wedding ring—he wore one though Tamsin had never heard him mention a wife—around and around his finger.

"She died," he said, without preamble. "My wife. Four years ago. Fever." He said it the way he reported depths and bearings, flat and exact, because that was the only way he could say it. "After that I stopped belonging anywhere. The survey team was the closest I'd come. And then Amadi read the ledger and started asking questions, and Okafor-Adeyemi went into the water, and I understood that belonging to something can kill you faster than belonging to no one." He looked up. "Reyes isn't lying to us. That's what frightens me. Liars I know how to handle."

"He's not lying," Tamsin agreed. She stood at the painted-over porthole, pressing her palm flat against the grey circle, as if she could feel the water through the paint. "He's worse. He's honest inside a system that isn't. He'll protect us right into a filing cabinet."

"You think he'd bury it?"

"I think he already knows he'll bury it, and he hates himself for knowing, and he's hoping we make it easy for him by giving it up voluntarily so his hands stay clean." She turned. "Everyone who wants that ledger wants it *gone*, Kwesi. Boateng wants it gone so the gold comes up clean. The families want it gone so the names stay respectable. Reyes wants it gone because gone is the only place it's safe. Nobody—and I mean nobody—is standing on any deck in the world saying *let it be read.*"

"Except us."

"Except us." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Two divers and a chest of paper against a navy, a fleet, and a hundred and sixty years of very polite silence."

Kwesi was quiet for a while. The ship rolled gently, riding a swell they couldn't see, and somewhere above them boots crossed the deck in unhurried pairs.

"There's a third option," he said at last. "You keep saying sell it or sink it or seal it. But there's a fourth thing. You copy it. Every page. Before anyone gets their hands on the original. Then whatever happens to the chest—archive, fire, sea—the names survive somewhere no one can order lost."

Tamsin stared at him. "Copy it onto what? We don't have a camera. We don't have—"

"We have time," Kwesi said. "We have however long it takes Reyes to lose patience, and men like him always have more patience than they admit and less than they claim. And I have spent eleven days in a cave memorizing survey data because paper burns. I can memorize a ledger, Tamsin. Page by page. Names, sums, ships, ports. It's what I'm *for*. I'm a hydrographer—I hold numbers so maps can be made later." His voice dropped. "Give me the chest tonight. By dawn I'll have the first thirty pages in my head, and after that it doesn't matter what anyone does to the paper."

She thought of her father then—of him treading water in some other decade, holding a brass compass under the surface, choosing which truth to carry up and which to leave drowning. She had spent her whole life thinking he'd stolen a treasure. Now she understood he'd stolen a burden, and passed it to her wrapped in salt.

A knock at the cabin door. One knock, soft, and the sentry's silhouette shifting aside.

Reyes entered without waiting, closed the door behind him, and stood a moment in the small room's stale air, looking suddenly tired in a way the wardroom hadn't allowed. He held a folded paper in one hand.

"A signal came in," he said. "From the harbormaster at Bonny. Eze Boateng's flagship put to sea six hours ago with eight vessels and a decompression barge. He's filed no flight plan, given no destination." He let the paper hang from his fingers. "But his lead captain bought charts last week. Charts of the sector where your compasses cross, Miss Okafor. Someone talked. Someone always talks."

Tamsin felt the floor tilt under her, though the sea was calm.

"So here is where we are," Reyes went on. "In perhaps eighteen hours, a man with no conscience and a private navy arrives at the most important wreck on this coast, and I have one patrol boat and orders I cannot follow and cannot ignore." He looked from her to Kwesi and back, and for one unguarded moment Tamsin saw the whole architecture of him—the oath, the owners, the dead survey team, the long slow rot of doing the correct thing in an incorrect world. "I asked you downstairs for the ledger. I'm asking you now for something else. Tell me where the wreck truly is. Not the navy's coordinates. The real ones. Let me get there first."

"And then what?" Tamsin asked. "You raise it? You seal it? You bury it deeper?"

"And then," Reyes said, "we decide together—out there, on the water, where I can still hear myself think—whether the truth goes into a drawer or into the light. I can't promise you the light, Miss Okafor. I can only promise you that I'm the last officer in this chain who hasn't been paid."

Outside the painted-over porthole, the first wind of the coming monsoon sighed through the rigging, a long low note like a held breath.

Tamsin looked at Kwesi. Kwesi looked back, and gave her the smallest nod—the nod of a man who had already memorized half a truth and trusted her with the rest.

"Eighteen hours," she said to Reyes. "Give us the chart table, a pot of coffee, and your word that nobody boards that skiff. Then I'll show you where the Meridian sleeps."

Reyes extended his hand across the small cabin, and above them, far off toward the southeast, the sky began, very quietly, to count the tides down.
