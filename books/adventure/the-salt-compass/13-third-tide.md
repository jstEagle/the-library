## 13. Third Tide

The *Vigil* smelled of diesel and institutional coffee, and Tamsin had spent two days learning its rhythms the way she'd once learned a new reef: where the sentries slowed their rounds, which hatch stuck, at what hour the galley's clatter covered the sound of a door. Reyes's ship was a floating argument about order—everything squared away, everything labeled, everything watched. She hated it the way she hated calm water over a drop-off. It looked safe. That was the lie.

They were being transferred to the naval station at Takoradi in the morning, Reyes had told them, all courtesy and steel. "For your own protection." The phrase again. Everyone who wanted something from her kept saying it, as if protection were a place you could be put and locked.

Kwesi sat across the small cabin from her, wrists still chafed from the flex-cuffs they'd cut off him after the first night. He hadn't spoken much since the detention began. He'd gone quiet the way deep water goes quiet—not empty, just holding everything down.

The supply transfer came at 0640, as it always did. A workboat came alongside with crates of provisions, and for eleven minutes the aft deck became a chaos of shouting men and swinging cargo, everyone's eyes on the crane, on the swell, on not getting crushed between hulls.

Tamsin had counted those minutes twice. Eleven was enough.

She wore a borrowed deck jacket she'd found folded in a locker, navy-issue, anonymous. Kwesi carried nothing but his survey notebook, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. They moved through the working crowd the way silt moves through water—unremarkable, going where the current of labor went. A bosun shouted at Kwesi to get that crate forward, and Kwesi got the crate forward, and no one looked twice at a man doing what he was told. Invisibility, Tamsin thought, was a skill the sea taught you early. You learned to be weather, not cargo.

The skiff was tied at the lower rail—a survey launch, one of the *Vigil*'s own, fuel tanks half full, keys in the ignition because navies trusted themselves. She dropped over the rail first, felt the boat rock under her weight, and reached up for Kwesi's hand. For a moment he hesitated above her, silhouetted against the grey morning, looking back at the ship that had held him.

"Kwesi," she said. Low. "Now."

He came down like a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for weeks.

The line slipped free. The *Vigil*'s shadow slid past them, and then there was only open water and the sound of their own engine, and Tamsin pushed the throttle flat because distance first, questions later.

No alarm. Not yet. Maybe the bosun thought they belonged to the workboat; maybe the workboat thought they belonged to the *Vigil*. Maybe Reyes already knew and was letting them run, the way a fisherman lets line out. She didn't look back. Looking back was how you hit things ahead of you.

By the time the naval base's headland sank behind the haze, the sky had begun to change. Not darkening exactly—thickening. The light went the color of old pewter, and the wind swung a quarter turn east, and Tamsin felt the shift in her teeth before her instruments confirmed it. Barometer falling. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore. It was arriving.

"Storm window's closing," she said. "Three tides, maybe less."

Kwesi nodded and opened his notebook against the console, weighing the pages with a carabiner. His hands were steady. His voice wasn't.

"Tamsin. There's something I have to tell you before we're out there. Before you trust me with your life on a line."

She kept her eyes on the water. "You faked your disappearance. I know that much."

"That's not what this is." He swallowed. She heard it over the engine. "The team—the others. Adjei, Lindqvist, the Okonkwo sisters. We found the ledger on the fourth dive. We brought documents up. And people started reading them." He stopped. Started again. "Adjei read the names first. Two days later his skiff was found burned on the beach at Dixcove. No body. Then Lindqvist went out alone at night and didn't come back, and the navy called it an accident, and I—" His jaw worked. "I knew it wasn't an accident. I knew after Adjei. And I said nothing. I stayed quiet because quiet had kept me alive my whole life. I'm a hydrographer. My whole job is measuring things without disturbing them. So I measured. I took notes. I hid in a cave while my colleagues drowned or burned, and I told myself I was waiting for the right moment, and the right moment never comes, Tamsin. It doesn't exist. Silence isn't patience. It's just silence."

The engine droned. A frigate bird hung motionless overhead, riding the thickening air.

"They died," he said, "because I did the math and decided I wasn't worth spending. And then I let you walk into the same water."

Tamsin steered through a long swell, feeling the boat's rhythm change under her hands, heavier now, the sea gathering itself. She thought about her father. About all the years she'd believed he'd simply been careless, a diver who misjudged a current, when really he had been carrying something too heavy to surface with. Silence killed divers. It was the first rule anyone learned: never dive alone, never hold your breath, never keep the fear to yourself. Her father had broken every rule at once, alone with his secret, and the sea had taken the payment.

"You stayed alive," she said finally. "That's not a crime."

"It is when the price was other people."

"I'm not the one to absolve you." She glanced at him. "But I'll tell you what I know about guilt. It's ballast. You don't dump it—you trim it. You use it to keep yourself upright in bad water. The moment you throw it overboard so you can feel light, you capsize into whatever's next." She turned back to the horizon, where the clouds had begun to organize themselves into towers. "You came out of that cave. You gave me the coordinates. You're here now. Trim it, Kwesi. Don't dump it."

He was silent a long moment. Then, quietly: "Does that mean you forgive me?"

"No." She said it without cruelty. "It means we're even enough to work together. Forgiveness is a tide. It comes when it comes. You can't schedule it."

Something in his shoulders loosened anyway, some fraction of a degree, the way a knot eases before it's actually untied. Neither of them believed in it yet. But belief could wait until they were ashore and alive. Right now there was only the running.

* * *

The site lay forty miles southwest, past the shelf edge where the water went from green-glass to ink. Tamsin ran the skiff hard through building seas, both compasses lashed to the console now—her father's brass and the twin from the strongbox, needles trembling in agreement, pointing the way no chart agreed with. Every few minutes she checked the barometer's slow fall and recalculated. Three tides. The monsoon would close the coast like a fist.

Halfway out, the radio crackled. Harbor traffic, garbled by static and distance—and threaded through it, unmistakable, the clipped cadence of naval channel discipline. She caught fragments: *unauthorized launch... Vigil... be advised...*

Reyes knew. Of course he knew. The question was whether he'd chase them or simply follow, patient as pressure, letting the storm do the work of custody. Tamsin switched the radio off. Whatever was coming would come faster or slower regardless of whether she listened to it arrive.

"She'll let us go," Kwesi said, reading her face. "For now. We're more useful loose. We lead her straight to what she wants."

"Then we won't lead her anywhere." Tamsin's mind was already working the problem the way it worked a wreck: entry points, hazards, what holds weight. "We run dark past the fleet anchorage, come in from the west with the storm behind us. Every sonar on that coast will be blinded by monsoon chop. Weather's not our enemy today. It's our cover."

Kwesi looked at her—really looked, the way a man looks at a current he's deciding whether to swim. "You've done this before."

"I've survived worse than this before. Different thing." She fed the skiff another notch of throttle and the bow lifted, spray coming aboard warm and metallic. "Hold the compasses steady. Tell me when the needles cross true."

The afternoon went pewter to slate. The swells came longer now, organized, marching in from the southwest like something summoned. Twice they ran through rain squalls that hit like thrown buckets and vanished, leaving the air steaming. And through all of it the two brass needles held their seaward insistence, quivering slightly, faithful as heartbeat.

Near dusk, Kwesi spoke without lifting his eyes from the instruments. "When we find it—the ledger. What are you going to do?"

"Read it."

"And after?"

Tamsin watched the water. Somewhere below that darkening plain lay a hull that had been waiting two centuries, full of gold and names, and one of the names was hers. "After," she said, "is a long way off. Get me there first."

But the question stayed with her, riding in the boat like a third passenger, as the last light failed and the first real rain came down and the needles pointed on, steady, into the black.

Behind them, far back along the grey seam of the horizon, a white shape turned out of the haze and began to follow. Not chasing. Following. The way pressure follows a diver down.

Tamsin saw it, said nothing, and drove on into the storm.
