## 14. Monsoon Warning

The radio woke her before the wind did.

It came through the little set in the skiff's cabin in the voice of the harbormaster's clerk, a young man named Dede whose diction Tamsin had always found too careful for a man who took bribes in cigarette cartons. *All vessels, all vessels. Meteorological bulletin. Cyclonic system Amara has strengthened overnight. Landfall expected within three tides. All small craft are advised to seek shelter. All salvage operations are advised to cease. All salvage operations are advised—*

She turned the dial and the voice smeared into static, and in the static, for a moment, she thought she heard her father's voice, the way it had gone thin and strange in the last month of his life, when he'd started talking to the sea as if it were a room full of relatives. *The water remembers, Tamsin. That's the whole problem with water. It remembers everything and forgives nothing.*

The wind had not arrived yet, but its outriders had. The air off the water had a pressure to it, a weight like a hand laid flat against her sternum. The lagoon was the color of old pewter, and the gulls were doing the thing she hated—flying inland in long, silent, purposeful lines, as if they'd received a memo the humans hadn't. The mangroves at the edge of the channel stood with their roots exposed, waiting for a tide that would drown them to the knees.

Three tides. She did the arithmetic the way her father had taught her, on her fingers, because numbers you counted on your body you didn't forget. Landfall in three tides meant the outer bands would reach them in two. It meant the window for diving the Meridian was one tide wide—maybe less, maybe a single ugly sliver of rising water and falling light—and every salvage man on the coast would be doing the same arithmetic with the same fingers.

And they would all be pointing at the same spot on the chart.

She sat down hard on the skiff's thwart, and the full shape of her mistake arrived in her chest like cold water filling a boot.

The borrowed skiff. Reyes's skiff, requisitioned from the naval depot, and every instrument aboard it wired to a depot that reported to a man who reported to a government whose founding families were inked into a lead-lined chest at the bottom of the sea. She had stolen it, yes—but she had sailed it, too. She had sailed it straight out to the true coordinates, running with the GPS chirping away in the console like a canary that couldn't stop singing, broadcasting her position, her heading, her every turn, to anyone with access to the fleet-tracking feed.

And the harbormaster had access. The harbormaster, whose new outboard engine had appeared the same week Boateng's third crane barge had arrived in port. The harbormaster, whose cousin ran the fuel dock where Boateng's fleet filled up without queuing. The coast was small. The coast was a single conversation, held over and over, in different mouths.

She had led them to it. Not the navy's false coordinates—the real ones. The place where the Meridian's hull slept with its gold fused into coral and its ledger of names waiting in the dark like a held breath.

*You lied well and hate it,* she told herself, and then: *Well. The sea has a long memory, and apparently so does a GPS unit.*

* * *

She found Kwesi at the survey team's old camp on the cay, because that was where he went when his thinking got loud—back to the place where his colleagues had vanished, as if he could interrogate the absence itself. He was crouched at the edge of the treeline with the waterlogged logbook open on his knees, pinning the curled pages flat with shells, and when he looked up at her his face did the thing it always did first: it checked her hands, then her eyes, then the horizon behind her. A man cataloguing threats. A man who had learned the hard way that threats arrive in that order.

"You've heard," she said.

"The radio's been screaming since dawn." He closed the logbook with a care that was almost tenderness. "Three tides."

"Less. The outer bands come early. My father used to say the monsoon sends its cousins ahead to see if the house is worth robbing."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive. "Then say it, Tamsin. Say what we both know."

"They know where we went."

She watched him absorb it. He was a hydrographer; he understood data the way she understood water, viscerally, as a thing with weight and direction. She saw him run the same chain she had run—the skiff, the feed, the harbormaster, the money—and arrive at the same ugly terminus.

"How long?"

"Boateng's fast boats could be on site by tomorrow's low water. The crane barges are slower, but he doesn't need the cranes for the first lift. He needs divers. He has forty of them and a decompression chamber and no conscience about either."

Kwesi stood. He was a tall man who made himself small when he was frightened, shoulders curling inward like paper near flame, and she watched him fight it, watched him straighten by an act of will. "Then we can't dive it at all. If the fleet's on top of the site—"

"We dive it before they're on top of it."

"During the surge?"

"During the surge." She heard her own voice go flat and certain, the voice she used underwater, where panic was a luxury that got people killed. "Listen to me. Boateng is a businessman. Businesses don't gamble; they hedge. He'll wait for the weather window like everyone else. He'll mass his fleet at the false coordinates first—the navy's charts—because his bought man will tell him the skiff's track ended near there, and he'll want to check the cheap answer before he trusts the expensive one. That buys us hours. Maybe six. Maybe four."

"And if he checks the track properly?"

"Then we're diving in the middle of a salvage armada in a rising sea, and we'd better be fast." She crouched, took a stick, and began to draw in the sand, because her father had taught her that way too: the sea gives nothing to a man who won't put his plans where the tide can argue with them. "Here. The site. Here—" the stick scratched a long arc westward, "—the trench. Forty meters down to a silt floor that's never been mapped because no one had a reason. The Meridian sits on a shelf above it. The shelf is old reef, Kwesi. Dead coral, honeycombed. It's been holding that ship up for two hundred years, and it's tired."

He stared at the arc. Then at her. She watched him arrive at it the way she had arrived at it, somewhere in the small hours, in the space between one wave-sound and the next.

"You're not thinking about salvage," he said slowly.

"I'm thinking about the ledger."

"And the gold?"

"The gold is the bait, Kwesi. The gold is the thing that will keep forty divers and four barges and one greedy man anchored over that shelf while the monsoon comes down on all of them. The gold is the reason they'll die." She snapped the stick and threw the halves into the water, where they spun away on the first stirrings of the long swell. "I'm not raising anything. I'm deciding what the sea keeps."

* * *

They spent the middle of the day preparing, and preparation, Tamsin had learned, was mostly a form of prayer conducted with tools.

The skiff was too conspicuous, so they moved it. There was a fishing boat beached above the tideline on the cay's lee side, an old pirogue with a patched outrigger and an engine that coughed like a smoker, and Kwesi, who could sweet-talk any machine, spent two hours with his head inside its cowling while Tamsin stripped the naval skiff of everything that mattered: tanks, regulators, the good torch, the lift bags, the coil of line, the spare charge of the cutting gear, and—wrapped in oilcloth and lashed under the thwart where it had ridden since the cave—the lead-lined chest.

She had not opened it since the hold. She had not been able to. But she checked its lashings three times, pressing her palm flat against the cold metal through the cloth, the way you'd check a wound to confirm it was still yours.

The chest weighed what truth weighs, which is more than gold.

While Kwesi coaxed the pirogue's engine, Tamsin rigged the decoys. It had been his idea first, back in the cave, and it had grown in her mind until it was the whole architecture of the plan: if the fleet was coming, then let it come to a sea full of lies. They had six marker buoys salvaged from the survey team's stores, and she weighted each one differently, anchored each at a different spot along the shelf's arc—north of the wreck, south, east over open sand where nothing lay but a starfish and its ambitions. From each buoy she hung a length of scrap iron, a broken engine block from the camp, a chain, anything that would ring against a diver's hull-lister or sing back from a magnetometer with a voice like a promise.

*Let them find six wrecks,* she thought, driving the last anchor stake with a mallet whose blows the wind was already starting to eat. *Let them argue about which one is real. Let Boateng split his divers three ways and hate every minute of it.*

It was not a plan so much as a wager, and she knew it. She stood in the shallows, the mallet hanging from her hand, and looked west to where the horizon had begun to organize itself into something with intent. The cloud bank out there was no longer a cloud bank. It was a wall, low and bruise-colored, with a hard bright line of sea beneath it, and above it the light had gone the particular yellow that old fishermen called the lion's eye and would not work under.

Her father had worked under it exactly once. He had come back with a strongbox and a story, and a year later the sea had taken him anyway, as if it had merely been waiting for a convenient tide.

*The water remembers.* She flexed her fingers around the mallet's handle. *Then let it remember this: I'm not here to take. I'm here to give back. There's no law against that. There's no fleet that chases a woman running the wrong way with treasure.*

* * *

The fleet appeared an hour before dusk, and it was worse than she had feared.

They came around the headland in a loose, arrogant line, running lights already on against the early dark, and even at three miles she could pick them out by their silhouettes the way other people picked out faces. The two crane barges, squat and patient as hippos. The fast pursuit boat with its twin engines. The supply tender riding low with fuel and compressed air. And at the center of them, the flagship—Boateng's converted trawler, the *Adomaa*, with her derrick arm raised like a forefinger pointed at the sea, saying *mine, mine, mine* to everything beneath it.

She counted through Kwesi's binoculars, lips moving. "Eleven vessels. Forty, fifty divers. Chamber on the tender." She lowered the glasses. "He brought the chamber."

"He always brings the chamber," Kwesi said quietly. "It's how he recruits. *Dive for me, boy, and if the bends take you, I'll have you fixed by morning.* It's not mercy. It's maintenance."

They watched from the treeline as the fleet slowed and began to mill, and Tamsin felt her wager start to pay out in small, tense increments. The *Adomaa* anchored first—and anchored, she noted with a fierce private satisfaction, not over the true site but two miles north of it, near her heaviest decoy buoy, where the sunken engine block lay in the sand like a metal heart waiting to be heard. A launch went down to the buoy. Men leaned over its side. A grapple went over, came up empty, went over again.

"They're biting," Kwesi breathed.

"They're chewing," she corrected. "Biting is when they commit. He'll drop divers on the decoy at first light, and when they come up with sand and nothing else, he'll work down the line of buoys. That's a full tide, Kwesi. Maybe two, if the weather makes his instruments lie to him—and the weather is going to make his instruments lie to him all night long." She turned the binoculars south, to where the true site lay under its two hundred feet of green-dark water, unmarked now, its survey buoy cut loose and sunk by her own hand days ago. "Which leaves tonight."

The wind arrived while they watched, the first real gust of it, coming off the sea in a long flat shove that bent the casuarinas along the cay's spine and made the fleet's anchorage glitter with whitecaps. The barometer on the pirogue's little console had been falling all afternoon, and now it fell faster, the needle sliding down its scale like something letting go.

Kwesi shivered, though the air was warm. "No one dives in that."

"No one sensible."

"Tamsin." He turned to face her, and in the failing light his face was all planes and shadow, and she saw in it the same thing she had seen in the cave: a man who had already lost everyone once and was terrified of the arithmetic of losing again. "The surge will be running through that hull. The visibility will be nothing. The shelf is tired, you said so yourself. If it lets go while you're under it—"

"Then it lets go while I'm under it." She said it without drama, the way you'd state the tide time. "Kwesi. Look at them." She nodded at the fleet, at the lights coming on one by one across the water, at the *Adomaa*'s derrick finger pointing at the dark. "That's what happens when a man decides the sea owes him. He brings a chamber. He brings forty boys. He brings a derrick to pick the pocket of a drowned ship. And the sea takes its fee from all of them, and it always has, and it always will, and the only question the water ever asks any of us is the same one."

"What question?"

"Whether you're coming to take or coming to give." She shouldered the dive bag, and the weight of it settled against her spine, familiar as a hand. "I know my answer. The ledger comes up tonight—before the fleet finds its feet, before the weather closes the door. One dive. One lift. And then, when the time comes—" she looked west, at the wall of the monsoon, at the lion's eye closing over the water, and felt something in her chest that was not fear, or was fear refined past all its impurities into something that could be steered, "—the gold goes down into the trench, where no crane will ever reach it, and the Meridian can finally rest the way the dead were supposed to."

Kwesi was silent a long moment. The wind pulled at them both. Out on the water, the *Adomaa*'s floodlights snapped on, washing the swell white, and somewhere across that lit distance Eze Boateng was standing on his bridge, no doubt, watching his bought sea and counting his unborn fortune.

"And if you don't come back up?" Kwesi asked.

It was the only question that mattered, and he asked it the way a hydrographer would, plainly, wanting the data.

"Then you take the chest and the pirogue and you run south, and you publish it without me, and you tell them a woman with two compasses brought it up out of the dark." She smiled, and found that the smile was real. "The sea answers to me, Kwesi. It's the one debt I've never defaulted on. Now help me launch—while the light's still ours."

The pirogue slid off the sand and into the rising water, and behind them the monsoon's wall climbed the sky, and ahead of them, under two hundred feet of darkening green, a drowned ship waited with its names and its gold, holding its breath for one tide more.
