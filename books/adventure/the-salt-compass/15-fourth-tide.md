## 15. Fourth Tide

The storm did not arrive all at once. It arrived the way debt arrives—first a whisper, then a weight, then your whole life bent under it.

Tamsin felt it in the water before she saw it in the sky. She hung at fifteen meters in the black above the Meridian's rail, her dive light swallowed to a coin of yellow, and the swell came through her body like a slow fist closing and opening. Above her, the surface was not a surface anymore but a ceiling of hammered tin, flexing, groaning, leaking threads of lightning down through the fathoms. The barometer had been falling since dusk. The harbor radio had said landfall in three tides. This was tide four by her count, and the sea already knew what was coming and had begun clearing its throat.

She checked the lift bag's line one more time. Knots were a language her father had taught her before letters, and she spoke it now with numb fingers, by feel, because looking up meant looking at that ceiling and thinking about how far away air could be.

*One chest,* she told herself. *Not the gold. Not the ship. One chest of paper.*

Paper was heavier than people thought. Lead-lined, Kwesi had said, heavier still—the merchants who packed it had wanted their names safe from salt and fire both, as if either element could be trusted to keep a secret better than men did. The chest sat in the hold beneath two centuries of collapsed decking, wedged where a beam had fallen across it like a forearm over a sleeping child's face.

Kwesi's voice crackled through the full-face mask's comm, thin and sanded down by static. "Decoy buoys one and two are set. Moving to three."

"Copy." Her own voice sounded foreign inside the helmet. "How's the surface?"

A pause long enough to mean something. "Ugly. Swell's building from the southwest. Fleet's holding off for now—they don't like these conditions any better than we do."

"But they'll come anyway."

"They'll come anyway," he agreed. "Boateng doesn't spend money on weather reports. He spends it on making weather irrelevant."

She let out a slow breath and watched the bubbles climb away from her face, wobbling up toward that hammered ceiling, and she thought: *that's what I'm doing too.* Climbing toward something that might crush her on the way.

She descended into the hold.

* * *

Inside the Meridian, the storm became music. The hull groaned a low bass note that she felt in her molars; the surge pushed through the breached stern in long exhalations, stirring up sediment in pale ghosts. Her light carved a tunnel through the murk, and everything beyond the tunnel was suggestion—ribbed shadow, the suggestion of timber, the suggestion of all the years this place had kept its mouth shut.

She had been diving wrecks since she was nineteen. She knew the grammar of them: how a ship dies, how it settles, how it lies about its own structure afterward. But the Meridian lied differently. Other wrecks felt empty, the way houses feel empty after the movers have gone. This one felt *occupied*. Two hundred souls had crossed an ocean chained in this hold, and the hold remembered. She didn't believe in ghosts the way some divers did, crossing themselves before descents, leaving rum on the rail. But she believed in memory held in wood and iron the way she believed in rust—as a force, patient and total.

The fallen beam lay where they'd left it three days ago, when they'd first opened the hold and found the gold fused into coral gardens—bars gone green and strange, grown over like eggs in a nest no bird would ever return to. Wealth that had become reef. There was a sermon in that, if she'd had time for sermons.

The chest waited beneath it. She'd rigged the lifting sling on the second dive, testing each splice twice, and now she clipped the lift bag to it and began to feed air from her pony bottle.

The bag bloomed in the dark like a pale lung.

She watched the line come taut. Watched the beam shudder. Somewhere above her, sediment rained down through her light in silver curtains, and the hull answered the strain with a sound like a whale mourning, and the chest moved—one centimeter, two—and then slid free of the debris with a grinding reluctance that made her think of every debt collector who had ever pried anything loose from her hands.

*Easy.* She guided the bag upward, hand over hand along the guideline she and Kwesi had run from hold to hatch to open water. The chest rose ahead of her, swaying, lead-lined and stubborn, carrying its cargo of names. Somewhere inside that iron skin was a page with *Okafor* written on it in a merchant clerk's careful hand. She had read it once, by lamplight, and had not slept after. She carried it now the way you carry a bone you've broken yourself—to remind you what your own strength is capable of.

Halfway up through the broken decks, her light caught movement.

Not fish. Fish scatter. This held position.

A glow, faint and green-white, maybe thirty meters off her port side, beyond the hull, outside—a diver's light, filtered through two hundred years of planking and a wall of stirred silt. Then a second glow. Then the unmistakable pulse of a surface-supplied umbilical catching the current, snaking past a porthole like a black eel investigating a wound.

Boateng's divers. In this swell. Already.

Her heart went from steady to hammering so fast she could hear it in the mask, and for one white-hot moment terror took her completely—cold, total, the old animal panic that had nearly drowned her at twenty-two when her regulator iced free in the Canary Current. She hung motionless in the water, chest rising and falling too quickly, watching the lights drift along the outside of the hull like lanterns carried by men walking a fence line.

Then her father's voice, the one that lived under her sternum, said what it always said in the water: *Panic spends air. Fear spends nothing. Be afraid, and keep working.*

She killed her light.

Darkness closed over her like a hand. For ten seconds she floated blind, listening—the comm hissing, her own breath, the hull's groaning—and mapped the world by sound and memory instead of sight. The guideline ran up and to starboard, through the tween deck gap, out the cargo hatch. The divers' lights swept along the outer hull at roughly midship height. They were inspecting, not penetrating. Not yet. They'd found the site because the skiff's borrowed radio had betrayed her coordinates, and now they were walking the Meridian's skin like buyers at an auction, assessing.

They would find the breach at the stern eventually. Everyone always found the way in. Wrecks want to be entered; that was the terrible truth of them.

She moved.

Hand over hand along the guideline, no light, feeling for the knots Kwesi had tied at five-meter intervals—two wraps meaning straight, a single hitch meaning obstacle. Her world shrank to touch. The chest rose ahead of her, invisible now, a presence on the line more than a sight, tugging gently upward like something eager to leave. She understood it. She wanted to leave too.

The lights outside swung. Swept closer. Through a shattered gunport she saw them clearly for one heartbeat—three divers in commercial gear, hard-hat rigs, their umbilicals trailing back up toward a floodlight halo on the surface where Boateng's crane barge sat anchored directly over the grave. Directly over it. They had anchored on top of the dead without knowing they stood on a graveyard, or knowing perfectly well and not caring, which amounted to the same thing in the ledger of the world.

One of the divers turned his head. His light lanced through the gunport, through the water where she hung—

—and she dropped.

Not a swim. A fall, controlled, releasing the lift line and letting negative buoyancy take her down past the gunport's angle, folding herself behind a rib of the hull as the beam of light probed the space she'd occupied half a second earlier. It lingered. Moved on. Came back. These were not tourists; these were Boateng's best, men who cleared forty thousand cedis a season to find things other people wanted hidden, and the one at the glass was not satisfied.

He reached through the gunport with a gloved hand and played his light along the interior.

Tamsin pressed herself into the curve of the rib, chest against cold iron, and made herself small in the way only poor people and prey animals know how. Her breath rasped. She slowed it by force. In through the nose, out slow, the way Papa had taught her when she was nine and scared of the deep end: *the sea can hear a frightened heart, Tamsin. Give it nothing to hear.*

The light passed over her fin tips. Passed over the guideline. Paused on the guideline.

Of course. The guideline. A line running into the dark interior of a wreck nobody had dived until this week was a signature as good as a name. He tugged it, twice, testing, and Tamsin felt the tug travel down the rope into her gripping hand like someone knocking on the door of her body.

Above her, unseen, the chest continued its slow ascent on the lift bag, alone, climbing toward a surface full of enemies.

* * *

"Kwesi." She kept her voice flat, barely above a whisper, though whispering underwater was superstition—sound carried too well for softness to matter. "Three divers on the hull. Surface-supplied. Crane barge anchored on station."

Static. Then: "I see their lights from here. I'm at decoy four, north of the trench marker." A pause. "Tamsin. Tell me you're not still inside."

"The chest is on the lift bag. It should be breaking surface about now, fifty meters west of your position, unless—" She did the arithmetic and hated the answer. "Unless their topside crew sees it come up. Which they will. Their floodlights cover that water."

Another pause, longer. When Kwesi spoke again, his voice had the careful evenness of a man standing very still on dangerous ground. "So we have perhaps four minutes before someone in a RIB runs out and puts a hook on that bag."

"Less. Boateng pays for speed."

"Then give me a problem worth four minutes."

She looked around the darkness of the tween deck, at the wreck she had memorized the way other people memorize scripture, and her fear turned over, the way a wave turns, and became something else. Something colder and more useful. Terror was fuel; her whole life had taught her to refine it fast.

"The engine room," she said. "There's a compartment aft of the boilers—old fuel bunker, steel, mostly intact. If someone opens the seacocks, it floods fast and heavy and it'll pull a slick of trapped oil and sediment out through the bilge vents. Black water bloom. From the surface it'll look like the wreck is bleeding—like something big broke open down there. Every diver they have will converge on it, because Boateng's people are trained to read trouble as treasure."

"You want to sink their attention."

"I want to *spend* their attention. It's the only currency they respect." She was already moving, pulling herself aft along the guideline's anchor point, into the section of the Meridian she knew best because it was the section most like the wrecks she'd grown up salvaging—engine rooms, machinery spaces, the guts of things. "Their divers are at midship. The engine room is seventy meters aft. Even if they turn immediately, the oil cloud will take minutes to develop and longer to disperse, and in this swell their topside won't risk pulling divers and repositioning. They'll investigate from where they are."

"And you?"

"I'm going through the hull breach at the stern. The chest comes up west, I go out east, we meet at the skiff." She reached the bunker compartment and found the seacock wheel exactly where the plans of ships like this one always put it, furred with a century of tubeworms. She wrapped both hands around it. "Kwesi. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does, the ledger matters more than either of us. Get it to the journalists. All of it. Don't edit me out of the story and don't edit my family in. Just the names. All the names."

"Tamsin—"

"Turn the wheel with me, Kwesi. On three. I need your hands on the other side of the hull—there's an access plate over the valve stem, welded flange, you'll see the seam."

Silence for three seconds. Then, quietly, the sound of a man deciding: "Going."

* * *

What followed was the strangest collaboration of her life: two people turning one valve from opposite sides of a dead ship's skin, unable to see each other, connected by a comm signal that kept fraying like old rope. She braced her boots against the bulkhead and hauled; somewhere beyond the steel, Kwesi hauled too, and between them the seacock gave—a shriek of calcium-scarred metal, then a grind, then the deep hydraulic gulp of the sea being invited into a room built to keep it out.

Black water bloomed.

She felt it happen before she saw it—a pressure shift, a cold tongue curling around her fins—and then her light showed the bunker filling with ink, oil rising in iridescent sheets, sediment boiling up off the deck plates. The Meridian exhaled through its wounds, a great slow hemorrhage of two hundred years of preserved darkness, and the cloud rolled out through the bilge vents and the broken plating and spread into the night water like smoke from a burning house.

Through the comm, distant and distorted, she heard the salvage barge's topside erupt—shouts, a whistle, engines. The divers' lights at midship swung aft, converging, hungry. Men trained to smell money in misfortune, moving toward the smell.

*Go,* she told herself. *Now, while the whole ocean is looking at the wrong thing.*

She swam for the stern breach.

The swell had strengthened while she worked; she felt it even down here, the whole wreck flexing around her like a living thing in pain, plates ticking, rivets popping somewhere overhead with sounds like distant gunfire. The breach gaped ahead—a wound torn in the hull decades ago, edges furred with coral, wide enough to drive a skiff through if skiffs drove underwater. Beyond it: open water, black and heaving, lit fitfully by the distant glare of the crane barge.

And hanging in that glare, sixty meters up, silhouetted against the surface like a moon made of money—the lift bag. Rising. Steady. With the chest beneath it, climbing through enemy light.

Two of Boateng's divers were already ascending toward it.

"No," she said aloud, and the word made a bubble that wobbled up past her face, and she kicked off through the breach into the open sea.

The surface was war. She came up in the trough of a swell that lifted her ten feet and dropped her into shadow, and in the strobing flashes between wave crests she saw it all: the crane barge blazing with work lights, men scrambling on its deck; a RIB launching off its davit, bow slapping down hard, driver gunning for the lift bag; Kwesi's skiff, dark and unlit, bobbing a hundred meters west with Kwesi himself hauling the chest over the gunwale with hands she could see shaking even at this distance; and above it all the clouds, lower now, banded and boiling, the monsoon's leading edge dragging its fingers across the water.

The RIB was going to reach him before he got the bag aboard.

Tamsin did the only thing left. She pulled her own surface marker, yanked the inflator, and shot her safety sausage up beside the RIB's track—then dove again, deep, fast, and swam directly under the boat's hull, banging it twice with her tank, the universal diver's distress signal, forcing the driver to throttle down and look, because even Boateng's men stopped for a diver alone in building seas. Liability. Paperwork. Even greed keeps an actuary.

In the thirty seconds she bought, Kwesi cut the bag free, let the empty lift balloon fly off into the dark like a runaway ghost, and dropped the chest below his gunwale under a tarp of netting. By the time the RIB's searchlight swept his skiff, he was just a lone fisherman in bad weather, holding up a thermos, waving cheerfully, cursing fluently in three languages about the state of the sea.

Tamsin surfaced on the far side of the skiff, out of the light, and climbed aboard with arms that had stopped being arms and become rope.

* * *

They ran without lights for an hour, Kwesi at the tiller reading the swells the way old sailors read faces, Tamsin bailing with a bucket because the seams were weeping and the wind was backing and every kilometer south was a kilometer the storm hadn't reached yet. Behind them, the crane barge's lights burned on over the Meridian, and occasionally a flare went up—Boateng's people signaling, searching, furious at a black cloud and an empty lift bag and a night full of decoy buoys blinking their idiot hearts out over empty water.

It wasn't until they were past the shoal line, with rain beginning to arrive in fat, warm, intermittent drops, that Tamsin knelt on the deck boards, peeled back the netting, and put her bare hand flat on the chest's iron lid.

Cold. Wet. Real.

Inside it: the names. The gold's true manifest, the part that had never been weighed. Her family's name among them, written in a clerk's careful colonial hand, priced and filed like cargo.

"We have it," Kwesi said. He was soaked through, grinning and gray-lipped with exhaustion, and he looked younger than she'd ever seen him and older too, both at once. "We actually have it."

Tamsin looked back north, at the smudge of the barge lights dying behind the rain, and felt no triumph. What she felt was the swell still moving through her bones, and the knowledge settling into place like a stone into mud: they had the ledger, yes—but Boateng had her coordinates confirmed, her skiff identified, and a monsoon coming in behind which he could hide anything, including murder.

And somewhere in that fleet, she was certain, Eze Boateng himself was drying his hands and smiling, because a man like that did not lose a chest of paper. He simply added it to what he was owed.

The rain thickened. Kwesi turned the skiff south, into the dark, and the sea rose up around them like a fist learning to close.
