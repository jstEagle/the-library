## 16. Fifth Tide

The storm did not arrive so much as it was revealed—peeled back out of the sea like something that had been waiting beneath the surface all along, patient as a debt.

Tamsin felt it first in her teeth. She had been asleep in the lee of the cay with the ledger chest locked under her arm and a tarp over her head, and she woke to a pressure behind her eyes, a low ringing hum that had nothing to do with sound. Old divers called it the water's warning. Her father had called it God clearing his throat. She sat up, spat salt, and looked east.

The horizon had gone the color of a bruise going bad.

Wind came off it in long flat gusts that bent the palms along the spit until they hissed like a crowd shushing itself. The swell had turned from blue to green to a deep, ugly olive, and where yesterday there had been six or seven of Boateng's vessels strung across the site like beads on a wire, now there was smoke and spray and confusion. She could see them scattering through the rain-haze—one tug running for the sheltered anchorage with its crane boom lashed down, two dive boats wallowing beam-on to the seas, their crews scrambling to recover lines before the swells snapped them. A barge had dragged anchor and was sliding sideways toward the reef shelf, its hull groaning loud enough to carry even over the wind.

The fleet was breaking apart. Not destroyed—Boateng's money didn't destroy easily—but scattered, delayed, forced to spend its strength just staying afloat.

Tamsin stood at the tideline and let the wind push against her chest, and she counted what she had left. One compass—her own, her father's brass needle still swinging true and seaward. One ledger chest, lead-lined, heavier than sin. One borrowed skiff, beached above the high-water mark with a hairline crack in the transom she hadn't told Kwesi about. And one man, currently asleep under the tarp twenty meters back, breathing in the shallow rhythm of someone who had finally stopped running.

Kwesi had rigged the decoy buoys through the worst of the fourth tide, working half-blind in the surge, and he'd come up shaking so hard she'd had to hold him steady against the gunwale while he coughed half the Atlantic out of his lungs. He'd earned this sleep. She wouldn't wake him yet.

She looked at the skiff and made her decision the way she made most decisions: quickly, and then lived inside it.

They couldn't run. The crack in the transom would open like a mouth in these seas, and the harbor radio had been screaming landfall warnings since dawn—three tides, maybe less, before the monsoon's leading edge came ashore and closed every window on this coast. They couldn't hide; Boateng's enforcers had already walked the cay twice looking for them, and Reyes's patrol boat sat on the northern horizon like a held breath. What they could do was wait, and watch, and keep the chest dry.

So Tamsin Okafor waited, and watched, and kept the chest dry, while the fifth tide came in around her ankles and the world went gray.

* * *

It was midday when the black tender crossed the reef channel.

She saw it from two hundred meters—a low, powerful RIB cutting through the confused seas with an arrogance no other vessel on the water could afford, taking waves that would swamp anything smaller as though they were suggestions. It ran straight past the wreck site, straight past the struggling dive boats, and came directly for the cay. For her beach. For her skiff.

Three men in it. And standing in the bow, one hand on the rail, a broad silhouette in a yellow foul-weather coat that she would have known anywhere on this coast, because she had seen it hanging in the harbormaster's office, in the bank manager's waiting room, in the doorway of her own shack on the morning they'd come to inventory her father's tools against her debt.

Eze Boateng did not send men when he could come himself.

Tamsin woke Kwesi with a hand over his mouth. His eyes opened the way a drowned man's do—wide, panicked, searching—and she pressed one finger to her lips and pointed down the beach. He understood. He slid backward into the palm scrub without a word, taking the flare pistol and the knife, and left her alone at the tideline with the chest and the choice she'd been making since the day her father's lungs filled with water.

She could have run. There was time, maybe. But the chest weighed forty kilos and the soft sand took your legs, and Boateng's men would have caught her in the open with witnesses nowhere, and men like Eze Boateng handled problems differently when nobody was watching. Better to stand. Standing was the only thing she'd ever owned outright.

The tender grounded in the shallows and the three enforcers waded ashore, and then Boateng stepped down into knee-deep water without waiting for help, which surprised her. She had expected him to be carried. Instead he walked up the beach through the surf with his coat streaming, a big man gone soft at the edges but still carrying himself like a man who had never once been told no by anything that mattered, and stopped ten feet from her, and smiled.

"Tamsin Okafor." He said her name the way a man reads a figure off a balance sheet. "You know, I've been buying debts on this coast for thirty years, and you are the only person who ever made me come stand in the rain myself."

"Could have stayed home," she said. "I hear the weather's better."

He laughed—a real laugh, deep and unbothered, and that frightened her more than the enforcers flanking him with their hands loose near their hips. Men who laughed at insults were men who had already decided how things would end.

"Look at you." He gestured at her—at the salt-crusted clothes, the rope-burned hands, the chest sitting between her and him like a third party in the negotiation. "Half-drowned, hunted by my people, hunted by the navy, sitting on a beach that will be underwater by tomorrow night. And still standing there like the ocean owes you something." He shook his head, almost fondly. "Your father was the same way. Did you know that? Kofi Okafor stood on a deck once and told me to my face that some things belonged to the dead. Cost him everything. Cost you everything, if we're honest about whose debt you've been diving to pay."

"Don't say his name."

"Why not? I knew him better than you did, girl. I knew him in the years when he still talked about the Meridian like it was a religion." Boateng took another step closer, boots crunching on wet sand, and lowered his voice as though the enforcers were not his own men. "Here is what I know. You have the documents. My divers found the marker buoys you planted—the real ones, not the toys you floated for my fools—and they found the survey team's equipment cached in the sea cave, and they found drag marks on the sand inside it. Two sets of footprints, Tamsin. You and the hydrographer who died on paper four months ago. So don't insult us both by pretending the chest at your feet is full of fishing charts."

The wind gusted. Rain came in slantwise, needling her neck. Behind Boateng, the scattered fleet crawled through the murk like wounded animals, and somewhere beyond them the navy ship held its station, watching, always watching.

"So here is my offer," Boateng said. "And understand that I am only making it once, because the tide is rising and I have a flagship anchored over the richest hole in West African water and a window closing faster than any window I have ever worked against." He spread his hands, and the rain ran silver off his sleeves. "Every cedi you owe me—gone. Every lien on your father's estate, every tool they inventoried, every debt your name has carried since you were nineteen years old—erased. Not deferred. Erased. And beyond that: a vessel. A proper salvage vessel, not that floating coffin you call a workboat. A crew. A license with my name behind it, which on this coast means a license that never gets questioned. Your own fleet, Tamsin. Your own flag. Everything your father worked for and never touched, handed to you tomorrow morning, signed and sealed."

"And all you want," she said quietly, "is the truth."

"All I want," said Eze Boateng, "is the asset. Call it whatever sentimental word you like. That chest contains the location of forty tons of gold and the legal architecture to claim it clean—provenance, ownership chains, insurance records, everything a colonial government wrote down because they were arrogant enough to think their paper would outlive their empire. It did. It's sitting in a lead box on a beach, and I am the only man alive positioned to lift it before the sea takes the site for good." His eyes were pale and patient and absolutely certain. "You cannot raise that gold yourself. You cannot protect those papers from Reyes, from me, from the storm. You are holding a winning lottery ticket in a flood, girl, and I am offering you dry ground and a future. This is not a threat. This is arithmetic."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her chin. Somewhere behind her in the scrub, she knew, Kwesi was listening, and she hoped he was hearing what she heard—not the offer, but the shape underneath it. The way Boateng talked about the ledger as paper and the gold as tonnage and the dead as provenance. The way he said *girl* like a man pricing a commodity.

"You're right about one thing," she said. "My father told you some things belong to the dead. He was wrong, though. It's not some things." She put her hand flat on the chest lid, feeling the cold of the lead through the wet canvas. "It's all of them. Every name in that book. Every family that got fat. Including mine—I've read it, Eze. I know what an Okafor was doing in 1841, and I know what the gold in that hold paid for, and I know that if I sell it to you, I'm just the last entry in the ledger. Another collaborator. Another signature."

Something moved behind Boateng's eyes—not anger, exactly. Recalculation. A man watching an asset fail to appreciate.

"That's a beautiful speech," he said. "Speeches don't clear debts."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But they tell you who you're dealing with."

Boateng sighed, and the warmth went out of him all at once, like a lamp turned down. When he spoke again his voice was almost gentle, which was the most frightening register he owned.

"I offered you a future," he said. "You chose a box of dead men's confessions. Fine. But understand the terms of what happens next." He nodded, and the enforcer on his right stepped forward and held out an open palm, and Boateng took from it a small object wrapped in oilcloth that Tamsin recognized a half-second too late.

Her shack. Her dockside. The strongbox she'd pried open on the second night, the twin compass with its needle pointing seaward—he had it. He'd had it since the raid on her camp at the anchorage, since the night she'd thought she'd hidden everything that mattered. She watched him unwrap it with thick careful fingers, and the brass caught what little light the storm allowed, and her stomach dropped through the sand and into the sea.

"You have two keys," Boateng said. "Now you have one. And one key opens nothing—it only points. Points at a hole in the ocean that my flagship is already anchored over, with cranes rated deeper than anything your father ever dreamed of, and a dive schedule that starts when this squall breaks." He tucked the twin compass into his coat, over his heart, like a man pocketing a watch. "So here is your deadline, Tamsin Okafor. Sixth tide. Landfall comes, the sea closes, and whatever is still down there belongs to whoever's holding position when the window shuts. Bring me the ledger before the sixth tide turns, and everything I promised stands—debt, vessel, fleet, future. Don't bring it, and I raise the gold without your permission and spend the rest of my life making sure the names in that book stay exactly as drowned as the men who wrote them." He turned to go, then paused, and glanced back at her with something that might have been respect in a harder man. "Your father made the same choice, you know. Kept his little compass. Kept his principles. And the sea took him anyway, and the debt came to you instead. Principles are expensive in this country, girl. I hope you can afford them."

He waded back to the tender. The enforcers followed. The engine roared, and the black RIB carved away into the rain, and Tamsin stood alone on the beach with one compass, one chest, and a deadline measured in tides.

* * *

Kwesi came out of the scrub when the tender was a smudge on the horizon. He didn't ask what happened—he'd heard enough—but he stood beside her at the tideline, dripping, and after a while he said, "He took the second key."

"He took the second key."

"Which means he doesn't actually know the true coordinates. He knows the site roughly—from the buoys, from the cave—but the triangulation needs both instruments, and he only has one." Kwesi frowned out at the water, and Tamsin watched the hydrographer surface in him, the man who read oceans for a living. "Unless he thinks the navy's charts are accurate. Unless he anchors over the wrong depression and spends the sixth tide dredging sand."

"He'll find the wreck eventually," Tamsin said. "He has divers, sonar, time enough before landfall. One key plus patience equals the same answer as two keys plus skill. Just slower." She turned the remaining compass over in her hands. The brass was warm from her body, the needle trembling seaward with a persistence that felt less like magnetism than insistence. "But slower is something. Slower is a tide. Maybe two."

"And what do we do with our tide?"

Tamsin looked at the chest. Forty kilos of lead and paper and the whole rotten architecture of a fortune, sitting in the rain on a beach that would soon be gone. She thought about her father's voice saying *some things belong to the dead*, and about the entry that read *Okafor* in faded iron-gall ink, and about the look on Boateng's face when he'd pocketed the compass—not greed, exactly. Relief. The relief of a man who believed everything in the world could be bought if you simply named the right price, and who had just watched someone refuse to name one.

"We stop running," she said. "We stop hiding. We take this chest out to the site ourselves, tonight, in the worst water either of us has ever dived—and we decide, once and for all, whether the truth gets sold, gets published, or goes back to the bottom where it came from." She met Kwesi's eyes. "Because if I leave that decision to Boateng, or to Reyes, or to the storm, then I'm just another heir spending an inheritance I didn't earn. And I have spent my whole life being owed to and owing. I'm done being both."

Kwesi was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "The transom on this skiff is cracked."

"I know."

"It won't make the crossing in this swell."

"I know that too." Tamsin shouldered her end of the chest and nodded inland, toward the cay's far side, where the survey team's abandoned camp still stood—generators, fuel drums, and, if the logbook had told her true, a rigid inflatable with a hull rating far beyond anything she'd ever owned. "But dead men left us a better boat. Come on. We've got one tide to steal it, and the sixth tide coming fast."

Behind them, the sea rose against the sand, patient as a creditor, and began to take the beach back.
