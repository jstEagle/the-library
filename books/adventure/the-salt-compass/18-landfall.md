## 18. Landfall

The monsoon did not arrive so much as descend.

Tamsin felt it first in her teeth, a pressure like a thumb pressed against the hinge of her jaw, and then in the harbor itself—the water going strange and glassy for an hour before dawn, the fishing boats hauled up on the sand like animals that knew better than to argue with the sky. The radio had been screaming since midnight: *landfall within six hours, all small craft seek shelter, harbor closed, harbor closed, harbor closed.*

She stood at the window of Kwesi's rented room with her father's compass open in her palm and watched the horizon turn the color of an old bruise.

"They're not hauling up," she said.

Kwesi came to stand beside her, a mug of tea forgotten in his hand. Beyond the breakwater, past the line of beached pirogues and shuttered fuel depots, three vessels rode at anchor in the outer roads—Boateng's fleet, lights burning through the murk, cranes already swinging out over their decks like the arms of men rolling up their sleeves.

"The harbormaster sold him the window," Kwesi said. "I told you he would."

"He's not riding it out. He's working it." She turned the compass over. The needle swung lazily, seaward, indifferent to the storm gathering above it. "Look at his positioning. He's not anchored for weather. He's anchored *on station*." She handed the compass to Kwesi and pointed. "There. The big one. That's the *Adinkra Star*. And she is sitting directly over the coordinates I gave Reyes."

Kwesi went very still. "The false ones."

"No." Her mouth was dry. "The true ones. The ones I never gave anyone." She thought of the borrowed skiff, its cheap VHF set, the mayday relay she'd made during the escape from Reyes's custody—coordinates shouted into an open channel while the barometer fell, because there had been no other way to get the patrol boats off their tail. She'd told herself the channel would be drowned in static by now, that no one sane would be listening. But Boateng hadn't gotten rich by being sane. He'd gotten rich by listening when everyone else was running.

"He bribed the harbormaster for the recording," she said. "Or one of his captains heard it live. Either way—he knows where we were pointing."

Kwesi set down the mug. It rattled against the sill. "Then we've lost."

"We haven't lost anything yet." She was already moving, pulling her dive bag from under the cot, checking seals by touch because the light was failing fast. "He knows the coordinates. He doesn't know the site. He doesn't know the hull is broken along the port side, or that the hold sits under forty meters of collapsing sand, or that his divers will need slack water inside a surge cycle that isn't going to give them any. And he doesn't know about the trench."

"The trench is a hundred and ten meters," Kwesi said quietly. "Nobody works that depth in a monsoon."

"Nobody works *any* of this in a monsoon. That's the whole point." She zipped the bag. "He thinks the sea is closing a door. I'm telling you the sea is opening one. For about four hours, every other boat in this water will be hiding behind the breakwater, and the only sonar picture on this coast will belong to a man who's too busy counting gold bars to look at what's swimming underneath him."

Kwesi looked at her for a long moment. Rain began to fall—not the warm scattered drops of the afternoon but a hard diagonal sheet, drumming on the tin roof like applause from a hostile crowd.

"You want to go out in that," he said.

"I want to go out in that *first*," Tamsin said. "While his divers are still rigging. While his crane operators are still arguing about load limits. There's a gap between landfall and full storm—maybe two hours, maybe less—when the wind will be building but the swell won't have organized yet. If we're on site before the *Adinkra Star* finishes anchoring properly, we can work beneath her shadow. His sonar will read us as debris. Everything floating off a wreck in weather like this reads as debris."

"And if it doesn't?"

She slung the bag over her shoulder and met his eyes. "Then we find out how fast we are."

* * *

They stole the skiff openly, which was to say they walked down to the fisherman who leased it, put money in his hand—most of what remained of Tamsin's cash—and told him the truth: that they needed a boat in weather no sane person would sail, and that they could not say why, and that if they didn't come back he should tell no one they'd gone. The old man looked at the sky, then at the money, then at Tamsin's face, and something in it must have reminded him of someone, because he said only, "Bring back my engine even if you don't bring back yourselves," and turned away to batten his own shutters.

The harbor mouth was a gauntlet. Inside the breakwater the water lay flat and oily, but the moment they cleared the stone arms the world changed—the swell already running at two meters and building, gray-green walls lifting the skiff and setting her down again with a slap that jarred Tamsin's spine. Rain hit her face like flung gravel. Kwesi took the tiller while she navigated, both compasses' worth of knowledge compressed now into one brass instrument and a waterproof chart case strapped to her thigh, his survey data transcribed onto her slate in figures she'd memorized twice over.

"Bearing!" Kwesi shouted over the wind.

"One-nine-five! Hold her at one-nine-five until the needle settles!"

The compass sat in its housing on the thwart, lashed down with cord, and Tamsin watched it the way other sailors watched the horizon. The needle did not point north. It pointed seaward, toward the Meridian, toward everything her father had died close to and never reached—and in the pitching gray light it seemed to tremble, not from the boat's motion but from eagerness, as if the instrument itself had waited sixty years for someone foolish enough to follow it into a storm.

The fleet fell astern by degrees. Through the rain she could see the *Adinkra Star*'s deck lights smeared into long yellow wounds across the water, and once, briefly, the flare of a welding torch as Boateng's crew modified something—a lift frame, a cutting gantry—against the clock. They were working. They were working *now*, in weather that would ground every legitimate operation on the coast, because Eze Boateng had done the same arithmetic she had: the sea was closing a door, and whatever wasn't through it when it shut was lost forever.

"He'll have divers in the water within the hour," Kwesi called back. "Maybe sooner. He won't wait for the anchor pattern to settle."

"Neither will we."

The swell grew. Three meters, then four, the skiff climbing faces of water that stood up green and translucent before dropping them into troughs deep enough to hide the world. Tamsin wedged herself against the console and let her body learn the rhythm the way she'd learned it as a girl diving wrecks off her father's boat—don't fight the sea's timing, join it, breathe when it breathes, move when it moves. Fear was there, cold and specific in her chest, but beneath it ran something else, something older and stranger: the feeling she always got on the threshold of a dive, the sense of standing at the edge of a country where she was more herself than she ever was on land.

The needle steadied. One-nine-five held.

"There!" Kwesi pointed. Ahead, half-veiled in rain, a shape rose from the water that was not water—a buoy, riding high on its mooring, its strobe blinking with desperate regularity. The survey team's marker, snagged on the Meridian's rail these many tides ago, still doing its patient duty for masters who were dead or fled.

And beyond it, dimly, the fleet.

The *Adinkra Star* loomed out of the murk like a cliff that had learned to float, floodlights blazing down her sides, her crane boom extended over the stern, a cluster of orange-suited figures swarming her dive station. She had anchored directly over the site, exactly as Tamsin had predicted, her hull a great iron lid nailed down over the Meridian's grave. Beneath her keel, somewhere in the churning dark, the colonial ship lay waiting with her gold and her ledger and her thousand names.

"Sonar shadow," Tamsin shouted. "We go under her port quarter. Cut the engine."

Kwesi killed the motor and the skiff became a leaf, surging and wallowing, and they shipped paddles and pulled by hand, silently, into the acoustic blind spot beneath the flagship's hull. From this close, the vessel's sounds came down through the water like weather of another kind: the groan of her anchor chain, the thud of machinery, and—distinct, rhythmic, terrible—the splash-and-hiss of divers entering the water off her stern platform. One. Two. A pause. Three.

"They're going down," Kwesi whispered.

"Of course they are." Tamsin checked her gauge, her mask, the knife at her calf. Below them the water was black and getting blacker, the last daylight strangled by the storm cloud overhead. "Boateng told them the sea closes the window. What he didn't tell them is what the sea does to men who dive a monsoon surge at depth." She thought of the men she knew on those crews—young Kofi from the fuel dock, the twins from Tema who'd taught her to run a hook line—and her jaw tightened. "Greed bends faster than nitrogen. Let's hope it bends slower than steel."

She rolled off the gunwale into the dark.

* * *

The water took her the way it always did—cold shock, then silence, then the long slow settling of her body into its second language. Ten meters down the chaos above faded to a distant drumming, the storm translated into pressure and pulse. Twenty meters and the floodlights of the *Adinkra Star* became pale ghosts overhead, wavering columns of illumination that did not reach the bottom but only stirred it, like lanterns carried through fog.

She leveled off at thirty-five and hung in the gray, listening.

There. To seaward: the rasp of exhaust bubbles, the stutter of dive lights sweeping the seafloor. Boateng's divers were on the move, fanning out from the flagship's shadow in pairs, their torches carving frantic cones through the suspended silt. They were looking for the wreck the way men look for a thing they've been promised—fast, greedy, badly. In a monsoon surge, at depth, with visibility measured in arm-lengths, speed was suicide. She watched one pair swim directly over a field of debris and miss it entirely, their lights sliding across iron that had been waiting since 1849.

Kwesi descended beside her, and together they moved on the opposite bearing, slow and low, following the compass needle in her hand like a pilgrim following a relic. The needle pointed unerringly seaward, through the murk, through the noise, and Tamsin followed it with the particular calm that came over her only underwater, where the debts and the collectors and the fleets all dissolved and there was nothing left in the world but pressure, breath, and direction.

The Meridian announced herself before they saw her: a change in the texture of the seabed, silt giving way to ballast stone, then the sudden vertical wall of her hull rising out of the gloom like the foundation of a drowned cathedral. Tamsin's light found the rail, the marker buoy's snagged line, the great wound along the port side where the sea had been entering her hold for a century and a half.

And above her, faint but unmistakable, a new sound: the thrum of a winch engaging, transmitted down the *Adinkra Star*'s anchor chain into the bedrock of the site. They were lowering something. A frame. A grapple. Men were reaching into the hold even now.

Tamsin looked at Kwesi through the murk. He held up his slate: *CHARGES?*

She shook her head. Not yet. Not tonight—that was tomorrow's work, the scuttling, the final tide. Tonight there was only one thing left to do before the storm swallowed everything: confirm the hold, fix the charges' placement, and get out alive with the knowledge in her head and the ledger's location written nowhere but her memory.

Her light swept along the hull toward the hold entrance—and stopped.

A diver's light was already inside.

Not Boateng's orange-suited commercial crews. This light moved differently—slow, methodical, alone. A single figure, working without a tether, without a buddy, drifting through the hold entrance with the unhurried economy of someone who had been there before.

Tamsin's blood went cold beneath her suit.

Because she knew that rhythm. She had seen it a hundred times, in mirrors, in her father's old film reels, in every diver who ever loved a wreck more than he feared it.

Someone else had come to the Meridian alone, ahead of the fleet, ahead of the storm—and whoever it was had just switched off their light, as if they knew they were no longer alone.
