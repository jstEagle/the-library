## 19. Scuttle

The sea had turned the color of bruised fruit, and Tamsin Okafor went down into it anyway.

She rolled off the gunwale of the borrowed skiff into water that hit her like a slap—warm on the surface, cold as a lie beneath. The monsoon wind was already on them, dragging white lace across the swells, and above her the *Meridian*'s position was a chaos of anchors and hulls, Boateng's flagship squatting over the wreck like a vulture that had learned patience. Floodlights stabbed down through the murk in trembling columns, turning the water into something like a cathedral, something like a trap.

Kwesi's face appeared over the edge of the skiff, masked in spray. "Comms are garbage past thirty meters," he shouted. "You go dark, you're alone down there."

"I've been alone down there my whole life," she said, and let go.

The descent swallowed her.

She had made a thousand dives and this was the first one that felt like walking into a house where someone was waiting. The surge reached her at ten meters and began to work her body, shoving her sideways, hauling her back, the sea deciding whether it wanted her. She rode it the way her father had taught her—not against it, not with it, but *inside* it, a leaf in a river that happened to know where the river was going.

Below her, the fleet's divers hung in the floodlights like moths, a dozen of them, kicking up silt in great brown clouds as they fought the wreck's hatches and the storm's schedule both. She could hear them through the water—compressed-air shrieks, the clang of tools on coral-cased iron, the wordless language of men who had run out of time. Boateng had spent the night bribing the weather and the harbor master and God, and now he was spending his divers the same way, one breath at a time.

Tamsin angled away from the lights, into the wreck's shadow side, where the hull fell away into black and the sonar shadow of the flagship's own mass gave her cover. The charges rode her harness in a canvas bag, four of them, scavenged from a decommissioned dredge and wired by Kwesi's careful hands. Enough to crack what the sea had already spent two hundred years weakening. Not enough to be called a bomb by anyone who wasn't looking.

The *Meridian*'s hull rose out of the dark to meet her, and even now, even with everything, her breath caught.

She had touched it once before, in the first tide of all this, when the site was still hers and secret. Then it had been a shape, a mystery, a promise. Now it was a grave she had read the headstone of. She knew the names in the hold. She knew her own name was among them, inked in a clerk's careful colonial hand: *Okafor, coastal agent, palm oil and provisions, account settled in kind.* Her family had fed on this ship's cargo the way coral feeds on iron—slowly, thoroughly, without ever admitting it was eating.

The hull was furred with a century and a half of growth, soft as velvet in places, knife-edged in others. She swam the length of it by touch and memory, counting frames, until she found the seam Kwesi's survey data had promised: the fracture line where the old ship had grounded, partially, before sliding to its rest, a scar running forty meters along her flank like a badly healed wound. The coral had stitched it shut. The coral would not hold.

She planted the first charge in the scar's deepest point, pressing the sticky composite against bare iron where a plate had sloughed away, and worked the detonator wire back along the hull, anchoring it every meter with tape that fought the current like a living thing. The surge had built since she'd left the surface. It picked her up and set her down against the wreck, hard, and she felt a rib of coral open her forearm through the suit, a bright cold line of pain. Blood in the water. She thought, absurdly, of her father's compass needle, always swinging seaward, always pointing home to this.

"Four minutes of work," she told herself, and it was a lie, and she kept working anyway.

* * *

Above her, Eze Boateng stood on the bridge of the *Prosperity's Answer* and watched his money try to drown.

He was not a man who prayed, but he was a man who negotiated, and he stood at the window negotiating with the storm now, the way he negotiated with everything—firmly, with the assumption of eventual victory. Below, in the floodlit water, his divers worked the *Meridian*'s main hatch in pairs, hauling the cutting rig by hand because the crane operator had refused a fourth lift in the building swell. The gold was down there. He could feel it the way other men felt weather in their joints: four hundred bars, coral-fused, patient, *his* by right of the only law he had ever respected, which was the law of who got there and who didn't.

The harbormaster's man had given him the coordinates two nights ago, and Boateng had known even as he paid that the numbers were Tamsin Okafor's gift and Tamsin Okafor's trap, and he had come anyway, because the alternative was to let a debt-ridden girl with a dead man's compass outlast him, and he would rather drown.

He watched the water. He did not see, in the black beyond his own lights, the small shape moving along the hull with a bag of wire.

What he saw was his dive supervisor's face when the man came up the ladder, gray-lipped, shouting over the wind: "Current's turning, sir. Surge is going to be past safe limits in an hour. Maybe less. We should pull the teams."

"We should," Boateng agreed pleasantly, "raise the gold."

"Sir—"

"Every man who brings up a bar gets a year's pay. Every man who doesn't gets a story to tell in the bars at home about the night he was a coward." He said it without heat, the way he said everything, because heat was for men who couldn't afford calm. "The window is closing. So is my patience. Put them back in the water."

The supervisor looked at him for a long moment, and Boateng watched him do the arithmetic every man who worked for him eventually did: what his family cost, what his pride cost, what the difference was. The man went back down the ladder.

Boateng returned to the window. The sea hammered the glass. He found, somewhere beneath the calculation, a feeling he did not often permit himself, and it took him a moment to name it, because it was so old he'd thought it dead: it was joy. The pure, cold joy of a man about to take what the world had told him he couldn't have. His father had fished. His father's father had fished. Eze Boateng had looked at the water at nine years old and understood that fish was what poor people took from the sea, and he had spent his whole life learning what rich people took.

Down there, in the dark, the girl was probably watching him. Good. Let her watch. She had the ledger, and the ledger was paper, and paper was a story, and stories could be bought late at night like anything else. But the gold was the gold. The gold was the sentence the world had written in its own hand, and no one—no diver, no navy, no storm—was going to take the pen out of his grip.

He did not feel the faint concussion travel up through the hull a few minutes later, a soft knock from the deep, like something turning over in its sleep. He felt the flagship shudder and assumed it was the swell.

It was the first charge.

* * *

Tamsin felt it in her teeth.

The composite charge was small, tuned, shaped—a scalpel, not a hammer—and the *Meridian* took it the way old things take bad news: quietly, deeply, with a long groan of settling that she felt through her whole body where she pressed against the hull. Above her, forty meters up, the floodlights swung as the fleet reacted, and a storm of bubbles descended through the dark like inverted rain.

She swam to the second point. The scar. The wound her family's money had sailed away from.

Her hands were shaking and she let them, because the shaking was honest and everything else tonight was not. She thought of the hold below her: the gold fused into its coral shroud like amber around flies, and the lead-lined chest, empty now, its contents photocopied and scattered and burning through the coast like fire through dry grass—Kwesi's work, done from the skiff while she rigged, the scans going out to three journalists, two historians, and a list of descendant communities that included, in its cold alphabetical way, her own. The truth was already free. It had been free since the fifth tide. Everything below her now was just the weight of it, the part that had to go back.

*You could keep one bar,* said a voice in her that sounded like Eze Boateng and, worse, sometimes sounded like her. *One bar, melted down, no serial, no story. One bar and you never dive for a creditor again.*

*And then I'm the clerk in the ledger,* she answered it. *Coastal agent. Account settled in kind.*

She planted the second charge.

The third went into the stern section, where the hull was thinnest, where the sea had been eating for a century and a half and had left only a shell of iron and intention. She worked fast now, because her gauge was falling and the surge was no longer pushing in gusts but in long sustained shoves, the whole ocean leaning on her like a hand. Somewhere above, a diver's scream came down through the water, thin and warped—a man hit by a swinging crane load, or a line parted, she couldn't know—and the fleet's lights swung again, and in the confusion of shadows she moved along the hull like the thing the navy had never managed to make her: invisible.

At the fourth and last point she stopped.

Below her, past the scar, past the stern, the seabed fell away into the trench—the protected deep, the one place on this coast the government had actually drawn a line around, a cold black crack in the shelf where the water went down past two hundred meters into pressure and permanent dark. She'd found it on Kwesi's charts and understood it at once for what it was: the sea's own vault. Nothing salvage-built could reach it. Nothing salvage-funded would survive trying. It was the one grave on this coast that even greed had never figured out how to dig up.

She pressed the last charge to the hull, and then, for one breath, she put her palm flat against the *Meridian*'s iron and stayed there.

"I know what they did," she said into her mask, to the ship, to the hold, to the three hundred people whose crossing this had been and whose names were now loose in the world like released birds. "I know what my family did. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to rest."

The sea shoved her. She took it as an answer.

She swam clear, unspooling the detonator wire to the limit of its length, and found a shelf of coral forty meters off the flank, and hung there in the black with her thumb on the firing switch, and looked up.

The floodlights were directly above the wreck now. The fleet had moved—she could see the flagship's anchor chain descending like a great pale vine, the cutting rig's umbilicals, the divers clustered at the main hatch in their swarm, and at the center of it all the *Prosperity's Answer*, squatting on the storm like it owned the sky too. Boateng was up there. She could feel him the way you feel a stare, the whole mass of his wanting bearing down through the water column, and every diver below him was a man spending his body to buy his family another year of a life Boateng had priced for them.

*Fury at how power spends other people's bodies*—she had learned that phrase about herself on a night like this, and now she did the only thing she could with fury, which was to aim it.

She thought of the divers. Counted them. Watched.

"Kwesi," she said into the dead radio, knowing he couldn't hear, knowing he'd be watching the surface for the shockwave, trusting him to have done what they'd planned: the long horn blast, the recall signal, the lie about a gas leak in the flagship's own system—anything, everything, to pull the men off the hull. "Call them up. Call them *up*."

The floodlights flickered. And then, blessedly, they began to move—the swarm of divers rising, the rig swinging clear, the shapes ascending in slow reluctant spirals. All but one light, one cluster, one stubborn knot of shapes that stayed at the hatch, and Tamsin understood with a cold drop in her stomach that one of them wasn't rising because one of them wasn't a diver.

The flagship's tender was down there. Boateng had gone down to his claim.

* * *

Eze Boateng had put on a suit he had not worn since he was a young man, because he did not trust the last hour of the window to anyone else's hands.

He was not a good diver. He knew this. He was a rich one, which had always been better, but tonight the arithmetic had failed him—the storm had eaten his schedule, the girl had eaten his certainty, and so he had come down himself, into the floodlit roar, to stand on the *Meridian*'s deck in person and put his hands on the hatch that stood between him and the only thing he had ever wanted without a price tag.

The surge hit him and he grabbed the rail and held on, this powerful man, this man who converted everything into assets, converted now into a payload of fear and stubbornness hanging off a dead ship's rail. Through his mask he could see the hold's mouth below the collapsed grating, the first glint of coral-fused gold, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and he hated, with a purity that surprised him, that he could not lift it alone.

Above him, his divers were leaving. He saw them going up and screamed into his mask at no one, at the storm, at the girl, at the whole ungrateful architecture of a night that had promised him everything and was now, visibly, closing its hand.

He did not see the flash.

He felt it.

* * *

The sea broke open.

Tamsin fired the charges in sequence—stern, scar, seam—and the *Meridian* came apart the way old grief comes apart, all at once, having waited years for permission. The sound reached her through the water not as noise but as a blow, a fist of pressure that shoved her off the coral shelf and tumbled her end over end into the dark. The floodlights above swung wildly, strobing the catastrophe: the hull's flank peeling open like a book, the stern section folding, and the gold—the coral-shrouded, century-patient gold—sliding free of the broken frames in a slow glittering avalanche, down the scar, down the slope, down and down into the trench, bars and bars and bars of it, four hundred small suns falling into a dark that would keep them.

The flagship's anchor chain screamed taut. Above, the *Prosperity's Answer* bucked as the shockwave and the surge hit her together, and Tamsin, spinning in the black, saw her lights lurch, saw a crane boom whip and part its cable, saw the whole fleet's order dissolve into a scatter of running lights and emergency flares.

And below, at the hatch, one shape did not rise.

She saw him in the last of the floodlight—saw the gold go down past him like a river of coins, saw him reach for it, actually *reach*, his arms opening toward the falling treasure as if he could gather it back, as if a man could hold a river. The trench took the gold and did not give it back, and the wreck groaned and shifted, and the surge took the *Meridian*'s broken stern section and swung it like a door.

"Surface," Tamsin screamed into her dead radio. "Boateng—*surface*—"

Whether he heard her she would never know. The floodlights died—one, then the other, the flagship's generators swamped or abandoned—and the dark came down complete, and in the dark she heard the sea close over the place where he had been: a long, deep, settling sound, the sound of a claim being foreclosed.

She kicked for the surface with everything her body had left, through falling silt and rising bubbles and the last drifting glitter of coral dust, up through the bruised water, up into wind that hit her face like a wall and rain that hit like stones. The skiff was there—Kwesi hauling her over the gunwale with hands that were shaking as badly as hers—and for a moment they just lay in the bilge, two small wet animals in a world of storm, while around them Boateng's fleet ran for the harbor like a herd breaking for the treeline.

The *Prosperity's Answer* was last. She could see it through the rain, riding low and listing, its floodlights dead, its anchor chain still down—still down, still hooked to the deep, because somewhere on that bridge or below it, no one had yet admitted the claim was lost. A flare went up from her deck, red and desperate, and the wind took it sideways.

"Did it work?" Kwesi shouted, and she couldn't answer, and didn't have to, because below them the sea had already gone quiet in the way it does over a finished thing.

Tamsin got her hand on the skiff's throttle. Her forearm was bleeding through the suit, her lungs burned, her ears rang with the sea's fist, and none of it was anything. The gold was in the trench. The ledger was in the world. The compass in her pocket—her father's, the one Boateng had taken and she had taken back off the flagship's tender before the dive, the theft that had started this whole last tide—pressed against her hip like a hand.

Then the flare from the flagship went up a second time, and this time she read it, because her father had made her learn the code before he taught her to swim, and three red stars meant one thing and one thing only.

*Man overboard. Not recovered.*

She looked at Kwesi. Kwesi looked at the storm, and the listing ship, and the black water where Eze Boateng had gone down holding onto his claim, and said the only thing left to say:

"The navy's going to want to know who sank their crime scene."

Through the rain, out of the dark to the east, a searchlight came sweeping toward them across the water—long, deliberate, official—and did not waver.
