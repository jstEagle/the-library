## 20. The Reading

The rain had stopped two days before the reading, and the town smelled of wet wood and drying nets, of salt lifted off the harbor by a wind that finally seemed to have finished its work. Tamsin stood in the doorway of the old customs house—borrowed, not owned; nothing in her life was owned anymore except the truth—and watched the light come up gray-gold over water that had tried very hard to kill her.

Her hands were still wrong. The left one especially, where the line had run through her grip during the last dive, peeling skin in a long red ribbon that the clinic at the mission had dressed with gauze and iodine and a nurse's flat disapproval. She flexed it now, watching the tendons move under the bandage like something caged. In six days she had gone down to the Meridian three times, come up twice with her lungs burning and once not entirely certain she was still the same woman who had gone down. The gold was in the trench now, past four hundred meters of cold black water, in a canyon so steep and deep that no fleet on this coast could reach it before the sea finished sealing it in silt. She had heard Boateng's flagship's engines die above her as she swam away from the charges, heard the great ship groan on its anchor in the swell, and she had felt—she would not lie to herself, she had felt—the specific grief of watching a man choose to drown rather than be poor.

She had surfaced into the tail of the monsoon with the ledger chest lashed to her back, and Kwesi had pulled her over the gunwale of the skiff with both hands and said nothing at all, which was the only prayer either of them knew how to say anymore.

Now the chest sat on a table in the customs house, lead-lined, coral-scabbed, its lock long since surrendered to a pry bar and Kwesi's patient surveyor's hands. Inside it: paper. Two centuries of paper. Names.

Tamsin had read every page. That was the thing nobody understood yet, standing out there in the square, arriving in buses and borrowed trucks and on foot from villages whose names she was only now learning were in that book—that she had read all of it, alone, by lamplight, over three nights while the storm walked up and down the coast outside. She had read the merchant houses of Liverpool and Lisbon. She had read the factors and the fort commanders. She had read the shipping manifests with their terrible tidy columns, tonnage and insurance and *losses*, human beings entered as cargo and written off like spoiled grain. And on the forty-seventh leaf, in ink gone brown as old blood, she had found the name Okafor—not once but eleven times, an ancestor who had provisioned the forts, sold palm oil and yams and gunpowder to men who sold people, whose signature curled at the end like a man pleased with his own hand.

*Papa never knew,* she thought, for the thousandth time. Or he had known, and that was why he drowned himself in other people's wrecks and whispered about the Meridian like a man warning his daughter away from a well he'd already fallen into. Either way, the debt had come down the family like an inheritance of salt, and here she stood at the end of it, holding the receipt.

Kwesi came in from the square, shaking rain off a canvas tarp he'd rigged over the door. He moved carefully these days, like a man relearning his own body after the cave. His beard had gone ragged; there was a new gray in it that hadn't been there a month ago.

"They're coming," he said. "More than we expected. There's a bus from Accra with university people on it. And—" he hesitated, "—there are cameras."

"I know."

"Reyes is here too. Standing at the back of the square. No uniform."

Tamsin looked out. The commander stood exactly where Kwesi said, hatless, hands clasped behind him, watching the customs house the way a man watches a tide gauge. Not threatening. Measuring.

"He could shut this down," Kwesi said quietly. "One radio call. Martial law, evidence seizure, some charge about national security. Nobody would even be surprised."

"He won't." She wasn't sure how she knew. But she had seen Reyes's face in the detention room aboard his ship, when he'd told her the founding families were in the ledger—had seen not menace but exhaustion, the look of a man carrying a weight he'd been told was duty and had slowly recognized as shame. "He didn't join the navy to guard graves. He just took him a while to notice."

* * *

They came in through the morning the way water comes into a breached hull—first a trickle, then everything at once.

Journalists first, two from the capital papers, a radio woman with a recorder held together with tape, a freelance photographer whose lens Tamsin eyed the way she'd eye a barracuda. Then historians—a thin, intense man from the university who shook Kwesi's hand too long because he couldn't believe it was really him, the hydrographer who had vanished, the ghost of the survey team standing damp and bearded in a customs house. Then the others. The ones without credentials.

They came in family groups mostly, or alone, wearing their Sunday clothes on a Wednesday. Old women with hymnals. A fisherman who introduced himself simply as Mensah—no relation, he said, then looked at Kwesi and said, *maybe relation, we will see what the book says.* A schoolteacher from the interior who had traveled nine hours because her grandmother used to sing a song about a ship that never arrived, and the grandmother had died still wondering, and the teacher had promised herself she would find out why.

Tamsin stood at the table and did not make a speech. She had drafted seven speeches in the night and burned them all in the lamp, figuratively speaking; there was nothing she could say that the book could not say better and worse.

"This ledger," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, sea-ruined, "names everyone the Meridian made rich. On both sides of the water. It names the merchants in Europe. It names the factors here. It names the families who supplied the forts." She paused. Her bandaged hand lay flat on the lead chest, and she felt through the gauze the faint cold of the metal, like touching a coffin lid. "It names mine. Okafor. Eleven entries. My family fed the trade for thirty years."

The room went very quiet. The schoolteacher closed her eyes. Somewhere a camera clicked and Tamsin flinched and hated herself for flinching.

"So when you read it," she said, "understand that I am not handing you someone else's history. I'm standing inside it with you. Every name in here has descendants in this square or across the ocean, and none of us chose what our blood did. We only choose what we do with the knowing."

She stepped back. Kwesi lifted the lid.

* * *

What happened next, Tamsin would remember for the rest of her life in fragments, the way divers remember bad descents—not as continuous time but as moments separated by gaps of pure pressure.

The thin historian reading aloud, voice cracking on a manifest entry, having to stop, having to start again. The radio woman holding her recorder toward the pages instead of toward anyone's mouth, as if the dead should do their own interviews. The fisherman Mensah finding his own surname in the factor lists and sitting down hard on a bench, and Kwesi sitting beside him, two strangers becoming possibly cousins in the space of a single column of numbers. The schoolteacher finding the ship. Finding the date. Finding, in the margin, in a clerk's careless hand, the word *arrived*, and pressing her palm flat against the page and weeping without any sound at all, and Tamsin understanding that grief and relief can occupy the same body at the same time the way two currents occupy the same strait.

And through all of it, at the back, Commander Reyes, motionless, listening.

He came forward near midday, when the crowd had thinned to those who could not bear to leave, and stood at the table across from Tamsin. Up close he looked older than his file photograph, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"The ministry sent orders this morning," he said. "Classified document. National heritage asset. Sealed custody." He recited it flatly, like a man reading his own eulogy. "I'm supposed to take the ledger into protective storage."

"And?" Tamsin said.

Reyes looked around the room—at the schoolteacher still bent over the open page, at the old women, at the photographer carefully not photographing anything, waiting. At Kwesi, who had risen and stood at Tamsin's shoulder without being asked.

"I've spent nineteen years in that service," Reyes said. "I've recovered wrecks. I've signed off on salvage reports that went into drawers and never came out. I told myself it was order. Stability. That some things are safer in the dark." He took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirttail, a small domestic gesture that suddenly made him unbearably human. "Then my survey team started dying, and I discovered what the dark was actually for. It isn't protection. It's ownership. The dark belongs to whoever paid for it."

He put his glasses back on and met her eyes.

"There will be no seizure order executed today. I'll file a report stating the ledger was destroyed in the scuttling—water damage, total loss. Which, technically—" the ghost of something almost like humor crossed his face, "—will even be true of the original. Copy what you need. Publish what you must. I'm done being the lock on someone else's door."

"You'll lose your commission," Kwesi said.

"I'll lose a salary," Reyes corrected. "I lost my team because I followed orders slowly. I don't intend to lose anything else following them quickly." He straightened, and for a moment he was again the naval officer, spine and shoulders, the uniform he wasn't wearing more visible than if he'd worn it. "For what it's worth, Ms. Okafor—what you did out there, sinking the gold—it was the single most lawful act I've witnessed in this entire affair. Everything else was theft. You just returned stolen property."

He turned to go. At the door he stopped, half-turned.

"My grandmother came from a fishing family near Prampram," he said, to no one in particular. "I always wondered why we had land other families didn't." He looked back at the table, at the brown-inked names. "Now I'll know whether to be proud or ashamed. Probably both. That's what truth costs—everything except itself."

Then he was gone, walking out into the bright square, a tall man getting smaller, and Tamsin realized she was gripping the table edge hard enough to hurt her ruined hand, and that she was crying, silently, uselessly, the way the schoolteacher had wept—with relief and grief in the same breath, two currents in one body, and she let them run.

* * *

By evening the copies were made—an old duplicating machine from the mission school, coaxed back to life by the radio woman, printing page after gray page while the queue outside stretched into the dusk. Tamsin worked the crank until her blistered hand bled through the gauze, and refused every offer to rest. This, at least, was simple labor. This, at least, was hers.

The original she wrapped in oilcloth and gave to the historian from the university—not to keep in a drawer, she made him swear, but to digitize, translate, publish entire, in English and Portuguese and every coastal language the names had been torn from. He swore it with his hand on the lead itself, which seemed fitting. Contracts sealed on coffins bind deepest.

Last of all, near full dark, an old woman approached the table. She wore white, head to foot, and carried her hymnal under her arm like a shield. She looked at Tamsin for a long moment with eyes gone pale as sea glass.

"You're the Okafor girl," she said. "The diver."

"Yes, ma'am."

"My mother told me stories about your family. Bad ones." She said it without cruelty, the way you report weather. "She said they grew fat while the rest of us grew few." She set her hand on the ledger, gently, the way you touch a grave marker. "But my mother also said the Okafors' luck turned. Said the sea took payment back, generation after generation. Your father. Now you, nearly." She studied Tamsin's bandaged hand, the salt scars on her forearms, the wreck of a life written on her body. "Maybe the debt's paid now. Maybe a woman who gives the gold back to the water buys the name clean."

"It doesn't feel clean," Tamsin said honestly.

"No," the old woman agreed. "Truth never does. That's how you know it's true. Lies feel wonderful going down." She patted the ledger once, like blessing it, and shuffled out into the night humming a hymn Tamsin's grandmother might have sung.

Kwesi appeared beside her with two cups of tea from the mission urn, strong enough to stand a spoon in. They drank in silence, watching the last of the crowd disperse, the copies disappearing into bags and bundles and hands, the truth radiating outward through the dark like heat off a stone.

"Boateng's company filed for receivership this afternoon," Kwesi said at last. "Harbor radio. The fleet's being impounded piece by piece. Some of his divers are asking about work." He paused. "I told them you might be hiring. For survey work. Honest charts."

Tamsin looked at him. "Do I have a boat?"

"You have a third share in a skiff and a reputation that just became worth more than a fleet." He smiled, tired and real. "It's a start."

She laughed—actually laughed, a sound that surprised her out of her own chest, rusty and salt-crusted and alive. Then she stopped, because through the window she saw the harbor, and beyond the harbor the sea, lying calm and enormous under the first clear stars in a week, and beneath that calm, four hundred meters down, in a trench no light had ever touched, the Meridian settling into its final silence with its fortune dissolving into the dark it had always belonged to.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would borrow the skiff, and take out air, and go down one more time—not for salvage, not for proof, not for anything anyone could buy or seize or classify. Just to say goodbye. Just to leave something of her father's where it had always been trying to return.

She drained her tea and reached for the duplicating crank. There were still names to print. There would be names to print for years.

Outside, the sixth tide was coming in.
