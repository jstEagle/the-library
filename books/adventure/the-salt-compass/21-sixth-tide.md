## 21. Sixth Tide

The morning came in the color of hammered tin, and Tamsin Okafor woke before it, as she always did, her body still keeping the old clock of tides even now that no one was chasing her across them.

She lay a while in the narrow bunk of the *Adaeze*, listening to the hull breathe. Six months since the monsoon. Six months since Boateng's flagship went down over its own greed and the gold slid into the trench where no crane could follow. Six months since she had stood on a dock in Lagos with wet paper in her hands and watched the ledger's names go out into the world like sparks off a grinding wheel—some of them catching, some of them burning the hands that held them.

The boat rocked. Somewhere above her, Kwesi was already up; she could hear him moving through the small cabin, the particular care he took with things that weren't his, setting the kettle down soft, latching the chart locker with two fingers so the brass catch wouldn't ring. He had been on the boat three weeks now, helping her run survey work for the fisheries cooperative, and in that time she had learned the whole vocabulary of him: the way he hummed when he was nervous, the way he went silent when he was certain, the way he checked a bearing twice and then a third time only when it mattered.

She had named the boat for her mother, which was a thing she had never told anyone, because her mother's name lived nowhere else now except in a parish register and on the transom of a nineteen-foot workboat with a rebuilt diesel and a paint job gone patchy at the waterline.

Tamsin rose, dressed, and went out into the grey-gold light.

* * *

The harbor at dawn smelled of diesel and drying nets and the green rot of the mangrove line to the south. She walked the dock with a thermos of tea and found Kwesi already at the stern, coiling line with the economy of a man who had once done it under Navy discipline and had never quite let the habit die.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early."

"You're up early *on purpose*." He tied off the coil and looked at her, and there it was—the third-time check, the bearing taken twice. "Today's the day, isn't it."

She didn't answer right away. She leaned on the rail and watched the water move under the pilings, black and patient, carrying its light like a debt it intended to repay slowly.

"The compass," she said finally. "I'm taking it back."

Kwesi nodded slowly. He had known. She could see that he'd known since the night she'd told him about the letter from the historian in Accra, the one that had arrived folded around a photograph of the Meridian's manifest, asking permission—*asking her permission*, as if the wreck were hers, as if anything were—to include the Okafor entry in the public archive. She had read the entry forty times by then. *One hundred and twelve souls shipped, season of 1791, factor: J. Okafor, agent for the coast trade.* Her own blood, written in another man's copperplate hand, selling other people's children.

"You don't have to explain it to me," Kwesi said.

"I know I don't." She turned the thermos cup in her hands. "But I want to say it out loud anyway. Because if I don't say it out loud, it stays a feeling, and feelings drown people. You taught me that."

"Pretty sure you taught me that."

"Then we're both well educated." She smiled, and it cost her something, and she let it. "Papa kept that compass hidden for thirty years. He stole it from men who wanted to sell it abroad, and he carried it like a sin and a shield both, and he never told me what it was until the water was coming into his lungs. And then I carried it the same way. Like it was his ghost. Like if I held it tight enough, I'd finally understand him." She looked out at the harbor mouth, where the swell came through in long grey shoulders. "But it was never his ghost. It was just brass and glass and a needle somebody magnetized two hundred years ago. The weight was mine. I put it there myself."

Kwesi said nothing. That was one of the things she trusted about him—he knew when silence was the generous thing.

"So I'm giving it back to the sea," she said. "Not throwing it away. Giving it back. There's a difference. Throwing away is what you do with trash. This—" she touched the pocket where the compass sat, wrapped in oilcloth, riding against her hip like a second heartbeat, "—this is a return. The way you return something borrowed."

"And after?"

"After, we've got a hydrographic run for the co-op. Shallow banks north of the cay. Paying work." She drained her tea and set the cup down. "Honest depth soundings for honest money. Papa would have laughed himself sick."

Kwesi laughed too, quietly, and cast off the bow line.

* * *

They ran south-southeast for two hours, the *Adaeze* punching through a modest chop with the new propeller singing its clean note, and all the way out Tamsin watched the water change beneath her the way other people read newspapers. Green going to teal going to that deep indigo that meant the shelf falling away. Fishing pirogues scattered along the near grounds, their sails leaning into the morning wind. A navy patrol boat far off to the west, heading elsewhere, and she felt nothing about it at all, which was itself a kind of wealth.

Reyes had resigned in the spring. She'd heard it thirdhand, from a journalist who wanted a quote about it, and she'd given none. Whatever reckoning the man was doing, he was doing it alone, and she had decided that was his tide to swim, not hers.

The site came up on the sounder at half past nine: the trench, the scarp, the shadow of the Meridian lying broken along the rim where the charges had opened her and the current had finished the argument. Protected water now—there was a buoy with the ministry's colors moored at the edge of it, and a plaque order pending, and Kwesi had drafted the text himself, sitting cross-legged on the deck one evening with a pencil behind his ear:

*Here lies the Meridian, lost 1804. Beneath her rest the goods of her voyage, returned to the deep by consent of the living. Let no one raise them. Let everyone remember.*

The ministry had cut his last sentence. He had not fought them on it. "The sea will remember whether they print it or not," he'd said.

They anchored inside the buoy line, over sand, and Tamsin began to kit up.

It was a simple dive, by her standards. Forty meters, slack water, no current worth naming, visibility running maybe twelve meters in the post-monsoon clarity. But her hands moved through the ritual slowly, deliberately, checking each strap and valve the way her father had taught her before she was tall enough to see over the gunwale of his old skiff. *The water forgives nothing, Tamsin. So forgive yourself everything beforehand.* She hadn't understood that for years. She thought she was beginning to now.

"You want me topside or down?" Kwesi asked.

"Topside. Keep the watch. If I'm not up in thirty-five minutes, come down after me—but you won't need to."

"How do you know?"

"Because for the first time in my life," she said, settling the tank onto her back, "I'm not diving toward something. I'm diving *from* something. It's a much shorter trip."

He laughed, and handed her the compass, unwrapped from its oilcloth.

* * *

Her father's compass sat in her palm like a small sleeping animal.

Brass case gone honey-dark with age. Glass scratched in one corner where a coral head had kissed it, years ago, in another life. The needle swinging lazy and true, pointing north, pointing home, pointing nothing at all now that its twin lay in an evidence locker in Accra, catalogued and photographed and destined, eventually, for a museum case with a card explaining what it had unlocked.

Two instruments made to find a fortune. One had helped sink it. The other was going back to the dark.

Tamsin rolled off the side of the *Adaeze* and let the water take her.

Descent was the part of diving she loved without reservation—the pressure gathering around her like a slow embrace, the light thinning from gold to green to blue-grey, the noise of the surface dying away until there was only her breath, huge and rhythmic, the engine of a small solitary world. At ten meters the harbor's whole loud century fell off her. At twenty-five the cold came up to meet her, and with it the trench.

She leveled off and swam the rim.

The Meridian lay along the scarp like a fallen cathedral. Six months of current had dressed her wounds—soft coral already claiming the rail where the survey team's marker buoy had snagged, feather-duster worms blooming along the deck seams, a school of barracuda hanging in the water column above her like a thought that wouldn't resolve. Where the hull had burst, the sand below was smooth and undisturbed, a pale apron spreading out into the blue darkness of the trench itself, and somewhere down that slope, beyond any lung or winch or greedy arithmetic, lay the gold. Fused with coral. Fused with time. Becoming reef.

She had dreamed about it, the first weeks. Dreamed of the bars stacked in the dark, dreaming of being stolen. But the dreams had thinned and stopped, and she understood now that the gold had never needed her forgiveness. Gold doesn't grieve. Only the living do that, and only the living can stop.

She hung in the water above the wreck's quarterdeck, breathing, watching her exhalations climb in silver coins, and took out the compass.

For a long moment she simply held it, letting the sea look at it and it at the sea.

"Papa," she said, and her voice came out as a stream of bubbles, shapeless, private. "You kept this from them your whole life and it ate you alive, and I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't ask sooner. I'm sorry I thought the silence was shame when it was really just love wearing armor." The needle swung, indifferent, beautiful. "You wanted to keep it out of their hands. I get that now. But you never got to choose what it meant to *you*. So I'm choosing. And what it means is finished. What it means is *done*."

She found the place she had chosen weeks ago, on the last survey run—a low ledge of coral-encrusted deck beside the base of the mizzen mast, sheltered from the current, visible to any diver who ever came this way on a permit and a clear day. Not hidden. Not displayed. Just *there*, the way a grave marker is there.

She laid the compass on the ledge, face up, needle swinging.

Then, because her father had raised her to finish what she started, she reached out and gave the bezel one gentle turn, so the instrument's last act was a deliberate one—and the needle settled, quivered, and pointed straight down the slope of the trench, into the deep, at the fortune it had spent two lifetimes guarding.

Pointing at the truth. Keeping it company.

Tamsin hung there a moment longer, her eyes stinging inside the mask, and then she kicked away, rising, rising, the wreck shrinking beneath her into its long sleep, and the light opening above her like a door.

* * *

She broke the surface into a world gone bright. The tin-colored morning had burned off into hard blue, cumulus stacking themselves along the horizon like cargo nobody claimed, and the *Adaeze* rode her anchor fifty meters off, Kwesi standing at the rail with his hand shading his eyes.

"Well?" he called, hauling her fins up first, then her arm.

"It's done."

He helped her over the gunwale and she sat down hard on the deck plates, pulling her hood back, letting the sun land on her face like a blessing she hadn't applied for. Kwesi crouched in front of her with a towel and a thermos and the particular expression of a man trying very hard not to make a ceremony out of something that deserved one.

"For the record," he said, "the co-op voted yesterday. Unanimous. The *Adaeze* is yours as of today. Ours," he corrected, before she could speak. "*Ours.* Half shares. My name goes on the registration Monday."

"Kwesi Mensah, boat owner. Your Navy friends will faint."

"My Navy friends can learn to swim." He poured tea into the cup lid and pressed it into her hands. "Debt-free, both of us. Do you understand that? Do you actually understand what that is? I did the math last night. Between the survey contracts and the salvage-school fees and the co-op dividend—we're free. Nobody owns a piece of us. First time in my adult life."

Tamsin looked at him over the steam. His face was open and lit and a little afraid, the way faces get when a man says a true thing he can't take back, and she thought: *this is what inheritance is supposed to feel like. Not a ledger. Not a debt. A door left open.*

"First time in mine too," she said. "And I intend to be insufferable about it."

They laughed, and the laugh went out over the water and came back changed, softer, and neither of them tried to explain what they meant to each other, because the boat knew, and the sea knew, and some things survive translation better unspoken.

* * *

They weighed anchor midday and ran north for the shallow banks, and Tamsin took the helm.

It was ordinary work and she loved it with her whole chest: reading the water ahead for the color-shifts that meant sandbars, calling depths to Kwesi at the sounder, logging stations in the co-op's ledger—*that* kind of ledger, the kind that recorded fish stocks and channel changes and the slow honest news of a living coast. At the bank they dropped the transect lines and worked the afternoon, and a pod of bottlenose dolphins came through the shallows hunting mullet, and for twenty minutes neither of them logged anything at all.

On the run home, with the sun going down behind them and the water turning to hammered copper, Kwesi spoke from the chart table without looking up.

"There's a letter came for you at the co-op office. Came Tuesday. I forgot it in all the fuel-run chaos." He fished it out of the chart drawer and held it up. "Official-looking. Ministry seal."

Tamsin's hands stayed easy on the wheel. Six months ago a ministry seal had tightened every muscle she owned. Now it was just wax and paper. "Open it."

He slit it with his knife and read, and she watched his eyebrows climb. "It's the plaque," he said. "The final text. They restored your sentence, Kwesi. *Let everyone remember.* Word for word. They want confirmation before it's cast." He looked up at her. "They also want to name the protected zone. Formally. On the charts."

"What name did they pick?"

"They didn't. They're asking *you*."

Tamsin steered into the last of the light. Ahead, the harbor lights were coming on one by one, small warm constellations rising out of the shore, and somewhere among them was a dock, and a shack, and a life she had built with her own two hands out of nothing but stubbornness and salt water.

She thought about names. About the ones in the ledger, bought and sold and stained. About her father's, spoken finally without flinching. About her mother's, riding the transom behind her, keeping the wake clean.

"Meridian Reach," she said. "Put that down. Not for the ship—for the line. Every meridian's just a line somebody drew, and every line's a choice about what counts as center." She glanced back at him, and grinned, tired and whole. "Might as well draw one that remembers."

Kwesi wrote it down, and the pen scratched, and the *Adaeze* ran home through the copper water toward the lights, carrying nothing that anyone could steal.
