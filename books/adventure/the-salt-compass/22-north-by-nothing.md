## 22. North by Nothing

The morning came in grey and silver, the way the harmattan months arrived on the coast—dust thinned to a haze over the water, the sun a pale coin behind it. Tamsin Okafor stood at the edge of the training dock with her boots off and her feet bare on the warm planks, watching six young divers bob in the shallows like a scatter of dark seeds.

"Stop fighting it," she called. "The current isn't your enemy. It's a sentence. Read it from the beginning."

One of them, a wiry boy named Efua's-brother—she still couldn't keep all their names straight, though she was learning, the way she'd once learned the names of wrecks—lifted his head from the water and spat salt. "It's pushing us toward the rocks."

"It's telling you where it's been," she said. "Rocks mean a gap in the reef. A gap means cold water underneath. Cold water means the tide turned an hour ago, which means you've got maybe forty minutes before it turns again and pushes you back out to sea. Now tell me what you do."

He thought about it, treading water, his face working through the arithmetic of it. Behind him the others had gone quiet, listening.

"You don't swim against it," he said finally. "You let it carry you along the reef line until you find the slack water behind the point."

Tamsin nodded once. "That's the whole trade, right there. Everything else is just gear."

She sat down on the dock edge and let her legs hang in the water while they worked the exercise again, slower now, more carefully. Six months ago this dock had been somebody else's—a fish-smoking platform belonging to old Maame Adjoa, who'd traded it to her for dive lessons for her grandsons and first pick of whatever the reef gave up that was legal to take. The shack at the shore end was still hers, though, dragged plank by plank from the old harbor after Boateng's people stopped looking for it. Inside were her tanks, her regulator kit, two crates of training masks she'd bought secondhand from a diving school in Takoradi, and a wall where she pinned things that mattered.

There weren't many things on the wall yet. Her father's chart, browned and soft as cloth at its folds, took up most of one side—the same chart she'd carried through every chapter of the last year, its penciled soundings worn to ghosts. Beside it hung the photograph of him at twenty-two, standing on a survey boat with men whose faces history had since named for her: some of them honest, some of them bought, one of them, she'd learned from the ledger's margins, already paid in advance by a merchant house whose descendants still sat in parliament. And below the photograph, held by four brass tacks, the letter.

She'd gotten it eleven days ago. She could have recited it by heart.

*Dear Ms. Okafor,* it began, in a historian's careful hand translated into careful type, *the reading you made possible has begun to change what we thought we knew. Not only the names—we expected names—but the shape of the silence around them. Three families have come forward voluntarily. One has endowed a scholarship in the name of a woman listed in the ledger not as property but as a partner, erased from every other record until yours. You will perhaps understand when I say that we found her in your document the way one finds a face in dark water: suddenly, and then everywhere.*

*History owes you a debt it does not know how to pay. I hope you will accept this letter as a down payment.*

It was signed *Adaeze Nwosu, Department of History, University of Ghana, Legon*, and beneath the signature, in ink, a postscript: *P.S. The woman's name was Abena. I thought you would want to know it.*

Tamsin had read that postscript perhaps fifty times. Each time it did the same thing to her chest—a slow pressure, like depth coming on, and then a strange lightness, like surfacing.

Abena. A partner. Erased everywhere except in a ledger written by men who thought they were recording wealth and had accidentally recorded a person.

She looked at the letter now, then out at the water, where Efua's brother—Kofi, his name was Kofi, she'd get it—was leading the others along the reef line exactly as she'd told him, their fins cutting quiet Vs in the morning swell.

* * *

The students came ashore at noon, sunburnt and loud, and she fed them rice and smoked fish off the back of her boat—a nineteen-foot open hull she co-owned with Kwesi Mensah, whose name was painted on the transom next to hers in letters she'd let him choose because he'd insisted on doing the painting and she'd insisted it be done properly. *MERCY OF TIDES*, the boat said. He'd defended the name with the solemnity of a man christening a child. She'd rolled her eyes and secretly loved it.

Kwesi arrived as they were eating, walking down the beach with his survey case under his arm and sand in the cuffs of his trousers, looking like what he was: a man who had traded a government salary for a smaller life with better weather in it.

"They're asking questions in Accra again," he said, sitting down heavily among the students and stealing a piece of fish without apology. "Committee hearings. Reyes testified last week."

"What did he say?"

"That the navy's position is that the Meridian site lies within a protected marine zone and that no salvage operation was ever authorized there." Kwesi smiled thinly. "Which is technically true. No salvage operation was ever authorized anywhere near there. He's learned to say true things that hide inside bigger false things. He was always good at that."

"Do you hate him?"

Kwesi considered it with the seriousness he brought to everything, chewing slowly. "No. I hate what he was part of. But he chose not to stop us at the end, and then he chose to resign, and those are two more choices than most men in his position ever make." He shrugged. "I spent too long being silent myself to throw stones at a man who finally spoke quietly."

Tamsin said nothing. She'd heard his confession on the run to the site in the storm, in the teeth of the monsoon, his voice raw over the engine noise—I let them die, I stayed hidden while they read the ledger and started drowning—and she'd forgiven him, or started to, and neither of them had ever fully believed the forgiveness was complete. But it didn't need to be complete. It just needed to be carried. That was another thing she taught the students without saying it aloud: some weight you don't drop, you just learn to dive with it.

"Boateng's fleet is being sold off," Kwesi added. "Piece by piece. The flagship went to a Nigerian company last month."

"And Boateng?"

"No one knows. Some say Lagos. Some say he never surfaced at all that night and the sea kept him." He looked at her. "Which do you believe?"

Tamsin watched the water for a moment. "I believe the sea answers to no one," she said. "Not even to me. Especially not to me."

* * *

She took the afternoon class out past the reef, to a wreck she'd found in her first month of teaching—a coastal trader, small, unremarkable, sunk sometime in the fifties with a hold full of cement bags long since hardened into artificial coral. It was perfect for training. Nothing on it worth stealing, which meant everything on it worth learning.

She went down with them, three at a time, and watched their bubbles rise in silver strings through the green light. She showed them how the hull's shadow shifted with the sun's angle, how a compass needle trembled near the iron fittings, how to hover without touching so that a century-old deck plate stayed exactly where history had put it.

On the way back up, hanging at fifteen feet for the safety stop, she felt it again—that thing that had been happening more often lately, in the quiet moments underwater. Not the old hunger. Not the diver's greed for what lay below. Something else. A kind of attention. As if the sea were not a vault anymore but a library, and she had finally learned to read.

Her father had never learned that. Or maybe he had, and it had killed him—because a man who reads the water knows what's buried in it, and a man who knows what's buried can be drowned for knowing. She'd spent a year angry at him for dying, then a year understanding why, and now she was somewhere past both: grateful, in a complicated way, for the compass he'd stolen and the debt he'd left her, because both had forced her to become someone who chose instead of someone who inherited.

The compass itself lay in a trench two hundred meters down, beside a wreck full of coral-fused gold that no human hands would ever lift. She'd left it there on the sixth tide, kneeling on the seabed in the blue dark, setting it upright against the Meridian's rail like a marker on a grave. Its needle had swung once, pointing seaward, pointing at nothing, pointing at everything, and gone still.

Some instruments aren't for finding things, she'd thought. They're for keeping promises.

* * *

Evening came gold and slow. The students drifted home along the beach path, and Kwesi rowed out to check the mooring lines, and Tamsin stayed in the shack with the lamp lit, doing the accounts in a school notebook because she'd never trusted ledgers since—not even her own.

The numbers were good. Not rich-good. Enough-good. Enough to eat, enough to fuel the boat, enough to keep teaching the six students for free and take on two more next season if the reef cooperated. Eze Boateng's debt had died with his claim; the courts had dissolved it in the spring, and the paper that had haunted her father to his drowning was now, officially, nothing. North by nothing. That was what she'd started calling it, privately, when people asked her where she was headed: north by nothing, following no needle but her own.

A knock at the doorframe. Maame Adjoa's eldest grandson, holding out an envelope. "Came on the afternoon boat," he said. "Legon postmark."

Tamsin's heart did something complicated. She waited until the boy had gone before opening it.

Inside was a single page, and a photograph.

The page was from Adaeze Nwosu again. Shorter this time. *We have located Abena's descendants,* it said. *They live in a fishing town two hundred kilometers east of here. They have asked, through me, whether you might visit. They say they have a story about a diver who returned something to the sea, and they want to tell it to your face rather than read it in a newspaper. I told them you are not a woman who enjoys being looked at. They said neither are they. I think you will understand each other.*

And beneath the letter, the photograph: a group of people on a beach, old and young, squinting into the sun, holding a banner that read, in careful English, *THE CHILDREN OF ABENA REMEMBER*. On the end of the line stood a girl of maybe sixteen, holding the banner corner high, and she was looking directly into the camera with an expression Tamsin recognized instantly, because she saw it in her own mirror every morning.

The expression of someone who owns almost nothing, and is owed nothing, and intends to be free anyway.

Tamsin sat with the photograph for a long time. Then she rose, took four brass tacks from the tin on her shelf, and pinned it to the wall beside her father's chart, beside the first letter, beside the picture of the young man on the survey boat who had stolen a compass to keep a truth from being sold abroad.

Three generations of her family looked at each other across the wood. The grandfather who dove. The granddaughter who returned. And between them, in ink and newsprint, Abena—the woman the ledger had tried to make into cargo, and failed.

"Well," Tamsin said aloud, to the empty shack, to the lamp, to the sea breathing beyond the door. "Looks like I'm going east."

* * *

She sailed out at first light, alone, on a working morning—checking the moorings, hauling the crab pots she kept less for the money than for the discipline of hauling something. The haze had burned off and the water lay flat and bright as hammered tin. At the harbor mouth she paused, engine idling, and looked back at the shore: the dock, the shack, the wall inside it with its small museum of a life rebuilt from wreckage.

Then she turned the bow east, toward a town she'd never seen and people she'd never met, who remembered a name that history had tried to drown.

The compass on her dash pointed north, uselessly, faithfully, the way her father's had always pointed north before it learned to point seaward. She didn't need it. She knew the water. She knew the sentence it was speaking today—slack tide till noon, a long easy reach, fair running all the way down the coast.

North by nothing. East by everyone.

Tamsin Okafor opened the throttle and ran with the current, letting it carry her, at last, exactly where she chose to go.
