## 1. The Debt of Dry Ground

The broker found Ada Ashgrove at the bottom of a bottle in a canal-town cantina that smelled of diesel and old fish, which was fitting, because the job was about water and Ada had spent ten years trying to drown his name in everything except it.

"One last contract," the broker said, sliding a folded paper across the table. "Payment enough to clear every debt you carry and put a border between you and the rest of your life."

Ada didn't look up. "I don't take contracts anymore."

"Everyone says that. You're still here."

He was still here. That was the problem with disappearing: you had to keep doing it, day after day, in cantinas and way stations and the backs of freight barges, and eventually the effort of vanishing became its own kind of presence. Ten years of erasing himself from every ledger, every registry, every memory that might someday be subpoenaed. Ten years of being a man shaped like a absence. And still the brokers found him, because a man who is good at not being found is, by definition, good at finding.

He looked at the paper. It was a photograph, actually—a woman, thirty maybe, dark hair cut practical, eyes that looked at the camera like it owed her money. Below the image, a name: *Sefi*. Below the name, a list of stolen documents. Not jewels. Not coin. Paper. Concession permits, stamped with a consortium seal, that would decide who owned the water beneath the salt flats when the monsoon came and drowned them.

"She took nothing of value," Ada said.

"The consortium disagrees."

"The consortium would." He pushed the photograph back. "Find another ranger."

"There are no other rangers. There are rangers who track, and there are rangers who find, and there is exactly one man in this territory who can read hardpan after three days of wind and tell you which way a person went and why." The broker leaned in. "Sixty days. That's how long the permits stay valid. After that they expire unclaimed and the whole question goes back to the settlements, and the consortium would very much prefer that not happen. You have sixty days to catch a thief who has a head start and a reason."

Ada turned his glass in a slow circle on the table. The bottle was nearly empty, which meant the morning was nearly over, which meant he'd have to decide something, and deciding things was the one activity he had spent a decade practicing the avoidance of.

"How much?" he said, and hated himself for it.

The broker told him. It was enough. It was more than enough. It was exactly, precisely, the amount a man would need to pay off every creditor who still remembered his face, buy passage on a freighter going somewhere with rain he didn't have to track, and never sign his name again.

"Payment on delivery," Ada said. "Half now."

"Half now," the broker agreed, and counted out the coin, and Ada Ashgrove—no, not Ashgrove, he hadn't been Ashgrove in years, he was Ash now, or the Man from Nowhere, or whatever alias the way station clerk had scrawled into a ledger that morning—took the coin and walked out into the white glare of the salt flats and began, for the last time, to read the ground.

* * *

The hardpan told him everything, the way it always did, the way it had since he was a boy learning from a father who read the desert the way priests read scripture. A person crossing salt flat leaves a signature. Weight, stride, the drag of a heel, the hesitation at a crease in the crust where water once pooled and might pool again. Most trackers saw noise. Ada saw sentences.

Sefi's sentence began three days old and read like this: *I know you're coming. I know you're good. Here is everything you need.*

She hadn't hidden. That was the first thing that unsettled him. A thief with a three-day lead on a professional tracker should have walked in streambeds, on stone, in the shadow of ridgelines where the wind erased all. Instead she'd crossed the open pan in a straight line, boot prints crisp as type, pausing at intervals to do something he couldn't immediately parse—kneeling, maybe, or setting something down. At the fourth pause he found what she'd left: a strip of cloth tied to a salt-crusted stake, fluttering like a flag on a country that didn't exist.

Wayfinding. She was marking her own trail.

He stood over the cloth strip for a long time, hat brim dripping sweat onto the crust, and felt the first cold thread of something that wasn't quite fear. Thieves who wanted to be followed were thieves who wanted something from the follower. Every instinct he had, every scar, every year of reading ground for men who paid in coin and lies, told him to turn around. A legible trail was a trap with good manners.

He kept walking.

By the second day the trail told him more about her than any dossier. She walked long shifts and rested short ones—discipline, or desperation. She'd passed a dry well and not detoured to it, which meant she was carrying water and knew exactly how much. At a junction where three old survey roads met, her prints stopped for the length of a cigarette, two cigarettes, and then continued on the road that led not toward any settlement but out, toward the deep flats, toward the place on every map marked with the survey consortium's faded stamp: *Camp 7 — decommissioned*.

Nobody went to Camp 7. Camp 7 was where a government survey team had gone eleven years ago and not come back—eight men and women, sealed gear, a camp found standing with kettles on cold stoves and no tracks leading out. The flats had swallowed the story the way the flats swallowed everything, and Ada had been a young ranger then, and he remembered the search, and he remembered finding nothing, and he remembered how finding nothing had felt like the desert looking back at him.

She was going there. Deliberately. Leaving him a trail a child could read, straight to the one place in the territory where the ground itself had once refused to testify.

He made camp that night without a fire, as he always did, and lay on his bedroll looking up at stars so dense they looked like spilled salt, and he thought about the money, and the freighter, and the rain he wouldn't have to read. He thought about how the broker had said *one last contract* and how every contract for ten years had been one last. He thought about the way the thief's trail had been almost courteous, the way a host leaves a porch light burning for a guest she knows is coming.

*She wants me to find her,* he thought. *Which means she needs me. Which means I still have leverage. Which means I'm not done yet.*

He fell asleep with his hand on his knife, as always, and dreamed of nothing, which was the only country he'd ever successfully claimed.

* * *

On the fourth day the salt changed.

He'd have felt it even with his eyes shut—the crust went from the hard, bell-like ring of old pan to a duller thud underfoot, the signature of brine rising, of water moving underneath in the dark channels the monsoon would soon turn into lakes. The season was early this year. The air had that green, metallic pressure to it, like the sky was holding its breath. In three weeks, maybe four, the flats would drown, and every causeway and hardpan road would go under, and the whole territory would be a sea with islands of memory in it.

Sefi's trail bent with the ground now, following the high rims between the old channels, and Ada followed, and the trail grew fresher—hours old, then less—and he felt the familiar cold machinery of the hunt engage, the part of him that was genuinely good at this and genuinely hated it. Ahead of him, somewhere past the heat shimmer, a woman was walking toward Camp 7 with a consortium's permits in her pack, and behind her the money walked too, in his boots, in his debts, in the shape of the life he was trying to buy his way out of.

He crested a low rim at midday and stopped.

The trail ended.

Not faded. Not lost. Ended—her prints walking up to a line in the crust and stopping there, both boots planted, as if she had stood at this exact spot and considered the ground ahead. Beyond the line, the hardpan was unbroken, wind-scoured, innocent. It was as if she had walked to the edge of a page and stepped off the paper.

Ada crouched and put his fingers in her last footprint. The edges were still sharp. Hours, not days. She had stood here recently, within sight of where he stood now, and then she had ceased to exist.

He looked up. Three kilometers out, at the floor of a shallow basin, a cluster of shapes broke the horizon: canvas and poles and the glint of sun on metal. Tents. A camp.

Camp 7, standing exactly as it had stood for eleven years, waiting.

And beside Ada's right boot, planted in the crust where her trail ended, someone had set a small stone. On the stone, in charcoal, in a hand that wrote quickly but without hurry, was a single word:

*Welcome.*

Ada stood for a long time in the white glare, a man halfway gone from the world, reading the one message the desert had never taught him to parse. Then he shouldered his pack, and walked down into the basin, toward the camp that had swallowed eight people and was now, apparently, expecting a ninth.
