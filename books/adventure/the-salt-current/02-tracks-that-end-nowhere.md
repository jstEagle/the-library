## 2. Tracks That End Nowhere

The trail stopped being a trail an hour before I understood it had.

I'd been reading Sefi's sign the way a man reads a letter from someone he used to love—slowly, twice, looking for what wasn't said. Her prints were small, narrow-heeled, set down with a walker's economy. She didn't drag. She didn't wander. Every mile of hardpan between the canal cut and the flats told me the same thing: this woman knew exactly where she was going and had known it for days.

And she wanted me to know it too.

That was the part that kept snagging in my chest like a fishhook. Trackers learn early that most people are liars with their feet. They double back. They walk creek beds. They brush out their camps and scatter pepper behind them. Ten years of contract work had taught me every trick of erasure because I'd practiced all of them on myself first—I was, by any honest measure, the best at disappearing that I'd ever tracked.

Sefi did none of it. She walked straight as a surveyor's line across ground that offered her every excuse to hide. When the wind picked up at midday and should have scoured her prints within the hour, she'd apparently timed her crossing for the calm. When she passed the old dry well at Kiln Bend—a place where anyone could vanish into a shaft and come up a mile east—she walked past it like it was furniture.

She was leaving me a road. And you don't build a road for someone unless you want them to arrive.

I crouched over her last clear print and pressed two fingers into it. The edges were still sharp. Half a day old, maybe less. Ahead of me the hardpan gave way to the pale crust of the salt margins, and beyond that, low against the heat-shimmer, stood a cluster of shapes that had no business existing out here.

Tents. White government canvas, the kind stamped with the consortium's seal. A mast with a signal pennant hanging dead in the still air. An antenna wire drooping between two poles like a snapped nerve.

The contract had said she was headed for open country. It hadn't said anything about a camp.

I did what I always do when the ground starts telling me something I don't want to hear: I sat down, drank water I couldn't spare, and made myself look at the whole picture before I walked into it. The light was going long and amber. My shadow stretched out ahead of me toward the tents like something sent on ahead to check if it was safe.

It wasn't safe. I went anyway. That's the job. That's always been the job—the one clean thing about a man who's spent a decade making himself unclean, unlisted, unremembered. Ada Ashgrove doesn't exist in any ledger worth auditing. But his boots exist. His boots show up where people pay them to.

* * *

The camp was wrong from thirty feet out.

Not abandoned-wrong. Abandoned has a texture—scavenged canvas, ash pits dug out by hyenas, the particular disorder of things taken in haste or left in despair. This camp was *tidy*. The six tents stood pitched and guyed, taut as sails. The cook fire had been raked cold and covered with a flat stone, the way you do when you expect to come back tomorrow. A kettle sat on the stone, and when I lifted it, the water inside was clean and full.

Nobody boils a full kettle and walks away from it.

I moved through the camp the way I move through everything—quietly, counting. Six tents. Twelve bedrolls, rolled tight. Mess kit stacked in graded towers. Instrument crates lined up along the windward side of the largest tent, each one stenciled with the consortium's mark and sealed with lead-and-wire tags, the seals unbroken. A theodolite under oiled canvas. Sample tubes racked in foam like eggs. Everything squared, everything ordered, everything waiting for hands that hadn't come back for them.

No bodies. No blood. No tracks leading out.

That last one I checked four times, walking the perimeter in widening rings, reading the crust the way other men read scripture. Inside the camp there were plenty of prints—boot traffic, cart wheels, the scuff of daily life, all of it days old and baked stiff. Outside the camp, in a clean half-mile circle, there was nothing. Not fewer tracks. Nothing. As if twelve people had stood in the middle of their own camp one morning and been lifted off the earth like lint.

Wind doesn't erase that much. Wind doesn't erase *directionally*.

I'd found dead camps before. I'd found massacres, disease camps, a survey crew once that had gone mad on bad water and turned on each other—there are a lot of ways for people to end, and I've catalogued more of them than I'd like. This wasn't any of them. This was worse, because this wasn't an ending at all. It was a pause. The whole camp read like a sentence someone had stopped writing in the middle, pen still touching the page.

And somewhere under my ribs, in the place where I keep the things I don't say out loud, a thought formed that I immediately wished it hadn't:

*I know this feeling.*

I know what it is to be somewhere and not be there. To have your gear in order and your fire cold and no tracks leading out of you in any direction that matters. I'd spent ten years building myself into exactly this—a camp that looked lived-in from a distance and held no one inside it. Standing in the middle of theirs felt like standing in the middle of a mirror.

I got busy. Busy is how I pray.

* * *

The crates were the reason I stayed past sundown, and the land grant was the reason I never slept.

Most of them were standard survey stock—flow gauges, sediment cores, chemical assay kits, the machinery of measuring water that nobody had asked the settlements' permission to measure. I broke the lead seals on three crates with my knife, working carefully, because a broken seal is a message and I didn't yet know who I'd be sending it to. Inside: paperwork. Reams of it. Flow tables. Depth soundings. Correspondence in the consortium's house style, which is to say language arranged so carefully that it means nothing while sounding official.

I'm not a slow reader, but I'm a suspicious one, and suspicion is slow work. By the time the sun went down and the salt started giving back its stored heat in long purple shadows, I'd learned three things.

First: the survey team had been mapping the aquifer under the flats, and their numbers were good. Better than good. Whoever ran this crew knew water the way I knew trails.

Second: their final reports weren't here. Every crate had a slot in its packing list for the summary folios—the documents that turn measurements into claims—and every slot was empty. Which meant either the folios had been removed deliberately, or they'd left in someone's pack.

Or in someone else's pack. A thief's, maybe. A woman who stole documents instead of valuables, and whose trail led here like a finger pointing.

Third—and this is the one that put me down on a crate with my head in my hands—was folded into the bottom of the correspondence crate, beneath a false layer of assay forms, wrapped in oilcloth with the care people give to things that matter.

It was a land grant. Preliminary concession, countersigned, sealed, dated eleven months back. The consortium's claim to the water beneath the eastern flats, contingent on survey confirmation, contingent on registry filing, contingent on—

Contingent on nothing, actually, except the name in the grantor's line. Because the consortium hadn't claimed empty land. They'd claimed land with an owner, and they'd bought him out, and the signature authorizing the sale, the ink faded but legible in the last of the light, was written in a hand I would have known anywhere on earth.

My father's hand. The hand that taught me to hold a pencil. The hand that signed my childhood letters to school, that sketched channel maps in the margins of my arithmetic books, that wrote *back by monsoon* on the slate by our door the last time he left, and then didn't come back by monsoon, or ever.

*Ashgrove.*

One word, seven letters, and ten years of careful erasure came apart around it like wet paper.

I'd changed my name twice. I'd burned my ranger credentials once and forged new ones. I'd paid a clerk in Corren's Landing to lose my file and paid another to misremember ever seeing it. I had done everything a man can do to make himself a ghost, and I'd gotten good enough at it that some mornings I woke up genuinely unsure what my real name was until I said it aloud in the dark, just to hear whether it still fit.

And the whole time, the name had been sitting out here under the salt, waiting for me. Stamped on crates. Sealed in lead. Signed in my father's dying-year hand on a document that sold the only thing our family had ever owned that mattered—not the house, not the boat, the *water*. The rights underneath the ground my mother was buried in. Sold to the same consortium whose seal was on every tent in this silent camp.

I sat with it until the stars came out hard and white over the flats, and I'll be honest about what I felt, because a tracker owes the truth to the ground he reads: my first feeling wasn't grief. It was the old familiar relief of the debt collector's knock finally coming. *There it is,* something in me said. *There's the bill. You knew it was addressed to you.*

Then the dread arrived, right on schedule, the way it does after relief, and it arrived wearing a question I couldn't put down:

Where were the twelve people?

And why did a thief who steals permits leave a trail so clean that only a professional would bother following it—and why does that trail end here, at this camp, at this crate, at this name?

She hadn't lost me. You don't walk that straight by accident. She had *led* me, mile by honest mile, across forty hours of hardpan, to a land grant with my family's name on it.

You don't build a road for someone unless you need them to arrive.

I fed the cold fire pit a handful of scrub and lit it anyway, small, shielded, the flame barely taller than my thumb. Not for warmth. I told myself it was to boil the kettle's water, but that was a lie and I let it be one. I lit it because twelve people had left a fire raked and covered, expecting to return, and somebody ought to burn something in this camp besides silence.

The flame bent in a wind I couldn't feel, leaning east, out toward the dark flats.

Toward wherever Sefi was watching from, if she was watching. Toward whatever she'd wanted me to find here besides paper. I stared into that little fire for a long time, a man alone in a camp full of ordered, waiting things, and understood that the emptiness around me and the emptiness I'd built my whole life inside of were the same shape.

Mine had tracks leading out, though. I'd made sure of that, for ten years.

Hers—or theirs—had none at all.

Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird called once and went quiet, the way birds do when a person is moving nearby trying not to be heard. I didn't reach for my knife. I reached for the land grant instead, folded it along its old creases, and put it inside my coat, over the heart, where you carry things you haven't decided yet whether they're wounds or weapons.

"Alright," I said to the fire, to the camp, to whoever was listening from the reeds. "I'm here. What was it you wanted me to see?"

The fire leaned east and said nothing. But in the morning, when the light came up gray over the salt, I found what the dark had been hiding: fresh prints at the edge of my firelight circle. Small ones. Narrow-heeled. Set down facing the camp, then turned away.

She'd come close in the night. Close enough to watch me read her letter.

And she'd left something in answer to my question—a single sheet of paper weighted down with a stone, planted upright in the crust like a grave marker, where I couldn't have missed it.

I picked it up. It was a page torn from a survey folio—one of the missing summary documents—and written across the top in a quick, slanted hand were five words:

*Your father didn't sell it.*
