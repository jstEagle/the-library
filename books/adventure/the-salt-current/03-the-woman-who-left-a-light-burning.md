## 3. The Woman Who Left a Light Burning

Sefi lay on the spine of the ridge with her cheek against sun-warmed stone and watched the ranger work.

He was good. That was the first thing she'd noticed about him, three days ago, when she'd started leaving him a trail instead of erasing one. He read ground the way other men read letters—fast, fluent, without moving his lips. He'd stopped twice where she'd doubled back through her own prints, and both times he'd crouched, touched nothing, and simply looked until the ground confessed. Most trackers would have cursed the doubling. He'd treated it like a courtesy extended to him personally.

Now he moved through the dead survey camp with that same unhurried attention, lifting tent flaps with two fingers, checking kettles with the backs of his knuckles before deciding they were cold. He hadn't touched his rifle once. It rode slung across his back like something he'd forgotten he owned.

Through the binoculars she could see the moment he found the crates. His whole body changed—not stiffening, but settling, the way a man settles when he recognizes a room he's been in before. He knelt beside the stack of sealed cases, and even at four hundred meters she saw him go still in a way that had nothing to do with caution.

She lowered the binoculars and pressed her forehead to the stone.

*You didn't know,* she told herself. *There's no way you knew.*

It didn't help. She'd known for eleven days what was stamped on those crates, because she'd been the one who found the manifest, and she'd left the trail legible anyway.

* * *

Three weeks earlier, in the canal-town of Merro's Landing, Sefi had sat across from an old woman named Denna Okoye while Denna poured tea neither of them drank.

"The survey team came through in the spring," Denna said. "Very polite. Very official. They measured our wells, they measured the old flood channels, they asked who held title to what." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit Sefi had seen in a hundred people about to say something unbearable. "Then they went away, and a man came back with papers. He said the consortium's flow studies showed the water under our land wasn't ours. Said it fed down from the high flats, and the high flats were concession land now."

"Concession to whom?"

"That's the joke." Denna smiled with no humor in it at all. "The papers don't say. There's a permit number. There's a registry seal. There's a name for the consortium's holding company that I've never heard anyone pronounce out loud. But there's no name on the grant itself, because—" she tapped the table— "the permits haven't been claimed yet. They're sitting in the registry office at Corvel, waiting sixty days for whoever bought them to walk in and sign. And if nobody signs by the monsoon deadline, everything reverts."

"Reverts to whom?"

"To whoever held prior claim." Denna finally drank her tea. "Us. Three settlements. Two hundred years of wells and channels and graves. All we have to do is still exist as a legal fact when the sixty days run out."

"And the consortium will make sure you don't."

"They'll make sure the record doesn't," Denna said. "There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters anymore."

Sefi had gone to Corvel nine days later. She'd gone in through the records annex during the clerk's supper hour, and she'd taken exactly one folder: the unclaimed concession permits, six pages of vellum with registry seals still soft enough to impress a thumbnail into. She'd left everything else—the deeds, the tax rolls, the settlement claims—untouched, squared neatly in their drawers, because taking those would prove theft mattered and it didn't. The permits weren't evidence of anything. They were a clock. And clocks, unlike evidence, could be carried.

She'd walked out of Corvel with the folder inside her coat and the whole town's lamps burning behind her, and she remembered thinking: *this is the easiest thing I have ever done.* She remembered thinking it specifically so she wouldn't think the other thing, which was that somewhere in the consortium's ledgers there was a line item for this exact loss, already priced, already amortized, already budgeted against. Stealing from them was like stealing rain from the sky. They'd simply wait for the next season.

So she hadn't stolen to hurt them. She'd stolen to buy time. Sixty days minus nine. Fifty-one days for three settlements to find a lawyer, or a witness, or a miracle.

What she'd gotten instead, in the week after, was a name.

* * *

The ranger reached the largest crate and cut the seal.

Sefi watched him lift the lid, watched him take out whatever lay on top—a folded document, heavy paper, the kind that caught light—and watched him hold it at arm's length for a long time, the way a man holds something when his eyes need a second opinion.

Then he sat down on the crate. Not collapsed—sat, deliberately, like a man choosing a chair. He put the document on his knee and stayed there while the wind moved the tent canvas behind him in slow, breathing shapes.

She'd expected anger. Anger she could work with; anger was fuel and she knew how to bank it. What she hadn't planned for was this—the particular stillness of a man reading his own family name on government paper in the middle of nowhere, alone, with no one to perform for.

*Ashgrove.*

She'd found the name in the annex too, buried in the flow-study appendix, in a list of inherited water rights the consortium had recently acquired or was negotiating to acquire. *Ashgrove parcel, eastern salt margin, 340 hectares, seller deceased, heir unlocated.* Someone in the consortium's legal office had written *heir unlocated* with the same pen they used for everything else, and Sefi had understood immediately that this was the most valuable sentence in the building. An unlocated heir meant an uncontested sale. An uncontested sale meant the eastern salt margin—the only route the floodwater would take into the low basin, the route that would drown two of Denna's three settlements—was already theirs on paper.

Except heirs were never truly unlocated. They were just people who had worked very hard to become no one. And Sefi had spent the last ten days learning what kind of person did that kind of work.

The desert rangers kept no roster—that was half their legend—but they kept debts, and debts had edges, and along the edges you could find names. She'd paid a ferryman at the Salt Gate with a question instead of coin, and the ferryman had said: *there's one who takes contracts nobody else will finish. Ashgrove, they called him once. Calls himself nothing now.* She'd asked what he charged, and the ferryman had laughed. *Depends what you want tracked. If it's a thing, he wants money. If it's a person, he wants money and distance. He always asks how far you want them followed, and he always stops short of the end.*

*Why?*

*Because the end is where you have to decide what you are,* the ferryman said. *And he decided a long time ago.*

Sefi had thought about that for two days. Then she'd done the thing that made her hands shake every time she let herself remember it: she'd hired him. Through a broker, through two intermediaries, with money she'd earned hauling cargo up the levee stairs—money that would look, to any careful reader, like consortium money, which was the point. She'd given the broker a description of the thief: a woman, mid-thirties, traveling light toward the eastern flats, carrying documents. She'd described herself accurately, because accuracy was the only gift worth giving a tracker, and because she wanted him to find the trail.

She wanted him to find it the way you want a doctor to find the wound.

* * *

On the ridge, the ranger stood. He folded the document along its existing creases—carefully, she noted, the way you fold something that belongs to someone else—and put it inside his coat. Then he did something she hadn't predicted at all.

He built a fire.

Small, shielded, in the lee of the supply tent, using wood from the camp's own stores. A fire in an abandoned camp, at dusk, in open country, by a man whose entire profession was not being found. She watched him set the kettle from the cold stove onto the flames and understood, with a feeling like a floor tilting, that the fire was not for him.

It was a question. Or an invitation. Or a trap—she couldn't rule that out—but it was, unmistakably, a message: *I know someone is watching. I am making myself visible. Come down or don't.*

Nobody left a light burning in the desert unless they wanted company, or wanted to be forgiven for something.

Sefi lay on the stone and argued with herself until full dark.

* * *

Later—much later, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the ranger had rolled into his bedroll facing it, rifle still slung, a man who slept armed even when he slept generous—she let herself remember the night she'd actually taken the folder, because she made herself remember it every few days, the way you press a bruise to check whether it's healing.

Corvel's records annex smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the canal below the window. She'd been inside for forty minutes, long enough to learn the filing logic, long enough to confirm the permits were where the manifest said they'd be. She'd had a route out across the roofs. She'd had a boat waiting two bridges downstream. Everything about the night had been clean.

And then, pulling the folder free, she'd knocked a ledger off the shelf's edge—her own clumsiness, pure and stupid—and caught it, and in catching it her eye had fallen on the page beneath her thumb, and the page had been a flow study dated fourteen years back, and the study concerned a river she knew by its old name, the one her mother had used, the one that no longer existed because a consortium survey had rerouted it to feed a refinery district, and the rerouting had drowned a valley, and the valley had contained a village, and the village had contained—

She'd stood in the dark of the annex holding a stolen folder and a stranger's ledger and she had not been able to breathe.

They'd called it an engineering necessity afterward. They'd called it a hydrological realignment. Her father had called it Tuesday, in the last years, when grief had worn all the way through him and come out the other side as flatness. *The river moved, Sefi. Rivers move.* As if it had wandered off on its own like a bad animal, rather than being taken by men with seals on their letters.

She'd put the ledger back exactly where it fell. She'd finished the job. She'd rowed away with the permits against her ribs, and somewhere in the middle of the river she'd understood that she hadn't stolen them for Denna's settlements at all—or not only, or not first—and that this knowledge would live in her now, next to the other knowledge, the way silt lives in a channel: invisibly, permanently, shaping everything the current did afterward.

Guilt was useless as motive. She'd learned that young. Guilt pointed backward. But guilt braided with resolve—that was a rope, and rope you could climb.

* * *

The problem, she admitted to the stars, was that fifty-one days had become thirty-eight, and thirty-eight days was not enough for lawyers. Lawyers needed writs and witnesses and travel time, and the consortium owned every court within two hundred miles of the flats. Thirty-eight days was barely enough for anything.

Unless the sale itself was rotten.

She'd spent the last week pulling that thread. The Ashgrove parcel bothered her—not the existence of it, but the timing. The seller had died in late winter. The purchase had closed eight days later. Eight days was fast even for the consortium, and fast purchases usually meant either a desperate buyer or a coerced seller, and the consortium was never desperate. Which left the other thing. And a coerced sale, proven, voided everything—no sixty-day clock, no reversion clause, no argument. Just a dead man's signature extracted under pressure, and a court that might, if the right document surfaced, tear the whole concession open.

But proving coercion required an heir willing to stand up in daylight and say *that was my father's hand and he would not have signed it freely.* An heir who existed on paper. An heir with a name.

An heir who had spent ten years sanding himself down to a silhouette.

Sefi watched the fire's last coals pulse and thought about the ferryman's words again. *He always stops short of the end.* A man who had arranged his whole life around never arriving anywhere. She could lead him to the truth about his father—she believed she could; the thread was there, and she had fingers. But she could not make him claim it. Claiming was the one act no one could do for another person.

Still. She needed him. Not his rifle, not his tracking—she'd managed alone this far. She needed the name. The name was the key that fit the lock, and the man attached to the name was the only person alive who could turn it, and he didn't even know the lock existed.

That was the part that sat worst in her chest, worse than the theft, worse than the bruise-pressing memory of the drowned valley: that she was using him. That she'd drawn him across eighty miles of hardpan with a trail polished like a shopkeeper's window, and that every respectful doubling-back, every deliberately readable print, had been a form of lying. Kind lying. Lying shaped like honesty. She was getting good at it, and getting good at it frightened her more than the consortium did.

Below, the coals settled with a sound like a sigh. The ranger shifted in his sleep and did not wake.

Sefi slid back from the ridgeline, silent on the stone, and began working her way down the far side. Tomorrow she would let the trail go cold for a day—let him feel the loss, let him understand she was choosing him, not fleeing him. The day after, she'd leave something better than tracks. A note, maybe. Or nothing at all: just a place where the trail ended cleanly at a door she'd left unlocked, so that finding her would be his decision, made with his own eyes open.

She owed him that much. She owed him more than that, and the debt was growing daily, compounding like interest, and she suspected that by the end of it she would owe him things she couldn't calculate yet.

Thirty-eight days. Somewhere behind her, a man slept beside a fire he'd lit for a stranger, holding a document with his own name on it, and tomorrow he would follow her again because following was the only direction he knew.

Sefi reached the bottom of the ridge, checked the stars, and turned east toward the canals.

Behind her, very faint, the last coal of his fire went out.
