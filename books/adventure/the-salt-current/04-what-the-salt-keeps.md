## 4. What the Salt Keeps

The drag marks started a half mile past the empty camp, where the hardpan gave way to cracked clay that held a print like wet plaster holds a face.

Ada crouched over them for a long time before he touched anything. Two parallel grooves, eighteen inches apart, running northwest. Something heavy had been pulled across the flat, and whatever did the pulling had not been careful about it. The grooves wavered, cut sideways, doubled back on themselves in places. A man dragging another man, or a man too far gone to walk straight, hauling himself along by his arms while his legs forgot their work.

He measured the stride with his thumb and forefinger. Short. Shorter than his own by a third. Whoever made these had been moving at the speed of a dying animal, which is to say: steadily, without any thought of stopping, because stopping was the one thing left that they understood would kill them.

Behind him, the survey camp sat in the morning light like a stage set an hour after the play ended. Tents staked and buttoned. Kettles on cold grates. Crates stacked in perfect rows, every seal unbroken, stamped with the consortium's blue wax and—on three of them, in older paint beneath the newer stencil—a name he had spent ten years sanding out of every ledger between here and the coast.

Ashgrove.

He hadn't let himself think about it yet. That was a discipline, same as reading sign, same as rationing water. You could carry a thing without opening it. He'd carried worse.

Ada stood, rolled his shoulders until something in his back cracked, and followed the grooves northwest.

* * *

The trail told him things the way trails always did, if you let them talk long enough.

It told him the dragged man had come from the camp's eastern edge—the instrument tent, by the layout—and had not gone through it so much as fallen against it. There was a smear of dried blood on the guy-line, low, at knee height. Not much blood. Wounds that bled little were either very shallow or very deep, and men who dragged themselves eighteen inches at a stride across open salt were not walking off shallow wounds.

It told him someone else had been there after. A second set of prints, lighter, deliberate, circling the drag trail twice like a dog working a scent. At each circle the prints paused—weight settled, both feet planted—and then moved on. Checking on him, Ada thought. Or checking that he was still moving. He turned the thought over and found he didn't like either version.

And it told him, two miles out, where the dragger had stopped to rest. A shallow depression in the clay, body-shaped, with a scatter of small dark stones around it that Ada recognized after a moment as not being stones at all. Dried dates. Someone had left food along the route. Three deposits now, spaced at intervals a failing man could just barely reach between collapses.

Sefi's work. It had to be. The thief who left a legible trail was a thief who wanted company, and apparently she also wanted her company alive.

He ate none of the dates. He noted their position, marked the spot with a cairn of three flat rocks because the flat offered nothing else to mark with, and kept moving. Somewhere behind him, in the direction of the canal towns, a woman who had stolen sixty days' worth of paper was watching him through glass, and he found he didn't mind as much as he should have. That was its own kind of warning sign, and he filed it with the others.

* * *

He found the dropped instrument at noon, when the light went flat and the horizon dissolved into white on white and he had to navigate by the drag marks alone, bent nearly double, one hand trailing the clay.

It was a theodolite. Consortium issue, brass and mahogany, the kind of instrument that cost more than a ranger made in five years. It lay on its side in the grooves as though it had been set down gently and then simply never picked up again. Ada turned it over. The lens cap was on. The leveling screws were backed all the way off, stored position, travel-ready—which meant it hadn't been dropped in panic. It had been abandoned in exhaustion, or in despair, or in the specific delirium of a man who no longer believed instruments could measure what he was seeing.

Scratched into the varnish of the case, in a hurried hand, were four words:

*IT RISES AT NIGHT*

Ada looked out at the flats. Salt to the horizon, baked white, cracked in polygons like the skin of something enormous drying out. No water within fifty miles in any direction except the deep aquifer under the pan, which every survey since his grandfather's time agreed was brine, useless, dead. The water everyone was fighting over wasn't even good water. It was a number on a permit. An abstraction worth killing for precisely because it didn't exist yet—it existed only when the monsoon came down and the floods turned the flats into a shallow sea for six weeks, and whoever held the rights to the recharge basins owned everything the flood left behind.

*It rises at night.* Rising from what? From where?

He put the theodolite in his pack. Not because he needed it. Because leaving it felt like agreeing with whatever had happened to the man who'd set it down.

* * *

He caught up with the drag marks' owner an hour before dusk, in a place where the clay gave out entirely and the ground became a crust of salt so thin it rang faintly underfoot, like porcelain.

The man had crawled into the lee of a tilted slab of evaporite—a natural windbreak, the only one for miles—and there he lay, on his side, knees drawn up, one arm stretched out toward the last cache of dates, six feet short of reaching them.

He was alive. Ada knew it from thirty yards by the movement of his chest, fast and shallow, and by the flies, which always found the living before the dead because the living were warmer.

"Ranger," Ada said, from a distance, hands visible. Old habit. You announced yourself to the hurt ones; surprise killed more people in the desert than thirst did.

The man's head turned. His eyes took a long time finding Ada, and longer focusing, and when they did they were the worst kind of eyes—not frightened, not pained, but *busy*. Eyes doing arithmetic that wouldn't resolve.

"You're not—" The voice was gravel in a tin cup. "You're not from the team."

"No. Desert ranger. Contracted." Ada knelt at arm's length, the way you did with strangers and wild things both. "Can I look at you?"

The man laughed, or coughed, or both. "Look all you want. Won't find what's wrong with me on the outside." He was maybe forty, sunburned down to the bone, lips split, hands trembling with the fine constant tremor of severe dehydration. His clothes said survey technician: canvas coveralls, consortium patch at the shoulder, boots worn through at the ball. "Vasquez. Tomás Vasquez. Hydrological instrumentation." He recited it like a prayer he'd forgotten the meaning of.

"Ada." He didn't give a surname. The name sat in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed years ago and learned to talk around.

"Ada." Vasquez tried the word, nodded slowly. "You came from the camp."

"I did."

"Then you saw the gear. All sealed. All packed." A dry rasp that might have been a laugh. "We packed it ourselves. That's the part nobody will believe. We weren't taken. We *left.*"

"Left where?"

Vasquez's eyes came up and fixed on him, and Ada saw the moment the arithmetic finally resolved into terror.

"The water," Vasquez said. "You're standing on it."

"I'm standing on salt."

"You're standing on *it.*" The man's hand crept out and pressed flat against the crust, as though taking its pulse. "Underneath. The whole pan. It's not brine. That was the falsification—that was the whole point of the falsification, don't you see, the old surveys called it brine so nobody would ever drill, but it's not brine, it's *fresh*, it's a sea under the desert, a freshwater sea, and it's pressurized, and we opened the boreholes—" His breath hitched. "We opened nine boreholes and capped eight and the ninth one we couldn't cap, and now it rises. Every night. You can hear it. Put your ear to the ground at night and you can hear it coming up."

Ada kept his face level. Delirium had a grammar, and this was textbook: the grand revelation, the conspiracy, the numbers that only the speaker could see. Ten years of ranger work had taught him to nod at exactly the right intervals.

But his eyes had already drifted, without his permission, to the theodolite scratch. *It rises at night.* Written before the delirium got bad enough to stop a man from capping his own lens. And to the drag marks, which led away from the borehole field—he'd crossed the edge of it yesterday, nine concrete collars sunk flush with the pan, looking like nothing, like wellheads, like drainage covers—and back toward the camp, and then out here.

Delirium had a grammar. So did evidence. They didn't always agree, but when they did, you paid attention.

"How much does it rise?" he asked.

Vasquez blinked. As if the question itself was a kindness no one had offered him in days. "A handspan the first night. Two by the fourth. We measured. I *measured*, that was my whole job, measuring, and the numbers—" He squeezed his eyes shut. "Okafor said destroy the data. Said if the consortium knew the aquifer was fresh and pressurized, they'd never report it, they'd just file the permits faster, because pressurized means artesian, artesian means the water comes up *free*, no pumping costs, and the permits would be worth—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Worth everything. Worth more than anyone's ever stolen."

"And Okafor?"

"Gone. They're all gone. We scattered on the fifth night when the water came up inside the camp perimeter, actual water, standing water, in the *desert*, and Marlow went out to check the ninth borehole and didn't come back, and Okafor said run, so we ran." His voice was fraying now, unraveling thread by thread. "I ran the wrong way. I knew I ran the wrong way. But the maps—" He fumbled at his chest, and Ada realized the man had been clutching something against his sternum this whole time, under the coveralls, wrapped in a strip of cloth. "I kept the maps. Somebody has to keep the maps."

His fingers wouldn't work. Ada unwrapped the bundle himself: oilcloth, tied with wire, and inside it a roll of survey sheets, hand-annotated, dense with figures, and beneath them a single page in a different hand—older, slower, the handwriting of a man who wrote letters, not reports.

He read only the first line before the world went narrow and quiet.

*If you are reading this, then I did not get the proof out in time, and my son will have to decide whether his name is worth anything.*

The signature at the bottom was a name Ada had spent a decade refusing to write.

His father's.

* * *

Dusk came the way it always came on the pan—all at once, the white going gold going violet going black, the temperature dropping twenty degrees in forty minutes like a door swinging shut.

Vasquez lasted until the last of the light.

There was nothing to be done. Ada had known it from thirty yards out. The man was past the point where water helped; giving him a full canteen would only kill him faster, and Ada told him so, honestly, because the dying deserved honesty more than comfort. Vasquez nodded at that. Seemed relieved, almost.

They talked instead. Or Vasquez talked, in fragments, and Ada listened, and did the other thing rangers do for the dying, which is stay. He learned that Vasquez had a sister in Corriander who thought he worked in an office. That he'd joined the consortium because the pay was triple the universities'. That on the fifth night, standing in ankle-deep water that had no business existing, he had laughed until Okafor shook him, and he couldn't explain why, even now, even at the end. Something about how funny it was. How funny it all was.

"It rises at night," Vasquez said again, near the end, quieter. "Listen for it. You'll hear it. Like the desert swallowing."

And then, with the stars just beginning to come out, in the middle of a sentence about his sister:

"—she puts salt on everything, Tomás, you use *too much*—" a smile, aimed at no one, "—too much salt—"

And stopped.

Ada sat with him until the body cooled, because you did that. Then he searched it gently, took the name tag and the consortium badge—for the record, for whoever came asking, though he suspected no one would ever ask—and buried the man under the salt crust with his hands folded on his chest and the empty date wrapper tucked between them, because the man had crawled six feet short of food and Ada could not fix that, but he could make sure he was buried fed, in however small a fiction.

He stood over the grave for a while.

Ten years, he had kept a rule. One rule, simple, load-bearing: *you track what you're paid to track, you report what you're paid to report, and what happens after is not yours.* Neutrality. It had kept him alive and it had kept him hollow, and he had told himself those were the same thing.

But Vasquez had died six feet from dates Sefi had left for him, and Ada had walked past those dates twice, and somewhere out in the dark a freshwater sea was rising under the pan, uncapped, unreported, rising toward a surface that sixty days from now would belong to whichever thief held the right pieces of paper. And the papers in his pack—his father's handwriting on the top sheet, *my son will have to decide*—were not contract property. They were not anybody's property. They were a dead stranger's maps and a dead man's letter, and neither of them cared about Ada's rule.

He thought about the sealed crates back at the camp. Ashgrove, stamped in old paint. His father had sold the family's water rights to this consortium—Sefi's theft had made that much clear—and the survey team had come out to make the sale real, and the survey team had vanished, and the man who'd crawled farthest had died holding proof that the sale was built on a lie.

Neutrality said: deliver the maps to your employer, collect your fee, disappear.

Something else said: the desert is swallowing, and you heard it swallow a man tonight, and you know exactly whose name is on its throat.

Ada stood at the grave until the cold drove him back to his bedroll. He did not sleep. Around midnight, true to a dying man's words, he put his ear to the ground—and heard, far below the salt, faint and rhythmic and rising, something that was absolutely not silence.

At first light he broke camp, turned northwest, and did not go back for his fee.

Two miles on, he found fresh tracks crossing his own—light, deliberate, pausing at each of his cairns—and beside the third cairn, placed where he could not miss it, a single date and a scrap of paper with one line in a quick, slanted hand:

*Registry closes in fifty-one days. Bring the maps.*

She had been reading his trail the whole way. Of course she had. She'd written it for him.

Ada stood a long moment, the note in his hand, the maps against his ribs, his father's name sitting in his mouth like a stone he was, at last, considering spitting out.

Then he pocketed the note, adjusted his heading to match hers, and walked.
