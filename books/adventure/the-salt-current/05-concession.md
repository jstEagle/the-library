## 5. Concession

The market at Qesh-Vadi smelled of diesel and cardamom, the way all canal towns did—engine oil from the pumps that lifted the water, spice from the traders who moved along it. Sefi stood at a fruit stall she had no intention of buying from and let the crowd carry its noise over her. A hundred conversations. None of them about her. That was the whole point of a market. You could be anyone in a crowd, even no one.

She had been no one for eleven days.

In the satchel against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth and then in a fishmonger's stained apron and then in nothing else because she couldn't stop touching it, were the permits. Fourteen pages of stamped vellum. Concession Grant 9-through-22, Salt Flats Hydrological Survey and Extraction, Consortium of the Lower Basin. She had read them so many times she could recite the clauses like prayers, and like prayers they meant nothing except that someone, somewhere, had wanted them said.

She bought a pomegranate she didn't want, to justify her shadow at the stall, and turned the fruit over in her fingers while she read the permits for the fifteenth time through the oilcloth, from memory, because she had memorized them the way other people memorized the faces of their children.

*Grant effective upon countersignature. Term of concession: sixty days from date of issue, renewable upon claim. Unclaimed at expiry, rights revert to prior holders under settlement law.*

Sixty days. She had stolen them on day three.

Fifty-two left.

She turned the pomegranate again. Its skin was splitting at the crown, showing the dark seeds inside, and she thought: *that's what the flats look like from the air when the monsoon comes. Cracked open, and everything underneath suddenly visible.*

The prior holders. That was the phrase the lawyers had used, the ones she'd paid in a canal town two hundred kilometers south, with money she'd earned hauling freight and stolen back from the same consortium in bribes she'd documented. *Prior holders under settlement law.* It sounded clean. It was not clean. The prior holders were three settlements—Ashfall, Teren, the reed-villages of the lower marsh—who had worked the seeps and the shallow wells for six generations without a single document any registry would honor. Six generations of digging, and on paper they owned nothing, because paper was a thing you needed money and literacy and a fixed address to acquire, and the settlements had none of those things in any combination the consortium's lawyers would accept.

The permits, if they were claimed and countersigned, would convert the whole basin into consortium ground. Every seep. Every well. Every channel the monsoon would fill in forty days and drain in a hundred. The survey team had gone out to map it all, to make the theft look like science, and Sefi had gone into their field office in Teren and walked out with the only thing that made the science legally real.

She had expected the consortium to roar. She had expected riders, checkpoints, her face on every post from the flats to the coast. There had been some of that—a reward notice in Teren, badly drawn, making her look like a man with a beard—but mostly there had been silence, and silence frightened her more. Silence meant they had decided she was not the problem. Silence meant they had decided the permits themselves were not the problem, which could only mean they had another path to the same end, and Sefi had spent three sleepless nights working out what that path was before she understood.

They would let the permits expire.

Not all of them. Just enough. The consortium would file a *replacement claim*—a fresh grant, countersigned by a registered claimant with standing, someone with a name on a deed somewhere in the basin's tangled history—and the settlements' reversion rights would be voided, because reversion under settlement law required that no valid heir contest the transfer. No contest, no reversion. The water would pass to the consortium as smoothly as water finding its level, and the thief with the oilcloth satchel would be holding fourteen pages of expired paper and nothing else.

They needed an heir. Someone whose name was already in the old records, whose claim predated the settlements' oral tradition, whose signature a registry clerk would accept without question. The consortium's clerks had been quiet for eleven days because they were not looking for her. They were looking for a name.

Sefi had found the name first.

* * *

The records office in Qesh-Vadi was a long room above a chandler's, staffed by one ancient clerk and a ceiling fan that turned with the dignity of a dying animal. Sefi had come in three days running, posing as a freight agent verifying a consignment, and on the third day the clerk had grown bored enough to talk, and bored clerks told you everything.

"Basin grants," she'd said, "pre-consolidation. I'm checking a chain of title."

"Nobody checks chains of title." The clerk had said it with something like nostalgia. "Used to be disputes. Now there's just the consortium and everybody else."

"Indulge me."

He'd pulled the ledgers down, and there it was, in the third volume, in ink gone brown as old blood: the Ashgrove grant. Section forty, the eastern seep line, deeded in the grandfather's time, held through the father's, and then—

SOLD. The entry was a single line, a date eleven weeks old, and a transfer stamp so fresh its ink still had a faint sheen. Sold to the Consortium of the Lower Basin, consideration undisclosed, *all rights, title, and interest conveyed.*

Eleven weeks. The old man had sold the water and died, and the timing was so close together that Sefi had sat in the chandler's stairwell afterward with her head in her hands, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do. Had he known he was dying? Had he sold because he was dying—cashed out, cut loose, let the desert take the rest? Or had the consortium come to a sick man's bedside with a pen and a number and the particular patience that institutions have with the dying?

It didn't matter. What mattered was the line beneath the sale, the one the clerk had read aloud when she asked, in a voice of professional indifference: "Estate unresolved. Heir of record: one son. Whereabouts unlisted." The clerk had shrugged. "Happens all the time out here. People walk off. The name stays in the book until somebody claims it or the sun eats the page."

The name stayed in the book.

Ada Ashgrove. She had the name now, and the name had a face, because she'd spent a day in Teren asking careful questions of careful people, and the careful people had told her about the ranger who tracked through the flats like a ghost—no ledger, no post, no history, a man who took contracts and left no signature anywhere except in the sand, and who was very, very good.

They'd also told her he'd taken a contract eleven days ago. Recover the stolen documents.

So the man the consortium would need was the man the consortium had already hired, and neither of them knew it yet. The thought had come to her on the stairwell with the force of a physical blow, and she'd laughed—once, harshly, startling a cat—and the cat had looked at her like she was mad, and maybe she was, because the plan that assembled itself in her head was the plan of a madwoman:

*Let him catch me.*

* * *

Now, at the fruit stall, she finished the pomegranate she'd bought as a prop and wiped her hands on her knees and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, what the plan actually was.

It was not: let him catch me, then run, then lose him, then negotiate. That was the version she told herself at night, the version with exits built into it, the version where she remained a free agent who was *using* him.

The real plan was simpler and worse. She could not reach the registry before monsoon closed the causeways—not alone, not with the flats flooding, not with consortium riders on every levee. She needed someone who could read the water the way she could read a crowd, someone who knew the flats' drowned geometry from the inside. And the only such person within three hundred kilometers was currently following her trail across the hardpan with a contract in his pocket that said *bring back the documents.*

So she had left the trail legible. Not sloppy—she was too good for sloppy, and he was too good for sloppy to work anyway. Legible. A trail with its chin up. A trail that said *I know you're reading this, and I'm not afraid of you, and I have time.* She'd camped where the wind wouldn't erase her sign. She'd broken camp at dawn so her tracks would be crisp. She had, on the fourth night, left a fire burning down to coals in a windless hollow, because a woman running scared doesn't leave a fire, and she wanted him to know she wasn't running scared.

And now the plan required her to be caught.

She stood in the market noise and felt the weight of that settle onto her, and it was heavier than the satchel. Because here was the thing she could not say to the fruit seller or the bored clerk or the cat in the stairwell: the settlements needed an heir to *contest* the sale, not just to exist. Reversion required a living Ashgrove standing in a registry office, in daylight, under his own name, signing his name, becoming visible for the first time in a decade. Everything she knew about the man—everything the careful people in Teren had told her—said that visibility was the one thing he had spent ten years buying his way out of, at a price she could guess but not imagine. Nobody erased themselves that thoroughly without a reason with teeth.

So her plan was: catch the ghost, and then ask the ghost to become a man again. To stand in the light and be counted. To spend the one thing he'd paid everything to be rid of.

*You're going to use him,* she told herself, and the thought had no sting, which was worse. *You're going to take the one thing he has left and spend it on people he's never met.*

She tried to make the sting come. She thought of Ashfall, of the seep line the surveyors had staked with orange flags, of old Marta saying *the water was here before the consortium, it'll be here after,* and how Sefi had wanted to believe her and couldn't, because she had seen another settlement believe the same thing about another river, once, and she had been in that water when it came. She thought of her brother's name, which she did not say, even inside her own head, most days.

The sting wouldn't come. What came instead was the arithmetic, cold and clean: three settlements, four hundred souls, against one man's solitude. The arithmetic always came out the same way. That was what the consortium had never understood about people like her—people from the drowned places. They thought desperation made you sloppy. Desperation made you *precise.*

She left the market by the fish gate, where the canal ran green and slow between mossy walls, and stood on the little bridge and watched the water move. In forty days this canal would be brown and furious. In a hundred it would be a trickle. The whole basin breathed once a year, and everyone who lived here had built their lives around the breath.

The permits were warm against her ribs, or she imagined they were, fourteen pages of paper that were worth nothing and everything, depending entirely on whether a man she had never met could be persuaded to stop being no one.

She had watched him through binoculars once already, at the survey camp—had watched him move through the empty tents with that terrible economy, touching nothing, reading everything, and she had seen the moment he found the land grant with his family name on it, because he had gone very still, the way water goes still over a deep place, and she had understood that the hook was set. Not the hook she'd baited. A deeper one. The camp had done it. The emptiness had done it. A man who has made himself empty recognizes the shape.

*He'll come,* she thought. *Not for the contract. For the name.*

She had three days of legible trail left before the hardpan gave way to the canal country, where her tracks would end and her plan would have to stop being a trail and start being a trap. A drying warehouse on the Teren road, high and empty, one door. She'd scouted it that morning. She'd already worked out which crate to hide behind, which line of retreat she'd surrender, which of her knives she'd let him see.

She was so busy rehearsing the surrender that she almost missed it: a boy on the far levee, maybe twelve, running the towpath with a consortium courier's sash flapping at his hip, shouting the market news the way couriers did when the news was paid for. She caught only fragments over the water.

"—survey camp at the flats, found empty—ranger's asking after a woman, dark hair, travels alone—reward stands, reward stands—"

Sefi smiled at the green water. So. The consortium hadn't gone quiet after all. They'd gone *loud* in the small towns, using the courier network, which meant they wanted her found by proxies—cheap, deniable, and if a bounty hunter put a knife in her, the permits could be recovered from her body without a single consortium signature on anything. It also meant they still didn't know about the heir. The couriers said *woman, dark hair.* Not *Ashgrove.* Not yet.

The clock was running on two fronts now. Fifty-two days on the permits. However many days until the consortium's clerks found the same ledger entry she had, and understood that the cleanest path ran through a ghost's name.

She pushed off the bridge rail and went to buy passage on a freight barge to the Teren road, and to be seen buying it, and to leave the barge at the first lock and walk the rest, so that anyone following her paper trail would follow it to the warehouse and nowhere else.

*Fifty-two days,* she thought, as the market noise fell away behind her and the towpath opened up ahead, long and pale in the afternoon light. *One ghost. One name. One chance.*

And somewhere behind her, already, she knew—she could feel it the way the settlements felt the monsoon building in the high country, a pressure before any weather—a man was walking her trail, reading her fire, getting closer to the woman who was going to ask him for the only thing he had left.

She just hoped, walking fast into the flat gold light of the late afternoon, that when the time came, she would be able to look him in the eye while she spent him.
