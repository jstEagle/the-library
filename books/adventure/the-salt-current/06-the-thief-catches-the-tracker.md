## 6. The Thief Catches the Tracker

**Sefi**

She had been watching him for three days, and in that time he had done four things that told her everything.

He watered his horse before himself. He burned his own dung chips rather than cut the greasewood, even when the greasewood was right there. He slept in a hollow with his back to stone and his face to the open ground, which meant he expected to be found and wanted to see it coming. And on the second night, when he thought no one was watching, he had stood at the edge of the dead survey camp for a long time, holding that land grant in both hands like it was something that could bite.

Sefi lay in the shadow of a salt-crusted levee with her binoculars against her cheekbone and felt the old, familiar weight of what she was about to do. Using people was a skill like any other. She was good at it. Being good at it had never once made it feel clean.

He was following her drag marks now—the false ones, the ones she'd laid with a rolled tarp weighted with stones, curving east toward the canal towns. A ranger of the old school would have read the lie in them inside a day. This one had read it in half a day and then kept following anyway, which was more interesting. Either he was tired, or he was curious, or he had already understood that the trail was an invitation and had decided, for reasons of his own, to accept it.

She had left it readable on purpose. That was the part she kept turning over at night. She could have vanished—she was good at vanishing, it was practically her trade—but she had walked her trail like a woman leaving a lamp in a window. Come find me. Come find me, specifically, Ashgrove, whoever you are, because I need you and you don't know it yet.

The permits rode against her sternum in an oilcloth wrap, sixty days from expiry, and every hour she spent watching him was an hour off that clock.

He broke camp at first light and moved east along the levee road, and she gave him an hour's head start, then packed her own gear and cut the angle toward the drying warehouse at Kiln Bend, where she had decided—after much argument with herself—to let this end, or begin.

* * *

**Ada**

The drag marks were a lie. He had known since the second morning.

Real weight left a signature: the deep scuff at a pivot, the way a load settled when its carrier rested. These marks were even, patient, drawn rather than dragged. Someone had made them for him, the way you'd leave a door unlocked for a guest. It should have insulted him. Instead it did something worse. It made him feel *seen*, and he had spent ten years paying good money to never feel that.

He followed the lie anyway, because the lie was the only honest thing on the salt.

The canal towns announced themselves before you saw them—a smell of wet mud and drying fish, then the sound, which he'd forgotten a desert makes when water moves through it: that low, constant chuckling under everything, like the ground talking in its sleep. Kiln Bend sat where an irrigation canal made a lazy elbow, half the buildings on stilts, the other half slowly losing the argument with the waterline. The monsoon was close. You could feel it in the way the air lay on your skin like a damp hand.

The warehouse stood at the bend's outer curve, a long barn of a thing where fish were hung and salted, and its doors stood open, and that was wrong. Every door in Kiln Bend was shut against the coming rain. Every door but one.

Ada sat his horse in the road a long moment, feeling the trap's shape with the same attention he'd give a snare in the brush. Then he dismounted, tied off, and walked in, because a man who had spent a decade reading other people's trails had finally read his own: it led here, and it had always led here, and the only question left was who was waiting.

She was at the far end of the drying floor, in the aisle between the hanging racks, sitting on a salt crate with her boots crossed at the ankle, eating dried fish with the unhurried air of someone who had paid for the privilege.

"You took three days," she said. "I budgeted two."

"You laid a false trail a child could read."

"A trail a tired man could read." She brushed salt off her fingers. "There's a difference, and you found it in a day and a half, which is why you're here and not still out there following it with dignity."

He stopped ten feet from her, out of lunging range, in the aisle's center where the light from the high windows fell. Old habits. She noted them the way he noted her: professionally, and more thoroughly than was comfortable.

"You're Sefi."

"You're the man who's been following my homework." She tilted her head. "Do you have a name, or do I keep calling you the ranger? Because I've been calling you the ranger in my head, and I've started to feel rude."

He said nothing. She nodded as if he'd answered.

"That's fine. I know your name anyway. It's on the paper in your saddlebag. The land grant." She let that sit. "Ashgrove."

The name landed in the quiet of the drying racks, among all those hung fish turning to salt, and Ada felt it go through him like a hook finding old scar tissue. He kept his face still. She watched him keep it still, and he saw her see that, and hated the whole economy of it.

"Stolen documents," he said. "That's the contract. Permits. I bring them back, I get paid, I disappear. You can save us both the speech."

"The speech is the product, ranger. The permits are just paper." She reached into her shirt and drew out the oilcloth wrap, and set it on the crate beside her, and put her hand flat on top of it. "You could take them. You're armed, I'm sitting down, and you're faster than you look. Take them. Ride back to your employer. Collect. Vanish." She shrugged. "And in sixty days the consortium holds the water under every settlement on these flats, three thousand people start paying rent on rain, and you'll be gone somewhere, invisible, which is what you wanted. Right? That's the whole shape of you. I've watched you for three days. Invisible is your religion."

"Say what you want."

"I want you to look at this." She unwrapped the oilcloth with two fingers, quick and practiced, and turned the top page toward him. "Then I want you to tell me what your father's signature is doing on a consortium concession deed."

* * *

**Sefi**

She had planned the moment for weeks and it still went through her like a blade going in. That was the thing nobody told you about using people: the plan worked, and the working of it cost.

His face didn't move. She'd known it wouldn't—she'd watched it not move for three days, watched it not move at the empty camp, watched it not move over the dead man's grave he'd dug with his hands because there was no shovel. But his eyes moved. His eyes went to the page and stopped, the way a man stops at the edge of a well he thought had been filled in years ago.

She gave him the silence. Silence was the tool she trusted most. Let him fill it.

He didn't. That was the other thing about him. He could out-wait her, and they both knew it, and something in the air between them shifted from negotiation to something more like recognition.

"Where did you get this," he said finally, and his voice had changed—not softer, lower. A voice reading its own sentence.

"From the survey camp you already visited. The one with no tracks out." She turned the page so he could see the whole of it. "Concession deed. Water rights, all aquifers under the eastern flats, held in perpetuity by the Meridian Survey Consortium. Signed by T. Ashgrove, as rights-holder. Witnessed by a consortium notary. Dated eleven weeks before your father died." She let the date hang there. "You didn't know."

"No."

"No," she agreed. "I believe you. That's the strange part. I've been lying to people for a living for nine years and I can usually tell, and I believe you didn't know your father sold your family's water out from under you before he died." She folded the oilcloth back over the deed, slowly. "Which means either you're the best liar I've ever met, or somebody sold it and never told you, and I know which one I'd bet on, because I've met the consortium."

"The permits," he said. "The ones I'm contracted to recover. What are they, exactly?"

"Concession permits. The consortium's claim isn't valid without them—the deeds are the sale, the permits are the government's blessing on the sale, and the government's blessing expires if unclaimed in sixty days. After that, under settlement law, the water reverts to the settlements that have worked it for a hundred years." She smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile. "The consortium knows this. That's why they want them back. That's why they hired you, though I'd guess they didn't tell you that part either. They told you *documents*. Valuable. Stolen." She stood up off the crate, unhurried, and the fish racks swayed faintly in the draft from the open doors. "They hire men like you because men like you don't ask what the documents *do*."

"I'm asking now."

"Good. Because here's my offer, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to take it." She held up one finger. "The registry is at Caudal, four days down the causeways, and the causeways close with the first real storm—maybe eight days, maybe six, and the rain's coming early this year, I can smell it. I need to reach the registry and let those permits lapse on the record. I can't do it alone, because the consortium has agents on every levee between here and Caudal, and I'm one woman with a boat." A second finger. "You're a ranger. You know the flats, you know the water, you know how to move without being seen. And—" the third finger, and here she paused, and made herself say it, because the offer was worthless if she flinched from it, "—and the permits can't lapse if an heir contests the sale. If an Ashgrove shows up at that registry and claims standing, the whole thing goes to arbitration, the consortium wins on paper, and everything I've done is worth nothing. So I need you to *not* do that. I need you to help me make your own name disappear from that record. Permanently. That's the trade."

The warehouse creaked. Somewhere outside, a child was shouting about boats. Ada Ashgrove stood very still in the aisle of hanging fish, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done for him: the payment he'd never collect, the debts, the vanishing, the name he'd been trying to bury for ten years—here she was, offering to bury it for him, at the price of a journey.

"Why me," he said. "There are other trackers. Cheaper ones."

"Because the trail had to be one *they* couldn't follow. I left it legible to you specifically. The camp, the grant, the name—I needed a man who'd read all that and keep walking instead of reporting it." She held his eyes. "And because I think somewhere under all that dust you'd rather drown three thousand strangers than help the people who emptied that survey camp and left a man to die in the reeds. I think you followed a dead man's trail for two days and it changed something. Am I wrong?"

He didn't answer. The silence went on so long she started counting the swaying racks to keep her hands still.

* * *

**Ada**

He should walk out. That was the correct move, the move the old Ada—the one from before, the one who had tracked for the consortium once, briefly, badly, and never again—would have called professional. Walk out, report the location, collect, vanish. The permits were not his problem. The settlements were not his problem. The name on the deed was not his problem, because he had spent ten years and most of his savings making sure he had no name, no debts but the ones he was paying off, no father, no water rights, no *standing*.

That was the word she'd used, and it was the one that stuck in him like a fishhook. *Standing.* The legal fact of mattering. He had built a life on the legal fact of not mattering, and here was a thief in a fish warehouse telling him that the only way to save three thousand people was for him to keep not mattering—actively, deliberately, with effort—and he could feel how much he wanted to say yes and how much the wanting frightened him.

Because the other thing, the thing under the thing: his father had signed. Eleven weeks before dying. His father, who had taught him to read water before he could read words, who used to say the Ashgroves kept the eastern channels the way a man keeps a promise—had signed it all away and told him nothing. Or been made to sign. Or—

He looked at her. She was watching him with the patience of a woman who had already won and was only waiting for him to catch up, and he found he didn't entirely hate it, which was its own alarming data point.

"Conditions," he said.

Her eyebrows went up a fraction. "Go on."

"One. I don't take orders. I take contracts. We agree the terms now, in words, and after that I decide how it's done." He held up a finger the way she had, and saw her mouth twitch. "Two. I see everything you have. Every paper. If there's a page in that wrap that touches my family, I read it before we go anywhere. Three—" and here he paused, because the third condition was the one that cost, and they both knew it, "—when this is done, whatever's done, you tell me why you really stole these. Not the settlements. Not the water. You. The real reason. I track a lie for a living, Sefi. I'll know if you're giving me the shaped version."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, quickly buried, but not quickly enough, and he marked it the way she'd marked his stillness. There was a real reason. There was a wound under all this, and it was hers, and she would rather show him her teeth than show him that.

"Agreed," she said. "All three. And one of mine." She picked up the oilcloth wrap and held it out to him, and he didn't take it, and after a moment she set it down on the crate between them like a wager. "You carry these. If we're searched and I have them, I'm a thief. If you have them, you're a ranger recovering stolen property. Your invisibility is worth more to me than my honesty. Use it."

It was, he had to admit, a good trade. It was a good trade the way a snare is a good snare—elegant, and made to close.

"And when we reach Caudal," he said, "and the permits lapse, and your settlements get their water. What happens to me? I'm the loose end. I'm the heir who didn't contest. I'm the man who knows what you did."

"You're the man who's going to get on his horse, or in my boat, whichever, and ride away and be no one again, and I'm going to let you, because I keep my contracts." She shouldered her pack. "That's the whole beauty of it, ranger. You get to vanish. I get my water. Nobody has to be anybody."

Outside, the light had gone strange—the flat, bruised, greenish light that the desert put on before rain, the light his father had called *the sky loading its weapon*. Somewhere down the canal, someone was shouting to get the boats tied. The monsoon was coming early.

Ada looked at the oilcloth wrap on the crate. Sixty days of other people's future, weighing less than his hat.

"All right," he said. "Caudal. Before the causeways close."

She smiled then, and it was the first real smile he'd seen on her, and it changed her face entirely, and he understood with a sinking, vertiginous clarity that he was in a great deal of trouble that had nothing to do with permits.

"Then we'd better move," Sefi said. "My boat's moored at the south bend, and there's a man on the levee who's been sketching it since yesterday. Consortium. Which means we leave tonight—"

She stopped. Her head turned toward the open doors, toward the road, where a horse stood tied and three figures in traveling gray had just come around the warehouse wall, walking with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the ground they stood on.

"—or now," she said.
