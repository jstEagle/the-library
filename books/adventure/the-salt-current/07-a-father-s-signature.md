## 7. A Father's Signature

The boat was smaller than it looked from the levee, which Ada supposed was the point. A flat-bottomed skiff with a patched sail and a hold deep enough to hide a woman, a stolen satchel, and now him. Sefi poled them out of the canal-town's shadow while the market behind them woke up shouting about fish prices, and neither of them spoke until the town was a brown smudge on the horizon.

Then she dropped the satchel between them on the deck planks like she was setting down something that might bite.

"You wanted to see what your father signed," she said. "Sign."

He didn't move. The morning light came off the water in long flat sheets, and the satchel sat there, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with survey-grade cord—the kind the consortium used, the kind he'd seen on evidence bundles in his ranger days. His hands stayed where they were, one on the tiller line, one flat on his knee.

"Say it again," he said.

"Your father sold your family's water rights to the Meridian Flow Consortium. Six weeks before he died." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I stole the permits because those rights are the hinge everything else swings on. If an Ashgrove heir contests the sale before the concession expires, the whole chain of title wobbles. If nobody does—" she shrugged, "—the water under the flats belongs to men who've never been thirsty."

Ada looked at the satchel for a long time. Ten years of practice at not wanting things made it easy to look at anything that way: a document, a woman, a future. Just objects on a deck. Just weather.

But his hands weren't cooperating. They'd gone cold around the knuckles.

* * *

He opened the satchel because not opening it would have meant another night of questions, and questions were heavier than paper.

Inside: deeds, mostly. Concession instruments bearing consortium seals, flow surveys stamped and countersigned, a registry abstract listing parcel numbers he recognized the way you recognize the shape of your own hand without looking at it. Parcel 44. Parcel 45. The two springs his grandfather had tapped, the seep his grandmother had lined with stone, the shallow well that had never once gone dry even in the year the rest of the basin cracked like old paint.

His father's signature sat at the bottom of each page. *Tomas Ashgrove.* The same slanted, impatient hand that had signed Ada's first hunting license, that had filled out his mother's death record, that had written *gone to work the east canals, back by planting* on the note that had turned into ten years of silence.

Ada read the date. Then he read it again, because the first time his eyes had slid off it like water off wax.

Six weeks. His father had signed away the water six weeks before dying, and had died without telling him, without writing to him, without so much as leaving the papers where a son might find them and burn them out of spite. He'd just sold it. Sold the only thing the family had ever owned outright, the thing Ada had spent a decade pretending he didn't carry in his chest like a second heartbeat.

"He was sick by then," Sefi said quietly. She'd stopped poling; the boat drifted. "The registry clerk who processed it told me. Coughing blood for a month before he signed. The consortium's man came to him three times."

"So they waited until he was dying." Ada heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "That's not coercion. That's just patience."

"It's how they do it everywhere." She resumed poling, slow strokes, keeping them in the deeper channel. "They don't steal. They wait. They find the man who's drowning and offer him a rope priced at everything he owns."

Ada turned pages. His father's signature deteriorated across the documents—not in legibility but in pressure, the ink going thin, the hand going careful, a man conserving strength even in his letters. On the last deed, dated nineteen days before the death record, there was a clause added in different ink, different hand: *Seller acknowledges receipt of consideration in full.*

Consideration in full. Forty years of his family's sweat, priced and paid, while the buyer's clerk wrote tidy summaries and the seller coughed into a rag.

"I tracked people for these people," Ada said. It came out before he could stop it. "Early on. Before I learned to erase myself. Fugitives, mostly—but twice it was claim-jumpers, men who'd squatted on parcels Meridian wanted. I brought them in. I never asked what happened after."

Sefi didn't say anything. She let the silence sit there, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

It was past noon when he found the anomaly, and he found it the way he found everything—by noticing what didn't fit.

The flow surveys. Consortium hydrologists had surveyed the flats eighteen months ago, measuring the aquifer, projecting monsoon recharge, establishing the baseline data on which the concession valuation rested. Standard work. Ada had read a hundred such reports in his ranger years, enough to know their rhythm: measurements, seasonal adjustments, projections, signatures.

These were too clean.

Every reading clustered. Not suspiciously clustered—a layman would never see it—but clustered all the same, the way a tracker knows the difference between deer sign and deer sign dragged through the brush to fool him. Real field data has noise. Wind in the readings, heat, instrument drift, a bad day, a hung-over technician. These numbers had no bad days. Eighteen months of measurement and not one anomalous entry, not one recalibration note, not one instrument flagged for service.

Nobody's that good. Nobody's that lucky. Somebody had smoothed the data, and smoothing data on an aquifer survey means one thing: the real numbers said something the consortium didn't want on record.

"What are the real numbers?" he asked.

Sefi glanced back. "What do you mean?"

"The flow data's falsified. Flattened. Somebody went through these surveys and took the noise out, and you don't take noise out unless the noise says the water's moving somewhere it shouldn't." He held a page up to the light, as if the sun could show him the erasures. "Where does the water go?"

For a moment she just looked at him, and he watched her decide something—watched the calculation run behind her eyes, quick as a card sharp counting a deck.

"The settlements' hydrologist—an old man named Ferro, dead now—filed a complaint two years back. Said the eastern seeps were running salt-heavy out of season. Said the aquifer under the flats was being drawn down from the north, from outside the basin, in quantities no registered well could account for." She paused. "Meridian bought his silence with a pension. He took it. Then he drowned during last year's flood, and his notes burned with his house, supposedly."

"Supposedly."

"Supposedly." Her jaw tightened. "If someone was pumping the aquifer from outside the basin—if Meridian's own wells were draining the flats dry before the monsoon ever arrives—then the concession valuation is built on fiction. The water they're buying doesn't behave the way their own surveys say it does. And the survey team that measured it honestly—"

Ada felt it land before she finished saying it. The empty camp. The cold kettles. The gear sealed and stacked, waiting for pickup that never came. Nine professionals who vanished without tracks leading out.

"They measured true," he said. "Didn't they. They ran honest instruments, got honest numbers, saw what the flattened data was hiding—and then they disappeared."

"That's the story I've been chasing since I stole these." Sefi's voice had gone low. "I thought the permits were the weapon. Maybe they're just the bait. Maybe whoever erased that survey team is still watching for anyone who pulls this thread."

The boat creaked. Water slapped the hull in a rhythm that suddenly sounded less like peace and more like a countdown.

Ada looked down at his father's name on the last page. *Tomas Ashgrove*, signing away water he'd spent his life protecting, in a hand gone thin with sickness, six weeks before a death that had come conveniently fast for everyone involved.

And the anger that rose in him wasn't the anger he'd expected. For ten years he'd carried a coal aimed at his father—for the note, for the silence, for the abandonment dressed up as work. He'd assumed the sale would feed that coal. Proof the old man had finally thrown away even the memory of them.

Instead he sat on a stranger's boat holding proof of something else entirely: a dying man cornered by patient men, signing what he couldn't refuse, and then—what? Dying quietly? Or trying, in whatever time remained, to unsign it?

"You knew my father?" he asked.

"No." Sefi shipped the pole and sat down across from him, forearms on knees, close enough that he could see the salt-crack lines at the corners of her eyes. "But the man who processed the deed did. Registry clerk, old fellow named Ovid. He told me Tomas Ashgrove came back to the registry office twice after signing. Twice. Demanding copies of everything, demanding to know the resale clauses, demanding to know if the concession could be contested by an heir." Her mouth twisted. "Ovid said the old man left the second time with a full abstract and a look like a man planning a war he wouldn't live to finish."

The wind shifted. Somewhere north, over the flats, thunder muttered—the season clearing its throat.

Ada folded the deeds along their original creases, slowly, giving his hands work so they wouldn't shake. His father hadn't abandoned the water. His father had been robbed of the chance to fight for it—or had fought, in whatever small, doomed, clerical way a dying man could, and been erased for it, the same way the survey team had been erased, the same way Ada had spent a decade erasing himself.

Same system. Same method. Wait, buy, flatten, vanish.

"The registry," he said. "The causeways close when the monsoon lands. How long do we have?"

"Nine days. Maybe eight, if the season comes down early." Sefi was already reaching for the sail line. "Why?"

"Because if my father spent his last weeks gathering proof, it'll be filed somewhere, or hidden somewhere, or carried by someone who hasn't been killed yet." He stood, bracing against the boat's sway, and felt something in his chest unlock that had been rusted shut for ten years—not forgiveness, not yet, but the end of the war he'd been fighting with a ghost. "And because I'm done tracking strangers for other people's money. From here on, we follow my father's trail."

Sefi raised the sail. Canvas filled with a crack like a shot, and the boat leaned into the current, running north toward the registry and the closing causeways and whatever waited at the end of a dead man's unfinished war.

Behind them, far to the south, the sky over the flats had gone the color of a bruise.
