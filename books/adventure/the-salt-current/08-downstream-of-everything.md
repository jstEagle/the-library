## 8. Downstream of Everything

The canals began to fall the morning after they left the warehouse, and Ada learned that Sefi steered a boat the way she stole: without apology.

She stood at the tiller with her weight on one hip, reading the water the way he read ground. Where he would have seen flat brown current, she saw the seam where two flows met, the slack water behind a piling, the place where debris rode faster than it should and told her the channel below had narrowed overnight. She never announced these readings. She simply adjusted, two degrees here, a hand's width there, and the boat went where she meant it to go.

He sat in the bow with his back against a crate of her stolen permits—her *evidence*, she insisted, though the word still sounded like theft to him—and watched the levees slide past.

They were running the old irrigation works now, the great arterial canals dug before either of them was born, when someone had believed the desert could be taught to bloom. The belief hadn't held, but the ditches remained, and every year at this season they emptied toward the flats, shedding their water ahead of the monsoon so the channels wouldn't burst. Falling water was honest water. It showed you its bones: sandbars surfacing like the backs of animals, dead gates standing open, the stumps of orchards drowned forty years ago rising black from the shallows as if reaching for something.

"You're staring," Sefi said.

"I'm watching."

"For what?"

"Levees." He didn't turn around. "We've been shadowed since the second lock."

Her hands went still on the tiller. Not frozen—*still*, the way a hunter goes still, which told him she'd known too and wanted him to say it first. "How long?"

"Two riders on the east bank since dawn. Gray cloaks, no pack gear, horses too good for canal patrol." He let a beat pass. "One of them has been matching our speed exactly. That's not following. That's escorting."

"Consortium?"

"Who else walks the desert in gray and rides like they paid for the horse with other people's money?"

Sefi laughed once, short and without much humor in it. She brought the boat around a bend where an old spillgate leaned into the current like a drunk against a wall, and when the channel straightened she said, quietly, "I know the one on the bay horse. Vessik. He ran enforcement out of the registry town for six years. He's not here to stop us."

"No?"

"He's here to see how far we get." She said it the way you'd report weather. "If we make the lower causeways, they'll know the permits are moving and they'll have time to decide whether to burn the registry early or just buy whoever's left in it. If we founder—if the boat grounds, if we fight, if anything slows us—they'll take me off this boat and the permits will be back in consortium hands by nightfall, and no one will ever know they were gone."

Ada turned around then. He looked at her across twenty feet of wet deck, this woman who had spent weeks being the shape of his failure, and found that up close she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you'd figure it out anyway, and I'm tired of lying to the only person on this water who isn't trying to kill me."

It wasn't trust. He understood that immediately. It was something more practical than trust—the decision that lying had become more expensive than honesty, made by a woman who did arithmetic like that in her head all day long. He recognized it because he'd been making the same calculations about himself for ten years.

"Then we don't founder," he said.

* * *

They fell into watches the way stones fall into water—quickly, and with ripples neither of them examined.

Sefi took dusk to midnight. Ada took midnight to dawn. The middle hours belonged to him because he volunteered them, and she let him, and neither of them discussed why a man might prefer to be awake while the world was dark. He knew why. The dark was when he'd always done his best work, and his best work had always been alone, and some part of him still believed that if he kept enough of the night to himself, none of this—her, the permits, the name stitched into sealed crates in a dead man's camp—could fully claim him.

The nights were warm and enormous. The canal ran through country that had once been farmland and was now a kind of ruin that pretended to be landscape: broken aqueducts, terraces spilling down hillsides like unspooled thread, the occasional lone palm that had survived every drought and flood and change of government through sheer stubbornness. Bats worked the air above the water. In the distance, sometimes, dogs barked at nothing, or at everything.

On the third night he heard her come up on deck instead of sleeping, and he didn't announce that he'd heard her, and she didn't announce that she'd come, and for an hour they stood at opposite rails watching the same water go by in the dark.

"He sold it," she said finally. "Your father. The rights."

"You've said."

"I say things twice when I want to know if they're true." Her voice came across the deck low and even. "You haven't asked me a single question about it. Three days on this boat and you haven't asked what he got, or who he signed with, or whether he was in the room when the survey data was falsified. A man who doesn't ask questions is a man who already knows the answers hurt."

Ada watched a bat cross the moon. "What do you want me to say? That it surprised me? My father spent my whole childhood selling things. Water rights were just the last thing left."

"That's not what the deeds say."

His head came around. She was leaning on the rail, half-turned toward him, her face unreadable in the dark—and then deliberately unreadable, he realized, the way his own face had been trained to be. Two people on a dark boat, both fluent in the same silence.

"The deeds say what?" he asked.

"They say he tried to buy them back." She pushed off the rail. "Six weeks after signing. Same notary. There's a letter attached to the file—I haven't opened it. It's addressed to an heir." She crossed the deck toward the cabin hatch, and paused beside him close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and river on her clothes. "I'm not giving it to you tonight. You'd read it angry, and angry men steer badly. But I needed you to know it exists, so you'd stop pretending you don't care."

She went below. Ada stood at the rail for a long time afterward, listening to the water, feeling the shape of an unopened letter sitting somewhere below his feet like ballast.

* * *

The agents on the levees grew bolder as the canals fell.

By the fifth day they weren't bothering with distance. Vessik himself appeared at the third lock, standing on the gate platform with his hands clasped behind him, watching their boat descend between the stone walls with the expression of a man inspecting livestock. He was broad and gray-templed and utterly unhurried, and when Sefi brought the boat alongside the lock wall to wait for the water level, he crouched, and spoke down to them in a voice pitched to carry over the water.

"Sefi Maran." He pronounced it carefully, like a man tasting a name he intended to use later. "You've cost us four months of survey work."

"And you've cost three settlements their water," she called back. "I'd call us even."

Vessik smiled without warmth. His eyes moved to Ada, and stayed there, and Ada felt the look like a hand on the back of his neck. "And the ranger. Ashgrove, isn't it? We keep excellent records. You tracked for us twice, years ago. The Kesselrun contract. The man in the western hardpan." He tilted his head. "There's a standing offer on your file. It hasn't expired. Come in, bring the permits, and you'll be paid triple your last rate, with a clean ledger and passage anywhere you want to go. You've spent ten years erasing yourself, ranger. We're the only ones who can guarantee it stays erased."

The lock water rose around them, slow and indifferent. Ada looked up at the man and felt something he hadn't expected—not temptation, exactly, but the ghost of temptation, the memory of how easy it would be. One sentence. One nod. Ten years of careful emptiness, honored and rewarded.

"We'll reach the causeways," Ada said.

Vessik studied him a moment longer, then straightened. "Everyone says that," he said, "right up until the water decides otherwise." And he stepped back from the edge, and was gone into the crowd on the gate platform, and the lock finished filling, and Sefi opened the downstream gates herself because the lockkeeper had stopped coming to work sometime that week.

Neither of them spoke until the town was a mile behind them.

"He offered you your life back," she said at last.

"He offered me a version of it."

"Is there a difference?"

"There always is." He took the tiller from her, and she let him, and they ran the falling canal side by side into the long gold light of evening. "That's the trap of it. They don't offer you what you lost. They offer you a copy, and the copy is comfortable, and you live inside it until you forget the original ever existed."

Sefi was quiet for a while. Then, without looking at him: "Did you forget yours?"

He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he allowed himself to think anymore. "No," he said. "That's the problem."

* * *

On the sixth night the wind changed, and everything after that smelled like rain.

It came out of the south, a wet green smell riding over the dry salt smell of the flats, and Ada woke in the bow at midnight already knowing what it meant. The monsoon wasn't coming. It was *here*—days early, the way the old watermen used to mutter it always came when people had grown confident. He stood up and found Sefi already at the tiller, staring south, her hair moving in a wind that had no business being warm and damp at the same time.

"How long?" she asked.

"Three days to the causeways if the canals hold. Maybe less if we push." He gauged the sky, the pressure behind his ears, the taste of the air. "But the causeways flood first. If the storm comes up before we cross, we won't cross at all. We'll be stuck on the wrong side of open water with the permits and half a boat."

"Then we push."

They pushed. They ran the falling canals through the last of the night and all of the next day, trading the tiller in shifts so short they barely qualified as rest, and somewhere in that grinding rhythm something shifted between them that neither of them named. It happened in small ways. She started leaving tea on the crate beside him without being asked. He started taking the tiller through the narrow cuts without waiting for her to surrender it. When she slept on deck in the afternoon heat, he adjusted the sail a degree to shade her, and when he caught himself doing it, he didn't stop.

They learned each other's silences. Hers came in the evenings, when the settlements' lights showed along the distant levees—she'd go quiet and watch them with an expression he eventually understood was grief, because those were her people's lights, burning on water that might belong to them again or might not, depending on paper she carried and a man she'd cornered in a warehouse. His silences came at dawn, when he checked the southern horizon out of habit and found it empty, and remembered that there was no one anywhere whose job it was to know where he was.

Two kinds of loneliness, running the same canal.

On the seventh evening, moored in the lee of a drowned orchard with the first thunderheads stacked on the horizon like siege engines, Sefi sat down beside him on the deck and handed him the letter.

It was soft with age and damp-stained, folded twice, sealed with wax that had cracked along one edge. His father's handwriting was on the front. *To my son, if he ever claims to exist.*

Ada held it and did not open it.

"Not yet," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because tomorrow we cross the causeways, and after that there may not be a quiet place to read it for a long time." He tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay like a coal. "When I open this, I want to be able to do something about what it says."

Sefi nodded slowly. Then she looked south, at the storm stacking itself against the sky, and her voice when it came was very quiet.

"Ada. The lights are out."

He followed her gaze. Along the far levees, where the registry town should have shown its nightly scatter of lamps above the waterline, there was nothing. No lamplight. No harbor lanterns. No watch fires. Just a long black gap in the world where a town used to glow.

The wind came off the flats carrying smoke.
