## 9. The Registry Burns Early

We smelled it before we saw it.

Sefi caught it first—a sweetness under the canal stink, like burnt sugar and hot iron—and her hands went still on the tiller before her mind caught up to what the smell meant. Ada noticed her stillness before he noticed the smoke.

"What," he said. Not a question. He'd read her face too well already, and she hated how fast that had happened, how a week of shared watches and split silences had taught him her tells.

"Smoke," she said. "Ahead."

They came around the last bend of the causeway channel with the sun going down behind them, and there was the registry town of Qesh-Marr burning against the sky.

It wasn't chaos-burning. That was the thing Sefi understood in the first ten seconds, the thing that turned her stomach from dread into something colder and harder. Chaos burns unevenly—here a roof, there a wall, people running with buckets, fire jumping where the wind takes it. This burned *orderly*. Three columns of black smoke rose from three points: the registry hall itself, the records annex behind it, and the clerk's quarters at the far end of the compound. Someone had chosen what to burn and in what sequence. Someone had started with the paper.

"They're early," she said.

Ada stood in the bow, one hand on the mast step, watching the flames with the flat expression he wore when he was keeping himself from feeling something. "The monsoon's not due for six days. The causeways close in four."

"So they moved the schedule." She heard her own voice come out level and was grateful for it. Underneath the levelness she was doing arithmetic that kept refusing to balance. Sixty days until the permits expired unclaimed. They were on day fifty-one. The registry was the only place a contest could be filed, the only clerk authorized to receive it, the only copy of the settlement rolls that proved who held ancestral claims to the water beneath the flats. Burn the registry, burn the rolls, burn the clerk's memory of them—and when the sixty days ran out, the consortium's claim would stand uncontested not because it was valid but because nothing would exist to argue against it.

"They knew we were coming," Ada said.

"Or they always meant to do this and the timeline just moved up." But even as she said it she didn't believe it, and she could tell by the set of his shoulders that he didn't either. The agents on the levees. The chase through the drowned channels two days ago. The way the pursuit had fallen back once they'd entered the last stretch of open canal—as if they no longer needed to stop the boat, because the destination itself was being erased ahead of it.

She brought the boat alongside the downstream quay, into the shadow of a grain warehouse whose owner had clearly decided tonight was a good night to be closed. The heat reached them across thirty meters of water, a dry pressure on the face like opening an oven.

"You can wait here," she said.

Ada looked at her. In the orange light his eyes went almost colorless. "You're going in there."

"I'm going in there."

"For paper."

"For the only paper left in the world that matters." She was already coiling the mooring line. "The clerk's name is Ovidan Rell. He's kept the settlement rolls for thirty years. If the annex is burning, the originals are gone. But Rell worked from a working ledger—daily entries, cross-references, everything he needed to rebuild the rolls if the vault ever flooded. He told me about it two years ago when I came through asking questions I shouldn't have been asking. He thought I was an archivist." A breath. "He was half right."

"And if Rell's inside?"

"Then I get him out too."

"Sefi."

She stopped. It was the first time he'd said her name without an angle on it—not testing it, not using it as a handle, just saying it—and she felt it land somewhere below her ribs.

"If it's a trap," he said, "it's a very good one. They let us run this far. They burned exactly what we need. And now the woman who stole their permits wants to walk alone into a building full of smoke." He paused. "I've set traps like this. I know what they smell like."

"It smells like burnt paper," she said. "That's all it smells like."

But her hands weren't steady on the line anymore, and she knew he could see it.

* * *

The town had emptied toward the water. That was the pattern in canal towns during a fire—you didn't fight a blaze you couldn't reach; you got your family into a boat and you saved what floated. So the streets between the quay and the registry compound were rivers of abandoned things: a shoe, a child's wooden bird, a market stall with its awning still laced but its goods gone. Somewhere a dog was barking in a rhythm too exhausted to be fear. The heat thickened as she walked, and the light through the smoke turned everything the color of old brass.

Two consortium men stood at the compound gate. She saw them from the cover of a well housing—gray coats, gray faces, the particular boredom of men guarding a job already done. One held a lantern. Neither held a weapon ready, which told her they expected no opposition, which told her the fire was expected to finish everything.

There was another way in. There was always another way in; towns are built by people who expect to be robbed eventually. Behind the clerk's quarters—the third fire, the one already collapsing inward—ran a drainage cut that surfaced inside the compound wall near the records annex. She'd used it two years ago to slip out after her conversation with Rell, and she'd sworn then never to use it again because using a man's kindness twice made it theft.

She used it anyway.

The annex door was hot but not yet burning. Inside, the smoke hung at head height in a slow-rolling ceiling, and below it, in the knee-deep clear air, the shelves stretched away into the dark like rows of standing coffins. She could hear the fire eating the roof above her—a wet, chewing sound—and every few seconds a shelf at the far end shuddered as something collapsed somewhere overhead.

The daily ledger wasn't on the shelves. Of course it wasn't. Rell was a careful man; careful men keep the working book within arm's reach of the chair they die in.

She found him in the reading room off the main hall, and for one full second she thought she'd found his body. He was slumped over the table among drifts of ash that had come down the chimney like gray snow, and his hand was still moving—slowly, tremblingly, but moving—writing in a ledger with a pen that had almost no ink left in it.

"Rell."

His head came up. His eyes were red-rimmed and streaming, whether from smoke or grief she couldn't tell, and for a moment he looked straight through her. Then focus arrived.

"The archivist," he said. His voice was a ruin. "Or whatever you are."

"We need to go. Now."

"I'm not finished." He pulled the ledger toward himself with both hands, absurdly protective, a man sheltering a loaf of bread from gulls. "The roll of Meridian Bend. The roll of the Three Wells. The roll of—" He coughed, a deep tearing cough, and spat something dark onto the ash. "If the originals burn, this is all that's left. Thirty years of my handwriting. Do you understand? Everything those people own, everything they can prove, it lives in this book or it lives nowhere."

She understood. That was the terrible thing—she understood completely, because the permits in the oilcloth wrap against her ribs were the same kind of object, paper that was really water, ink that was really lives. She crouched beside him.

"Rell. Listen to me. The consortium burned your vault tonight. They will burn this room next. Whatever you haven't written down in the next five minutes dies with you unless you walk out of here carrying it." She took his wrist. His pulse fluttered like a trapped moth. "Come with me. Both of you."

For a moment he wavered. She watched him weigh it—thirty years in this chair, the weight of a life measured in entries—and she watched the fire make the argument for her as part of the ceiling came down in the main hall with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

"My legs," he said simply. "They don't work so well anymore."

"Then I'll carry you and the book both, and you can insult my technique the whole way."

Something moved in his ruined face that might have been, in a different life, a smile. He closed the ledger, wrapped it in the oilcloth from his desk drawer with the automatic precision of decades, and pushed it across the table to her.

"Not me," he said. "The book goes with you. I go nowhere—I'd never survive the channels, and you know it, girl. But there's something else." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord a key, small and brass and worn nearly smooth. "The strongbox under the floor of my quarters. Copies of the survey flow-data. The real numbers, before the consortium's men corrected them. A young ranger brought them to me eight months ago and asked me to hold them until someone came who'd know what they meant."

The floor tilted under Sefi. "A ranger."

"Desert service. Ashgrove, he said his name was." Rell's streaming eyes fixed on hers. "Old man. Half dead already. Said his son had gone invisible and he wanted to leave something visible behind. I never understood what he meant." He pressed the key into her palm and folded her fingers over it. "Do you understand?"

She did not answer. Her throat had closed around the shape of Ada's name.

Above them, the roof of the annex gave way.

* * *

She came out through the drainage cut with the ledger strapped inside her coat and the key biting its teeth into her fist, and the world outside had gone from brass to blood—the fire fully loose now, the whole compound roaring, sparks rising in a column that bent east with the wind. Behind her, through the grate, she could hear Rell moving back toward his chair. She had offered twice. He had refused twice. Some men choose their fires.

At the mouth of the cut she stopped, because there was a figure standing in the alley with a drawn bow, and the figure was not Ada.

Gray coat. Consortium issue. Young, maybe twenty, holding the bow the way conscripts hold weapons—correctly and without love. His eyes went from her face to the smoke-stain on her collar to the shape of the ledger under her coat, and she watched him arrive at the arithmetic: this was the thief, this was the prize, this was the moment his superiors had spent weeks trying to engineer.

"Don't," she said.

"You're under writ of recovery," he recited. "Surrender the documents and—"

The arrow never left his string, because Ada came out of the dark behind him like something the flood had sent, and there was no elegance in it—one hand taking the bow limb, one forearm across the boy's throat, down, done, the whole thing lasting less time than a heartbeat. Ada knelt on the boy's chest, took the arrows from his quiver and snapped them one by one and dropped the pieces in the mud, and then looked up at Sefi with an expression she had never seen on him before.

Not anger. Recognition.

"Ashgrove," she said. "Your father. Eight months ago. He came here."

The boy under Ada's knee made a small sound. Ada looked down at him, and something passed over his face—she would think about it later, turning it over like a stone found in a riverbed—and then he stood and stepped back and let the boy scramble away into the smoke, empty-handed, alive.

"He what?" Ada said.

* * *

They didn't talk on the boat. The fire lit the channel ahead of them the whole way downstream, so that they rowed out of Qesh-Marr through their own long shadows, and the heat followed them for a kilometer before the night finally closed in cold behind.

Sefi sat in the stern with the ledger on her knees and the key in her pocket and told him all of it: the old man in the doorway of the registry, the flow-data, the words *his son had gone invisible*. She watched the sentences land in him one at a time, each one striking someplace different—confusion at the data, pain at the son, and at the last, the word *invisible*, something that went deeper than pain, down where he kept the things he never talked about.

"He was gathering proof," she said. "Before he died. He was trying to undo the sale."

"You don't know that."

"Rell said—"

"Rell said an old man left a box." Ada's voice was very controlled, which she had learned was worse than his uncontrolled voice. "Old men say many things. My father sold our water and died and none of it required a conspiracy. Men sell what they have and leave. It's the oldest story there is."

"Then why does the consortium want you dead?"

That stopped him. The oars kept moving—he never stopped moving, that was his nature—but his eyes came up and held hers across the dark water.

"They don't want me dead," he said slowly. "They want me *found*. The agent on the levee, the one who offered—" He shook his head. "They've known where I was since the flats. If they wanted me buried, I'd be buried."

"Because you're the heir." She said it plainly, because there was no longer any use being gentle with it. "Because if an Ashgrove contests the sale, the permits lapse and their claim collapses. And because if an Ashgrove *doesn't*—if you stay invisible, if you stay gone—they win by default. You're not a target, Ada. You're the whole game."

The oar-drip counted seconds. Somewhere behind them Qesh-Marr glowed on the horizon like a coal that wouldn't die, and ahead the channel ran black toward the marshes and the defectors and the sixty days bleeding down to forty-nine.

"There's a strongbox," she said. "Under the floor of the clerk's quarters. His key." She held it up; it caught the distant firelight. "Flow-data. Real numbers. Your father put them there."

Ada looked at the key for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—not softer, exactly, but aimed differently, the way a current changes direction around a new rock.

"The quarters were the third fire," he said. "The one that was already down to the foundations when we arrived."

"Yes."

"Then whatever was in that box—"

"Burned. Or—" She turned the key over in her fingers, and hope did what hope always does, which is find the crack in any wall. "Or someone got there first. Rell said a young ranger brought the data. Rangers travel in pairs sometimes. And the defectors in the marsh—the survivors of the vanished team—if anyone else knew what your father was carrying—"

Ada was already reaching for the chart case. "The marsh camp. Two days' run upstream if the channels hold."

"The channels won't hold. The monsoon—"

"Then we run ahead of it." He unfolded the chart across the thwart between them, and in the dying light from the burning town behind, his finger found the marshes, the reed country, the drowned labyrinth where the vanished survey team had gone to ground. "Forty-nine days. A burned registry. A dead clerk's key. And a box that may or may not exist under a floor that may or may not be ash." He looked up at her, and for the first time since she'd known him, Ada Ashgrove smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "These are the best odds we've had all season."

Sefi laughed—short, involuntary, surprised out of her—and then put the key away against her skin, where it lay cold as a promise.

Behind them, Qesh-Marr burned. Ahead, the water ran toward the reeds, and somewhere in the reeds, if the fire hadn't taken it, an old man's last visible thing was waiting to be found.
