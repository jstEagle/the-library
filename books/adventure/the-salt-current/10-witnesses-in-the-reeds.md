## 10. Witnesses in the Reeds

The marsh swallowed the causeway three days after the registry burned.

We poled into it at first light, Sefi at the bow, me at the stern, pushing through water the color of weak tea. Reeds stood ten feet tall on either side, dense as a wall, and above them the sky was the flat gray of tin. Somewhere behind us, the smoke from the registry town still rose—a smudge on the horizon like a thumbprint on glass.

Sefi hadn't spoken much since she'd come out of that fire with the clerk's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. She'd burned her hands doing it. She wouldn't let me look at them.

"Left here," she said finally, pointing with her chin at a gap in the reeds so narrow I'd have called it a shadow. "The old survey channels. They flood every season but nobody dredges them anymore."

"You know this country."

"I know every country the consortium has ruined." She said it without heat, the way you'd recite a shipping manifest. "There's a blind back in here. A place where the water goes still and the reeds close over. My father used it when I was a girl, running contraband salt. Nobody's found it since."

I didn't ask how she knew it still existed. I'd learned in the last week that Sefi didn't guess. She either knew or she didn't say.

We poled for an hour. The channel narrowed, widened, forked, narrowed again. Twice we passed drowned fence posts with wire still strung between them, the top strand just breaking the surface, and once the roof of a barn, gray shingles furred green with algae. The monsoon hadn't even properly arrived yet. This was just the preamble, the water feeling out its old paths like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

Then the channel ended at a wall of reeds that looked exactly like every other wall of reeds, and Sefi shipped her pole.

"Here," she said.

"It's a wall."

"It's a door." She reached forward and parted the stalks with both hands, and there was a gap, and beyond the gap the water opened into a pool maybe forty yards across, ringed so completely by reeds that from the air it would have been invisible. A blind, all right. A room with no door and no ceiling.

We tied the boat to a dead willow and I got my first good look at the place. There was a platform on the far side—old timber on pilings, sagging but sound—and on the platform, a stack of crates under a tarp. And beside the crates, a cookfire, cold.

I put my hand on my rifle before I thought about it.

Sefi saw the motion. "If someone's here, they've been here a while. That tarp's been patched twice." She paused. "And they left the fire ring neat. That's not how you leave a camp if you're running."

It was how you left a camp if you were hiding and disciplined about it. I'd left a hundred camps that way myself. I didn't say so.

We crossed. The crates were government issue—stenciled with the consortium's surveyor's mark, the coiled serpent around a plumb line. The same mark I'd seen on the sealed gear at the empty camp, a hundred miles and a lifetime ago. The tarp had been patched with strips of surveyor's flagging tape, red and white, sewn down with fishing line. Somebody here was making do.

Sefi lifted the tarp's edge. Underneath: instruments. A theodolite with a cracked lens. A case of glass plates, each one labeled in a careful hand. And ledgers—dozens of them, water-stained, their spines cracked from use.

"Survey records," she breathed. She pulled one out and opened it, and her face did something I'd never seen it do. It went young. "These are the originals. These are the *field* originals. The flow measurements, the aquifer readings—" She looked up at me. "Ada. If the consortium falsified the data, the real numbers are in here. This is the proof. This is everything."

"Then why is it under a tarp in a swamp?"

"Because whoever brought it here didn't want it found." A voice from the reeds behind us. Low. Even. The voice of a man who had practiced not being heard for a long time. "Until he was sure of who was looking."

I turned with the rifle up and found myself looking at the business end of a scattergun held by a woman standing waist-deep in the reeds as if the water were a floor. She was sixty, maybe older, gray hair cut short as a man's, sunburned to the color of old brick. Behind her, two more figures rose out of the reeds—one young, one older, both holding poles like they'd been born holding them.

None of them looked like corpses. None of them looked like the vanished survey team should look, after a season of official silence and my own imagination filling the gap with bodies.

"Put it down," the woman said. "Slow. You're in my blind, and in my blind, I'm the law."

I put it down. Sefi raised her hands a fraction, showing the burned palms, which was either surrender or a message, and I couldn't tell which.

"You're the survey team," Sefi said. "The one that vanished."

"We're the survey team that *left*," the woman said. "There's a difference, and the consortium has spent a year and a fortune making sure nobody learns it." She waded closer, the scattergun steady. "Names. Yours."

"Sefi. Of the drowned villages."

Something moved in the woman's face at that. She lowered the gun an inch. "Ryn's daughter."

"You knew my father."

"I signed his death certificate." She said it flatly. "Or the paper that pretended to be one. I'm Odile Vess. I was lead surveyor on the Salt Flats concession. And you," she said, turning to me, and the gun came up again, not quite at me, but near, "I don't know. Which is a problem, because you've got the look of a man the consortium has spent money on."

"Ada," Sefi said, before I could decide whether to lie. "Ada Ashgrove."

The change in the woman was total and instantaneous. The scattergun came down. The two in the reeds went still in a different way—not the stillness of threat but the stillness of people watching something they'd waited for.

"Say it again," Odile Vess said quietly.

"Ashgrove," I said. "It's a common enough name."

"It is not." She waded past me, past Sefi, to the stack of crates, and pulled a ledger from the middle of the stack—a different ledger, older, its cover dark with handling. She opened it to a page near the front and held it out.

The handwriting on that page was my father's.

I knew it the way you know a face in a dream—badly, and then all at once. The heavy downstrokes. The way he crossed his *t*s like fence rails. I hadn't seen that handwriting since I was nineteen years old, since the morning I walked out of his house and told him I'd rather be no one's son than his.

"Your father surveyed this marsh," Odile said. "Twenty-two years ago. Before the consortium, before the permits, before any of it. He mapped these channels by pole and chain, and his numbers were so good that when the consortium needed a baseline to falsify, they stole *his*." She tapped the page. "Every fake reading they've filed in the last three years deviates from this book. Every one. He was the honest baseline they built the lie on top of."

"Then he sold them," I said. My voice came out strange. "I know about the sale. The water rights. I've seen the deed."

"You've seen the deed they *filed*." Odile closed the ledger and held it against her chest like a child. "Sit down, Ashgrove. Both of you. This is a long story and the water's rising and I'd rather tell it once."

We sat on the edge of the platform. The young one—his name was Pell, I'd learn—brought tea that tasted of reed and smoke. The older one, a heavy man with a surveyor's squint, sat apart and watched the channels and never once looked at us, which told me he was the sentry and the rest of it was theater for my benefit.

"Three years ago," Odile began, "the consortium filed for the concession. They needed baseline data to justify the diversion works—the canals, the levees, the whole machinery of owning the water. They hired a survey team. Nine of us. We took the readings. And the readings were wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Not wrong. *Inconvenient.* The aquifer under the flats doesn't behave the way their model needed it to. The flood pulse is bigger than they claimed. The recharge zones are where the settlements say they are, not where the consortium's maps put them. If we filed honest numbers, the concession arithmetic collapsed. So they asked us to adjust."

"And you adjusted."

"I filed a protest," Odile said. "In writing. Which was, in retrospect, the stupidest brave thing I have ever done. Two weeks later, one of my team drowned in a dry season. A week after that, another fell ill with something the doctors couldn't name. The consortium's message was clear without being sayable. So we did what your father did before us." She looked at me. "We disappeared. Nine surveyors, vanished without tracks, into the biggest marsh on the continent. Let them spend their money looking for bodies."

"The camp," I said. "The sealed gear. The kettles."

"A stage set. We walked out through the flooded channels at night, on paths your father's old maps showed and theirs didn't. They still don't know we're alive. Officially, we're a mystery, and the mystery is useful to them—dead witnesses can't testify, and dead men's data can be rewritten at leisure." She set the ledger down between us. "We've spent a year copying, cross-checking, hiding. Waiting for two things. A legal door, and a courier we could trust."

"The permits," Sefi said. "The registry."

"The permits," Odile agreed. "And then the registry burned, and we thought the door had closed. And then a thief and a tracker come poling into my blind, and the tracker's name is Ashgrove." She leaned forward. "Do you know what your father was doing the last year of his life, boy?"

"I know what he wasn't doing. He wasn't writing to me."

"He was working for us. Quietly. He'd found out what the consortium did with his baseline—found out they'd used his own honest numbers as the skeleton for their lie, and that they'd bought his water rights with the proceeds of a fraud built on his life's work. It ate him. He came to us through intermediaries. He carried messages. He was gathering the chain of evidence—the deeds, the falsified filings, the protest I filed, the deaths. He had a packet, Ashgrove. A complete packet. Enough to break the concession in any honest court." She stopped.

"Where is it?"

"He was supposed to deliver it to us the week he died." Odile's voice didn't soften, because it couldn't afford to. "The consortium found out. There was an 'accident' on the causeway road. The packet never arrived. We've spent two years assuming it was lost, or burned, or in a consortium vault."

The tea was cold in my hands. I was looking at my father's handwriting and hearing a story I had no architecture for: not the man who sold everything and died fat on the proceeds, but a man who spent his last year crawling toward a redemption he never got to make, and died for it on a road, alone, because his son had walked out and taken the only other witness to his life with him.

"He didn't sell us," I said. It came out before I could shape it into something presentable. "He didn't—"

"The sale was coerced and the signature was the price of staying alive long enough to fight it," Odile said. "We have the letters. His letters. He wrote to us the way he never wrote to you—because he thought shame was a debt you pay in private." She watched me take that. She was kind enough not to watch too closely. "The packet might still exist. He was a careful man. Careful men don't carry everything in one saddlebag."

"Then where?"

"That's the question that kept us alive this long, and it's the question we can't answer from a blind in a marsh. What we *can* do is this." She put her hand flat on the stack of ledgers. "The originals. The falsification, documented. The protest, with my signature. The death certificates I signed that were lies. Nine witnesses, alive. If the registry can be rebuilt—your clerk, Sefi, the one who kept a ledger—if there's any legal door left anywhere in this delta, we can walk through it. But not as ghosts. Ghosts get buried twice."

"And as what?" Sefi asked.

"As claimants. As plaintiffs. As the survey team that never died." Odile looked at me, and her eyes were the flat gray of the sky. "And as heirs. Because there's a deed out there with an Ashgrove signature on it, and a contest requires an Ashgrove's hand against it, and every day that passes, the consortium files one more paper making their theft into law."

The water in the blind had risen an inch since we sat down. I could see it on the pilings—two dark lines, the old flood and the new. The reeds hissed. Somewhere out in the drowned country, thunder was deciding whether to commit.

"My father is dead," I said.

"Your father is *evidence*," Odile said. "Dead men can't be coerced. Dead men's names carry weight that living ones spend years earning. And you—" she pointed at me, and for the first time the flatness in her voice cracked, just a hair, and I saw under it a woman who had been hiding for a year and was tired down to the waterline, "—you have spent ten years learning how to be no one. I'm telling you that the only person who can do what needs doing next is *someone*. That's the price. That's always been the price."

Sefi's burned hand found my sleeve. She didn't grip it. She just rested it there, the way you'd rest a hand on a fence rail, and said nothing, and the nothing said everything she'd been not saying since the warehouse.

The sentry in the reeds stood up suddenly, pole in hand, head cocked toward the western channels.

"Boat," he said. "One boat. Running dark. Coming up the old survey channel."

Odile was on her feet before he finished. The scattergun was up. The whole blind changed from a room into a fort.

"How long?" she asked.

"Minutes. They're not poling. They've got an engine."

Nobody poled at night with an engine through channels that weren't on any consortium map—unless they'd gotten the map from the same place Odile's team had gotten theirs. Unless someone had talked. Unless someone had followed us.

Odile looked at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing all her life: what a stranger costs, what a stranger's name buys, and which one you can afford.

"Ashgrove," she said. "You wanted to stay invisible. I'd suggest you start practicing. Because if that's who I think it is in that boat, then the man they're looking for isn't me."

* * *

We killed the fire and sank the ashes. The crates went back under the tarp, and the tarp under cut reeds, and the ledgers—Odile kept those in a waterproof case that went into the water itself, tied beneath the platform on a line, where no searcher would think to look for paper. Then we took the boats into the reeds and waited, poles shipped, breathing through our mouths.

The engine sound came up the channel like a slow saw. It stopped maybe two hundred yards out. Voices—two men, unhurried, the unhurriedness of men with authority. A lantern beam swept the reed tops, and the light passed over us the way weather passes over a stone.

Then one of the voices said, clear across the still water:

"Ashgrove! We know you're in there. Old friends want a word."

I had heard that voice before, years ago, across a desk in a consortium waystation, when I'd signed papers I didn't read and took coin I didn't ask about. The man who'd paid me for my first erasure. The man who'd taught me that a name was just a thing you could sell.

Odile's hand closed on my wrist in the dark. Not holding me back. Holding me *here*.

"Well," she whispered, and there was almost a smile in it. "Seems your father's name has finally found you. Question is, boy—what are you going to answer to?"
