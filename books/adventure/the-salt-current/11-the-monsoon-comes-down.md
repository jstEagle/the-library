## 11. The Monsoon Comes Down

The wind arrived before the rain did.

Ada felt it first as a pressure behind his ears, the same sensation he'd known since childhood when a storm stacked itself up over the far ranges and began to lean on the world. He was standing watch at the edge of the defectors' camp, in the reeds where the marsh met open sky, and the sky had gone the color of old tin. Not clouded—*stacked*. A wall of it, purple-black, rising from horizon to horizon like something being poured out of heaven by a careless hand.

"Kess," he called over his shoulder. "How long?"

The woman who'd led the survey team—and who had once, in another life, signed her name beneath Ada's father's on flow reports Ada now kept folded against his chest—came up beside him. She looked at the wall of weather the way a surgeon looks at a wound she's already decided to open.

"Hours," she said. "Maybe fewer. The birds left this morning. Did you notice? Everything with wings is gone."

He had noticed. He'd told himself it meant nothing. That was the old habit, the ranger's habit: explain away every omen until the ground itself betrayed you.

Behind them, the camp stirred. Nine people, all that remained of a survey crew of thirty-one, plus Sefi, plus himself, plus a leaking flatboat named—by one of the younger defectors, a boy called Tam with salt-cracked lips and an irrepressible grin—the *Unlikely*. They had spent three days in the reeds cataloguing evidence: falsified flow data, coerced signatures, letters in Ashgrove handwriting that Ada still could not make himself read past the first line. Three days of quiet work. The weather had been patient with them.

It was done being patient.

"We move everything to high ground?" Kess asked, but she already knew the answer. There was no high ground. There was reeds, water, and the boat, and the boat sat low in the water even unloaded.

"No," Ada said. "We move everything *aboard*. If the camp floods, we lose the cache. If the cache floods, we lose the only proof those permits were bought with lies."

Sefi appeared at his elbow without seeming to arrive, the way she always did. Her hair was tied back, her sleeves rolled, and she carried the oilskin packet of permits strapped across her chest like a second heartbeat. She'd worn it there since the registry burned. She would wear it into the grave, Ada suspected, or into whatever came after graves.

"You heard him," she called to the camp. "Boat. Now. Everything waterproof goes aboard, everything else gets buried under the cook tarp and written off."

There was no argument. That was the strange mercy of imminent catastrophe—it simplified committees. The defectors moved with the brisk economy of people who had spent years hiding from an organization that owned the water, and they loaded the *Unlikely* the way soldiers load a retreat: fast, silent, nothing precious left behind.

Ada worked the bowline while Tam ferried armloads of wrapped instruments down the reed-choked bank. The air had gone thick, syrupy, tasting of ozone and wet stone. Somewhere far off—not so far as he wanted—a sound rolled across the flats that was not thunder. It was lower than thunder. It was the sound of a very large amount of water deciding, all at once, to be somewhere else.

"That's the head of it," Kess said quietly. "The flood pulse. When the monsoon breaks over the highlands, the whole system fills in hours. Rivers that have been dry for a decade remember what they are." She looked at Ada. "Your father mapped this pulse. Did you know that? Every gauge station on the upper channels. He used to say the desert doesn't drown, it *wakes up*."

"I don't want to talk about my father."

"I know." She took the coil of rope from his hands and shouldered it. "But you're going to need to know what he knew, in about two hours. So start listening now."

---

The first drop hit the deck like a thrown stone.

Then ten drops. Then the sky simply came apart.

It did not rain; it *descended*, a vertical ocean, a cataract with no edges, and within ninety seconds Ada could not see the bow of the boat from the stern. The lanterns turned to smears of yellow. Sound collapsed into a single roaring note, vast and featureless, the voice of something that had no interest in being understood. The reeds that had sheltered the camp for three days lay flat as combed hair. And the water—

The water rose.

Not gradually. Not the polite inch-by-inch of ordinary floods. The marsh *swelled*, the way a chest swells with a breath, and the *Unlikely* lifted off the mud it had been resting on and swung sideways on its line like a horse feeling fire under it. Ada grabbed the gunwale and felt the whole vessel tremble beneath him, alive, alarmed.

"Cut us loose or we'll snap the cleat!" someone shouted—Tam, maybe, or nobody, maybe just the storm talking.

Ada cut them loose.

For a moment they drifted free in the dark, nine souls and a thief and a tracker on a flat-bottomed boat in the middle of a drowning world, and then the current found them and took hold, and the *Unlikely* went downstream faster than any boat Ada had ever steered had any right to go.

"Bail!" Sefi screamed, and he understood why a half-second later, when the deck tilted under him and he saw the water coming over the transom in a sheet—not spray, not seepage, but a green-black hand pouring itself aboard. The leak. The leak they'd been nursing since the canal towns, patched twice with pitch and once with a prayer, had opened like a mouth.

They bailed.

That was the whole of the next hour. There was no navigation, no plan, no conversation beyond the minimum grammar of survival—*bucket, pass, bail, again*. Ada knelt amidships with a dented cooking pot and threw water over the side until his shoulders burned and his hands went numb around the handle. Beside him Sefi bailed with a hat whose brim had torn away, and across from her Kess bailed with a section of split bamboo, and Tam bailed with both cupped hands because he was fifteen and stupid and brave, and the water came in faster than four people could throw it out, and then five people, and then six, everyone who wasn't holding a line or holding on.

Lightning opened the sky and showed Ada the world for one white instant: brown water to every horizon, drowned reeds, a floating cook-tarp turning slowly like a jellyfish, and the *Unlikely* sitting three inches deeper in the flood than she had any business sitting.

Three inches. He'd spent ten years reading country by inches. He knew exactly what three inches meant.

"She's going down," he said. Not a question. A report.

"Then we keep her afloat until she changes her mind!" Sefi shouted back, and laughed—actually laughed, wild and soaked and terrified, and Ada thought: *this is what she sounds like when she's closest to dying.* He filed it away the way he filed away tracks, the way he filed away everything, because filing things away was how he stayed alive, and then he hated himself for it, and then he bailed some more.

---

The camp, when the lightning showed it to them, was gone.

Not washed away—*drowned*. The tents stood to their poles in brown water, sagging, their contents blooming outward in slow clouds of paper and cloth. The evidence cache they hadn't gotten aboard was somewhere under two meters of new river. Kess watched it disappear with a face that didn't change at all, which was how Ada knew how much it cost her.

"The copies," she said. "Tell me the copies made it aboard."

"Every page," said a defector named Oren, hugging an oilskin roll to his chest like an infant. "I carried them myself."

"Then the camp was only ever a place we slept." Kess said it firmly, the way you say things you intend to believe. "A place is not a purpose."

The current carried them past the drowned camp and out into what had been, yesterday, a chain of shallow sloughs and hummocks, and was now simply sea. Inland sea. The monsoon had come down on the whole basin at once, and the flats that Ada had crossed dry-shod a lifetime ago—or three weeks ago, time had gone strange—were becoming the thing they had been before men named them: a shallow, violent, temporary ocean, salt and fresh fighting each other in the dark.

And the *Unlikely* sank lower. Inch by inch. Bail by bail.

Somewhere past midnight—Ada couldn't have sworn to the hour—the arithmetic stopped working. Six bailers, one widening seam, and a hull taking water faster than human arms could answer. He felt it in the deck's response to his knees, the soggy lethargy of a boat that has begun to think of itself as a reef.

"We need to lighten her," he said. "Everything that isn't people or paper goes."

They threw the cook gear. They threw the spare canvas, the tool chest, the trade goods, a crate of survey instruments worth more than most towns' annual harvest—Kess herself pitched it over the rail without a flicker, which told Ada everything about what kind of woman she was. The boat rose a grateful finger's width.

It lasted twenty minutes.

Then the storm, as if insulted, leaned harder, and the water came back, and they bailed, and bailed, and bailed.

Ada's arms stopped reporting in. His hands moved on their own, pot up, pot over, pot down. Somewhere in the rhythm he stopped being a man with a history—a deserter from his own name, a son of a signature he hadn't read, a tracker who had tracked for the consortium and never asked what the numbers fed—and became simply a creature keeping other creatures above the line. It was, he realized dimly, the cleanest he had felt in ten years. Terror stripped everything else away. There was no room in a sinking boat for the man he'd been avoiding. There was only the next bucketful, and the person beside him doing the same, and the strange wordless contract forming between them with every throw: *I will not stop if you do not stop.*

Sefi's shoulder pressed against his. He pressed back. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that a bucketful wouldn't say better.

---

Dawn, when it finally came, was less a sunrise than a rumor—a gray thinning of the violence, the rain slackening from cataract to merely torrential. Ada lifted his head and discovered he was lying in six inches of standing water on the deck of a boat that was, impossibly, still a boat.

Around him, the others. Oren asleep sitting up, still clutching the oilskin roll. Tam gray-faced but grinning at nothing, his hands blistered from cupping water for six hours. Kess at the steering sweep, having taken the last shift without asking anyone, her hair plastered to her skull, her eyes fixed on the drowned horizon with the patience of bedrock. And Sefi, curled against the mast base, awake, watching him.

"You counted," she said.

"What?"

"You've been counting the bail-outs. I could see your lips moving. You count everything."

He had. Four thousand and some. He hadn't known he was doing it. "Habit," he said.

"It's a good habit." She wrung out her sleeve and looked out at the water. "Look."

He looked. The flats were unrecognizable and yet—he knew them. That drowned hummock had been a marker cairn his father had built when Ada was seven. That half-submerged line of dead acacias marked the old channel, the one the maps had lied about, the one his father's private charts had shown true. The flood had erased the desert and somehow made it legible again, the way a negative makes a photograph. He was navigating his own childhood.

"There," Kess said suddenly, pointing east. "Smoke."

Thin, stubborn, human smoke, rising off a rise that was still—barely—an island. Another party riding out the flood. Or worse: the consortium's boats, hunting them through the drowned channels, using the chaos as cover.

Sefi's hand found the permit packet at her chest. Ada's hand found the bail-pot. The *Unlikely*, riding low and stubborn, drifted toward the smoke with the current's full blessing, and none of them had the strength left to argue with it.

"Everyone bail," Ada said hoarsely. "One more time. All together."

They bailed.
