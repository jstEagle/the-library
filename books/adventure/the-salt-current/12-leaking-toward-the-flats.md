## 12. Leaking Toward the Flats

The leak announced itself on the second day, politely at first—a seep along the port seam that the bilge cloth absorbed without complaint. By the third morning it had stopped being polite.

"Bail," Sefi said, and handed me the gourd before I'd finished waking. She was already bailing with the other one, her arms moving in a rhythm she didn't have to think about, water over the side in a flat silver arc, again, again. "It's coming through faster since dawn."

I took my end of the work. The boat was a canal skiff, thirty feet of shallow-drafted stubbornness, built for slow rivers and patient men, and it had never been meant for open water. But the flats were not open water either—not yet, not quite. They were something stranger: a desert drowned to the depth of a man's chest, so that the drowned things still showed. Fence posts. The crowns of tamarisks. A windmill's fan blades breaking the surface like the fin of some prehistoric fish, turning slowly when the wind moved them, creaking out its last complaint into the flood.

We were adrift on the Salt Current itself—the old name for it, the one the settlements used, the one the maps had retired a generation ago. When the monsoon came down hard enough, the whole basin filled from every direction at once: canals overflowing, washes running backward, the flats rising until they became a shallow sea forty miles across with no shore you could point to. The defectors' camp was under six feet of brown water. Their evidence was lashed under our thwarts in oilcloth. Nine of us in a boat built for four.

Old Meric sat at the stern with his hands folded, watching me navigate, or watching me fail to. The other survivors—Toma, the two sisters whose names I kept trading back and forth in my head, the clerk's boy who hadn't spoken since the registry burned—were bailing, sleeping, or staring at the horizon as if it owed them an explanation.

"Where are we?" Toma asked. It was the question he asked every hour, and every hour the answer was the same, and every hour he asked it anyway, because asking was a kind of ballast.

"Two miles off the old Kessel road," I said. "Give or take."

"You can't know that. There's no road. There's no anything."

"There's a culvert," I said, and pointed. He looked where I pointed and saw nothing, because what I was pointing at was a darkening in the water the size of a wagon bed, twenty yards to starboard, where the current ran differently than the current around it. Where the water moved against the wind instead of with it, there was a channel underneath. Where the channel ran, there had been a road. Where the road ran, there had been a culvert, and the culvert was still down there doing its job, funneling the flood along a path it had followed for eighty years.

Meric grunted. It was the first sound of approval he'd made since we left his flooded camp, and I felt it land somewhere in my chest like a coin dropped down a well.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about growing up in a drowning country: you learn the ground twice. Once as ground, and once as the thing the water remembers.

My father taught me the first version. We walked the flats when I was small enough to ride his shoulders, and he named everything—every dry wash, every salt pan with its own particular crust, every rock that leaned. *That's the Anvil,* he'd say. *You can find the Anvil from anywhere if you lose yourself. Stand on it and you're never more than a day from home.* I hated those walks. I hated the heat and the naming and the way he treated the desert like a relative he was fond of but never called.

What he never told me—and maybe never knew, because the monsoons of his lifetime were smaller, meaner things—was that all of it would come back to me upside down. That the Anvil would be a ripple pattern on the surface where the current split around a stone. That the Kessel road would be a seam of darker water. That the tamarisk stand behind our house, the one I climbed to get away from him, would be a green island the size of a table, and that I would steer toward it with my heart going like a hammer, because home was under it, and home was gone, and home was also right here.

I had spent ten years making myself unfindable. Burned the ledgers, changed the name on the ranger rolls, let my face go forgettable and my trail go cold. And now here I was, standing in a leaking boat on top of my childhood, navigating by memory of a man I'd spent ten years hating, and finding—I kept testing the word, turning it over, looking for the flaw in it—finding my way.

* * *

The patching was Sefi's idea, which stung, because it should have been mine.

"We can't bail forever," she said on the third afternoon, hauling up the canvas we'd salvaged from the defectors' flooded tents. "The seam's opened a hand's width. Every hour it opens more. We pitch a patch over it or we swim."

"With what pitch?" Meric asked. "We've got half a pot."

"Then we use half a pot." She said it the way she said most things—no argument in it, just the shape of a decision already made. She'd been like that since the storm. Terror had burned something out of her, or burned something clean, and what was left was very simple and very fast.

The problem was that pitching a hull from inside a floating boat is a job for a contortionist or a fool. The seam ran along the port side, below the waterline, which meant someone had to go under. The water was warm as bathwater and opaque as tea, and it was full of things the flood had loosed—fence wire, drowned livestock bloated white, the occasional snake riding a branch and furious about it.

"I'll do it," I said.

Sefi looked at me. "You've done it three times."

"And I'll do it better the fourth time."

"That's not how practice works," she said, but she was already tying the rope around my waist, her fingers quick at the knot, and I understood that this was how we argued now—by agreeing, quickly, while pretending we hadn't.

I went under.

Underwater on the flats is another country. The light comes down brown-gold, in shafts, and everything is soft-edged and slow. I found the seam by feel—my father's voice in my head again, absurdly, saying *learn it with your hands, boy, your eyes will lie to you in the dark*—and pressed the canvas patch against the planking while Sefi fed down the pot of pitch on a string, heated in a tin over our little spirit stove until it smoked. Working pitch underwater should be impossible. It isn't, quite. You press the hot mass into the gap and it blooms outward like a black flower, sealing itself against the cold water, and you hold it there counting seconds while it sets, and the count is the longest minute of your life, and then you kick for the light.

I came up gasping. Sefi's hand was on my arm before I'd finished surfacing, steadying me, and she didn't take it away for a moment longer than steadying required.

"Hold?" she asked.

"Hold," I said. "For now."

"For now," she repeated, and something passed between us that neither of us named, the same unnamed thing that had been passing between us since the warehouse, since the canals, since the night she fell asleep sitting up against the mast and I sat awake watching the levees for agents and watching her, in equal measure, and told no one, including myself.

* * *

On the fourth day the wind died completely, and the flats became a mirror, and navigation got strange.

With no wind and no current worth the name, the surface went glass-still, and the drowned world showed itself doubled. I stood at the bow and looked down and it was like flying. Fence lines ran away beneath us in perfect perspective. A dead orchard stood in rows, its trees leafless and pale, fruit still hanging—salt-preserved, inedible, ghostly. And there, off the port bow, unmistakable even after twenty years: the Anvil.

I hadn't seen it since I was fourteen. It was a granite shelf the size of a barn roof, and the flood had turned it into a shallow reef, and the water broke over its upstream edge in a line of ripples as fine as stitching. I stared at it until my eyes burned.

"What is it?" Sefi said quietly. She'd come up beside me without my hearing her, which should have frightened me and didn't.

"That's the Anvil."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was, I suspected, how she'd gotten everything she'd ever stolen—by waiting for people to fill their own silences.

"My father used to say you could find your way home from it. From anywhere on the flats, you make for the Anvil, and from the Anvil you're a day from the house." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He made me walk it. Made me prove it, summer I turned twelve. Dropped me at the south pan with a skin of water and told me to find it. Took me nine hours. He was waiting on it when I got there, sitting cross-legged like it was a porch, and he didn't say well done. He just said, *now you can't be lost.*"

"Was he right?"

"He was right." I looked at the reef, at the fine stitched line of ripples, at the orchard standing in its rows beneath us like the bones of somebody's whole life. "I've been trying to get lost ever since. Ten years. Do you know how much work it is? Staying lost? It's a full-time occupation. It's harder than tracking. Anybody can vanish. Staying vanished—" I shook my head. "You have to keep doing it every single day. Refuse every ledger, every handshake, every name. It's a job you can never finish, because the day you stop, you're somebody again."

Sefi watched me with an expression I couldn't read, or could read too well.

"And today?" she said.

Today. Today I had steered us true across forty miles of drowned childhood by landmarks only I remembered. Today I had gone under the water four times to save a boat carrying eight strangers and a woman I—well. Today I had answered to *Ashgrove* twice without flinching, because Meric had started using it, having learned it from Sefi, and I had not corrected him.

"Today I'm navigating," I said.

She nodded slowly, as if I'd said something larger than I had, or exactly as large.

* * *

The fifth day, the leak came back.

Not the old seam—the patch held—but a new one, aft, where the hull had taken a knock during the storm we'd never actually seen, some submerged fence post or gate frame striking us in the chaos. It opened slowly, almost apologetically, and by noon we were bailing in shifts again, and by evening the shifts were getting shorter and everyone knew what shorter shifts meant, and nobody said it.

It was the clerk's boy who saved us, in the end. He hadn't spoken since the registry—since he'd watched the consortium's men feed ten years of settlement records into the fire while he ran with the one ledger Sefi thrust into his arms. All that silence, five days of it, and then at dusk he tugged Sefi's sleeve and pointed at the flooded tamarisk island off our starboard bow—the island I'd been steering toward all day without telling anyone why—and held up both hands, and shaped a word with his mouth: *house.*

"He's right," I said. My voice cracked on it. "There's a house there. Under the trees. My house."

Sefi looked at me. "Your house is under the water."

"The house is under the water. The loft isn't. It was a stone foundation—my father overbuilt everything, it was the one thing he overbuilt—and the loft floor sits above the old high-water line. If the monsoon hasn't topped it—" I did the arithmetic and didn't like any version of it, but it was arithmetic with a floor under it, unlike the alternative. "We can put in. Haul out. Dry wood, solid ground under the boat, time to re-pitch every seam properly. Two days' work and we're seaworthy again."

"It's your childhood home," Sefi said. Not an objection. A sounding.

"It's a roof," I said, and heard how unconvincing it was, and gave up on convincing anyone, including me.

We came in on the last light. I took us through the tamarisks on the deep channel—I knew the deep channel; I'd poled a flatboat through it at fifteen, smuggling, though that's a story for a different accounting—and grounded us gently on the slope below the house, and the keel touched bottom for the first time in five days, and the sound of it went through the whole boat like a bell. Eight people sat very still in the fading light, listening to the extraordinary ordinary fact of not drifting.

The house stood above us on its stone base, black against the last orange band of sky, the tamarisks grown up around it like a family that had moved in during our absence. Water lapped the foundation two feet below the loft line. The door was gone; the windows were dark holes. Somewhere inside, a beam creaked in the swell, the house breathing.

Nobody moved. Then Meric stood, joints cracking like the beams, and looked up at the dark doorway, and then at me.

"Ashgrove," he said. "This yours?"

And I stood in the shallows of my own drowned front yard, ten years of vanishing pooled around my ankles, and heard myself answer:

"Yes."

The word cost nothing. That was the astonishing part. I'd assumed it would cost everything—that claiming the name would be like opening a vein. Instead it was like setting down weight. Like the moment in deep water when you stop fighting the current and discover it has been trying to carry you all along.

We unloaded in the dark, passing bundles hand to hand up the slope—the evidence, the ledger, the pitch pot, the children's faces pale above the gunwales—and I went in first, because it was my house, and struck a light.

The loft was as I remembered it and nothing like I remembered it. Dust and bird nests and my father's tools hanging on the wall where he'd left them, handles furred grey. And on the workbench, under a hide of dust thick as felt, a strongbox with the Ashgrove brand burned into the lid.

Sefi came up the ladder behind me and stopped when she saw it. Her eyes went from the box to my face and back.

"Ada," she said carefully. "Is that—"

The box was locked, and the lock was rusted, and I broke it with my father's own hammer, one blow, and lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, were papers. Deeds. Correspondence. And on top, in a hand I knew better than my own, a letter addressed not to the consortium, not to the magistrate—

To me.

*Ada.* Nothing else. Just my name, in my father's writing, waiting on a workbench in a drowned house for a son who'd sworn he'd never come back.

Behind me, out on the black water, a lantern blinked twice from a long way off. Then again. Steady. Deliberate. Someone signaling—or someone searching.

Sefi saw it too. Her hand found my shoulder.

"Whatever's in that letter," she said, "read it fast."
