## 13. What Sefi Stole

The rain came in the third hour of the second day adrift, a fine gray hiss against the tarpaulin that sounded less like weather and more like something being whispered just out of earshot. Sefi sat with her back against the mast step, knees drawn up, watching the water rise in the bilge and counting the seconds between bails. Ada had taken the first watch. He was good at watches—good at the whole silent economy of the boat, the way he moved without asking where things were, the way he read the sky before she did. It should have made her trust him faster. Instead it made her nervous, because competence like that had a history behind it, and histories were what she'd spent six years avoiding.

The defectors' boat rode low behind them on a long painter line, half-swamped but stubborn, carrying four people who had once been the government's most trusted surveyors and were now refugees in their own country. Yara was asleep under oilcloth. The old man—Dessal, he'd said, though everyone called him the Cartographer now—sat at the stern of the trailing vessel with his hands folded, watching the drowned flats go by as if he were reading them.

Sefi bailed. Counted. Bailed again.

"You're going to wear a hole in the deck," Ada said from under the tarp.

"You're going to let the boat sink while you sit there philosophizing."

"I'm not philosophizing. I'm listening to the hull." He tilted his head, and she saw it then—the way he actually was listening, the way a man listens to a horse's breathing or a patient's chest. "She's talking to us. She says we're taking water through the garboard seam, slow, manageable. She says the wind will back around by evening."

"She talks a lot for a leaking boat."

"They all do." A pause. Rain on canvas. Somewhere out on the flooded flats, a bird cried twice and went quiet. "You've been staring at me for an hour."

"I've been staring at the bilge."

"The bilge is behind you."

Sefi looked down at the bail bucket in her hands, its bottom furred with salt crust, and understood that she had run out of small maneuvers. They were three days out from the marshes, two days from anywhere, sealed together under a square of canvas no bigger than a grave blanket, and the storm had stripped away every excuse she owned. There was nothing left between them but the truth, and the truth was a heavy thing to carry alone across open water.

"Ada," she said. "Do you know why I stole the permits?"

He didn't answer right away. Under the tarp his face was all planes and shadow, the desert written into him even here, even now. "I assumed you needed money."

"I need money. Everyone needs money." She set the bucket down. "That's not why."

* * *

Her village had been called Tessel's Landing, which was a joke, because there had been no landing at Tessel's for forty years—the river had moved, the way rivers do, sliding west a few meters each season like a sleeper turning over—and the name was all that remained of whatever ambition had founded the place. Two hundred people. A date grove. A school with a roof made from a decommissioned airship's envelope, silver-blue, that glowed at dusk like a piece of fallen sky.

She was nine when the engineers came.

They arrived in white vehicles with no markings except a serial number, and they were polite in the way that people are polite when politeness costs them nothing. They ran instruments along the riverbank. They interviewed the elders about water tables and seasonal flow. They gave the children candy wrapped in foil printed with a stylized droplet, and Sefi kept hers for weeks without eating it because the foil was pretty, and she can still remember the exact blue of it, and she has never forgiven herself for that—for loving the wrapper of the thing that killed her home.

"They called it a hydrological correction," she told Ada. The rain hissed. The boat talked softly to itself beneath them. "That's the phrase I want you to hold onto. Hydrological correction. Not diversion. Not seizure. Correction—as if the river had made an error and they were fixing it. As if two hundred people living where they'd lived for six generations were a typo."

Ada said nothing. He was good at saying nothing; she'd noticed that early, back on the hardpan, reading his trail. But he turned toward her fully now, giving her the whole of his attention, and she found—to her surprise—that she could speak under that attention rather than despite it.

"The consortium wanted the river's headwaters for the new canal scheme. The southern branch. Tessel's Landing sat on the old channel, and moving the water meant erasing us—not with soldiers, nothing so honest. They rerouted the flow upstream, quietly, over one dry season. The river didn't flood us. It simply stopped coming." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced this part so many times, in so many safehouses, that it came out smooth as river stone, and she hated the smoothness. "By the time anyone understood what was happening, the wells were failing. The date grove went first. Then the animals. People left in ones and twos, and each departure made it easier for the next family to leave, because a village is only real while enough people agree to stand in it."

"And you stayed."

"My mother stayed. She filed complaints. Eleven of them. I helped her copy them out because my handwriting was better than hers." Sefi laughed, a short ugly sound. "Eleven complaints to eleven offices, and every office sent us to another office, and the last office sent us back to the first. That's when I learned the shape of the machine. It isn't cruel. Cruelty would be simpler. It's a circle drawn so large you can't see the curve—you just keep walking straight and arrive where you started."

"What happened to your mother?"

The question landed exactly where he must have known it would land. She respected him for not softening it.

"She stayed until the end. The end was the year the government declared the district 'nonviable' and offered relocation stipends—paper money for paper people. She signed. She said, at least it's acknowledgment. At least someone wrote our names down before—" Sefi stopped. Started again. "She died the following winter, in a resettlement block outside Harrowgate, of a fever that a village well and a decent doctor would have laughed at. I was fourteen. The stipend paid for the funeral and half a year of rent, and after that I was nobody's daughter in a city that had never heard of Tessel's Landing."

The rain thickened. Out on the water, the defectors' boat creaked, and Dessal's voice carried faintly across the gap, steady, telling Yara something about the depth of the channel ahead. Sefi pulled the tarpaulin edge tighter against the wind and found that her hands were shaking—not from cold, or not only from cold.

"So when I tell you the permits are revenge," she said, "I want you to understand that revenge is the smallest word for it. What I actually want is the opposite of a hydrological correction. I want the record corrected. I want the names written down. Sixty days, the permits stay unclaimed, the rights revert to settlement law, and for the first time in the history of these flats, the water belongs to the people standing on it instead of to men in white vehicles with serial numbers."

"That's not revenge," Ada said quietly. "That's restitution."

"Don't make it noble. I've done ugly things to get here." She held up her hands, palms out, as if the evidence were written there. "I've forged documents. I've blackmailed a registrar. In Corren I burned a warehouse full of consortium shipping records, and the fire spread to the building next door, and a family lost everything they owned, and I have never gone back to find out if they were all right, because going back would mean knowing, and knowing would mean—" She stopped. Breathed. "Penance. That's the other word. The permits are my penance. Every thief knows you can't steal back what was taken. You can only steal forward. Steal something that hasn't been taken yet, and give it away before it becomes yours."

Ada was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, rougher, as if something had shifted in his throat.

"I tracked people for them," he said.

Sefi went still.

"Not the way you think. Not always. At first it was legitimate work—desert ranger, missing prospectors, runaway debtors, the ordinary sadnesses of the hardpan. But the consortium contracts paid best, and I told myself the targets were always criminals. Squatters on concession land. A woman who'd stolen survey equipment." His jaw worked. "A woman who'd stolen survey equipment," he repeated, and looked at Sefi, and she felt the sentence close around them both like a hand. "Six years ago. Near the southern branch. I brought her back to a consortium processing station at Vell's Crossing, and I collected my fee, and I never asked what happened to her. I told myself it wasn't my business. That was the whole architecture of my life, Sefi—it wasn't my business. Other people's water. Other people's villages. Other people's disappearances. I erased myself from every ledger I could find so that nothing would ever be my business again."

"Why?" The question came out of her before she could weigh it. "Why does a man choose to be no one? You're not a coward. I've watched you walk into a burning registry and dive into floodwater. Cowards don't do those things."

He almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes.

"Because being someone nearly destroyed me once already," he said. "My father was Ashgrove of the eastern flats—warden's line, three generations, a name that opened doors and closed others. And I watched what the name cost him. Watched him sign things he hated signing, shake hands with men he despised, trade pieces of himself a meter at a time until there wasn't much left. I decided the only way to survive having a name was to have none. So I became a tracker with no surname, no holdings, no debts I couldn't walk away from. I got very good at leaving." He paused. "And then I took one last contract to fund the final disappearance, and the trail led me to a dead camp full of gear stamped with my own family name, and I understood that the world doesn't care how thoroughly you erase yourself. It keeps a copy. Somewhere, it always keeps a copy."

The rain eased. Through a gap in the tarpaulin Sefi could see the light changing—the gray lifting toward pewter, the wind backing around exactly as the hull had promised. On the trailing boat, Dessal raised a hand: channel deepening ahead, all well.

"Your father," Sefi said carefully. "The deeds aboard this boat. Have you read them?"

"Some."

"Then you know he sold the water rights. And you know when." She watched his face. "Three weeks before he died, Ada. Three weeks. A man doesn't liquidate a three-generation inheritance three weeks before his death unless he's been forced to—or unless he's doing something with the money that he doesn't want recorded. The defectors have correspondence. Dessal knew him. When we're through the channel, when there's solid ground under us, you're going to hear what your father was actually doing in those last weeks, and it's going to rearrange everything you think you know about why he was the way he was."

Ada looked at her for a long time. The rain had stopped entirely now, and the light through the canvas gap fell across his face in a thin bright bar, and Sefi thought: there it is. The crack. The place where the erasure ends and the man begins.

"You're telling me this now," he said slowly, "because you confessed first. Because absolution works better as a trade."

"I'm telling you because we're both thieves," Sefi said. "You stole yourself away. I stole my way back. And neither of us believes yet that what we did can be forgiven—but we're both still rowing toward the same registry, so we may as well stop pretending we're strangers."

Something moved through his expression then—too quick to name, gone before it finished arriving. He reached past her, took up the bail bucket, and began working it in an easy rhythm, water over the side, water over the side, and the boat rose fractionally in the flooded dark, and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder under the tarpaulin like conspirators, like penitents, like the only two people left in a drowned world who knew each other's worst sentences by heart.

Absolution, Sefi thought. Neither of us believes in it yet. But we're practicing.

It was near dusk when Dessal's voice came across the water again, and this time it was not steady.

"Sefi. Ada." The old man stood in the stern of the trailing boat, pointing north with an arm that did not tremble, which somehow made it worse. "Lights. Three of them. Running without lanterns shaded—they're showing themselves on purpose." He lowered his arm. "Consortium colors. And they're not searching the channels."

"They're waiting for us," Sefi said.

"No," Dessal said. "Worse. They're heading for the registry ruins—and they're moving like men who've just learned the deadline moved too."
