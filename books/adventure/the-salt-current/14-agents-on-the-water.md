## 14. Agents on the Water

The rain came in sheets now, not gusts, and Ada had stopped trying to tell where the sky ended and the water began. The marsh had drowned three days ago. What had been reed beds and mudflats was a single gray sea dotted with the crowns of dead trees, and their boat—a flat-bottomed skiff the defectors called *the Mule*—rode it like a leaf rides a gutter.

He took the bow watch because his eyes were still good, even if everything else about him felt borrowed. Behind him, Sefi bailed with a dented pot, steady as a metronome. Old Hask sat at the tiller with a tarp over his shoulders and a river chart gone soft as cloth in his lap. The other two defectors—Marrow and a young surveyor named Pell—huddled under the canvas amidships, guarding the oilcloth bundle that held everything: the falsified flow tables, the coerced signatures, the letters in his father's hand.

Ada tried not to look at the bundle. Looking at it made something move in his chest that he didn't have room for.

"Channel's widening," Hask said. "That's wrong. Should be narrowing here."

"Everything's wrong," Marrow said. "The whole basin's wrong. The consortium rerouted half the watershed and then lost track of its own lies."

"That's how we win," Pell said, too bright, too young. "They can't fake data on water they can't find."

Nobody answered him. The rain filled the silence for them.

Ada swept the horizon again and stopped sweeping. Two miles back, maybe less, where the drowned treeline broke the gray: movement. Not waves. Waves didn't hold formation.

"Hask."

"I see 'em." The old man didn't lift his head from the chart. "Two boats. Outboards. They've been running our wake since the reeds."

"You knew?" Sefi's voice came sharp over the bail-pot.

"I hoped I was wrong." Hask finally looked up, and his eyes were calm in the way of men who had already survived one disappearance. "They'll have radios. We don't. They'll have fuel to burn. We don't. So we don't race them. We lose them."

"How do you lose boats in open water?"

"You don't lose them in open water." He tapped the chart. "You take them somewhere boats shouldn't go."

* * *

The drowned channels were an old irrigation grid beneath the flood, and only Ada knew them—not from maps but from childhood, from summers when the water ran low and he'd walked these levees barefoot with his father's hand around his wrist, naming every gate. Now the gates were a fathom down and the levees were sandbars with trees growing out of them, and he steered by memory alone, calling turns to Hask before the old man could see them.

"Hard left at the leaning palm. Then straight until the second gatepost—there'll be a roof under you, don't stop."

"A roof," Marrow repeated, white-knuckled on the gunwale.

"The Ferro homestead. Flooded eleven years back." Ada heard his own voice go flat. "The consortium called it a survey adjustment."

Sefi looked at him across the bailing water. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. They had confessed their worst to each other under a tarpaulin two nights ago, and since then there was a new quiet between them, not comfortable exactly, but load-bearing.

The first consortium boat fell behind at the leaning palm—their pilot wouldn't commit to the submerged fence line. The second was braver or stupider, and it hung on their wake through the gate channel, close enough now that Ada could see the men in it: gray slickers, no insignia, the particular stillness of professionals.

"They're herding us," Hask muttered. "Watch. They'll cut ahead and block the outlet."

He was right. Where the channels braided back together at a drowned crossroads—an old market square, church bell tower poking from the water like a broken tooth—the second boat swung wide and settled itself across the exit, engine idling, patient as a trap.

Three men in it. One stood at the bow, and even at forty yards, even through rain, Ada knew the set of his shoulders.

Corven. His old training officer. The man who had taught him how to walk so quietly that ledgers forgot you existed.

Hask killed the engine. The Mule drifted. Rain hammered the canvas.

"Options," Sefi said quietly.

"We run the shallows left of the tower," Ada said. "It's eighteen inches deep over the old wall. They won't follow."

"And if they do?"

"Then we fight in eighteen inches of water against three armed men, and I like our odds less than I'd like."

"There's a third option," Corven called across the water, and his voice hadn't changed either—level, warm at the edges, the voice of a man offering you a chair. "Ashgrove. Isandro Ashgrove, ranger first class, retired from nothing because you were never officially there. You want to talk options? Come aboard. Just you. Your friends can keep their boat and their lives, both of which are currently on loan."

Ada's hand found the gunwale and gripped it.

"Don't," Sefi said.

"He hasn't asked me anything yet."

"He's about to ask you to sell us. That's always the question."

Corven's boat held position, rocking gently, obscene in its stability. "I know what they told you," he called. "That you're a loose end. That the file on you is thick enough to drown in. Here's what I know that they don't: the director wants you *found*, not filed. Ten years you spent making yourself invisible—that's a skill, son, and skills like yours don't grow on trees. Come back. Full amnesty. A posting wherever the map ends. Or—" he spread his hands, almost apologetic, "—we do this the wet way."

"The wet way," Marrow whispered. "He means—"

"I know what he means," Ada said.

His old life was sitting right there in a boat, offered up like bread. A name cleared, a debt erased, a door back into the world on the world's terms instead of his exile's terms. All it cost was four people in a leaking skiff and a bundle of paper that proved a consortium had drowned a valley and forged its way into owning the water under it.

All it cost was becoming, permanently and officially, the man he had spent ten years pretending he wasn't.

"Ask them," Sefi said softly, "what happened to the last people who took the offer."

But Ada was already looking at Corven, and Corven was already smiling, because Corven had trained him and knew exactly which hook fit which fish.

"You're tired, Isandro. You've been tired since your father died. Tired men make clean choices. This is the cleanest one you'll ever get."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and afterward none of them agreed on who moved first.

Pell later swore it was Marrow, hurling the boat's anchor into the shallows to foul the consortium propeller. Sefi swore it was herself, cutting the Mule hard toward the church tower. Ada privately believed it was Hask, who simply put the tiller over without a word, the way he did everything—without a word.

The Mule surged. The consortium boat's engine roared in pursuit, then shrieked—a grinding clang as the anchor line wrapped the shaft—and in that screaming moment of advantage Ada did the only thing he could think of that Corven would never predict.

He gave himself away.

He stood up in the bow, in full view, and shouted across the water—not to Corven, but to the two crewmen flanking him, the hired men, the ones whose loyalty was a wage and nothing more:

"The flow tables are real! The surveys were forged! Ask yourselves why the pay is this good!"

And then the Mule was through the gap past the bell tower, hull scraping stone, and behind them the drowned square erupted with shouting, and whether it was doubt or just chaos, Ada couldn't tell and didn't wait to learn.

They ran the flooded streets for an hour, walls rising on both sides, windows at eye level full of green water and old silt. It was Sefi who noticed, first, the weight of the boat sitting wrong.

"The bundle," she said.

They tore open the canvas amidships. The oilcloth was there. The bundle inside it was not—only a decoy, blank ledger pages weighted with a brick, tied off in the same knot.

Marrow went the color of the water. "The cache. The real cache—it was in the *other* bag, the one we—" He turned to Pell. "We stowed it in the reeds before the storm. We were going to double back—"

"The reeds are forty feet underwater," Hask said.

Silence, except for rain and the drip of the bail-pot. Eleven years of evidence—his father's letters, the coerced deeds, the falsified tables—sitting in a waterproof case at the bottom of a drowned marsh, and somewhere behind them, engines restarting.

Ada looked at Sefi. Sefi looked at Ada.

"You know where it sank," she said. It wasn't a question. He'd been the one carrying the bag when the storm hit; he'd lashed it to the reed clump himself.

"I know where it sank," he said. "And I know what's coming after it."

Because Corven knew too. Corven had seen the bag. And whatever else Corven was, he was thorough, and the first thing a thorough man does with a drowning valley is send divers down to where the truth sleeps.

Hask put the tiller over, back toward the reeds, back toward the deep water, back toward everything they'd just barely escaped.

"Son," the old man said, eyes on the gray horizon, "you'd better hope your father's maps show what's under that water better than theirs do."

Ada opened the oilskin packet at his belt—his father's hand-drawn charts, rescued from the defectors' camp—and found, marked in faded ink at the drowned reed beds, a depth notation in his father's writing, dated three weeks before the old man died.

Someone had measured those waters. Someone had known.

"Dive at dawn," Ada said. "Before they do."
