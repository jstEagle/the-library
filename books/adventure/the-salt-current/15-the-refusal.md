## 15. The Refusal

The agent's name was Corven. I knew it the way you know a scar—by touch, not by looking. Ten years ago he'd signed my first contract, back when I still used my full name on paper and thought a ranger's badge was a kind of armor instead of a leash.

He stood at the bow of the consortium skiff with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting for rain he'd already been promised, and he said the words I'd spent a decade making sure no one could say to me.

"Ashgrove. Or do you prefer Villalobos now? We have both in the files."

The channel around us had gone the color of pewter, drowned salt flat stretching to every horizon, dead tamarisks standing in six feet of water like a congregation that wouldn't leave. Behind me, Sefi held the tiller of our overloaded boat with one hand and a boathook with the other, and the defectors' two remaining crew crouched low among the salvaged crates—the ones that hadn't burned, the ones we'd already failed to protect once.

Corven smiled. He'd always had a smile like an open ledger: everything itemized, nothing hidden, nothing free.

"Here's what I know," he said. "You tracked for us eleven years. You were good. You were *discreet*. And then you fell off every map we own, which took talent, and I've always respected talent." He gestured at the water between us as if it were a table he could slide a contract across. "Walk away from this boat. Walk away from her. The registry fire goes on your record as an accident you tried to prevent. Your debts are cleared. Your name is clean. You can be invisible again—or better than invisible. Visible, respectable, paid. Your choice."

I felt the offer land in my chest the way cold water lands—not sudden, but everywhere at once, finding every gap.

That was the thing about Corven. He didn't threaten first. He offered first. The threat was just the receipt.

* * *

I want to be honest about how long I stood there.

Not because I was tempted to take it—I want that on the record too, even if the record is only this chapter, only me talking to nobody—but because I understood exactly what he was doing, and understanding didn't make it hurt less. He wasn't offering me safety. He was offering me the person I used to be, shrink-wrapped and returned to me like lost luggage. Eleven years of tracking debtors and claim-jumpers and inconvenient witnesses for men who owned the water under other people's feet. A decade of erasing myself so thoroughly that when Sefi laid her trail across the hardpan for me to find, legible on purpose, respectful on purpose, it was the first time in years anyone had spoken to me in a language I recognized.

She'd left a light burning in an empty camp because she knew a man who wanted to disappear would follow anything that looked like a door.

And here was Corven, holding open another door.

"Take your time," he said kindly. "The water's not going anywhere. Neither is she." His eyes flicked past me to Sefi, and something in the flick told me the offer had a second page I hadn't been shown. "Though I should mention—we have a standing instruction regarding the permit thief. Alive is preferred. Not required."

Sefi didn't flinch. She'd told me once, under tarpaulin in the dark, about the village the first survey drowned—how the river came sideways in the night, how the water didn't hate them, it just didn't know they were there. She'd learned to stand in front of things that didn't know she was there. Consortium agents. Floods. Me, in the beginning.

"You're wasting your breath," she called to Corven, conversational, almost friendly. "He already decided. He just likes hearing himself decide."

That wasn't true. But it was true enough to shame me into motion, and maybe that's why she said it.

* * *

Here is what I actually decided, in those long seconds while the pewter water slapped both hulls and the drowned tamarisks kept their silent congregation:

I decided that invisibility had never been mine. That was the lie I'd been living inside for ten years. I thought I'd erased myself, but erasure is a service someone else performs—you pay for it with obedience, and the payment never stops, and the ledger stays open forever. Corven had my names in his files. Both of them. Every alias I'd worn since I walked out of my father's house had been filed, cross-referenced, priced. I hadn't disappeared. I'd been *stored*.

A man who owns your absence can sell it back to you whenever he likes.

So the only way out was through the part of me he couldn't file. The part that had gone into the flood for a stranger dying of thirst on the hardpan. The part that navigated by drowned landmarks only a boy who grew up on these flats would remember. The part that had spent three days bailing beside people whose names I still half-knew, and found, somewhere in the rhythm of it—bail, pass, bail, pass—that I didn't want their names. I wanted their company.

Corven was offering me my old life. My old life was a storage unit.

"No," I said.

Corven tilted his head, genuinely curious, the way a surveyor is curious about ground that doesn't match the map. "No?"

"No." Louder, so the defectors' crew could hear it, so Sefi could hear it, so the water could hear it if water keeps records, which on these flats I'm starting to believe it does. "I'm done being in your files. So I'm going to do something about that."

I reached inside my coat and took out the credential case.

* * *

Every ranger carried one. Oilskin wallet, brass clasp, government stamp. Inside: the commission paper with my full legal name, the tracking license, the consortium rider that most rangers pretended not to read—the clause that made us contractors in fact and law enforcement in appearance, the clause that let the consortium deputize us against anyone whose claims inconvenienced its surveys. I'd read that clause at twenty-two and told myself it was fine, it was the system, everyone worked within the system. At thirty-two I'd stopped telling myself things. I just kept the wallet, because keeping it meant I could still prove who I'd been, and proving who I'd been was the last thread tying me to a world I was afraid to rejoin.

Ten years of carrying proof of myself so I'd never have to be myself.

I opened the brass clasp.

Corven saw it and his smile changed—it went from ledger to arithmetic. "Ashgrove. Think about what you're—"

"I've thought about everything," I said, "for ten years. It's the only thing I did out there. You don't need to track a man who never stops running through himself."

I struck the fire steel against the boat's iron rail, caught the spark in the oilskin, and held the wallet up while it caught.

It burned fast. Oilskin wants to burn; it's been wanting to burn since it was made. The commission paper curled first, my full name browning at the edges—*Isandro Ashgrove Villalobos*, all of it, the whole inherited weight—and then the license, then the rider, the clause, the system, all of it going up in a bright ragged flag of flame that I held high over the gray water like a signal.

Because that's what it was. That was the whole point.

A fire on open water, in failing light, at the end of the dry season's last calm day, is visible for miles. Levees, causeway towers, the high decks of consortium barges—all of it flat country, all of it sightlines. Any watcher with glass would see a flame where no flame should be, and any competent pursuer would read it the way trackers read tracks: as intent. As a beacon. As *here*.

Which is exactly what I wanted them to think. Just not where I wanted them to come.

"The east channel," I said quietly, without turning around, and Sefi was already moving—she'd understood before I struck the spark, because of course she had, she was the one who'd taught me that a trail can be legible on purpose. The defectors' crew bent to their oars. Our boat swung away from Corven's skiff, west wall of the channel sliding between us, and I stood in the stern holding my burning name aloft like a lantern for every spy on the levees to fix their glasses on.

"There he is," Corven called after me, and there was no kindness in it now, only the flat satisfaction of a man updating a file. "There he *is*. Isandro Villalobos. Do you know what happens to deserters on the water, ranger? There's no hardpan to vanish into anymore. The monsoon's coming. Everything floods. Everything floats. Everything gets *found*."

"Then you'll know where to send the letter," I said, "when the permits lapse."

His face did something small and expensive at that—the first crack in the ledger. They'd counted on the permits surviving until claim. Sixty days, and the clock was theirs. Unless the heir contested. Unless the heir stood in daylight and signed his name.

They'd known an Ashgrove heir existed. What they hadn't priced was the heir choosing it.

I let the last corner of the wallet burn down to my fingers and dropped the blackening scrap into the flood. It hissed, spun, sank. Ten years of stored absence, filed at last in the only archive that doesn't keep copies.

* * *

We ran the west channels hard for an hour, poling through drowned scrub, using the dead orchard rows for cover, and when the light finally failed we tied up in the lee of a flooded grain elevator with its top floor still above water, and nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Old Wenna—one of the defectors, a hydrologist with forearms like dock rope—climbed down from the watch perch and looked at me the way you look at weather that's changed.

"They'll have your description on every boat by morning," she said. "Bounty, probably. Consortium bounties pay in land grants. Half the levee towns will hunt you for a fence post and a deed."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway. In front of witnesses." She shook her head slowly. "You know what you were before tonight? Cargo. Useful cargo, but cargo. We carried you because Sefi vouched." She put her hand out, palm open, the way boat people seal things when there's no paper worth trusting. "Tonight you're crew."

I took her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and it closed around mine like a lock seating home.

Sefi came aft and sat beside me on the crate rail, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a while we watched the east channel where the pursuit lights were already moving—three, four, five points of lamplight converging beautifully, precisely, on the exact stretch of empty water where a man had publicly destroyed himself an hour ago.

"They'll figure it out by midnight," she said.

"By then we're through the narrows."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. "You know what you gave up tonight."

"My name."

"You gave up the *option* of having no name," she said. "There's a difference. From tomorrow, everywhere you go, you're the man who burned his papers and turned Corven down to his face. That's not anonymity. That's a reputation. Reputations are just names with weather on them." She glanced at me sideways. "Scared?"

I thought about it honestly, because she'd earned honesty and I was out of practice at anything else.

"Yes," I said. "But it's a different scared. Before, I was scared of being found. Now I'm scared of being *needed*. It's worse. But it points forward."

Sefi laughed—a short, real laugh, the first I'd heard from her since the registry burned. Then she sobered, and nodded east, toward the dark line where the flats ended and the ruins of the registry town lay somewhere beyond the drowned causeways, two days' pole and sail if the weather held.

"Then hold on to it," she said. "Because the filing window closes in nine days, the storm season opens in five, and as of tonight, the only Ashgrove signature the consortium fears is sitting in an open boat with a bounty on his head."

Far off to the southeast, below the horizon, the sky flickered—once, twice—dry lightning walking the edge of the coming monsoon, counting us down.

Old Wenna appeared at the hatch with a lamp and a chart, and behind her the whole crew was looking at me. Not at Sefi. At me. Waiting to see which way the current ran.

"All right," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, and realized I was signing something right now, in the dark, with no ink. "Here's how we get to the ruins."
