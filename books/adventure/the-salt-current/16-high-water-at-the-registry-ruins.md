## 16. High Water at the Registry Ruins

The registry town announced itself by smell before sight—wet char and river mud, the scent of a thing that had burned and then been drowned. Sefi stood in the bow of the boat as they came around the last bend of the canal, pole in hand, and watched the ruins rise out of the morning haze: a long stone building with its roof gone to ash, its walls standing like ribs, half-walled now by monsoon water that had climbed three feet up the masonry and left a tideline of silt.

Behind her, Ada said nothing. He'd been saying nothing for two days, since the defectors' leader, Maro, had pressed his father's hidden letter into his hands and told him what it meant. She could feel the silence coming off him like heat off stone.

"You see it?" she asked.

"I see it." His voice was rough. "It's smaller than I thought a registry would be."

"Everything that decides who owns water is small," Sefi said. "That's how they get away with it."

She poled them in past a drowned fence line, past the black stubs of trees that had shaded the clerks' courtyard once, and brought the boat alongside the loading dock where the water now lapped at the second step. The hull scraped stone. Somewhere inside the ruin, something moved—a deliberate sound, wood against wood, the sound of a person who wanted to be heard but not surprised.

Sefi raised both hands off the pole, empty. "We're not consortium," she called. "We came about the permits."

A long pause. Then a voice, dry as the flats themselves: "Everyone who comes here says that first."

* * *

The clerk's name was Odile Vess, and she had rebuilt her life out of memory and salvaged shelving in the one corner of the registry whose walls still held a roof. She was perhaps sixty, thin as a reed, with ink stains worked so deep into her fingers they looked like bruising. Her reconstructed office was a miracle of order: shelves built from door planks, ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, a counting table balanced on bricks above the flood line. On the wall behind her, drawn in charcoal on the smoke-blackened stone, was a map of the entire registry's holdings—the whole legal skeleton of the salt country, reproduced from a head that had carried it for thirty years.

"They took the records," she said, pouring tea from a dented kettle into cups that had been mended with pitch. "All of them. I watched them burn eleven years of conveyances in the yard while the town stood and did nothing, because the men with the torches were armed and the town was not." She handed Sefi a cup. "But paper is only the memory of decisions. The decisions themselves—" she tapped her temple "—I kept those."

"How much?" Ada asked. He'd taken the chair nearest the door, the way he always did, and Sefi had stopped remarking on it.

"The flow surveys. The concession schedule. The Ashgrove sale." Odile looked at him without surprise. "You have the look of him. The father. He sat in that same posture, guarding the door of a room he'd already decided to leave."

Ada's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"You knew him," Sefi said.

"I filed his signature four times." Odile set down her own tea. "And I'll tell you what I told your friends in the reeds, ranger, when they came asking: the record I can rebuild will hold in any honest court. But an honest court is not the problem. The problem is the filing itself."

She rose, went to a shelf, and drew out a ledger bound in sailcloth. Opened it flat on the counting table. The pages were dense with her small, upright hand—dates, parcel numbers, names, all of it reconstructed, each entry initialed *OV, ex mem.* From memory.

"This is the concession schedule," she said. "Sixty days from issuance, the permits expire unclaimed if no valid holder files. We are at day fifty-one. Nine days." She turned a page. "The consortium knows this. That's why they burned the registry early—they believed that destroying the record would destroy the deadline, because a deadline requires a document to be measured against. They were almost right. They didn't count on me, and they didn't count on you bringing me proof their survey data was falsified, which Maro's people have, which converts this reconstruction from a curiosity into evidence."

"So we file," Sefi said. "Today. Now."

"With what name?"

Sefi opened her mouth and found nothing there.

Odile's eyes moved to Ada. "The permits were issued against the Ashgrove grant. The sale that conveyed the rights to the consortium was executed under the Ashgrove name. Under settlement law—and this is law older than the consortium, older than the government, law that runs under everything like groundwater—an invalid conveyance can only be contested by the party to it or their heir. In person. By signature. A thief cannot file. A witness cannot file. A ranger with no name in any ledger for ten years certainly cannot file." She closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a thing that has teeth. "An Ashgrove must stand here, in daylight, write his full name on a contest of his father's deed, and swear to it before witnesses. And then every man the consortium can hire will know exactly where he is, and exactly what he's worth, and exactly whom he loves."

The last words landed in the room and stayed there.

Sefi looked at Ada. He was staring at the ledger—at the reconstructed entries, at the place where his family's history had been reduced to dates and figures in a stranger's careful hand—and his face had gone very still, the way it went still before he said something true.

"That's why you needed me," he said quietly. Not to Odile. To Sefi.

"Yes," Sefi said. There was no version of this conversation that survived lying, so she didn't try one. "From the market. From the moment I learned an heir existed. I tracked you as carefully as you tracked me, Ada. I made my trail readable because I needed a tracker, and I kept you close because I needed a name. Both things were true at once. I'm not going to insult you by pretending otherwise now."

"And the rest of it?" His voice didn't rise. That was worse. "The canal runs. The watch shifts. The—"

"Was also true," she said. "You know it was. You bailed water next to me for six days. You dove into a flood for me. None of that was strategy. But the strategy was real too, and I should have said it sooner, and I didn't, because I've spent my whole life believing that the truth is a thing you release in pieces so no one piece can be used against you." She set down her tea. Her hands were steady; she made them be steady. "So. Here is the whole piece. I need your name. Your name is the key. And using you as a key was the plan from the beginning, and I am sorry, and I would do it again, because three settlements' worth of people drink from that aquifer and I have already buried one village."

The silence after that had weight, like deep water.

Odile, to her credit, looked down at her ledger and gave them the privacy of pretending to read.

* * *

Ada walked out onto the flooded dock alone. Sefi let him go. Some doors you had to let a man open himself or he'd never forgive you for the opening.

She stayed at the counting table with Odile, and the old clerk, without looking up, said: "You love him."

"That's not relevant to the filing."

"Everything is relevant to a filing. That's the whole profession." Odile dipped her pen, blotted it. "I watched his father sign those deeds. Did Maro tell you? No—he wouldn't know, he wasn't in the room. I was. Consortium men stood on either side of the elder Ashgrove while he signed, and one of them held a document I was not permitted to read, and the old man's hand shook so badly the signature came out broken, and the consortium agent said, warmly, 'Take your time, sir,' which is what men say when time is precisely the instrument being used against you." She looked up. "Three weeks later he was dead. I have spent nine days rebuilding this record, and I will tell you what I believe in my bones: he signed to buy time, and he died spending it. If his son contests that deed, he finishes what his father started. That is not a trap, girl. That is an inheritance. They are often the same shape."

Sefi's throat was tight. "He spent ten years erasing himself. Every ledger, every roster, every record. He told me once that a name is just a handle other people use to pick you up."

"And now the only way to save the water is to let himself be picked up." Odile smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of someone who had watched governments burn paper for thirty years and outlived all of them. "Yes. That's usually how it goes. The thing you surrendered is always the thing they demand back."

Through the doorway Sefi could see him at the dock's end, standing in the flat grey light, the letter from his father folded in his jacket over his heart. He wasn't reading it. He was just standing, the way desert people stand when they're listening to ground.

* * *

He came back in an hour. The light had shifted; rain was building somewhere west, a bruise-colored wall of it over the flats, and the canal had begun to breathe against the dock stones in a longer, slower rhythm.

"Nine days," he said.

"Eight," Odile corrected. "Filing closes at dusk on day sixty. Today is fifty-two."

"Then we file tomorrow," Ada said. "Not today."

Sefi frowned. "Every hour—"

"Every hour, yes. And if I walk into that registry with my name wet behind the ears and the consortium's scouts on the levees already marking me, we lose everything in an afternoon." He laid his father's letter on the counting table, unfolded it, weighted the corners with the teacups. "Maro said my father gathered proof. Where is it? If I'm going to contest the deed, I don't want to contest it with a clerk's memory and a stolen permit. I want to contest it with his evidence. The falsified flow data. The coercion. All of it, in one hand, presented once, in front of witnesses, so they only get one shot at me." He looked at Sefi, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the weary tracker, not the drowning man, but the son of the man who'd signed with a shaking hand. "One shot. We make it count."

"The defectors' cache," Sefi said slowly. "The evidence they hid in the marshes. The agents took it during the chase."

"Took some of it," Ada said. "Maro told me the survey originals—the sealed field books, the ones with the real flow numbers—were never in the cache. They were split up. Carried separately. Because the defectors trusted no single hiding place." He tapped the letter. "My father's letter references a strongbox. Left with 'the woman who keeps the light.' I thought it was a figure of speech."

Odile had gone very still.

"It isn't," she said. "There's a lighthouse on the drowned spit north of the old causeway. Abandoned since the canals changed course. A woman named Brech keeps it—keeps it lit, actually, every night, has for years, though no boat needs it anymore. Everyone thought she was mad." The old clerk's voice had dropped, reverent, the way people speak about things they'd dismissed and suddenly understand. "She was the registry's night custodian for twenty years. When the burning started, she walked out with the seal case and the original conveyance file and nobody stopped her, because who robs a madwoman?"

"Ashgroves," Sefi said softly. "Ashgroves rob madwomen. It's practically a family trade."

Something that might have been a laugh crossed Ada's face and was gone.

"The spit is a day's run upstream," he said, "against the current, with the storm coming. And the consortium will guess, eventually, that if the registry burned and the clerk lived, the originals went somewhere. They'll be watching the water between here and there."

"Then we go tonight," Sefi said, "ahead of the worst of it, and we come back with the strongbox and file on day fifty-eight, with two days of margin and everything in one hand." She was already moving, rolling the oilcloth off the spare supplies, and her blood had that singing feeling it got when a plan stopped being a hope and became a schedule. "Odile. Have the contest document drafted. Everything except the signature. Blank line, witnessed, ready."

"And if he doesn't come back?" Odile asked mildly. "If the consortium takes you on the water?"

Sefi paused. Looked at Ada. He met her eyes, and neither of them looked away, and the space between them was full of everything they'd said and everything they hadn't.

"He'll come back," she said. "He's an Ashgrove. It turns out we're terrible at staying lost."

* * *

They loaded the boat at dusk, working fast and silent in the habits they'd built on the canals—her hands and his passing gear without words, the tarp lashed, the bail bucket in reach, the patched hull riding low but true. Rain began as they pushed off, soft at first, testing.

At the dock, Odile Vess stood with her lamp raised, an old woman holding a small light against a large dark, and called across the water:

"Ranger! When you sign—sign it the way your father couldn't. Steady hand. Let them watch it shake and yours won't."

Ada raised one hand in answer. Didn't turn around.

Sefi took the stern pole and drove them out into the rising current, toward the drowned spit and the woman who kept the light, and behind them the ruined registry sank back into the rain like a ship going down with its papers in order. Eight days. One blank line. And somewhere ahead, in the dark on the water, the first lanterns of the consortium's patrol boats were already lighting up along the levees like a string of cold stars—all of them, she understood now, waiting for exactly one name.
