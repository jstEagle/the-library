## 17. Inheritance

The letter came to me in a tin that had held pipe tobacco once, back when the world still shipped luxuries down the canals. The defectors' leader—a broad, gray-bearded man called Oren Vashti, who had been my father's junior surveyor in another life—handed it to me in the reed shelter with both hands, the way you hand a man something breakable.

"Your father gave it to me the week before he died," Oren said. "He said, *If anything happens to me, find the Ashgrove who doesn't want to be found.*" A dry sound, almost a laugh. "I told him that described every Ashgrove I ever met."

I didn't open it right away. I sat with it in my lap while the marsh ticked and gurgled around us, while Sefi mended netting across the shelter and pretended not to watch, and I understood that a letter could weigh more than a crate of sealed survey gear. Ten years I had spent making myself weightless. Unlisted, unrecorded, unclaimed. I had burned through names the way other men burned through boots. And now here was a rectangle of oilcloth and paper with my family's gravity in it, waiting for me to pick it up.

The tin was sealed with pitch. My father's pitch—the particular amber resin he rendered from mesquite bark, the smell of which I had not thought about in a decade and which arrived in my nose now whole and undiluted, like a hand on the back of my neck. I pried the lid with my knife and my hands were steady, which surprised me. I had expected them to shake.

Inside: a letter in a hand I knew better than my own. And beneath it, wrapped in waxed linen, a sheaf of documents—flow tables, correspondence, a draft affidavit signed by three of the survey team, the same three who had vanished into these reeds. Oren watched me lift them and said, quietly, "That's the other half of why we're alive. Your father got us out. He told us the consortium would need us dead or discredited before the falsified surveys could stand. We chose dead-on-paper."

I looked at the letter. I still didn't open it. Sefi had stopped pretending to mend the net.

* * *

I read it at dusk, alone, at the edge of the reed bed where the water went silver and the first mosquitoes rose in columns. My father's voice on the page was exactly his voice—economical, dry, allergic to self-pity—and somehow that was the worst part, because I had spent ten years telling myself a story about a man who had sold his family's water and died a collaborator, and the story had been load-bearing. I had built my disappearance on top of it. Remove it and I didn't know what stood.

*Ada. If you are reading this, then you found Oren, which means you stopped running, which means I am allowed to hope.*

*The facts first, because you will want the facts before you will want anything else. I did sign the concession. I want you to know that I signed it with a gun on the table, which is not an excuse, only a fact. They came to the house in the dry season. Three of them and a lawyer, and the lawyer did the talking, and the gun was the survey captain's, and it stayed on the table the whole conversation like a fourth guest. They knew about you. They knew where you were posted. They knew the name you were using at the time, which I had not known, which is how I learned my son had gotten better at disappearing than I had ever taught him to be.*

*Sign, or the heir becomes a problem. That was the sentence. I signed.*

I had to put the letter down. The reeds moved. Somewhere out in the channels a bird made its flat complaining cry, and I sat with my father's sentence—*sign, or the heir becomes a problem*—and felt the whole architecture of my decade tilt.

He had known where I was. All those years I'd congratulated myself on my invisibility, on my clean ledgers and my burned trails, and my father had known where I was the entire time, and had said nothing, and had let me believe I'd escaped him. Not out of cruelty. Out of the same instinct that had made me run: if I don't look at you, they can't use you. We had been hiding from each other in the same house of a world, ten feet apart and both convinced we were protecting the other.

I picked it up again.

*After the signing I did the only thing left to a man who has sold the water under his own children's feet. I started collecting the proof that the sale was rotten. The flow data is falsified—I can show you where, and Oren can swear to how. The survey team's instruments were calibrated to read the aquifer low. Low readings mean the flats look worthless, which means the settlements' counterclaims look worthless, which means the consortium's permit stands uncontested when the monsoon floods everything and the water has to belong to someone. They are not surveying the water, Ada. They are deciding, in advance, who it will have drowned.*

*I have affidavits. I have the original calibration logs. I have a letter from the consortium's own counsel, careless in a way lawyers are when they think no one will ever read a thing. It is enough. It would have been enough months ago. But a contest requires an heir to file it in person, under his own name, at the registry, and I cannot be that heir anymore because I made myself a dead man the same way you made yourself a ghost—we were always better at the same tricks, you and I.*

*So here is the thing I have been circling for three pages, because I am a coward on paper even when I was not one in a room with a gun on the table.*

*I need you to be Ashgrove. Once. In daylight. Where they can see you.*

I read that line four times. The light was going. The mosquitoes found me and I let them.

*You will hate this, and you should know I hate it too, and that I have spent two years looking for another way and there isn't one. The law in this country is old and stupid and it has exactly one virtue: it does not recognize ghosts. A claim made by a dead man is void. A claim made by an unregistered man is void. The consortium has spent a generation teaching everyone on these flats that the safest thing to be is no one, because no one can own water and no one can contest it either. They burned the registries' cross-indexes in the last reorganization. They let the settlement rolls go to rot. They have engineered a whole territory of people with no standing, and then they write permits that only standing men can challenge.*

*Your name is the last unspoiled instrument on the flats. I know what it cost you. I know what I cost you. I am not asking you to forgive me in this letter; I am asking you to file a document. Forgiveness can come after, if you want it, or never, and either way the water will belong to the people who have drunk it for a hundred years and not to men who calibrated their instruments to lie.*

*If you do this, they will come for you. Not with a gun on a table this time—they have learned that trick makes martyrs. They will come with offers, and then with lawyers, and then, if all that fails, with weather. I want you to go in knowing it. I have watched you survive ten years of nothing, Ada, and I have thought, in the worst nights, that nothing is exactly what I trained you for. I am sorry for that. I am proud of it. Both are true and I no longer have time to reconcile them.*

*The tin is yours. The name is yours. It always was. I just held it in the water too long and it slipped.*

*Your father,*
*Tomas Ashgrove*
*who signs his full name, one last time, so you will know it can be done.*

I sat until full dark. The marsh sounds came up around me—the tick of water, the far engines of the world—and I did the arithmetic a man does when a decade of his life gets repriced in one evening.

I had thought my father had sold us and died comfortable. He had died alone in a dry-season house with a lawyer's signature drying on the worst act of his life and a tin of evidence under the floorboards, gathering proof against the men who had broken him, unable to use any of it because he had taught his son too well how to be no one. Every mile I had run, I had run for both of us. Every ledger I'd erased had been one more name struck from the rolls the consortium wanted empty. I hadn't been escaping my father. I had been finishing his sentence.

* * *

Sefi found me there. I heard her come through the reeds the way she did everything, without a sound until she chose to make one, and then she sat down beside me in the dark without asking, which was its own kind of permission.

"You read it," she said.

"He knew where I was. The whole time."

"I know."

"You knew."

"I suspected. Oren confirmed it after you went to sleep the first night." She pulled her knees up. "I didn't tell you because you'd have run. You were already deciding whether to run. I could see it in how you checked the boat lines."

"I wasn't going to run."

"You were going to run," she said, not unkindly, "the way water goes downhill. Not a choice. A property." She was quiet a moment. "Read me the part you keep rereading. You've got it memorized. I can tell because your lips move the same way every time."

I almost laughed. Instead I said, "*I need you to be Ashgrove. Once. In daylight.*"

"And?"

"And they'll come for me. He says so himself. Offers, then lawyers, then weather."

"We've had the weather," Sefi said. "It leaked."

That was true. The monsoon had already tried to kill us once and had mostly succeeded in making us a crew. I looked at her, a shadow beside me with the permits' whole war folded up inside her, and I understood suddenly and completely why she had left the trail readable across the hardpan. She hadn't been careless. She had been fishing, and the bait had been legibility itself—the one thing a man who had spent ten years erasing himself could not resist: a trail someone wanted followed. She had known that before I did. Maybe she'd known it before I knew it.

"You need a signature," I said. "That's why the trail. That's why all of it. You needed an heir, and I was the only one left on the rolls."

"I need the water to belong to the settlements," she said. "You're the instrument. I'll not pretend otherwise—I've lied to enough people for one life." She turned to look at me, and even in the dark I could feel the level weight of her attention. "But I'd have pulled you out of the flood with no permits in the world, Ada. Those are two different facts and I've stopped trying to make them one."

We sat with that. Out in the channels, a lantern moved—Oren's watch rotation, the defectors keeping their small dead-man's-alive vigil—and I thought about my father's last line. *Who signs his full name, one last time, so you will know it can be done.*

Ten years of aliases, and the man had known the whole time, and the whole time he had been leaving me a door out of it. Not back into the old life. Into a new one with the same name on it.

"They'll offer me my old life," I said. "The consortium. Oren told me they already know a ranger's been running with you. They'll figure out who."

"They will."

"And when they do—"

"Then you decide in front of witnesses," Sefi said. "That's the whole trick of standing in daylight. Everything you do, you do where it counts."

* * *

I opened the waxed linen by lamplight in the shelter, and the defectors gathered around the way men gather around a fire, and Oren laid it out for me page by page: the calibration logs with the doctored constants, the flow tables where the aquifer read a third of its true level, the consortium counsel's letter with its careless phrase—*the heir problem resolves itself if the elder Ashgrove's line remains unregistered*—and the draft affidavit, signed by three men the government listed as missing, presumed drowned.

"Presumed drowned," one of them said, a thin woman with surveyor's squint named Calla. "We've been drowned for two years. It's not bad, once you get used to it. You'd know."

"I would," I said.

"The question," Oren said, "is whether you'd like to be found. Because the moment you file, every record in this territory un-erases you. Debt rolls, ranger service, your father's estate. The consortium will know where you sleep by the second day. And the registry clerk—" he hesitated, "—the registry burned early. You know that. What's left of the rolls is in a clerk's memory and a salvaged ledger. Filing will be ugly and slow and public."

"Public is the point," I said.

Sefi looked up from the permits across the lamplight. For the first time since I'd known her, she looked like a woman letting go of a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine.

"Sixty days," she said. "Less now. The permits lapse if contested by a valid heir before the deadline. File, and the whole claim collapses—no valid holder, rights revert to the settlements under settlement law. It's not a battle. It's a signature."

"It's a signature they'll kill to prevent."

"Everything worth signing," Sefi said, "is a signature someone would kill to prevent."

I took out my knife and my father's letter and I did not burn anything and I did not sign anything, not yet. Instead I read the last page once more, aloud, because Oren asked, and because I think my father would have wanted witnesses—the same instinct, finally, that had put the gun on the table's opposite: a thing done in front of people is a thing that happened.

Then I folded the letter along its old creases and put it inside my coat, over the left side, where a man keeps the things that have become load-bearing.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we go to the ruins. And I file under my own name."

Oren nodded slowly, like a man watching a survey finally close. "Tomas will rest easier."

"My father," I said, "never rested easy in his life. But yes."

Outside, the marsh had gone still, the flat pre-storm stillness the flats make before the sky opens, and far off to the south, where the registry ruins stood black against the last light, I could see the consortium's lanterns already moving along the levees. They knew we were here. They had probably always known. Offers first, my father wrote. Then lawyers. Then weather.

The lanterns were coming, and the sky was stacking itself with the next storm, and somewhere in my coat, over my heart, a dead man had signed his full name so that his son would know it could be done.

Let them come. I had a name, and for the first time in ten years, I intended to use it.
