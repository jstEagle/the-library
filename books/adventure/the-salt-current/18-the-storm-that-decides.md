## 18. The Storm That Decides

The storm did not arrive. It descended, all at once, like a verdict.

Sefi felt it first in her teeth—a pressure change so sharp it ached—and then in the water itself, which had been rising for two days and now began to move with intent. The flooded registry ruins sat on their knoll of old masonry like an island that had forgotten how to be land, and around them the drowned flats stretched gray-green to every horizon, churned by wind into something between sea and argument.

"They're coming," Ada said.

He didn't need to say from where. Sefi had seen them too: three hulls sliding out of the rain-haze to the south, running before the wind with the arrogant confidence of boats that had never been asked to apologize for anything. Consortium colors. Consortium engines. The lead vessel was a survey launch, heavy, armored at the bow, built for pushing through ice and bureaucracy alike.

"How long?" the clerk asked. His name was Merrin, and he had rebuilt his ledger from memory in a leaking scriptorium while the sky tried to kill him, and he had not once complained, which Sefi found more heroic than any sword-work. He clutched the record now against his chest like an infant.

"Ten minutes," Ada said. "Less, if they stop being careful."

The defectors were already moving. Old Vessal—Ada's father's friend, the man who had carried a dead man's letters across half a desert—was lashing the evidence chest to the strongest beam of the ruined tower, wrapping it in oilcloth with hands that shook only slightly. The others formed a line at the waterline, such as it was, where stone steps vanished into brown flood.

Sefi stood beside Ada at the broken parapet and watched the launches come, and felt the strangest calm settle over her, the kind she'd learned in the year after her village went under the rerouted river: when everything you have is already staked, fear becomes a luxury you postpone.

"The filing," she said. "Merrin signs the moment they're committed. Not before."

"Merrin signs the moment I tell him," Ada said, "and not one breath earlier." He was watching the water, reading it the way he read hardpan—the set of the chop, the direction of the current threading through the drowned streets below. "They'll come at the north landing. It's the only approach deep enough for those hulls in this blow."

"And we'll be there."

"We'll be there."

* * *

The storm peaked as the launches closed.

It came down in sheets now, rain like flung gravel, wind screaming across the open flats with nothing left to slow it. The world reduced itself to noise and gray and the white scars of waves breaking over submerged walls. Ada stood at the north steps with a boat hook in his hands and six people behind him, and he thought, distantly, that ten years ago he would not have been here. Ten years ago there would have been no one behind him, because there would have been no one whose survival he was willing to be responsible for.

That man had burned his credentials on the water four days ago. This man was still learning what he'd bought with the fire.

The lead launch came out of the murk all at once, huge, its bow wave a rolling wall, and above the engine noise a voice called through a speaking horn: "Ashgrove! Last chance!"

He knew that voice. Agent Corvel, who had offered him his old life back over open water, who had smiled like safety was a thing that could be handed across a gunwale.

"You can still walk away," the horn boomed. "Hand over the clerk and the papers. You'll be a ranger again by spring. A name nobody remembers is worth nothing out here, Ashgrove. A name everybody remembers is worth less."

Ada looked back once—at Sefi, soaked through, grinning like the storm belonged to her; at Merrin clutching his ledger; at Vessal standing straight as a piling despite his years—and then forward at the launch bearing down on the steps.

"He's right," Ada called back, loud enough to carry. "A name nobody remembers can't contest a fraudulent sale. So I'm going to remember mine very loudly."

He swung the boat hook into the launch's steering oar as it ground against the steps, and the battle began.

* * *

It was not a battle anyone would write songs about. It was chaos wearing the shape of one.

Hull ground against stone; men swarmed or failed to swarm; the storm took sides with no loyalty at all. A defector named Osk went into the water and was hauled back by his collar, coughing. Sefi fought with a gaff hook and a lifetime of fury, keeping two boarding agents tangled in rigging rope. Merrin, absurdly, bravely, sat in the lee of the tower wall with the ledger inside his coat, reciting property descriptions under his breath like prayers.

And through it all the water rose, and rose, and rose.

Ada understood, in the way a body understands before a mind consents, that the storm was the true enemy of both sides—that the consortium's launch was being battered against the ruins as savagely as they were, that every man on that deck was fighting gravity and flood as much as him. He used it. He let the swell do his work, timing his pushes to the waves, shoving boarders back into the churning gray rather than trying to hold ground that didn't exist.

Then the second launch rammed the first.

Whether it was panic or incompetence or the storm simply deciding, the trailing vessel struck its sister amidships with a crack like the world's spine breaking. Men screamed. Rigging came down. And the wake of the collision—a standing wave, green and enormous—swept across the north steps and took Sefi off her feet.

Ada saw it happen the way you see a lamp fall: completely, helplessly, in the instant before it breaks. She went sideways, arms wheeling, one hand slapping wet stone and finding nothing to grip, and then the water folded over her and she was gone—not under, but *away*, pulled off the steps and out into the flood as if the flats had reached up and claimed what they'd been owed since her village drowned.

"*Sefi!*"

He was moving before the word finished leaving him. Later he would not remember deciding. He remembered the cold—a cold that went through flesh like a blade through canvas—and the roar, and the last thing he saw above the surface: Vessal's face, shouting something lost to the wind, and Merrin still signing, still writing, pen scratching through the deluge because the law does not pause for weather.

Then the flood closed over Ada Ashgrove's head, and the storm decided everything else.
