## 19. Overboard

The rope went taut and then it didn't.

I heard the sound before I understood it—a wet, tearing snap, like a branch giving way under snow—and then Sefi was gone, over the gunwale's edge and into the white water as if the flood had simply reached up and taken her. One moment her hand was on the rail, knuckles white with rain; the next there was only the storm, roaring through the drowned channels of the registry ruins, and the consortium boat swinging wide to come around again.

I did not decide to dive. That is the thing I will remember longest—not the cold, not the fear, but the absence of decision. Ten years of calculating every step, of weighing exits before entrances, of never once moving without knowing where the movement ended—and in that single instant all of it was nothing, a ledger burned, because she was in the water and I was on the boat and the distance between those two facts was intolerable.

I went over the side.

The flood took me like a fist closing. The monsoon had turned the flats into a sea without a floor—brown water churned with silt and salt, visibility measured in inches, current running every direction at once because the old channels were fighting the new ones underneath. I had grown up on dry ground. I had spent my whole life learning what the desert does to a body, how to read thirst, how to ration sweat. None of it meant anything here. Water was its own country with its own laws, and I was an illiterate drowning in them.

I went down. The storm above became a muffled drumming, then a rumor, then nothing. My boots filled. My coat dragged at me like hands pulling me deeper, and some calm, distant part of my mind—the part that had tracked thieves across hardpan for a decade—observed with professional interest that I was going to die, and that this was inefficient.

Then I remembered the maps.

My father's maps. The ones the defectors had pulled from their oilskin wrapping three nights ago, spread across a flooded camp table while rain hammered the canvas: hand-inked charts of the salt flats, drawn decades before any survey team arrived, before any consortium existed. Channels marked in his cramped, careful script. Currents annotated in the margins—*flood tide runs east along the old riverbed, then bends south at the split stone.* He had mapped the water beneath the desert the way other men mapped roads. He had known, years before the monsoon ever came down, exactly which way the flood would run.

And he had written one more line, in the corner of the chart of these very flats, so small I'd almost missed it: *A swimmer swept off the ruins should let go and trust the bend.*

Let go.

Every instinct in my body screamed against it. Every muscle I owned wanted to claw upward toward air, toward the storm, toward the surface where Sefi had vanished. But instinct had put me on boats for consortium men once, had kept me invisible for ten years, had sold my father's name for silence—and instinct, I was learning, could be wrong about everything that mattered.

I stopped swimming. I opened my hands. I let the current take me.

It was like being forgiven by something enormous. The water seized me and carried me—not down, not into the dark, but sideways, fast, along a channel I couldn't see but could suddenly feel, a smooth lane in the chaos where the old riverbed ran. My shoulder struck something soft—silt, maybe, or drowned reeds—and then the current bent, exactly as the margin note promised, south at the split stone, and the pressure changed, and I rose.

I broke the surface gasping, thirty yards downstream of the fight, close enough to see the two hulls grinding against each other in the white water, close enough to hear the defectors shouting from our deck. And twenty feet beyond them, snagged against a half-drowned wall—the registry's old foundation, black stone rising out of the flood like a rib—was Sefi.

She was clinging to the stonework with both hands, head barely above water, and even at that distance I could see she was done. Her arms were shaking. Each wave that hit the wall drove her fingers a little looser. She wasn't calling out. She'd stopped calling out. That was the worst part—that she had already concluded no one was coming, and was spending her last strength on holding on instead of hoping.

I swam for her.

The current fought me the whole way, and I fought it badly, burning strength I didn't have, swallowing half the channel. But I knew now which way the water ran, and I used it—letting it carry me in a long arc rather than fighting straight across, riding the bend the way my father's ink had said to ride it, until the wall rose in front of me and my hands found stone slick with rain.

"Sefi."

Her eyes found mine. They were wide and unfocused, and for a heartbeat I thought she was already too far gone to know me. Then her grip shifted—one hand leaving the wall to seize my collar with a desperation that nearly took us both under.

"I have you," I said, and the words tore out of me rawer than I meant them. "I have you. Let go of the wall. I have you."

She looked at me, and I watched her do the hardest thing a person can do in deep water: believe someone. Her fingers opened. The flood tried to take her back and I wrapped my arm around her chest and locked my wrist and held on, and the current slammed us both against the stone and held us there like it was deciding whether we were worth keeping.

Above us, the two boats were still locked together. I could hear the consortium agents shouting, hear the grind of hulls, hear Tam's voice cutting through it all with orders. None of it mattered. There was only the wall, and the water, and the woman shaking in my arm, and the strange, furious clarity of holding on to something after ten years of holding on to nothing.

"Ada." Her voice was a ruin. Barely a voice at all. "You jumped."

"You're welcome."

"I saw you jump." She turned her head against my shoulder, and rain ran off her chin, and she was laughing or crying or both, I couldn't tell and didn't try. "You hate water. You told me. You told me you'd sooner cross a salt pan barefoot than—"

"I lied," I said. "About a lot of things."

The wave came then—the big one, the one the storm had been winding up for an hour, the kind of swell that doesn't break so much as arrive. It lifted us off the wall together, threw us into the channel, and for one endless second we were both in the flood again, spinning, and I thought: *this is how it ends, not with the permits, not with the name, but with my arm around her and the water winning.*

But the current bent. South, at the split stone, just like the map said. Just like my father knew it would.

It set us down almost gently against the shallows behind the ruined registry, where the flood ran thin over broken flagstones, and I crawled out of the water dragging her with me, and we lay on the stone in the rain, breathing like bellows, alive.

* * *

The storm broke within the hour.

Not gradually—it simply spent itself, the way monsoons do when they've finished their work, the rain slackening from sheets to threads to a fine drifting mist, the wind dying back to something almost tender. The light came up gray and silver through the cloud, and the drowned ruins of the registry town emerged around us like bones surfacing in a tide pool: roofless walls, a bell tower leaning at an angle no architect intended, water lapping at windowsills that hadn't seen sky in sixty years.

Our boat came around the eastern wall with Tam at the tiller and the defectors bailing with anything that would hold water, and they pulled us aboard without a word, because there are moments when questions are a luxury. Sefi sat wrapped in every dry canvas the boat possessed, shivering, her lips still blue, and I sat beside her because sitting anywhere else had become unthinkable.

The consortium boat was gone. Whether they'd limped off damaged or simply decided the storm had made salvage of us all, I didn't know and didn't care. We had lost the evidence cache in the chase—we'd lost it hours ago, in the channels, and everyone on this boat knew it—but we were alive, and alive was a currency I had never learned to spend until now.

"They'll regroup," Tam said quietly, at the tiller, watching the mist. "Storm bought us a day. Maybe two. After that they'll come for the ruins, and the clerk, and anyone standing near either."

"Then we file first," Sefi said.

Her voice was still wrecked, scraped raw by swallowed water, but the steel under it was intact. She always sounded like that, I realized—like someone speaking through damage. I had mistaken it, in the warehouse, in the canal towns, for hardness. It wasn't. It was what remained after everything soft had been drowned, and she had kept going anyway.

"The deadline's tomorrow at dusk," she went on. "The clerk has the partial record. All it needs—" She stopped. Turned her head and looked at me, and the look held everything she wasn't saying.

"All it needs is an Ashgrove," I finished.

Rain dripped off the canvas overhead. Somewhere in the ruins, water was finding its way down through sixty years of dust, filling the old cisterns, running the old channels—the ones my father had drawn, the ones the consortium had falsified, the ones that had caught me like a hand catching a falling man.

"When I was under," I said. "In the channel. I stopped fighting. I let the current take me, and it saved my life. Your father's maps—" I corrected myself, because the word *your* had already begun to feel wrong in my mouth. "*My* father's maps. He drew that bend forty years ago. He knew exactly where the water would run, and he wrote it down small enough that I'd almost miss it, and then I didn't miss it, and I'm alive because of it."

Sefi said nothing. She was watching me with that expression she had, the one that waited people out.

"I spent ten years making sure no record of me existed anywhere," I said. "No name, no trail, no signature. I thought that was freedom. Then I dove into a flood for a thief, and the only reason I'm breathing is a piece of paper my father signed." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Paper. It keeps beating me."

"Is that a yes?" she asked.

I looked out at the ruins—at the bell tower, at the clerk's window glowing faintly behind its salt-crusted glass, at the water running silver through the streets of a town the world had given up on. Tomorrow at dusk, the permits expired. Tomorrow at dusk, an Ashgrove either stood in daylight and contested the sale, or the water beneath these flats belonged to men who falsified surveys and burned registries and left survey teams to vanish.

An Ashgrove. In daylight. As himself.

"I need to see the letter again," I said. "My father's letter. Before I sign anything, I want to read every word he wrote me."

Sefi reached under the canvas, into the oilskin pouch strapped against her ribs—the one thing she had refused to surrender even to the flood—and drew out the folded pages, soft as cloth from handling, and put them in my hands.

"He wrote it to be found," she said. "By you. When you were ready."

Outside, the mist was lifting off the water, and somewhere east, past the drowned streets, the light was beginning—thin and gold and absolutely ordinary—to come up on the last day.
