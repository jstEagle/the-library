## 20. Sixty Days, Spent

The clerk's name was Oviden, and he had rebuilt a government from memory.

Sefi watched him work in the flooded atrium of the old registry, standing waist-deep in water that had once been marble floors and is now something closer to a lagoon. He'd lashed his salvaged ledger to a raft of door planks and floated it before him like an altar, and around him, on every surface that still rose above the waterline—window ledges, the capitals of pillars, the flat top of a shattered filing cabinet—he'd copied out what he could remember of sixty years of land records. His handwriting was beautiful. That was the thing that undid her, watching it. The consortium had burned everything he'd spent his life keeping, and his first act afterward was to make the letters again, each one rounder and more careful than the last, as if the burning had been a test of whether he meant them.

"You're staring," Ada said beside her.

"I'm counting." She nodded at the pillar where Oviden had chalked a number that morning: fifty-eight. Days remaining on the permits, crossed through each dawn with a wet thumb. "Fifty-seven now."

"Then we should go up."

"We should wait." She said it more sharply than she meant to, and Ada looked at her, and she made herself soften it. Ten weeks on the water together had taught her that he read sharpness the way other men read weather—as a sign to brace. "The deadline isn't the problem. The problem is what happens at the deadline. If nobody files, the permits lapse clean and the rights revert under settlement law. But if the consortium can produce *any* valid holder—even a forged one, even a dead man's proxy—they get sixty more days, and by then the flood season's over and they'll have their pumps in the ground. So we don't just need the clock to run out. We need witnesses who saw it run out."

Ada was quiet for a moment. Below them, Oviden dipped his pen, blotted it against the raft, wrote.

"You've been planning this since the warehouse," he said. Not accusing. Almost admiring.

"I've been planning this since my village went under the river," Sefi said. "The warehouse was just where I stopped needing to plan alone."

* * *

They spent the next two days doing nothing, which was the hardest work either of them had done all season.

The defectors' camp had moved into the registry ruins after the storm—the marsh survivors, the survey team that had walked off into the salt rather than falsify another flow table—and there were nineteen of them now, crowded onto the upper floors of a building that had been built to hold four hundred clerks. They bailed, they patched, they stretched canvas over the broken skylights, and Sefi made herself useful and made herself invisible, because the plan required her to be neither seen nor missed until the hour it required her to be both.

It was Ada who couldn't sit still. She found him on the second evening on the roof, under a sky rinsed clean by the storm, studying the drowned causeways through the ranger's glass he still carried even though he'd burned the credentials that gave him the right to it.

"Consortium boats?" she asked.

"Two, holding at the far levee. Not advancing. Not leaving." He lowered the glass. "They know the deadline too."

"Of course they do. It's printed on the permits they stole the right to print." She sat down beside him on the hot slate. Below, the water lay flat and gold, and somewhere under it were streets she'd never walk and a courthouse she'd never argue in, and above it was the last hour of a ten-year theft, ticking. "They're waiting for us to make a mistake. Come out armed, give them a fight they can call a rebellion. Or come out running, give them a chase they can end. Anything but this."

"This being?"

"Sitting on a roof watching ink dry." She smiled despite herself. "My whole life, Ada, I thought the way you beat people with more boats than you was to be faster or meaner or willing to drown yourself sooner. It took me until this year to learn there's a third way. You let their own paper eat them."

He turned that over. She watched him do it—the small stillness he got when something landed deep, the way he looked not at her but slightly past her, at some horizon only he could see. A decade of erasing himself had left grooves in the man, and lately she'd been watching him decide, groove by groove, to climb back out of them.

"Your father would have liked this part," she said, before she could stop herself.

"My father liked paperwork," Ada said. "It's the only thing about him I ever understood." Then, quieter: "The letter said he died gathering proof. Do you think he knew it would come to this? An heir, a signature, a clock?"

"He knew the law better than anyone alive," Sefi said. "I think he built the trap and died trusting someone would eventually spring it. He just didn't expect it to be his son."

Ada didn't answer. Out on the levee, the consortium boats showed their lights as the light failed, two yellow eyes in the dark, patient as debt.

* * *

On the fifty-seventh day, Oviden finished copying the settlement rolls.

He did it at dawn, in front of everyone, standing on the raft in the flooded atrium while the defectors lined the balconies above him—not because he'd asked for an audience but because nobody could stay away. He read the final entries aloud in a voice cracked from smoke inhalation, the names of the three settlements and their headmen and their boundary stones, the claims that predated the consortium by a generation, the law that said when a concession lapses unclaimed, the water beneath it returns to those who have drawn from it longest. When he finished, no one cheered. They stood in the dripping silence and listened to the water move through the ruined building, and Sefi understood that they were listening to it differently now. As theirs.

"The permits expire at sundown on the sixtieth day," Oviden said, looking up at the balconies. "Under statute, lapse requires three conditions. First, that the permit term has run its full course—I have the issuance dates memorized; they're true. Second, that no valid claimant has filed a contest or a renewal. Third—" and here his voice steadied, the clerk reasserting itself over the survivor—"that the lapse occurred in the presence of witnesses competent to attest to it. Two at minimum. Named. Signatures witnessed and countersigned."

"And the consortium?" one of the defectors called down. "They'll contest the attestation. They'll say our witnesses are rebels."

"They will," Oviden agreed placidly. "And then they will discover that one of the two witnesses must be a licensed officer of the registry, and I am the only one left, and my license cannot be revoked by anyone whose records I hold." He almost smiled. It was the most dangerous expression Sefi had ever seen on a bureaucrat's face. "They burned the building. They forgot to burn me."

That night Sefi climbed to the roof and found Ada already there, sitting where they always sat, and she realized with a strange lurch that she'd started thinking of it as *their* place, that she'd started thinking of half this drowning ruin as hers, and that some traitorous part of her was already grieving the day she wouldn't need any of it anymore.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

"You don't have to do it this way. You know that. We could run the papers out to the settlements, file from their side, keep your name out of the open record entirely. It would take longer. It might not hold. But your name wouldn't be on anything the consortium can read."

"It's already on everything they can read," Ada said. "It's been on it since before I was born. Ashgrove. Water rights. Inherited." He looked at her. "You told me once you needed me whether I knew it or not. You were right, and I hated you for it, and then I stopped hating you, and I've been trying to figure out which of those happened first ever since. But here's the thing you got wrong, Sefi." He turned the glass in his hands, not looking through it. "I didn't spend ten years erasing myself because I wanted to be free of my name. I did it because I was ashamed of what I did under it. There's a difference. Erasure doesn't fix shame. It just hides the evidence." He paused. "Signing tomorrow isn't me coming out of hiding. It's me deciding the hiding didn't work."

She wanted to say something worthy of that. What she said was: "If the boats come, get behind me. I'm smaller and meaner."

Ada laughed—a real one, short and startled out of him—and in the dark below, the consortium's lights burned on, patient as debt, and the water rose a finger's width against the walls, and somewhere in the flooded dark Oviden slept with his ledger lashed to his chest like a child.

* * *

The sixtieth day came up red.

Not storm-red—clarity-red, the desert kind, the whole eastern sky gone the color of a forge before the sun cleared the levees, and Sefi stood on the roof and understood that the world was going to make this day beautiful regardless of what happened in it, which felt like mockery and felt like mercy and might have been both.

The consortium moved at midmorning. Of course they did. Two boats off the levee became five, then seven, running the drowned causeway toward the registry in formation, engines low and even, flying no colors because they didn't need to. Everyone in the ruins heard them. Nobody panicked. Sefi gave the orders she'd rehearsed for two days—the defectors to the lower windows, unarmed, visible, hands empty; the survey evidence wrapped oilcloth-tight and moved to the third position she'd chosen, not the first or second, because the consortium had watchers who'd mapped the obvious ones; Oviden to the atrium with his ledger and his pen and his ink protected under oilcloth like a relic.

And Ada she sent to the roof.

"Not hidden," she told him. "Standing. Where every glass on that water can see you. If they want a name to aim at, give them the name. It's the one thing we have that they can't counterfeit."

He looked at her a long moment. "You've gotten good at spending people," he said.

"I've gotten good at spending the ones who volunteer," she said. "Get up there."

From the atrium doorway, waist-deep, ledger on its raft, Oviden watched the boats come with the calm of a man who had already survived the worst thing that could happen to his work. "Miss Sefi," he said as she passed. "A question, if you please. When this is done—when the permits lapse and the rights revert—what will you do?"

She hadn't expected the question and hadn't prepared an answer, and so the truth came out unarmored: "I don't know. I've been stealing for so long I don't know what I am when there's nothing left to steal."

Oviden considered this with the gravity he gave all entries. "In my experience," he said, "most people are what they were before, plus what they saved." And he turned back to his ledger and began, unhurried, to rule a fresh page.

* * *

The boats stopped forty meters out.

Sefi stood at the broken window on the second floor, in plain view, hands empty, and counted. Seven hulls. Thirty-odd men. On the lead boat, a woman in consortium gray with a speaking-trumpet she didn't raise yet, and Sefi knew her by reputation if not name—the kind of officer who won concessions the way weevils won granaries, slowly and from inside.

The trumpet came up. "By authority of the Meridian Water Consortium, this structure is subject to recovery of corporate property. Surrender the stolen documents and the persons responsible, and the remainder of the occupants will be conveyed to dry ground and released."

Nobody in the ruins answered. That was the plan. Silence was the plan. Every second the consortium spent talking was a second off the permit clock, and the sun was climbing, and the clock was the whole war.

The trumpet again, harder: "You have one hour."

And then, from the roof, clear across the flat water, a voice Sefi had learned to hear through storms: "You have fifty-nine minutes and a problem."

She closed her eyes. *Oh, Ada.* She'd told him to stand where they could see him. She hadn't told him to give a speech.

He came to the roof edge, and the morning put him in silhouette, and she heard the consortium boats go quiet—not the engines, the *men*, thirty people realizing at once whose outline that was, because the consortium kept files the way Oviden kept ledgers, and somewhere in one of them was a ranger named Ashgrove who had tracked for them once and vanished, and here he was standing on the roof of the thing they'd burned, in daylight, under his own name.

"My father held these water rights before your consortium bought them," Ada called, and his voice carried the way voices carry over water, flat and far. "The purchase was coerced. The surveys backing it were falsified. I have the proof, and I have the standing, and as of sundown today, if no valid claimant files, the concession lapses and the rights revert to the settlements under settlement law—which means that everything your boats are floating on stops belonging to you at sunset, and starts belonging to people who were drawing from it before your company existed."

The gray woman raised the trumpet. "An unfiled claim is worth nothing. Signatures require a registry. Your registry is ash."

"Come aboard and see," Oviden said mildly, from the flooded atrium, loud enough to carry. "Bring your own pen."

What happened next took eleven minutes, and Sefi spent all of them at the window with her heart going like a bilge pump, because the gray woman did the arithmetic faster than her men did. Sefi watched it land on her face in stages: first contempt, then doubt, then the cold recalculation of a person realizing that the building in front of her contained a licensed registry officer, a living heir with documentary standing, nineteen defectors who were witnesses to everything, and a legal clock that made every hour of standoff an hour closer to the consortium's claim dying of natural causes. Force was the consortium's language, but force required a target, and the target had spent the entire season making himself into paperwork.

The gray woman lowered the trumpet. Raised it. Lowered it again.

Then one of her boats peeled off—just one, sliding away east toward the levee, fast, carrying a single passenger—and Sefi knew exactly what message it carried and exactly where it was going, and she allowed herself, for the first time in ten years, to laugh at the sight of a courier running to tell power that the paper had turned on it.

"They're falling back to the levee," Ada reported from the roof, later, when the formation had withdrawn to its old distance and the standoff had settled into its long last hour. He came down through the skylight, rope-burned and sunlit, and stood dripping on the stair above her. "They'll try something before sundown. They always do."

"Let them," Sefi said. "We're not fighting them anymore. We're outlasting them. There's a difference, and it's the whole point."

He sat down beside her on the stair, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the water in the atrium below caught the noon light and threw it up the walls in trembling coins.

"When I sign," Ada said quietly, "it goes in the record. My name, my hand, my father's sale contested by his son. After today there's no version of the story where I'm a rumor again."

"I know."

"You knew that when you picked me. On the flats. You read my trail and you knew the trail ended at a signature."

Sefi looked at him. "Yes," she said. "I'm a thief, Ada. I steal the thing people value most and hold it where they can see it until they come to take it back. You valued being no one. I stole that." She shrugged. "You can have it back at sundown, if you still want it. But I don't think you will."

Below them, Oviden ruled another line. Above them, the sun leaned west. Somewhere on the water, the consortium waited for a reply from powers that had none to give, because the reply was already written, dated sixty days ago, and expiring at dusk.

* * *

The last hour, they all came up to watch.

Nineteen defectors and a clerk on the roof of a burned registry, sitting on slate still warm from a red dawn, facing west, where the sun went down over the salt flats that had spent a season learning to be a sea. No one talked much. One of the marsh women had brought up a kettle and made tea over a spirit flame, and the cups went around, chipped and mismatched, and Sefi held hers and watched the light go long and amber and thought about her village under the rerouted river, about how she had sworn the only justice left in the world was the kind you took, and how it had turned out there was a kind you waited for instead, patient as water, wearing stone down by showing up every day.

"Fifteen minutes," Oviden said, checking a watch he'd kept running through fire and flood.

At ten minutes, the consortium boats lit their lamps, early, aggressive, a show of permanence. At five minutes, a runner came up from the lower windows: the gray woman's launch was moving, slowly, toward the registry, trumpet raised, though whatever she was saying was lost across the water, and nobody tried very hard to hear it.

At one minute, Ada took out the pen.

Sefi had watched him kill a man once, in the channels, quick and necessary, and had thought she'd seen the worst thing fear could make of him. She'd been wrong. His hand shook now, over a piece of paper, in the last light, and she reached over and covered it with hers, and felt the tremor run through both of them, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the moment wasn't already saying.

"Read it to me," he said. "All of it. I want to hear every word before I'm the one who said it."

So Oviden read, by spirit-flame, in his cracked careful voice: the contest of sale, the citation of the coerced deed, the witness lines, the statutory lapse, the reversion clause, the names of the three settlements, the boundary stones, the water beneath. And when he finished, the sun touched the levee, and the consortium's lamps guttered yellow against a sky still full of light, and Ada Ashgrove signed his full name for the second time in ten years, and the first time that mattered.

"There," Oviden said, blotting it. He sounded, Sefi thought, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. "Entered and attested. The concession lapses as of this date, unclaimed by any valid holder. Reversion effective immediately under settlement law." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and his voice was perfectly level. "The water belongs to the settlements. Effective at sundown. Which was"—he checked his watch—"four minutes ago."

For a moment, nobody moved. The tea steamed. The water lapped the walls. Out on the darkening flats, the consortium's lamps burned on, lighting nothing that belonged to them anymore.

Then the marsh woman began to laugh—quietly at first, then helplessly, the laughter of a woman who had spent a season bailing—and it went around the roof like the tea had, cup to cup, and Sefi found herself laughing too, and Ada's hand found hers and held on, and below them the new sea moved against the old registry, gentle now, like something that had finally been given permission to be what it was.

"Sixty days," Sefi said, when she could speak. "Spent."

"Paid," Ada corrected. "There's a difference."

But the gray woman's launch was still coming, slow across the dark water, trumpet silent now, and Sefi watched it and felt the triumph cool into caution, because power that loses on paper does not usually concede—it regroups, and renegotiates, and comes back with something worse than boats.

"They'll contest the attestation in court," she said. "Within the week. They'll bring lawyers and forged proxies and twenty years of purchased precedent."

"Let them file," Ada said. "Paper against paper. We know how that ends now."

But it was Oviden, gathering his ledger to his chest, who answered the real question, in the mild voice of a man reading the fine print of the future: "Court requires a registry to receive filings, Miss Sefi. And registries require buildings. And buildings—" he gestured gently at the drowned, burned, magnificent ruin around them "—require wardens. The settlements will need to appoint one. Soon. Someone who knows the water, and the law, and the difference between them."

Sefi looked at Ada. Ada was looking at the water, at the place where the salt met the fresh, where the first green things were already coming back.

"Don't look at me," he said. But he was smiling.
