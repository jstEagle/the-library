## 21. The Current Runs Both Ways

The hearings were held in the registry ruins, because there was nowhere else big enough and dry enough, and because the clerk—Maren, her name was Maren, a small woman with ink-stained knuckles and a memory like poured concrete—insisted that justice should happen where the records died. They strung canvas over the burned rafters. They set benches on the flood-silted floor. Water still stood in the corners in shallow silver sheets, and when the light came through the gaps in the roof it moved across those sheets all day, so that everyone who testified did it standing in a room that shimmered, as if the building itself hadn't decided yet whether it was drowned or saved.

Ada sat through all of it.

He told himself he was there for Sefi, whose testimony ran three days—the theft, the permits, the canal-town market, the warehouse where she'd let him corner her like a fish she'd already decided to keep. He told himself he was there for the defectors, six of them now, hollow-cheeked and marsh-thin, who stood before the tribunal one at a time and laid out the falsified flow data with the flat patience of people who had rehearsed dying and gotten interrupted by something better. He told himself he was there because the settlements had asked him to be, and because after everything, refusing them felt like a kind of drowning.

But the truth was simpler and worse: he was there because for the first time in ten years, his name was spoken aloud in public and he didn't flinch.

"Ashgrove," the tribunal chair said, reading from the recovered letter. "Elias Ashgrove's son. You confirm this?"

"I confirm it."

"Your father's sale of the water rights was executed under duress. The consortium's agents held a lien against his boat, his home, and—" the chair paused, and Ada watched him decide whether to say it, "—against you. Your location was known to them throughout the period in question."

Throughout the period in question. Ten years of erasure, of burning ledgers and crossing out names, of becoming so good at not existing that even the consortium's men had eventually lost the thread—and all of it, every careful year, had been a line item in someone else's leverage. His father had known where he was the whole time. Had carried that knowledge into every negotiation like a stone in his chest, and signed anyway, and then spent his last months gathering proof to undo what he'd signed, and died with the proof hidden in a marsh cache waiting for a son who never came home.

Ada sat very still while the shimmering light moved across the floor.

"You may step down," the chair said gently.

He stepped down. Sefi was waiting at the back, arms crossed, watching him with that expression she wore when she was deciding whether to let him have a feeling or make him work for it. She uncrossed her arms.

"Well?" she said.

"He knew where I was." His voice came out rougher than he meant it to. "Ten years. He always knew."

Sefi nodded slowly, the way you nod at weather. "And?"

"And I spent a decade running from a man who could have found me any day he wanted, and never once did." Ada looked at the water sheeting across the floor, at the light moving on it. "He just left the door open and waited."

"That sounds like someone I know," Sefi said. "Someone who left a trail readable on purpose and then got sulky about being followed."

"I wasn't sulky."

"You were extremely sulky. You pointed a rifle at me in a warehouse."

"You broke into my—"

"The tribunal is recessing," Maren called from the front, in the tone of a woman who had heard two decades of arguments and intended to outlive all of them. "Deliberations resume tomorrow. Ashgrove. A word."

* * *

Maren's office was a corner of the ruins walled off with salvaged shutters, and it smelled of lamp oil and wet ash and, faintly, of the salt flats themselves—that mineral breath that came off the receding flood now everywhere in the lowlands, the season turning, the water looking for its way home. On her desk lay a document that made Ada stop in the doorway.

It was an appointment. He knew the form. He'd seen it once, folded in his father's strongbox among the maps, blank then, waiting. Warden of the Restored Waterways. The post Elias Ashgrove had held for nineteen years before the consortium made it cheaper to break him than buy him.

"We're reestablishing the wardenship," Maren said. "Settlement law, ratified last week, all four settlements voting. The canals need maintaining. The levees need eyes. The flow data needs an honest keeper, and we've learned the hard way what happens when it doesn't have one." She slid the paper toward him. "The tribunal voted unanimously. Even the defectors. Especially the defectors—they said you navigated the flooded flats by landmarks only your father ever mapped. They said the currents seemed to know you."

"They're being generous."

"They're being accurate. There's a difference." Maren laced her ink-stained fingers together. "I'll be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the currency of the new administration. This post has a cost. You'll sign your name to things. Everything, actually—every inspection, every ruling, every ledger. Your name will be in the consortium's files within the month, and their files survive even when their claims don't. There are men on those levees who lost money when the permits lapsed. Some of them will remember your face. Some of them already do."

"I know," Ada said. "One of them offered me my old life back in the middle of a flood."

"And you burned your credentials instead."

"It seemed efficient at the time."

Maren almost smiled. Then she didn't, and pushed the paper another inch toward him. "Take tonight. That's not doubt—that's procedure. A man who signs this in haste will resent it in winter, and winter is when the waterways need a warden who doesn't resent them."

Ada picked up the paper. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, or maybe that was just ten years of running pressing down through his hand. He thought about the first time he'd erased himself—a ferry manifest, a single struck-through line, the strange vertigo of watching his own name dissolve under a pen stroke and feeling lighter for it. He had chased that lightness for a decade. He had mistaken it for freedom, the way a man in the desert mistakes a mirage for shade, and the thirst had never once gone away.

"Tonight," he agreed.

* * *

He walked the levees at dusk.

The flood was going out. You could hear it if you stood still—a vast, patient whispering, water sliding off the hardpan back into the channels, off the channels into the old riverbed, off the world and back toward the salt. In another month the flats would be flats again. Already the drowned landmarks were surfacing: the top rail of a fence his father had built, the crown of the surveyor's marker at the old boundary, the dead tree they used to call the Ferryman because it stood alone in the shallows with its arms out. As a boy he'd believed the Ferryman would carry you across if you left an offering. He'd left a knife once. His father had laughed until he cried, then explained tides to him for an hour, which was better than most religions managed.

Sefi found him at the Ferryman tree. She usually did; he'd stopped asking how.

"Maren offered you the post," she said. Not a question. News traveled fast in a settlement of four hundred people who had nothing to do but rebuild and gossip.

"She did."

"And?"

"And I'm supposed to decide by morning whether I want my name in every file the consortium ever copies, on every levee their remnant agents can find me, for the rest of my life." Ada turned the paper over in his hands. "She calls it a cost. I call it the exact thing I spent ten years making sure could never happen."

Sefi leaned against the tree, which creaked like an old man agreeing. "You know what I stole, originally? Before the permits?" she said. "Nothing. I was eleven. My village went under when they rerouted the river, and I learned that the people who drown other people never write their own names down. There's always a committee. Always a signature that isn't quite theirs—an agent signing for a firm, a firm signing for a trust. Accountability diffused until it evaporates." She looked at him sideways. "You did the same thing in reverse. You diffused yourself until *you* evaporated. Same trick. Same lie underneath: that if no name attaches to the deed, the deed never happened."

"The deed happened either way."

"The deed happened either way," she agreed. "So the only question worth asking is whose name stands next to it when it does." She pushed off the tree and faced him fully, and the last light caught the scar along her jaw, the one she'd never explained and he'd stopped needing explained. "Sign it, Ada. Sign it and mean it. Because I've read enough consortium paperwork to tell you what happens to unwatched waterways, and I'd rather fight you over a bad inspection than bury another village."

"That's the least romantic proposal anyone's ever made me."

"I'm not proposing. I'm testifying." But she was smiling, barely, the way she did—as if smiling were a resource to be spent carefully—and she took his hand around the rolled paper and held it a moment longer than the argument required. "Whatever you decide, decide it as a man who exists. I didn't drag you through a monsoon so you could go back to being a rumor."

They walked back along the levee in the dark, and neither of them spoke, and it was the good kind of silence, the kind they'd learned on the water—two watches overlapping, each keeping half the night so the other could sleep.

* * *

He signed at dawn.

Not in Maren's office—he couldn't have said why, except that some things shouldn't happen behind salvaged shutters. He signed at the registry table, in front of the whole room, with the hearings reconvened around him and the light coming through the broken roof and moving on the water sheets in the corners. Maren read the appointment aloud. The tribunal chair witnessed. The defectors stood, all six of them, which nobody had asked them to do.

Ada took the pen.

His hand did not shake, but it wanted to, and he let it want, because the trembling felt honest in a way that steadiness never had. He wrote *Ada* — the name he'd kept, the short blade of it, the one he'd used on ferry manifests and ranger contracts and, once, in a warehouse, pointing a rifle at a thief who'd planned the whole encounter. Then he paused. One breath. Two.

Then he wrote the rest.

*Ashgrove.*

Two syllables. Nineteen years of his father's service. A lien held against a son's life. A letter hidden in a marsh cache, waiting. A dead tree called the Ferryman, arms out over the shallows. All of it in eight letters, and the pen scratch loud in the quiet room, and when he finished, the sound of the nib lifting from the paper was the loudest thing he'd heard in ten years.

Maren blotted the page and read it back, formal as scripture: "Warden of the Restored Waterways. Ada Ashgrove."

Nobody applauded, exactly. What happened was quieter and stranger: the room exhaled. Four hundred people's worth of held breath, released at once, and somewhere in it Ada understood that they hadn't been waiting for his signature. They'd been waiting to see if he'd flinch. He hadn't. That was the whole ceremony.

Afterward, Sefi found him on the steps, where he stood looking out at the flats going gold with morning, the flood lines retreating down the hardpan like a tide remembering itself.

"So," she said. "Warden."

"Warden."

"First order of business?"

Ada took the folded map from inside his coat—his father's map, the one that had predicted the currents that saved them both, annotated now in two hands, his father's cramped precision and his own newer, harder lines. He unfolded it against his knee.

"There's a channel north of the Ferryman tree that silted wrong during the flood," he said. "If we don't cut it before the dry season sets, the eastern settlement loses its intake. And after that—" he looked up, and found he was smiling without rationing it, spending freely, like a man newly solvent, "—after that, there's everything else. Levees. Gauges. Ledgers with my name in them, all of them, forever."

"Forever," Sefi repeated, tasting it. "That's a long contract for a man who used to pay debts so he could disappear."

"I'm not disappearing." He folded the map and handed it to her—half the work, half the watch, the way they'd learned to split a night. "I'm staying. There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life learning exactly how large it is."

Far out on the flats, the water kept leaving, patient and unhurried, finding its old channels, and where it went, green was already following—first at the margins, then everywhere, the current running both ways at last.
