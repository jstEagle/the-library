## 1. Miles, Not Years

Casper Quintana did not count days. Days were a currency that spent itself whether you watched it or not, and he had long ago stopped watching. He counted miles instead.

Fourteen miles since dawn across the White Reach, where the salt crust cracked under his boots like old porcelain and the horizon bent upward at its edges, a mirage of water that never arrived. Sixty-one miles since the last town. Nine hundred and forty since the winter his life had ended and this other thing—this pursuit—had begun in its place. He kept the numbers the way other men kept prayer: silently, constantly, without much hope attached to them.

The salt flats at midday were a country of light. It came up from below as well as above, bounced off the crust until a man squinted even facing away from the sun, until his shadow seemed to pool beneath him like something drowned. Casper wore a brim hat gone soft with sweat, a dust-colored coat, and carried everything he owned in a pack that weighed less than his regret. On his hip rode a ranger's iron—a short, heavy bar of blackened steel that could crack a skull or signal a distant patrol, though there was no patrol within three hundred miles to signal. The rangers of the Salt Territories had thinned to almost nothing. He was, as far as he knew, the last one who still filed reports. Nobody read them. He wrote them anyway. Habit was the only architecture left in his life, so he built with it carefully.

Ahead of him, half a day's lead if she hadn't slowed, rode Ilsa Vey.

He thought of her the way a man thinks of weather—not with affection exactly, not with hatred either, but with the intimate attention of someone whose survival depends on reading every shift. Five years of it. He knew her habits better than anyone alive, possibly better than she did herself. She watered her horse at dusk rather than dawn because dawn riding was cooler and she was stingy with water out of some old scarcity he'd never learned the source of. She camped on high ground but never the highest point—she didn't like being silhouetted against the sky, which meant she'd once been hunted by someone who shot from ridgelines. She left false trails going one direction and true ones going another, and her false trails were always *too* clean, too deliberate, little gifts of misdirection that she couldn't help laying down the way some people can't stop humming.

And she was heading for the dry wells.

That was the thing about the White Reach: nothing lived out here except what passed through it. The wells had been sunk by freight carriages two generations back, before the salt trade collapsed, and most of them had gone brackish or dry. But not all. There was water under the flats in slow dark veins, and Ilsa Vey knew where those veins ran better than any living soul except, perhaps, Casper Quintana—who had learned it from her, in a sense, by five years of watching where she went when the canteens got light.

She'd robbed the assay office in Corium Crossing eleven days ago. That was how these things always started: a theft, small and strange, the kind that made no sense. She'd left behind the silver. She'd left behind the salt-scrip, which was as good as coin out here. What she'd taken was a locked strongbox from the back room, heavy as a child's coffin, and Casper had seen the contents listed in the manifest before the clerk's ink dried: *echo-salt, raw, uncut, approx. nine pounds.*

Nine pounds of echo-salt. Enough to buy a district. Enough to buy silence, or a memory, or a lie told so perfectly it became true. And enough to tell him one certain thing: Ilsa Vey wasn't stealing for money. She never stole for money. In five years he had catalogued her takings the way a naturalist catalogues birds—salt crystals from a shrine at Dune Rest; a jar of pressed lichen from a dead woman's garden; a tuning fork from a chapel that no longer held services—and never once anything a thief could eat or spend. She collected things that remembered. He didn't know why. He had stopped, somewhere around mile six hundred, pretending he'd figure it out by catching her.

Catching her wasn't the point anymore. He understood that the way you understand your own heartbeat: not as a decision but as a condition. The chase was the shape his days took, and a man needs a shape or he spills.

* * *

He found her sign at the third dry well, an hour before sundown, and the finding of it gave him the particular grim pleasure of a card player turning over exactly the card he'd named aloud.

The well sat in a shallow depression ringed with broken masonry—the old freight station, its walls dissolved into salt-eaten stumps. The wellhead itself was capped with a rotted plank cover, and around it the ground told its story plainly to anyone fluent in ground. Hoofprints, shod, coming in from the northeast at a trot. A horse stood a while here—there was the churned crescent where it had shifted weight, the darker patch where it had breathed hard against the crust. Two sets of boot prints, one set heavier than the other, walking to the wellhead and back twice.

Two people. That was new.

Casper crouched and read the prints the way other men read letters. The second set was narrow, light-heeled, the stride short—someone young, or tired, or both. They'd come in together, which meant Ilsa had picked up a passenger, and Ilsa Vey did not pick up passengers. In five years she had traveled alone with the consistency of a star. So either the passenger was cargo—and given the strongbox, cargo was possible—or the passenger was trouble she hadn't been able to leave behind.

He circled the depression slowly, reading. The plank cover had been pried up and set aside carefully, not thrown. Inside the well shaft, the darkness smelled of wet stone and old rope. He lowered his lantern on the frayed line still rigged there and saw, thirty feet down, the glint of standing water. Not dry after all. Brackish, probably—white-rimmed at the edges, the kind of water that tasted like licking a knife—but water.

They'd drawn it up. There were the drag marks of a full bucket, and beside them, pressed into the salt crust, a single clear detail that made the back of Casper's neck tighten: a boot print with a distinctive double-stitch along the sole, slightly pigeon-toed on the left foot.

Ilsa Vey's boots. He'd tracked the wear pattern of those boots through four hundred miles of badlands two years ago, after she'd resoled them in Harrow Bend with leather from a particular tannery, and he had watched the stitches multiply season by season like rings in a tree. Left foot turned out. He could have identified her tracks blindfolded, in the dark, in his sleep—which was, more or less, how he did identify them now, since his sleeping hours and waking ones had long ago blurred into a single continuous vigil.

But the bucket drag marks said they'd filled water and *stayed*. And then—

He stood, turned a slow circle, and felt the flat open around him like a hand opening to show it was empty.

No tracks leaving.

Not north, not south, not east toward the drowned canyon country, not west toward the freight road. The depression held their arrival plain as handwriting and their departure not at all. Which meant one of two things: they were very good, or they were very close.

Casper had spent five years learning which of those two things Ilsa Vey was, and the answer was both, always both, and the *both* was what made her worth the miles.

* * *

He made camp without fire in the lee of the broken wall, ate cold jerky and harder bread, and did not sleep so much as rest his eyes while the rest of him listened. This was ranger craft, the oldest kind: you let the desert be your ears. Wind moved salt crystals across the flats in a fine continuous whisper, and under that whisper, if you tuned yourself right, you could hear the difference between wind-sound and weight-sound. A boot on crust has a particular crisp fracture-note. A knee joint, older, has a lower complaint.

Sometime past midnight, in the hour when the cold comes up out of the ground like something exhaled, he heard the knee.

He didn't move. He lay with his breath shallow and his hand loose near the iron and listened to the sound cross maybe forty feet of darkness toward him, unhurried, and stop.

"You're getting old, Quintana," said a voice from the dark, low and dry as the flats themselves. "You used to hear me at sixty feet."

"You used to be worth hearing at sixty feet." He sat up slowly. His heart was doing something complicated that he declined to examine. "There were two sets of prints."

"There were."

"Where's your friend?"

"Asleep. Or gone. Depends how the next few minutes go." A pause, and then she stepped into the edge of his vision, where the starlight off the salt made her a gray outline: slight, wiry, hood back, face a pale smudge he knew better than his own reflection because he'd studied hers in mirrors of water and window glass and once, memorably, in the polished lid of a strongbox very like the one she'd taken from Corium Crossing. "You followed the manifest."

"I follow everything."

"No," she said, and there was something in it—not mockery, something flatter and stranger—"you follow me. Everything else is just terrain."

He should have answered that. He had answers stockpiled for it, ranger answers, duty answers. Instead he heard himself say, "You drew water from a brackish well. You've never done that. You'd ride twenty miles for clean rather than drink white water."

"I've changed."

"People don't change."

"You'd know," Ilsa Vey said. "Five years and you haven't."

The words landed somewhere he hadn't armored, which was most places, and he covered it by standing, brushing salt from his coat, making himself large and official. "The strongbox. Echo-salt, raw cut, nine pounds. That's archive-grade material. That's not shrine trinkets and pressed flowers, Vey. That's the kind of take that gets people killed, and not by me."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because in five years I've built a profile of you that fits like a glove, and none of it explains nine pounds of raw echo-salt. You don't sell. You don't use—at least not in any way I've ever found traces of. You *collect*. So either you've finally found a buyer, or—"

"Or I'm delivering," she said quietly.

The wind moved across the flats and the salt whispered and for a moment neither of them spoke, and Casper became aware that this was the longest conversation they had ever had. Five years of near-misses, of her silhouette cresting a ridge ahead of him, of notes pinned to fence posts in her slanted hand—*Slow down, Quintana, you'll give yourself a heart attack before you catch me*—and never once a conversation. Conversations required two people willing to stand still, and stillness was the one thing neither of them had ever offered the other.

"Delivering to whom?" he asked.

"To someone who pays in things worse than money."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have room for tonight." She shifted her weight, and he saw her glance—quick, involuntary—toward the broken wall on the far side of the depression, toward wherever the second set of boot prints had gone, and something in her face did a thing he had never seen it do in five years of study.

It looked afraid.

"Ilsa." He used her first name deliberately, a card he rarely played. "Whatever you're carrying, whoever's waiting at the end of that trail—you know I'm going to follow it. You know that better than anyone alive. So the only question is whether you tell me now, standing up, or I dig it out of the ground later, the hard way."

For a long moment she looked at him, and the starlight was just bright enough for him to see her decide something, and just dim enough that he couldn't tell what she decided.

"Casper," she said, and his name in her mouth sounded strange and worn, like a coin from a country that no longer existed, "do you remember the tide charts for the White Reach?"

He blinked. Of all the directions the night might have turned, this was not one he'd charted. "What tide charts? There are no tides. There hasn't been water out here in two hundred years. That's the whole point of—"

"The old survey maps," she said. "Your ranger academy. First-year cartography. The Salt Territories were seabed once. There are charts. There are *depths*, marked in fathoms, under all this salt." Her voice had gone careful, level, the voice of someone reciting coordinates to keep from saying something else. "Do you remember what the deepest sounding was?"

He did remember—he remembered the classroom, the smell of chalk and lamp oil, a younger self who still believed the territory would need him—and the number surfaced now with absurd clarity, rising like something coming up from deep water. "Forty fathoms," he said. "Sounding taken at a place the old surveyors called the Cave of Admission. Ranger legend. Half of it's myth."

"Yes," Ilsa Vey said. "Half of it is."

And far out across the flats, beyond the broken walls, beyond the whispering salt, came a sound that Casper Quintana had never heard in all his years in the White Reach and would spend the rest of his life unable to forget.

Water. Moving.
