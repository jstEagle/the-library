## 2. The Tide That Shouldn't Come

Casper Quintana woke to the sound of water where there should have been no water.

He had made camp on a shelf of black stone above the flats, as he had done a hundred nights before, and he knew every sound that country made: the tick of cooling salt, the wind combing through the wiregrass on the rise, the far-off groan of the earth settling as the day's heat bled out of it. This was none of those. This was a long, low hush, rhythmic and patient, like something breathing in its sleep. Like surf.

There was no sea within two hundred miles of the Salt Horizon.

He sat up with his hand already on his rifle and looked out at a world remade. The flats—those blinding white miles of crusted salt that he had crossed so many times he could have walked them blindfolded by starlight alone—were gone. In their place lay water, dark and still and vast, stretching to a horizon that no longer matched his memory of it. The moon hung low and fat over it, laying a road of silver across the surface, and the surface breathed. Rose, fell. Rose again. A tide, working at land that had never once in recorded history felt one.

For a long moment Casper did not move. He was a man who trusted his instruments more than his eyes, and he checked them now out of habit: the barometer needle pinned hard against its stop, the way it did before flash floods in the high country. The temperature had dropped six degrees since midnight. And the compass—his father's old brass compass, the one he carried without quite knowing why, because a ranger's father's compass was not the sort of thing you left behind even when it pointed true north like any other—lay in his palm trembling, the needle shivering through a slow half-circle as though it could smell something under the water.

"Well," he said aloud, to no one, because talking to no one was another habit five years had carved into him. "That shouldn't be."

He climbed to the highest point of the rise while the sky went from ink to iron to a pale bruised pink, and watched the sun come up over an inland sea. It was beautiful. That was the thing he would remember afterward—not the wrongness first, but the beauty, the flats transformed into something out of the stories rangers told green recruits around the fire. Water lapped at the base of the rise, clear near the shore, shading down into depths that swallowed the light whole. Things moved in those depths. Once, briefly, he saw a shape pass beneath the silver road of the moon's reflection—a long shadow, deliberate, unbothered—and decided not to think about it.

The rise stood perhaps forty feet above the new waterline, a spine of ancient rock jutting up out of the salt like the back of a drowned animal. It was maybe three hundred paces long and fifty wide, clothed in wiregrass and wind-twisted juniper, and Casper walked its length slowly, taking stock the way he'd been trained to take stock twenty years ago, when he'd been a young ranger with a family waiting for him at the edge of the territory and a future that had seemed, if not bright, at least predictable.

Water enough to drown the flats would drown the lowlands too. The border settlements. The trade roads. Every route off this rise except the one that led back east, into country he'd already searched twice. He was stranded. Not dangerously—not yet—but stranded all the same, and stranded men had time, and time was the one thing Casper Quintana had spent five years refusing to sit still inside of.

He measured his days in miles. That was the arithmetic he lived by now. Not years—years were what other people had, people whose lives kept their shape—but miles covered, sign read, cold camps made and broken. Fourteen hundred miles in the first year, chasing her. Eleven hundred the next. He didn't count anymore; counting implied an end, and some part of him had quietly arranged things so there would never be an end. Ilsa Vey was the reason the horizon stayed ahead of him instead of behind. As long as she ran, he followed, and as long as he followed, he never had to arrive anywhere—never had to stand in the doorway of an empty house, or look too long at the small grave markers behind the mission at Dry Fork, or answer the question that waited underneath every mile like a stone under shallow water: *what are you actually running toward?*

So when he saw, halfway along the rise, a set of boot prints in the salt mud that hadn't been there at midnight—he was almost relieved.

They were small prints, narrow-heeled, set close together, the left foot landing a fraction heavier than the right. An old injury, years ago, a fall he'd once tracked her across nine days of badlands by that tell alone. He crouched over them and felt the particular stupid warmth he always felt and always hated—the tracker's pleasure at a familiar trail, which was also, if he was honest, just loneliness recognizing itself.

She was on the rise. Stranded, like him.

* * *

He found her at noon, sitting on the eastern tip of the rock spine with her back against a juniper and her boots off, drying her feet in the sun as though she were on holiday.

Ilsa Vey was not what the wanted notices described. The notices said *dangerous*, and she was, but they always drew her wrong—hard-eyed, heavyset, a brute with a knife. In person she was slight and quick-boned, with dark hair cut short and practical and a face that might have been pretty if it ever committed to an expression. She wore a thief's grey, patched at the knees, and she had heard him coming—of course she had heard him coming; she'd been hearing him come for five years—and she didn't reach for a weapon. She just watched him approach across the grass with the weary courtesy of someone greeting a coworker.

"Quintana," she said. "You look terrible."

"You're trespassing on a ranger observation point."

"It's a rock." She wiggled her toes at the sun. "And we're both trespassing on it until the water goes down, which—" she nodded at the flooded flats, and for the first time he saw something in her face that wasn't insolence. It took him a moment to name it, because he'd never seen it there before. It was unease. "Which it isn't going to do. Look at the shoreline. It hasn't dropped a hand's width since dawn."

He looked. She was right. Tides came in and tides went out; that was their nature, even impossible ones. But the water lay against the rocks exactly where it had lain at first light, patient as a held breath.

"How long have you been here?" he asked.

"Since before you woke up." She pulled her boots back on, unhurried. "I was crossing the flats at night, same as always. Heard the water coming. Nearly didn't make the rise." She glanced at him sideways. "You've gotten slow, by the way. Used to be you'd have found me by midmorning."

"I wasn't looking for you."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "You're always looking for me. It's the only thing you do."

The words landed harder than they should have, precisely because they were true and she knew it and said it the way you'd note the weather. Casper crouched at the edge of the rise, put his fingers in the water, and let the silence do what silence did between them—which was not, despite everything, feel like an enemy's silence. Five years of pursuit taught a man things he didn't want to know. He knew she slept on her left side. He knew she paid for meals with coins arranged heads-up, a superstition she'd deny. He knew she talked to herself in empty buildings, narrating her own movements like a story she was telling someone who wasn't there yet. He knew her better than anyone alive, and she knew him, and neither of them had ever said so, because saying so would mean admitting what the chase actually was: the closest either of them had come, in years, to having a companion.

"The tide," he said. "You've crossed these flats for how long?"

"Longer than you've chased me."

"Have you ever seen water out there?"

"No." She said it flatly, and then, quieter: "But my grandmother used to sing a song about it. A nonsense song. *When the sea remembers the salt, the door below will open.* We thought it was about baking." Her mouth twisted. "Ranger legends say something similar, don't they? You get that look. The one where you know more than you're saying."

"There's a story," Casper admitted. "Old ranger lore. The Cave of Admission. They say there's a cave mouth in the salt country below the waterline, sealed under the crust, and it only shows itself when the land is ready to admit something. Or confess something. The versions disagree." He hesitated. He had not spoken this much to another living soul in months. "My father used to say the cave was where the river goes to remember where it's been."

Something changed in her posture. Small, but he caught it—he caught everything about her; that was the whole cursed problem. "Your father," she repeated. "The cartographer."

"You know about him."

"I know a lot of things about you, Quintana. Occupational hazard." But she was looking past him now, out at the water, and her voice had gone careful. "Look there. Due south, where the rise drops into the deep channel. What do you see?"

He turned. The southern end of the rock spine fell away steeply into dark water, and beneath the surface, where the morning light struck at the right angle, there was a shape in the drowned rock. A regularity. Edges too straight for geology, a darkness too deliberate for shadow—an archway, vast and ancient, standing open in the underwater cliff face like a held-open door. As he watched, the light shifted and the arch filled with a slow pulse of pale luminescence, deep down, rhythmic, like something breathing in its sleep.

"That shouldn't be visible," Casper said. His own voice sounded strange to him. "If that's real, it's been under the salt for centuries. The crust out there is thirty feet thick in places. There's no way—"

"The land is admitting something," Ilsa said softly. "Or confessing." She got to her feet, and for the first time in five years of pursuit, Ilsa Vey looked afraid—not of him, not of capture, but of the water. "Quintana. Why did you really come out here? Don't give me the ranger patrol answer. Nobody patrols the deep flats. There's nothing out here to protect and nothing worth stealing." She turned and looked at him directly, and her eyes were sharp and tired and older than her face. "Except whatever it is we've both been circling without saying."

He wanted to lie to her. It was practically doctrine—she was a thief, a fugitive, his quarry; you didn't hand your quarry your reasons. But the water breathed against the rocks, and the arch glowed in the deep, and the compass in his pocket trembled against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and he was so very tired of carrying everything alone.

"A month ago," he said, "reports started coming in from the border villages. People dreaming the same dream. Water over the flats. A door under the water. Old folks waking up crying, calling names of people dead fifty years—names they swore they'd forgotten." He paused. "And then last week, a drover came into Fort Meridian raving that he'd met his dead brother walking the salt at night, solid as sunrise, who told him the river was rising and everyone should go upstream before it finished remembering."

Ilsa had gone very still. "That's why you rode out."

"That's why I rode out."

"And the chase? Five years of the chase—was that a report too?"

He didn't answer, and she nodded as if he had, as if his silence were the most honest thing he'd said all day. She walked to the edge of the rise and stood looking down into the glowing dark, arms crossed against a chill that had nothing to do with the wind.

"I wasn't stealing valuables," she said abruptly. "All these years. Whatever you found in my packs, whatever the Magistrate's warrants say—it wasn't for money. I was collecting something else. Out here. In places like—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Places like that."

"Echo-salt," Casper said quietly.

Her head snapped around. "How do you know that word?"

Because he'd found it in her abandoned camps, he almost said—in little wrapped bundles of crystalline salt that hummed faintly when held to the ear, that whispered when warmed by a fire, that had made the hair on his arms stand up the one time he'd dared hold a shard to his own head and listen. Because he'd told himself it was evidence and kept a piece anyway, in a box, under his bedroll, and on bad nights he took it out and held it and almost heard—

"Rangers hear things," he said. "What does Corvane want with it?"

The name landed between them like a dropped knife. Ilsa's face closed so fast and so completely that it was like watching a door barred from inside, and Casper understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has tracked one person for five years, that he had just asked the one question she could not afford to answer—that answering it would cost her more than capture, more than death, maybe more than whatever she was.

"I don't work for Corvane," she said.

"You flinched."

"I flinch at lots of things." But her hand had gone, unconsciously, to her left sleeve—to the place, he realized, where he'd once glimpsed the edge of a brand or a scar she never explained. "Forget it. Forget I said anything. The tide's not dropping, the water's full of things that glow, and there's a door down there that ranger songs say leads to the place where lost things go. We're stuck on this rock together whether we like it or not, so here's my proposal: we don't talk about the Magistrate, we don't talk about warrants, and we don't talk about the last five years. Truce. One day. Maybe two, if the water behaves."

"And after?"

"After," she said, "we go back to being what we were." But she said it strangely, without conviction, the way you recite a prayer you've stopped believing, and she wouldn't meet his eyes.

* * *

Night came down on the inland sea, and the water glowed.

It began at dusk—a faint phosphorescence along the shoreline, blue-green, delicate as frost—and by full dark the whole flooded flat burned with soft light, mile upon mile of it, so that the rise stood like an island in a lake of stars. Fish-shaped shadows moved through the glow, trailing sparks. And beneath everything, steady as a drumbeat, the arch in the southern cliff pulsed its slow pale rhythm, brighter now, closer to the surface than it had been at noon.

Casper stood watch because standing watch was the only prayer he knew. Behind him, Ilsa slept in fits, muttering, her hand curled around a knife she hadn't bothered to hide from him, which was its own strange kind of trust.

Near midnight, the whispers started.

At first he thought it was the wind. Then he thought it was the echo-salt in his pack, humming. Then he understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the night air, that the voices were coming from the water—from the glow itself, from thousands of tiny lights rising and falling with the tide. They weren't words, exactly. They were fragments. A woman laughing. A child counting to ten and losing her place. Someone humming a lullaby he knew, God help him, a lullaby he knew, and he was moving before he decided to move, down the rocks to the waterline, kneeling, reaching out—

"Casper." Ilsa's voice, awake now, sharp with alarm. "Don't touch it."

His fingers hovered an inch above the luminous water. In the glow directly below his hand, a small light rose toward the surface, and in it—for a heartbeat, for less than a heartbeat—he heard a girl's voice, bright and bossy and eleven years old forever, saying the thing his sister used to say every time he left home:

*Bring me back something shiny.*

The light sank. The whisper faded. Casper knelt at the edge of the impossible sea with his heart hammering and his eyes burning, and behind him Ilsa Vey said, very quietly, in a voice stripped of all its armor:

"Now you understand why I was stealing it."

Below the southern cliffs, the great arch flared—once, twice—like a door being knocked upon from the other side.
