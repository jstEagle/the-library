## 4. The Magistrate's Men

The smoke came before the riders.

Casper saw it first as a smudge on the western horizon, a gray thumbprint against the pale morning sky where no cookfire had any business being. He had spent five years learning to read the salt flats the way other men read faces—the way light bent off a crust at dawn told him where the brine lay shallow, the way wind combed the dust told him what had passed through and when. Smoke was a language too. This one said: many horses, moving fast, and burning something that did not want to burn.

He was on his feet before he finished understanding it.

Below the rise, the tide still held—black water lapping at the salt in long slow breaths, as if the flats had become some vast sleeping animal. The cave mouth yawned beneath the waterline, half-drowned, its throat glowing faintly with that strange interior light Ilsa had refused to explain. And beside him, wrapped in a threadbare coat three sizes too large for her, stood the woman he had chased across a thousand miles of nothing.

Ilsa Vey was watching the smoke too. That was the first thing wrong. A thief surprised by riders would be watching the riders. She was watching the cave.

"You knew they were coming," Casper said.

"I suspected." Her voice was flat as the horizon. "Suspecting and knowing are different animals."

"Five years," he said. "Five years I've chased you, and you've never once let me catch you by accident. So tell me why you're standing here now, letting me look at you in full daylight, close enough to count the freckles."

She almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Because counting freckles is about to be the least of your problems, ranger."

The smoke thickened. He could smell it now—not woodsmoke, not dung-fire, but something mineral and acrid, like a forge fed on bone. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the morning wind: singing. Or something like singing. A high, thin keening that rose from the drowned cave mouth and frayed apart over the water.

The echoes, he thought. The voices in the salt. Whatever those men were burning, it was making them scream.

* * *

They came down the old survey road at a canter, twelve riders in the slate-and-silver of the Magistrate's territorial guard, and behind them a wagon drawn by two mules that moved with the sullen patience of animals that had learned not to spook at anything. At their head rode a man in a long coat the color of dried blood, his face wrapped to the eyes against the salt glare, so that only his mouth showed—a mouth that seemed permanently set around the shape of a word he enjoyed saying.

Casper pulled Ilsa down behind the spine of rock at the rise's crest. She came without resistance, which frightened him more than anything else that morning. Ilsa Vey did not hide unless hiding was necessary. Hiding meant she could not simply talk her way out, or steal her way out, or vanish into landscape she knew better than her own hands.

"Corvane's men," she murmured. "That's Brannoc leading them."

"The Magistrate sends his own enforcer to the middle of nowhere?"

"He sends Brannoc wherever things need to stop existing." She was watching the riders with the fixed attention of someone reading a document written in a language she hated. "Casper. Listen to me. When they find us—and they will find us—you say nothing. You let me do the talking."

"Why would I—"

"Because they already know your name." She turned and looked at him then, really looked, and there was something in her face he had never seen in five years of pursuit across the flats: pity. "They've known it longer than you'd believe."

He didn't have time to ask what that meant. Below, the riders had reached the waterline and halted. Brannoc dismounted, waded calf-deep toward the cave mouth, and made a gesture—casual, almost bored—that sent two of his men back to the wagon. They returned carrying clay jars the size of a man's head, sealed with wax and stamped with a sigil Casper recognized from border postings: a circle bisected by a line. The mark of the Magistrate's Office of Records.

The jars went into the water. A moment later the surface of the tide began to boil—not with heat, Casper realized, but with sound. The water itself seemed to shudder, and the keening from the cave swelled into a chorus, dozens of voices, hundreds, rising through the flooded entrance in bubbles of silver light that burst against the air and scattered like startled birds.

"What are they doing?" His voice came out hoarse.

"Harvesting," Ilsa said. "Or destroying. With Corvane it's usually both." She exhaled slowly. "Those are dissolving salts. Ranger-grade alchemy, if your academy ever taught you anything useful. Pour them into an echo-chamber and everything stored in the formations comes loose. Then the wagons collect what floats."

"Collects what—" He stopped. Started again. "The voices. The dead people talking in the salt. You're telling me they can be *collected*."

"Bottled. Labeled. Filed." She said each word like she was setting down stones. "I know because I'm the one who does the collecting, Casper. Has been for six years. Every theft you ever chased me for—every vault, every reliquary, every dusty archive between here and the Verge—I wasn't stealing gold. I was stealing echo-salt. Fragments of the dead. Memories pressed into mineral." She finally turned to face him fully. "For the Magistrate's private archive. For Corvane's collection."

The world tilted slightly. Casper found himself gripping the rock beneath him as though the rise might tip him into the flood.

Six years. Which meant the last year of his family's lives, Ilsa Vey had already been serving. Which meant—

"No," he said.

"Yes," she said gently, and the gentleness was worse than cruelty. "And it gets worse, ranger. But first we have to survive the next ten minutes, because Brannoc just looked up at this rise, and he has eyes like a hawk that's been starved on purpose."

* * *

Brannoc's voice carried across the water without effort, the way certain voices do, built for command the way a hook is built for meat.

"Ranger Quintana." Not a question. "Come down. Bring the thief. The Magistrate extends his compliments and his patience, and I'd advise you to spend both wisely."

Casper rose slowly, hands visible, the old ranger habit of surrender-as-calculation settling over him like a familiar coat. Twelve riders. One rifle, ten rounds. A flooded flat between him and them. The arithmetic was poor and getting poorer.

Ilsa stood beside him, and he noticed she had gone very still in a particular way—he'd seen it once before, years ago, through a spyglass outside the customs house at Dry Fork, seconds before she'd walked out of a locked room past four guards who swore afterward no one had passed them. Stillness as a weapon being drawn.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"They'll take me either way," she said. "The only question is whether they take you too. And whether you're alive when they do."

"That's my concern."

"It stopped being only yours the moment you started chasing me." For one unguarded instant something flickered across her face—something that in another life, another story, might have been called regret. "You should have asked yourself sooner why a thief worth five years of your life was always exactly one day ahead of you. Never two. Never caught. Never gone."

The words landed somewhere deep and cold. He filed them away, because Brannoc was waiting, and because some part of him understood that the answer to that question was a door he could not yet afford to open.

They descended to the waterline.

Up close, Brannoc was bigger than rumor suggested, a slab of a man whose wrapped face gave him the featureless authority of a monument. His eyes above the cloth were pale and patient and entirely without warmth, and they went to Ilsa first, the way a handler checks on valuable livestock.

"Vey. Six weeks overdue. The Magistrate is displeased."

"Tell him the tide interfered," Ilsa said. "Tell him the Cave of Admission opened, which his own surveyors said was legend. Tell him I have information about the source that's worth more than six weeks of his displeasure."

Something shifted in Brannoc's posture. Interest, or its counterfeit. "The source."

"The Well of Return. The river runs backward for a reason, Brannoc. Your master has been bottling the dead for a decade and he still doesn't know where the river *starts*. I do. Or I can find it." She spread her empty hands. "Kill me and he never learns it."

Behind Brannoc, one of the riders laughed softly. The enforcer didn't turn, but his mouth tightened inside the wrapping.

"And the ranger?" Brannoc's pale eyes slid to Casper. "Quintana. Five years hunting this woman across every flat and ford in the territory. The Magistrate finds your dedication... notable."

"Notable how?" Casper kept his voice level.

"Notable enough that orders concerning you predate your chase." Brannoc said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest, without emphasis, and somehow that made it land harder than any threat could have. "You were to be kept occupied. Moving. Away from the river country. Away from questions." He paused, and the pause was surgical. "It seems the occupation worked too well. You became difficult to recall."

The flats tilted again. Casper heard his own breathing, loud and strange. *Kept occupied.* Five years. Five years of miles instead of days, of purpose instead of grief, and the purpose had been issued to him like equipment, like a saddle and a rifle, by a man in a citadel he'd never seen.

Beside him, Ilsa had gone very still again—but differently now. Watching him. Waiting, he realized, to see what he would do with the truth. Whether he would break, or burn, or run, or—

He drew his rifle.

He didn't aim it. He simply held it, angled at the ground between them all, and said, in the flat tone he used for reporting deaths, "Then the Magistrate's orders are out of date. Because I'm done being occupied."

"Casper." Ilsa's voice, low and urgent.

"Twelve riders, ranger," Brannoc said. "One rifle."

"One rifle," Casper agreed, "and a flooded flat, and a cave full of screaming dead behind me, and nothing left to lose that hasn't already been stolen. Do the arithmetic, enforcer. I've been doing it for five years. I'm very good at it."

For a long moment nobody moved. The tide breathed against the salt. Somewhere under the water, the cave's chorus had gone quiet, harvested or smothered, and the silence it left felt like a held breath.

Then Brannoc laughed. It was a terrible sound, muffled by the wrapping, like laughter heard through a wall.

"Oh, the Magistrate will want you alive after all," he said. "This is better than obedience. This is *material*." He raised one hand, and the riders' rifles came up in a single smooth motion, twelve black bores tracking Casper and Ilsa both. "Take them. Alive. The thief for the archive, the ranger for—"

The world exploded.

Not the riders—the *water*. Behind Brannoc, the tide heaved upward in a column of spray and silver light, and out of the flooded cave mouth came a sound Casper felt in his teeth: a voice, vast and layered, a hundred dead throats speaking at once, and the word they spoke was not a word at all but a *name*, and the name was *Quintana*, and the mules screamed and bolted, dragging the wagon sideways, and the horses broke in every direction at once.

Later, Casper could never reconstruct those ninety seconds. There was spray, and light, and Brannoc shouting orders that dissolved into the panic, and Ilsa's hand hard around his wrist hauling him backward up the rise while the riders' formation shredded itself against animals gone mad with terror. There was the strange certainty, burned into him forever, that whatever had spoken his name from under the water had known him—had *recognized* him, the way the ferry-legend ghosts of the drowned country were said to recognize their own.

Then they were running, the two of them, along the spine of the rise with the chaos boiling below, and the flats stretched eastward, endless and blinding, offering nothing but distance.

* * *

They ran until the sun stood high enough to burn the shadows out from under their feet, and then they walked, and then they stopped in the lee of a collapsed survey marker, gasping, and the flats around them were empty in every direction.

Isla sat down heavily against the broken stone. Casper stood over her, rifle cradled, watching the horizon they'd come from. Nothing followed. Yet.

"You planned that," he said finally.

"I hoped for it." She wiped salt sweat from her face. "Echo chambers hold charge. Dissolving salts agitate them. Sometimes the whole chamber discharges at once. I've seen it happen in a vault in Corvane's own citadel—put three men on the floor for a week." She looked up at him. "I didn't know it would say your name. That part I want you to believe."

"Why would it say my name?"

"Because the dead in that cave included people who knew you." She held his gaze, and he watched her decide something—watched the decision cost her. "Your sister's voice was in there, Casper. I heard it last night. I didn't tell you because there was no version of telling you that didn't end with you walking into that cave alone, and the tide doesn't recede for days."

His sister. Mara, who sang off-key in the kitchen. Mara, whose voice he had not heard in five years except in the failing theater of his own memory, and even that was going, wearing thin as an old coin. It had been in the salt. It had been *harvested*, jarred, stamped with a circle bisected by a line, hauled away in a wagon toward a citadel where a man collected the dead like stamps.

Casper sat down. It wasn't a decision; his legs simply ended their contract. The rifle lay across his knees and he stared at it and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that everything he had been for five years was furniture in someone else's room.

"Talk," he said. "All of it. Now. While I'm too tired to hate you properly."

So she talked. Six years ago, her brother—she said the word *brother* like a door opening onto a dark room and immediately closing—had gotten himself indebted to the Magistrate's office, and the debt had been transferable, and Ilsa Vey had signed herself into service to clear it. Echo-theft, she explained, was delicate work; the salt remembered best near places of strong feeling, battlefields and sickrooms and flooded villages, and retrieving fragments intact required a thief's hands and a thief's nerve. Corvane paid in protection and in small glass vials of distilled memory, which his servants were permitted to drink on high holidays, tasting lives that weren't theirs.

"And the chase," Casper said. "Us. Five years."

Her jaw tightened. "Orders. I was told to stay ahead of you. Always one step. Close enough to keep you invested, far enough to keep you hungry. The Magistrate wanted you moving, ranger. He wanted you *spent*." She hesitated, and he saw her choose the next words with visible care. "There's more to it than that. About why you specifically. But I won't say it out here on open salt with my heart going like a drum. If I'm going to lose you, I'd rather it not be in the middle of a desert with his riders behind us."

"That's not how honesty works."

"No," she agreed. "Honesty works much worse than that. Usually all at once, usually too late." She got to her feet, joints cracking, and faced east, where the land began its long imperceptible climb toward the drowned canyons and the river country beyond. "Here's what I can give you now. A bargain."

"Name it."

"I lead you upriver. To the source. The Well of Return—the thing Corvane actually wants, the reason for the archive, the reason for you. Everything you've lost, everything that's been taken and bottled and filed—it comes back to its owner at the source. That's the legend, and I've seen enough of the edges of it to believe the legend undersells it." She turned back to him. "In exchange, you keep me alive. Not out of duty. Out of leverage—I'm the only map to the source that Corvane doesn't already own, and the moment Brannoc reports in, every bounty paper in the territory has both our names on it. We're the same animal now, ranger. Two targets, one arrow."

Casper looked at her for a long time. Five years of studying this woman from a distance—her stride, her campsites, the way she banked a fire and the way she lied—and now, at arm's length, she was almost a stranger again, because everything he'd thought he knew had been staged. Except. Except the steadiness of her eyes right now. That hadn't been in any of the reports he'd filed with himself over five lonely years. That was new. Or it had been there all along, waiting for daylight.

"If you're lying," he said, "about the source, about my sister, about any of it—"

"I'm lying about some of it," she said simply. "Everyone is lying about some of it. The trick is figuring out which lies are load-bearing." She shouldered her pack. "Coming?"

Far to the west, a thread of smoke still rose from the drowned cave, thin and straight in the windless air, like a candle lit for the dead. Casper took one last look at it—at the place where his sister's voice had screamed and been silenced in the same hour—and then turned east, toward the river that ran backward, toward the source of everything taken.

"One condition," he said, falling into step beside her.

"Name it."

"When we get there—whatever the Well gives back—I drink first. My family's memories come home before Corvane's empire gets a drop. Swear it."

Ilsa Vey walked in silence for thirty paces. Then: "Sworn," she said. "And Casper—when we get there, you may wish you'd never sworn me anything at all."

The flats ended that evening, and the drowned canyons began, and far upstream, in a citadel of black stone, a man was pouring himself a glass of someone else's life.
