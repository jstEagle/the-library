## 6. The Drowned Village

They smelled it before they saw it: woodsmoke and wet stone, and beneath both something sweeter, like honey left too long in the sun. Casper had been following the river's backward current for two days, and in all that time they had passed nothing living—not a bird, not a lizard, not so much as a beetle crossing their path in the drowned canyons. The silence had begun to feel like a held breath. Now, rounding a shoulder of red rock where the water narrowed and quickened, he caught the smoke on the wind and felt something in his chest loosen that he hadn't known was clenched.

"There," Ilsa said, and pointed with her chin.

The village clung to the canyon wall like a barnacle. Houses of timber and pale stone rose in tiers from the waterline, their lower stories dark and windowless, drowned to the second floor and abandoned there—doors opening onto nothing, staircases descending into green water, a rope swing hanging motionless over a flooded street. Above the waterline the village continued, rebuilt and rebuilt again, each tier a little newer than the one below, so that the whole settlement looked like the rings of a tree cut open to show its years. Lanterns hung from lines strung between rooftops. Smoke rose from chimneys that had been moved upward three times, four times, their old scars still visible on the walls below.

"Merrow's Landing," Ilsa said. "I thought it was a story."

"Most of the river is a story," Casper said. "That's never stopped it."

They came down to the village by a switchback path cut into the rock, and as they descended, the people of the Landing turned to watch. Casper counted them the way he counted everything—out of habit, out of the ranger's reflex for threat and number. Forty, perhaps fifty souls, and not one of them looking at him the way strangers looked at a stranger. There was no suspicion in their faces. There was no curiosity either. What he saw instead, in face after weathered face, was something he had no name for: a kind of quiet recognition, patient and unstartled, as though he were a letter they had been expecting for years and had long since stopped waiting for.

An old man mending a net looked up, took in Casper's dust-caked coat and Ilsa's hood, and said only, "You'll want the ferrywoman. She takes strays."

"I'm not a stray," Ilsa said.

"No," the old man agreed mildly, and went back to his net.

The path led them along a street that ended in water—literally ended, the cobbles running down into green depths where a drowned doorway stood open below the surface, silt drifting through it like breath. Boats were tied where carts should have been. A child sat on a rooftop fishing with a line and a bent pin, and did not look up as they passed, though her line twitched and she pulled in a small silver fish with the ease of long practice. On a wall beside them, painted in fading white, someone had written: *THE RIVER TAKES NOTHING IT DOES NOT GIVE BACK.*

"Cheerful place," Ilsa murmured.

"They believe it, though," Casper said. "That's the strange part."

The ferrywoman's house stood at the village's lowest inhabited tier, a broad, low building with its foundations lapped by the river, its porch posts wrapped in barnacles to the height of a man. A boat was tied at her door the way another woman might keep a horse at hers. The door itself stood open, and inside, lamplight the color of old honey fell across a room crowded with bottles.

Casper stopped on the threshold.

Bottles everywhere—on shelves, in crates, hanging from the ceiling in nets, packed in straw under the table. They were not all the same. Some were ordinary glass; some were salt-carved, opaque and milky; a few were so old their surfaces had gone cloudy as sea ice. And in many of them, when the lamplight caught them at the right angle, something *moved*—a slow silver turning, like fish in deep water, like light on the underside of a wave.

"Memories," Ilsa said quietly. Beside him, Casper felt her go very still, in the particular way she went still around valuable things. "Bottled. I'd heard of it. I didn't believe it."

"You'd know more about that than I would," Casper said, and did not look at her, and heard her not answer.

The ferrywoman sat by the window with her back to them, mending something—a sail, a net, a shroud; Casper could not tell. She was small and brown and wrinkled as driftwood, with a rope of gray hair coiled at her neck and hands that moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had done the same work for seventy years. She did not turn when they entered.

"Sit," she said. "There's stew. It's fish. It's always fish."

They sat. There was stew. It was fish. It was good.

The ferrywoman mended and did not speak, and the river outside her window made its low backward murmur, and Casper found, to his surprise, that his shoulders were coming down from around his ears. He had not been in a room with a fire and food and an old woman mending since—since. He let the thought go where it wanted and did not follow it. Five years of practice. He was very good at not following.

It was the ferrywoman who broke the silence, and when she did, she turned at last, and her eyes found Casper's face and stopped there.

She looked at him for a long time. The mending stilled in her hands. Outside, the river murmured; inside, the bottles turned their slow silver turnings; and the old woman studied his face with an expression that moved through several weathers—surprise, then something like sorrow, then a settled, careful blankness, the look of a woman closing a door on a room she has decided not to enter.

"You have your grandmother's chin," she said finally.

Casper's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I knew a face," the ferrywoman said. "Faces come up the river the way fish do. Some of them you recognize." She set down her mending. "Eat your stew."

"You knew my grandmother," Casper said again. "Which grandmother. What was her name. Where—"

"Ranger," the ferrywoman said, and her voice was still mild, but under the mildness was bedrock, "I have lived on this river longer than your territory has had a Magistrate. I have pulled drowned men from the water who sat up and thanked me. I have bottled the last hour of the dying for their families, and I have poured those bottles back into the river when the families could not pay, because the river keeps what it is given and gives it back when it chooses, and that is not my business and never was." She leaned forward. The lamplight moved in the milky glass of the bottles behind her, and for a moment they seemed all to be turning toward Casper, attentive. "So when I tell you that I know your face, and that I will not say from where, you will believe that I have my reasons, and that my reasons are older and deeper than your questions."

Casper set down the spoon. His heart was doing something he did not authorize it to do—something quick and young and hopeful, the heartbeat of a boy before, before, before. "My family," he said. "The border raid. Five years ago. The village of—"

"I know what you're going to ask," the ferrywoman said.

"Then you know I'm going to keep asking it."

"Yes," she said. "That's why I'm not answering. Answers given too fast are like fish caught too young. They don't keep." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to a shelf, and Casper watched her hands move among the bottles—past this one, past that one, pausing, moving on—until they stopped at a bottle near the end, small, old, its glass gone cloudy as fogged ice. She did not take it down. She rested two fingers on it, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you had decided not to wake.

"Your face," she said, without turning, "came up the river once before. In a bottle. A long time ago, and not so long. The river gives things back when it chooses, ranger. It has been choosing you for a while now." She took her hand away. "Eat your stew. You'll want your strength for the questions you haven't asked yet."

Behind him, Ilsa had not touched her food. Casper could feel her watching—not him, the ferrywoman—with the fixed, hungry attention she gave to anything she'd been sent to find.

* * *

They were given a room above the ferrywoman's boathouse, two pallets and a lamp and a window over the water, and Casper lay awake listening to the river run backward beneath the floor. The house creaked. The barnacles on the porch posts clicked and shifted, a sound like small teeth. Somewhere below, in the drowned lower story, water moved through rooms where furniture still stood, and once—Casper could not have sworn to it, and did not swear to it—he heard, through the floor, through the water, a sound like a voice, or many voices, the way a shell holds the sea.

He got up. He told himself it was the ranger's habit, the perimeter walk, the count of exits, and some of that was even true. But his feet took him down the stairs and out onto the porch and along the waterline of the village, where the lanterns burned low and the boats knocked gently at their moorings, and he walked past the flooded street with its open drowned door, and past the wall with its painted promise, and stopped at last where an old woman sat alone at the end of a dock, mending by moonlight.

"You don't sleep either," she said, without looking up.

"The river's loud."

"It's saying your name," the ferrywoman said. "It's been saying it for days. That's why the tide came. That's why the cave opened. The river is very patient, but it is not subtle."

Casper sat down on the dock beside her, his boots over the black water. Below him, deep down, something silver turned.

"Tell me," he said.

"No."

"You said I'd want my strength for the questions I haven't asked. So ask them for me. Ask them and answer them. I'm tired, grandmother-of-no-one, I'm tired in a way that miles don't fix, and there is a woman in that house who knows more than she's told me, and a Magistrate behind us, and a river ahead of us that runs the wrong way, and I am so tired of not knowing."

The ferrywoman set down her mending. She looked at him then, fully, in the moonlight, and her face was the face from the lamplight again—surprise, sorrow, the closing of a door.

"I'll tell you one thing," she said. "One, because you asked tired, and tired men shouldn't carry heavy things, but you'll carry this one whether I give it to you or not, so better I hand it to you gently." She looked out at the water. "The bottle I wouldn't take down tonight. I know what's in it. I've known for eleven years, since it came up the river into my nets, and I have not opened it, and I have not sold it, and I have not poured it back, though I have stood at this dock a hundred nights meaning to."

"What's in it?" Casper asked, and his voice came out strange, thin, a boy's voice.

"A memory," the ferrywoman said. "Bottled the hour it was lost. The river brought it to me, and the river does not bring things to the wrong places." She turned and looked at him, and her old eyes were wet in the moonlight, and her voice, when it came, was gentle as the water below them. "It's a woman's memory, ranger. A young mother, in a house at the edge of a village, hearing riders come at a hour when no riders should come. And the last thing in it—the very last thing, before the bottle seals—is her voice, calling a name. Calling it the way you call a child in from the dark."

The dock swayed. The river murmured. Casper sat very still, and the five years stood up inside him, all of them at once, the whole measured distance of them, every mile he had ever counted.

"The name," he said.

"No," said the ferrywoman. "Not tonight. Tonight you sleep, because tomorrow you go upstream, and upstream is where your questions live, all of them, and they are heavier than the one I'm carrying for you." She took up her mending again, and her voice went mild and ordinary, a woman at a window. "But I'll tell you the thing I saw in your face, since you'll only lie awake wondering. It wasn't your grandmother's chin, boy. It was your mother's face. She came through here once, years ago, with a map she wouldn't show anyone, asking after a well that remembers. And I knew then, and I know now, and I said nothing then either, because the river gives back when it chooses, and not when we do."

She did not look at him again. Casper walked back along the waterline in the dark, past the drowned door, past the painted wall, and the words on it seemed to move in the lantern light—*the river takes nothing it does not give back*—and he climbed the stairs to the room where Ilsa slept with her back to the door and her hand, even in sleep, near her knife, and he lay down on the pallet and did not sleep at all.

Near dawn, the compass in his pocket—he had not shown it to Ilsa, had not shown it to anyone, a ranger's keepsake he had carried five years without knowing why—grew warm against his chest. He took it out. The needle was not pointing north. It was pointing upstream, steady and sure, into the highlands where the river climbed backward toward its source.

And beneath the floor, beneath the water, in the drowned rooms of the ferrywoman's house, the voices were murmuring again—and this time, clear through the green dark, unmistakable, a woman's voice was calling a name.

His name.
