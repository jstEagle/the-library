## 7. What the Ferrywoman Knew

The ferrywoman's name, if she had one, was not offered. She had told them to come at dusk, when the water in Merrow's Landing ran quietest, and so Casper and Ilsa came down through the drowned streets as the sun went the color of old copper, wading the last stretch where the road gave way to river.

She was waiting at the landing itself—a stone shelf worn smooth by a century of boots and hulls—beside a flat-bottomed boat that looked older than the village around it. She was small, wrapped in an oilskin coat gone gray with salt, her hair bound up in a cloth that might once have been white. Her hands were the remarkable thing: broad-knuckled, scarred at every finger joint, the hands of someone who had spent sixty years hauling rope against current. They were perfectly still on the gunwale, though the water beneath the boat moved without pause.

"You brought him," she said to Ilsa. Not a question. Her voice was low and dry, like wind over shale.

"I brought him," Ilsa said. "Whether that was wise is still under review."

The old woman made a sound that might have been laughter in a younger throat. Then she turned her eyes on Casper, and he felt the look of them go through him like a fish-spear through shallow water—clean, unhurried, finding the bottom.

"Sit," she said, nodding at the boat's thwart. "Both of you. What I have to say doesn't keep well above water."

Casper did not sit. Five years of tracking a thief had taught him that the moments before bad news arrived had a texture all their own—the way the air went flat, the way his own pulse became audible. He knew this texture. He had learned it standing over the burned ruin of a border homestead, reading ash for answers it would never give.

"Say it standing," he said.

The ferrywoman studied him a moment longer, then shrugged, a small motion inside the big coat. "Suit yourself. It'll hurt more upright."

* * *

She began, as he would later realize all good liars and truth-tellers do, somewhere other than where he expected.

"Do you know what I do here?" she asked. "In Merrow's Landing."

"Ferry," Casper said. "And barter bottled memories. We saw the market."

"Bottled memories." She said the words the way a priest says a heresy. "That's what we sell the travelers who pass through. Little vials of echo-salt steeped in riverwater. A first kiss. A mother's lullaby. The smell of a house you grew up in. Cheap things. Kind things. The kind of memory a person can lose and survive losing." She lifted one hand from the gunwale and turned it over, palm up, showing him the deep calluses. "I've been poling this crossing for fifty-one years. Do you know what I remember, ranger? Everything. Every face I ever carried. Every word spoken in my boat. That is not a gift. That is what happens when you live your whole life on water that runs from the Well."

Ilsa shifted beside him. "You're saying the river carries it. Memory. All the way downstream."

"The river remembers everything it has ever touched," the ferrywoman said. "Everything the Well has ever held, everything it has ever given back or taken away—it all moves through this valley in the water. Why do you think the tide came wrong, ranger? Why do you think the sea reached into caves that have been dry since before your grandmother's grandmother? Something upstream has been pulling. Drinking harder than it ever drank before. And the river is rising to meet its master."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. "You didn't bring us here to talk about hydrology."

"No." She looked at him again, and this time she did not look away, and he understood that everything before had been courtesy. This was the thing itself. "I brought you here because I poled a boat, eleven years ago, carrying three people upstream who should never have gone. A man, a woman, and a girl of about nine years old who kept asking her brother when they'd be home."

The world did not tilt. That was the strange part. Casper had always imagined that catastrophic news would arrive with some physical drama—the ground opening, the sky falling. Instead the evening simply continued. A bird called somewhere in the drowned orchard. The water slapped the hull. His own body stood there, breathing, while something behind his ribs quietly came apart.

"My father," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Mapped the highlands. Surveyed the river approaches. It was official work. Territorial survey."

"It was," the ferrywoman agreed. "And then it wasn't. Because your father was very good at his work, and what he found at the end of it was the approach to the Well of Return. Not the legend. The actual thing. The glacier arch, the pool, the seal upon it. He mapped it, ranger. He drew the only true map of how to reach it that has existed in four hundred years." She paused. "And then he tried to give that map away. To the rangers' command. To anyone who would take it out of Corvane's reach."

"And Corvane found out."

"Corvane found out." The old woman's voice did not soften. She seemed to believe kindness, in this particular matter, would be a form of insult. "There was no border raid, Casper Quintana. There was no raiding party, no crossers, no fight at your family's door. There was a fire set by men under the Magistrate's seal, timed to burn hot and fast and leave exactly the evidence a grieving brother would expect. You were meant to find ash and grief and nothing else. You were meant to stop looking."

"But I didn't stop looking."

"No. That was the error in their design. Or the flaw in you—they never decided which." She leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight from the village caught the deep lines of her face. "So Corvane adapted. If you wouldn't stop looking, he would make sure you looked in the wrong direction forever. He needed a thief. Someone plausible, skilled, worth five years of a ranger's obsession. Someone who could be seen stealing across half the territory, always just ahead of you, always leading you away from the river, away from the highlands, away from the truth."

Casper turned his head slowly and looked at Ilsa.

She met his eyes. She did not flinch, did not look at the water, did not perform innocence or guilt. She simply stood there in the failing light with her jaw set, and said, "He told me the night we met in the cave. About the echo-salt. About the orders. He knows now."

"He knows some," the ferrywoman said. "Does he know the rest? Does he know that your first assignment—Ilsa Vey, tell him or I will—was not to steal anything at all?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Casper counted them the way he counted everything, in the old units, miles and markers, and hated himself for it even as he did it.

"My first assignment," Ilsa said, and her voice was steady in the way voices are steady when steadiness is costing everything, "was to let myself be seen. Stealing. From a garrison storehouse near Saltmere. By a young ranger named Casper Quintana. The report said he was recently bereaved, exceptionally persistent, and emotionally compromised. I was told to become the thing he couldn't stop chasing." She swallowed. "I was nineteen years old. I didn't know what the chase was for. I thought I was covering a smuggling route. It was two years before I understood I was the bait, and by then—" She stopped. Started again. "By then he knew my habits better than anyone alive. And I knew his."

The words landed in Casper like stones dropped down a well, each one taking a long time to reach bottom.

Five years. Five years of dry wells and cold camps and the small fierce joy of closing a gap, of reading her trail like scripture, of knowing she took her water at dawn and favored high ground and left no sign but the sign she wanted left. Five years of a purpose so complete it had replaced the grief it was built on top of, the way a lid replaces a wound. And none of it had been real. Not the pursuit. Not the quarry. Not the man he'd believed himself to be—the tireless, incorruptible hunter. He had been a dog on a leash so long he'd mistaken it for a horizon.

"They stole them twice," he heard himself say. It came out almost conversationally, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "My family. First they took them. Then they took my grieving. Made me spend it on her instead." He nodded at Ilsa, and saw her take the nod like a blow. "Five years I could have spent learning how to carry it. Instead I spent it learning your boot size."

"Casper—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, not hard, just final. "Not tonight. Tonight I need to hear the rest of it." He turned back to the ferrywoman. "My father's map. Where is it?"

* * *

"Burned," the ferrywoman said. "Mostly. Your father was clever—he split the knowledge. Put half in the map and half in a thing no one would think to confiscate." She reached inside the oilskin coat, and Casper's hand went to his knife before his mind caught up, and the old woman saw it and smiled without warmth. "Easy, ranger. If I'd wanted you dead, I'd have let the river do it years ago."

She drew out a bundle wrapped in waxed canvas, tied with cord gone soft with age, and held it a moment before offering it—as if weighing whether the thing in her hands were a gift or a sentence.

"Your father stopped here, the night before they took him. Him and your mother and the girl. I poled them upriver myself, because he asked, and because I owed him—he'd pulled my son out of a flood once, years before. He sat right where you're standing and he told me what he'd found and what it would cost. He said, *If anything happens to us, there's a woman on this crossing who keeps everything. Give her the compass.*" The ferrywoman's still hands finally moved, untying the cord. "Eleven years I've kept it. Eleven years I've watched the flats for a young man with his stubbornness and his walk, because he told me his son would come someday, chasing something he couldn't name. He said, *He won't know why he's coming. But he'll come.*"

She opened the canvas.

Inside lay a compass. Brass-cased, dented at the bezel, the glass scratched nearly opaque. It was a ranger's instrument of the old pattern, the kind issued before the territory standardized, and it hung from a leather cord darkened by decades of neck-sweat. Casper knew it instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. His father had carried it on every survey. As a boy Casper had been allowed to hold it exactly once, for the length of a meal, on the strict condition that he not breathe on it.

"Take it," the ferrywoman said.

He took it. It was lighter than he remembered, or heavier; his hands had stopped reporting accurately. The brass was cold. In the scratched glass he could see his own face bent and doubled, and behind it, faintly, the lamplight of Merrow's Landing burning like a distant fire.

"It's broken," Ilsa said gently, leaning in. "The needle's not settling."

"It's not broken," the ferrywoman said. "Hold it level and watch."

Casper held it level. The needle swung once, twice, in slow wide arcs—and then, instead of settling toward any north at all, it stopped at an angle that pointed neither along the shore nor across the water but *up*, tilting against the glass as though trying to climb out of the case, straining toward the darkening heights upstream.

"What does it point at?" Ilsa asked.

"Not at," the ferrywoman said. "*Toward.* Your father reworked it, ranger, in the months before the end. He took the lodestone out and replaced it with a sliver of echo-salt cut from the Well's own seal—the only piece ever pried loose and lived to tell of it. It doesn't point north. Nothing up there is north. It points at memory. At the greatest concentration of lost things in the world." She tapped the glass once with a scarred finger. "Right now it's pointing at everything you've lost. And everything Corvane has stolen. And everything the river is about to give back, whether the Magistrate wills it or not."

Casper stared at the needle straining upward into the dusk, trembling with a fine constant vibration, like a living thing scenting home.

"Why give it to me now?" he asked. "Eleven years. Why wait?"

"Because until three days ago, giving it to you would have been a death sentence for us both," the ferrywoman said. "Corvane's riders watch every road into the highlands, and a man carrying that compass is a man carrying the map your father died for. But the tide changed the rules. The river's pulling. The Well is waking. And Corvane is drinking so hard and so fast that even his own enforcers are starting to remember things he paid them to forget." She glanced at Ilsa. "Ask her what Brannoc did at the toll bridge two seasons back. Ask her why the Magistrate's own enforcer suddenly can't raise his whip hand to children." She turned back to Casper. "The design is cracking, ranger. Yours most of all. Which means the time for keeping things safe is over. The time for spending them has begun."

Casper closed his hand around the compass. The brass warmed against his palm, or he imagined it did, and the needle went on straining upward, patient as gravity, certain as grief.

"My sister," he said. "The girl in the boat. Did she—"

"I don't know," the ferrywoman said, and for the first time her voice roughened, and he believed it was the hardest sentence she'd spoken all evening. "They went up past the falls and I never poled that high again. But I'll tell you what I do know, and you can carry it or throw it in the river. Your father didn't map the Well to use it. He mapped it because he believed no one should hold a power that big alone—not a magistrate, not a king, not a god. He believed the map should exist so the thing could be *watched*. And if his son is anything like him, then somewhere in that stubborn skull of yours is the same thought, waiting for its chance."

She picked up her pole then, plainly done, and set the boat's nose toward the middle channel where the backward-running water hissed over the shallows.

"Go upstream," she said. "Follow the needle. And when you get to wherever it's taking you—do what your father would have done. Not what Corvane will do. Not what the Well offers. What *he* would have done."

* * *

They climbed back up through the drowned streets in full dark, the lanterns of Merrow's Landing bobbing in windows at eye level and below it, the whole village lit like a ship at night. Ilsa walked a pace behind him, and Casper knew it was deliberate—giving him room, or watching his back, or both, and he found he could no longer tell the difference between her guilt and her care, and suspected she couldn't either.

At the top of the rise, where the village gave way to the canyon trail, she finally spoke.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the first year was an assignment. The second year was duty. Years three through five—" She stopped. Tried again. "Years three through five, I was running because running was the only way I knew how to stay near the one honest thing in my life. Even if the honesty was a lie."

Casper stood with the compass in his fist, the needle pulling at him like a second heartbeat, pointing up the black throat of the canyon toward everything that had been taken.

"I can't do this tonight," he said.

"I know."

"But tomorrow," he said, "you're going to tell me everything else she didn't. Every order. Every report you sent. Every year. And Ilsa—" he turned, and the needle-light of the compass caught nothing, but she stepped back anyway, "—if I find out you've been holding one more piece of this to protect me, our alliance ends at the next fork in the river."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and turned up the trail.

Casper followed. Behind them the river ran backward in the dark, whispering over stones it had already passed, carrying everything it remembered toward the sea—and ahead, far up where the snowfields began, the compass strained against its casing like a thing alive, pointing the way to the place where his family had gone and not returned, where a magistrate hoarded the dead, and where, if the ferrywoman was right, the whole stolen design of his life had been built to end.

He walked. For the first time in five years, he was not following a thief.

He was following his father.
