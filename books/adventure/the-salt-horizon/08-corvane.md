## 8. Corvane

The citadel of Vhal Maren stood on a spur of black granite above the salt flats, and from its highest window the Magistrate Corvane could watch the whole white world lie still beneath him. He liked it best at dusk, when the flats went the color of old bone and the horizon dissolved into a smear of heat and light, because it was then that the land most resembled the inside of a memory—featureless, preserved, waiting to be read.

He was reading one now.

The vial sat in its cradle of leaded glass on the writing desk, no longer than his forefinger, filled with a liquid that caught the last sun and held it. It had no label. None of them did anymore. Labels were for men who needed reminding, and Corvane had drunk this particular memory so many times that he could have found it blindfolded among ten thousand others, by weight alone.

*Elena, in the garden at Merrow's Landing, before the water took the lower terraces.*

He uncorked it with a small silver key he wore on a chain against his sternum, and poured.

The distillate moved like oil and smelled like rain on hot stone—the smell of her garden, though there had been no rain in the territory for eleven years. He drank it in a single swallow, as one takes medicine or poison, and let it carry him down.

---

She was kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, transplanting something green and stubborn, and she did not hear him come up the path because she never did; she lived inside her work the way he lived inside his ledgers. Her hair had come loose from its braid and stuck to the damp of her neck. The memory was an early one—one of the first he'd ever distilled, back when the process was crude and the vials held only fragments—and so it flickered slightly at the edges, like a candle guttering. But her hands were perfect. Her hands were always perfect.

"You're standing in my light," she said, without turning around.

"There is no light," he said, because he had learned the memory's script long ago and knew his own lines. "It's evening."

"Then you're standing in what's left of it." She looked up at him then, and smiled, and the smile was the thing he paid for, night after night, year after year. "Sit down, Aldous. If you're going to haunt me, at least do it at eye level."

Her voice said *Aldous*. No one living called him Aldous. No one living called him anything but Magistrate, or worse things behind doors, in the register people believed he couldn't hear. In eleven years of rule he had collected every whisper, every denunciation, every trembling confession extracted in his name, and not one of them—not a single soul in the territory—knew that his name was Aldous Corvane, and that a woman with dirt under her fingernails had once used it like a hand laid flat against his chest.

He knelt in the garden beside her. The memory obliged him, as memories do, rearranging itself around his presence, giving him a body in the scene, a shadow on the flagstones. This was the refinement he had paid the echo-thieves fortunes to achieve: not merely to watch the past, but to stand in it. To be a man again, briefly, in a world where he had been loved.

"How long will you keep doing this?" she asked him.

And here the memory faltered, because she had never asked that question—not in life, not in this garden, not on any day the original recording contained. But the distillate had been drunk so many hundreds of times that it had begun to erode, the way water erodes stone, and new channels had worn themselves into it, and through those channels came words she had said elsewhere, other evenings, other arguments, spliced together now into a question she had never asked but which was, somehow, more true than anything she'd actually said.

"As long as I need to," he answered.

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

She turned back to her planting. And because the erosion ran deeper than either of them could stop, she said—her mouth shaping syllables drawn from some other hour entirely, some door slammed in another year—"You're keeping me in a jar, Aldous. That isn't love. That's taxidermy."

The memory ended. They always ended too soon now, fraying at the close, and he came up out of the chair gasping as if surfacing from deep water, his heart slamming, his eyes wet, his hands cold on the arms of the seat.

Outside the window, the flats had gone fully dark. Somewhere below, in the archive levels carved into the mountain's roots, ten thousand vials gleamed in their racks like a harvest of swallowed stars.

Corvane sat for a long time, breathing.

Then he rang the bell that summoned Brannoc.

---

Brannoc did not knock. Brannoc had stopped knocking years ago, when it became clear that the Magistrate preferred his enforcer the way he preferred his memories: instantly available, requiring nothing of him, asking no questions about where he'd been. The man filled the doorway like weather—a head taller than anyone else in the citadel, gray at the temples, with a face that had been handsome once in a blunt, soldierly way and was now mostly scar tissue and patience.

"Magistrate."

"She's failed." Corvane rose and walked to the map table, where the territory lay rendered in brass and enamel, the salt flats a sheet of polished white metal that seemed, in the lamplight, to breathe. "Vey. Three years of service, three years of flawless extraction, and now she's gone dark at the exact moment the tide opened the cave. You understand what that means."

"I'd rather hear you say it."

Corvane permitted himself the ghost of a smile. Brannoc was the only man alive who spoke to him that way, and he permitted it for reasons he examined rarely and never enjoyed examining: because Brannoc had known Elena too, had carried her coffin, had wept at the graveside with a grief so plain and unengineered that Corvane had envied it with a violence that frightened even him.

"It means," Corvane said, "that she has remembered why she serves me. Or rather—that she has remembered what I hold over her, and has decided to test whether I'll spend it." He traced a line across the white metal with one finger, from the flats toward the drowned canyons upstream. "She thinks the river will hide her. She thinks distance is a currency she can pay me in. She's wrong, but she's not stupid, and stupid would be easier."

"And the ranger?"

"Quintana." The name left Corvane's mouth with a strange taste, part satisfaction, part something older and less comfortable. "Five years I've kept that man running. Five years, and he never once questioned his luck—that a thief should always be one valley ahead, one day behind, close enough to wound his pride and far enough to keep hope alive. Do you know how much engineering that requires? How many informants paid, how many trails salted?" He laughed, softly, without warmth. "Grief is the finest leash ever devised, Brannoc. Better than gold. Better than fear. A man who grieves will run forever if you tell him the thing he lost is just ahead."

"He's reached the cave."

"He's reached the cave." Corvane's finger stopped on the map, on the place where the underground river surfaced, far upstream, past canyons no surveyor had returned from. "Which means he's two weeks from the Well. Perhaps less, if Vey is guiding him, and she will be—she knows the route better than my own cartographers, because I made certain she learned it. Every piece on the board moves where I placed it." He straightened. "Except that pieces have begun to want things. That's the flaw in every design. Eventually the pawns remember they have hands."

"What are your orders?"

Corvane walked to the cabinet against the far wall. It was iron-banded, locked with three keys, and inside it, in velvet slots, rested perhaps forty vials—the working stock, the ones he drew from nightly. He selected one, held it to the lamp, and watched the light swim in it.

"This one," he said, "is a mother's memory of teaching her son to swim, in the old reservoir at Dunmark, before the drought. I took it from her personally, four years ago. She begged. Not for herself—for the boy, that he might keep it. I gave her a counterfeit in exchange, a blank with a little salt-shimmer in it, and she thanked me." He set the vial down without drinking. "Do you know why I'm telling you this?"

"No, Magistrate."

"Because tomorrow you're going to ride into country where people have begun to remember what I am, and I want you to understand that I have never once lied to myself about it. I am not a reformer awaiting the right moment. I am not a steward holding power in trust. I am a man who steals the dead out of the mouths of the living, and I intend to go on doing it until the Well gives me the only vial I have ever truly wanted." His voice did not rise. It never rose. That was the thing the territory had learned to fear—not the volume of him but the terrible economy of it, every word weighed and paid for. "Retrieve Vey. Alive. She is the finest thief I have ever owned and I do not discard tools that cut. As for Quintana—"

He paused. Something moved behind his face, brief as a fish under ice.

"—kill him cleanly, if you can. He has earned that much. He has spent five years being useful to me without knowing it, and there is a debt in that, and I pay my debts." He turned back to the window, to the dark flats. "But if it comes to a choice between the Well secured and the ranger breathing, choose the Well. There is nothing down that river that matters more than what waits at the end of it."

"And if Vey refuses to come?"

Corvane was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—lower, almost gentle, which was worse.

"Then remind her of her brother. Remind her that his freedom exists on paper I wrote, in ink I mixed, and that paper burns very easily." He picked up the swimming-lesson vial again, turned it once in his fingers, and put it away. "Go at first light. Take six men and the fast boats. And Brannoc—"

"Magistrate."

"If you find yourself hesitating—if you find some stray mercy growing in you like a weed—come to me before you act on it. I can prune it. I have the means." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which made it terrible. "That is not a threat. It is an offer. It is the kindest thing I know how to say."

Brannoc bowed, exactly as deeply as protocol required and not a hair further, and left.

---

Alone, Corvane did not sleep. Sleep had become a negotiation he lost more often than he won; the dreams were full of unlabeled vials, and in them Elena's face kept resolving into the faces of strangers whose memories he had drunk, until he woke unable to say whether he had ever loved a woman or only consumed the idea of one, the way a fire consumes a portrait—faithfully, thoroughly, and leaving nothing but the shape of the burning.

Instead he descended.

The archive levels were cold by design; the distillates kept better in the cold, and besides, he liked the chill. It felt honest. Torchlight ran along rows of racked glass descending into the mountain's roots, level after level, each rack holding hundreds of vials, each vial a stolen evening, a stolen voice, a stolen ordinary Tuesday that some farmer or ferryman or child had believed was theirs alone. He walked the aisles slowly, trailing his fingers along the racks the way other men touched rosaries.

Here: a grandmother singing in a language the territory had outlawed. Here: a soldier's last letter home, undelivered, drunk instead by the man who signed his death warrant. Here, in a case apart, behind glass, guarded by its own lock—the family section, the private collection, the ones he did not drink because drinking them would be surrender and he was not ready to surrender.

Quintana's father, mapping the river approach in a boat by lantern-light, narrating his bearings aloud in the careful voice of a careful man. Quintana's sister, laughing at something off-frame. Quintana's mother—

He stopped at that rack. He did not take the vial down. He had taken it down twice in five years, and both times he had stood in a kitchen that was not his, watching a woman who was not Elena roll dough and hum, and the wrongness of it had been so acute, so physical, that he had vomited afterward into the archive's drain and sworn never again.

And yet he kept them. All of them. The whole family, bottled and shelved like preserves against a winter that would never come.

*Because they're collateral,* he told himself, as he always told himself. *Because the father mapped the approach and the son must never learn of it, and dead men's memories are the surest lock ever forged.* It was even true. It simply was not the whole truth, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone: that somewhere in the dark of him, alongside the love and the grief and the eleven years of disciplined ruin, lived a collector's appetite that had outgrown its object entirely. He no longer wanted Elena back. He wanted *everything*, because everything was the only quantity large enough to fill the space she'd left, and the space grew yearly, the way a wound grows in a starving body.

At the bottom of the lowest level, behind the last rack, a ladder led down to a sealed door, and beyond the door, a shaft that the surveyors swore connected to fissures running all the way upstream—to the drowned canyons, to the backward-running river, to the source. On quiet nights, if he pressed his ear to the iron, Corvane could hear it: a sound like breathing, like a tide very far away, like the territory remembering something in its sleep.

Soon, he thought. Two weeks. Perhaps less.

He climbed back up through his kingdom of stolen hours, and at the top, before dawn, he allowed himself one more vial—the garden, the dirt, the loose braid, the smile. The erosion had spread. This time, in the middle of it, Elena looked directly at him—out of him, out of some stratum of himself he hadn't known he'd poured into the glass—and said, in a voice assembled from a hundred broken fragments:

*"What will be left of you, when you've drunk everything?"*

The memory ended. Corvane sat in the dark with the empty vial in his hand, and outside, far to the south, the first light found the salt flats and turned them silver, all the way to the horizon, all the way to the place where a ranger and a thief were already moving upstream.

He rang for his steward.

"Ready the boats," he said. "All of them."
