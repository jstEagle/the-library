## 9. Rapids of the Forgotten

The river ran backward, and by the third day Casper had stopped arguing with it.

They'd launched at dawn from a shelf of black stone where the underground water surfaced—a narrow, cold vein that should have been flowing toward the flats and instead climbed stubbornly inland, threading through canyons so deep the sky was a rumor. The current pulled them along in a battered skiff Ilsa had stolen from Merrow's Landing with the ferrywoman's grudging blessing, which was to say: the old woman had watched them take it and said nothing, only touched two fingers to her lips and then to the gunwale, some blessing or farewell that Casper chose not to ask about.

He sat in the stern with the oars mostly for show. The river did the work. It always had, he was beginning to understand—this whole valley did its work in secret, patient as salt.

Behind them, faint but persistent as a toothache, came the sound of Brannoc's boats.

"You hear that?" Ilsa asked anyway, though she knew he did. She crouched in the bow like a figurehead carved by someone in a hurry, her dark hair tied back with a strip of sailcloth, one hand trailing in the water as if she could read the river's mood through her fingertips.

"Two engines," Casper said. "Maybe three. They're not hurrying."

"They don't have to." She turned her head just enough that he caught her profile against the gray light falling down the canyon walls. "Brannoc knows this river. He's run prisoners up it before. That's what the boats are for—the archive doesn't send carts into these canyons. It sends boats, because everything the river takes is a memory they don't have to buy."

Casper rowed anyway. Not because it helped, but because his hands needed something honest to do.

They passed under an arch of stone hung with pale curtains of salt, and the sound behind them grew no closer and no farther away, keeping pace like a debt.

* * *

By midday the canyon narrowed until the walls leaned close enough that he could have touched both sides with the oars. The water quickened. Somewhere ahead, low and continuous, came a sound he recognized from ranger training years ago—the sound every recruit is taught to fear more than gunfire, because gunfire ends quickly and water does not.

"Rapids," Ilsa said.

"I heard."

"You're gripping the oars like they're the last true thing in the world."

"They might be."

She almost smiled. In five years of watching her across distances measured in rifle ranges and mirages, Casper had catalogued perhaps nine of her smiles, and most of those had been aimed at him over something she'd just stolen. This one was different. Smaller. Aimed at nothing, or at both of them.

"The tolls start here," she said, and the smile went away. "I should have told you last night. I'm telling you now because—" She stopped, jaw working. Honesty, Casper had learned, cost her visible effort, like lifting something heavier than it looked. "Because you deserve to know what this river charges before it takes it."

"Tolls."

"Echo-salt." She nodded at the canyon walls, and he saw it then, really saw it: the white crust thickening on the stone, growing denser as they moved upstream, glittering in the thin light like frost that had never known warmth. The same substance she'd been stealing all those years, shard by shard, for a master she wouldn't name. "It grows thickest where the river runs fastest. And when you pass through it—when the spray touches you—it takes. One memory. One, no more, per crossing. That's the toll. Nobody knows how it chooses."

"That's a story for children."

"It's a story the ferrywoman told me with a straight face while handing me a boat," Ilsa said. "And Brannoc's men will be running these rapids right behind us, and every one of them will pay it too. So there's that."

Casper looked back downriver. The sound of engines echoed off the stone, distorted, directionless. "How fast?"

"Faster than we want."

He shipped the oars and took the steering sweep. "Then we go through clean and count our fingers after."

"That's not how it works," Ilsa said quietly. "You don't get to choose what it takes. That's the whole cruelty of it. You pass through, and later you notice the hole."

* * *

The first rapid hit them like a decision made by someone else.

The river dropped, gathered itself, and threw them forward between walls of salted stone, and the world became noise and cold and the white flash of spray. Casper had run whitewater twice in his life, both times on the border rivers in flood season, and neither time had felt like this—like being handled, like cargo. The skiff spun, straightened, spun again. Ilsa shouted something he couldn't parse over the roar and pointed left, hard, and he threw his weight on the sweep and the boat obeyed just enough to miss a fang of rock that would have opened their hull like a letter.

Then the spray came down on them—not water alone. Water laced with salt-fine glitter, a mist that tasted of copper and, absurdly, of someone else's childhood: a half-second of a smell, bread baking in a kitchen he had never stood in, gone before he could name it.

The rapid ended as abruptly as it began. They shot out into a long pool where the canyon widened and the water went glassy and thoughtful, and for ten heartbeats neither of them spoke.

"I still have everything," Casper said finally.

"So do I." But Ilsa's voice had changed. She was staring at her own hands, turning them over, as if checking whether they belonged to her. "That's wrong. We should feel lighter. Everyone says you feel lighter."

"Everyone who says that is selling something."

"No." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wide and dark and genuinely afraid, which on Ilsa Vey was rarer than rain. "No, Casper. We haven't paid yet. The toll comes when it comes. The salt marks you going through. It collects on the way out."

As if the river had heard her, the current picked up again.

* * *

There were four rapids before the gorge proper. They learned the rhythm of them: the drop, the roar, the blind white chaos, the sudden pools of silence where they counted themselves like survivors of a battle. Between the second and third, Ilsa found a ledge and they dragged the skiff up to rest and eat dried fish and drink from a skin of water that tasted faintly of stone.

It was during that meal that Casper noticed the first hole.

Not in himself. In her.

She'd been talking about Merrow's Landing, about the ferrywoman, about the way the villagers bartered bottled memories like coin—and mid-sentence she stopped. Her mouth stayed open. Her brow creased, slow, like frost forming on a window.

"What?" he said.

"I was saying something." She pressed her fingers to her temple. "About the Landing. There was—I had a reason for telling you that. There was a thing I was about to say." She stared at the river. "It's gone. Not slipped. Gone. There's a shape where it was, like a word on the tip of your tongue, except the tongue's gone too."

"The second rapid," Casper said. "The spray hit us hardest on the left side. You were sitting left."

She didn't answer. She was running through herself the way a merchant counts stock after a robbery, and he watched her do it, watched her face move through inventory and arrive, somewhere near the end, at a loss she couldn't locate and therefore couldn't grieve properly—which he understood, suddenly and completely, was its own kind of horror. To lose something and not know what. To feel the absence without the outline.

"I had a brother," she said at last, very evenly. "I know I had a brother. I remember selling him out—that's the ugly part, I remember that fine, Corvane made sure I remembered that part because it kept me obedient. But his name—" Her breath caught. "His name was—"

Nothing came.

"Casper." Her voice cracked down the middle. "His name was—"

"Ilsa." He reached across the skiff and took her wrist. Her pulse was hammering. "Look at me. It's one name. Names come back. People write things down. When we're clear of the salt, we'll find someone who knew him, and—"

"You don't understand." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard from her, worse than any threat she'd made across five years of flats. "I traded everything for him. My freedom, my face, five years of being the Magistrate's little ghost—and the one thing I kept, the one thing I hid so deep even Corvane couldn't distill it out of me, was his name. Because as long as I had his name I could find him again. As long as I had his name, it wasn't all wasted." She looked at her hands. "The river found it anyway. The river reached into the deepest room in my house and took the one thing on the table."

Casper held her wrist until her breathing slowed. He didn't say it gets better. He didn't say anything. Some griefs you honor with silence, and this was one, and she seemed to know it, because after a while she squeezed his hand once—hard, brief, almost angry—and let go.

"We keep moving," she said. "If we stop, Brannoc pays the same tolls and catches us besides."

* * *

The third rapid nearly killed them. The fourth did worse.

It happened the way she'd described: no pain, no moment of taking. Just the drop, the roar, the salt-mist hanging in the air like breath in winter, and then the long pool after, and Casper sitting in the stern doing his silent arithmetic—oars, boots, compass, knife, Ilsa—when he reached for the thing he reached for every night of five years, the sound he kept folded in the pocket behind his sternum like a letter too precious to read often:

His mother's voice.

It wasn't there.

He knew she'd had a voice. He knew facts about it the way you know facts about a country you've never visited: she'd sung while cooking; she'd called his name in a particular rising way when he'd been out past dark; she'd whispered prayers over his sister's fever. He knew all of it the way a man knows the plot of a book he read once. But the sound itself—the grain of it, the warmth or roughness of it, the exact music of *Casper, mijo, come inside*—

Gone. A door in a house he'd lived in all his life, and behind it, nothing.

He must have made a sound, because Ilsa turned. Whatever she saw in his face, she recognized it instantly. Recognition passed between them like a lamp lit at a window.

"Yours too," she said.

"She sang," he said. His own voice sounded foreign. "I can't— I know she sang. I can't hear it."

Ilsa crawled aft through the rocking skiff and put her arms around him, and it was awkward and graceless and smelled of salt and sweat and river, and he let her, because there was no one else in the world left to hold the shape of what he'd lost. Five years he'd carried that voice through every mile of the flats, telling himself the chase was for justice, for duty, for the territory—but underneath, in the honest dark, he'd known it was so he'd have a reason to stay upright, a reason stitched to the memory of why anything mattered. And the river had taken it in four seconds of spray, casually, the way a customs officer takes a knife.

"I'm sorry," she said into his shoulder. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry."

"It's a toll," he managed. "You said. It's just a toll."

"It's not just anything." She pulled back and gripped his face in both hands, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "Listen to me. The salt took the sound. It did not take her. It did not take that she loved you, or that you loved her, or that she sang—you know she sang, you *know*, and knowing is yours, and it can't reach that. Do you hear me? It can take the music. It cannot take that there was music."

He nodded, because words weren't available.

Behind them, distant now but coming, the engines.

* * *

They ran the gorge in the last light.

The canyon walls closed overhead until the sky was a jagged ribbon of violet, and the echo-salt blazed on the stone around them with a faint phosphorescence, millions of crystals drinking the last photons and giving back a pale sea-light, so that they shot through the dark between glowing walls like a spark through a fuse. The rapids came one after another now, merged into a single sustained violence, and Casper steered by instinct and Ilsa's shouts and the strange luminous glow of the salt itself, and somewhere in the middle of it he began, horribly, to laugh—a short, cracked sound torn out of him by a wave—and heard Ilsa laughing too, high and wild, both of them laughing because the alternative was screaming and laughter at least kept the hands steady.

They shot out of the gorge mouth into open water under the first stars, and the river went calm as a held breath, and the glow fell away behind them, and the silence that followed was enormous.

They drifted. Neither rowed. The current still carried them upstream, gentle now, patient, toward whatever waited at the source.

After a long time, Ilsa spoke.

"When we were in the third rapid," she said, "you put the boat exactly where I would have put it. Before I called it."

"Luck."

"No. I've been chased by you for five years, Casper Quintana. I've watched you read ground, read weather, read me. You've spent five years learning my habits better than anyone alive—including me." She turned in the bow to face him. "Tonight I learned yours. And I keep thinking: Corvane built that chase. He built it to keep you running, to keep you away from the truth. Five years of his design. And somewhere inside his design, without either of us planning it, something got built that he didn't order." Her voice dropped. "Something real. He made a thief worth chasing, and he made a ranger worth fleeing, and the joke of it—the beautiful, terrible joke—is that what came out the other end is two people who run rapids like they've done it together all their lives."

Casper looked at her across the dark water. His mother's voice was gone. Her brother's name was gone. They were, both of them, poorer than they'd been that morning, and he could not remember the last time he had felt less alone.

"Get some sleep," he said, which was the closest he could come to answering. "I'll take first watch."

She lay back in the bow, one arm under her head, and within minutes her breathing slowed into something unguarded—he'd never once heard her sleep in five years; thieves sleep in shifts, and she'd been running longer than that. He kept the sweep light in the current and watched the stars wheel slowly backward over the canyon rim, and he practiced, quietly, the thing Ilsa had told him: *she sang.* Holding the fact like a coal. Refusing to let the fact go cold.

Near midnight, far downstream, the engine sounds entered the gorge.

And then, faintly, carried up the black water on the river's backward breath, came screaming.

Not the scream of men hitting rapids. Something else—short, cut off, multiplied. Three voices, maybe four, each one ending too soon, the way a shout ends when the shouter remembers something so terrible he forgets to make noise.

Casper sat very still in the drifting skiff and listened to Brannoc's boats pay the toll, and understood that the enforcer had chosen to run the gorge at night, in the dark, with no light but the salt—chosen it, because stopping meant losing them.

The screaming faded. The engines did not.

Upstream, around the next bend, the river glowed faintly white, and the air had begun, very softly, to whisper.
