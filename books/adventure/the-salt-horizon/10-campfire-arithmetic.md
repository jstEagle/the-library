## 10. Campfire Arithmetic

The river gave them back to land the way a pickpocket returns a wallet—reluctantly, and lighter than before.

Casper hauled himself onto the gravel bar on hands and knees, coughing water that tasted of stone and old winters. Behind him, the rapids roared on through the canyon, indifferent as a jury. Somewhere upstream, the current had taken the last of Brannoc's torchlight, and somewhere along the run it had taken other things too—things he could feel as absences rather than remember losing, like teeth missing from a comb.

He counted what he had left. It was a ranger's habit, inventory as prayer: one knife. One waterskin, half full. One compass that pointed at memory instead of north. One companion, already standing at the bar's far edge, wringing out her hair with the brisk efficiency of someone who had nearly drowned before and refused to be impressed by it.

And somewhere in the last mile of white water, the sound of his mother's voice.

He knew it was gone the way you know a room has gone dark—not by seeing the darkness but by failing to see anything else. He tried to reach for it. *Casper, mijo, come inside, the wind's turning.* He could feel the shape of the sentence, the shape of the kitchen, the shape of her hand on his hair. But the voice itself—the particular grain of it, the way she said his name like a door opening—had been paid to the canyon, and the canyon did not give receipts.

"You lost something," Ilsa said. Not a question. She'd gone quiet after the rapids, quieter than he'd ever known her, and he understood now that she was carrying her own absence, walking carefully around it the way you walk around a bruise.

"So did you."

"Yes."

They didn't say what. Some arithmetic you do alone.

* * *

The gravel bar was a long gray tongue of land maybe forty paces end to end, wedged between the river's two mouths as the channel split around a drowned spur of canyon wall. High, dry-ish, defensible. The water on either side ran fast and cold and spoke in two voices, one on each ear, like a town gossiping about them.

Casper built the fire small. Driftwood, bleached pale as bone, and the last of the oilcloth from his pack to coax it. Ilsa watched him work and said nothing, and the silence between them had changed texture since the falls, since the archive, since she'd told him the truth with her eyes down and her hands open like a woman showing she carried no weapon. The silence used to be tactical. Now it was just silence, and it was almost—almost—comfortable.

He caught himself almost enjoying it and felt the old alarm go up in him like a flag. Comfort was a trap. Comfort was what you felt right before the border raid, right before the well went dry, right before every bad thing that had ever taught him to keep moving. Five years of chasing a thief had been five years of never sitting still long enough for the grief to catch him, and now here he was, stranded, firelit, sitting still, and the grief was walking up the gravel bar toward him at the speed of a man who knew he had all night.

He fed the fire another stick and said, "We should talk about tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Ilsa said, "is a rumor. We're alive tonight. That's the whole of the news."

"Tomorrow we follow the channel north until it climbs. The compass wants the highlands. Corvane wants us, and Brannoc's men will have found their boats by now, and—"

"Casper." She said his name the way his mother used to, and he flinched before he could stop himself, because it wasn't the same voice, couldn't be, the canyon had made sure of that, but it was close enough to hurt. She noticed the flinch. She was the kind of person who noticed everything; it was how she'd survived as a thief, and how she'd survived as a liar, and how, he suspected, she was surviving as a woman telling the truth for the first time in years. "Sit down. The river took our toll. Let's see what it left us."

He sat. The gravel was cold through his soaked clothes. Across the fire her face was all planes and shadows, and he thought, not for the first time, that he knew her habits better than anyone alive and had never once known her.

"Your brother," he said. "The one you sold out. Tell me."

* * *

She was quiet long enough that the fire had to be fed again before she spoke, and when she did, she spoke to the flames, which was easier than speaking to him.

"His name was—"

She stopped. Her hand went to her sternum, pressing, as if checking that something was still there.

"His name was—" she tried again, and this time she laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it. "It's gone. The canyon took it. I felt it go under the third rapid, like a fish slipping off a hook. I can see his face. I can see the room we slept in, the crack in the plaster over his bed, the way he bit his nails down to the quick. I can remember everything about him except what people called him."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry yet. Wait until you hear the whole sum." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, made herself small in a way that was nothing like the Ilsa Vey of the salt flats, the Ilsa Vey who could vanish into heat shimmer and reappear three miles ahead of him. "Corvane didn't take me. I want that understood. Everyone assumes the Magistrate's people took me the way they take everyone, with chains and threats. No. I walked into his citadel and offered. I was nineteen and my brother was fifteen and he'd gotten himself on the wrong side of a debt in Merrow's Landing, the kind of debt they collect in fingers. Corvane's clerk told me what the Magistrate paid for echo-thieves. Room, board, protection. And one favor, discretionary, at the Magistrate's pleasure."

"Your brother's debt."

"My brother's freedom. Corvane paid it himself. Signed the paper in front of me. I remember the wax seal going down like a small red moon. And I remember thinking: this is the easiest bargain anyone has ever offered me. Five years of running errands—stepping into old rooms and cutting echo-salt from the walls where the dead had shouted, where children had been born, where anyone had ever wept—and in exchange, Tam gets to live."

"Tam," Casper said quietly.

Her whole body went still. Then, slowly, she turned her head and looked at him across the fire, and her eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once, which was a combination he had never seen on a human face and hoped never to forget.

"Tam," she repeated, tasting it. "Tam. Yes. That's—" She pressed the back of her hand against her mouth for a moment. When she lowered it, she was composed again, mostly. "You gave that back to me. The canyon took it and you gave it back and I don't even know how to—"

"It's not a debt," Casper said. "I've had enough of debts."

"Everything's a debt. That's the whole lesson of the last five years." She looked back at the fire. "I served him four years. Corvane. I was good at it—better than good, he said. I could hear the salt, did you know that? Most thieves have to guess where the echoes are thickest. I could hear them. Rooms full of the dead, singing under the plaster. And every month I'd ask about my brother, and every month the clerk would say the Magistrate's pleasure is not yet concluded, and I was too afraid and too useful to press. And then last year they let me go home. Gave me three days, said it was a reward. I walked to the village where Tam was living. The neighbors were very kind. They fed me. They told me he'd taken sick in the spring fever, two years before. They buried him under the plum tree because he'd once said, as a joke, that plums were the only thing worth dying near."

The fire popped. On either side of the bar, the river ran on.

"I sat under that tree until dark," Ilsa said. "And then I walked back to the citadel and asked Corvane for an assignment, any assignment, the longer the better. And he gave me one. A long one. A ranger on the salt flats who had to be kept moving. Kept busy. Kept from asking questions about the border raid, about the drowned canyon, about anything north of the flats. He told me: make this man chase you, and don't let him catch you, and don't let him stop."

Casper looked at his hands. They were steady. He'd trained them to be steady through worse than this.

"I know," he said.

"You know what I did. You don't know what it was." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "Four years, Casper. Four years I learned your habits. You check your backtrail twice at dawn and never at dusk. You water your horse before yourself, always, even in the bad years. You whistle when you're closing in on me—three notes, flat—and you don't know you do it. I could have lost you permanently a dozen times. There were passes. There were boats. There was a whole coast, once, and I stood on the bluffs above it and watched you make camp below me, and I could have walked down to the water and been gone from your life forever, and I didn't, and do you know why? Not duty. Corvane's leash wasn't in my head, it was in my heart, and by the end it wasn't even that. It was that you were the only person in the world who was looking for me. Every day. Five years. Nobody had ever looked for me before. My own brother didn't look for me when I left—he was fifteen, he was glad of the money. But you. You crossed the salt in the dry season. You slept in your boots. You made me the center of your whole life, and God forgive me, I needed to be the center of somebody's."

The words landed in Casper like stones dropped down a well. He listened to them fall and fall and heard no bottom.

"And the worst of it," Ilsa went on, quieter now, "the part I can't get out from under, is that you were chasing me to avoid grieving, and I was letting you chase me to avoid grieving, and Corvane built the whole machine out of two broken people and called it strategy. He didn't even have to be clever. He just had to find the two loneliest people in his territory and aim them at each other."

* * *

The fire burned down to coals and Casper fed it again, because his hands wanted something to do and because the night off the water was colder than any night on the flats, the cold coming up out of the stone itself, old cold, canyon cold.

"I had a sister," he said.

Ilsa didn't move. She had the good sense not to.

"Marisol. She was older by two years, which she never let me forget and I never actually minded. My father mapped the high country—ranger work, before the river went bad—and my mother kept the accounts, and Marisol was going to take over the ferry at Merrow's Landing someday, except she was twelve and in no hurry. I remember—" He stopped. Tested the memory like ice on a pond, putting his weight down carefully. It held. "I remember the three of them arguing about whether the north channel would flood that year. My father said yes. My mother said he always said yes. Marisol said she hoped it flooded because she wanted to see the cave. The Cave of Admission. She'd heard the legend from the ferrywoman. She wanted to hear the dead sing."

He looked up. "I wasn't there when they died. That's the thing I've never told anyone, not the rangers, not the reports. I was three days south, checking a boundary line. I came back to ash and a Magistrate's seal on the door and a report that said border raid, and I believed it, because believing it was simpler than believing that the world would take all three of them and leave me. And then Ilsa Vey robbed a dry well forty miles east, and I went after her, and I never stopped going after her, because as long as I was going after her I had a reason to get up in the morning that wasn't a kitchen with no one in it."

"You could have grieved and chased," Ilsa said gently. "People do both."

"No. That's the arithmetic of it. Grief is heavy and the chase is fast and you can't carry both. So I chose fast. Every morning I chose fast. Five years of mornings." He turned his hands toward the fire. "And now I find out the chase itself was built for me. Corvane didn't just take my family. He took my mourning. He took the one thing that was mine, the one honest thing, the nights I would have sat up with Marisol's name in my mouth, and he spent them for me, spent them on salt flats and stolen water and a thief who was never really running from me." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what I measured, all those years? Miles. Not days. Not years. Miles. Because miles add up to something. Days just go by. And now I'm sitting on a rock in the middle of a river that runs backward, and I've lost my mother's voice, and my father was murdered for a map, and the only person who ever understood my mornings is the woman I spent five years trying to catch."

Ilsa reached across the fire and took one of his hands.

Her fingers were cold from the river and calloused from the salt, and she held his hand the way you hold a wild thing—not gripping, just present, just saying *here, here, here.* He didn't pull away. That was the whole confession, right there, in the not-pulling-away.

"For one night," she said, "let's stop measuring. No miles. No years. No debts, no tolls, no counts of what we lost in the rapids or what Corvane owes us or what we owe each other. The river's doing the moving tonight. We're just cargo." A ghost of the old thief's smile. "Cargo doesn't keep books."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we start the books again, and the first entry is the highlands, and Corvane, and whatever the compass wants. But tonight—" she looked around at the gravel, the two voices of the river, the small defiant fire, "—tonight is just a night. We've never had one. You've been chasing and I've been chased since before either of us could walk straight, and I don't think either of us has ever once had a night that wasn't part of the pursuit."

He looked at her for a long time. The firelight did what firelight does, softening the sharp things, and he let it.

"All right," he said. "One night."

* * *

They didn't talk much after that, and it wasn't the old silence, the tactical one. It was the silence of two people who had finally said the heavy things out loud and found the world didn't end. Ilsa spread her soaked jacket on a flat stone to dry. Casper took off his boots and poured out the river and set them by the coals, and for a while the only sounds were the water and the wood and, once, far off in the high dark of the canyon, something that might have been a bird and might have been an echo and might have been Marisol, wanting to see the cave.

He didn't chase the sound. That was new. He let it pass over him like weather, and it hurt, and the hurt was clean, and clean hurt, he was discovering, was a different animal from numbness. Numbness was a debt. This was payment.

"Tam," he said, after a while, just to say it. "Tell me about him. The plums. The nails. Everything the canyon left."

And she did—she talked until the fire was embers and the embers were ash, about a boy who bit his nails and loved plums and once tried to sell the ferrywoman's cat to buy his sister a birthday ribbon, and Casper listened, and somewhere in the listening he understood that this was what family was. Not blood. Not the past. Just this: two people on a rock in the middle of the dark, telling each other the names of what they'd lost, so the names wouldn't be lost too.

He slept, eventually, and for the first time in five years he did not dream of the chase.

He woke before dawn to Ilsa's hand on his shoulder, and her face above him was pale in the gray light, and her eyes were fixed on the water.

"Casper," she said. "Look at the river."

He rolled to his feet and looked. The channel that had run backward through the canyon all this way—the impossible, upstream current that had carried them north against all sense—had stopped. Both voices of the river had gone silent. The water stood still as poured glass from bank to bank, and in its surface, faint as breath on a window, he could see lights. Dozens of them. Far upstream, high in the black shoulder of the highlands, a glow like a city under ice, and the still water leading to it like a road.

The compass in his pocket had gone warm as a living thing.

"It's waiting," Ilsa whispered. "The river's waiting for us."

Casper picked up his boots. The night was over. The books were open again. And the first entry, written in still water and far light, was the source.
