## 11. The Toll Bridge

They smelled the bridge before they saw it—woodsmoke and tallow and something sweeter underneath, like sugar burned in a closed room. The river narrowed there, pinched between two walls of black stone, and someone had spanned the gap with a structure that had no business standing: a bridge of lashed timber and rope and old wagon beds welded together with iron scraps, sagging in the middle like a tired animal. Fortifications crowned both ends. Watchtowers built from ship masts. Lanterns strung on wire, burning a greenish flame that Casper recognized with a ranger's old unease—echo-fire, the kind that fed on memory instead of oil.

"Smugglers," Ilsa said. She had gone still in the way she went still before anything dangerous, which was also the way she went still before anything interesting. "The Tollmen. I've heard of them but never run their water."

"You've heard of them from whom?"

"From everyone I've ever robbed." She smiled without much humor. "Reputation travels faster than stolen goods."

Casper studied the bridge through the spyglass he'd taken off a dead rider two canyons back. Figures moved along its length—a dozen, maybe more, wrapped in coats stitched from mismatched furs, each coat a different animal, as if they'd decided long ago that belonging to any one thing was a risk. At the near end, a woman sat on an upturned crate beside a brass scale older than any settlement downstream, its pans black with use.

"They'll want toll," he said.

"It's not coin." Ilsa's voice had flattened. He'd learned to read her flattening; it was the sound of her deciding how much truth she could afford him. "The river takes memories at the rapids whether you consent or not. The Tollmen just made a business of consent. You pay them a memory of your choosing, and they let you keep everything else."

"And if you have nothing worth taking?"

"Then you don't cross." She looked at him sideways. "Or you swim. And the river takes what it wants anyway, and you come out the other side missing something you didn't get to pick."

The morning was cold enough that their breath hung between them like a third traveler. Since the gravel bar, since the fire and the confessions traded like wounds compared, something had shifted in the geometry of them. They still walked apart. But the distance had become a choice rather than a habit, and choices, Casper was learning, weighed more.

He thought about what he could afford to lose. It was a strange arithmetic. Five years ago he would have said *nothing*—that every memory of Marta and the boy was load-bearing, that removing one would bring the whole house of himself down. But the rapids had already taxed him. The sound of his mother's voice was gone, and he was still standing. That was the horror of it, he realized. Not the loss itself but the discovery that he was a building with rooms he'd never known about, and some of them could be emptied without the roof falling in.

"I have one thing they might want," he said.

Ilsa looked at him. "What?"

"The day my family died. All of it. Every hour."

She flinched. Actually flinched, this woman who had watched Brannoc's boats without blinking. "You'd give that up? To walk across a bridge?"

"To get upstream before Corvane does. Yes." He said it flatly, because if he said it any other way he would hear the lie in it. "It's the only memory I own that a smuggler would call precious. Grief is currency out here. Everyone knows that."

"Casper." She almost never used his name. When she did, it landed like a hand on the shoulder. "You don't have to prove anything to me. Not after the fire."

"This isn't proving." And that part was true, at least. It was strategy. It was survival. He repeated those words to himself the way a man repeats a prayer he no longer believes, hoping the rhythm will do the work the faith won't.

* * *

The Tollmen met them forty yards out, as if the bridge had a radius and they could feel intruders entering it. There were six of them, and the woman with the scale came last, walking slowly, carrying the brass balance against her hip like a child. Her face was a map of small decisions—dozens of tiny scars where memory-tolls had been cut and collected, Casper guessed, though he couldn't have said how he knew. Ranger training, maybe. Or maybe the river was already teaching him things.

"Toll," the woman said. No greeting. Her voice was smoke-cured, unhurried.

"We're passing upstream," Casper said. "Two travelers. We can pay."

"Everyone says they can pay." She set the scale down on a flat stone with the care of a priest arranging relics. "Fewer can. A memory has weight, ranger. Real weight. We've weighed ten thousand of them, and we know when one is light."

"I know how it works."

"Do you?" Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of someone who had looked into too many people's pasts and stopped seeing anything else. "Because most people think they're giving up an event. You're not. You're giving up the event and everything hanging from it. Give us your wedding day and you lose your wife's laugh, because you only ever heard it there. Give us your mother's face and you lose your own reflection, because she's the one you learned it from." She tilted her head. "So. What are you offering?"

Behind him, Ilsa had gone very quiet.

"The last day of my family," Casper said. "The border raid. All of it."

The woman's expression didn't change, but something behind her eyes did—a flicker, the look of a merchant hearing a number higher than expected. "That's heavy freight. That's the kind of memory men kill to buy back." She gestured, and two of the Tollmen brought forward a shallow basin of dark glass, and in it, a lattice of echo-salt crystals already grown, waiting to be seeded. "Kneel. Hold the basin. Think of nothing else. If your mind wanders while we harvest, we take the wandering too, and you won't know what's missing. Most never do."

Casper knelt. The stone was cold through his trousers. He held the basin and looked into the crystal lattice and began, deliberately, to remember.

Marta at the window with flour on her hands. The boy chasing a dog that wasn't theirs but acted like it. Smoke on the horizon that he'd misread as a cookfire. The ride home too fast, the gate broken, the house—

"Stop."

The woman's voice cracked across his skull like a whip. His eyes opened. The crystals in the basin were glowing faintly, drinking, but thin—the glow of a lamp low on oil.

"That's not the whole day," she said softly. "That's the first hour. Where's the rest?"

And here was the moment. Casper had planned for it since the fire, turning it over the way a man turns a stone in his pocket, and now that it had arrived it felt smaller and colder than it had in his head. Because the truth was simple: he had not offered the whole day. He couldn't. The whole day was the last thing he owned of them, and some part of him—some part that had spent five years pretending to be a tracker and was actually just a man hiding inside a chase—had refused, absolutely, to surrender it.

So in the night, by the coals, he had done something he had never done in fifteen years of honest ranging. He had shaved slivers from the echo-salt Ilsa carried—the fragments she'd stolen for Corvane, the ones they hadn't yet spent—and pressed them into a blank crystal, and shaped them with everything he knew about how memory felt from the outside. He had built a counterfeit. A memory of the raid assembled from salt that had held other people's grief, dressed in the shapes of his own. From arm's length, indistinguishable. From inside, hollow.

The Tollwoman was looking at the basin. Looking at him. The pale eyes narrowed.

"There are hours missing," she said again.

"Grief does that," Casper said. "It burns sections clean out. Ask anyone who's lost a family. The mind protects itself. Whole hours go dark."

It was a good lie. It was even a true-adjacent lie, because grief *had* burned holes in him, just different ones. The Tollwoman stared at him for a long time, and the lanterns hissed overhead in their green flames, and somewhere on the bridge above, rope creaked against wood.

Then she reached into her coat and drew out a small tuning fork of white metal and struck it against the basin's rim.

The note that rang out was wrong. Casper heard it immediately, and he saw by her face that she heard it too. Real memory hummed when struck—a full, round tone, the sound of a thing that had actually lived. Counterfeit salt sang thin, with a rattle underneath, the ghost of whatever strangers' grief had been melted into it.

The Tollwoman lowered the fork. She looked at Casper with an expression he would carry for a long time afterward—not anger, not disgust. Recognition. The look of a woman meeting a fellow professional.

"You forged this," she said.

Ilsa's breath caught behind him. One of the Tollmen shifted, and Casper heard the whisper of a blade leaving a sheath.

"Yes," Casper said.

He said it before he could stop himself, before the smarter coward in him could construct a denial, and later he would wonder why. Maybe because lying twice to the same face is a weight most spines refuse. Maybe because some part of him wanted to be caught—wanted someone, anyone, to see clearly through the careful numbness he'd worn for five years and name it fraud.

The Tollwoman crouched down to his level. Up close she smelled of tallow and river water and, faintly, of the sweet burnt-sugar scent from the fires.

"Fifteen years I ran this bridge," she said. "Do you know what I've learned? Every person who comes here lies about what they're willing to lose. They offer the memory they can bear to give, not the one they owe. That's human. That's fine. We price around it." She tapped the counterfeit crystal with one scarred finger. "But this—this is different. This is a man who decided the past is material. Who looked at the one sacred thing he had left and thought: *I can make another one.*"

"I did what I had to do to cross."

"Of course you did." She stood. "That's the worst part. It worked."

She turned to her people and spoke in a register Casper didn't catch, and the tension in their shoulders eased by degrees—not all the way, but enough. Then she looked at Ilsa.

"You. Your turn. And before you open your mouth—" she raised the fork warningly, "—know that I've been told to watch for a thief who works for the Magistrate. Echo-thief. Small, quick, left-handed. There's a bounty on her that would buy this bridge."

The world went very narrow and very quiet. Casper's hand moved toward his knife without consulting him.

"She's not with Corvane anymore," he said.

"Everyone says that too." The Tollwoman sighed, and for a moment she looked profoundly tired, a woman standing at the exact center of a war over memory, weighing strangers' souls on brass pans because someone had to. "Pay, thief. Honestly. And then both of you cross, and neither of you looks back, and if the Magistrate's people ask, I never saw you. Not because I'm merciful. Because I'm busy."

Ilsa knelt. Casper watched her hold the basin, watched her choose—and he understood, watching her face, that she was not choosing lightly, that whatever she gave up would be real and whole and gone forever. Her lips moved once, silently, the way people say goodbye to things no one else can see.

When the crystals glowed, they glowed fully, richly, without a single false note. The Tollwoman struck the fork and the tone came out round and true, and something in her posture softened.

"A sister's voice," she murmured, reading the harvest the way a blind woman reads a page. "Singing. You kept that five years past losing her." She sealed the crystal into a lead-lined case. "That was worth more than anything the ranger offered. Even the fake."

* * *

They crossed the bridge in single file behind a Tollman guide, planks flexing underfoot, the river visible through gaps in the timbers—running backward, patient, silver-green in the morning light. Halfway across, Ilsa fell into step beside him.

"You lied to them," she said quietly. "For me. If they'd turned me over—"

"I lied for both of us. Don't make it noble."

"It wasn't noble." She was silent for three planks' worth of crossing. "It was worse than noble. Do you understand what you did back there? The Tollwoman wasn't angry about being cheated. She was frightened. People who counterfeit memory—people who treat the past as raw material—that's what Corvane does. That's the whole architecture of him. And she looked at you and saw the beginning of it."

Casper gripped the rope rail and looked down at the backward-running water. "I know."

"You know, and you'd do it again."

"Yes."

Below them, far under the bridge, something pale turned in the current—not a fish. A shape like a face, surfacing and sinking, the way lost things rise in this river. He watched it until it was gone.

At the far end, the guide took their names—or rather, refused them. "No names on the north side," he said. "Names are memories too. Everything past this bridge belongs to the source. Walk soft."

They walked soft. Behind them the green lanterns burned on their wires, feeding on the harvested day Casper had sold them—a day that had never happened, humming its thin false note over the black water.

And ahead, half-hidden in the gorge's shadow, Casper saw what the smugglers had built their bridge to guard against seeing: a road climbing the canyon wall, wide enough for wagons, freshly cleared, with wheel ruts cut deep and new.

Corvane's wagons. Days old at most.

"He's ahead of us," Ilsa breathed.

"No," Casper said, kneeling to press two fingers into a rut, feeling the cold freshness of turned earth. The compass in his pocket had begun, very faintly, to tremble. "He's not ahead. He's waiting. These tracks don't cross the bridge—they stop at it. Someone told him exactly where we'd have to come through."

He stood slowly and looked back the way they'd come, at the Tollmen moving small and distant along their swaying span, and understood that the false memory he'd sold might be the cheapest thing he'd paid that morning.

Someone had priced their crossing before they ever reached it.
