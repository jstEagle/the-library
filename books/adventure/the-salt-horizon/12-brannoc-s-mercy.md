## 12. Brannoc's Mercy

The gorge came at them the way grief comes—suddenly, and from a direction they hadn't thought to watch.

Casper heard it first: not hoofbeats, not oars, but the particular silence of a man who has stopped pretending to sneak. Brannoc had been behind them for two days, patient as sediment, letting them do the work of finding the river's hardest passage so he could arrive at its end without breaking a sweat. Now that patience ended. The sound of him was the absence of birdsong along the rimrock, a hush moving downstream faster than they could move themselves.

"We're boxed," Ilsa said. She stood at the lip where the canyon narrowed to a throat of white water, the river roaring thirty feet below, the walls rising sheer on either side. Above them, on the shelf of rock they'd climbed toward since dawn, the path forked—one branch dead-ending in scree, the other climbing back east, toward Corvane's territory, toward everything they'd spent nine days fleeing. "I told you he wouldn't lose us. He's not a man who loses things. He's a man who collects them."

Casper checked the compass out of habit. It lay in his palm pointing not north but upstream, toward memory, steady as a heartbeat. His father's compass. The ferrywoman's gift. He'd begun to think of it less as an instrument than as a small stubborn conscience he carried in his pocket.

"Then we don't run," he said. "We stand."

Ilsa looked at him the way she always did when he said something that would have gotten a smarter man killed—as if cataloguing it for later evidence. Wind off the glacier-fed water pulled at her hair. Nine days of rapids and toll bridges and cold camps had carved her finer, or maybe just revealed what had been there under the thief's polish all along: someone tired in a way that had nothing to do with miles.

"He'll kill you," she said. "That's the order. Me he takes back alive. You're the loose thread."

"I've been a loose thread for five years." Casper turned to face up-canyon, where the hush was coming. "Threads hold things together. Maybe that's why nobody's managed to cut me yet."

* * *

Brannoc came around the bend of the trail like weather.

Casper had seen him once before, years ago, across the salt flats at a distance that made him more rumor than man—a shape on a horse with Corvane's black banner hanging limp beside him. Up close he was worse and better than the rumor. Worse because he was enormous in the way of men built by purpose rather than appetite, shoulders like a doorframe, hands scarred to the wrist. Better because his eyes were wrong for an enforcer. They were gray and quiet and deeply sad, the eyes of a man reading a letter he wished he'd never opened.

He carried no drawn weapon. That was the first strange thing. A long knife rode his hip and a crossbow was slung across his back, and both stayed exactly where they were.

"Ranger." His voice was gravel poured slow. "Vey."

"Brannoc." Ilsa's hand had gone to her belt knife, and Casper saw her knuckles whiten around it, and saw also that she knew as well as he did how useless it was. "You've gotten slower. Took you two days to close a gap you used to close in one."

"I let you run." Brannoc stopped ten paces away, close enough that the spray off the gorge misted all three of them. "The Magistrate wanted you tired. Tired people tell the truth." His gaze moved to Casper, and something in it shifted—not softening, exactly. Recalculating. "You're smaller than I expected. The man who's kept Vey running for five years. Half the garrison bets on you like you're a sport."

"What are the odds tonight?" Casper asked.

"Tonight the odds are that I put a bolt through your chest and carry her back in irons, and the Magistrate opens the Well by month's end and begins rewriting every past between here and the sea." Brannoc said it flatly, a report, nothing performed about it. And then he did the second strange thing. He sat down. Lowered himself onto a boulder at the trail's edge with the heavy grace of a landslide deciding against itself, and looked out over the gorge, and said nothing for a long moment.

Ilsa's eyes found Casper's. *What is this,* they said. Casper had no answer. He kept his weight forward on the balls of his feet and waited, the way you wait out a flash flood—knowing the danger isn't diminished, only that movement makes it worse.

"You're wondering," Brannoc said finally, still watching the water, "why I'm not doing it."

"The thought crossed the gorge," Casper said.

"Three years ago the Magistrate gave me a gift." Brannoc turned his head, and now those gray eyes fixed on Casper with an awful directness. "A vial. Distilled, he calls it. Drawn from the echo-salt, refined in his citadel until one swallow holds a whole memory, clean and bright as glass. He drinks them nightly, did you know that? Sits alone in his tower drinking other men's lives." A muscle moved in his jaw. "Mine was mercy. He called it a calibration. Said an enforcer who remembered what compassion felt like could better predict the movements of compassionate prey."

"And?" Ilsa asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"And I can't un-remember it." Brannoc laughed then, one dry note with no humor in it, a stone dropping down a well. "Do you understand what he did? He thought he was sharpening me. Instead he put something in me that won't come out. I have a memory that isn't mine living in my chest like a swallowed coal. I remember—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I remember kneeling in a burned house in the border country, lifting a child out from under a beam, and the weight of her, and the way she grabbed my collar with both fists because she decided I was safe. I wasn't there. It isn't mine. I've never held a child in my life. But I know exactly what her hair smelled like. Smoke and milk. I will know it until I die."

The gorge roared beneath them. Somewhere above, wind worried at the rimrock with a sound like pages turning.

"That's the raid," Casper said. His voice came out strange, thin. "The border raid. That's—"

"Quintana's Crossing. Yes." Brannoc looked at him fully now. "It's your memory, ranger. Or a piece of it. The Magistrate harvests from everywhere. He must have liked the shape of it—mercy in a ruin, a rescuer's hands. He bottled it and fed it to me like medicine." He rose from the boulder, slow, and for a moment his shadow covered them both. "So when I corner you above a gorge with orders to kill you, understand what stands between my orders and my hands. A memory of being kind. One that belongs to a stranger. To you, maybe. I can't un-remember compassion. He made sure of that. He just never asked himself what else I couldn't un-remember."

* * *

Ilsa moved first—but not to fight. She stepped sideways, putting herself half between Brannoc and Casper, which was absurd, which was a moth stepping in front of a storm, and Casper felt something turn over in his chest at the sight of it.

"You're going to let us go," she said. Not asking. Naming it, the way she named things she wanted true.

Brannoc didn't answer at once. He unslung the crossbow instead, and both of them went rigid—and then he simply held it, turning it over in his huge hands like a man examining a tool he'd inherited from a father he hated.

"Do you know what the Magistrate means to do at the Well?" he said. "Not the story he tells the garrison. Not *restore order, heal old wounds.* I've sat outside his door at night. I've heard him talk to the bottles." He set the crossbow down on the rock between them, deliberately, within reach of any of the three of them—an offering or a test. "He doesn't want to remember the valley. He wants to own it. Every mind from Merrow's Landing to the high passes, emptied and refilled to his design. Grief edited out. Disobedience edited out. Whole villages walking around with his version of their lives in their heads, grateful for memories he sold them. He calls it the Great Return." His mouth twisted. "There's nothing great in it and nothing returning. It's a theft with a ceremony attached."

"You served him twelve years," Ilsa said. "You collected for him. You brought him crates."

"I did." No flinch. "And every crate I carried, I told myself the same lie you told yourself, Vey—that it was for your brother, that it was temporary, that the man giving the orders must have a reason underneath the reason. Then I drank one vial of stolen mercy and understood that there is no underneath. There's just a man who lost his wife and decided the whole world should lose itself with him." He looked out at the gorge again, at the white water hammering through the throat of stone. "She died fleeing him. Did you know that? Everyone in the citadel knows and no one says it. Whatever he finds at that pool, it won't be the woman he remembers. Memory keeps the truth even when we bury it. That's the one law the Well can't repeal."

Casper's hand had drifted, without his deciding it, toward the compass in his pocket. Through the leather he felt its small insistence, pointing upstream, pointing toward the source, toward the pool beneath the glacier arch where every lost thing surfaced like fish rising. *His family's true memories come freely,* something in him insisted, the ferrywoman's voice or his father's or his own. *Unbought.*

"Why?" he asked aloud. "Why warn us? Why give up twelve years of service for two strangers on a ledge?"

Brannoc considered him for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into something almost private.

"Because last night I camped below you on the river, and I heard you talking in your sleep. Both of you. Vey saying a name over and over—some brother. You calling out to people I've read about in the Magistrate's files, ranger. Your file is thick. Did you know that? Five years of reports on your movements, your habits, the wells you checked, the trails you favored. Someone wrote you like a character in a story." He paused. "And I thought: this is what he does. This is what he did to me. He writes people into shapes that serve him. The enforcer. The thief. The grieving ranger who needs chasing so he never looks upstream." His jaw worked. "I drank a memory of saving a child from a fire, and it ruined me for his work. So consider this my repayment. Consider it the only mercy I have left—the borrowed kind."

He stepped back. Once. Twice. Picked up the crossbow and slung it across his back again, and the gesture was so ordinary, so much the motion of a man going off duty, that it was somehow the most frightening thing he'd done.

"Listen to me now, because I'll only say it once and then I'm the Magistrate's dog again until I decide otherwise." The gray eyes burned. "Corvane knows another way into the highlands. The pass at Gray Saddle—it's longer, but it doesn't follow the river, and it lets him come at the source from above while you come from below. He left three days ago with forty men and enough empty bottles to drain a county. You have perhaps two days. Less, if the snowmelt runs fast." He glanced at Ilsa. "Your bargain holds, Vey. He keeps you alive, you lead him to the source. I'm not breaking that. I'm just telling you both what waits there. Forty men. A man who drinks strangers' souls. And a pool that gives back everything—including, if he gets there first, a world rewritten in his image."

"And you?" Casper asked. "When he finds out you let us go?"

Brannoc smiled, and it was terrible, because it was the smile of a man who had already drunk his own ending and found it bearable.

"When he finds out, he'll offer me a vial to make me forget today. Some warm memory of loyalty, some soldier's pride, distilled and sweetened." He turned and began walking back up the trail, away from them, his boots striking the stone with the patience of a clock. "And I'll drink it, probably. That's the joke, ranger. That's the whole joke. He'll try to cure me of mercy with another stolen memory—and it won't take, because you can't drown a thing that's already learned to swim."

* * *

They watched him go until the trail bent and took him.

Neither spoke for a while. The gorge filled the silence with its white noise, and the spray beaded on Casper's face, cold as confession. Finally Ilsa sat down on the boulder Brannoc had vacated, still warm faintly from his weight, and put her head in her hands.

"He's right," she said, muffled. "About the files. About the chase being written. I've known since the archive station and I keep waiting for you to hate me properly and you keep—" She lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, mostly at herself. "Why aren't you angry? He just handed you your own life in pieces. Your sister under a beam. Your memory in an enforcer's chest. Five years scripted by a man who drinks the dead."

Casper took out the compass. Held it flat on his palm between them, its needle trembling, then settling, pointing upstream toward the source, toward the Well, toward whatever waited there besides forty men and a magistrate's hunger.

"I'm furious," he said quietly. "I've been furious since the ferrywoman. But anger's just another way of measuring, Ilsa. Miles of grievance. Days of score-keeping. I did five years of that already and it got me nowhere but excellent at running." He closed his fist around the compass. "Brannoc drank a memory that wasn't his and it made him more himself, not less. Maybe that's the trick of the whole cursed valley. What you remember owns you. What you choose—" He looked up the trail, then at her, then at the narrow climbing path beyond, the one that led toward the highlands and the glacier and the end of everything. "What you choose is the only thing that's actually yours."

Isla studied him for a long moment. Then she stood, and rolled her shoulders like a woman picking a lock, and nodded once toward the path.

"Two days," she said. "Gray Saddle. Forty men." A ghost of the old thief's grin surfaced, worn thin but real. "Better odds than we started with."

They climbed. And far below them, unheard, the river ran backward toward the sea, carrying home everything it had ever taken.
