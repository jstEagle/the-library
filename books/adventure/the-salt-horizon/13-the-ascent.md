## 13. The Ascent

The river began to climb on the third morning out of the Toll Bridge, and by noon it had stopped pretending to be a river at all.

Casper noticed it first in the sound. Water running downhill made a certain argument with the land—a steady, reasonable murmur, water explaining itself to stone. This was different. The current moved against the grade in long, muscular pulses, sliding uphill over shelves of slate as if the mountain were pouring itself into the sea instead of the other way around. He crouched at the edge of a pool no wider than a wagon bed and watched a leaf drift *up* the fall, turning slowly, patient as a pilgrim.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Most of what we've done this week isn't," Ilsa said behind him. She had her hood up against the spray, and her voice came out flattened by it. "You get used to impossible. It's the possible things that start to feel strange."

He looked back down the canyon they'd climbed through—down, though they had walked up—and saw the water descending toward them from above, silver threads braiding out of the mist like something being woven rather than spilled. The highlands rose ahead in gray terraces, patched with old snow that refused the season, and the air had changed too. Thinner, colder, but crowded somehow. Full in a way air shouldn't be.

He heard the whispers for the first time an hour later.

At first he took them for wind moving through the scree fields, that dry hiss of gravel settling. But wind doesn't say names. Wind doesn't repeat a child's laugh three times in a row, softer each time, like someone practicing losing it. Casper stopped on the trail with his hand on his knife and listened to a voice—not any voice he knew, a man's, worn smooth as river glass—murmur something about a kitchen, about bread, about a door left open too long. Then it was gone, and another took its place, a woman counting under her breath, and then a fragment of a song in a language he didn't speak, sung by someone who clearly had loved the person they were singing to.

"The whispers," he said.

Isla had gone very still ahead of him. "The forgetting ground," she said. "That's what the smugglers called it. Everything forgotten near here comes up out of the rock. Like breath through limestone." She pulled her hood back. Her face in the gray light looked older than it had at the bridge, or maybe just less defended. "They used to say you could stand up here all night and hear your whole life come apart again. Every memory anyone ever let go of, drifting around looking for somewhere to land."

"And people live up here?"

"People avoid up here." She started walking again, faster than she needed to. "The ferrywoman's stories said the river remembers its way home by following the whispers. They rise off the valley floor and the water climbs after them. That's why it runs backward. It's not defying anything. It's just going where the lost things go."

Casper walked a while without answering. His father's compass hung at his chest on its cord, and every few minutes he checked it without deciding to—the habit of five years, the needle that pointed not north but toward memory, toward whatever the Well of Return wanted found. All morning it had been swinging in slow, drunken arcs, sweeping across the whole face of the dial like a man searching a crowd for a face.

* * *

By afternoon the landscape stopped resembling anything he had words for.

The canyon walls opened into a valley of white stone, carved by water into shapes that suggested architecture without committing to it—arches, colonnades, stairways that ended in air. Snow lay in the hollows out of season, and where the snowmelt ran, it ran uphill, feeding thin streams that climbed toward the peaks. The whispering had thickened until it was less like voices and more like weather. Casper caught pieces of things: a debt forgiven; a face remembered wrong on purpose so it would hurt less; the smell of rain on a road someone had walked away from forty years ago. Some of the whispers were in his own cadence, his own flat ranger's drawl, saying things he had never said, and those were the worst, because they sounded like the man he might have been if the flats hadn't taken everything.

His father's compass went mad at the edge of the white valley.

It didn't swing anymore—it spun, fast and frantic, the needle blurring, and the brass case grew warm against his chest, warm enough that he fumbled it free and held it in his palm and watched it whirl like something trying to escape its own body.

"It's never done that," he said.

Ilsa didn't answer. He glanced up and found her standing ten paces off, facing away from him, her shoulders drawn up around her ears. She had been quiet since morning—quiet in a way that wasn't her usual economy. Ilsa's silence was usually a tool, honed and deliberate, deployed to make other people talk. This was different. This was the silence of someone carrying something heavy up a hill and refusing to set it down because setting it down meant admitting how much it weighed.

"Ilsa."

"I heard you." She still didn't turn. "It's close. That's why it's spinning. It can't decide which lost thing to point at first, because up here there are too many." A pause. "Or because it's pointing at two things at once."

"What two things?"

She turned then, and her expression was one he'd catalogued over five years of pursuit and one week of alliance: the look she wore when she was calculating whether a lie would hold. Except now, he realized, she wasn't calculating whether to lie to him. She was calculating whether the truth would survive the telling.

"The Well," she said. "And me."

The compass needle shuddered, slowed, and locked—snapped to a bearing hard enough that he felt it in his teeth. It pointed up-valley, toward a notch in the peaks where the snow glowed faintly blue, and it did not waver again. Whatever indecision had gripped it was finished. It had chosen, or it had been forced, and either way it now pulled at his sternum like a hook set in flesh.

"We should make camp before dark," Ilsa said, and walked toward the lee of a broken arch, and did not look at him again until the fire was lit.

* * *

The fire burned strangely in the highlands—low and blue-edged, giving more light than heat, as if even flame were half-remembering itself here. They ate in silence. Above them the stars came out hard and close, and between the stars and the stone the whispers drifted, and Casper found he could almost sort them now, the way a ranger sorts animal sign: fresh tracks and old ones, griefs still wet and griefs gone to dust.

A child's voice passed through camp around moonrise. Not passing through—*lingering*, circling the fire twice, saying something about a dog, about a promise involving a dog, and then fading up-slope toward the notch in the peaks. Toward the Well. Casper watched the place where the voice had been and felt the hair rise on his arms.

"They go to it," he said quietly. "The forgotten things. They climb toward it on their own."

"Everything lost wants to be found," Ilsa said. "That's the whole legend. That's the whole trap." She was sitting with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, staring into the blue fire. "The Well doesn't lure anyone. It doesn't have to. People walk into it willingly, every one of them, because they're already walking toward their dead. The Well just stands at the end of the road everyone's already on."

"You've seen it."

"No." Too fast. Too flat. "No one sees it. That's the point of it."

Casper set down his tin cup. Five years of reading this woman across salt flats and burned campsites and the aftermath of her thefts had taught him the shape of her evasions better than her confessions. When Ilsa Vey lied, she embellished—gave details, color, texture, a thief's instinct to make the false thing valuable enough to be believed. When she told the truth, she stripped it bare and stood behind it like a shield held backward.

"You've read about it," he said. "In Corvane's archive. You've read accounts of it. Of what happens to people who drink."

Her jaw tightened. The firelight moved on her face and she let it, using the shadows the way she used darkness on a job—to hide the parts that would give her away.

"There was a ranger once," she said finally. "Before your time. Halvard something—he had a name, it's in the reports, I read it four times and I've lost it, which up here is probably a joke with a knife in it. He reached the Well alone in the year the Magistrate's grandfather was consolidating the territory. He drank." She unwound one arm from her knees and turned her palm up, studying it. "The report says he came back down the mountain remembering everything he'd ever lost, perfectly, in order, forever. And that he sat down at the bottom of the trail and stayed there for eleven days, and then he walked into Merrow's Landing and asked every person he met whether they'd seen his daughter, because the memory was so complete he couldn't tell it apart from having her back. He kept asking for years. Everyone in the village learned to be kind to him. He died believing she was coming home."

The whispers circled the fire. Somewhere above them, snowmelt climbed a cliff face in silence.

"That's what drinking costs," Ilsa said. "Not the memory. The difference. You stop being able to tell what you have from what you had." She looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes in the blue light were the flattest he'd ever seen them, which from Ilsa meant they were holding back a flood. "And some people don't come back at all. There are gaps in the archive. Pages cut out. Corvane keeps a list of everyone who's ever reached the source, and half the entries just end."

Casper was quiet for a long time. The compass lay against his chest, steady now, certain, pulling.

"You didn't have to tell me that," he said.

"Yes, I did." She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Because tomorrow we'll be close enough that you'll hear it. The Well. You'll hear everything you've ever lost calling to you, and it will sound exactly like mercy, and I need you to know, before that happens, that I know what it sounds like too." Her voice didn't break. It did something worse—it went careful. "I need you to know that when we get there, there are things I'm going to have to say. Things I should have said at the bridge, or the village, or the rapids, and didn't, because every mile I waited was a mile I got to keep not saying them."

Casper's hand had gone, without his permission, to the compass. "Say them now."

"No." She shook her head, small and final. "Not here. Not with—" she gestured at the whispering dark, at the whole listening valley, "—everything forgotten eavesdropping. Some things should only be said once, in front of one witness." She pulled her bedroll from her pack, and her movements had gone brisk and mechanical, the efficiency of a woman building a wall out of ordinary tasks. "Get some sleep, ranger. Tomorrow the mountain gets steep."

* * *

He did not sleep, or slept the way the valley seemed to permit sleep—in fragments, surfacing and sinking, each time coming up into a different chorus of whispers. Once he woke to hear his own mother's voice, or something shaped like it, humming the tune she used to hum while mending, and he lay rigid in his bedroll with his heart slamming until it faded up-slope, joining the procession of lost things climbing toward the notch in the peaks. Once he woke to find Ilsa awake too, sitting upright, watching him—or watching past him, at the fire, at the mountain, at whatever she was dreading—and when she saw his eyes open she lay down again without a word, and neither of them pretended afterward that it hadn't happened.

Dawn came up the color of old bone. The compass pulled.

They broke camp without discussion and climbed. The trail—if it had ever been a trail—followed the backward river through terraces of snow, and the whispering thickened until Casper stopped trying to separate the voices and simply let them move through him like weather. He heard his sister. Twice. Once laughing, once saying his name in the particular exasperated way she'd saved only for him, and both times the sound came from above, from the notch, from the blue glow in the ice that was growing brighter as they gained height, and both times every part of him leaned toward it like a plant toward light, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what Ilsa had meant last night. It sounded like mercy. It sounded like a door opening in a wall he'd spent five years pressing his forehead against.

Ilsa walked behind him and said nothing at all.

Near midday the valley narrowed to a throat of ice, and the river—climbing beside them, patient, faithful—drew level and then drew ahead, hurrying now, and the compass at Casper's chest beat against his sternum like a second heart. Through the throat of ice he could see open sky beyond, and a basin, and something pale rising at the center of the basin that might have been a glacier arch or might have been a doorway, and beneath the whispering, beneath everything, he could hear it now: a low, vast note, like a bell struck underwater, like ten thousand voices breathing in unison.

Ilsa stopped walking.

Casper turned. She stood in the shadow of the ice with her hands loose at her sides, and her face was the face of someone standing at the edge of a decision that could not be unmade, and when she spoke, her voice came out roughened, scraped nearly clean.

"When we get there," she said, "whatever I tell you—you have to remember that I'm not the same person who took the assignment. Do you understand? Whoever I was when Corvane gave me your name, whoever I was when I made myself worth chasing—I'm not her anymore. Five years is long enough to become the lie you're living. Long enough that the lie stops being a lie." Her eyes were bright and terrible in the blue light. "But you deserve the truth anyway. Even now. Especially now."

Above them, through the throat of ice, the pale shape resolved—stone, ancient and carved, framing a darkness that breathed.

The compass locked hard toward it and did not move again.

"Then tell me at the top," Casper said, and turned, and climbed.
