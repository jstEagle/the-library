## 15. Confession at the Falls

The falls had no name on any map Casper had ever carried, which was fitting, because the water that fell from them was not water in any way he'd been taught to understand. It came off the glacier in ropes of white, struck a shelf of black stone, and shattered into mist that hung in the air like something reluctant to land. The sound was enormous and constant, and beneath it—so faint you had to stop breathing to catch it—the whispering. Everything forgotten nearby, rising with the snowmelt. He'd heard it all day as they climbed: scraps of lullabies, half-arguments, the names of dead dogs, a woman's voice repeating *the left-hand drawer, always the left-hand drawer* as if it were a prayer.

They made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, close enough to the falls that the spray silvered their bedrolls, far enough that they could speak without shouting. Ilsa built the fire small, the way she always did—a thief's fire, meant to be smothered in three breaths—and cooked the last of the dried fish without looking at him.

She hadn't looked at him since the archive.

Casper sat with his back against the stone and turned his father's compass over in his hands. It still pointed past the Well, past everything, steady as a heartbeat, and he found he could not decide whether that steadiness comforted him or accused him. In the crates below the forward station there had been bottles labeled QUINTANA in a clerk's careful hand. Bottles of his family. And among the ledgers, a page dated five years ago, ink still sharp:

*Subject C.Q. — pursuit authorized. Asset V. assigned. Maintain engagement indefinitely.*

Asset V.

He looked up. Ilsa was watching the fire the way people watch a thing that might bite them.

"Say it," she said.

"You first."

* * *

She told it plainly, which was worse than any weeping would have been. She fed the fire a stick and spoke in the flat, economical voice of someone reciting an inventory, and the falls filled every pause so she never had to rush.

"I was nineteen. My brother Tomas and I worked the toll bridges south of the flats—you remember them, you've seen what they are. Corvane's men came through collecting. Not taxes. Names. They kept lists of who remembered what, and my brother had a memory they wanted. Something he'd seen, before the waters rose. I never learned what. They gave me a choice: his freedom for my service. I signed. Everyone signs. That's the thing no one tells you about Corvane's territory—there's no door marked *conscription*. There's just a ledger, and your name goes in it, and after that you're ink."

The fish spat in the pan. She turned it.

"The first years were ordinary theft. Echo-salt from the caves, bottled memories from the smugglers' caches. Then they gave me a file." She reached into her coat—not for a weapon; Casper's hand moved anyway, five years of habit—and drew out a folded paper gone soft as cloth from handling. She didn't offer it. She just held it, looking at it like a scar. "Your name. Your sister's name. The date of the raid. A map of your patrol routes. And one line of instruction."

She recited it from memory, and her voice did not change, and that was the most terrible part: *"Become worth chasing."*

"They didn't want you caught," she said. "They wanted you occupied. Your father mapped the approach to the Well. Corvane couldn't find him while Quintana rangers were watching the highlands, so he took the family upstream and left the son down on the salt, running in circles after a ghost he'd learn to love." She finally looked up. Her eyes were dry and very dark in the firelight. "I was the ghost."

* * *

Casper said nothing for a long time. The falls roared. Somewhere in the roar, faintly, a child was laughing—the echo of some laughter lost on this mountain decades ago—and it wound through the confession like a needle through cloth.

He tried to find the edge of it, the place where the betrayal began, and could not. Every mile he'd measured came back to him at once: the dry well where he'd found her bootprints circling twice before leaving, clever, deliberate, a signature. The market town where she'd let him see her on a rooftop at dawn, silhouetted, unhurried, giving him hope to run on. The night in the canyon country when rain had trapped them both under opposite ledges of the same rock and he'd spoken to her across six feet of falling water and she'd answered, and he'd lain awake afterward thinking: *someday, when I catch her, I'll ask her about that.*

Five years. He had built a life out of her, the way a starving man builds a meal out of smoke. And the whole time—

"Was any of it real?" His voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw by the altitude or the question.

"No." Then, immediately, because she was Ilsa and lying to him had apparently become impossible somewhere on the river between the rapids and here: "That's not true. I don't know how to answer it honestly. Ask me a smaller question."

"Fine." He leaned forward. "The dry well. Year two. You circled your own tracks twice. Why?"

A muscle moved in her jaw. "Because you were a day behind and out of water, and I knew you wouldn't drink from a well I'd touched unless you thought I hadn't been there. So I made prints that said *she passed in a hurry, going north*, and I cached a skin of water under the north ledge where you'd find it and tell yourself you'd out-thought me." She looked back at the fire. "You drank it. You always drank it. You thought you were winning, Casper. I made sure of it. That was the job."

"And the ledges. In the rain."

"That wasn't the job." Said fast, almost angry. "That was—I don't know what that was. I stopped knowing what was the job around year three. That's the confession underneath the confession, if you want it." She set the pan down. Her hands were steady; her voice finally wasn't. "Corvane ordered a performance. A thief worth chasing. Clever, cruel, always one step ahead, always worth another hundred miles. And I performed it. For two years I performed it, and then somewhere on the flats—it was spring, the salt was blooming white, you nearly caught me at the ruined windmill and you had your hand around my wrist and you weren't even angry, you were just *glad*, glad to see me, like I was the only fixed point in your life—" She stopped. Breathed. "Somewhere in there I stopped acting. I don't know the exact day. There's no day. You can't mark it, that's the horror of it. One morning you're following orders and one morning you're leaving water for a man because you can't stand the thought of him thirsty, and both mornings look identical from the outside, and you don't get to know which one you're living in until someone puts a ledger in front of you with your own name written as *asset*."

"Year three," Casper said. "The windmill was year three."

"Yes."

"So for two years I've been chasing a woman who—" He couldn't finish it. He made himself finish it. "Who was already free of them. Who could have told me. Any night, any campfire, any of a hundred times when we were the only two living things in sight. You could have said *your family didn't die in a raid, your father is upstream, Corvane built your grief into a leash.* Two years, Ilsa."

"Yes," she said again. Just that. She didn't dress it up, didn't reach for excuses, and he understood suddenly that this was its own kind of cruelty—the honesty he'd spent five years trying to beat out of her with miles and miles of pursuit, given to him now all at once, unasked for, unbearable.

"Why?"

"Because telling you ended the chase. And the chase was—" She faltered for the first time. "It was the only thing I had that was mine. They owned my service, my brother's life, my name in their ledger. But the chase belonged to us. To you and me. Nobody else in the world knew it the way we knew it. And I thought—if I tell him, it becomes theirs too. Corvane designed it. It stops being ours." She shook her head slowly. "That's not a reason. I know it's not a reason. It's the truth, though, and you asked for the truth, and I'm not going to ask you to forgive it, because I wouldn't."

* * *

She didn't ask forgiveness. That was the thing he kept returning to as the fire burned down and the spray thickened toward evening and the falls went on saying whatever the falls say to themselves at the top of the world. She laid it all out—the staged near-misses, the planted clues, the orders she'd obeyed and the ones she'd quietly bent, the water skins, the false trails that kept him alive and kept him running—and then she sat back and asked for nothing. No absolution, no argument, not even a verdict. She simply handed him the whole architecture of his last five years and waited to see what he'd do with it.

And the worst of it, the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone, was that he could not make her a villain. He tried. He sat there turning her crimes over, looking for the black heart at the center, and found instead a nineteen-year-old girl trading herself for her brother's life, and losing the brother anyway, and then being handed a new assignment: *become loved enough to be followed.* Both of them instruments. Both of them played. Corvane had taken a grieving man and a conscripted woman and fitted them together like gears, and the machine had run for five years, and now the machine had brought them here, to a fire under unnamed falls, and neither of them knew anymore which parts of what they felt were design and which parts had grown wild in the cracks of it.

"I need to walk," he said.

"Casper—"

"Not away." He stood, knees stiff with cold. "Just away from the fire. Stay here."

She nodded once and looked back at the flames, and did not watch him go, and somehow that was its own mercy.

* * *

He walked upriver along the falls' rim, into the snowfield, until the firelight was a coal behind him and the world was blue dusk and white. The whispering was louder here, away from the roar—everything ever forgotten on this mountain, murmuring up through the snowmelt. He stopped walking and stood in it and let it happen to him.

His mother's voice came first, or something shaped like it, saying his childhood name the way she'd said it when he'd tracked mud into the house. His sister next, laughing at something, the laugh stripped of context, pure sound, a bell rung in an empty room. He stood in the middle of his own family's debris and understood that these were strays—fragments shed by the archive downstream, leaked into the watershed, lost even from Corvane's careful hoard. Even the Magistrate couldn't keep everything. Even hoarded memory leaked.

He thought about turning back. Not to camp—to the Well. He could feel the thought moving through him with its own pulse: *let Corvane have it, let him open his vault, and drink, and have them whole.* Five years of fragments, and the Well offered the entire animal. What was a valley full of strangers' returned memories against that? What was anyone's grief but his own, scaled up?

The compass was in his hand. He didn't remember drawing it. Its needle lay steady, pointing past the Well, past the glacier, past anything he had a name for, and it did not waver while he stood there hating it and hating Ilsa and hating Corvane and hating, most of all, the five-year boy he'd been on the salt flats, grateful for every crumb of a chase that was built to feed on him.

Behind him, far below, the fire was a spark. Ahead, the glacier arch waited, and under it the pool where everything lost rose like fish.

When he walked back down, Ilsa was awake, sitting up, the paper with his name still in her hands. She'd smoothed it flat against her knee. She held it out to him without a word—the original orders, *asset V., become worth chasing*—and he took it, and fed it to the fire, and watched it curl.

Neither of them slept. Neither of them spoke again that night. But sometime before dawn, in the gray hour when the falls seemed briefly quieter, Ilsa shifted in her bedroll and said, to the dark, in a voice he was maybe not meant to hear:

"There's a second seal. On the Well. My handlers only ever described the first."

Casper sat up slowly. "What do you mean, a second seal?"

"I mean," she said, "that the compass isn't pointing at the Well. It's pointing at whatever the Well is keeping in."
