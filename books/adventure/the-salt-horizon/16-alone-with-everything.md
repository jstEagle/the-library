## 16. Alone With Everything

Casper walked out of the camp while the light was still going, before Ilsa could say anything that would either soften him or harden him further, because he could not have borne either. He took the bedroll, the water skin, and the compass, and he left the fire burning behind him like a small accusation.

The highlands did not permit silence. That was the thing no ranger legend had prepared him for—the whispers. They rose out of the snowfields and the thin air the way heat shimmer rises off the flats, except that heat says nothing, and this said everything. Fragments of the forgotten, drifting loose in the cold: a woman counting stitches, a child arguing with a dog, a farmer muttering the names of his fields like a prayer. None of it was his. All of it pressed against him like hands at a door.

He climbed until the camp was a spark below and the glacier arch was a pale suggestion above, then he made his own fire in the lee of a granite shoulder, laid out the bedroll, and did not sleep.

He had spent five years learning how not to think. It was a discipline, like tracking. You kept your eyes on the sign—the scuffed stone, the bent grass, the water hole with one cup too many—and you did not look at the horizon, because the horizon was where Marta's laugh lived, and the smell of his father's hands after a day with the survey instruments, and the particular way his mother said his name when she was tired, *Casper, mijo, come eat*, with the syllables worn smooth as river stones. The chase had been a mercy. He understood that now, understood it the way you understand a trap only after the jaws close: Corvane had built him a road out of his own grief and posted Ilsa Vey at the far end of it like a lure, and Casper had run that road gladly, gratefully, for five years, measuring his life in miles so he would never have to measure it in losses.

And now the road had ended, and there was nowhere left to look but the horizon.

The whispers thickened as the night deepened. That was the pattern, he was learning—the cold drew them up, or the dark did, the forgotten things rising like fish to a night-fed surface. He pulled the blanket to his chin and told himself he would not listen.

He listened.

*Casper.* Faint, threaded through the wind off the glacier, so thin he might have made it out of his own wanting. *Casper, mijo, come eat.*

He sat up so fast the fire spat.

It wasn't her. It couldn't be her. It was the air, the echoes, some dead woman's voice snagged in the ice a hundred years ago and now loosened by the river's backward pull, and it had only happened to sound like—

*Casper.* Closer. Warmer. The tiredness in it, the particular worn-smooth quality of the syllables, and beneath that the smell that came with the voice, impossible, absurd, of corn and chili and the smoke of the cookfire in the courtyard at Merrow's Landing, except it wasn't Merrow's Landing's cookfire, it was *home's*, it was the courtyard he had not stood in for five years because there was nothing left of it but ash and a border raid and a hole in the world shaped like three people.

"Stop," he said aloud. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Stop it."

The wind did not stop. The wind had no reason to obey him. It carried his mother's voice up the slope and past him toward the glacier, and it carried other things too—his sister, now, Marta, seventeen and furious about something, her voice bright and sharp as a knife being honed: *You never listen, you never—* and then the laugh, the laugh that ruined the fury, the laugh that always ruined it, because Marta could not stay angry, Marta had never once in her life finished being angry, and Casper lay on the frozen ground with his eyes open and his hands over his ears and understood that he was being robbed.

Not robbed—*recruited*. That was worse. The night was not taking anything from him. It was offering. It was laying out his dead one by one on the snow like a merchant laying out cloth, saying *here, here, all of it, everything you lost, and all you have to do is walk the other way.*

Because that was the thought, wasn't it. The thought he had been outrunning since the archive, since the crates with his family's name stenciled on the slats, since Ilsa's confession had detonated in the space between them and left him walking into the snow. The thought was simple and it was monstrous and it wore the face of a gift:

*Let Corvane have the Well.*

Let the Magistrate drink. Let him open the seal and pour the valley's memory back into the world, every bottled year, every stolen voice—and in that flood, intact, unbroken, would come his father's last day and his mother's last morning and Marta's last laugh, whole, not fragments, not whispers, not the wind's cruel approximation but the true and complete record of the people he had loved. He could stand at the pool's edge and drink and have them back—not their bodies, no, never their bodies, but everything else, the whole of them, every memory they had ever made, and he could live inside those memories the way Corvane lived inside his wife's, nightly, faithfully, a man keeping a shrine with his own bloodstream.

What was the valley's freedom against that? What was the abstract liberty of ten thousand strangers he had never met?

He knew the answer. He had known it since the ferrywoman put the compass in his hand. But knowing an answer and surviving a night are different skills, and the night was long, and the whispers were patient, and grief is the oldest salesman in the world.

*You never listen,* Marta said, from everywhere. *You never listen, you never—*

"I'm listening now," he whispered. "Marta. I'm listening."

And the wind laughed the way she laughed, and it ruined him.

* * *

Somewhere past the middle of the night he got up and walked, because lying still had become unbearable. The snow crunched under him with a sound like grinding salt. The glacier arch loomed ahead, a bridge of blue-white ice spanning a dark throat of stone, and beneath it, he knew, the river ran backward toward its source, toward the pool where every lost thing rose like a fish to the light.

The compass was in his hand. He didn't remember drawing it. His father's compass, brass worn to shine at the edges, the needle that pointed not north but toward memory—toward the Well, the ferrywoman had said, toward the thing itself.

He held it flat and looked at it the way he had looked at a thousand tracks. *Read the sign. Don't look at the horizon.*

The needle swung.

Not steadily, the way it had locked on the ascent, but wildly, drunkenly, sweeping through its arc like a thing in pain—and then, at the top of a swing, it stopped. Hard. Vibrating slightly, like a struck bell.

And it was not pointing at the arch.

Casper turned the compass in his hand, checked it against the stars, checked it again. The arch was behind him and to the left; the needle ignored it. The needle pointed up-slope and to the right, toward a shoulder of black rock where nothing showed but snow and stone and starlight—toward something past the Well. Beyond it. As if the source of the river were not the end of the line at all. As if memory itself had a source further on, some deeper water the Well only drew from, and his father—his father who had mapped the approach, who had died for knowing the way—had known that too.

*The compass is a key, not a guide.* The ferrywoman's voice, or maybe his own memory of it, worn smooth now too.

He stood a long time in the cold with the needle steady in its strange direction, and the whispers came up around him, and for the first time all night he listened to them without flinching. Not his dead. Other people's dead. A miller's song. A wedding argument. Someone teaching someone else to whistle. The whole valley's forgotten, drifting loose in the thin air, waiting for a tide to carry them home.

And the thought came again—*let Corvane have it*—but it came quieter now, and it came wearing its true face, which was not a gift's face at all. It was Corvane's face. It was the face of a man who drank his dead wife nightly and grew colder with every dose, who had built an archive of other people's griefs because his own had hollowed him out past saving. Casper had spent five years becoming a man who did not grieve. He could see, now, in the Magistrate, the far end of that road: a man who grieved *nothing*, who felt *nothing*, who kept a fortress full of bottled voices because his own house had gone silent.

If he drank at the Well, he would drink his family whole. And in ten years he would be Corvane. In twenty he would be worse. He would sit in a high room with a perfect memory of Marta's laugh and no one left alive to laugh with, and he would call it love, and it would be the most elaborate tomb ever carved out of a living man.

"No," he said to the wind.

The wind did not answer. But somewhere downslope, faint, a woman's voice—not his mother's, a stranger's—was teaching a child to whistle, and the child's attempts came back thin and wrong and joyful, and Casper found his eyes were wet and freezing on his cheeks, and he let them be.

* * *

He came back to the camp at first light, and Ilsa was awake, sitting up with her knees drawn in, watching the trail. She had not slept either; he could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes, in the way her hands were still.

"You walked all night," she said.

"Part of it."

"Into the whispers." It wasn't a question. She knew the highlands; he remembered now that she had never denied knowing them. "They get everyone eventually. The dead ones' voices. They tell you exactly what you want to hear, because they're made out of what you want to hear. Corvane's men used to come back from patrol up here talking to people who weren't there."

"I know what they are." He crouched by the dead fire and began raking ash over the coals, not because it needed doing but because his hands wanted work. "They're not liars, though. That's the trap. Everything they say is true. Marta's voice is Marta's voice. It's just—" He stopped. Started again. "It's just not her. A memory of a laugh isn't a person who laughs."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. "That's the whole war," she said. "In one sentence. That's what we're carrying up this mountain."

He looked at her then, really looked, past the betrayal that was still lodged in him like a splinter he had decided to carry rather than cut out. She looked small in the gray light, and stubborn, and afraid—not of the glacier, he thought, but of what she would have to say when they reached the pool. She had gone quiet on the ascent and had not come all the way back.

"The compass did something," he said. "Last night. It stopped pointing at the Well."

She went very still. "What do you mean, stopped?"

"Locked on something past it. Upslope, east of the arch. There's something beyond the source, Ilsa. My father mapped the approach to the Well—but I don't think the Well was what he was mapping toward. I think it was a door on the way to something else."

"Nothing's past the source." But her voice said she was reciting, not reasoning. "The legends end at the pool. Everything ends at the pool."

"Legends end where people stopped walking." He stood, shouldered the bedroll, and held the compass flat between them. The needle held its strange bearing, steady as a heartbeat, pointing up-slope into the white. "My father didn't stop walking. Come on. We'll be at the arch by midday, and I want the high ground before Corvane's riders find the trail."

Ilsa rose slowly. She looked at the compass a long moment, and then at him, and something in her face shifted—relief, or its close cousin, dread.

"Casper," she said. "When we get there, there are things I have to—"

"I know."

"No. You don't. Not all of it."

"Then say it at the pool." He turned up-slope, into the wind, into the whispers, into the white country where the forgotten drifted like snow that had refused to fall. "You'll have your chance. I'll have mine. And whatever's past the Well—" he touched the compass through his coat, feeling the needle tremble against its bearing like a pulse, "—I intend to find out before anyone drinks anything."

Behind them, faint on the wind, a dead woman was still teaching a living child's echo to whistle, and the highlands carried the sound up the slope like an escort, like a blessing, like a warning that everything they were walking toward had been waiting a very long time to be heard.
