## 17. The Source

The river stopped pretending to be a river on the third morning of the ascent.

Casper noticed it first as a sound—or rather, the absence of one. For days the water had chattered beside them, backward-running and whisper-laced, carrying fragments of forgotten things the way ordinary rivers carry silt. Now it went quiet, sliding over smooth stone in a silence so complete he could hear his own pulse in his ears. The channel narrowed. The walls of the gorge rose around them, blue-white with ancient ice, until the sky was nothing but a seam of light overhead.

Then the gorge opened its throat, and they stepped through.

The Well of Return lay beneath an arch of glacier ice older than any map Casper had ever drawn—a cathedral of frozen water, translucent as sea glass, glowing faintly from within as though the light of every dawn it had ever witnessed had been stored there and never spent. Beneath the arch, the river pooled into a basin perhaps thirty paces across, so clear it seemed less like water than like absence, a hole cut into the world.

And the pool was rising.

Not with water. With memories.

They surfaced like fish—Casper could think of no other word for it. Shapes moved beneath the surface, pale and flickering, and now and then one rose close enough to catch the light and break apart into something almost visible: a kitchen at dusk, a dog barking at nothing, a woman laughing in a doorway, a hand letting go of another hand. Each one glimmered, hung for a heartbeat just beneath the skin of the pool, and sank again, patient as seed grain waiting for spring.

Ilsa stood at the edge with her arms wrapped around herself, and she did not look at the pool. She looked at her boots.

"It's real," Casper said. His voice came out strange, flattened by the acoustics of the arch, as if the ice were repeating it somewhere above them in a language made of cold.

"You sound surprised."

"I've been chasing you for five years on rumors thinner than this. I'm allowed to be surprised that any of it was true." He crouched at the pool's rim but did not touch the water. He had learned caution about beautiful things the hard way, and the hard way had cost him everything once already. "Your turn to be honest. This is what Corvane sent you to find fragments of?"

"This is the source of all of it." Ilsa finally lifted her eyes, and in the glow of the arch her face looked younger than he'd ever seen it, and more afraid. "Every echo-salt shard I ever stole was a crumb fallen from this table. Every bottle in his archive. Everything." She paused. "There's something else."

"I know there is. You've been walking like a woman carrying a full bucket since the falls."

She almost smiled. It didn't take. "When we're done here. Whatever happens here—I'll say it. But if Corvane's men come before I get the chance—"

"They won't find the route."

"Brannoc knew the route."

"Brannoc let us go." Casper stood, brushing frost from his knee. "That's not the same as telling Corvane where we went."

"Casper." She said his name the way she said very few things: plainly, without armor. "You've met the Magistrate. You've seen what he does to men who show mercy. Brannoc's hesitation bought us three days, maybe four. We've used two."

He wanted to argue. He didn't, because she was right, and because even as she spoke, the compass at his belt—the one that pointed not north but toward memory itself—gave a slow, shuddering rotation and settled, needle trembling, aimed directly down into the pool.

His father's compass. His father, who had mapped this approach. His father, who had not died in a border raid but been taken upstream, taken apart, bottled.

Casper knelt again at the water's edge, and this time he let himself look properly. The memories rose and fell. A birthday. A funeral. A first kiss under a bad roof during good rain. An entire life's worth of small lost things, drifting in the dark like lanterns someone had let go of downstream.

Somewhere in that water, he thought, was his mother's voice—the one the rapids had stripped from him as toll. Somewhere was his sister's laugh, preserved whole instead of broken into salt-echoes. Somewhere was the last thing his father ever saw.

He did not drink. But God help him, he wanted to. The wanting sat in his chest like a coal, and he understood, finally, why Corvane had built an empire on this hunger. It wasn't power the Well offered first. It was comfort. Power was only what comfort became when a man decided no one else should have any.

"We should move away from the edge," Ilsa said quietly. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case either of us is weaker than we think we are."

* * *

They made camp in a hollow of stone above the basin, close enough to hear the pool's near-silence, far enough that its light reached them only as a pale wash against the arch. There was no wood this high; they burned a twist of dried scrub and ate cold rations, and neither of them spoke much. The whispers in the air had changed up here. Down in the lowlands they had been a murmur, a rumor of forgetting. Here they were distinct—half-heard sentences, the ends of arguments, the beginnings of lullabies—all the things ever lost within a hundred miles, come home to circle their source like birds before a storm.

Casper slept in pieces. In the gaps between sleep he dreamed without dreaming: images surfacing in him the way they surfaced in the pool. His sister Marta, nine years old, teaching him to whistle through a blade of grass. His mother's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists. His father at a table by lamplight, drawing lines on vellum, lines that led here—and looking up, once, as if he knew someone would follow them someday.

He woke before dawn with wet eyes and frost in his lashes, and Ilsa was already awake, sitting with her back to him, watching the approach to the basin.

"There's dust on the lower trail," she said.

He joined her. Far below, winding up through the gorge they had climbed, a line of riders moved at a pace that meant discipline—too fast for comfort, too steady for panic. Behind them, on the switchbacks, came torchlight in daylight, which meant they expected to be underground or behind ice before nightfall.

"How?" Casper asked, though he knew.

"Brannoc's captured men," Ilsa said. "Or Brannoc himself. Or someone talked. It doesn't matter how. What matters is—" she counted, lips moving, "—four hours. Maybe five, if the horses founder on the ice."

"Then we have four hours to decide what we're doing when they arrive."

"Do we? I know what I'm doing." She turned to face him, and the wind off the glacier pulled at her hair. "I'm going to stand between Corvane and that water if I have to. I owe this place a debt I can't describe yet. But you—" her voice caught, steadied, "—you need to decide now, while there's still time to decide honestly. Because when he gets here, he's going to offer you exactly what you want most in the world. And he'll be able to deliver it. That's the thing people don't understand about men like Corvane. They don't lie. They don't have to. They just own the thing you'd sell your soul for, and wait."

"And what do you think I want most?"

"I think you want your family back," she said. "Whole. Remembered. Alive, if the stories are true and the Well can give back more than memory. And I think when he offers it, some part of you is going to say yes, and I can't—I won't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I just need to know which man walks down to that pool with me. The one who breaks things, or the one who takes the deal. So I know who I'm standing next to."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. Five years of pursuit, and she had never once asked him a question that required an answer. That had been the shape of their intimacy: motion, distance, the elegant grammar of the chase. Now she was asking, and he found he had no elegant grammar left.

"I don't know," he said. It was the truest thing he'd said in years. "Ask me again in four hours."

* * *

Corvane arrived in the fifth hour, on foot, having abandoned his foundering horses two miles below, and he arrived alone.

His guard halted at the mouth of the gorge—six men in the Magistrate's grey, spears grounded, breath steaming—and Corvane walked the last stretch of ice unaccompanied, unhurried, his long coat sweeping frost, his face lit from below by the glow of the arch so that he seemed to rise out of the Well's own light. He looked, Casper thought, less like a man arriving at a destination than like a man returning to a house he'd been robbed of.

"Ranger Quintana." Corvane's voice carried in the cold without effort, pitched perfectly to the acoustics as if he'd rehearsed in better cathedrals than this. "And my thief. Ilsa. You've cost me a great deal of money and a great deal of patience, and I find, standing here, that I resent neither. Do you know why?"

Neither of them answered. Corvane smiled as if they had.

"Because you've done what forty surveyors and three expeditions could not. You've brought me to the door." He spread his hands, taking in the arch, the pool, the pale shapes rising and sinking beneath its surface. "Look at it. Look at it, Quintana. Have you ever seen anything so honest? Every grief in the valley, floating right there where anyone might reach it. No priests, no fees, no lies about how loss builds character. Just the thing itself, kept safe. All I have ever wanted is to keep it safe."

"You want to own it," Casper said.

"I want to *keep* it. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of governance." Corvane drifted closer to the pool's edge, and Casper saw the hunger in him plainly now—not the theatrical appetite of a tyrant but something rawer, a thirst worn down to the stone. "Do you know what I do every evening, ranger? I drink a thimbleful of distilled memory and sit with my wife for one hour. She has been dead eleven years. She laughs at my jokes. She argues with me about the irrigation levies. She is more alive to me than any living soul in my territory, and every morning I wake up poorer by one day, and every evening I buy her back. That is not tyranny. That is love, conducted through the only means left to me."

"It's addiction," Ilsa said.

"It is *devotion*," Corvane snapped, and for one instant the mask slipped and Casper saw the man underneath—not a monster, worse: a widower. Then the mask returned, smoother than before. "But we are not here to discuss me. We are here to discuss you."

He turned to Casper fully, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—warmer, lower, the voice of a man offering a seat by a fire.

"Quintana. Your father mapped the approaches to this place. Did you know that? A ranger, like his son. A careful man. He understood what the Well was before anyone in the valley had a name for it, and he recorded everything—every route, every toll, every danger—in his own memory, because he trusted no page. When he came upstream to warn the settlements, my men intercepted him. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. He fought. He lost. And everything he was went into a bottle, where it has sat, undisturbed, in my archive, for five years."

The coal in Casper's chest ignited. "You killed him."

"I *kept* him," Corvane said softly. "Which is more than the border raid would have done. Which is more than the river would have done. Death scatters, ranger. I preserve." He gestured at the pool, where a memory shaped like a lamplit table surfaced and sank. "And I am prepared to make you an offer that no magistrate in history has made a man he could simply kill."

He drew from inside his coat a single flask—dark glass, silver-capped, unremarkable except that the frost refused to form on it. He held it up between two fingers.

"Your family," Corvane said. "All of them. Your father's final memory, intact, including the map and the warning he died trying to deliver. Your mother's voice—the one the rapids took from you; yes, I know about the toll, I know everything that happens on this river. Your sister's laugh, her whistling, her ninth birthday, her last morning. Not echoes. Not salt-shards. The whole of them, restored to you in a single draught, and after—" he nodded toward the Well, "—access. Standing access. You could sit at this pool every evening for the rest of your life and be with them. Perhaps"—and here his voice dropped to something almost gentle, almost kind, and that was the cruelest thing in it—"perhaps more than remembered. The old stories say the Well gives back what was truly lost. I have never dared test that claim myself. But you could. You could stand here and call them, and see what rises."

The wind stopped. The whispers in the air hushed, as if the whole valley were leaning in to hear the answer.

"All I ask," Corvane finished, "is that you step aside. Let me seal the approach. Let me govern the source—ration it, protect it, keep it from the mob and the smugglers and the well-meaning fools who would drain it dry in a season. You keep your family. I keep order. Everyone keeps what they actually care about." He extended the flask. "Step aside, ranger. Take them back. No one has to know what it cost, because nothing needs to cost you anything at all. That is the miracle of this place. Grief, ended. On my terms, yes. But ended."

Casper did not move.

The flask hung in the frozen air between them, and in the pool below, the memories rose faster now, crowding the surface—his mother's hands, Marta's grass-blade whistle, his father's lamplight—as if they had heard their names called and were pressing against the skin of the water, waiting to be let out.

Beside him, Ilsa had gone very still. And Corvane stood with the patience of a man who had waited eleven years and could wait five minutes more, watching the ranger's eyes, watching the war behind them, watching temptation put its hand on the shoulder of the only man who might stop him—

—and far below, at the mouth of the gorge, a single grey figure broke ranks from the guard and began to climb.
