## 18. Corvane's Wife

The Well of Return did not look like power.

It looked like a wound in the glacier's belly—a pool no wider than a wagon circle, ringed in blue-white ice that glowed from within like a lantern held behind skin. The water was so clear it seemed absent. Casper could see to the bottom, and the bottom was not stone but memory: shapes drifting there, pale and slow as fish, rising and sinking in the dark. Some were small as coins. One, near the edge, was large as a door, and when it turned he caught a flash of something inside it—golden light, a kitchen, a woman laughing—and had to look away before it took him whole.

The air above the pool hummed. Not sound, exactly. More like the feeling of standing near a struck bell after the ringing stops.

Ilsa stood at his shoulder, close enough that he could feel her breath, shallow and quick. She had not spoken since the ascent. She had not spoken since the archive, since the crates with his family's name stenciled on their lids, since the memory of his father's last hour played out in Corvane's bottled light while she stood beside him and said nothing. Five years of silence between them on the flats, and now silence again—but it was a different animal now. It had teeth.

"Quintana." Corvane's voice carried across the ice without effort, the way a blade carries across a whetstone. "You came all this way to watch me drink."

The Magistrate stood at the pool's far rim with six guards fanned behind him, crossbows leveled, snow sifting off their oiled cloaks. He had not hurried on the march up. He had walked the last mile with his hands clasped behind his back like a man touring his own garden. Up here, away from the citadel's shadow, Corvane looked smaller than his reputation—narrow through the shoulders, gray at the temples, with the fine-drawn face of a man who had spent decades indoors among things worth more than people. Only his eyes betrayed him. They were the eyes of someone who had not slept a full night in years, and who had stopped wanting to.

"You don't have to do this," Casper said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. The whispers in the highland air pressed against his ears—the accumulated murmur of everything forgotten nearby—and it was hard to make his own thoughts heard over theirs. "You've read what's down there. You know what the toll is."

"I know exactly what the toll is." Corvane smiled, and it was almost gentle. "That's why I'm the only man in the valley qualified to pay it. Do you know what I've given up for my territory, ranger? Sleep. My health. My wife." He gestured at the pool with one white-gloved hand. "And it asks for more. It always asks for more. But I have never once flinched from the price of remembering."

Behind Casper, Ilsa made a small sound—not quite a word. He didn't look at her. Looking at her would undo him, and he needed to be whole for whatever came next.

"Your men burned a cave full of dead voices," Casper said. "They'd have burned this too, if you'd let them. That's not remembering. That's hoarding."

"Hoarding." Corvane tasted the word. "Yes. Brannoc used that word once, before I sent him back down the mountain. He learned it from you, I imagine, or from her." A flick of the eyes toward Ilsa. "Words are cheap, Quintana. Let me show you what I actually keep."

He knelt at the water's edge.

Casper moved without deciding to—one step, two—and the nearest guard's crossbow snapped up with a ratcheting clatter. Corvane raised a hand without looking back, and the bolt stayed loosed only by the width of a hair.

"Watch," the Magistrate said. "Then judge me."

From inside his coat he drew a vial—not glass, but something older, horn or bone, stoppered with wax gone amber with age. The liquid inside was dark and slow, moving against the tilt of the vial the way the river below had run backward. He worked the wax free with his thumbnail. The smell reached Casper a heartbeat later: rain on hot stone, bread, woodsmoke, something floral and long-dead. A whole life, distilled to a swallow.

"Maren," Corvane said, and his voice changed on the name—thinned, cracked, human in a way nothing else about him was. "Every night for eleven years, I drink a little of you. Every night you come back. Is that so monstrous?"

He drank.

* * *

For a moment, nothing. The pool went still. Even the whispering air seemed to lean in.

Then Corvane shuddered, and the vial dropped from his fingers into the snow, and Casper watched eleven years of careful tyranny come apart in the space of three breaths.

It began in the hands. They opened—slowly, fingers uncurling like something blooming—and then they began to shake. Corvane's head tipped back. His mouth formed a word that wasn't a word, just the first letter of one, over and over: *Muh. Muh. Muh.* Tears cut bright lines down the gray of his cheeks, and he did not wipe them, and Casper understood with a lurch that the Magistrate had forgotten how—he had wept alone in the dark for so long that the mechanics of public grief had rusted out of him.

"She—" Corvane's voice broke and reformed, younger suddenly, stripped of its courtroom polish. "No. No, that's not—that isn't how it—"

"What?" Casper took another step. No one stopped him. The guards were watching their master the way sailors watch a storm come over the horizon. "What do you see?"

Corvane turned his head. For the first time since the flats, he looked directly at Casper, and there was nothing behind his eyes but a man drowning in his own cellar.

"She's running." The words came out flat with horror. "In the memory. She's running down the east stair and she's carrying the little bottle, the one with our wedding in it, and she's crying, and I'm—I'm at the top of the stairs and I'm telling her to stop, and she won't stop, she—" His breath hitched. "She's afraid of me. In my own memory. She's afraid of me and I never saw it. Eleven years I've drunk this and I never once got past her face. Her beautiful face. I drank the beginning every time. The garden. The music. I never drank to the end."

"The end was always in the bottle," Ilsa said quietly.

Everyone turned. She had come around the pool's rim, slow, hands visible, and she stood now between Corvane and the water with the glacier light turning her half to sculpture. Her voice didn't shake. Casper knew what that cost her; he could see it in the tendons of her neck.

"I bottled some of those memories myself, Magistrate. Your archivists ordered the deep draws—the ones past the sweet part. Someone has to go past the sweet part or the archive lies." She swallowed. "Your wife didn't die of the fever. That's the official record, isn't it? The fever, the winter, the tragedy. But your own collectors brought back the truth because truth is all echo-salt keeps. She died on the river road, heading downstream, away from you. And the last thing in her was fear. Not of the road. Of going home."

"No." Corvane rose. He swayed as he did it, a big tree finding it had rotted at the root. "No, she loved—she chose me—we were—"

"You emptied her." Ilsa's voice stayed level, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "A sip a night, Magistrate. You thought you were keeping her alive. But memory doesn't work like wine in a cellar. Every time you drew from her, you drew from *her*. The woman in the citadel got thinner and thinner where it mattered. By the last winter she couldn't remember why she'd married you. She could only remember that she had. So she ran. And you had her death recorded as fever because the truth would have cost you the territory."

The wind moved through the glacier arch above them, and the whispers in it gathered, and for a moment Casper would have sworn the whole cavern spoke with one voice—not words, but assent. The pool's pale shapes rose toward the surface, dozens of them now, pressing upward like fish at feeding time, drawn by the taste of a broken man's grief.

Corvane looked at his empty hand. At the vial in the snow. At Ilsa. At Casper. And Casper watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes—the same arithmetic, he realized, that had run behind his own for five years: *if I can just get enough of it back, if I can just hold enough, the loss will stop hurting.*

"That's not true," Corvane said. But he said it the way a man says a thing to a locked door, testing whether it will open anyway. "She wouldn't. Maren wouldn't."

"Drink again," Ilsa said softly. "Drink to the end this time. You have the whole bottle in you now. See where she was running *to*."

And Corvane—who had commanded territories, who had emptied minds by decree, who had turned a thief into a lure and a grieving man into a hound and called it governance—Corvane knelt back down at the edge of the Well of Return and cupped water in his two shaking hands like a beggar.

"Don't," Casper said. The word tore out of him. Because he knew—God help him, he knew—what it was to want the past so badly you'd drink anything that promised it. He had chased a woman across five hundred miles of salt rather than sit still with two graves. He was not better than this man. He had only been poorer. "Corvane. Whatever it shows you, it'll be true, and you can't put it back. Believe me. There are things I can't unknow now, and they're eating me alive."

The Magistrate paused, hands cupped, water trembling between them. In the surface below his knuckles, a memory-fish the size of a songbird circled, and inside it Casper glimpsed green grass and a small girl's shoes and felt his sister's presence so sharply his knees nearly buckled. *Lark.* Even here. Even now, she found him.

"My lord," said the eldest guard, and his crossbow dipped. "Perhaps the ranger—"

"Silence." But there was no command left in it. Corvane stared into his cupped hands, and the glow of the Well lit his face from beneath, and for one suspended moment he looked like every man Casper had ever escorted out of the deep desert—lost past the point where maps helped.

Then he drank the Well itself.

* * *

What came out of him was not screaming. Casper had expected screaming. What came out was worse: laughter. Thin, delighted, disintegrating laughter, the laugh of a man hearing a joke told at his own expense by the only storyteller he'd ever trusted.

"She was coming back." Corvane rocked on his knees, water dripping from his beard. "Do you see? All this time—all these years I've hated the river road, cursed the smugglers who used it, posted riders on it—she was on the river road *coming home*. She got as far as Merrow's Landing. She was going to tell me she was frightened. She was going to ask me to stop. She wrote it down—there's a letter, the ferrywoman kept it, it's in the village, it's been in the village for eleven years waiting for me—" He looked up, and his face was wrecked, and wet, and terribly young. "She died a mile from home. A mile. If I had ever once ridden out to meet the road instead of sending others—"

The guards had lowered their weapons entirely. One of them sat down in the snow without appearing to decide to.

"And the rest of it," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "The rest of what you drank. Say it, Magistrate. You owe the valley that much."

Corvane closed his eyes. When he spoke again, each word came out like a stone pulled from a wound.

"She was afraid of what I'd become. That's in the archive. That's in *my* archive—I paid for it, I built the vault it sleeps in. 'He counts people the way he counts bottles,' she told the collector. 'One day the counting will be all that's left.'" He opened his eyes. "Eleven years ago a dying woman looked at what I was becoming and named it, and I bought her words and locked them in a crate and never read them, because reading them would have meant stopping."

The pool surged. Casper felt it before he saw it—a pressure change, a gathering, the way the flats felt the hour before an impossible tide. The memory-shapes below the surface had stopped circling. They were rising now, all of them, hundreds, thousands, a slow silver column ascending through the clear water toward the ice-arch overhead, and the whispering in the air had become a roar that was somehow still only a whisper, and the compass in Casper's coat—the compass that pointed not north but toward memory—was hammering against his ribs like a second heart.

Corvane looked up at the rising column, and then at Casper, and something passed over his face that Casper had never expected to see there: not rage, not command, but a question. The question of a man standing at the edge of everything he'd built, holding the match.

"You came to destroy this," Corvane said. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost hope. "That's why the compass sings. That's why the Well is rising. It knows why you came."

"Stand your men down," Casper said.

"They won't fire." Corvane laughed again, softer, hollowed. "Look at them. Half of them have family in the valley. Half of them have losses they've been pretending don't ache." He got to his feet—unsteadily, one hand pressed to his chest as though holding shut a door that wanted to burst open. "I have spent eleven years buying other people's yesterdays because I couldn't survive my own. And the whole time, the answer was in a crate with my name on it, and I was too much of a coward to open it."

He turned to face Casper fully, and behind him the column of memories broke the surface of the Well and hung there, gleaming, a pillar of everything lost.

"So do it," Corvane said. "Break it. Break it before I remember how to be what I was."

Above them, the glacier groaned, and the first crack ran across the ancient seal like lightning finding ground.
