## 20. Aftertide

The water came down the valley like a rumor made flesh.

Casper stood on the high bank above Merrow's Landing and watched it move—not the violent rush of a flood, not the brown violence of a river breaking its banks, but something gentler. A silver sheet of it, sliding through the drowned streets, filling doorways that had been empty for years, climbing the walls of houses whose owners had long since forgotten why they'd built so close to the waterline. The river was returning what it had taken, and it did so with the courtesy of a guest who knew the house better than its current inhabitants.

And with the water came everything else.

He heard it before he understood it. A sound rising out of the valley floor like heat shimmer given voice—hundreds of voices, thousands, overlapping and braided, some weeping, some laughing, some doing both at once in the ragged way people do when grief and joy arrive in the same breath. The memories Corvane had hoarded for decades were pouring back into the valley, finding their owners the way rain finds the ground: everywhere at once, asking nothing, simply falling.

Below him, Merrow's Landing was coming apart in the best possible way.

An old man stood knee-deep in the flooded lane outside the chandlery, holding his head with both hands, and when Casper looked closer he saw the man was smiling so hard his face seemed about to split. A woman dropped the basket she was carrying and sat down in the water without caring, her mouth moving around a name—*Tomas, Tomas*—as if tasting it after years of famine. Children who had never known their grandparents' faces were suddenly being held by people sobbing into their hair, whispering stories into small ears as fast as they could speak them, terrified the memories would evaporate if not told immediately, poured from one vessel to another before they could spill.

The ferrywoman stood at the head of her dock, dry-eyed, watching it all. When Casper came down the path to stand beside her, she didn't turn.

"You broke it," she said. Not a question.

"I broke it."

"Good." She was quiet a moment. "My husband's face. I'd been paying smugglers for years, little bottles, little sips, trying to keep him. This morning I woke up and there he was, whole, no charge." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were the gray of the river in winter. "You gave away the thing everyone would have killed for."

"It wasn't mine to keep," Casper said. "None of it ever was."

She nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd suspected since the day she'd first seen his face and refused to say what she recognized there. Now, apparently, she'd decided to say part of it.

"Your father stopped here once," she said. "Before you were born, or just after—I was younger than you are now. He asked me about the river's temper. Whether it forgave." She looked back out at the water. "I told him no. I was wrong. It just needed someone willing to break the lock instead of pick it."

Down in the streets, the tide of return was still rising. Casper watched a boy of perhaps ten run barefoot through the shallows shouting a word over and over—a word Casper couldn't hear clearly, but whose shape was unmistakably a name, a dead person's name, said aloud in daylight by a child who had never been allowed to know it.

His own chest felt strange. Lighter and heavier at once. Since the Well had broken, since the compass had shattered the seal and the whole archive had come loose in one great exhalation, his family's true memories had come to him freely—not distilled, not bottled, not rationed—but arriving the way weather arrives, in their own time, at their own scale. His mother's hands kneading bread. His sister's terrible singing, performed with total conviction. His father at the map table, tracing the river's backward course, saying *the source remembers us, Casper, remember that if anything happens*. And underneath all of it, the truth he'd learned in the crates at the forward station: the raid was staged, the deaths were ordered, and the chase that had eaten five years of his life had been designed by a man who wanted him tired, angry, and far away.

The grief was still there. He understood now that it always would be. But it had stopped being a wall he pressed his forehead against, and become something more like a room he could walk through—one with windows.

"Ilsa," the ferrywoman said, nodding toward the dock's end. "Been standing there since dawn. Hasn't said a word to anyone."

*

Ilsa Vey had packed light, which was to say she had almost nothing, which was to say she had exactly what she'd started with five years ago: a knife, a coil of rope, and the clothes she stood in. She'd added a single item since the Well—the empty bottle that had once held her brother's name, which she'd carried out of the collapsing station and then, at the last moment, hadn't thrown away. She couldn't have explained why. Some things you keep not because they hold anything but because they held something once.

She watched the valley fill with other people's reunions and felt the peculiar loneliness of the honest thief: everyone around her being handed back what was theirs, while she stood holding only the knowledge of what she'd done. The memories she'd stolen for Corvane were gone now too—released, scattered, returned to their rightful owners across the whole territory. Somewhere in Merrow's Landing a woman was getting back a lullaby Ilsa had pried out of a dying grandmother's house. Somewhere upstream a man was remembering his daughter's wedding because Ilsa had carried the bottle in a saddlebag for eleven months, keeping it safe, telling herself that was different from stealing it.

It wasn't different. She knew that now. Safekeeping and theft could look identical from the outside; the difference was only whether you intended to give it back, and she hadn't known her own intentions for years.

Casper's boots sounded on the planks behind her. She didn't turn.

"The ferry leaves at midday," she said. "Southbound. I checked."

"There's no southbound ferry today," Casper said. "Marta's taking people upriver. Half the valley wants to see where their memories came from."

"Then I'll walk."

"To where?"

She shrugged, a small sharp motion, a thief's shrug, economical. "Away. That's the direction I know best."

Casper came and stood beside her at the rail, looking out at the water. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, two brothers who hadn't spoken in nine years—not since the toll bridge had taken the memory of their quarrel and left them strangers to their own anger—were wading toward each other through the flooded street, and the moment they met, they hit each other, hard, and then embraced harder, and the whole street laughed.

"I've been thinking about arithmetic," Casper said.

"That's a bad sign in anyone."

"Five years, I measured my life in miles. Miles covered, miles between me and you, miles between me and the day my family died. It was a way of not counting anything that mattered." He turned to look at her. "Then I learned the miles weren't even real. Corvane drew the route. He picked the wells you'd rob, the trails you'd take, the near-misses he wanted me to have. My whole chase was a map somebody else made."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "I know. I walked it."

"And here's what I keep coming back to." He faced the water again. "Everything between us was designed. The meeting at the dry well. The stranding on the rise. Every conversation we ever had, I have to wonder—which parts were you performing? Which parts were his script?" He shook his head slowly. "Except one. Last night, on the gravel bar, when you told me about your brother. Nobody wrote that. There was no reason for it. No leverage, no assignment. You just told me the truth because the truth was in you and it wanted out."

"Casper—"

"So I'm not going to ask you to stay as payment. You don't owe me. If anything the ledger runs the other way, and I'm not interested in ledgers anymore—they're just miles with numbers on them." He looked at her directly, and she saw that his eyes were wet and that he wasn't ashamed of it. "I'm asking because I want you here. Not the thief I chased. Not Corvane's instrument. You. The woman who lost a brother twice and kept walking anyway. The woman who drank Corvane's distilled mercy secondhand through Brannoc's story and still flinches at kindness like it's a trap. I want the person who's going to exist now that nobody's writing her lines."

Ilsa stared at him. Her hands had gone still on the rail, and he realized he'd never seen her hands still before—in five years of pursuit and three weeks of alliance, she had always been in motion, always half-turned toward an exit. Now she stood rooted, and it frightened her visibly, being rooted.

"You don't know what you're asking," she said finally. "I spent five years being worth chasing. It's the only shape I trust. You take that away and I don't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't know what's left when nobody needs me to run."

"Then stay until you find out," Casper said. "No debt. No design. Just days."

The word hung between them. *Days.* Not miles. Not years, even—he wasn't presumptuous enough to offer years. Days. Counted one at a time, chosen one at a time, each one revocable, each one hers.

Ilsa looked out at the valley. The tide had reached its gentle crest; the water stood clear and bright in the streets, and the voices had softened from weeping into something like conversation, the whole settlement talking itself back into existence. An old woman was teaching a song to a girl on a doorstep. Someone had lit a cookfire in a house that had been underwater an hour ago, and smoke rose straight up in the windless air, and the smell of it—bread, someone was baking bread—drifted across the dock.

"My brother used to say," Ilsa began, and her voice caught on the name, which she had gotten back in the rapids along with everything else, "Dario used to say that freedom isn't having nowhere to be. It's having somewhere you'd choose." She swallowed. "I never understood him. I thought he was being soft."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was the smart one in the family, and I sold him out anyway, and he forgave me before I even remembered what I'd done." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like a woman closing accounts. "All right, Quintana. Days."

"Just days?"

"Just days." Then, because she was still Ilsa Vey, and five years of performance doesn't wash out in a week: "But I get the window bed at the waystation. And if you snore, I'm telling everyone in the valley. They'll believe me. I have a reputation."

Casper laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first one she'd ever heard from him, and possibly the first in five years—and the sound of it went out over the water and joined all the other sounds of the valley putting itself back together.

*

They spent the afternoon the way people spend afternoons when the world has ended and restarted: uselessly, wonderfully.

Marta the ferrywoman put them to work, because Marta put everyone to work, poling survivors and supplies through the new channels of the old town. Casper hauled crates of salt-fish from the upper storerooms while Ilsa, to his open astonishment, taught a knot-tying lesson to a ring of children who had appointed her their leader within minutes of discovering she could walk a rope up a drainpipe and over a roof beam. She denied enjoying it. The children knew better. Children always know better.

Toward evening the whole town gathered on the hillside above the waterline, hundreds of them, sharing food from cellars that the returning river had somehow left untouched—as if the water had known which stores to spare. Someone brought out fiddles. Someone else brought out a memory that was also, functionally, a dance, a reel that half the crowd remembered simultaneously and taught the other half by demonstration, laughter correcting the mistakes faster than any instructor could.

Casper sat with his back against a warm stone wall, Ilsa beside him, and let the evening happen to him.

His mother's voice was in his head—not the echo-salt counterfeit he'd lost in the rapids, but the true original, returned with the tide, complete with the slight rasp that came of thirty years of desert air. He tested it silently, the way you test a healed limb. *Casper. Come in out of the sun.* It held. It was hers. It cost nothing.

"You're smiling," Ilsa observed.

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late. I'm memorizing it. In case someone bottles it someday." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Though I suppose we've seen what happens to bottled things."

"What happens?"

"They come home," she said. "Eventually. If somebody breaks the seal."

Farther down the hillside, the ferrywoman was talking with a circle of neighbors, and Casper caught fragments—talk of rebuilding the lower streets, of the river's new course, of travelers already appearing on the southern roads. Word moved fast in a valley. Word of a broken Well moved faster. By spring, he guessed, people would come from every territory, seeking lost things: names, faces, songs, the locations of old mines and older graves. They would find, most of them, what the villagers of Merrow's Landing had found—that what was truly lost was rarely gone, only locked, and that the locks were mostly ones they'd built themselves, or agreed to, or failed to notice.

Some of them would need help. Guides. People who knew the river's temper, and the canyon routes, and the difference between a memory worth recovering and a past best left buried.

People, in short, like a ranger who had learned to stop measuring miles, and a thief who was learning there were directions other than away.

"They'll come, you know," Ilsa said quietly, following his gaze to the road. "Pilgrims. Seekers. The desperate ones worst of all."

"I know."

"Some of them will want what Corvane wanted. To drink deep. To live in yesterday." She turned to look at him. "What do we tell them?"

Casper considered it. Below them the fires burned, the fiddles played, the river ran bright and backward-free through its restored channel, carrying snowmelt down from the highlands at last in the proper direction, the way rivers were supposed to go—or maybe it still ran backward, maybe it always would, but now it ran backward generously, giving instead of taking.

"We tell them the truth," he said. "That we tried living in the past. Both of us. Professionally." He smiled. "And that the future has better rooms."

Ilsa laughed, and leaned her head briefly against his shoulder—an act of trust so small and so enormous that neither of them remarked on it—and together they watched the first stars come out over a valley full of returned voices, while somewhere below, in the rebuilt lamplight of Merrow's Landing, a child was asking her grandmother to start the story from the beginning, and the grandmother, who had all the time in the world, obliged.
