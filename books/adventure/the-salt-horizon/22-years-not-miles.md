## 22. Years, Not Miles

The waystation stood where the river forgot to be wild.

It had been a ferryman's house once—Merrow's Landing still called the stretch below it the Old Crossing—and Casper had rebuilt it plank by plank through the wet spring, learning the grain of river-timber the way he had once learned the grain of salt crust: by hand, by failure, by the slow accumulation of small knowledge that adds up to a life. The walls were silvered cedar. The roof was slate from the highlands, hauled down in Ilsa's boat over three trips she complained about the entire time and would have fought anyone who tried to take from her. A lantern hung above the door, green glass, lit every dusk.

Travelers came to the green lantern the way moths came to white ones.

They came on foot out of the recovering valley, on flatboats poled up from the coast, on mule trains from territories so far east they'd never heard of Corvane except as a cautionary tale told to children who hoarded things. They came because word had traveled—the way water travels, finding every low place—that at the waystation on the Old Crossing you could ask after what you'd lost. And because Casper Quintana had learned something in the year since the breaking, something no ranger manual had ever contained:

Most people don't want their lost things back. They want permission to stop looking.

He understood that better than any man alive. He'd spent five years proving it in the negative.

* * *

On the morning of the anniversary, the first traveler arrived before the mist lifted off the water—a broad woman in a fisher's oilskin, leading a boy of perhaps nine by the hand. The boy's eyes were red-rimmed and set with the particular stubbornness of a child who has decided grief is a project.

"He lost his dog," the woman said, when they were seated at the long table under the window, tea steaming in clay cups Ilsa had traded for in the Landing. "Last summer. Ran off during the second tide. We've walked the whole valley."

Casper looked at the boy. "What was his name?"

"Biscuit." The boy said it like an accusation, as though the world owed him better.

"And what did Biscuit do, mornings?"

The boy blinked. "What?"

"Mornings. Before anything else happened. What did he do?"

The boy's frown deepened, then wavered. "He... put his head on my feet. Under the blanket. Even in summer. He got too hot but he did it anyway."

"Did he do anything else? Something only he did?"

A long silence. Then, slowly, the boy began to talk—not about losing Biscuit but about having him: how he barked at the ferry bells and hid from goats; how he ate a whole boot once and was sick behind the woodpile and looked proud about it; how on cold nights he pressed his whole weight against the boy's back like a heated stone. He talked for the better part of an hour while his mother wept quietly into her tea, and when he finally ran out of words, Casper asked, "Do you feel like you've found him?"

The boy considered this with terrible seriousness. "I feel like I never lost him. Not all of him."

"That's how it works," Casper said. "The Well didn't give people their memories back. It gave them back to themselves. There's a difference, and it's the difference that matters."

The woman left coins on the table. Casper pushed them back. "Tell people in your territory the lantern's lit," he said. "That's payment enough."

After they'd gone, Ilsa leaned in the doorway drying her hands, watching him with the expression she wore when she thought he wasn't looking—the one that had taken him most of the year to learn to bear without flinching.

"You're good at that," she said.

"I'm good at knowing what people are actually chasing." He turned his cup. "Took me five years of practice on the wrong person."

"You weren't wrong about my habits."

"No," he agreed. "Just about everything else."

She laughed—short, real, unguarded—and went out to check the boat lines, and Casper sat a moment longer in the warm ruin of the morning, counting nothing at all.

* * *

Midday brought the Landing's ferrywoman, old Sera, poling her flat barge upriver with two passengers aboard and a cargo of bottled light for the winter market. She tied off at the station's dock without asking, the way she always did now, and accepted tea the way she always did—with a look that said she remembered refusing to speak to him once, and found her own past reticence amusing.

"The valley's talking," she said. "Down at the Landing. About the anniversary."

"Is it?"

"They want a marking. Bells, maybe. The children are making paper boats—they say the memories came back on the water, so the boats should go on the water." She sipped. "They'll come up here at dusk whether you invite them or not. You know how pilgrims are. Worse than fishermen."

"We'll light the lantern early," Ilsa said from the dock.

Sera studied them both a moment with those eyes that had recognized something in Casper's face before he knew it himself—his father's compass-bearing, the mapmaker's hunger, maybe just the family jaw. Whatever it had been, she'd never said, and he'd stopped needing her to.

"One more thing," she said, rising. "There's a traveler coming up from the south. Alone. Asked at the Landing whether the Magistrate's men ever come this far anymore." Her voice stayed level, but her hands stilled on the pole. "I told him the truth. That they don't. That the archive is broken and the citadel's new council burned the distilleries last autumn. But he kept asking. Like a man checking a wound to see if it's closed."

"Description?" Ilsa asked, and her voice had gone quiet in the old way, the way it used to go quiet on the salt flats when she was calculating distances and exits.

"Big. Gray at the temples. Walks like a man carrying furniture." Sera untied her line. "You'd know him, I think. Both of you."

Brannoc. Casper felt the name settle into the room like cold air under a door. The enforcer who had drunk distilled mercy once and could not un-remember compassion; who had let them pass above the gorge with a warning that had proven truer than any of Corvane's proclamations. They hadn't seen him since the breaking. Since the tide of returned memory had swept the valley and emptied the Magistrate of everything he'd stolen—including, presumably, whatever leverage had once held Brannoc's loyalty together.

"When?" Casper asked.

"Tomorrow. Maybe the day after. He's walking slow." Sera stepped onto her barge. "Like a man walking toward something he isn't sure wants him."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing for the vigil without speaking much of Brannoc. This was another thing the year had taught them: fear could share a house with joy if you didn't let either one run the kitchen. Ilsa mended the dock line and re-hung the storm shutters, which was her version of thinking. Casper split wood until his shoulders burned, which was his. Between them the work got done and the dread got carried, evenly, the way they carried everything now.

At dusk the Landing's people came upriver as Sera had promised—two flatboats crowded with lanterns and children and paper boats folded from old letters, old ledgers, old pages torn from books whose stories everyone had finally gotten back. The villagers moored along the bank and climbed to the station's clearing, and Casper watched the crowd form around the green-lit door and felt the thing he still hadn't gotten used to: being a fixed point. For five years he had been motion itself, a rumor in a dust cloud, a figure always receding. Now he was an address. People planned around him. Children ran to him with questions about the flats, about tracking, about whether the salt still sang at night. (It did, he told them. Quieter now. Like everything.)

Ilsa brought out the big kettle and the honey cakes she made badly and defended fiercely, and the evening filled up with the ordinary music of a valley remembering itself. An old man played a fiddle with two strings and made them suffice. Women who had spent a year relearning their own names argued cheerfully about recipes. Somewhere near the fire, a girl was telling the story of the breaking to younger children, and getting it wrong in all the ways that stories get improved—giving Casper a sword he'd never carried, giving Ilsa a speech she'd never made, giving the Well a roar instead of its actual sound, which had been a sigh, vast and relieved, like a held breath released across an entire territory.

Casper listened to the wrong version and found he loved it. True things didn't need guarding anymore. They could be bent and brightened and passed around like bread. That was what memory was for, when it wasn't a weapon: not accuracy. Company.

At full dark, they launched the paper boats.

Hundreds of them, candle-lit, sliding out onto the current that ran backward no longer—it ran forward now, obedient and plain, down toward the sea—and the whole bank fell silent as the little flames scattered downstream. Casper stood at the water's edge with Ilsa's shoulder against his, and in the reflection of the fires he saw, for a moment, faces that weren't there: his sister, laughing at something; his mother, saying his name in the voice he'd lost in the Rapids of the Forgotten and been given back freely, unbought, on the day the Well broke. His father, holding a compass that pointed at nothing anymore, because there was nothing left to find.

They come back on the water, the children said. So the boats go on the water.

He didn't weep. He was past needing to. But he stood there a long time, letting the faces visit, and when they faded he found Ilsa watching him with the firelight in her eyes and no pity in her face at all—only recognition, thief to thief, of what it costs to carry the dead and what it costs to set some of them down.

"An hour," she said softly.

"What?"

"You're doing it again. Counting." She nodded at the boats going out. "I can see it. You're measuring how long the vigil lasts, how many boats, how many people. Old habit."

"Am I?"

"You always know exactly how many miles are between things. It's why you were impossible to shake." She bumped her shoulder against his. "So tonight, don't count miles or boats or years. Count hours. Just today's. Tell me how many we've had."

Casper considered. The morning with the boy and the dog. The midday with Sera and the shadow of Brannoc. The wood-splitting, the kettle, the fiddle, the boats. The shoulder against his.

"Four good ones so far," he said. "And the night's not done."

"Then we'd best not waste the rest standing at the edge of it." She took his hand—no ceremony, no design, the way she did everything now, plainly—and drew him back toward the fire.

* * *

Brannoc came at dawn, as men who walk slowly toward uncertain welcomes often do.

Casper saw him first from the window: a big shape on the south road, gray-templed, moving with that deliberate heaviness Sera had described. Ilsa appeared at his elbow without a sound, a knife already loose in her sleeve out of pure reflex, and he touched her wrist and felt her ease by a single degree. Not relaxed. Willing to wait.

They met him at the gate, the three of them, in the pink light with the river talking behind everything.

Brannoc stopped ten paces out. Up close he looked hollowed—big frame gone slack, eyes that had once been instruments now searching for their purpose. He held something wrapped in oilcloth, and he stood turning it in his hands like a man who had rehearsed a speech on a long road and forgotten all of it.

"Magistrate's dead," he said finally. "Heart, in the winter. Small death. Small man, in the end, once the archive was gone. Just his own life left, and it wasn't enough to hold him up." He looked at the ground. "I stood guard outside his door when it happened. I could've gone in. I didn't. I'm not sure yet what that makes me."

"It makes you a man who let a tyrant die alone," Ilsa said, "instead of helping him. Most people never get the chance to make that choice. You took it."

Brannoc nodded slowly, as if setting the words down somewhere he could find them again. Then he unwrapped the oilcloth.

The compass.

His father's compass—the one that had pointed not north but toward memory, the key that had shattered the Well's seal—lay in Brannoc's broad palm, dented, salt-stained, alive. Casper stared at it. He had buried it a year ago at the dry well where the chase began, in the flats, a hundred leagues south. He had knelt in the salt crust and laid it down and walked away, and he had not looked back, because looking back was the old arithmetic.

"It came to me," Brannoc said. "I don't know how else to say it. After the breaking, when the memories flooded back—I started dreaming of it. Every night. Pointing. Not north. Toward—" he gestured vaguely at the river, the station, the two of them, "—here. I walked the flats and found it sitting on top of the salt where you buried it. Uncovered. Clean. Like it had dug itself out." His hand shook slightly. "I tried to leave it. I buried it twice more. It doesn't stay buried. So I carried it, because I thought—" he stopped, jaw working, "—because I thought maybe it wasn't done pointing. Maybe it was pointing at what I owe. And I owed you both a debt I couldn't pay in coin, so."

He held it out.

Casper didn't take it. He looked at the needle swinging gently in the dawn light, seeking, settling, seeking again—and understood, with a calm that surprised him, that it wasn't pointing at the station or at him or at any lost thing at all. It was pointing downriver. Toward the sea. Toward everything the returned memories would become as they spread outward into the world, generation by generation, changing shape, getting brighter and less accurate and more beloved. It was pointing forward. It had always been a key, and keys only point at doors.

"Keep it," Casper said.

Brannoc's head came up sharply.

"You heard the stories growing up same as everyone. You know what the Well cost this valley. You know better than most—you carried Corvane's orders in your own two hands." Casper closed the enforcer's fingers back over the compass. "It won't stay buried because it isn't meant to be buried. It's meant to move. Take it north past the highlands, or west past the coast, wherever the road takes you. When it stops pointing—if it ever stops—then you'll have arrived wherever you were going. Same as the rest of us."

For a long moment Brannoc said nothing. Then something moved through his ruined face that might have been the distilled mercy surfacing one more time, or might simply have been relief—the look of a man given permission to stop being what he was built to be.

"There's talk," he said gruffly, shouldering his pack. "In the eastern territories. Of other wells. Older ones. Nothing proven—smugglers' tales, mostly. But the council will send someone eventually to check. Someone official." He glanced between them, and for the first time in Casper's memory, the man almost smiled. "If they're smart, they'll send someone who knows the river. Someone who counts hours instead of miles."

He walked on north along the bank, big and slow, the compass riding in his pack like a small patient heart, and did not look back either.

Ilsa watched him go, then turned to Casper. "Other wells," she said.

"Smugglers' tales."

"Most true things start that way." She looked downriver, where the last of the anniversary candles had washed ashore against the pilings, still burning somehow, stubborn little flames in the reeds. "We could ignore it. Let someone else walk that road."

"We could."

She waited. He knew she was waiting; she'd learned his silences the way he'd once learned her trails, and she gave them room now instead of trying to outrun them.

Casper looked at the waystation behind them—the silvered walls, the green lantern guttering out its night shift, the long table where strangers unburdened themselves of searches they no longer needed. He looked at the river running plain and forward. He thought about the boy and the dog, about the difference between finding a thing and finding yourself able to hold it. He thought about five years measured in miles, and one year measured in hours, and which of the two had actually contained his life.

"Not yet," he said at last. "Not this year. If the council sends someone, we'll tell them what we know, which is where the river goes and what it costs. And if the tales turn out to be true—well." He took Ilsa's hand. "We keep a waystation. Waystations are where journeys start. Nobody says they have to be somebody else's."

Isla smiled—the flat, crooked, entirely unsentimental smile he had chased across a desert for five years without ever once seeing it aimed at him, and which he now received daily, free of charge, no pursuit required.

"Hours, then," she said. "Starting now."

"Starting now," he agreed, and turned with her back toward the door, toward the kettle and the morning's first travelers, toward the long, unhurried, unmeasured country of the years ahead—which, he was beginning to understand, would be enough. More than enough. They would be, at last, exactly as many as he needed.
