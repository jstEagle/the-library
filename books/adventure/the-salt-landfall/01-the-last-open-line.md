## 1. The Last Open Line

The maintenance car had a name once—someone had painted *LITTLE WREN* on its brass plate in looping script—but the salt air had eaten the letters down to ghosts, and Sable Oyelaran had long since stopped reading them. He called her the Mule, because she was stubborn, ugly, and carried more than she should have to.

She rattled along the coastal spur at a walking pace, which was all she could manage and all he wanted. The line here hadn't seen a scheduled train in eleven years. Grass grew up between the ties in pale tufts, silvered by wind, bending away from the sea like a congregation leaning from a sermon. To his left the land fell away in terraces of dune grass and rusted fence wire; to his right, beyond a crumbling retaining wall, the ocean worked at the cliffs with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry.

Sable rode standing, one hand on the tiller lever, one foot rocking the pump bar in a lazy rhythm that kept the Mule's flywheel humming. His coat was oilcloth gone gray as driftwood, his boots were two different ages of leather stitched together by his own hand, and his face—the parts visible under the brim of a hat that had survived three storms and one fire—was the kind of face port towns forgot before the ferry finished unloading. That was deliberate. Faces were currency, and he spent them carefully.

Ahead, the rail gleamed wrong.

He saw it a hundred meters out: a shine where there should have been none, a bright scar across the brown of neglected steel. He brought the Mule down to a crawl, set the brake pawl, and stepped off into grass that came up past his knees.

Kneeling, he ran his thumb along the railhead. A crack. Hairline, running diagonal from the web up through the running surface, maybe forty centimeters of it, hidden until traffic or weather or plain bad luck polished it open. On an active line it would have been flagged, ground, replaced. Here it was just another wound nobody would ever come to dress.

"Nobody," Sable said aloud, to no one, "except me."

He talked to himself on the line. Eleven years of talking to himself. It was either that or listen to the sea, and the sea only ever said the same thing: *keep going, keep going, keep going.*

He unloaded the tools.

It took him the better part of four hours. He jacked the rail, drilled the crack's ends to stop the propagation, fishplated the joint with hardware salvaged from a signal box two miles back—he'd stripped that box last spring, and felt mildly guilty about it even now, though the box had been dead longer than some of the towns it used to serve. He torqued every bolt twice, checked gauge with his worn brass calipers, and packed fresh ballast under the sleepers with the flat of a shovel older than he was.

When it was done he stood back, wiped his hands on his thighs, and looked down the empty line running north into haze.

A perfect repair on a dead road. A doctor performing surgery on a ghost.

"You're a fool, Oyelaran," he told himself, but he was already loading the tools back onto the Mule, because the alternative was leaving a crack in the rail behind him, and he had learned—had paid tuition, in blood and worse—to know exactly which things he could leave behind and which things followed him, whispering, all night.

Cracked rails followed. So did unfinished work. Everything else, he could walk away from clean.

* * *

The town was called Merrow's Rest, though the sign at the platform read MERROW'S RE T, the S having fallen sometime in the last decade, and the rest having eroded to a suggestion. Twelve houses, a cannery with a collapsed roof, a chapel whose bell had been sold to pay for a seawall that the sea had eaten anyway. And a station—a real one, stone-footed, slate-roofed, built back when the coast line mattered, when the empire had poured money into these rails like wine into a wedding cup.

The stationmaster was sweeping the platform when Sable rolled the Mule in at dusk.

He watched her do it for a while before announcing himself, the way he always did. Old man, seventy if he was sixty, sweeping grit off stone that no train would grace, in long unhurried strokes, the broom handle worn smooth and dark where his hands lived. There was a lamp lit inside the station office. There were flowers in a clay pot on the platform bench. Geraniums, red as signal lamps.

Somebody still swept this platform every day for trains that didn't come.

Sable knew what that meant. He'd met a dozen of these men and women up and down the coast—the ones the railroad had forgotten to release, pensionless, purposeless, keeping their stations like widows keep houses. They were the loneliest people he knew, and he knew loneliness the way a cartographer knows coastline.

Evening found him in the stationmaster's kitchen, because the old man—Brayle, his name was Brayle—had appeared at the Mule's side as Sable was making camp and said, "You fixed the crack at Kilometer Forty-Seven."

"I fixed a crack."

"At Forty-Seven. I've walked that line every Sunday for six years. I know every flaw in her like I knew my wife's moods." Brayle had a voice like gravel settling. "Come eat. You'll want food after working iron all day, and I've got stew enough for two, and company enough for neither."

The stew was mutton and turnip and too much salt, and it was the best thing Sable had eaten in a month. He ate three bowls and pretended not to notice Brayle counting.

"You're a trackman," Brayle said. Not a question.

"I'm passing through."

"Passing through men don't drill stop-holes in cracks. Passing through men ride over flaws and thank God they made it." The old man leaned back, chair creaking. "Where'd you learn? Empire mainline school?"

"Here and there."

"Hm." Brayle let it go the way a man lets go a fish too small to keep, but Sable saw him note the weight of it. "There's thirty kilometers of spur south of here. Branches off toward the old quarry at Vell. Line's sound under the rust—sounder than this main stretch, truth be told, because nobody's run anything heavy enough to hurt it. If a man wanted to put in a season's work, he could bring twenty kilometers of dead road back to life."

"For what?"

"For the cannery trucks. For the mail sledge in winter. For the simple satisfaction of it." Brayle's eyes were pale gray, the color of the sea before weather. "For the sake of a line being open."

Sable set down his spoon. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast and was worrying the eaves. Somewhere down the coast a buoy clanged, patient as a heartbeat.

"You sweep this platform every day," Sable said. "Why?"

Brayle didn't take offense. He looked out the dark window a long moment. "Because the day I stop is the day it's truly dead. And I'm not ready for it to be truly dead. Are you?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do." The old man gathered the bowls, stacked them with the care of a man who owned few things and honored each. "Stay the season. Room's above the office—there's a bed in it, hasn't been slept in since my wife died, but it's a good bed. Pay's nothing, because there's nothing to pay with, but there's food, and there's work worth doing, and there's—" he paused, choosing the word with visible effort, "—company."

The word sat on the table between them like a coin someone had slid across.

Sable looked at it. He was very good at looking at things without touching them.

"I move on tomorrow," he said. "First light."

Brayle nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that somehow made it worse. "I thought so. Your kind always does."

"My kind."

"The kind that fixes things and won't stay fixed to them." The old man carried the bowls to the basin and began washing them, his back a stooped question mark against the lamplight. "I've buried a wife and two sons, Oyelaran—I know your name's not really whatever name you gave me, doesn't matter—and I'll tell you what I learned at the gravesides. Running out of time isn't the worst thing. Running out of *reasons* is. You've got reasons. I can see them dragging behind you like a broken coupler. One day you'll stop dragging them or they'll stop dragging you, and either way, you'll wish you'd chosen instead of just endured."

Sable said nothing. He climbed the narrow stair to the room above the office, lay down on the dead wife's bed, and stared at the ceiling beams until the lamp below went out.

He did not sleep. He listened to the sea say *keep going*, and somewhere around the third hour he realized he was also listening for something else—for footsteps on the platform, for a voice calling up the stairs, for any reason at all to stay—and hearing none, he hated how much he'd wanted to hear one.

That was the ache of it. That was the whole rotten heart of it. Loneliness chosen was still loneliness; it just came with the extra weight of knowing the door had been locked from the inside, by his own hand, every single time.

* * *

He was up before dawn, packing the Mule by lamplight, moving quiet as respect for the sleeping house.

But Brayle was already on the platform. Of course he was. Standing at the edge in his coat, breath fogging, holding something wrapped in cloth.

"Track grease," the old man said, handing it over. "Rendered tallow and graphite. My own mix. Better than what you're using—I could tell by the squeal on your axle."

Sable took it. It weighed more than grease should have. Inside the cloth, beneath the tin, was bread and hard cheese wrapped separately, enough for three days.

"Brayle—"

"There's a culvert at Kilometer Sixty-One, on the south spur, where the drainage cuts under the embankment. Stone-faced, empire-built, still solid." The old man's voice was flat, practical, giving directions and nothing else. "If weather turns, you can shelter under it. Keeps the wind off."

Sable looked at him. The east was going gray behind the station roof; the geraniums on the bench stood dark and silent in the half-light. "You're telling me where to shelter."

"I'm telling you where the culvert is. What a man does with a culvert is his own affair." Brayle stuck out his hand, and Sable took it, and the grip was iron and dry and lasted one second longer than strangers' grips last. "Whatever name you're wearing this month—it suits you fine. Safe rails, trackman."

Sable climbed onto the Mule, rocked the pump bar, and got the flywheel singing. At the end of the platform he made the mistake of looking back. Brayle was already sweeping. Long strokes, unhurried, grit off stone, for trains that weren't coming, in light that wasn't yet day.

Sable turned his face north and did not look back again, because looking back was a habit he could not afford, and because if he looked back twice he might not leave at all, and then who would he be? The man on the Mule was the only self he had left that fit. A man who stayed became a man who could be found. A man who could be found became a man who owed. And owing was the one cargo he had sworn, eleven years ago, in a season he did not name even to himself, never to carry again.

The line curved north along the cliff top. The sun came up over the water behind him, laying his shadow out ahead on the rails like a warning, or an escort.

At Kilometer Fifty-Four he smelled smoke on the wind—not hearth smoke, not the sweet green reek of burning kelp the shore folk burned. Something sharper. Char and hot metal, riding the northeast wind from the direction he'd come.

He stopped the Mule and stood a long moment, listening.

Behind him, far down the coast, a smudge rose against the brightening sky. Too far to read. Fires happened. Canneries burned, chapels burned, careless men burned their own fields.

But Sable had spent eleven years learning to read the coast the way other men read faces, and there was a grammar to smoke, and this smoke said *deliberate*. This smoke said somebody was cleaning up after themselves, or clearing ground for something coming, and both possibilities moved a cold finger down his spine.

He pumped harder. The Mule groaned up to her best speed, such as it was, and the wheels took up their rhythm against the rail he had mended with his own hands—the rail that now, he understood with a certainty he couldn't explain and didn't try to, he was riding toward something rather than away.

At Kilometer Sixty-One, where the drainage culvert cut under the embankment, Sable Oyelaran found a dying man.
