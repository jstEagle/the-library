## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The culvert was singing.

Sable heard it before he saw it—a thin, wet whistle threading through the wind off the flats, the sound of air pushed through a gap too small for it. He'd spent eleven years listening to rails and rolling stock, to the language of metal under strain, and he knew that sound the way a mother knows a child crying in another room. Something was breathing where nothing should breathe.

He brought the maintenance car to a stop with a squeal of hand-brake and sat a moment in the gray morning light, one boot braced on the lever, listening. The spur he'd been following had been closed for six years—officially decommissioned when the coastal line's charter lapsed and the empire's accountants decided salt water ate more profit than it carried—but the roadbed was still good under the rust, and Sable had learned long ago that dead lines were the safest places for a man who didn't want to be found. Nobody watched a line that wasn't on the books. Nobody asked questions at a station that no longer existed.

Except today something in the drainage culvert beneath the embankment was asking for him without words.

He climbed down with his tool bag over his shoulder, boots crunching on ballast gone soft with salt bloom. The culvert mouth opened out of the embankment like a throat, three feet of corrugated iron running back into darkness under the rail bed, built half a century ago to carry runoff from the high ground down to the flats. Water should have been trickling out of it. Instead there was only that whistle of moving air—and beneath it, fainter, a sound that raised every hair on Sable's arms.

A man groaning.

Sable crouched at the culvert mouth. Inside, twenty feet back where the iron gave way to old stone masonry, there was a shape. He could make out legs first, sprawled and wrong, then the pale smear of a face turned toward the light.

"Hey." His voice came out flat, careful. "You alive in there?"

The shape moved. A hand rose, trembling, and beckoned.

Every instinct Sable owned told him to walk away. That was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall: keep moving, keep your name short, keep your debts at zero and your attachments at none. A dying man in a culvert was somebody else's problem, and becoming involved meant becoming known, and being known meant being found, and being found—

The hand beckoned again. And the man said, in a voice scraped down to gravel: "You're a rail man. I can tell. You stopped."

Sable swore quietly at himself, at the morning, at whatever god arranged these things, and crawled into the dark.

* * *

Up close the man was worse than the shape had promised. Sixty-odd years old, maybe, or fifty worn to look it—weathered brown as old rope, with a prospector's beard going gray in patches and eyes that were still shockingly clear, still burning, even as the rest of him guttered. He'd been shot low in the belly; Sable could see the wound without wanting to, a dark soaked mass below a leather coat, and the smell that came off it was the smell he'd learned to dread in the war years, sweet and rotten at once. The man had propped himself against the culvert's stone throat with a coil of rope under his head, and around him lay the archaeology of a hard life: a canvas pack slit open, a dented tin cup, a lantern with its glass cracked, and scattered papers that might once have been charts.

"Gut-shot," the man said, before Sable could ask. "Two days now. Or three. Hard to count down here." He managed something that was almost a smile. "You're the first thing with a heartbeat that's come along since."

"Who did this?"

"Does it matter?" The man coughed, and the cough cost him visibly, his whole frame shuddering against the stones. "Matter is you stopped. Rail men don't stop. Not anymore. Not on the dead lines." He studied Sable with those clear eyes, cataloguing—the tool bag, the grease-stained cuffs, the way Sable knelt balanced and ready the way men do who've slept rough too long. "Name's Ede. Ede Korrish."

"Sable." Just the one name. It was all he ever gave.

"Sable." Ede turned the word over like a coin he was checking for shave. "Well, Sable. I'm going to die in this culvert. I've made my peace with the arithmetic of it. What I haven't made peace with—" he stopped, jaw working, and for a moment the burning eyes went somewhere far away, down some other dark tunnel, "—is the rest of it. The part that's still owed."

"I can get you to a doctor," Sable said, though they both knew it was a lie. The nearest one was two days back along the coast, and this man had maybe hours.

Ede laughed—a real laugh, shocking in its warmth, cut short by another cough. "A doctor. That's kind. No. Listen to me, rail man, because I don't have the wind for speeches and I'm going to make you a bargain whether you like it or not." He fumbled at his coat with a hand that shook like a compass needle near iron, and Sable, hating himself, reached out and helped him—hating the touch, hating the intimacy of it, hating how natural his own hands made the motion.

From an inner pocket Ede drew a cylinder wrapped in oilcloth and sealed at both ends with wax gone amber with age. He held it in both palms like something consecrated.

"You know the Meridian Star?"

Everyone on the coast knew the Meridian Star, the way everyone knew a ghost story. Salvage wreck, forty years down, lying out in the Salt Flats where the old seabed line ran before the empire flooded the shallows and abandoned the route. A treasure ship, the stories said. A fortune in gold, sunk within sight of land, guarded by tides and silt and the simple fact that nothing stayed found out there.

"Storybook stuff," Sable said.

"Storybooks are written by people who got paid." Ede's grin was crooked. "I found her. Six years I worked the flats, charting currents, diving the slack waters, following a trail of documents I'd rather not tell you how I came by. And six months ago I put my hands on her hull." His voice dropped, went reverent and ragged at once. "There's an air pocket in her, Sable. Old wreck, deep hull, trapped air in the aft compartments where she settled bow-down. It's dying—the tide works at it every cycle, eats a little more room. Six tides from the next spring low, maybe less, and the sea takes the last dry space aboard. After that she's just another hole in the water."

Sable looked at the wax-sealed cylinder. He could feel the shape of what was coming, the way he could feel a storm in his knee. "And you want me to dive her."

"I want you to do something harder than diving." Ede's hand closed over Sable's wrist, grip shockingly strong for a dying man. "I have a partner. Had. Dess Corvane. We found her together, and then Dess decided a share wasn't enough, and I woke up in this culvert with his knife work in my gut and my pack slit for the charts." His breath whistled. "He'll come looking for the map. He knows I kept the master copy separate. He's patient, Dess. Patient as rot."

"So give him the map."

"And let him take it all? Let him sell my daughter's birthright to the Vane fleet?" Something flared in Ede's face, fierce and young. "No. Here's the bargain, rail man, and hear all of it before you say no, because I can see no forming on your face like weather."

* * *

Sable tried to stand up. The culvert was too low; he cracked his head on the corrugated iron and sat back down hard, which drew another broken laugh out of Ede.

"Sit," Ede said. "Listen."

"I'm not a delivery service. I'm not a hero. I fix track and I move on."

"I know exactly what you are. You're a man running so fast he hasn't noticed he left everything behind." Ede said it without cruelty, which was worse. "I was that man once. Forty years ago. Then Wren was born and I had a reason to stop, except I never learned how, so I told myself I was providing. Out here, hunting, always one more season, one more strike, and then I'd go home rich and be a father worth the name." He looked down at the cylinder between them. "She'll be twenty-three now. Port Calloway. Her mother died when she was nine, and after that it was just letters. Letters and promises. 'Next season, little bird. Next strike.' Twenty-three years of next strikes."

The whistle of air through the culvert mouth filled the silence. Somewhere above them, out on the flats, a curlew cried its thin falling note.

"The map shows the approach channel, the tide windows, the hull breach, the safe. The safe is the point—not the loose gold, though there's gold, plenty, take your share and welcome. In the captain's safe there are papers. Charter papers. What the Meridian Star was really carrying when she went down." Ede's eyes fixed on Sable's, and the burning in them was fever now, but underneath the fever was bedrock. "You bring my share to Wren Korrish, Port Calloway, the net-menders' row by the fish gate. You tell her—" his voice caught, actually caught, and the big weathered hand trembled against the stone, "—you tell her her father was coming home. That he was always coming home. That he loved her every day he was gone, even the days he didn't write. Can you say that? Can you carry words that heavy?"

"No," Sable said honestly.

"Good. Honest men make better couriers than liars." Ede pressed the cylinder into Sable's hands. It was warm from the dying man's grip, and heavier than wax and paper had any right to be. "Six tides, Sable. Spring low comes fast this month. If you're not through the breach before the sixth tide, the air pocket's gone and Dess or the Vane fleet or the sea itself takes everything. So you run. I know you know how to run. Run toward something for once."

"I didn't agree to anything."

"You crawled into my culvert." Ede smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of blood and peace. "That's agreeing. That's how it starts, every time. Somebody stops."

* * *

They talked while the light in the culvert mouth shifted from gray to white to the long amber of late afternoon. Sable told himself, the whole time, that he hadn't agreed—that he was only waiting out the man's last hours because leaving felt worse than staying, and that feeling meant nothing, feelings were weather, and he'd built his whole life on the understanding that weather passes.

Ede made him recite the tide tables twice. Made him repeat the approach: the drowned signal box on the old seabed line, third piling past the box, follow the chain down. Made him swear—not on any god, Ede didn't ask for gods, but on the rails themselves, which Ede seemed to think was the sturdier oath. "Track doesn't lie," he said. "A rail either holds the weight or it doesn't. Swear on that."

Sable swore. He heard his own voice do it, steady and flat, and felt something shift in his chest like a plate settling in a bridge under load—a small structural failure, invisible from outside, that would matter later.

Near dusk Ede began to fade. The burning eyes dimmed to coals. He talked less about maps and more about a girl mending nets with her mother's quick hands, about a house with blue shutters he'd never finished paying for, about the smell of bread on a street called Fish Gate Row. Sable sat in the dark beside him and listened, and did not leave, and understood with cold clarity that he was already lost—that the moment he'd crawled into this culvert, some account had been opened in his name that he would spend the rest of this story trying to settle.

"Water," Ede whispered, near the end.

Sable held the dented tin cup to his lips. Ede drank a little, and looked up at him with the last of his clarity.

"She'll be angry," he said. "At first. At you, for being the messenger instead of me. Let her be angry. Anger means she hoped." His breath rattled. "Then she'll grieve. Stay for that too, if you can bear it. And then—" the ghost of the crooked smile, "—then show her the map, rail man. Show her what her father found. Not the gold. The truth of it. She deserves to know what I really was."

"What were you?"

Ede Korrish looked past him, up through the culvert mouth at the first stars, and his answer was almost too quiet to catch:

"Coming home."

He died an hour after full dark, between one rattle of breath and the next, so gently that Sable didn't notice for several minutes. Then he noticed, and sat alone in the culvert with a dead man's map in his lap and the tide whispering somewhere out beyond the embankment, counting itself down.

* * *

He buried Ede above the high-tide line, in the soft ground behind the embankment where the salt grass grew, digging with the same shovel he used for ballast and taking longer about it than efficiency required. He marked the grave with a length of scrap rail driven upright into the earth, because a rail man deserved a rail, and stood over it for a while in the wind saying nothing, because there was nothing in him equal to the occasion.

Then he climbed back onto the maintenance car and unwrapped the cylinder under the weak glow of his hand lamp.

Inside the wax seal: a chart of the Salt Flats drawn in a prospector's careful hand, annotated in three colors of ink, dense with depths and tide windows and current arrows—and beneath it, folded twice, a letter addressed in a rounder, older hand:

*To Wren. From your father. Open when he cannot.*

Sable stared at it for a long time. The lamp hissed. Out across the dark flats, the sea was turning over, beginning its long patient pull toward the spring low.

Six tides.

He thought about setting the whole thing down right there—map, letter, grave, all of it—and rolling north until the coast ran out of coast. It would be easy. It was the only thing he was good at.

Instead he found himself doing the arithmetic, the engineer's reflex he couldn't kill: distance to Port Calloway, distance to the flats, the tide tables Ede had drilled into him, the hours in a day. The numbers assembled themselves into a route, and the route became a plan, and the plan sat in his chest like a weight he'd agreed to carry.

"Damn you, old man," he said quietly, to the fresh-turned earth. "You stopped me."

He turned the maintenance car south, toward the flats, and opened the throttle, and the dead line sang under his wheels all the way into the dark.
