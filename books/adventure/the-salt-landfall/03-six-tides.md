## 3. Six Tides

The lamp was a bad one. Sable had bought it in Harrow's Bend for two coppers off a woman who swore it burned clean, and it did not, and he'd known it wouldn't, because nothing in the world cost two coppers and worked right. But its flame was steady enough, and the wick was long enough, and by midnight the wax-sealed map lay flat on the plank table of his rented room with the light pooling over it like something spilled.

He hadn't wanted to open it.

That was the truth he kept circling, the way his thumb kept circling the edge of the wax seal without breaking it. Ede Korrish had died holding this thing against his chest like a child, blood coming up through his shirt in slow pulses, and every minute Sable had spent pressing the wound closed had been a minute he'd known—known the way an engineer knows when a boiler's gone past saving—that the man was already leaving. The map wasn't payment. It was a handoff. And Sable had taken it because a dying man shouldn't die begging.

Now it sat there under the bad lamp, and opening it felt less like unwrapping a gift than like cutting into something that might still be alive.

He broke the seal anyway. He was, whatever else, a practical man.

The map was drawn on salt-cured hide, the kind that stayed supple for decades if you kept it dry, and it was good work—better than good. Ede Korrish had been more than a prospector; he'd been a surveyor once, or trained by one, because the lines were ruled true and the depths were marked in a hand that understood what depths meant. The old coastal rail line ran down the left margin like a spine, every station named, several of them crossed out in different inks at different times—a record of closures, of lines dying one by one as the empire pulled back from the coast and let the salt take them. Sable knew those stations. He'd ridden past their skeletons for six years. Seeing them inked here, alive and named, made something in his chest pull tight and refuse to explain itself.

And there, where the rail line met the drowned country—the Salt Flats, the seabed that the tide gave back twice a day like a creditor who couldn't quite decide to forgive the debt—Ede had drawn the wreck.

*Meridian Star.* A salvage hulk, sunk forty years back with her holds sealed, sitting in a pocket of trapped air beneath the flats like a held breath. Ede had marked her position three ways: by the ruined signal box on the old seabed line, by a leaning channel marker, and by soundings. Three ways, because one way could fail. That was surveyor's discipline. That was a man who'd spent years getting ready to come back to this spot and had never stopped preparing for the trip.

Sable sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the stationmaster's dog barked twice at nothing and gave it up.

He should burn it. The thought arrived fully formed, cold and reasonable, wearing his own voice. Burn the map, forget the promise, ride north on the morning freight and be nobody again by noon. The Vane fleet was real—he'd seen their masts in Port Calloway harbor, black and patient as a row of knives—and men who hunted wrecks for a living did not stop hunting because a stranger asked them to. Every hour this map existed, it was a weight tied to him. And Sable Oyelaran had spent six years learning exactly how little weight a man could carry and still move fast.

But the promise came with it. That was the trap Ede had built, whether he'd meant to or not: the map and the daughter, Wren of Port Calloway, the share that belonged to her. Burn the map and the promise died with it, and then Ede Korrish would have died holding out his whole life's work to a man who threw it on the fire before the body was cold.

Sable looked at his hands. They were still faintly stained from the culvert, from the pressure he'd kept on a stranger's gut while the stranger talked about his girl—how she could mend a net faster than any woman on the crew, how she had her mother's patience and his own stubbornness, God help everyone, how she'd be angry, she'd be so angry, but she'd understand, she'd know he tried—

He hadn't promised quickly. He remembered that. He'd made the man say it twice, made him name the port, name the daughter, name the share, because a promise given carelessly was a lie with better manners. And then Ede had smiled—not grateful, *relieved*, like a man setting down a load he'd carried too far—and said, "Knew you would. You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a man who fixes things other people broke."

Sable had wanted to tell him he was wrong. He'd fixed rails and couplings and water pumps for six years, and every one of them had belonged to someone else's life. He'd never once fixed his own.

He got out the tide tables instead of thinking about it further.

* * *

The harbormaster's office in Harrow's Bend sold tide tables for a copper, and Sable had bought the current quarter along with lamp oil and hard bread, telling himself it was habit, telling himself a man who worked the coast needed to know the water even if he never touched it. Now he spread the tables beside the map and began, in the way that steadied him most completely in the world, to calculate.

This was the work he loved and would not admit to loving: numbers behaving themselves. Tide heights in columns. The flats' exposure windows marked in the margins by some previous owner in a fussy pencil hand. The Meridian Star's air pocket, per Ede's notes, flooded on anything above a nine-foot tide—and the tables said the tides were building. Not spring tides yet, but climbing toward them, each one taller than the last, the sea leaning in like a listener.

He counted.

Six. Six usable low-water windows before the tides climbed past nine feet and stayed there, drowning the approach channels, sealing the wreck's pocket under water too deep and too stirred to dive. After that, the Meridian Star would keep her secret through the whole dark season—and the Vane fleet would have all winter to sweep the flats with their sonar arrays, methodical as a comb through hair, until they found her.

Six tides. Twelve days, near enough. And the first window opened tomorrow at dawn.

Sable did the distances next, because the numbers were the only part of this that didn't frighten him. Harrow's Bend to the flats' edge: two days by the maintenance car if the track held, and the track on the closed spur was a coin flip at best. Then the crossing itself—Ede's notes were specific, almost tender, about the crossing. *Go at slack water. Follow the old line's ballast ridge; it stands above the silt. Do not trust the channel marker; it leans a degree too far east since the storm of '89.* Two days' work to reach the signal box, half a day through it if the mechanism still turned, and then the wreck.

Four days out. Four days back, against a rising tide schedule that would eat his margin like rust ate iron. No room for error. No room for weather. No room for the fleet.

He set down the pencil and found his hand wasn't entirely steady, and he was annoyed at it, the way he was always annoyed at his own body for insisting on having feelings.

Because here was the arithmetic underneath the arithmetic, the sum he'd been avoiding since the culvert: the Vane fleet didn't need six tides. Ilsa Vane ran sonar sweeps on a grid pattern—he'd heard the salvagers talk in Port Calloway taverns, back when he'd been dumb enough to sit in taverns—and a grid pattern over the flats would cross the wreck's coordinates within days unless someone moved first. And moving first meant being seen, and being seen meant leading the fleet straight to the one thing Ede Korrish had spent eleven years protecting.

Unless he went alone. Unless he went quiet, no crew, no witnesses, no wagon tracks wider than a maintenance car's, in and out inside four windows with the gold—or whatever Ede had actually found down there—wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to his back like any other tool.

Alone. The word fit him the way a well-worn coupling fits a pin. Alone was the shape his life had been cut to, deliberately, carefully, the way you'd cut a key. He'd learned the craft of it: leave before they learn your name, work for food not wages, sleep in the maintenance sheds rather than the inns, be useful and be gone. Six years without a single person knowing where he slept twice running. It had cost him things he didn't itemize anymore, because itemizing was how you started wanting them back.

And now a dying man had handed him a daughter's inheritance and a deadline measured in tides, and every instinct he owned said: *run alone, run fast, run light.*

So why did he keep looking at the map's margin?

Because Ede had written there. Small, cramped, in the last ink of the last entry: *Wren reads water better than me. Never told her enough.* And beneath that, a single line that had nothing to do with surveying at all:

*If you're reading this, you're the second man I ever trusted. Don't make me wrong.*

Sable read it four times. The lamp guttered, caught, guttered again. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, smelling of salt and distance, the smell that lived in everything on this coast, the smell that had gotten into him so deep he sometimes thought it was the only honest thing about him.

"Damn you, old man," he said quietly, to a room with no one in it.

It wasn't sentiment. He made himself be clear about that. It was tactics. A dead prospector's trust was worth nothing on the open market—but a promise kept was currency everywhere, and Sable had survived six years on the coast by understanding what things were worth. Deliver the share to Wren Korrish, and he'd have done the one clean thing available to him. That was all. That was the whole ledger. He was not going to care about her. He was not going to linger in Port Calloway afterward being thanked. He would hand over the share, tell her how her father died—bravely, uselessly, the way most men died out here—and he would be on the northbound freight before she finished crying, because staying for grief was how people got attached to you, and attachment was a debt, and debts were how a man stopped moving.

He told himself all of it, in order, like reciting a coupling inspection. And it mostly held. It mostly held the way a patched boiler holds—well enough at low pressure, in a cool room, with nobody watching.

Then he blew out the lamp, and in the dark the fear arrived on schedule.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He packed instead, because packing was a form of prayer for men like him: everything in its place, everything weighed, everything chosen. Tools first, always tools—wrenches, the good pry bar, spare valves, a coil of wire rope. Rations for five days, though the trip was eight. Water in the steel cans, filled to the brim so he could hear if they leaked. The map rolled in oilcloth and lashed inside his coat, against his chest, where Ede had kept it.

At some point he stood at the window and watched the last of the night drain out of the sky over the flats, and admitted the thing he'd been not-admitting since the culvert.

He was afraid.

Not of the fleet—Ilsa Vane's cutters were dangerous, but danger was just physics with teeth, and physics could be solved. Not of the crossing, either; the flats killed careless men, but he had not been careless in six years. What he was afraid of was smaller and older than both. He was afraid of the moment on the flats when the tide turned and the water came back across the seabed faster than a horse could gallop, and he would be standing alone on a drowned railroad line with a dead man's map, and there would be no one—no one—to call to. He had chosen that. He had chosen it so many times it had worn smooth, like a handle. But choosing it again, now, with Ede's handwriting pressed against his chest, felt less like freedom than like walking into the sea on purpose.

"You've got the look," the old man had said. *A man who fixes things other people broke.*

Sable picked up his pack. It weighed what it always weighed. It felt heavier than it had ever weighed, and he knew perfectly well that the difference was not in the straps.

Downstairs, the stationmaster was already awake—old men on the coast kept farmers' hours even with no trains to meet—and had coffee on the stove, and a job offer rehearsed and waiting, Sable could see it in his face, the permanent post, the cottage, the small decent life laid out like track ahead of an engine.

Sable took the coffee. He drank it standing. He set down the cup, and said, "There's a spur south of here that needs three ties replaced before it'll hold a car," which was not an answer, and was also the only answer he had.

The stationmaster looked at him for a long moment with eyes that had watched forty years of men come through and keep going. "Tide'll be low at dawn," the old man said. "If a man was fool enough to cross the flats, that's when he'd start."

"That's when," Sable agreed, and went out into the gray light to check the wheels of his maintenance car, and did not look back at the warm room, because looking back was the first rivet in a chain, and he could not afford a single link.

The flats waited south of town, vast and pale and glistening, holding their breath between tides. Somewhere out there under the silt, the Meridian Star kept her long-held breath of air, and somewhere beyond her, black sails were already sliding down the coast like a closing hand.

Six tides.

Sable pumped the handcar lever, and the wheels began to turn, and he ran—alone, fast, and lighter than he'd ever feel again—straight into the counting.
