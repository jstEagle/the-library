## 4. Wren of Port Calloway

The nets came in at dawn smelling of iron and rot, and Wren Korrish mended them the way her father had taught her: fast hands, slow heart.

"Left hand leads," she murmured to herself, working a torn mesh back together with a bone shuttle worn smooth by two generations of Korrish fingers. "Right hand follows. Don't fight the twine. Let it tell you where it wants to go."

Around her, the net-mending crew of Port Calloway worked on the long stone quay in a loose semicircle, backs to the water, faces to the low morning sun. Old Marta Two-Tides sat at the far end with her knees propped on a salt barrel, her fingers moving through the mesh without her eyes ever leaving the harbor mouth. Tam Ossler, seventeen and all elbows, kept snagging his shuttle and swearing under his breath until his grandmother cuffed him without looking up. The gulls wheeled and screamed over the fish sheds, and somewhere behind the smokehouses a dog was barking at nothing, the way it did every morning, as if it had made a solemn career of it.

Wren had been mending these nets since she was six years old. She knew every knot in her hands the way other girls knew prayers. And like every knot, they held a shape that could not be undone—only worked with, only carried forward.

Her father had taught her that too. *Everything that tears can be tied again,* Ede Korrish used to say, holding up a broken net so she could see the light through the hole. *You just have to find where the ends went.*

He'd been gone eleven months now. Eleven months past the day he was due home from the flats, past the last letter that smelled of pipe smoke and cheap ink, promising—*this one's the big one, little wren, this one buys us the boat and the dry dock and your mother's headstone carved proper*—promising the way he always promised, which was completely, and never quite truthfully.

She tied off the repair, bit the twine clean, and let the mended section fall into her lap. Through the hole she'd just closed, for half a second, she could see the gray water of the harbor. Then the mesh settled and the hole was gone, and there was only net.

That was the trick of grief, she'd learned. You didn't fix it. You just kept closing holes until you forgot which ones were yours.

"Wren." Marta Two-Tides hadn't turned her head. "Harbor master's got a stranger asking after you."

Wren's shuttle stopped mid-stroke.

"He came in on the freight spur last night," Marta went on, still watching the water, her voice pitched exactly loud enough to carry and no louder. "Rode a maintenance car down the closed line like he owned it. Slept in the station loft. This morning he bought bread and lamp oil and asked the harbormaster two questions. One was about tide tables for the flats."

Wren's heart gave a single hard knock against her ribs. "And the other?"

Marta finally looked at her. The old woman's eyes were the color of weathered rope, and they missed nothing that happened within sight of salt water, which was everything that mattered.

"The other was about a prospector named Ede Korrish."

* * *

She walked down the quay slowly, because running would mean something, and everyone in Port Calloway would read what it meant.

The town clung to its shelf of rock above the harbor like a limpet: fish sheds with their scales of silvered shingle, drying racks hung with herring like laundry, the crooked lane up to the chapel where her mother's name was cut into a slate marker her father had sworn to replace with granite. At the bottom of the lane, near the harbormaster's office, stood a man she didn't know, talking to Harbormaster Pell.

He was tall, with the lean, hard-used look of someone who'd been carrying himself for a long time on very little. Dark hair going early gray at the temples. A canvas coat patched at both elbows, and hands that were wrong for a drifter—too precise, too careful, the hands of a man who fixed things rather than broke them. He had the stillness of the rails about him, she thought. The kind of quiet that comes from long distances traveled alone.

Pell saw her first and did the small thing with his eyebrows that meant *here's trouble, and I'm washing my hands of it.*

"This is her," Pell said. "Wren Korrish. Now I've done my part." He retreated into his office with the speed of a man who had learned, in forty years on the waterfront, that some cargo you don't sign for.

The stranger watched her come. He didn't smile. That, more than anything, told her something was wrong, because strangers smiled at her—she was young, she was alone, she was standing in a fishing town where friendliness cost nothing. This man looked at her the way people look at a door they're afraid is locked.

"You're Wren," he said. Not a question.

"I'm Wren." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough to see the salt dried white along the seams of his coat, the road dust ground into his boot leather. "And you are?"

"Sable." He said it like a word he'd borrowed and wasn't sure he'd return. "Sable Oyelaran. I work the coastal lines. Maintenance."

"There is no coastal line anymore. They pulled the charter three years ago." She said it flatly, watching him. "So either you maintain ghosts, or you've come a long way to lie to me about something small before lying to me about something large."

Something flickered across his face—not offense. Recognition, almost. As if she'd surprised him by being exactly what he expected and nothing he'd prepared for.

"Ede Korrish," he said. "I'm looking for Ede Korrish."

The world tilted slightly, the way it does when a floorboard you've trusted gives a warning creak. She kept her feet. She kept her face still. She had learned stillness on this quay, mending nets while the whole town watched her wait for a man who always promised to come back.

"Why?" she asked.

"Because—" He stopped. It was a real stop, a full halt, the kind a train makes when the signal goes red. She saw it happen: the sentence he'd built on the ride down the coast, rehearsed in the dark of some station loft, hitting an obstruction it hadn't mapped.

"Because?" she prompted, when he didn't finish.

"Because we met. On the line. He said—if anything happened to him, I should come here. He talked about you." His jaw tightened. "He talked about you a lot."

*He talked about me.* Her father, who wrote letters full of jokes and omissions. Her father, who had once gone four months silent and come home with a new coat for her and no explanation of where the money went. Her father, who lied beautifully, generously, the way some men sang.

"If anything happened to him," Wren repeated. Each word placed carefully, like stones across deep water. "What kind of anything?"

Sable looked past her shoulder, out at the harbor, at the boats, at anywhere that wasn't her face. "I don't know yet," he said. "I was hoping you'd know where he'd be. When he'd be back."

And there it was. There was the hesitation, plain as a snapped strand in a net.

She had spent her whole life reading men who came to this harbor with things to hide. Smugglers with honest eyes. Debt collectors with friendly voices. Her own father, radiant with promises. Hope was a dangerous thing in Port Calloway—it got into your blood like cold water, numbing everything before it drowned you—and Wren had learned to test it the way she tested any line: pull gently, watch how it runs, feel for the place where it snags.

This man's line snagged everywhere.

He asked where Ede would be instead of telling her where Ede was. He said *if anything happened* instead of *when I left him*. He wore guilt the way some men wore borrowed coats—a size wrong, sleeves too short, visible at the wrists no matter how he arranged his arms.

But underneath the guilt, when he finally met her eyes, there was something else. Something that looked, impossibly, like honesty trying to be born.

"When did you last see him?" she asked quietly.

"Eleven days ago." He didn't flinch from it this time. "South of Graywater. He was headed for the flats."

"Alone?"

"No." A pause. "With me. For part of the way. We shared a fire." His hand moved toward his inner pocket—an old habit, guarding something—and stopped halfway, and returned to his side. "He had business out there. Salvage. He wouldn't say what."

"Salvage." She tasted the word. Her father's letters had been full of salvage, full of *one more season*, full of the great score that would fix everything. "Did he seem well? Was he hurt? Was anyone following him?"

"Wren." Sable said her name carefully, as though setting down something fragile. "Why do you ask if someone was following him?"

"Because my father has been followed his whole life," she said. "By creditors, by partners he's crossed, by the kind of luck that runs behind a man like a debt collector. Because he promised me eleven months ago he'd be home by the spring tides, and it's nearly the neaps of the following year. Because you came here on a dead rail line in the night and slept in a station loft rather than an inn, and asked about tide tables before you asked about a living man." She stepped closer. "And because you keep looking at my father's name like it's written on a stone."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The gulls screamed. The dog behind the smokehouses redoubled its argument with nothing. Somewhere out past the breakwater, the sea kept up its long, patient conversation with the shore.

"I can't tell you what I don't know," Sable said at last. "I swear that. But I'll tell you what I do know, and then I'm leaving, because there's a ship I need to catch and a schedule that won't wait."

"You're leaving." Of course he was. They always left. "Where?"

"Port Calloway is the last stop before the flats," he said. "I need passage across, or a wagon, or anything with wheels and a wind. Six days. Maybe less."

Six days. Something cold slid down her spine, because she knew the flats, knew what the old pilots said about them—the Meridian shallows where the seabed opened twice a day like a lung, where wrecks surfaced and drowned again with the turning of the tide, where men went to pull treasure out of the deep and the deep pulled back.

Her father had been talking about the flats for a year. About a wreck. About a star.

"What ship?" she asked.

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters if I'm on it."

His expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a door swinging shut, a decision being made. "You're not."

"My father is missing on the Salt Flats," Wren said, "eleven days after he was seen with you, and you're the only person in the world who knows which direction he was walking. So either you take me with you, or you spend the next six days wondering which cove I found my own boat in, and whether I beat you there." She held his gaze. "I've read currents since I was nine years old, Mr. Oyelaran. Better than any chart. Ask anyone on this quay. The flats don't frighten me. What frightens me is sitting here mending nets while the answers walk away on someone else's boots."

"That's not—"

"And before you decide I'm a burden," she went on, because she could see the refusal assembling itself behind his eyes, brick by brick, "consider this: whoever was following my father will eventually follow his map to the same place. If he had a map. If there's something out there worth killing for." She let that sit between them. "You hesitated when I said his name. You hesitate every time the talk turns to what happened to him. I've spent my life around liars, Mr. Oyelaran, and you're the worst liar I've ever met—which means you might be the only honest man to come through this harbor in years. Honest men get killed keeping promises alone."

He stared at her. Behind him, Pell's curtain twitched in the harbormaster's window, and Marta Two-Tides had abandoned all pretense of mending, and the whole quay was pretending very hard not to listen.

"I came here to deliver something," Sable said finally, very quietly. "Not to pick up passengers."

"Deliver what?"

His hand went to his inner pocket again. Stopped again. And in that half-motion, that aborted reach toward whatever he was carrying against his chest, Wren felt the ground shift beneath all her suspicion—because she knew that gesture. She'd seen it on her father a hundred times. It was the gesture of a man carrying something that isn't his, something entrusted, something heavy in a way weight has nothing to do with.

"Mr. Oyelaran," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "what did my father give you?"

He didn't answer. He looked at her the way a man looks at deep water when he's already committed to crossing it—measuring, dreading, unable to turn back. Then he glanced over her shoulder at the harbor, and his whole body went rigid.

She turned.

Out past the breakwater, riding the morning swell with the easy arrogance of things that owned the water, three ships stood in toward the harbor mouth. Black-hulled, sharp-bowed, flying a pennant she didn't recognize—a white chevron on slate gray. They weren't fishing boats. They weren't coasters. They moved like wolves that had caught a scent and were in no hurry about it, because the scent had nowhere to run.

"Vane," Sable breathed beside her. The word came out flat and cold, and she heard in it everything he hadn't said—every answer he'd swallowed, every reason he'd hesitated. "They're early."

"Who's Vane?"

"Salvage fleet." He was already moving, his hand closing around her elbow—not rough, but urgent, steering her toward the shadow of the fish sheds. "Wren, the man who gave me that map—your father—he didn't just disappear. Someone gut-shot him in a culvert south of Graywater, and I held him while he died, and he made me promise—" He stopped himself, jaw working, as if the words had gotten ahead of his courage. "He made me a promise. And I intend to keep it. But not with those ships in this harbor."

The world went very simple and very loud. The nets, the quay, the gulls—all of it receded, and there was only the sentence hanging in the salt air: *gut-shot him in a culvert, and I held him while he died.*

Her father. Dead. Told to her by a stranger on a Tuesday morning, between the fish sheds, with the smell of herring in the air.

She should have fallen down. She should have screamed, or wept, or done any of the ten thousand things the stories said a daughter does. Instead she heard her own voice, small and clear, coming from somewhere below the roaring:

"The promise. What was the promise?"

Sable looked down at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop trying to shield her from it. "To bring his share to his daughter," he said. "In Port Calloway. To you."

"Then you haven't finished the job." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, hard, once, the way you tie off a knot—fast, so it holds. "My father's share is worthless to me if the men who killed him get the rest. And those ships are here for the same thing he died for. So you're going to tell me everything, Mr. Oyelaran—right now, in this shed, before they land—and then you and I are going to figure out how to get to that wreck before they do."

"They'll be watching the harbor. Watching you."

"Then we'd best not leave from the harbor." She was already thinking, already moving, pulling a tarpaulin off a stack of crab pots, revealing the supply wagon her crew used to haul gear along the beach road—broad-wheeled, salt-stained, unremarkable. "Marta keeps a skiff at the north cove. Pell owes me for three years of silence about his brandy shipments. And you—" she jabbed a finger at his chest, at the pocket where the map lay hidden, "—you know the flats' tide tables better than you let on, or you wouldn't have asked. Six tides, you said. So we've got six tides, and I've got a father's promise to collect, and you've got a dead man's debt to pay. We leave within the hour."

Sable stared at her. Really stared, the way a man stares at a mechanism he doesn't understand and cannot deny is working.

"You're certain?" he said. "Once we're on that road, there's no turning back. Vane doesn't stop. Whatever else your father was—whatever he found out there—they will chase it to the end of the empire."

"Good," Wren Korrish said, and heard her father's laugh somewhere inside her own voice. "Then they'll be chasing both of us. And I've been waiting eleven months for someone worth chasing alongside."

Above the fish sheds, the first of the gray ships rounded the breakwater, its pennant snapping in the wind like a whip finding its rhythm. Below the quay, hidden in the shadow of the shed, a broad-wheeled wagon waited with its back to the sea.

And in the space of one held breath, hope sharpened into purpose, and Wren stepped up onto the wagon bed, and did not look back.
