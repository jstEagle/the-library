## 5. The Vane Fleet Weighs Anchor

The harbormaster of Port Calloway had a face like wet paper and a conscience to match, which was why Ilsa Vane had bought it three years ago and kept it oiled ever since.

She stood at the rail of the *Charity of Iron*, her flagship, watching dawn come up gray over the harbor mouth while the man himself climbed the accommodation ladder with the careful slowness of someone carrying something heavy in his coat. Not money—she'd paid him in advance, always in advance, because men who were paid after a job learned to negotiate, and men who negotiated had opinions, and opinions were the first symptom of loyalty. He carried news instead, and news was heavier.

"Captain." He reached the deck, tugged his cap, and did not quite meet her eyes. A wise habit. "Word from upcoast. There was a killing at the Meridian culverts. A prospector."

Ilsa did not move. The wind moved for her, pulling at the collar of her oilskin, and she let it, because a captain who flinched at every gust taught her crew to watch for flinching. "Name?"

"They're saying Ede Korrish."

Something turned over behind her ribs, slow and cold, like a boiler lighting on a winter morning. She kept her face the way she kept her ledgers: balanced, legible to no one but herself. "Gut-shot?"

"So the story runs. Robbery, most like. Or an old partner settling accounts—the culvert towns are full of that kind of arithmetic." The harbormaster wet his lips. "There's more, Captain. Before he died he was seen talking to a railroad man. Drifter type. Off the closed spur lines. And—" he hesitated, and she let him hesitate, because hesitation told you where a man's fear lived and where a man's fear lived was where your leverage grew, "—and there's talk he passed something along. Papers, maybe. A map."

"A map," Ilsa said.

"Yes, Captain."

"To what?"

He swallowed. "They didn't say. But Korrish spent eleven years walking the flats, and everyone knows what he was walking toward."

Everyone knew. That was the trouble with salvage country: secrets sank slower than bodies, and both eventually came back up. Ilsa Vane had known about Ede Korrish for six of those eleven years—not his name at first, just a shape in her intelligence: a lone prospector buying tide tables, asking quiet questions about the old seabed rail line, paying in dust gold for things a man only buys when he's closing in on something. She'd had him followed twice. Both times he'd lost her watchers in the maze of drainage channels north of the flats, moving like a man who'd grown up in country that wanted him dead.

She'd never sent blades. That was not patience; patience was what you called restraint when you were too proud to admit you were waiting. It was *arithmetic*. A prospector who found the wreck alone would die alone with it, or come to market, and either way the fleet would collect. Killing him early risked the one thing she could not replace: the location itself. You could buy men, ships, harbormasters, whole governments on some coasts—but you could not buy coordinates out of a dead man's skull, and she had watched smarter captains than herself try.

Now someone else had done the killing for her, and the coordinates had gone walking upcoast inside a stranger.

"Thank you, Master Pellam," she said. "You'll find the usual arrangement has been doubled."

His face did the wet-paper thing again, gratitude and terror fighting for space. Doubled payments made men bold and then made them careless, and careless men got caught, and caught men talked, and talk was the one current she couldn't dredge. But she needed the harbor eyes now more than she needed them quiet. "One thing more. This drifter. Railroad man. What name?"

"No name yet, Captain. The spur towns don't ask. He mends track for meals and moves on before anyone learns it."

Ilsa smiled, and saw the harbormaster take a half step backward before he caught himself. "How industrious," she said. "Dismissed."

* * *

The *Charity of Iron* rode at anchor with her sisters strung out behind her like beads on a wire: the *Long Patience*, the *Drowned Bell*, the *Salt Tithe*, four cutters and two heavy-lift barges, all flying the black-and-white chevron of the Vane house. Nine hundred tons of salvage fleet, and every rivet of it hers, built over nineteen years from nothing but a drowned father, a stolen dinghy, and a talent for being the last one standing when the water finished its work.

Her father had been a wrecker too, in the small ugly way of the old coast—beach pirates who stripped hulls at night and sold the brass by the pound. He'd drowned ten miles off Calloway when she was nine, chasing a cargo that wasn't worth his boat, and Ilsa had learned her first great lesson standing on the dunes watching the sea give back nothing: *the sea keeps everything, and gives up nothing for free.* Everything since had been a variation on that lesson. The empire decommissioned its coastal lines and let the towns rot; the sea took the wrecks; and the Vanes took what the sea was done with. It was honest work by the standards of honest work, which is to say it was theft with better bookkeeping than most.

But the Meridian Star was different. Everyone in the salvage trade knew the story the way sailors know weather signs: the Star had gone down in the flats forty years ago carrying pay bullion and, rumor insisted, papers worth more than the bullion—a charter, some said, though no one living had seen it. Eleven years ago Ede Korrish had started looking in earnest. Six months ago, Ilsa's listeners in the culvert towns reported he'd stopped looking and started *preparing*—buying rope, air bladders, a diving bell secondhand from a scrap dealer in Grayharbor. A man doesn't prepare like that unless he knows where to put the bell.

And now he was dead, and whatever he knew was in motion, and motion was the one state of affairs Ilsa Vane refused to leave to chance.

She went below to the chart room, lit the lamp, and spread the flats charts across the table. The Meridian Flats were eight miles of seabed that showed at low water and drowned at high, scored with tidal channels that rearranged themselves every storm like a liar rearranging his story. Somewhere under that gray skin lay a wreck with an air pocket that had held, impossibly, for forty years—or so Korrish's preparations implied—and an air pocket was a clock. Sooner or later, this season or the next, a spring tide or a shifted channel would find it and drown it forever, and everything aboard would go from treasure to geology.

Six tides, if the stories about the wreck's condition were true. Maybe fewer. Her own divers' estimates varied wildly, and variation was just another word for ignorance wearing a uniform.

She heard boots on the companionway ladder—two sets, unhurried, and knew them both. First Mate Oren Dask came in first, big through the shoulders, patient as ballast. Behind him came Dess Corvane, and Ilsa felt the familiar sour taste, faint as copper, that Corvane always left on the back of her tongue.

Corvane was lean and charming and dressed like a man who expected to be forgiven, and he had arrived at her table eight months ago with a proposal: partnership. He claimed to have been Ede Korrish's partner once, before the falling-out, before Korrish went solitary and bitter, and he claimed—this was the part he leaned on—to know how Korrish thought, where he'd look, what he'd do when he found it. Ilsa had taken the meeting because information was information, and she'd kept him aboard because a man who'd betray a partner once was a man whose methods you wanted inside your walls where you could watch them. But she had never once confused him with an asset. Assets appreciated. Corvane merely waited.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard enough." Corvane dropped into the chair across the chart table without being asked, which was a liberty, and she noted it the way she noted all liberties: filed, dated, accruing interest. "Korrish is dead and gave away the map before he bled out. To a stranger. A *railroad* man." He said the word like it tasted bad. "Captain, we should have moved on Korrish months ago. I told you."

"You told me," Ilsa said mildly, unrolling the flats chart and weighting its corners, "and I declined, because dead men don't dive, and you"—she looked up, and let him hold her gaze for exactly as long as made him comfortable and then one second longer, until it didn't—"have a documented history of what happens around you when maps change hands. Korrish walked away from you alive. That's the only reason anyone believes you were ever partners at all."

Oren Dask, by the door, made a sound that in a lesser crew would have been a laugh.

Corvane's smile stayed fixed, which took practice, and Ilsa granted him the professional respect of recognizing it. "Then let's not waste time relitigating," he said. "The map's loose. Every hour it moves upcoast is an hour we chase rumor. Give me two riders and I'll run the spur line towns. I know how drifters travel—I *was* one. I'll find him."

"You'll find him," Ilsa agreed, "and then you'll do one of two things. Either you'll bring me the map and the man, or you'll bring me the map and a story about how the man tragically resisted. And I will know which, because I always know." She tapped the chart, on the deep channel that cut between the flats and the open water. "You sail on the *Salt Tithe*. Oren commands the search pattern. You advise. You do not act alone. Are we clear?"

"Crystal," Corvane said, and his eyes did the thing they did when he thought he was hiding something, which was shine slightly, like a coin at the bottom of clear water.

* * *

They weighed anchor within the hour, which scandalized the harbor. Fleets like hers did not move on rumor; they moved on certainty, and certainty took days of provisioning and pilot clearance. But Ilsa had provisioned for a spring campaign three weeks ago, quietly, on the pretext of a routine sweep of the northern grounds, and the pilots owed her, and rumor was only uncertainty wearing borrowed clothes. The truth underneath the rumor was simple: a dying man had given away the one thing in the world worth her whole fleet, and the tide was running against her.

By noon the flats were abeam, gray-green and endless, the drowned rail line visible at low water as a dark seam running out toward the horizon like a vein in the world's wrist. Ilsa stood at the bow rail and read the water the way other people read scripture. Out there, under that skin, sat forty years of patience. Air pockets failed. Channels migrated. Wrecks that survived a decade got eaten in a season. The sea was not keeping the Meridian Star for her; the sea kept everything for itself, and every hour she spent was an hour the sea spent winning.

Which meant speed, yes—but controlled speed. A fleet that arrived exhausted and scattered would lose to a single clever man on a maintenance car. She'd seen it happen. She'd *made* it happen, twice, to captains who mistook urgency for competence.

So she ran the fleet at cruising steam and set her mind to the other arithmetic: the man himself. A drifter who mended track for meals and vanished before names could stick. Such men were common as gulls and about as anchored, and most of them would sell a dying stranger's map for a bottle and a warm bed inside a day. If her new friend Master Pellam's harbor gossip was accurate, this one hadn't sold it yet—which meant either stupidity or principle, and principle was rarer and therefore more predictable. Men with principles could be counted on to do the principled thing. He'd deliver the map's promise. He'd go where the promise pointed. All she had to do was stand where the pointing ended and wait.

Patience, her father used to say, was just greed that had learned to swim.

* * *

They found Ede Korrish's camp on the second day, tucked in the lee of a collapsed signal box on the old seabed line, three miles inside the flats where the tide left it dry for six hours at a stretch.

It was a poor thing: a canvas lean-to gone gray with salt spray, a rusted stove, crates sorted with a hoarder's precision—rope, candles, a cracked diving helmet wrapped in oilcloth, tide tables going back eleven years, annotated in a cramped hand. Evidence of a life narrowed to a single point, the way a river narrows before it drops over an edge.

Corvane stood among the crates with an expression Ilsa studied longer than she studied the camp. He looked, she decided, like a man reading his own obituary and finding it inaccurate. Whatever he'd believed about Korrish's progress, the reality was worse: Korrish had been ready. Days, maybe hours, from making his attempt. Which meant the map in a stranger's hands was not a rumor. It was a fact with a head start.

"Burn it?" Oren asked.

Ilsa walked the camp slowly, touching nothing, cataloguing everything. On the crate that served as a desk lay a ledger of debts and favors, and beside it, weighted under a stone, a bundle of letters tied with string, addressed in a schoolroom hand to *Wren Korrish, Port Calloway*. Unsent. Years of them, by the thickness of the bundle.

A daughter. Ilsa stood very still over the letters for a long moment, feeling the shape of the thing assemble itself: a dying man, a daughter he'd never met, a promise carried by a stranger. Sentiment. The whole enterprise was built on sentiment, and sentiment was the most reliable material in the world—it never wore out, never drifted, always pulled exactly the weight it promised. A man carrying a dead prospector's promise would not sell the map. Would not abandon it. Would walk it straight upcoast to Port Calloway, or straight out onto the flats, following the coordinates like a train on rails.

Rails, she thought. Of course. A railroad man would follow the old line. The drowned signal boxes, the causeways, the seabed track—all of it mapped, all of it public knowledge to anyone who'd worked the coastal roads. She didn't need the map. She needed only to think like the man carrying it, and she had been thinking like desperate people her entire life.

"Leave the letters," she said.

Corvane looked up sharply. "Captain—"

"The letters stay. Burn the rest." She turned for the boats. "Let anyone who comes looking after us find a camp searched and a daughter's name sitting in plain view. Grief makes people stupid, and stupid people follow obvious trails, and obvious trails are how I keep my fleet concentrated instead of scattered across eight miles of mud chasing a drifter." She paused at the edge of the camp and looked back once at the little gray lean-to, the annotated tide tables, the eleven years of a man's life laid out in crates. "Burn it hot. I want the smoke visible from the ridge line."

Oren struck the lantern oil himself. The canvas caught with a soft whump, and the salt wind took the smoke low and fast across the flats, a gray banner unwinding toward the water.

They were back aboard before the tide returned. From the *Charity of Iron*'s stern rail, Ilsa watched the smoke stand over the flats like a question mark, and did the arithmetic one final time. Six tides, perhaps, on the air pocket. Five now, by the time any drifter worked the tables and committed. The fleet would hold station off the northern channel, divers ready, barges rigged, and wait for the man with the map to do what promised men always did.

Come to her.

Behind her, unnoticed by the watch, Dess Corvane lingered at the rail a moment longer, looking back at the burning camp with an expression he believed was private, and Ilsa—who missed nothing that happened on her decks, and had built nineteen years on missing nothing—saw it plainly, and filed it, and said nothing at all.

Some tools you sharpen. Some you simply keep where you can see the edge.

The fleet turned north, into the deepening water, and began to wait.
