## 6. Stowaway

The trouble started with a weight that shouldn't have been there.

Sable noticed it at the tide-mark outside Port Calloway, where the road left the shingle and climbed toward the flats. The wagon sat wrong in its traces—stern-heavy, the springs compressed a half-inch deeper than they'd been when he'd loaded it before dawn. Twenty years of running rolling stock had given him an ear for engines and an eye for loads, and both were telling him the same thing: something extra rode behind him, and it breathed.

He didn't stop. Not right away. He let the mule keep its plodding pace and ran the arithmetic instead. A knife would be lighter than this. A thief after his supplies would have taken them already, back in Calloway's crooked lanes, rather than ride forty pounds of dead weight out into open country. Which meant whoever it was wanted to be found—or wanted to be carried.

Sable pulled up under a leaning windbreak of salt-cedars, set the brake, and said to the tarp over the supply crates: "You can come out on your own feet or I can drag you out by your boots. Your choice."

Silence. Long enough that he almost doubted himself.

Then the tarp shifted, and a girl unfolded from the gap between the water barrels and the spare axle, small and folded up like a letter someone had tried to cram back into its envelope. She couldn't have been more than nineteen. Dark hair cut short and practical, salt-chapped hands, eyes the gray-green of shallow water over sand—and he knew those eyes before she said a word, because he'd spent three days looking for reasons not to see Ede Korrish's face every time he closed his own.

"You're the one who talked to Marta at the net-sheds," she said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, wrapped around the wagon's rail, were not. "Asking about Ede Korrish. Asking if anyone knew where he'd gone last spring. You asked like a man who already knew the answer."

"I asked like a man doing a job," Sable said.

"What job?"

"None of yours."

"It's the only thing in the world that's mine." She climbed down without waiting for permission, landed light on the packed sand, and stood her ground with her chin up and her fists loose at her sides—the stance of someone who'd learned young that backing up got you nothing. "Wren Korrish. And you're going to tell me what happened to my father."

* * *

He told her none of it. He told her a version of it.

That was the compromise he made with himself somewhere between the second question and the tenth, standing beside the wagon while the tide came whispering back across the flats behind them. He told her that yes, he'd met Ede Korrish. That Ede had been hurt—badly—inland, alone, and that Sable had gotten to him too late to do anything but sit with him. That Ede had died asking for one thing, and Sable had given his word on it, and a word given to a dying man wasn't a thing he'd break, not even for a girl with her father's stubborn jaw.

"He didn't suffer long," Sable said, because it was true, and because it was the only mercy he had to give.

Wren didn't cry. He'd expected crying—he'd braced against it the way you brace against a coupling under strain. Instead she went very still, the way water goes still before it finds the crack in the seawall, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into something flat and hard.

"How."

"Gut wound. There was nothing—" He stopped. Started again. "There was nothing to be done. I'm sorry."

"That's not how." She stepped closer. "How did it happen? Who did it? Was it Dess Corvane? Was it the Vanes?" The names came off her tongue like stones she'd been carrying a long time. "Because he wrote to me. Every month, sometimes two letters. And then four months of nothing, and then Marta tells me a stranger's been asking after him with a face like a closed door, and I know—I *know*—that look. It's the look people get when they're deciding how much of the truth you can carry."

Sable looked at her. Behind her, the sea was turning itself over in long gray rollers, and the wind carried the smell of brine and rust off the decommissioned spur that ran north along the coast, the line he'd been following for six years because a moving man doesn't have to answer questions.

"Ede was prospecting salvage," he said finally. "Off the books. He found something the wrong people want. Whether they're the ones who caught up to him, I don't know for certain. But I'd bet the wagon on it."

"The Meridian Star." She said it quietly, and watched him flinch.

So Ede had written to her about it after all. Or enough of it. Sable turned away, checked the mule's harness he didn't need to check, and felt the old familiar itch start up between his shoulder blades—the one that said *leave now, leave fast, leave clean*. Six tides. He had six tides to reach the wreck, and every hour he spent standing in this wind being looked at by Ede Korrish's daughter was an hour the Vane fleet's sonar sweeps were crawling closer to the prize, and closer to him.

"You should go home," he said.

"No."

"You should go home, Wren. What I'm doing next is dangerous, and it's mine to do, and there's no place in it for—"

"For what? For her?" She laughed, short and bitter. "For the daughter he sent half his share to and never once came home to see? I've heard it all my life. *Ede's coming back, Ede's got one more season in him.* Well, he's come back now, hasn't he. In pieces. In you." She pointed at the wagon. "I'm coming."

"You're not."

"Yes," she said, "I am. Because either you take me, or I follow you, and I've grown up on this coast, engineer. I can read a tide table better than you can read that map, and I know every smuggler's path across those flats, and if you lose me I'll just be behind you the whole way making noise until you give in. My father trusted you with his last breath. The least you can do is trust me with the truth."

* * *

He left her at the crossroads inn at Fennick's Rest. That was the first time.

It was a mean little place, all tar-paper and driftwood pilings, the kind of station that had died when the coastal line was decommissioned and kept twitching anyway on fish trade and gossip. Sable bought her supper, paid the innkeeper double to lock the gate behind him, and rolled out at first light feeling like a man who'd drowned a dog. He didn't look back at the inn. Looking back was how you ended up going back.

He made nine miles before he heard it—a thin, rhythmic squeak from the rear axle housing, a sound no healthy axle makes. He reined in at a culvert, swearing under his breath, and pulled the inspection plate.

She'd hidden a message inside. Folded oilcloth, wedged against the bearing race where any competent mechanic would find it within a day, and any incompetent one would find it when his axle seized. Inside the oilcloth, in a quick, sure hand:

*Axle grease is low on the left hub. Also, you paid the innkeeper to lock the gate. You didn't pay the stable boy anything, and the stable boy owes me a favor. —W.*

Sable sat on the culvert edge for a long moment, holding the note, feeling something move in his chest that he hadn't allowed to move in years. It might have been anger. It was probably not entirely anger.

He drove on. He did not go back for her. That was the point—he was testing whether she'd follow, and telling himself the test was for her benefit.

She followed. Of course she followed. He picked up her trail at the old mile-marker 61, where the decommissioned spur crossed the coach road: fresh boot prints in the dried silt, small ones, walking with purpose, and on top of the marker post, weighted with a fishhook, a second note.

*Your left rear wheel has a cracked felloe. I saw it when you bought feed in Calloway and meant to say so. If it lets go on the flats you'll wish you'd listened to me about more things. —W.*

And beneath it, smaller:

*I know you're reading these. I know you're not stopping. That's fine. I'm patient. I grew up waiting for my father; I can wait out you.*

* * *

The second time, he didn't leave her anywhere. He simply never told her where he was going, and then went there.

Tide two was falling, and the map's outer ring of coordinates pointed to a flooded signal box on the seabed line—an old interlocking tower, sunk when the seawall failed eleven years back, that Ede had marked as the first key to the wreck's position. Getting to it meant crossing three miles of exposed flats at low water through channels that filled faster than a man could run. It was no place for anyone, least of all a girl who—

—who read currents better than any chart, apparently, because when Sable crested the dune above the flats with his tide tables and his compass and his carefully memorized route, there was Wren Korrish, sitting on the signal box's broken roof thirty yards out on the wet sand, drying her boots over a driftwood fire like she'd been born up there.

"How," he said, when he'd waded out to her, heart still hammering from the crossing he'd rushed to beat the water.

"Marta's cousin pilots a cockle boat out of the east channel. I traded him my father's brass sextant for a lift to the sandbar at slack water." She shrugged. "You took the long way around because you didn't trust the middle channel. The middle channel runs two hours longer than your tables say—it silts on the neap tides. My father taught me that when I was twelve. You'd have known that too, if you'd ever asked anybody who actually lives here instead of just passing through."

Sable stood there dripping to the knee, and the firelight moved on her face, and Ede Korrish looked back at him out of it—not the gut-shot man in the culvert but the prospector as he must have been, twenty years ago, teaching his daughter the water. Something in Sable's chest cracked along a seam he'd thought was welded shut.

"You could have drowned," he said. "Crossing that bar alone."

"I didn't."

"You could still drown. Tomorrow. The next day. Everything from here to the wreck is water that wants you dead, and men with guns besides, and I—" He stopped. Made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plain. "I promised your father I'd bring his share to you. Alive. If I drag you into this and you die, then everything I am is a lie, and I've spent six years making sure I'm not a lie."

Wren looked at him for a long time. Then she nudged the fire with a stick, and sparks went up against the graying sky, and she said, "He talked about you."

Sable went cold. "What?"

"In his letters. Not your name—he never used names, he was careful like that—but there was a man he wrote about. Said he'd met him on the spur lines years back. A railroad man who fixed a bridge nobody else would touch, worked three nights straight in freezing rain, and when the company offered him a bonus he wouldn't even take a meal. Said—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she steadied it— "he said some men run from people because they think they don't deserve them. And that he'd never met anyone who deserved people more."

The wind came off the flats and pushed at them both, and far out on the darkening water the first stars were coming up, and Sable Nakamura—who had refused a stationmaster's warm bed and a town's gratitude and every hand ever extended to him—found that he had absolutely nothing to say.

"So," Wren said, and held out the stick like a peace offering, or a challenge. "Are you going to tell me what's in the signal box? Or are we going to stand here until the tide comes back and settles it for us?"

He took the stick. He fed the fire. And when he finally spoke, it was the closest thing to surrender he'd managed in six years.

"Tide turns in forty minutes," he said. "Eat something. And from here on, you stay where I can see you."

From the ridge behind them, half-lost in the dusk, neither of them saw the glint of glass—a spyglass, lowered slowly, and the tall silhouette of a man in a fine coat turning away along the dunes with a smile.
