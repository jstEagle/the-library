## 7. The Salt Flats Crossing

The tide table said we had four hours and eleven minutes of exposed seabed between the causeway at Graymoss Point and the drowned rail bed on the far side. I'd checked it three times against the almanac in the wagon's lockbox, then a fourth time because the first three made my stomach hurt.

"You keep looking at that page like it'll change its mind," Wren said.

She was sitting on the tailboard with her boots off, feet dangling, mending a tear in her sleeve with a bone needle she kept behind her ear like a carpenter keeps a pencil. She'd been doing small things like that all morning—settling into the wagon as if she belonged there, as if two days ago I hadn't caught her hiding under the tarpaulin among my spare axles and hadn't tried, twice, to leave her behind.

"It won't change its mind," I said. "That's what worries me."

The Salt Flats opened out ahead of us like a second sky laid face-down on the earth. At low water they were a country unto themselves—miles of gray-green mudflat stitched with shallow channels that gleamed like spilled mercury, old fence posts standing up out of nothing, the ribs of a boat that had died a century ago. Somewhere under all that mud lay the coastal line itself, the rails I used to run, buried by the government's own engineers when they decommissioned the route. They'd buried the track but not the timetable; the sea still came through twice a day whether anyone rode it or not.

I backed the wagon down the ramp onto the flats, and the wheels sank to the hubs before I got ten yards.

"Stop," Wren said—not alarmed, just certain. "Don't fight it. Momentum's your friend out here. If you stop, you're a post. If you keep rolling, you're a boat."

I looked at her. Nineteen years old, maybe twenty, with her father's same stubborn jaw and none of his caution. Ede Korrish had been a man who planned everything twice and still died gut-shot in a culvert. His daughter planned nothing and survived anyway.

"Where'd you learn that?"

"Net boats." She pulled her boots back on. "Port Calloway mud's worse than this. You learn or you drown in six inches of water, which is the stupidest way there is to go."

I put the team forward again, slow and steady, and the wheels turned up a rope of wet clay behind us but kept turning. The wagon groaned, settled, and found its rhythm—a lurching, wallowing gait, half drive and half swim.

* * *

The first hour was almost easy. The flats were firm near shore, packed hard by centuries of tide, and we made good time along the ghost of the old ballast road, which ran just under the surface like a vein you could feel through the soles of your boots. I navigated by the map in my head—the one Ede had burned into me the night he died, when his hands were too slick with blood to hold the wax-sealed paper steady. The outer coordinates were gone now, traded away in a waystation parlor to Dess Corvane's smiling forgery of a friendship. But the inner route, the true line across the flats to the Meridian Star, lived only where Ede had put it: in me.

Which meant I couldn't afford to drown. It was an odd feeling, being indispensable to yourself.

Wren stood up beside me on the bench, one hand on the rail, scanning ahead the way other people read books.

"Bear left," she said. "There's a channel cutting across your line about half a mile out. You can't see it yet."

"I can see fine."

"You can see *surface*. Surface lies." She pointed with her chin. "See how the mud out there goes from gray to brownish? That's not dirt changing color. That's water sitting *in* the mud, a finger's width down. Where it goes dark in a ribbon, there's current underneath. Current means channel. Channel means the wagon goes in up to the box and stays there until the tide comes and finishes the job."

I bore left. Half a mile later the channel was exactly where she said, a running seam of silver water thirty feet wide, cutting the flats like a knife stroke. We forded it at a gravel bar she spotted before I did—a slight roughening of the surface, a texture change no chart would ever record—and the wheels only sank to the axle instead of the sky.

"How often have you crossed here?" I asked.

"Never." She said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the horizon. "My father talked about it enough. Every story started 'when I cross the flats,' and every story ended 'and then I come home.' He never crossed. He never came home. So I learned everything he told me twice over, just to spite him."

There it was again—that edge under her voice, grief whetted into something with a working edge. I didn't know what to do with it. I'd spent fifteen years perfecting the art of not knowing what to do with people.

"Ede was good at maps," I said finally. "Bad at promises."

"He was good at promises too. Just bad at keeping them." She glanced at me, quick and sharp. "You knew him one night. I've had him nineteen years. Don't tell me what he was."

Fair. I shut my mouth and drove.

* * *

The squall showed itself at the two-hour mark, and it showed itself the way trouble always does out on the flats: late, and all at once.

One minute the horizon west of us was a clean hard line, sea against sky. The next, that line went soft, smudged, and then grew a bruise-colored tower that leaned toward us like something deciding. Wind arrived ahead of it in flat gusts that shoved the wagon sideways and set the mud rippling in long gray waves, as if the whole floor of the world were water pretending otherwise.

I reined in and did the arithmetic I'd been dreading. Squall speed, our speed, distance to the high ground of the far causeway. The numbers came out ugly.

"That's early," Wren said. She wasn't asking.

"Tide tables don't account for weather. A storm surge can bring the water in an hour ahead of schedule." I turned the wagon off the ballast line, angling for the causeway. "We're going to be racing it."

"Then stop wasting time talking to me."

I flicked the reins and the team leaned into their collars, and we ran.

It wasn't really running. Nothing runs on the salt flats; the mud takes your speed as a tax on every yard. But the wagon surged and slewed and kept moving, spray of clay off the wheels, harness creaking, the horses blowing hard, and behind us the squall came on with a sound I knew from wreck sites—a low, wide hiss, like the sea drawing breath through its teeth.

Halfway across, the flats began to die.

I felt it through the wheels before I saw it: the firm pack going soft, then treacherous, each wheel punching deeper, dragging, the whole wagon settling by inches. The horses strained and the wheels spun and we lost headway, and ahead of us the channels that had been silver threads were widening, filling, linking up into a lattice of returning water.

"We're sinking," I said.

"No." Wren was already off the bench, out on the tailboard, reading the ground. "We're *pooled*. There's a difference. Look—" she grabbed my sleeve and pointed left, then right, then left again. "Water's coming in along those three lines. They meet right where we are in about ten minutes. But north of us, see the birds? Gulls sit on ground that stays dry longest. They know the flats better than any harbormaster. Go at the birds."

"The birds."

"You want to argue or drive?"

I drove at the birds.

It should not have worked. By every principle of surveying and drainage it should not have worked. But the gulls were standing on a low spine of shell bank that threaded between the filling channels like a causeway built by nobody, and we gained it, and the wheels firmed up, and for four hundred glorious yards we rolled on dry-ish ground while the flats drowned on either side of us.

Then the shell bank ended, and beyond it the last mile to the causeway was already a foot deep in moving water, and getting deeper.

I stopped the wagon. For one clear, cold moment I understood exactly how men die out here: not dramatically, but by stopping. By doing the sensible thing, assessing, calculating, while the sea walked in and took the arithmetic out of their hands.

"What are you doing?" Wren demanded. "Why are we stopped?"

"Water's over the axles ahead. If I push through, the team could swim or could go down. If I turn back—"

"There is no back. Look behind you."

I looked. Behind us there was no flats anymore. There was sea, coming on smooth and relentless, erasing the channels, erasing the shell banks, erasing the gulls' opinions. The squall's leading edge was a gray wall of rain maybe half a mile out, and under it the water moved like it had somewhere important to be.

Something in my chest went very quiet and very old. I knew this feeling. It was the feeling from every time I'd left a town before dawn, every time I'd refused a stationmaster's offer, every locked door of every life I hadn't let myself have: the certainty that the smart move was to cut losses, save yourself, survive alone. Fifteen years of practice, all of it pointing one direction—abandon the load, swim for it, live to run another day.

Except the load was a girl who read currents like scripture, and the map in my head was a dead man's promise, and if I swam for it alone I would reach the causeway and stand there on dry stone and be exactly what I'd always been, which was nothing, belonging nowhere, safe.

"Sable." Wren's hand closed on my arm. Hard. "Whatever you're deciding—decide faster."

I decided.

"Unhook the traces," I said. "Now. Leave the wagon."

She stared at me. "The supplies—"

"Are ballast now. The map's in my head, your father's share is in the strongbox and the strongbox floats, barely—we lash it to the near horse. We ride. It's the only math that works."

For a heartbeat she fought it—I watched her do the same sum I'd done, watch her arrive at the same ugly answer—and then she moved. No argument, no hesitation. She had her father's hands, quick and sure, and together we stripped the harness, dragged the strongbox up, lashed it with the trace chains, and got the two best horses saddled bareback from the pile of gear we were abandoning forever.

The water was at our knees when we mounted. The rain hit a moment later, horizontal and cold, and the world shrank to gray.

"Follow the birds!" Wren shouted through it.

"There are no birds!"

"Then follow *me!*"

And she spurred past me into the flood.

I want to remember that crossing honestly, so I'll say it plainly: I followed a nineteen-year-old net-mender through rising seawater in a squall, blind, trusting her over my own instruments, my own tables, my own fifteen years of self-preservation. She chose a line and held it, leaning low along her horse's neck, reading the water by some sense she couldn't have named and couldn't have learned anywhere but Port Calloway—where the current slackened, where it ran, where the ground rose a hand's width under the chop. Twice she hauled sideways without warning and I followed, and both times, seconds later, the water where we'd have been went from knee-deep to chest-deep over a drop-off that would have taken a horse down.

The third time, it took the strongbox.

I heard the trace chains part—one snap, swallowed by wind—and turned in time to see Ede Korrish's share of the Meridian Star gold tumble off the near horse and vanish into the gray. Gone. Four hours of crossing, a wagon, a season of supplies, and a dead man's fortune, all of it handed to the sea in one swallow.

Wren didn't even look back. "It floats!" she screamed. "Strongboxes float! Mark the spot and come back at low tide—it'll be hung up on the shell banks! *Drive!*"

So I drove.

The causeway rose out of the rain like a mercy—old rail embankment, stone-faced, built by men who'd believed in the coast once. We climbed it as the water climbed after us, hooves finding purchase, and hauled ourselves up onto the ballast shoulder as the sea closed over the flats below, smooth and complete, as if the whole country we'd crossed had never existed.

* * *

We sat on the embankment in the rain while the squall spent itself around us, horses blowing, both of us soaked to the skin and shaking—not from cold, or not only from cold.

Wren started laughing first. Quiet at first, then helplessly, bent double over her knees, rain streaming off her chin.

"What?" I said.

"You drove at the *birds*." She wiped her eyes. "You had charts and tables and a compass, and when it mattered you drove at the birds because a girl told you to."

"It worked."

"It worked because I was right, and you listened." She straightened, and the laughter drained out of her face, leaving something more careful behind. "That's the thing about you, Sable Oyelaran. Everyone out here says you're a man who doesn't trust anybody. But that's not what I've seen. What I've seen is a man who doesn't trust *himself* to need anybody. There's a difference."

I didn't answer. Out below us, the flats were gone, erased, and somewhere under that new sea sat my wagon, my tools, my whole drifting life—and floating in it, snagged on a shell bank, a strongbox with a dead man's name scratched on the lid.

"My father trusted everyone," Wren said, softer now. "That killed him. You trust no one. I'm starting to think that might kill you slower, but just as sure." She looked at me sidelong, rain-wet, unflinching. "So here's my offer. You get my father's gold back off those banks tomorrow, because you're the only one who knows the mark. And I navigate. And neither of us calls it needing. We call it *trading*."

It was, I realized, the first honest bargain anyone had offered me since Ede Korrish bled out in a culvert. Trade, not charity. Terms, not trust.

"Trading," I said.

"Trading." She spat in her palm and held it out, grinning like her father must have grinned over a fresh claim, and I shook it, and her grip was iron and salt.

Then her grin faded, and she nodded past my shoulder, south along the embankment, toward the drowned line.

"Sable. What's that smoke?"

Far off, at the edge of the rain haze, where the old coast road met the causeway, a thread of black smoke stood up against the gray sky. Too steady for a squall fire. Too deliberate.

Someone was burning something at the far end of the causeway. And the only thing at the far end of the causeway was the waystation—

—and the waystation was where Dess Corvane had last seen us alive.
