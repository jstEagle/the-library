## 8. The Partner

The waystation at Kilnrock had been built for a railroad that no longer ran, and it wore its obsolescence the way an old soldier wears a uniform that no longer fits—proudly, and with visible seams splitting at the shoulders.

Sable brought the wagon in off the flats road just past midday, when the light came flat and white off the salt and made every shadow a hard black line. The station house sat on a low shelf of rock above the tide line, its platform still intact, its rails rusted to a deep arterial red where they ran out into nothing a quarter mile east and simply stopped, as if the line had been amputated rather than abandoned. A water tower leaned against the sky at a drunken angle. A signal post stood stripped of its arms, like a tree struck by lightning.

"Charming," Wren said, from the wagon seat beside him. She'd barely spoken all morning. Since the crossing she had been quieter in a way Sable recognized and distrusted—the quiet of someone recalculating everything they thought they knew about you.

"It has a roof," he said. "And the stationmaster here owes me nothing, which means he'll charge us fairly."

"That's not how owing works."

"It's how I prefer it to work." He reined the mule up under the lean-to beside the platform and set the brake. "We take water, we feed the animal, we sleep four hours, and we're gone before the evening tide turns. The next stretch of flats road is soft going for half a day. We want it at dead low."

Wren hopped down and stood looking at the dead rails running into the salt. "My father talked about this place. Kilnrock. Said there was a woman who kept the lamp burning in the window even after the last train, because sailors used it as a bearing." She paused. "He said a lot of things about places. It's strange hearing them arrive one by one."

Sable said nothing. He was already unstrapping the water skins, and his hands were doing what his hands always did when his mind wanted to be elsewhere—checking, tightening, counting. Buckles. Axle bolts. The wax-sealed tube riding flat against his ribs inside his coat, where it had ridden since a drainage culvert two hundred miles south, warm as a second heartbeat.

That was when he saw the horse.

It was tied at the far end of the platform, and it was wrong for Kilnrock the way a knife is wrong on a dinner table. Too fine. A bay gelding with legs like a racehorse and a saddle whose leather had been oiled recently enough to shine even in this flat light. Someone with money had passed through, or was passing through now.

Sable went very still, the way he'd learned to go still in yards full of men who read motion the way engineers read stress lines.

"What?" Wren said. She followed his eyes. "Nice horse."

"Yeah," Sable said softly. "Too nice."

The station door opened before he could decide whether to run.

* * *

The man who came out onto the platform was perhaps fifty, dressed in a long coat of good grey wool cut for travel rather than fashion, and he moved with the unhurried ease of someone who had never once needed to hurry in his life. His hair was silver at the temples and dark everywhere else, worn longer than current style, and his face was the kind of face that made you think of hearth fires and honest dealing—open, weathered, creased around the mouth in ways that suggested laughter was a habit rather than an ornament.

Only his eyes didn't match the rest of him. They were pale grey, almost colorless, and they did not laugh at all. They took in Sable, then Wren, then the wagon, then the mule, then the supply boxes stenciled with Sable's false shipping marks, in the order of a man inventorying rather than greeting.

"Sable Oyelaran," the man said. "Or whatever name you're carrying this month. I've heard three so far on the coast road. All of them dull."

"You have me at a disadvantage," Sable said.

"I'd be astonished if I did anything else." The man spread his hands, palms out—a gesture of harmlessness performed so smoothly it became its own kind of threat. "Dess Corvane. Ede Korrish's partner. Former partner. The distinction matters less than you'd think; partnership is one of those things, like marriage, that death only complicates."

Behind Sable, Wren made a sound. Small. Involuntary. Like a wire snapping under load.

Dess heard it. Of course he heard it. His gaze slid past Sable's shoulder and settled on her, and something moved through his face—something genuine, which was somehow worse than the charm. "Ah," he said quietly. "You'd be the daughter. Wren." He pronounced it carefully, as if he'd said it to himself many times over many years, rehearsing. "You have his chin. Did anyone ever tell you that? Ede had the stubbornest chin on the coast. You could hang a whole argument off it."

"My father," Wren said, and her voice came out level in a way that cost her visibly, "is dead."

"Yes," Dess said. "I know. I'm sorry for it. Genuinely." And the terrible thing, Sable thought, watching him, was that he seemed to mean it—or some part of him meant it, some compartment of the man that operated independently of the rest. "Ede and I quarreled, near the end. Badly. But quarrels are cheap and history is expensive, and Ede and I had eleven years of history. I'd have given a great deal for him to die old and rich and insufferable."

"And instead?" Sable asked.

Instead, Dess's smile returned, warm as banked coals. "Instead he died gut-shot in a culvert, alone, and gave away our mutual property to a stranger. Which brings us, I'm afraid, to business." He gestured toward the station door. "There's tea inside. Real tea, which in this country qualifies as a miracle, and a stove that works, which qualifies as two. We should talk indoors. The wind off the salt carries."

"We're not stopping," Sable said. "Water and feed, and we're gone."

"I know. You're always gone. That's your entire biography, Mr. Oyelaran—it's why you're so difficult to find and so easy to predict." Dess turned and walked toward the door without waiting, without checking whether they followed, with the absolute confidence of a man who knew the room would come with him. "Ten minutes," he called back over his shoulder. "After that I'll assume you'd rather conduct this conversation the other way, and I promise you the other way goes worse for everyone, but most especially for the girl, who strikes me as having inherited her father's talent for being present at inconvenient moments."

Wren looked at Sable. Her jaw was set—that chin, the stubbornest on the coast—and her hands were fists at her sides, and her eyes were bright with something between fury and grief.

"He knew my name," she whispered. "Before I ever gave it. How does he know my name?"

"Because Ede talked about you," Sable said. "For eleven years, apparently." He hated how much that explained—the letters, the share, the dying man's certainty that a stranger could find his daughter because the daughter existed vividly enough in the world to be found. "Stay close to me. Say nothing. Let him talk. Men like him tell you everything while they think they're telling you nothing."

"And if he wants the map?"

Sable touched his coat, over the tube, once. "Then we'll see how well I lie."

* * *

The station interior smelled of dust, coal oil, and—impossibly—fresh bread. The stationmaster, a stooped woman named Brack with forearms like dock rope, had indeed lit her stove, and indeed produced tea, and watched the three of them arrange themselves around her single table with the flat professional disinterest of someone who had seen every kind of passenger the coast could produce and rated them all equally tiresome.

Dess sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, facing the window, facing both exits, and managed to make the arrangement look like relaxation. He poured the tea himself, three cups, unhurried.

"Ede found the Star in the spring," he began, without preamble. "Eleven years we hunted together—two more before that, hunting other things, other wrecks, other rumors. We were good together. He could read water like scripture and I could read men, and between us there wasn't a lock on this coast we couldn't open." He sipped. "But the Star changed him. Or the idea of the Star did. He started talking about charters. Provenance. Paper." His lip curled faintly. "Paper, Mr. Oyelaran. Eleven years of blood and brine, and my partner falls in love with paper."

"What kind of paper?" Wren asked, despite instructions.

Dess regarded her with something almost like fondness. "The kind that makes men dangerous. The kind that says the ground under your feet belongs to someone it never belonged to—and worse, says it in ink, with signatures, in a form that lawyers can't argue with." He set down his cup. "You'll forgive me if I don't elaborate. Elaboration is leverage, and I'm not spending mine at a tea table."

"You want the map," Sable said.

"I want half the map." Dess held up two fingers. "Our agreement, Ede's and mine, predates you, predates the culvert, predates whatever deathbed sentiment made him hand our work to a railroad tramp. Half. That's all I'm asking. The outer seal, the approach coordinates, the tide windows—I'll do my own diving, thank you, I don't need your engineering tricks. You keep your half, keep your little pilgrimage to Port Calloway, keep the sentimental errand that lets you feel noble about a fortune you didn't earn." He smiled again, and again the smile didn't reach the grey eyes. "Everyone walks away rich. Everyone walks away alive. It's the best offer anyone will make you this year, and I include in that the fleet, whose offers are considerably less generous."

"The Vane fleet," Sable said.

Something flickered across Dess's face—there and gone, fast, but Sable caught it, because catching things was his trade. Not surprise. Confirmation. Dess had expected them to make the connection eventually, and had been waiting to see how they'd take it.

"Ilsa Vane pays well for information," Dess said mildly. "I sell information. I don't sell people, whatever you may have heard, and I certainly don't sell daughters. But yes. The fleet is coming. It left harbor early, on a bribed harbormaster's word, and it burns camps as it comes. If you're counting tides—and I suspect you are—you're counting faster than you were yesterday."

"You could have told them where we are," Wren said. "Right now. This station. Why haven't you?"

"Because a wreck divided two ways is worth more than a wreck seized by a fleet," Dess said simply. "Ilsa doesn't share. I do. It's the fundamental difference between us, and it's why I'll die wealthy in a warm bed while she dies wealthy on cold water, and we'll both consider it a fair outcome." He laced his fingers together on the table. "So. The map. Half of it. Now, please, while the tea's still hot and my patience—which is legendary—is still holding."

Sable looked at him for a long moment.

Then he reached inside his coat, drew out the wax-sealed tube, and laid it on the table between them—and watched, carefully, as Dess's eyes went to it with a hunger the man couldn't entirely disguise. Eleven years of hunger. It was the truest thing about him, and Sable filed it away: a man that hungry could be led, if you knew what he was hungry for.

"One condition," Sable said.

"You're in no position for conditions."

"Nobody's ever in a position for conditions. That's what makes them conditions." He broke the outer seal with his thumb—an act that cost him nothing, since the seal was already broken and re-waxed twice—and unrolled the map's outer leaf across the table, weighting the corners with the teacups. The old surveyor's ink showed the coast, the flats, the drowned rail spur, the approach channels. Everything except the final fix. Except the heart of it.

Dess leaned over it and went quiet in a way that was louder than any speech. His finger traced the channel marks, the tide tables penciled in Ede's cramped hand, the little annotations in the margins—*soft bottom here*, *current runs north on the turn*—eleven years of accumulated knowledge, and Dess read it like a man reading a letter from a dead friend, because that was exactly what it was.

"He numbered the soundings," Dess murmured. "He always numbered the soundings. Drove me mad." His voice had gone rough. Then it smoothed over, like water closing above a sinking stone, and the grey eyes came up. "Half. As agreed."

"There's a copy problem," Sable said.

Dess's hand stopped moving.

"Paper and salt air don't agree," Sable continued, conversationally, folding the map closed with steady hands while his pulse hammered somewhere up behind his teeth. "Ink bleeds, wax cracks, and I've had this thing through one squall already. So I made a duplicate in Calloway before I came north. Tracing, same scale, same annotations in my own hand—I copied Ede's notes word for word. You can compare them yourself, right now, if you doubt me. But the original stays with me. It rides with the girl, actually, separate from me, because I assume a man who reads others as well as you do assumes everyone else is reading him." He met Dess's eyes. "You get the copy. Full approach, full tide windows, everything short of the final fix—which I'll give you when we're standing on the site, both halves present, both partners' interests honored. That's not distrust, Corvane. That's arithmetic."

The silence stretched.

Brack the stationmaster refilled the kettle at the stove, loudly, having heard every word, caring about none of them.

"You traced it," Dess said at last. "In Calloway. Before you came north."

"Night one. Cheap lamp, bad paper. My hand isn't as pretty as Ede's."

"No," Dess agreed softly. "It isn't." And Sable watched him weigh it—watched the cleverness move behind the grey eyes like fish under ice, testing the surface, looking for the thin spot. Because here was the truth Sable carried in his chest like a swallowed stone: the forgery was good. He'd spent a full night on it, matching Ede's abbreviations, his smudges, even the grease spot from the culvert, reproduced with lampblack. It would pass a casual comparison.

But Dess Corvane had spent eleven years reading that map alongside Ede Korrish. He knew the weight of the original paper. He might know the smell of the wax. And sooner or later—on some dive boat, in some lamplit cabin—he would hold his copy against memory and find the one detail, the single sounding number or margin note, that Sable had gotten wrong or invented, and the whole elegant structure would come apart in his hands.

And when it did, he wouldn't be angry. Anger was loud, and loud men were predictable. Dess would be *interested*. He would recalculate. And then he would stop offering tea.

"Well," Dess said, rising, buttoning his coat, all warmth restored, all warmth meaning nothing. "Arithmetic I can respect. It's rarer than honesty and worth more." He took the folded copy Sable held out, weighed it in his palm, and tucked it away inside his coat without examining it—which frightened Sable more than any inspection would have. "You'll have my copy of the final fix when you send yours. Agreed terms. I'll be along behind you—two days, perhaps three. The flats road, then the causeway. Don't rush on my account. Rushing is how engineers die."

At the door he paused. Turned back. Looked at Wren, and for one unguarded moment the charm dropped entirely and left something older and wearier underneath.

"He kept your letters," Dess said to her. "All of them. Carried them in a tin against his chest, even diving. I used to tell him it would rust. He said paper keeps better than promises." His mouth twisted. "He was usually right about things like that."

Then he was gone, and they heard the fine bay horse take the coast road south at an unhurried trot, unhurried even leaving, because hurrying was for men with something to fear.

* * *

They didn't sleep. Sable repacked the wagon in the last hour of daylight with movements too quick and too precise, and Wren stood in the doorway of the station holding two cups of tea she'd forgotten to drink, watching him.

"You lied to him," she said finally. "The whole thing. There's no copy in Calloway. You forged it after the culvert. Before you even met me."

"Yes."

"You had it planned. From the first night."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She set the cups down on the platform rail, carefully, precisely, the way he handled tools. "I'm not angry. I want to understand. You looked at a dying man's map and your first thought was *who will come for this*, and you built a decoy before you ever rolled it open. What kind of life teaches a man to do that?"

Sable tightened the last strap on the supply box and let his hands go still.

"The kind where someone always comes," he said. "Every treasure has a shadow, Wren. Every map has a second reader. I've spent nine years staying ahead of second readers, and the ones I failed to stay ahead of—" He stopped. Started again, flatter. "It's not cynicism. It's maintenance. Same as track. You inspect for the flaw that hasn't shown yet, because by the time it shows, you're already derailed."

"And Dess?"

"Dess is the flaw." He swung up onto the wagon seat and offered her his hand, and after a moment she took it and climbed up beside him. "He believed me today. Maybe eighty percent. Maybe sixty. And he'll spend every mile between here and the wreck testing that remaining forty, because that's what he does—he reads men the way I read rail. And the moment he finds the weld that won't hold, he won't confront us. He'll simply start arranging the world so that confronting us isn't necessary."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the fleet knows roughly where we are and roughly where we're going, and Dess now holds a map that will lead his own divers to the Star whether we cooperate or not." Sable clicked his tongue and the mule leaned into the harness, and the wagon rolled out of Kilnrock under a sky going the color of old iron. Behind them, in the station window, Brack lit her lamp for the sailors, faithful to a railroad that no longer came.

Ahead, the flats waited, and beyond the flats the causeway, and beyond the causeway a wreck with six tides left—five, now—and somewhere on the roads between, a charming man on a fine horse, riding at their backs at his leisure, checking his copy against his memory, line by patient line.

Sable drove into the dusk and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the tea table, and the answer came out the same every time.

Two days. Three at the outside. Then Dess would know.

And Sable Oyelaran, who had spent nine years making sure no one ever knew where he was, realized with cold clarity that for the first time in his life, he could not afford to run.
