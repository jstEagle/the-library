## 9. Tide Three: The Drowned Signal Box

The old seabed line announced itself the way dead things do—by smell first.

Sable smelled it before he saw it: brine and rust and the sweet rot of kelp drying in the sun, a stink that had soaked into the flats so deep it would outlast the empire itself. Then the rails appeared, running out of the dune grass like the spine of some animal that had lain down to die a century ago and never finished.

He brought the wagon to a stop where the map said to stop, which was nowhere in particular—a fold of land between two tide channels, close enough to the drowned line that they could walk it, far enough back that anyone scanning the flats from the water would see nothing but salt scrub and a wagon that might belong to any of the hundred drifters who worked these margins.

"Signal box should be two miles east," he said, unrolling Ede's map across the tailboard for the tenth time since dawn. The wax seal was broken now; the vellum inside was soft as cloth from handling. "The map shows it standing."

Wren leaned over his shoulder. She'd been doing that all morning—not quite touching him, not quite giving him room—and he'd decided it was her way of auditing him. She counted everything. He respected it more than he could say politely.

"My father drew this part," she said. Her finger traced a notation in the margin, small and precise: *SB-7, door on the seaward face, third rib.* "He used to talk about signal boxes like they were churches. Said a man who understood them could make the whole coast obey him."

"He wasn't wrong." Sable folded the map away. "The Meridian Line ran forty signals between here and Port Calloway. One man at the right box could stop every train on the coast with a lever and a lamp."

"And one man at the wrong box," Wren said quietly, "could wreck them."

Sable looked at her. She was watching the horizon, not him, and there was something in her face he recognized—the look of someone doing arithmetic they didn't want to finish. He didn't ask what she meant. Some sums you let people keep private until they're ready to show their work.

They walked out onto the seabed line as the morning tide drained off it, the flats steaming faintly under a high white sun. The rails were still down. That was the thing about the coastal lines nobody outside understood—they hadn't been torn up when the empire abandoned them. They'd just been left. Rails, ties, ballast, all of it sinking by inches into the salt, a skeleton the size of a nation lying down in the shallows. Somewhere under that silt, Sable knew, were locomotives with their boilers full of trapped air, coaches with their windows dark, an entire civilization's circulatory system stopped mid-beat.

He'd spent eleven years walking along corpses like this one, keeping the last open stretches alive for towns too poor or too stubborn to let go. He'd told himself it was just work. Standing here now, with Ede's daughter beside him and Ede's map in his coat, he felt the lie of that settle over him like the flats' own damp.

"There." Wren pointed.

The signal box rose out of the tideline maybe half a mile ahead—a wooden tower on iron pilings, listing seaward, its shingles gone gray-green with algae, its lamp gallery hanging open to the sky like a broken jaw. It had been tall once, tall enough to see over the marsh when the marsh was water. Now the flats had risen around it, and it stood hip-deep in solid ground, a lighthouse that had woken up at the bottom of the sea.

*SB-7.* The number plate was still bolted above the door, crusted white with salt, but legible if you knew to look.

Sable felt something turn over in his chest. Not hope exactly. Recognition. He'd passed a hundred boxes like this in his years on the closed lines and never given them a second thought beyond whether the timbers were sound enough to shelter under. But this one had been drawn by a dying man's hand, marked with a third rib and a seaward door, and that made it different. That made it a door instead of a ruin.

They reached it as the shadow of the tower fell long across the wet sand. Up close, the structure was worse than it looked from a distance—pilings furred with barnacle, cross-braces sprung, the whole frame groaning softly whenever the wind came off the water. But the groan had a rhythm to it, and rhythm was information. Sable stood beneath the gallery with his head tipped back, listening, reading the tower the way other men read weather.

"Door's on the seaward face," Wren said. "Third rib."

"I heard you the first four times."

"You ignored me the first four times."

"I was listening." He moved around the base, counting ribs—the vertical pilings that framed the walls. First. Second. Third. At the third piling the wall planking had rotted through in a vertical seam, and behind the rot, half-hidden by a curtain of dried kelp, was a hatch. Iron-strapped, salt-welded, no bigger than a coffin lid.

Ede had known his boxes. Of course he had. A prospector working the flats for salvage couldn't survive a season without learning how the old infrastructure breathed—where the air pockets lived, where the flood channels ran. The map hadn't been guesswork. It had been literacy.

Sable set his shoulder against the hatch and pushed. Nothing. He braced both boots against the piling and drove again, and this time something gave—not the hinges but a bar somewhere inside, dropping with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The hatch swung inward six inches and stopped, and a breath of air came out of the dark interior, cold and mineral and impossibly old, air that had been sealed in there since before either of them was born.

Wren had gone very still. "It's breathing," she said.

"It's not breathing. It's equalizing." But he understood why she'd said it. The tower did seem alive—the groaning timbers, the exhaled air, the drip of trapped water finding its way down through the floors. "Tide's coming back in three hours. We work fast and we get out faster. Anything below the second floor stays flooded, and anything flooded stays shut. Agreed?"

"Agreed." She was already pulling the lantern from her pack. "You know I'm going in first anyway."

"I know you're going to try."

* * *

The interior of SB-7 was a cathedral built by accountants.

That was Sable's first thought, climbing through the hatch into the ground-floor chamber: narrow, vertical, lit in dusty shafts where the rotted planking had opened seams to the sky, and everywhere—everywhere—the machinery. Levers the length of oars rising through slots in the floor. Wire runs fanning out from a central drum like the roots of a tree. The great quadrant gears of the interlocking frame, each one taller than a man, mounted on the back wall in a row, their teeth green with verdigris but intact. Signalmen had stood in this room and thrown those levers, and thirty miles of rail had answered them—points changing, lamps swinging from red to green, trains flowing or stopping at a single human will.

The floor was ankle-deep in tidal silt. Above the second landing, Sable could see daylight through the collapsed roof of the lamp gallery. Below—he put his boot to the edge of the stairwell and felt the cold come up out of it—the lower levels were drowned, exactly as the map promised.

"The mechanism's below," Wren said, holding the lantern over the stairwell. The light went down and died against black water perhaps eight feet beneath them. "My father marked it here—" she tapped the vellum, "*below the frame, behind the third gear.*"

"Your father marked where it is. He didn't mark how to reach it." Sable crouched at the stairwell edge, studying the water. It was clear near the top, then green-black. Still. Very still. "This whole level floods twice a day. Whatever's down there has been underwater longer than we've been alive."

"So we wait for the next low tide."

"We don't have a next low tide. Tide three turns in—" he checked the sky, the light on the water, the habit of a lifetime, "—a little under three hours, and after that the window starts closing. Ede's numbers say the wreck's air pocket goes at tide five. If we're not through this box today, we lose the day, and we don't have days."

Wren's jaw set in a way he was beginning to recognize as inherited. "Then we go down now. You can hold your breath, can't you?"

"For about ninety seconds. Which is plenty of time to drown in eight feet of black water in a flooded tower full of things to snag on." He said it flatly, without drama, and watched her hear it. "There's another way. There's always another way. That's the entire profession."

He stood and turned to the interlocking frame.

The great gears stared back at him, patient as monuments. Behind the third gear—that was what Ede had written, and Ede had been a careful man—but the third gear sat flush against the wall, its mounting bolts sunk into masonry, no gap behind it wide enough for a hand, let alone a man. Unless.

Unless the gear moved.

Sable walked the row slowly, laying his palm flat against each wheel in turn, feeling for play. The first two were seized solid—salt had welded the shafts into their bearings decades ago. The third, though. The third shifted. Barely. A tremor under his hand, a whisper of metal moving inside metal, like a sleeper turning over.

"Wren. Light here."

She brought the lantern close, and he saw it: the locking lever for the third gear, a steel arm as thick as his wrist, running down from the gear hub into a slot in the floor. Every signal box in the empire locked its gears the same way—had to, or a stray vibration would throw a switch under a moving train and kill everyone aboard. The lock was a simple drop-bar. And a drop-bar, unlike a shaft, could be driven.

"We need a hammer and a punch. Something to drive the lock bar up out of its seat."

Wren was already digging in the wagon kit she'd insisted on hauling the two miles herself—she carried more than her share and pretended not to notice that he noticed. She came up with a four-pound maul and a cold chisel, and handed them over handle-first, the way you hand tools to someone whose trade you respect.

"When did you learn this?" she asked. "Signals. Interlocking frames. My father spent twenty years on the flats and he never learned to work one. He said it took a lifetime."

"It takes about six years, if you start young enough and have a teacher who hits you with a wrench when you guess." Sable fitted the chisel into the seam where the lock bar entered its guide. "I started at thirteen. Stationmaster named Okonkwo, up the Ashfall spur. Best and worst thing that ever happened to me."

"What happened to him?"

"He died at his post. Heart, in the middle of a winter shift. They found him with his hand still on the lever, holding a freight clear of a washout he'd already seen coming." Sable adjusted his grip. "I've been trying to be worth that ever since. It's a bad way to live. Don't recommend it."

He swung the maul.

The first blow rang through the tower like a bell, and the whole structure shivered, silt sifting down from the joists, somewhere above them a gull screaming off the gallery rail. The second blow bent the chisel. The third, struck true, drove the lock bar a quarter inch, and Sable felt it—the resistance change, the bar beginning to travel.

"Again," Wren said, and took the maul from his hands before he could argue. "You hold the chisel straight. Your hands are shaking."

"They are not—"

"They are. Give it here."

So they traded, and it shouldn't have worked—it was clumsy, improvised, two strangers sharing a tool in a rotting tower—but it did work. She swung with the flat, powerful stroke of someone who'd spent years hauling nets, and he kept the chisel true, and blow by blow the lock bar climbed out of its seat until, on the ninth strike, it cleared the guide entirely and dropped loose into the silt.

Silence. Then the third gear sighed.

It was a small sound, metal relaxing after sixty years of tension, but Sable felt it in his teeth. He set both hands on the rim of the wheel and pulled, gently, testing—and the gear turned. An inch. Two. The wire drum at the center of the room began to rotate with a low groan, paying out cable into the flooded stairwell, and somewhere below them, down in the drowned dark, something heavy released.

The floor shuddered. From the stairwell came a rushing, gulping sound—water moving, being displaced—and then, unmistakably, air. A column of bubbles broke the surface of the flooded level, boiling up silver in the lantern light, and the water level began, slowly, visibly, to fall.

Wren grabbed his arm. "What did you do?"

"Opened the drain culvert." His voice came out strange even to himself. He was staring at the descending water, and the wonder on his own face must have matched hers, because she laughed—a short, disbelieving bark. "The signalman's escape route. Every box on the coast had one—a scuttle to the tide channel, so the crew could get out if the tower flooded. It's been jammed shut for sixty years. The gear drives it. Your father found the mechanism. He just couldn't turn the key."

"And you could."

"I grew up turning keys like this." He watched the water drop past the first step, the second, uncovering stairs slick with black silt, and felt something open in his chest that he hadn't allowed open in eleven years. This was why he'd chosen the rails, back when choosing had still felt possible. Not the traveling. This. Dead machines waiting for one person who spoke their language, and the moment they woke. "Get the lantern. We're going down while it drains."

* * *

The mechanism room was small, brick-lined, and utterly dry.

Dry after sixty years underwater—or nearly, the floor still weeping through hairline cracks—but dry enough, and in the center of it stood the reason Ede Korrish had bled out in a drainage culvert rather than give up his map: a relay cabinet, iron, bolted to the wall, its door stamped with the crest of the Meridian Line and, beneath it, a smaller stamp Sable knew only from history books. The settler cooperatives' mark. The old communal railways, before the empire bought the charters for pennies and burned the paperwork.

Wren's lantern hand was not steady. "Open it," she said. "Please."

Sable worked the corroded latch with a knife tip, taking his time, because some doors deserved care. Inside, on a shelf above a nest of dead relays, lay a lead document tube, wax-sealed at both ends, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job perfectly.

He lifted it out and held it a moment without opening it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"This isn't the wreck," he said. "This is the proof the wreck exists. Coordinates, tide tables, the approach channel—everything Ede needed to sell the location, or to find it again if he lost the map." He turned the tube in the light. "He hid it here because a signal box is the one place on the flats a salvager wouldn't think to look. Too ordinary. Too much like work."

Wren reached out and touched the tube with one finger, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "He told me stories," she said. Her voice had gone rough. "When I was small. About signalmen who could stop the whole coast with one lever. I thought he was making it up to keep me quiet. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "He was telling me the truth the whole time. All those stories were instructions. He was teaching me this, and I was too young to know it."

Sable looked at her—at the lantern light on her face, at the tears she was refusing to let fall in front of him—and something in his chest that had been locked as long as the gear unlocked itself, quietly, without permission.

"He knew you'd come," Sable said. "Maybe not you specifically. But he knew whoever came would need to understand the stories. So he told them to the one person who'd carry them."

Wren wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, and took the tube from him. "Then we'd better not waste the lesson. How far to the wreck from here?"

Sable unrolled the map against the relay cabinet. The coordinates Ede had inked in the margin resolved in his head against the tide tables he'd memorized, and the arithmetic was ugly.

"Two miles north-northeast, under nine feet of water at high tide. We dive at slack water tomorrow morning—tide four." He looked up through the ceiling, toward the sound of the sea returning, faint and patient. "Which gives us tonight to get there, and one problem."

"What problem?"

He nodded at the lantern, at the shadows it threw up the stairwell—shadows that seemed, for half a heartbeat, to include a shape that wasn't theirs.

"Someone's been following our tracks since the causeway," Sable said quietly, closing the cabinet. "And they're closer than the tide."
