## 10. Fleet on the Horizon

Wren felt the fleet before she saw it.

She'd been walking point along the dune ridge with her father's old spyglass slung across her back, boots sinking into sand still cold from the night, when the wind changed. It came off the flats in a long gray breath, and underneath the salt smell was something else—coal smoke. Coal smoke meant boilers, and boilers meant ships, and nothing but the Vane fleet burned coal this far down the coast.

She stopped. Listened. The sound came next: a low, rhythmic thrumming beneath the wind, like a heartbeat buried in the earth.

Wren climbed the last rise on her hands and knees, keeping her body below the crest of the dune the way Ede had taught her when she was nine years old and he'd come home for one whole winter, teaching her things between stories. *Never show your shape against the sky,* he'd said. *The horizon is a wall, and anything that breaks it belongs to someone else.*

She broke it anyway, just an inch, just enough to fit the spyglass to her eye.

Masts.

Seven of them, strung out across the western haze like the ribs of some enormous drowned thing, sails furled, running under steam. They were coming down the old shipping channel, hugging the line where deep water met the flats, and even at this distance Wren could read them the way she read weather: the two small cutters running ahead like dogs off a leash, the fat supply barge wallowing behind, and at the center of it all a black-hulled ship with a white wheelhouse that sat on the water like a knife laid flat.

*The Vane flagship.* Her father had spoken that name only once, in a voice she'd never heard him use before or since—the voice of a man describing something that had already killed him, he just hadn't fallen over yet.

They were a day ahead of schedule. Maybe more.

Wren lowered the spyglass slowly, as if any sudden movement might carry seven miles across open salt. Her mouth had gone dry. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; somewhere inside, everything was screaming.

*Dess.* The name arrived without being summoned, cold and certain as the tide itself. Dess Corvane had taken Sable's forged map three days ago, had smiled his soft smile and wished them safe travels, and Wren had known—even then, watching him ride away with the copy tucked inside his coat like a love letter—that they were already dead men walking. You didn't fool a man like Dess Corvane. You only delayed him while he decided how he wanted to hurt you.

He hadn't even needed to follow them himself. He'd simply sold the coordinates' outer bearing to Ilsa Vane and let the fleet do what fleets do.

Wren slid back down the dune's inland face and ran.

* * *

Sable was under the wagon when she found him, flat on his back in the shade of its iron-banded belly, doing something violent to the rear axle with a wrench. He worked the way he did everything—quietly, completely, as if the rest of the world were a rumor he'd declined to confirm. She'd hated that about him once. Lately she'd begun to understand it was armor, same as her father's jokes, same as her own counting—she counted things when she was frightened, knots in nets, gulls on posts, and nobody ever knew.

"Boilermaster," she said. That was what she called him when it was urgent. He claimed not to know why she'd picked it. She knew exactly why: because it made him answer like an engineer instead of a ghost.

The wrenching stopped. "That bad?"

"Fleet's in the channel. Seven sail, steam up. Cutters ahead of the main body." She made herself say the rest plainly, because her father had taught her that too: *bad news doesn't get better with age.* "A day early. Maybe two."

Sable rolled out from under the wagon in one motion and stood, and Wren watched the news land on him—not on his face, which stayed level as a spirit gauge, but in his hands. His fingers went to his belt, touched the buckle, moved away. A tell. Three weeks on the road together and she'd collected his tells the way other people collected coins.

"Dess," he said. Not a question.

"He didn't even have to chase us." Wren heard her own voice go flat and hard. "He just posted us a letter to the wolves."

Sable walked past her to the base of the dune and climbed it fast, careless now of his silhouette against the sky, which told her more than his face did. She followed. At the crest he dropped flat beside her and took the spyglass without asking, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Below and beyond, the salt flats stretched to the horizon in sheets of hammered silver, blinding where the morning caught them, and out on the channel the seven masts crawled south with terrible patience.

"They're not making for Port Calloway," Sable said at last. "They're holding the channel line. Sweeping." He passed the glass back. "See the wake pattern on the lead cutters? Zigzag. That's a sonar sweep track. They're listening for her."

"For the *Star*."

"For the air pocket. A hull that size holds a chamber of trapped air for months if the seals hold. Every sweep pass, they drop a listening bell and wait for it to sing back." His jaw tightened. "Ede's map says six tides before the last seal fails. We're on tide four. They don't need to find us, Wren. They just need to be there when the wreck gives itself up."

Wren put the glass to her eye again and found the flagship. Even now, knowing what waited inside that hull—her father's letters, the charter, the truth about the lines that had been stolen out from under towns like hers—it was hard to feel anything but small. The *Meridian Star* was a dead ship under forty feet of brine. The Vane fleet was alive, armed, and patient as arithmetic.

"So we run," she said. Testing it. Watching him.

"We run." No hesitation. That was Sable all over—he'd decided before she'd finished climbing the dune, probably before she'd finished shouting his name. "There's a route north along the shingle bar. Hard going, but the cutters can't follow it and the main fleet won't leave the channel. We double back through the marsh country, come at the wreck site from the landward side on tide six, dive fast, and be gone before their evening sweep."

"And the map?"

"The map lives here now." He tapped his temple. "Dess has the outer seal. Vane has Dess's copy. Neither of them has the inner coordinates, because those are mine, and I intend to keep them that way."

It was a good plan. It was, Wren admitted, a very good plan—clean, engineered, every risk weighed and routed around, the way he'd reroute a broken rail. And it had one flaw, one flaw so large she was astonished he couldn't see it, except that she knew he could see it perfectly well and was hoping she wouldn't.

"You're leaving me at the waystation," she said.

Sable didn't flinch. That was almost worse. "Ostler Hask owes me for the axle work. There's a room behind the stable. Two days, maybe three, and I'll come back through—"

"No."

"Wren—"

"*No.*" She said it quietly, which somehow made it louder. Down on the flats a curlew cried, one long falling note, and she waited until it faded. "You want me to sit in a stable loft counting cracks in the ceiling while my father's ship floods and the people who killed him sail over it? While the truth he died carrying sits in your head like coin in a purse?"

"I want you alive."

"That's not the same thing!" It came out of her before she could soften it, and she didn't try to take it back. "My whole life, everyone who loved me decided what was safest for me and did it *to* me. My father left to keep me safe. My aunt sent me to the net crews to keep me safe. Everyone kept me safe right up until the day a stranger rode into Port Calloway wearing my father's promise like a borrowed coat." Her voice cracked and she hated it, and pushed through anyway. "I'm done being kept, Sable. Done."

For a long moment he said nothing. He lay there against the dune with the wind pulling at his collar, looking out at the masts, and Wren realized with a strange, distant clarity that he wasn't angry. He was afraid. She could see it now, plain as a signal lamp—this man who faced down cutters and causeways and the memory of whatever had taught him to run—afraid, specifically and precisely, of her. Of what happened to people who stood near him.

"My mother used to say," he finally said, so quietly she nearly missed it, "that the people who love you are the ones standing closest when the roof comes down."

"Your mother sounds like she understood roofs."

"She understood that I was always the one knocking them down." He exhaled, long and slow, and Wren watched him do the thing he did—watched the engineer take hold of the fear and start measuring it, weighing it, fitting it into the plan like a part that had to be machined to size. When he turned to look at her, his eyes had gone clear and cold and honest. "If you stay with me, you work. No passengers. If I say run, you run. If I say hide, you hide, and you stay hidden even if you hear me screaming. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"If you say run, I run. If you say hide, I stay hidden." She held his gaze. "Unless hiding gets you killed. Then all bets are off."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one would live if he'd ever built one. "That's not the bargain."

"It's mine."

He looked at her a moment longer, and then, deliberately, he looked away first. Wren counted it as a victory and filed it with the others.

* * *

They came down off the dunes as the sun climbed, and Sable became a different man—or rather, he became entirely himself, the self Wren had glimpsed only in fragments: the man under the wagon, the man at the signal box, hands moving with total certainty inside the world of machines. He turned the wagon off the track and into a fold of dune scrub where it would read, from the channel, as nothing at all—a shadow among shadows. Then he spread his charts on the tailgate and weighted the corners with bolts, and Wren crouched beside him and watched him think.

"Here." His finger came down on the shingle bar, the northern route he'd named. "This is where they'll expect us to run. So this is where we make them *see* us running." He drew a line with a grease pencil, then another, crossing it. "The old branch line comes out of the marsh country here—abandoned thirty years, but the bed's still solid. There's a locomotive rusting at the end of it. I saw her funnel from the ridge two days back. Boiler's probably shot, but she doesn't need to run. She needs to *look* like she's about to."

Wren frowned. "Look like she's about to?"

"Smoke. Steam. Movement on the old line, heading north toward the shingle bar." He tapped the crossing point. "Vane's cutters are hunting a wagon and two riders. Give them a locomotive instead, and they'll commit. Both cutters will swing north to cut off the rail line, because a locomotive is worth a prize crew's bonus and a wagon isn't. While they're chasing smoke—" the pencil swept south, down across the flats, to a small inked cross that made Wren's chest tighten, "—we go low, straight across the open salt, and reach the site on the next low tide."

"That's the decoy route from your plan," she said slowly. "Except you've put yourself on it."

"I've put a *locomotive* on it."

"With you driving it. Boilermaster, that boiler's been dead thirty years. You said yourself—"

"I said *probably* shot. There's a difference between a boiler that won't hold pressure and one that'll hold just enough, just long enough, if you know what you're doing." He said it lightly, and Wren heard the lightness for exactly what it was: a plate riveted over a hole. "I can fire her cold, walk the pressure up slow, and coast her down the grade onto the bar. Fifteen minutes of smoke and noise. Then I step off and let her roll on empty."

"And if the 'just enough' turns out to be 'not quite'?"

"Then I'll have learned something about optimism."

He smiled when he said it, and Wren did not smile back. She looked at the grease-pencil line, at the little mark where the dead locomotive waited at the end of its dead line, and she thought about her father, who had also always had one more plan, one more angle, one more *just long enough*. She thought about the drainage culvert she'd never seen, where that final plan had ended with him gut-shot in the dark, trading maps for promises.

"Take me with you," she said.

"No."

"Sable—"

"The locomotive needs one person. One. Any more weight and the margins I'm trusting my life to stop existing." He rolled the chart closed with brisk finality. "You'll be with the wagon, southbound, with Maren's boy driving. The moment the cutters commit north, you move. By the time they realize their smoke monster is empty, you'll be a mile from the site and I'll be walking off the bar to meet you."

It was a good plan. It was a very good plan. Wren stood there hating it with her whole heart.

Because she'd finally understood something, lying awake these past nights listening to him check the wagon axles by lantern light, long after he thought she slept: Sable Oyelaran planned to survive this. But he did not, anywhere in any of his plans, plan to *live*. There was a difference, and she could see it now as clearly as masts on a horizon. Every route he drew led through the danger and out the other side and then simply—stopped. No house at the end of it. No harbor. He was spending himself like a man spending stolen money, fast, before anyone asked where he got it.

Her father had been the same. She knew the type now. She recognized the disease.

And she would be damned, damned to the last tide, if she stood in a wagon and watched another one of them spend himself on her behalf.

* * *

They made the southern camp by midafternoon, in the lee of a beached derelict—a whaler's hull, bleached bone-white, half-swallowed by the sand—and while Sable checked the wagon's running gear for the hundredth time, Wren climbed to the top of the derelict's broken stern and sat with her knees drawn up, looking north.

The masts were closer. In the clear afternoon light she could count details now: the gunwale rails, the glint of glass in wheelhouses, the lazy spiral of coal smoke bending south on the wind. The lead cutter had turned. It was working a slow arc across the channel mouth, and Wren knew—Ilsa Vane's people were patient, methodical, thorough—that by tomorrow morning that arc would close, and the day after, the sweeps would begin to walk down the flats themselves.

Two days. Maybe less. Tide five, tide six, and then the sea closed the book on the *Meridian Star* forever, charter and gold and her father's last truth all drowned together.

*Not while I'm breathing.*

The thought arrived without drama, the way the truest ones do. Wren sat on the dead ship's bones and let it settle into her, heavy and calm, like ballast finding its place in a hull. She was afraid—God, she was afraid, her hands wanted to shake and her stomach was a knot of wet rope—but the fear wasn't telling her to run anymore. Somewhere between the dune ridge and this broken stern, it had changed jobs. Now it was telling her to *stay*. To plant herself like a piling, right in the path of the thing coming, and refuse to move.

Her father had run his whole life, chasing the proof that would fix everything, always one expedition short. Sable ran too, faster and quieter. Well. Someone in this story was going to stand still.

Below her, Sable emerged from under the wagon, wiped his hands on a rag, and glanced up at her perched on the wreck. For a moment neither of them moved. Then he raised one hand—not waving, exactly. Checking. Making sure she was real, still there, still his to protect.

Wren raised her hand back. And she thought: *you can draw all the routes you want, boilermaster. Tomorrow, when you climb onto that dead locomotive, I'm going to be on it. You can throw me off. You can outrun me. But you cannot out-stubborn the daughter of Ede Korrish, and you cannot, cannot, make yourself the only one who pays.*

Out on the channel, the lead cutter completed its arc and steadied on a new heading—north, toward the shingle bar, toward the old branch line, toward the rusting shape at the end of it that didn't yet know what was coming.

Toward the decoy.

Wren watched it come, and began, quietly, to count.
