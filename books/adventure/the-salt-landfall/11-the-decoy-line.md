## 11. The Decoy Line

The derelict sat where the map said it would: half-buried at the elbow of the old seabed line, a locomotive the color of a drowned whale, its wheels furred with salt bloom and its cab tilted like a man leaning against a wall to die. Sable walked around it twice before he touched it, the way he always did—reading the machine's posture first, listening with his hands.

"Number forty-one," he murmured. The builder's plate was crusted over but he knew the class by the shape of her running boards. Coastal freighter, sixty years old, built in the yards his father had helped pour foundations for. She'd been run hard and abandoned harder.

Wren stood back with the mules, arms crossed against the wind coming off the flats. "You're going to make that thing move."

"I'm going to make that thing *sing*." He patted the cold boiler like the flank of a horse. "The Vane cutters are running on sound tonight. Sonar sweeps every forty minutes, two boats working the grid between us and the wreck line. We can't outrun them across open flat—we're too heavy, they're too fast. But if something else is making noise out here, something louder, something that reads like a wagon engine or a salvage pump—" He shrugged. "They'll chase the song instead of the singer."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then we learn what their guns sound like up close." He was already climbing onto the running board, testing handholds. "Get the tarp off the tool chest. I need the long wrench and every scrap of firewood we've got."

* * *

He worked through the low sun and into the blue hour, and it was the best he'd felt in years.

That was the thing about machines that no one but an engineer understood: a dead one wasn't dead, it was only waiting. Forty-one had been left to the tide for a decade, maybe more, and the sea had gotten into everything—seized valves, fused fittings, a firebox full of hardened brine. But her bones were good. He could feel it through his palms when he laid them on the boiler shell. Someone had loved this engine once. The lubricator cups were clean brass, kept oiled right up until the day she was parked. The last crew hadn't abandoned her; they'd been taken from her.

"Talk to me," he whispered, cracking the first blowdown valve. Brine hissed out in a white rope and the smell of old ocean rolled over him. "What have you got left?"

By full dark he had water in the tank, drawn from the seep Wren had found under a shelf of rock—a trick of hers, reading the ground the way she read currents—and a fire laid in the firebox with the precision of a man defusing something. Which, he thought grimly, was exactly what this was. A boiler that had sat cold for ten years was a bomb with a slow fuse. You brought the pressure up gentle or you didn't bring it up at all.

"You know this is insane," Wren said. She'd built a windbreak out of driftwood and was heating flatbread on a stone, because apparently terror didn't excuse anyone from dinner. "You're going to sit inside a rusted kettle and light a fire under it."

"That's essentially the whole profession, yes."

"My father told me stories about men like you. Railroad men. He said you'd ride anything with wheels and apologize to it when it broke." She handed him bread without looking at him, which was how she'd started handing him things lately—not as gifts, just as facts. "He never said whether the apologies helped."

"They don't. That's why we keep making them." He ate fast, eyes on the pressure gauge he'd freed from corrosion with two hours of vinegar and patience. The needle sat at zero, trembling slightly in the wind, like something asleep.

Somewhere north, faint as a thought, came the pulse of the fleet's sonar. He'd learned to hear it that afternoon—a soft tick-tick-tick rolling across the flats, searching. Two cutters, sweeping the grid. Forty minutes apart. Patient as tide.

"Sleep," he told Wren. "I'll take first watch anyway. Pressure has to come up slow."

She looked at him for a long moment—the look she'd been giving him more often, the one that made him feel like a lock someone was patiently picking. "You'll wake me if—"

"If anything happens, you'll hear it. Probably everyone within six miles will hear it."

That, as it turned out, was the entire plan.

* * *

Midnight. The pressure needle climbed like a man wading upstream.

Sable sat in the cab with the fire door cracked, feeding it one stick at a time, watching the gauge the way other men watched a sleeping predator. Ten pounds. Fifteen. At twenty he tested the throttle—frozen—and spent forty minutes with penetrating oil and the long wrench persuading it, whispering curses and endearments in equal measure until it broke loose with a shriek that echoed off the flats like a gull crying.

At thirty pounds, he tried the whistle cord.

Nothing. The whistle valve was fused solid. Of course it was.

But that was fine. Better than fine. Because he hadn't come out here for the whistle. He'd come for the *drums*.

Under the cab floor, Forty-one carried her air brake reservoirs—two iron drums, each the size of a man, each holding compressed air once the compressor ran. And a compressor needed only steam. If he could get the little Westinghouse pump turning, he could charge the reservoirs, rig the release valve to cycle—open, close, open, close—and every time it opened, the drums would exhale with a sound like a giant clearing its throat. A rhythmic boom-boom-boom carrying miles across the flat salt pan, bouncing off the dune ridges, indistinguishable at sonar range from a heavy engine working hard. Or a salvage pump. Or anything the fleet wanted it to be.

It was, he admitted to himself, the most beautiful lie he'd ever planned to tell with machinery.

At forty-five pounds the compressor coughed, caught, and began to stroke. Sable laughed out loud, alone in the cab, a short bark of joy he hadn't heard from himself in years. "There you are," he told her. "There you are, old girl."

The drums filled. He rigged the cycling valve with a piece of clock spring and a cotter pin, set the interval, and stood back.

Boom. Boom. Boom.

The sound rolled out over the flats like the heartbeat of something enormous waking up. Three miles away, he'd have sworn, the sonar ticks stuttered.

"Go on," he breathed. "Chase it. Chase the ghost."

* * *

They came at two in the morning.

He heard them first as a change in the wind—a new note in it, the wet slap of hulls against water in a place where there shouldn't be water. Then searchlights, twin blades of white sweeping the dune line a mile south, converging on the sound. Sable flattened himself against the cab wall, heart hammering, and watched two Vane cutters slide up the flooded channel like knives drawn slowly from a sheath.

They were beautiful, in the way that sharks are beautiful. Low, black, hung with gear. Arc lights lancing down into the shallows. Men moving on deck with the economy of people who had done terrible things efficiently for a long time.

The lead cutter closed to three hundred yards of the derelict and stopped. A voice carried across the water, amplified, bored and dangerous: "*Unknown source, this is salvage vessel Kestrel, operating under Vane charter. Identify yourself.*"

Sable pressed himself lower. The cycling valve boomed on. Boom. Boom. Boom. From outside, he prayed, it sounded like a big engine laboring somewhere behind the hulk—like work, like weight, like a prize worth boarding.

"*Kestrel to boarding party. Put a boat over.*"

They were going to board her. They were going to find a dead locomotive and a cold firebox and—his stomach dropped—*the firebox*. Still warm. Still ticking with residual heat. And beside the track, a campsite with flatbread crumbs and mule dung and—

No time. No time to break camp, no time to do anything except the one thing he'd promised himself he wouldn't do, which was improvise with live steam.

The cutters' boat hit the shingle fifty feet from the loco. Four men, lanterns, rifles slung. Sable moved.

His plan, such as it was, lived in the gauges. Forty-one's pressure was at seventy pounds and climbing. The boiler was sound—he'd checked every seam, hammered every stay—but sound was a relative word on a ten-year-dead engine, and the safety valve was a question mark he'd chosen not to answer. Now the question mark had four men with rifles walking toward it.

If he opened the firedoor and drowned the fire, the pressure would fall and the boom-boom would die and the cutters would know instantly they'd been fooled. If he let it climb, the boiler might hold—or might, at some unpredictable poundage, decide to become a bomb with him inside it.

But there was a third option. There was always a third option; that was the whole religion of engineering.

The blowdown valve. The same one he'd cracked that first evening. Full-open, it would scream steam and drop pressure fast—but it pointed aft, toward the boat, and a jet of scalding vapor in the dark looked exactly like what it looked like: a boiler letting go.

He braced himself, said a quick apology to Wren for the noise, and hauled it open.

Forty-one screamed. A white lance of steam tore the night, whipping sideways in the wind, and the sound it made was apocalyptic—the death-cry of a machine, high and rising, echoing off the flats. On the water, the boarding boat slewed around. Men shouted. The Kestrel's arc light swung wildly.

And then, deep in the boiler, something went *clank*.

Not a loud clank. A small one. A wrong one. Sable's blood turned to ice water, because he knew that sound. Every engineer knows that sound. It was a stay bolt, somewhere down near the firebox crown, telling him the truth: the metal was tired, the pressure was high, and the conversation between them was over.

He shut the blowdown. Killed the fire damper. Grabbed the shovel and smothered the firebed in wet ash, working blind in the dark, steam still curling everywhere, and the whole time the gauge crept—eighty, eighty-five—and the clanking got company. A second bolt. A third.

"Come on," he whispered. "Come on, come on, come *on*—"

The world turned white.

* * *

Later, he would remember it in pieces, the way you remember any violence.

The sound first—not a bang but a *tearing*, like the sky ripping along a seam. Then weight, enormous weight, hitting him from behind and above, and the cab roof folding down over him like a lid closing on a box. Heat. A taste of copper and ash. The strange, floating calm of a man who has just understood that he is dying and finds, to his own surprise, that he is mostly annoyed about it.

Then cold. Cold water, shockingly, impossibly—cold hands dragging him by the collar through wreckage, over cinders, down off the running board into the shallow seep pool, and a voice yelling at him, raw and furious:

"*Breathe. Breathe, damn you—SABLE!*"

He breathed. It hurt like being born.

When his eyes focused, Wren's face was above him, black with soot, streaked where tears had cut channels through the grime. Her hands were shaking so hard she couldn't keep them on his shoulders. Behind her, Forty-one lay opened up like a dissected cadaver—the boiler shell peeled back along the crown seam, the firebox crown collapsed inward, steam rising off the wreckage in ghostly sheets. The blast had gone up and aft, away from him, because the torn stay bolts had been aft, because he'd been sitting forward, because of some small mercy in the geometry of ruin.

"The boat," he rasped. "The cutters—"

"Gone. They think she blew up with her crew aboard." Wren's voice cracked and steadied itself by force. "One of them shouted something about 'no survivors' and they ran for the ship. Your ghost train died very convincingly." She swallowed. "You almost died very unconvincingly. You were face-down in two inches of water and I thought—I thought—"

"I know." He tried to sit up and discovered that several parts of him had strong opinions against it. His left arm was burned from wrist to elbow, the skin tight and shining. Something in his side sang a thin bright note when he moved. "How did you find me? I told you to sleep."

"I did sleep. Until my bed exploded." She laughed, and it came out half sob, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I saw the flash. I ran. You were under the roof section. I couldn't lift it. I *couldn't lift it*, Sable, I thought you were—" Her jaw set. "I dug. I dug you out with my hands."

He looked at her hands. They were bleeding, the palms skinned raw, cinders embedded in the heels. She'd dug through hot wreckage barehanded.

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up one ruined hand, fierce. "Don't you dare say thank you like it settles something. Don't you dare make it a debt, because I have been keeping accounts since Port Calloway and I am *tired*, Sable. My father owed strangers his whole life and paid it with his blood in a culvert, and you owe me nothing, and I owe you nothing, and maybe that's the point. Maybe that's what family actually is—people who stop counting."

The word landed in his chest like a dropped anchor. Family. He opened his mouth and found nothing there, no clever deflection, no engineer's arithmetic. Just the ache.

"Your hands need binding," he managed finally.

"Your everything needs binding." She ripped the tail off her shirt without ceremony and started wrapping his burned arm, her movements rougher than they needed to be, which was how she said things she couldn't say. "We're both idiots. We're going to be idiots together from now on. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said, and meant it, and was frightened by how much he meant it.

* * *

Dawn found them in the lee of the wrecked tender, sharing the last of the flatbread and watching the Vane cutters withdraw down the channel, their searchlights clicking off one by one. The decoy had worked better than he'd ever dared hope. The fleet believed the ghost engine was destroyed—with its crew. For a day, maybe two, they'd sweep elsewhere, confident the flats held nothing but a grave.

Which gave them a day, maybe two, to reach the causeway.

Sable flexed his bandaged hand and studied the horizon, doing the arithmetic he didn't want to do. The causeway was a half-day's haul. Dess Corvane knew they were on the flats—had known since the waystation, since the forged map, since the moment his charm had slipped just far enough to show the knife underneath. And a man like Dess didn't wait at waystations forever.

Wren followed his gaze. "You're thinking about him."

"I'm thinking that we just spent our luck." He fed the last crust to the mule. "And that Dess doesn't believe in ghosts. Only in debts. And he thinks Ede's map owes him one."

Far off, at the edge of sight where the flats met the rising land, something glinted—not sunlight on water, but sunlight on steel. Small. Moving. Patient.

Wren saw it too. Her hand found his shoulder, steadying herself or him, he couldn't tell anymore.

"Sable," she said quietly. "Someone's already at the causeway."
