## 12. What Ede Never Said

The storm had passed them by in the night, sliding east along the coast like a thing with somewhere better to be, and what it left behind was a beach strewn with driftwood pale as bone and a sky scrubbed down to hard blue. They made camp in the lee of a fallen sea-stack, where the sand was dry and the wind broke apart overhead instead of finding their faces.

Wren gathered wood while Sable saw to the wagon—checking the axle that had taken punishment on the flats crossing, greasing what could be greased, muttering to the machine in the low voice he used for everything that mattered to him. She'd learned his moods by now, three days of travel and two of his failed attempts to abandon her. The muttering meant contentment. Silence meant worry. The particular silence he'd carried since the dune ridge, since the masts on the horizon, meant something worse than worry.

She built the fire small and hot, the way her father taught her—*smoke's a flag, girl, burn it low or don't burn at all*—and set the kettle over it. Then she sat back against the wagon wheel and took out the oilskin packet.

She'd been carrying it since Port Calloway. Since before she climbed into the supply wagon among the spare couplings and the tins of lamp oil, before she'd pieced together from Sable's careful face exactly what had happened in that drainage culvert. Her father's letters. Eleven of them, each one sealed with wax gone brittle as old honey, each one addressed to her in Ede Korrish's cramped, seaward-slanting hand.

She had never opened them.

That was the truth she kept turning over like a stone in her pocket: eleven letters, years of them, and she had never once broken a seal. Because opening one meant admitting they were all there would ever be. Because a letter you haven't read is still a promise. A letter you've read is just paper.

Sable came around the wagon, wiping his hands on a rag, and stopped when he saw the packet in her lap. He didn't ask. He was good at not asking; it was practically his native language. He lowered himself onto a piece of driftwood across the fire from her, accepted the tea she poured without comment, and looked out at the water with the expression of a man giving something privacy by pretending not to notice it existed.

"You knew him," Wren said. "My father."

"I knew him for about an hour." Sable turned his cup slowly. "Dying men talk fast. Trying to fit a whole life into the time they've got left."

"What did he say? About me?"

"That you were stubborn." A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite a smile, but related to one. "He said it like it was good news."

Wren huffed. It might have been a laugh. "He would say that."

The fire popped. Out past the tideline, gulls argued over something dead in the wrack. She worked her thumbnail under the first seal—the oldest letter, the wax gone gray—and felt her heart hammering like she was breaking into someone's house.

"His hands shook," she said, mostly to herself. "Near the end. You'd have seen it. He used to blame the cold."

"He blamed the cold," Sable agreed quietly.

The seal gave. She unfolded four pages covered edge to edge in that slanting script, and began to read aloud, because some things are too heavy to carry silently.

---

*"Wren—my girl—if you're reading this then either I'm dead or I've finally found my nerve, and knowing me it'll be both at once.*

*I have lied to you your whole life. Not the small lies, the kind a man tells to keep a child fed and hopeful. The big kind. I told you I was a prospector. I told you I hunted salvage, gold, whatever the sea kept in its pockets. I told you the wrecks I dove were about money.*

*They were never about money."*

Wren's voice caught. She steadied it. Across the fire, Sable had gone very still, the way he went still around anything that mattered—a mechanism, a threat, a truth.

*"I was a surveyor's clerk once, before you were born. Before the Consolidated Coastal came through with their charters and their ledgers and their guns dressed up as contracts. I helped draw the lines they used to take the land. That's the sin I've been diving under for twenty years, girl. I drew the map of the theft. I thought if I got far enough away from it, the ink would wash off my hands.*

*It doesn't wash off."*

She looked up. "He was a surveyor," she said. "For the railroad company."

Sable's cup had stopped turning. "Ede Korrish drew charts for Consolidated Coastal?"

"Apparently." Her mouth tasted strange. Twenty-two years of stories—her father the free diver, the salvage man, the romantic fool chasing sunken gold—and underneath all of it, a desk job for the empire. "Keep listening. There's more."

*"Here is what I never told anyone, because telling it gets people killed. In the last year before the lines closed, the Meridian Star ran the coast carrying charter documents between the company offices. Among them was the original landfall charter—the master document, signed and sealed, the one that shows every coastal grant from Halloway Point to the Salt Flats was obtained by fraud. Forged consent signatures. Communities that never sold a foot of ground, recorded as having sold it. My own handwriting on the survey overlays that made the fraud look legal.*

*The Star went down in the Flats with those papers still aboard. The company let it sink. Understand me—they LET it sink. A drowned charter can't be contested. Dead ships keep secrets better than dead men do.*

*But paper lasts, Wren, if the water stays off it. There's an air pocket in that hull. I've spent nine years finding my way to it. Not for gold. Gold is nothing. The charter is everything. With that document raised and read aloud in a public square, every stolen mile of coast belongs back to the towns it was taken from. Every town. Including—"*

Wren stopped.

"Including yours?" Sable asked softly.

"Including mine." Her voice had gone flat and strange. "Port Calloway. The net crews, the fisher families, the whole harbor quarter—they lost their foreshore rights in '61. My mother's family fished that water for six generations and then one morning a company clerk showed up with a paper saying they'd sold it. My grandmother spat at him. They took the boats anyway."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them the wagon ticked as its metal cooled. Sable set down his cup and leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for once he didn't look at the horizon. He looked at her.

"Go on," he said. "Read the rest."

---

*"You'll want to know why I never came home to tell you this. Why the letters instead of the man. Because the company has eyes, girl, and the moment I found the first fragment of the route into the wreck, I became worth more dead than alive. There's a salvage fleet—Vane's—that's been sweeping the Flats for years on the company's coin, pretending to hunt cargo while they hunt the same papers I do. If they raise that charter, it burns. If I raise it, it lives. That's the whole war, and I am one old man with bad lungs fighting it alone.*

*I made one partner along the way. Dess Corvane. He was true for a long time—or I needed him to be—but greed is patient and Dess is patienter. If you ever meet him, count your fingers after shaking his hand.*

*If I don't come home, find an engineer. The route into the wreck runs through the old seabed line—the signal box at Meridian Junction, the one they flooded when they pulled up the rails. I can't open it. It needs someone who thinks in machines, and I think in tides. Find a man who loves the track the way I loved the water, and trust him with the coordinates, because I won't be able to say them twice.*

*And Wren—my girl—whatever you feel when you learn what I was, don't you dare feel shame. Shame was mine to carry. Yours is the charter. Raise it. Read it loud. Make them give the coast back.*

*All my love, longer than the tide tables run,*

*Your father,*

*Ede"*

---

The last page fluttered in her grip. She realized her cheeks were wet only when a drop hit the paper and bloomed in the ink, and she wiped her face angrily with her sleeve, furious at the tears, at the letters, at the dead man who had loved her enough to lie to her for twenty-two years.

Sable hadn't moved. But something in his stillness had changed quality—from the stillness of courtesy to the stillness of a man recalculating everything.

"The signal box," he said. "Meridian Junction. That's on the map he gave me."

"You've seen it?"

"It's how he paid me. Wax-sealed chart, coordinates buried under three layers of cipher." Sable stared into the fire. "I thought it was treasure. Gold, salvage, something to sell in Calloway so I could hand you a bag of coins and walk away clean. That was the bargain. His share to his daughter."

"And now?"

"Now I know why Vane's fleet sweeps those flats year after year without ever raising anything worth the coal they burn." He laughed once, short and without humor. "They're not salvaging. They're erasing. Nine years of sonar sweeps and dredge runs, looking for paper."

"Not gold," Wren said. The word *charter* sat in her chest like a swallowed coal—hot, unswallowable, hers. "Paper."

"Do you understand what that document would mean?" Sable leaned forward, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "I've spent ten years riding lines the company closed. I've seen what happens to the towns when the rails stop. Calloway. Grayharbor. The whole settler coast—strangled on purpose, because a charter said the land was never theirs to begin with. One document in a captain's safe turns all of it upside down. Courts. Claims. Restitution." He shook his head slowly. "It's not worth gold. It's worth more than any gold I've ever heard of."

"My father died in a culvert," Wren said, "with this in his pocket." She held up the letters. "Shot by someone who wanted what he knew. And I sat in Port Calloway mending nets, waiting for him to come home with treasure, and he was out here dying for *us*. For the harbor quarter. For my grandmother's boats." Her voice cracked and she let it. "He never said a word. Not one word in eleven letters until the last one."

"People protect what they love by keeping it ignorant," Sable said quietly. "It's a disease. I recognize the symptoms."

She looked at him sharply. "What's that supposed to mean?"

He held her gaze, and for a moment she thought he'd answer—thought the whole shape of him, the running and the refusing and the leaving-before-dawn, might unfold right there in the firelight. Instead he reached over and nudged the kettle toward the flames.

"Nothing. Drink your tea."

"Sable."

"Wren." He said her name carefully, like setting down something fragile. "Tomorrow we cross to the junction box. Your father couldn't open it because he thought in tides. I think in machines. Between us we might manage what neither could alone. That's the work. Everything else—" he gestured vaguely at the night, the grief, the eleven letters, "—everything else keeps till the charter's in daylight."

But Wren wasn't finished. She gathered the pages, squared their edges with the care her father had used on his tide tables, and slid them back into the oilskin. When she spoke again, the tears were gone and something harder had taken their place.

"When we raise it," she said, "it doesn't go to a court first. It goes to the harbor quarter. My grandmother's grave first, then the fisher families, then the whole coast. Read aloud. Like he said. Loud."

"Loud," Sable agreed.

"And Vane's fleet doesn't get to burn it. Whatever it costs."

At that, Sable was quiet a long moment. The wind shifted, and far down the darkening beach something clattered—a loose sheet of tin, probably, or driftwood settling. But he turned his head toward the sound and held there, listening, and Wren watched the engineer in him surface: the man who counted exits, who weighed distances, who had already started solving tomorrow's problem tonight.

"They're a day ahead of schedule," he said at last. "Dess sold us out, which means Vane knows roughly where we're bound, even if she doesn't know why. By tomorrow night she'll have cutters working every approach to the junction line." He fed a stick to the fire. "We can't outrun them to the wreck. So we don't try."

"What do we do instead?"

"There's a derelict locomotive rusting on the old causeway spur, maybe four miles south. Boiler's intact—I checked it when we passed, habit. If I can get steam in her, I can make her sing on the old line. Fleet sonar reads a live engine on a dead track, they'll send cutters to investigate. Two of them, minimum. Maybe three." He glanced up, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the cold resolve from the ridge, but something brighter and more dangerous. "Every cutter chasing a ghost is a cutter not watching the junction box."

"You'd be on that locomotive," Wren said. "Alone. On a boiler you've never tested."

"I'd be on that locomotive," Sable confirmed, "doing the one thing I've ever been good at."

Wren looked at him—at this man who'd tried twice to leave her behind, who repaired track for strangers' suppers and vanished before dawn, who was now planning to sit himself on top of an exploding boiler to buy her father's charter one clear hour. Grief and fury and something that felt dangerously like faith all crowded together in her chest.

"Then I'm coming with you," she said. "I read currents better than any chart, and you'll need someone watching the water while you watch the gauges."

"We'll argue about it tomorrow."

"No," Wren said, and put the oilskin packet inside her coat, over her heart, where it belonged. "We won't."

Across the fire, Sable opened his mouth—she could see the objection forming, the old armor coming up—and then, for the first time since she'd known him, he closed it again. He looked at her sitting straight-backed against the wagon wheel with her father's letters against her chest, and whatever he saw made him nod, slow, like a man accepting terms he suspected were going to cost him.

"First light," he said. "We move."

Far to the south, past the black shoulder of the headland, a single light blinked on the horizon. Once. Twice. Deliberate.

Neither of them said what it was. Neither of them had to.

A fleet signaling its cutters home—or signaling them forward.
