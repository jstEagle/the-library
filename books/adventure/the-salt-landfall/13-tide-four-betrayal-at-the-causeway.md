## 13. Tide Four: Betrayal at the Causeway

The causeway showed itself an hour before low tide, a black spine of old rail bed rising out of the shallows like the back of something drowned and half-forgotten. Sable saw it first from the wagon seat and felt the familiar engineer's satisfaction—stone laid by men who'd believed the sea could be argued with—and beneath it, colder, the other feeling. The one that said *this is the only way across, and everyone who wants us knows it too.*

"We could go around," Wren said. She'd been reading the water again, kneeling in the wagon bed with her sleeves rolled, watching how the current bent around the sandbars the way other people read letters.

"By the time you went around, the tide would be back. We'd lose half a day." He flicked the reins. "Half a day is the wreck."

"Then we cross."

"Then we cross." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to the wrench under the seat, the one he'd been carrying since the waystation, since Dess Corvane's smile and the forged map folded into the man's soft white palm.

Dess had taken the forgery with both hands, actually, like a man receiving communion. That was what Sable kept turning over in his mind as the wheels found the first stones of the causeway. Not the threats—the charm. Dess had wanted to believe the copy was real, because believing it meant he didn't have to follow them anymore. A man like that didn't stay fooled. A man like that stayed *patient*, and patience on open flats with no cover was a knife waiting for a throat.

The causeway ran a quarter mile, maybe more, two walls of mortared stone with the old track still bolted down its center, rusted rails gleaming wet. On either side the tide pools lay shallow and glass-still, mirroring a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Their wheels clattered over the joints. Gulls lifted off the far end and wheeled, screaming, and did not come down again.

Sable noticed that last part. Birds don't leave a roost for nothing.

"Wren."

"I see it." She was already moving, low in the wagon bed, pulling the canvas tarp over the supply crates. "Far end. Three of them. Maybe four in the rocks below."

He counted without slowing—slowing told them you'd seen them. Four figures at the seaward end of the causeway, standing too casually beside a beached skiff, and two more crouched among the boulders on the north flank where the stone dropped toward the flats. Six. And leaning against the skiff, arms crossed, coat immaculate despite everything, a man whose posture Sable would have known anywhere: weight on the heels, chin up, the stance of someone watching a show he'd paid for.

"Dess."

"He was supposed to take your pretty forgery and run."

"He was supposed to." Sable's mouth was dry. "But I made one mistake building it. I gave him exactly what he asked for. Nobody trusts a bargain that comes easy."

The wind came off the open water and pushed against them, cold and smelling of iron and weed. Halfway across, the causeway narrowed where a section had collapsed into a tide pool—a gap of maybe eight feet, bridged by two rotting planks some fisherman had laid years ago. Sable reined in before it.

"There's no turning around now," Wren said quietly. "Not with them behind us if they've got a boat."

There was no boat behind them. Yet. But the tide was going out, and out there past the causeway the flats were beginning to breathe, acres of wet sand emerging from the retreating water, and any hull that drew more than two feet would be stranded until evening. That was their advantage. That was also why Dess was here instead of following—he'd known this was the choke point, the one place on the crossing where six tides of head start could be stolen back in ten minutes.

Sable stepped down from the wagon. "Stay with the mule."

"The hell I will."

"Wren." He turned and looked at her, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop arguing—not because she agreed, he could tell, but because she was learning him, the way he was learning her, and she knew he wasn't asking. "If this goes wrong, you drive east along the flats. Hard. You know the channel markers better than I do. You get to the Star without me."

Her jaw set. "You come back."

"That's not the deal."

"It's my father's map. It's my father's share. Everything on this wagon is mine by blood, Sable Oyelaran, so don't stand there making deals like it's yours to spend." She took the reins from his hand. "Go. Come back."

He went, because there was nothing else to do, and because some traitor part of him wanted very badly to come back.

* * *

The gap in the causeway was eight feet of black water over slick stone, crossed by planks that flexed like skin over a wound. Sable tested the first one with his boot, then his full weight, then walked it, arms out, the wrench hanging from his belt where a sword would hang on a better man. The wind tried to lean him sideways. Below, in the pool, something pale shifted—a crab the size of a dinner plate, or a hand. He didn't look twice.

On the far side the stone widened again, and forty yards on, Dess Corvane detached himself from the skiff and came strolling up the rail bed with his hands open and empty, the way he'd done at the waystation, the way he probably did everything, as if the world were a parlor and everyone in it a guest.

"Sable." Warm as banked coals. "I hoped it would be you."

"You hoped it would be anyone." Sable stopped ten feet away, out of lunging range, near enough to talk without shouting. Behind Dess, the four blades spread along the causeway edges with the unhurried economy of professionals. Two more were already moving along the north rocks, cutting off retreat to the flats. "The forgery wasn't good enough."

"The forgery was beautiful. Truly. The ink aging alone—" Dess shook his head, savoring it. "No, the forgery failed for one reason only. Ede Korrish never wrote coordinates left-handed. Thirty years I worked beside the man. He signed his own name right-handed and drew his lines left, every chart, every sketch. Your copy drew everything left-handed." He smiled, and it was almost fond. "You can forge a man's hand, friend. You can't forge thirty years."

"So you've been sitting here waiting."

"Since yesterday's tide. Do you know how many crabs I've eaten?" Dess glanced past him, toward the wagon, and the warmth in his face cooled by degrees, like metal losing the sun. "And the girl. Ede's girl. I confess I didn't expect that. He never mentioned her. Never mentioned anything, really, except the Star, and gold, and how we'd split it when—" He stopped himself, and something moved behind his eyes that might, in a better man, have been grief. "Well. When."

"When you sold him to the Vane fleet's scouts and let him walk into that culvert alone."

Dess sighed, genuinely regretful, a man disappointed in a colleague. "Ede was careless. Ede talked in taverns. I merely stopped pretending not to hear." He spread his hands again. "Here is what happens now, and I'll make it painless because I liked him, and because you built me such lovely work. You give me the true map—all of it, seal and all—and the girl keeps her father's share in coin, which I will personally deliver, minus my costs. You keep your life. She keeps hers. Everyone walks away richer except the dead, who don't count."

"And if I say the map isn't on the wagon?"

"Then I burn the wagon, and the mule, and we start the conversation again with less furniture." Dess's voice never rose. That was the worst of him; Sable realized his hands had curled into fists anyway. "I know it's not on the wagon, Sable. Look at yourself. Men who carry what matters keep it close." His eyes went, deliberate and slow, to the inside pocket of Sable's coat.

The wax-sealed map. The outer seal—Ede's crest pressed in grey salt-wax, the thing Dess had demanded at the waystation and been denied. Sable had carried it against his ribs for four days. It had stopped feeling like paper and started feeling like a bone.

Behind him, faintly, the wagon creaked. Wren, standing up in the bed. He didn't turn. Turning would tell Dess everything.

"You want the seal," Sable said. "Why? You had the forgery. You knew it was false. What does a piece of wax buy you?"

"A piece of wax buys me the *shape* of the truth." Dess took one step closer, and the nearest blade drifted closer too, matching him like a shadow. "Ede was cleverer than either of us ever credited. The seal isn't decoration, friend—it's the key. Press it into the wax at the inner fold and the heat of a palm opens the second layer. Without the seal, the inner coordinates are just paper wrapped around paper. With it—" he shrugged elegantly— "with it, even a man who memorized numbers is only carrying a memory. And memories can be persuaded out of people. Slowly. At leisure. On a boat."

The wind gusted. Somewhere below the causeway edge, water sucked and chuckled through the stones. Sable did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the waystation and hated the answer. Six blades. One wrench. Eight feet of planks between him and the wagon, and Wren on it, and the tide due to turn in under three hours. There was no version of this where he won clean. There was only the version where he chose what it cost.

"All right," Sable said.

Dess's eyebrows rose, pleased. "Sensible."

Sable reached into his coat—and pulled the map free, yes, but held it low, and as his arm came up he let the whole motion become a throw, the sealed packet spinning high and wide over Dess's shoulder, out over the north edge of the causeway, toward the rocks and the darkening water.

Every blade on that causeway looked up. Every blade moved.

Sable was already running the other way.

* * *

It should have ended there, honestly. He'd thrown the map into the sea—or seemed to—and the fight should have dissolved into men scrambling along rocks after a scrap of wax and paper. But Dess didn't scramble. Dess stood perfectly still while his hired knives broke formation, and his eyes came back to Sable with a new expression in them, the charm burned off at last, and what was underneath was patient and cold and very tired of being underestimated.

"The seal doesn't drown," Dess said. "Wax floats. Paper holds air. My men will have it in ninety seconds, and then, Sable, we finish our conversation about memories."

So it became a fight after all.

The first blade reached him at the gap in the causeway—a lean man with a hooked knife and salt-crusted boots, coming fast along the rail bed. Sable met him at the planks, where the footing was worst, and that was the whole of his strategy: bad footing, narrow ground, and an engineer's knowledge of exactly how much weight two rotten boards would hold. The man charged the planks at a run. Sable stepped aside at the last stride and swung the wrench low, not at flesh but at the plank itself, and the board split with a crack like a rifle shot. The knife-man went through it to the hip, screaming, tangled in the second plank, and Sable put a boot on his wrist until the hook-knife skittered away into the pool.

The second came from the rocks, vaulting up onto the causeway wall, and got a forearm across the throat for his trouble, and then it was grappling, ugly and graceless, two men rolling against the rusted rail. Sable's shoulder hit stone. The world rang. Hands found his collar, his hair; a knee drove into his thigh; and then the man's knife came around in a short, economical arc that Sable saw a half-second too late.

It opened his side, just below the ribs, a line of fire more than a cut, shallow and long, the kind of wound that doesn't stop you until it has finished soaking through everything you're wearing. Sable heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize. Then rage arrived, clean and simple, the oldest tool he owned, and he brought the wrench up under the man's jaw with everything behind it. The blade-man dropped like ballast.

Sable knelt on the causeway stones, one hand pressed to his side, breathing in wet red hitches. Across the gap, Dess was walking toward him. Unhurried. Alone—his men were down on the rocks, four of them combing the tideline for floating wax, and the fifth lay groaning by the broken planks. The tide pools had gone pink in slow ribbons.

"You've cost me two good knives and a hundred crowns in fees," Dess said, stopping at the gap's edge, on the far side of the ruined planks. Blood had spotted his cuff—one of theirs, flung wide. He looked at it with distaste. "And you haven't given me anything."

"You have the seal," Sable managed. "Or your dogs will. Isn't that what you wanted?"

"I wanted the map. The seal is the door, and you—" Dess tilted his head, studying him across the black water, and Sable watched the man do the same arithmetic he'd done, arrive at the same answer, and hate it. "You memorized it. Of course you did. An engineer. Numbers are your native tongue." He laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "Ede couldn't have picked a better courier if he'd tried. Did he try, I wonder? Dying in a culvert, handing his map to the one kind of man who'd carry it in his skull where no knife could reach it?"

"Ede picked a stranger passing on a closed line," Sable said. "That's all I was."

"No." Dess shook his head slowly. "Nobody's all anything. Here is the truth of our situation, friend, and I'll give it to you straight because you've earned straight dealing today: you have the numbers. I will shortly have the seal. Neither of us can open the Star alone—which means neither of us can afford to kill the other, which means—" he stepped back from the gap, spreading those empty hands one final time— "we are married, you and I. Until the wreck. Think on it."

He turned, whistled once, sharp, and his men began climbing back up from the rocks. And Sable, kneeling in his own blood on the drowned railroad his grandfather's generation had built and been robbed of, understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that Dess Corvane would follow them now, openly, all the way to the Meridian Star, because the seal would float and be found and the man would never stop smiling again.

The second was that the wound in his side was deeper than he'd told himself. When he stood, the causeway tilted. When he walked, the planks swayed though there was no wind in them. Wren was shouting his name from the wagon, small and furious across the water, and he raised one hand to show her he was alive, and the hand came away red, and he kept it raised anyway, because the alternative was letting her see him fall.

He crossed the broken planks on his knees, actually crawled the last three feet, and hauled himself up the far side with the wrench hooked over the rail like a climber's piton. Wren reached him before he reached the wagon. Her hands went to his side and came away scarlet and her face did something complicated—fear and fury fighting for the same ground.

"You threw it in the sea."

"They'll find it. Wax floats." He leaned on her, heavier than he meant to. "Which means he stops hunting us blind and starts following us. Which means—" the world grayed at the edges, and he gripped her shoulder to hold it steady— "which means from here on, we're racing him and the fleet and the tide, and the only map that matters is the one in my head. So if I die on these flats, Wren Korrish, you need to know the bearing. Listen to me. Listen—"

She shook him, hard, once. "You're not dying. You don't get to die. My father died so you wouldn't have to." She got her shoulder under his arm, and she was stronger than she looked, net-mender's strength, rope and salt and patience, and together they staggered to the wagon. "Get up on the seat. Press your hand there. Harder than that—you're an *engineer*, act like one."

He got up on the seat. He pressed his hand there. The mule leaned into the traces and the wheels crunched onto the old rail bed heading east, toward the deep flats, toward the drowned signal box, toward the Star, and behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound of Dess Corvane's men hauling a longboat off the rocks and the thin, carrying note of Dess himself, whistling.

Sable looked down at his soaked shirt, at the blood welling between his fingers with each jolt of the wheels, and did the only calculation left. Three hours to the signal box. The wound would slow him, weaken him, maybe kill him—but the numbers were safe, sealed in his skull where no knife could reach, and Dess had said it himself without knowing what he'd said:

Neither of us can afford to kill the other.

Until the wreck.

"Hold on," Wren said, and snapped the reins, and the Salt Flats opened ahead of them, vast and shining and hungry, as the fourth tide began to turn.
