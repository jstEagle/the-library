## 14. The Fleet's Offer

The gull came first.

Sable heard it before he saw it—a wrong-sounding cry, too flat, too steady, circling the driftwood camp twice at exactly the same altitude. Real gulls didn't fly like that. Real gulls didn't have brass fittings where their eyes should be.

He put a bolt through it with the hand-crank crossbow on the third pass, and the bird dropped into the salt crust fifty yards out and lay there ticking as its clockwork wound down.

"A messenger," Wren said, coming out of the lean-to with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, salt crusted white along her forearms. She'd been rendering brine for drinking water. She looked at the fallen gull the way other people looked at weather. "That's fleet work. Ilsa Vane doesn't waste clockwork on anything she doesn't mean to follow through on."

"It's been watching us since dawn."

"Then dawn was when she found us." Wren walked out across the crust, boots crunching, and picked the bird up by one wing. She turned it over in her hands, reading it the way her father must once have read tide tables—quickly, completely, without sentiment. "Harbor-make. Port Calloway foundry stamp. Someone sold her our heading."

"Dess," Sable said.

"Dess," she agreed, and set the gull down gently, almost respectfully, the way you'd set down a letter you didn't want to finish reading.

They had four days of supplies cached under an overturned dory, a half-mended wagon, and each other. That was the whole inventory of their war against the Vane salvage fleet. Sable stood in the white glare of the flats and felt the old familiar cold come up through his boot soles—the cold that had lived in him for eleven years, ever since the Marrow Line collapsed and he'd walked away from the screaming and never once looked back. The cold said: *you know how this ends. You know what you do now. You take the coordinates that live only in your head, and you walk north along the old seabed line until the salt becomes scrub and the scrub becomes road, and by the time anyone thinks to look for you, there will be nothing left of Sable Oyelaran but a rumor and a rusted maintenance car.*

The cold was patient. It had waited a long time. It knew all his exits.

But Wren was standing in the sun with her father's compass hanging open in her palm, and she wasn't looking north. She was looking at the horizon, where the sea breathed in and out beyond the flats' edge, and her jaw was set in a way that made something in Sable's chest hurt in a place the cold couldn't reach.

* * *

The terms came at dusk, riding the incoming tide.

Sable saw the boat first—a fleet cutter's long black hull sliding up a channel no chart would show, running dark, oars muffled. It grounded on the near shoal and two figures waded ashore carrying a lantern and a sealed tube. They stopped at the exact edge of bowshot from the camp, which told Sable they'd been briefed on his marksmanship, and one of them planted the lantern upright in the salt and the other drove the tube into the crust beside it like a flag.

Then they withdrew. No words. The cutter pulled back out into the dark channel and was gone, leaving only the lantern burning and the tide creeping up around it.

Wren wanted to go to it immediately. Sable caught her arm.

"It could be a shell charge."

"It could," she said. "It isn't. If they wanted us dead they'd have shelled the camp from the water at midnight and been done with it. This is negotiation. Ilsa Vane doesn't blow up things she's bargaining for." She paused. "She doesn't bargain for me, though. Not really. Open it."

He went alone, because if it was a trap it should cost one life instead of two, and because some part of him still moved on the old arithmetic even while another part watched it happen and hated it. He crossed the fifty yards with the crossbow slung and his ears straining for the click of a pressure plate, and there was none, and the tube was just a tube—brass, fleet-stamped, wax-sealed with the Vane sigil: a keel breaking a wave.

Inside, a single sheet of paper, written in a hand so controlled it might have been typeset:

*To the engineer.*

*You have my attention and my cutters' patience, which is more than most men earn. Understand your position plainly. My sonar sweeps cover forty square miles of these flats per day. By the sixth tide I will have found the Meridian Star whether you help me or not. Your head is worth nothing to me dead and less than nothing to you drowned.*

*The girl is currently aboard my flagship, alive, unharmed, and eating better than you are. She will remain so for exactly three tides. At the end of that time, either you deliver the wreck's inner coordinates to my picket boat at the Gannet Shoal beacon, or I return her to you the way the sea returns things it has finished with.*

*I am not a cruel woman. I am a thorough one. Do not confuse the two.*

*I.V.*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, though the words didn't change, and neither did the thing underneath them, the thing that had been waiting all day for permission to speak:

*Run.*

* * *

He didn't tell Wren right away. He sat by the fire with the paper in his lap and let himself do the thing he was best at in all the world, the thing that had kept him alive through collapses and cave-ins and eleven years of closed lines: he ran the problem.

Option one. He runs. North, tonight, alone, coordinates intact. The fleet can't search every drainage culvert between here and the mainland railhead; he'd made himself unfindable before, dozens of times, in worse country than this. In ten days he'd be a stranger with a new name in a town with no rails, and the Meridian Star would flood on the sixth tide and take the charter—and Ede Korrish's last truth, and Wren's inheritance, and whatever it was that had started growing in Sable's chest without his permission—down into the dark forever.

And Wren would drown anyway. Because Ilsa Vane did not make empty threats; everything about the woman's operation, from the clockwork gulls to the muffled oars, said a mind that wasted nothing, least of all cruelty deployed precisely where it would do the most work. If Sable vanished, Wren's death bought him nothing. It would be pure expenditure. Ilsa wouldn't even enjoy it.

So option one wasn't escape. Option one was just murder with extra steps, and the murdered party was everyone he—

Everyone.

He stopped there. Sat with it. The fire popped and spat salt.

Option two. He trades. Gives Ilsa the coordinates, takes Wren back, walks away clean. Except "clean" was a word that had never once applied to anything involving Dess Corvane or the Vane fleet, and a fleet that held the Meridian Star's location held the charter, and the charter was the whole reason Ede had bled out in a culvert rather than sell it. Handing it over meant Ede died for nothing. It meant the settler towns stayed robbed. It meant Wren spent the rest of her life knowing the man her father trusted had traded her father's life's work for her skin—which was, Sable admitted, exactly the trade he'd have made, and exactly the trade she'd never forgive, because Wren Korrish was not built for being saved at the expense of the truth.

Option three.

There was no option three yet. But there was a signal box on the drowned seabed line whose mechanisms he understood better than any man alive, there were sandbars out in that anchorage that shifted with every storm, and there were, scattered across these flats like seeds after a burn, people who owed the Vane fleet nothing but grief. A tide-pilot widow who could read channels blindfolded. Stokers who'd jumped ship in Coriander Bay and hadn't stopped running since. A stationmaster three days south who had offered him permanent work once, and whom he'd refused by dawn, because staying meant being known, and being known meant being lost.

The cold in his boots said: *you cannot coordinate strangers. You cannot trust them. You have never once asked anyone for anything you couldn't pay for, because asking is a debt and debts are how people get hold of you.*

Something newer, quieter, sitting beside the fire where Wren had been sitting an hour ago, said: *she stayed. On the causeway, with knives out, she stayed. And on the decoy line, when the boiler let go, she came into the steam for you. Nobody comes into the steam.*

Sable Oyelaran had spent eleven years perfecting the art of the exit. Emergency hatches on every engine he'd serviced. Back doors in every contract. A life arranged, like one of his own machines, so that it could always be abandoned at speed with minimal loss.

And now the machine had a part in it that didn't fit. A part that was currently asleep in the lean-to, breathing slow, with her father's compass clenched in one fist even in her sleep, and Sable found that he could not design her out of the system. Every route he calculated, she was already in it. Every exit map he drew included a second set of footprints, and when he tried to erase them his hand—actually, physically, his own hand—wouldn't make the marks.

"That's the trouble with building things," he said aloud, to the fire, to nobody. "Eventually they carry weight you didn't spec them for."

* * *

Wren woke near midnight and found him at the fire with the Vane letter flattened on his knee and a charcoal stub in his fingers, and she read his face the way she read currents—better than any chart.

"She has me," Wren said. Not a question.

Sable handed her the letter. He watched her read it, watched the fear move through her and get processed, converted, compressed into something harder, the way her whole body worked—nothing wasted, everything transmuted. When she finished she folded the paper along its existing creases with terrible neatness.

"She's right about one thing," Wren said. "By the sixth tide her sweeps find the wreck regardless. So the coordinates aren't leverage. They're a clock." She looked up. "Which means the only thing of value we hold is time. Three tides of it. What can you build in three tides?"

And there it was—the question no one had ever asked him. Eleven years, and every question had been *how fast can you fix it, how cheap, how quiet, then move along.* Never once: *what can you build?*

Sable looked out past the firelight to where the flats ran silver to the black line of the sea, and somewhere out there, anchored in a channel he could name from memory, lay a flagship with a girl's share of courage locked aboard it, and around it lay sandbars that moved like sleeping animals, and above it all hung a storm wall he'd been smelling in the wind since sundown, two days out, maybe three, building itself fat and slow over the warm water like a debt coming due.

"Ilsa Vane thinks she's negotiating with a man who runs," he said slowly. "Everything about her terms assumes it. The three-tide deadline. The picket boat at Gannet Shoal instead of a meeting. She's priced me as a coward, and she priced me right—for eleven years she'd have been right." He fed the charcoal stub to the fire and watched it flare. "But cowards run from danger. I've been running from something else this whole time. And she doesn't know the difference, because she's never met a man who builds emergency exits into everything except his own life."

"What does that mean?" Wren asked. "In terms of boats."

"It means," Sable said, "that tomorrow at first light we're going south, to collect a stationmaster who owes me a refusal. Then a widow who reads channels. Then whatever stokers Dess hasn't gotten to yet. And in three tides, Ilsa Vane is going to get her coordinates delivered to Gannet Shoal exactly as requested—" he smiled, and it was not a nice smile, it was the smile of a man looking at a boiler plate and seeing where it would fail— "delivered by a locomotive running hot down a line she doesn't know exists, ahead of a storm wall she can't outrun, onto sandbars I intend to introduce her cutters to personally."

Wren stared at him. Then, slowly, she grinned, and it was her father's grin, the one from the stories, the prospector's grin, the grin of a man who'd once talked his way past a customs house with a fish in his hat.

"You're going to ask them," she said. "For help. You're actually going to *ask*."

"I'm going to ask," Sable confirmed, and the word tasted strange in his mouth, like metal and rain, like the first minute of a storm. "God help me. I'm going to ask."

Far out in the dark, the sea began to speak in the low grinding voice it used before storms, and the tide came up the flats to touch the toes of their fire, and turned, and went back out again—one tide down, and the clock running.
