## 15. Crew of the Stranded

The tide-pilot's widow lived in a house built from the ribs of three drowned boats, and she answered the door with a gutting knife already in her hand.

Sable raised both palms. "Maren Voss? I need—"

"I know who you are." She didn't lower the knife. Behind her, lamplight fell through the doorway onto a floor of bleached planking, and Sable could see the shape of her life in it: charts pinned to every wall, tide tables inked on the ceiling beams where she could read them lying awake, a man's coat still hanging by the door six years after the sea took him. "You're the engineer who's been asking after stokers at the fish camps. The one whose girl got taken off the *Kestrel's* supply launch."

"Not his girl," Sable said. "My crew."

"Same thing, out here." Maren Voss stepped back and let him in, though the knife stayed loose in her grip like an afterthought. "Sit. Tell me why I shouldn't throw you back out."

He sat. The chair was made from a coil box; it smelled of tar and old rope. He told her everything—the wreck, the charter, Wren taken in the raid, Ilsa Vane's terms nailed to the waystation post in a captain's fair hand: *coordinates for the girl, sixth tide, or we dredge her out of the flats ourselves.* He told it plainly, without performance, because he had learned in the last two weeks that plainness was the only currency some people trusted.

When he finished, Maren turned her knife over once, twice.

"Ilsa Vane grounded my husband," she said. "Not her cutters—her *charts*. She bought the survey office's bad soundings and sold them up the coast as honest work, and Hal trusted them, and the *Kindly Tide* went down on a sandbar that wasn't on any chart worth the paper. Forty years piloting those waters." Her jaw worked. "They fished his coat out of the shallows. Just the coat."

"I'm not asking you to fight," Sable said. "I'm asking you to do what you've done your whole life. Read the water. I can't get to that anchorage without someone who knows where the flats will be shallow enough to run and deep enough to drown a cutter that follows us."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll go anyway and probably die, and Ilsa Vane keeps doing what she does, and nothing changes for anyone." He leaned forward. "But I don't think you're the kind of woman who says no. I think you're the kind who's been waiting six years for somebody to ask."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the flats breathed—the long exhale of the ebb, miles of wet silver opening toward the horizon.

Maren set the knife on the table between them, handle toward him, which he understood was either surrender or a test.

"The fleet anchors in Cutter's Bight," she said. "Four ships, maybe five now. You can't fight that."

"No. But I can ground it."

* * *

The stokers came to him, in the end, rather than the other way around.

He found the first two at the herring camp past Gray Shoal—a pair of fleet men gone over the side three nights before, hiding under tarpaulins among the fish racks, burned dark by boiler-heat and shaking with the particular fever of men who had deserted. Their names were Osk and Tammas, brothers, and when Sable crouched beside their shelter they reached for fish knives until he said the word *Wren*.

"She fed us," Osk said. His hands wouldn't stay still. "On the *Kestrel*, when they ran us double watches and the bread went green. She was a prisoner and she still cut her own ration in half for the stokers. So when the launch came back without her, and word went round the fire room about what the captain meant to trade her for—" He stopped.

"We banked the fires wrong on purpose," Tammas finished. "Slowed the whole squadron half a knot. Then we jumped."

Sable looked at these two hollow-eyed men who had wrecked their own livelihoods for a stranger's kindness, and felt something shift in his chest, some plate he'd welded over years ago groaning loose.

"I'm going into Cutter's Bight ahead of the storm wall," he said. "I need men who know fleet boilers from the inside. Not to fight. To *break* things quietly."

Osk grinned, and it was a terrible thing to see, all teeth and exhaustion. "Engineer, breaking things quietly is the only prayer we know."

By dusk there were four more: a harpooner discharged for striking an officer, a cook who could handle any small boat afloat, a signalman who had memorized Ilsa Vane's private flag codes during two years of pretending not to look, and a boy of perhaps sixteen named Finch who owned nothing but a slingshot and an encyclopedic knowledge of every channel marker on the coast. They gathered in Maren's boat-bone house while the wind rose, and Sable stood among them with chalk in his hand and Maren's charts unrolled across the table, and understood that he was doing something he had never once done in thirty-one years of keeping himself moving.

He was asking.

Not paying. Not trading. Not leaving coin on a table and walking away before anyone could learn his name. He stood in front of seven people who owed him nothing and said, "I can't do this alone," and the words came out rough, like a rusted bolt turning for the first time, and nobody laughed, and nobody left.

* * *

The stationmaster arrived on the midnight maintenance car.

Sable heard it first—that familiar clatter on the old spur line, wheels singing on rail he himself had re-torqued a season ago—and stepped out into the cold to see a single lamp swinging at the front of the little car, and behind the lamp, wrapped in three coats against the salt wind, old Hollis Brannigan, pumping the handcar lever with the stubborn rhythm of a man who had spent forty years making trains run on time through weather that wanted them dead.

"You refused my job offer twice," Hollis said, hauling the car to a stop at the end of the usable track. He climbed down stiffly. "Once by dawn, once by letter. Both times you were gone before the ink dried."

"Hollis—"

"Don't." The old man held up a hand. "I've watched you mend forty miles of dead line for strangers' suppers and never stay for the thank-you. I've watched you flinch every time somebody says your name too kindly. And now I hear you're mounting a raid on the Vane fleet with a widow and a handful of deserters, so I figure either you've finally lost your mind or you've finally found something worth standing still for." He reached into his coat and produced a canvas roll, which he tossed to Sable. It clinked. Tools—good ones, the station's best, missing from the inventory in ways Hollis would deny forever. "Either way, you'll want these."

"Why are you here?" Sable asked quietly.

Hollis looked out at the flats, black under the rising wind, then back at him. "Because forty years ago a man asked me to help him move a stranded train off a washed-out bridge, and I said the company wouldn't allow it, and we lost the bridge and the train and three good people waiting on permission." His voice didn't waver, but his eyes did, just slightly. "I don't wait on permission anymore. Where do you need me?"

Sable swallowed something that had been lodged in his throat since Ede Korrish died in a culvert holding his hand. "The decoy line," he said. "There's a derelict locomotive on the old seabed spur. If we can get steam in her, we can pull two cutters off our approach. It needs an engineer who knows dead track."

Hollis smiled like a man handed back a debt. "Son, dead track is the only kind I love."

* * *

They worked through the small hours in Maren's house, and it became, without anyone naming it, a war room.

Maren charted the storm wall's approach on her ceiling beams, reading pressure in her joints the way other pilots read glass. "It lands full force at the fifth tide," she said. "Which means the fleet will have every lantern doused and every lookout miserable. We ride the front edge of it—they'll never expect anything afloat in that."

Osk and Tammas drew the *Kestrel*'s deck layout from memory, arguing over hatch positions like brothers argue over everything. The signalman—his name was Pell—wrote out Ilsa Vane's private codes in careful rows: the pennant that ordered cutters to investigate, the lamp sequence that signaled all clear, the arrangement of lights that would make a derelict locomotive read, from a mile off, like a fleet tender running dark.

Finch sat in the corner sharpening a sling-stone against a whetstone and listening to everything, and when Sable caught his eye the boy said, "I know the channel markers in the Bight. Three of 'em are drifted off station. Fleet charts won't show it. Yours won't either." He nodded at Maren. "Hers might."

"They might," Maren said, and something almost warm crossed her face.

Sable stood back and watched it happen: seven strangers and an old man becoming a crew in real time, the way rails become a line—one length bolted to the next until suddenly there is somewhere to go. He had spent his whole life believing that crews were things you paid for, hired, released. That belonging was a station you passed through without stopping. Now he watched a widow pour tea for deserters, watched Hollis argue boiler pressures with Osk across a chart of stolen water, watched Pell teach Finch the flag codes out of pure delight at having a student—

—and understood that this was what Ede Korrish had been trying to tell him in a drainage culvert with his last breath. Not *deliver the money*. Not *keep the promise*. Something older and simpler: *find your people, engineer, before the tide comes in.*

"What's her location aboard?" Hollis asked, pulling him back. "The girl. When we take the anchorage, where do we find her?"

"Brig deck, most likely," Osk said. "Aft, below the quarterdeck. Two guards on the hatch, one inside."

"Three guards," Sable said. "And me."

They looked at him.

"I go in for her myself," he said. "You ground the fleet, break their boats, kill their lights. I go down the brig ladder and I walk her out. That part isn't strategy. That part is mine."

Maren studied him for a long moment, then rolled up her charts with the crisp authority of a woman closing a deal. "Then it's settled. Fifth tide, ahead of the wall. Engineer—you'll want to see what I've got in the boathouse."

* * *

The boathouse held the *Kindly Tide*.

Not the ship—her longboat, hauled out and turned hull-up six years ago, kept tarred and ready ever since, though Maren had never once launched her. Sable walked around her slowly, reading the lines of her the way he read rail gauge, and saw the care in every plank. A boat maintained for a voyage that would never happen, by a woman who couldn't bring herself to burn it.

"She draws eighteen inches light," Maren said behind him. "Twenty-two loaded. Shallow enough for the inner flats if the pilot knows what she's doing." A pause. "And she's fast, when someone rows her angry."

"It's perfect."

"It's a coffin I've been keeping aired." Maren put her hand flat on the hull, and her voice went low. "Hal would have laughed himself sick at the idea of me taking her out. Six years I've looked at her and seen the night the coat came ashore." She turned to Sable, and in the boathouse dark her eyes were bright and hard as ballast stone. "Tomorrow I'll look at her and see something else. That's what you've given me, whether we live or drown. Don't you dare apologize for asking."

Outside, the wind shifted, came around to the southeast, and began to build. Somewhere far off, beyond the flats, the storm wall was gathering itself over open water, and beyond it, anchored dark in Cutter's Bight, the Vane fleet rode at their cables with Wren Korrish locked below decks, counting hours.

Sable stood in the boathouse door and let the wind hit him, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. One boat. Eight people. Four ships. A storm. Odds that would send any sane man running for the open line and a new name.

He thought of Wren cutting her ration in half for stokers. Of Ede's hand going slack in his. Of Hollis saying *where do you need me* like it was the easiest question in the world.

"First light," he called back into the house, "we load her."

And from the dark, seven voices answered as one.
