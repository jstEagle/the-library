## 16. Tide Five: Storm Wall

The storm came off the deep water like a wall being pushed by God.

Sable stood on the causeway and watched it build—a bruise-colored rampart a mile high, lightning stitching its seams, the wind ahead of it already flattening the salt grass and dragging white spume off the flats in long banners. Behind him, the crew of the stranded made their last preparations in the lee of the old signal box, and he counted them the way an engineer counts rivets before a load test, because if he thought of them as people he would lose his nerve.

Maren Ostrow, the tide-pilot widow, stood at the bow of the flat-bottomed salvage barge they'd patched together from three wrecks and a prayer, reading the water with eyes that had outlived her husband's boat. The two deserting stokers—Brannic, huge and quiet, and young Pell with his burn-scarred hands—had the boiler lit and breathing, a temperamental thing salvaged from a dredge that had died on these flats twenty years ago. Old Hask, the stationmaster who had once offered Sable permanent work and been refused at dawn, gripped the throttle lever like a man holding onto a decade of quiet disappointment finally given somewhere to go.

And Wren was out there. Somewhere in that anchorage, locked below decks on Ilsa Vane's flagship or one of her cutters, alive because Vane wanted coordinates more than she wanted blood. For now.

"Wind's shifting," Maren called over the rising howl. "Storm'll hit the anchorage maybe two hours after we do. If we ride ahead of it, we arrive in chaos. If it catches us on open water—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

"Then we don't let it catch us." Sable swung up onto the barge. His knife wound from the causeway had been stitched with sail twine by Brannic, whose field medicine apparently consisted of sewing men like canvas, and every jolt of movement reminded him of it. He ignored it. There would be time to hurt later, if there was a later. "Hask, give me everything the boiler has. Not what's safe. Everything."

The old stationmaster's grin was the first genuinely happy expression Sable had ever seen on him. "Twenty years I've wanted someone to say that."

* * *

They ran ahead of the storm wall across the drowned seabed line, and it was the finest hour of engineering Sable Nakamura had ever been part of, though no journal would ever record it.

The flats at flood tide were a maze of channels and sandbars that shifted with every moon, but Maren knew them the way Ede Korrish had known the map—by inheritance, by grief, by years of piloting other people's boats through water that wanted them dead. She called the channels and Hask drove the barge through them, and Sable crouched at the bow with Wren's copy of the tide tables spread against the wind, calling corrections, his mind running the arithmetic of current and draft and freeboard like a locomotive at speed.

Behind them the storm wall ate the horizon. Ahead, the Vane fleet lay at anchor in the lee of Gullspit Shoal—a forest of masts, riding lights swinging, the flagship *Meridian's Bane* squatting at the center like a spider in its web. Two cutters patrolled the outer anchorage, their lanterns sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs.

"They'll see us coming," Pell shouted.

"No," Sable said, and felt the plan settle into place inside him, all its parts meshing, and for the first time since Ede Korrish had died in a culvert pressing a wax-sealed map into his hands, he felt something other than fear. He felt *audacity*, bright and clean as new rail. "They'll see something else. Brannic—when we clear the shoal line, kill the boiler. Full stop. We drift."

"Drift? Into an armed anchorage?"

"We drift," Sable said, "like wreckage. Because that's what the storm makes of everything tonight. And while they're squinting at us—" he pointed, back along their wake, toward the drowned signal box rising black out of the shallows a half mile astern, its lattice tower leaning like a drunk against the sky— "I'm going to make those cutters very busy."

* * *

He went alone, because no one else could do it, and because some part of him that had spent thirty years refusing help understood that this was the price of having asked for any.

The signal box again—the same flooded chamber where his knowledge had turned Ede's riddle into a door. But this time he wasn't opening anything. This time he was building.

The box had been more than a signal station; it had been a junction control for the old seabed line, wired into the trackside interlocking that once threw switches and set signals for trains crossing the flats. Most of that ironwork still stood, rusted but sound, running out along the old roadbed in both directions. And at the end of the seaward spur, where the line had once fed a coaling wharf, sat the thing Sable needed: a derelict switch engine, half-sunk in silt, its boiler intact, its whistle connected by buried pipe to a compressed-air reservoir that the tides had never quite managed to drown.

Ede's map had marked it. *Old Whistler*, Ede had written in the margin, in a hand gone shaky near the end. *Still holds air. Still sings.*

Sable waded chest-deep through the flooded box, found the air main, and opened it. The reservoir exhaled with a groan that he felt in his teeth—and then, when he seated the whistle valve just so, throttling the flow to a thin sustained scream, the *Whistler* began to sing.

It was not a ship's whistle. It was not a buoy bell. It was a locomotive whistle, high and mournful and utterly wrong for open water, and it carried across the anchorage like a ghost calling roll.

He'd planned for this part. What he hadn't dared plan for was how it would feel—standing hip-deep in a drowning room, hands on machinery sixty years dead, hearing it come alive under his fingers. Joy. That was the word. Pure, unreasonable joy, the joy of building something that works, and he laughed out loud into the dark, a wild sound torn away by the wind.

On the water, the patrol cutters' lights converged toward the sound. Toward the old wharf. Toward the channel that Maren had told him, an hour ago, ran between the Inner Bar and the Coalfish Shoal—a channel that at this stage of the tide was four feet deep, with sandbars on either side waiting like closed fists.

The cutters took the bait because the bait was perfect: a distress signal, in a storm, from a wreck site. Captains are trained to answer distress calls. It is the first thing they teach you and the last thing you forget.

Sable watched their running lights swing away from the fleet's heart, watched them enter the channel, watched the lead cutter heel as her helmsman tried to turn when the depth line sang too late—

The grounding was almost gentle. A shudder, a list, then the sickening grind of a keel biting sand, and the cutter settled onto the Inner Bar with her lights still burning, her crew suddenly very busy with pumps and panic. The second cutter, trying to swing clear, clipped the Coalfish Shoal's outer edge and slewed broadside, taking green water over her rails.

Two ships. Grounded without a shot fired, by a dead man's whistle and a living man's arithmetic.

Sable closed the valve. In the sudden silence he heard, faint across the water, the shouting begin.

* * *

The barge slid into the anchorage amid the confusion like a splinter working into skin.

Vane's remaining crews were scrambling—boats lowering toward the grounded cutters, lanterns swinging, officers shouting orders that the wind shredded. Nobody looked twice at a battered salvage barge driving hard through the rain-swept dark. In a storm, everything moving is assumed to be fleeing the weather.

Maren brought them alongside the *Meridian's Bane*'s shadowed stern quarter, where the cargo hatch stood, because Brannic had spent six years firing that ship's boilers and knew which watch kept the aft hold and which officer drank himself asleep by the second bell. The deck watch was one man, hooded against the rain, and Pell took him down with a boathook and a murmured apology before the man finished turning around.

Wren was in the aft hold. Of course she was—chained where she could hear everything, because Ilsa Vane was thorough, and thorough people keep their leverage close.

Brannic cut the lock. The hatch opened on darkness and the smell of bilgewater, and then a shape came up the ladder fast, wet-haired, wrists raw from irons, and Sable's chest did something complicated and painful when he saw her.

"You're late," Wren said.

"Traffic," Sable said.

Her eyes went past him, quick and counting—Maren, Brannic, Pell, Hask—and Sable watched her understand what she was looking at. Not a rescue party. A crew. Her father's promise, delivered by strangers who had stopped being strangers somewhere on the salt.

"You grounded the cutters," she said. "We heard the whistle from the brig. Da used to talk about the old Whistler. He said it still sang." Her voice caught, just once. "He was right."

"There's no time—" Sable started.

The storm wall arrived.

It hit the anchorage like a fist, rain going horizontal, the fleet's riding lights vanishing one by one into the murk, and above the roar came a sound that froze them all: the groan of the *Meridian's Bane*'s anchor chain, bar-taut, beginning to drag. The flagship was moving. Being pushed. Straight toward the grounded cutters and the shoals beyond.

"Back to the barge!" Maren screamed. "Now! If she comes down on us—"

They ran. Behind them the great ship's hull loomed out of the rain, closer than any hull should be, and Sable shoved Wren over the rail ahead of him and felt the barge tear free of the fenders as Hask gave the boiler everything and then some.

They cleared the *Bane*'s drifting bow by a length. Maybe less. Then they were running before the storm again, out of the anchorage, into the dark, with the wind screaming and Wren laughing—a wild, disbelieving laugh, the laugh of someone who had spent days chained in a hold listening to the sea decide whether she lived.

Sable looked back once. The flagship dragged past her grounded cutters, missing them by yards, and beyond her, through a tear in the clouds, the drowned signal box stood black against the lightning—one tide left, and the *Meridian Star* waiting beneath the flats with her safe unopened.

Then Wren's hand closed on his arm, and her face, lit by the boiler's glow, had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with the storm.

"Sable," she said. "There was another prisoner in the hold. They put him in yesterday, after the raid. He told me your name before they chained me next to him." Her grip tightened. "It was Dess. And he's not aboard anymore—he paid his way off at the supply raid, before they took me. He knows where the *Star* is. He sold me to buy himself a boat."

The storm roared. One tide left.

"And Dess," Wren said, "is already sailing for the wreck."
