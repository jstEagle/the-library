## 17. The Meridian Star

The tide left us ninety minutes.

I stood at the edge of the drowned channel with the map spread across my forearms, the wax seal broken, Ede Korrish's careful hand pointing down through forty feet of green-black water to the shape that had killed him. The Meridian Star. Not a legend. Not a drunk prospector's fever dream. A hull, sitting upright in the silt of the old seabed line, exactly where the map said it would be.

The water over the flats was shallow here — waist-deep at the channel's lip, deepening fast where the old navigation cut had been dredged a century ago and then abandoned to the sea. Through the surface I could see the ghost of it: a dark ridge, too straight to be rock, running north to south along the channel floor. Masts had rotted away or been salvaged long since. What remained was the bones of her, and whatever the bones were still holding.

"She's really there." Wren stood beside me, boots already wet, her father's oilskin coat buttoned to the throat. Her voice was very quiet, the way people talk in churches and graveyards. "He wasn't lying. All those years he wasn't lying."

"He was lying about a lot of things," I said. "Just not this."

The crew was already working. Marta Osei, the tide-pilot widow, had the dive lines rigged off the wagon's winch with the economy of a woman who'd buried a husband to this water and refused to let it take anything else. The two deserters from Vane's stoker deck — Brannic and the boy they called Tally — hauled the air barrels down the shingle, their fleet tattoos scrubbed with salt and lye but still faintly visible, brands they'd carry to their graves. Old Ferrow, the stationmaster who'd offered me permanent work a lifetime ago in another chapter of my life, stood watch on the dune ridge with a spyglass and a rifle he held like he'd actually used one before.

One tide left. The sixth. After tonight the channel would silt over for the season, and the wreck's air pocket — if there was still an air pocket — would flood for good. And somewhere out past the horizon, Ilsa Vane's flagship was burning coal toward us with the patience of a shark that had already smelled blood.

"Air's good for maybe twelve minutes on the barrels," Marta said, checking the valve on the old hard-hat rig we'd pulled from a flooded depot two chapters of this journey back. "Fourteen if you don't panic. Panic eats air faster than work does."

"I never panic," I said. "I worry with excellent posture."

She didn't laugh. None of them laughed anymore. That was how I knew we'd become a crew instead of a coincidence.

* * *

Going down was like sinking into a held breath.

The hard-hat rig was older than I was, brass and canvas and a faceplate scarred with a century of salt. The air hose snaked up to the surface barrel where Tally worked the hand pump in a rhythm Marta had drilled into him. Below ten feet the light went from gold to green to a darkness with weight to it. Below twenty feet I was walking on the seabed of a world that had once been dry land — the old coast road, drowned when the empire moved the sea walls and let the flats take what they took. I could feel it under my boots: rail bed. Ballast stone. The twin threads of track, rusted to lace, running off into the dark toward a station that had been underwater since before my grandfather was born.

I had spent my whole life on railroads, and I had never once stood on one like this.

The wreck rose out of the gloom at the edge of my lamplight, and I stopped walking.

She was a coastal steamer, maybe two hundred feet of her, lying on her starboard side with her port rail toward me like a man offering his hand. The hull was furred with barnacle and weed, the planking gone soft in places, but her lines were still proud under all that ruin. You could see what she'd been. Fast. New, once. A ship somebody had loved and somebody else had written off in a ledger.

And she was full of light.

Not real light — my lamp catching on something. I moved closer, and the something resolved, and my breath went shallow in the helmet, which Marta had specifically told me not to do.

Gold.

It was spilled down the wreck's interior like a landslide that had frozen mid-fall. Coin chests burst along their seams, bars of it half-sunk in silt, a scatter of it glittering in the beam like the wreck had been gut-shot and this was what bled out. Ede Korrish had been right. The Meridian Star's salvage was real, and it was worth more than every rail line I'd ever repaired put together.

I stood in the middle of a dead ship's fortune and felt — nothing. Or not nothing. I felt the engineer's eye going past the gold, along the deck beams, to the structure aft. Because Ede's map hadn't ended at the coin. There'd been a second mark, small, precise, in the captain's country. And Wren's letters — the ones she'd read to us by driftwood fire, the ones that had turned treasure into purpose — said the Star carried more than gold. It carried a charter. Paper. Proof that the coastal lines had been stolen, signed and sealed, from the settler towns that built them.

Paper doesn't survive forty feet underwater for decades. Unless somebody put it somewhere built to keep things alive.

The captain's safe would be an air pocket. If the compartment hadn't flooded. If the seal had held. Ifs, stacked like coin.

I found the companionway by feel, my gloves walking the ladder rungs, and went up into the ship's body — which, flooded and dark and breathing through a hundred wounds, still felt more like a ship than any place I'd lived in ten years. My lamp found the chartroom door hanging by one hinge. Beyond it, the captain's cabin, and against the aft bulkhead, exactly where Ede's mark said it would be:

The safe.

Iron, riveted, a dial worn smooth by a dead man's hand. And — I put my palm flat against it, hardly daring to believe — dry. Cold, but dry. The compartment's roof had collapsed in a way that sealed the cabin like a jar, and forty years of the sea had pressed against the door and never gotten in. Inside that iron box was a piece of paper that could give a coast back to its people.

I worked the dial. The mechanism was stiff, salted, half-seized. An engineer's problem. I've spent my whole life listening to locks and boilers and gear trains tell me where they hurt. I closed my eyes inside the helmet and listened, and the tumblers told me, and on the third try the door swung open with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside: a ledger, swollen but legible. A pistol, fused into a single rusted shape. A strongbox, small, lead-lined, sealed with the double-eagle stamp of the coastal charter commission.

And paper. Reams of it. The charter itself, folded in oilcloth, and beneath it — my lamp beam trembled and I let it — letters. Dozens. In a hand I knew, because I'd been reading it for six tides on a wax-sealed map. Ede Korrish had been here before. Or someone had carried these aboard and left them for the finding. Either way, the proof wasn't just the charter. It was the whole confession, signed by the men who'd stolen the coast, filed in a dead ship's safe like a verdict waiting forty years for a courtroom.

I took the oilcloth packet and the lead box and left the gold.

That was the moment, I think. Standing in a flooded cabin with a fortune at my feet and a piece of paper in my hands, choosing the paper. Every version of me I'd ever been — the drifter, the runner, the man who fixed track for food and vanished before dawn — would have taken the gold. It would have bought a life with no rails in it, no names, no debts.

I left it sitting in the dark for whoever came next, and I went up the line.

* * *

I broke the surface to shouting.

Wren hauled me over the shingle by the air hose while Tally kept pumping like his life depended on rhythm, and I got the helmet off and the world came back — wind, grey light, the smell of salt and coal smoke.

Coal smoke.

I turned. Ferrow was coming down the dune at a run, spyglass in one fist, rifle in the other, and behind him, out on the grey line of the horizon, three masts had grown where two had been an hour ago. The flagship. Ilsa Vane's *Ledger's Due*, black-hulled, running before the wind with her salvage derricks already swung out like a mantis raising its arms.

"She made nine knots on that last leg," Ferrow said, breathing hard. "Nobody makes nine knots in this wind unless they've a pilot who knows the flats better than we do."

Dess Corvane. Of course. He'd lost the map's outer seal to us at the causeway, but Dess had spent twenty years running these waters with Ede Korrish, and a man like that doesn't need coordinates. He needs a heading and a grudge. We'd given him both.

"How long?" Wren asked. She had the oilcloth packet pressed against her chest with both hands, like a child holding something living.

"Hour," said Marta, eyes on the water, on the tide line, on the arithmetic every flats-dweller carries in their bones. "Maybe less. Tide turns early with this wind behind it."

An hour. One dive's margin, and a fleet closing, and a partner-turned-predator somewhere between us and the water. I looked at the wreck's dark ridge beneath the surface, at the gold I'd left lying in it, and understood with a cold, clean certainty exactly how the next hour would go.

"Dess isn't coming for the charter," I said. "He doesn't know it exists, or doesn't believe it. He's coming for the gold. And when he gets here and finds a fortune sitting in an open hull with one tide left to take it—" I looked at my crew. My crew. When had that word stopped being a lie? "—he won't come alone. And he won't come politely."

"Then we take the gold too," Tally said, and reached for a dive line.

"No." Wren's voice cracked across the shingle like a whip. Everyone stopped. She was looking at the water, at the fleet, at me, and her father's coat was too big for her and she wore it like armor anyway. "The charter is what matters. The charter is what he died for. The gold is bait — it's always been bait. It's what brought Vane, what brought Dess, what killed my father. If we're still here when they arrive, we die for it too, and the paper drowns with us."

"She's right," Marta said quietly. "Tide-pilot's word. We run now, we live. We run heavy, we sink."

I looked at the horizon one more time. The flagship's masts had resolved into hull. I could make out the derricks. I could almost make out the men.

One tide left, and I had spent my whole life knowing exactly how to leave a place. It was the one skill I'd never let rust.

"Pack it," I said. "Charter, letters, strongbox. Lines and barrels last. Nobody dives again — we're done with the water." I turned to Ferrow. "The maintenance car's on the old spur behind the ridge. Can she run?"

Ferrow's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "She's been running since before you were born, lad. Question's whether the spur has."

"Then that's the question we answer in the next forty minutes." I hefted the lead strongbox — heavier than paper had any right to be, the way the truth always is — and looked back once at the Meridian Star, lying in her grave with her fortune spilled around her like a spent argument.

Behind us, faint over the water, came the sound I'd been dreading: a boat hull grounding on shingle, and a voice I knew, carrying across the flats with all its old charm burned down to something raw.

"Sable! You thieving son of a stationmaster — that gold has my name on half of it!"

Dess Corvane had arrived. And behind him, black against the grey, the *Ledger's Due* was standing in toward the flats with her anchor lights burning in daylight — a fleet coming to take possession of everything, the wreck, the water, and if they caught us, the paper that could hang them all.

I put the strongbox in the wagon, put Wren in the wagon, and put the spur under the maintenance car's wheels as the tide turned and the siege began.
