## 18. Sixth Tide: The Flood

The water came for them the way debt comes for a debtor—slowly at first, then all at once.

Sable felt it before he saw it: a change in the pressure of the air pocket deep in the *Meridian Star*'s hold, a faint thickening against his eardrums like the whole drowned ship drawing breath. He stood on the collapsed cargo grating with the captain's safe open at his feet, the charter tube in one hand and Ede's gold bars stacked in a canvas sling beside it, and he listened to the sea begin its final accounting.

"Sixth tide," Wren said beside him. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around the dive lamp's handle. "It's starting."

Above them, through three decks of flooded corridors and the gray-green lens of the shallows, the storm wall that had carried them here was still breaking over the flats. Even down here he could feel it—a rhythmic shudder through the hull, vast and patient, the ocean rolling its shoulder.

"We have maybe forty minutes of air pocket," Sable said. "Less if the surge pushes through the forward hatch."

"Then we go now." Wren slung the gold across her back. "You take the charter."

He took it. It weighed almost nothing. A rolled sheaf of vellum in a brass tube, and it had cost six tides, two fleets, one betrayal, and very nearly everything else. He sealed it into the oilskin wallet strapped against his chest, where Ede's map had ridden since a drainage culvert outside Harrow Bend.

They were twenty feet from the ascent line when the first boarder came through the stern breach.

* * *

Sable heard him before he saw him—the scrape of boots on the promenade deck above, transmitted through the hull like a whisper along a wire. Then a second set. A third. The sound of men who had trained on salvage wrecks and knew how to move inside one: quick, economical, unafraid of the dark.

"Dess," Wren breathed.

"And Vane's people with him." Sable killed his lamp. In the sudden black, the air pocket glowed faintly green from the light filtering down through the flooded skylights, enough to see shapes. "How many?"

Wren had grown up reading water; apparently she could read sound in it too. "Six. Maybe eight. Two coming down the main stairwell. The rest working aft toward us."

The math was ugly. One ascent line, rigged through the foremast companionway. Eight armed men between them and it. Above, at the surface, the crew of the *Stranded* waited with two longboats riding the last calm trough before the storm wall's leading edge—and Ilsa Vane's flagship *Ledger* lying off the site like a knife laid flat on the water, her remaining cutters circling.

"They'll want the charter," Sable said. "Dess wants it because it's worth more than the gold. Vane wants it because it ends her."

"And they'll want us dead so nobody can say what it says."

"Yes." He said it plainly, because Wren had earned plainness. "So we don't give them a fair fight. We give them an engineering problem."

She turned to look at him. Even in the green half-dark he could see her working it out—she'd spent a week watching him think, watching his hands move over mechanisms, and she was faster at following him now than anyone he'd ever worked with.

"The bulkhead," she said slowly. "Aft of the old boiler room. You said the frame was rotten."

"It's not rotten. It's *sound*. That's the problem—it's holding back the flooded section behind it, and it's only doing that out of habit." His mind was already moving through the ship the way water moved through it, finding the paths. "If I drop it—if I let the sixth tide into this compartment ahead of schedule—anything between that bulkhead and the stern breach goes under with them. And this compartment floods too. Eventually."

"How eventually?"

"Minutes. Not many."

Wren went quiet. He watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he'd already finished, and he watched her refuse to say it aloud, and something in his chest that had been locked shut for thirty years broke open quietly, without drama, the way good welds fail—not with a bang but with a slow giving-way.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"*No.* We both go up the line. We blow the bulkhead from above. There has to be—"

"There isn't. The release is a manual dogging lever on the far side. I designed worse doors than this for the Kestrel spur; I know how they die. Someone stands here and pulls it, and someone stands here when the water comes." He took her shoulders. Through the soaked wool of her coat he could feel her heart going like a hammer on a tie-plate. "Listen to me. Your father gave me one job. One. Everything else I've done since—that was mine to choose. This is mine too. But you have to let me choose it, or none of it means anything."

"I'm not letting you drown in my father's wreck."

"It's not his wreck anymore." He smiled, and found, to his genuine surprise, that the smile was real. "It's ours. All of ours. That's rather the point of the charter, if you think about it."

Above them, boots crossed the promenade deck. A voice called down the stairwell, echoing strangely through the flooded decks—Dess Corvane's voice, silk over gravel, pitched to carry:

"Sable! I know you're down there, you stubborn rail-rat! Give me the tube and walk away rich! Last offer I'll make clean!"

Sable looked at Wren. Wren looked at Sable. And then she did the thing that undid him completely: she nodded once, sharp as a signal flag, and said, "Then we do it your way—but I'm setting the charge, because your hands are shaking and mine aren't."

His hands weren't shaking. But he let her believe it, because it made her brave, and God knew she'd earned the right to be useful in the end of things.

* * *

The aft section of the *Meridian Star* had been a palace once. They could see it in the bones of the place even now, drowned and furred with salt-moss: the sweep of the grand stairwell descending into green murk, the gilt gone to tarnish, the chandeliers hanging like jellyfish. Ede Korrish had crawled through this ballroom a dozen times over ten years, mapping it by memory and lamplight, and he had never told his daughter stories about ballrooms. He'd told her about engine rooms. Sable understood why, suddenly, moving through the dark with the girl herself at his shoulder: some things you keep out of the stories so they stay yours.

They reached the boiler room bulkhead in four minutes. It rose out of the black water like a cathedral door—riveted plate, twelve feet high, sweating brine, holding back the drowned aft compartments by nothing but its own stubbornness and sixty years of rust acting as glue.

Sable put his palm flat against it. Cold as a grave lid. Beyond it, he could hear the sea pressing—actually hear it, a low sustained groan in the plate, the voice of the sixth tide asking to be let in.

"The dogging lever's on the other side of that access trunk." He pointed to a narrow vertical shaft, barely wider than a man's shoulders, running up through the bulkhead's spine. "I go up, pull it, the dogs retract, and the water does the rest. You get back to the ascent line. When this compartment starts flooding, you climb, and you don't stop climbing no matter what you hear behind you."

"What will I hear?"

"Me," he said honestly. "Climbing faster than you."

That was the lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so. The access trunk was a one-man shaft, and the lever needed weight held on it while the dogs retracted—ten seconds, maybe fifteen, with a wall of seawater arriving on the other side. Whoever pulled it would be standing in the throat of the flood.

Wren rigged the last of their flare powder against the bulkhead's lower seam instead—not to drop the plate, but to buckle it, to buy him seconds, to make the water come crooked instead of straight. She worked fast and precise, tying off the fuse with the same neat knots she used on netting, and when she finished she stood up and looked at him with her father's eyes, which was the cruelest thing about her.

"Ede sent you," she said. "And you came. Nobody comes. Everybody I've ever known talks about coming and nobody comes, and you—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "You came, and you stayed, and you built a crew, and you taught me how a signal box works, and now you're going to stand in a hole in a drowning ship and pull a lever, and I'm supposed to climb a rope and be grateful."

"Yes," Sable said gently. "That's the shape of it."

"I hate you."

"I know." He touched her cheek, brief as a spark gap. "Go be stationmistress of something someday. Go make them read the charter out loud in every town on the coast until the words wear grooves in people. That's how you thank me. Not with grief. With *use*."

From the direction of the grand stairwell: voices, closer now. Lamplight wobbling across the flooded ceiling of the ballroom, reflected and broken into trembling coins of gold. Dess's boarding party had found the deck plan in their heads, and they were coming down.

Wren kissed him on the mouth—quick, fierce, a signature on a contract—and shoved the flare fuse into his hand. "Thirty seconds after I'm clear, I light it from the line. Buckled plate buys you time. Use it."

Then she was gone, up through the dark toward the ascent line, and Sable Oyelaran stood alone in the boiler room of the *Meridian Star* with a brass tube of stolen history against his chest and a dogging lever waiting above his head, and he found that he was not afraid.

That was the strange part. Thirty years of running, thirty years of keeping his name small and his bags packed and his heart in escrow—and now, at the exact moment the bill came due, there was nothing in him but a vast, quiet clarity, like the flats at slack water. He thought of the stationmaster at Harrow Bend offering him permanent work, and how he'd been gone by dawn. He thought of the maintenance car, retired now or soon to be, sitting in the rain somewhere on the ridge with its wheels still pointed at the horizon. He thought: *I finally stopped.*

He climbed into the access trunk.

* * *

The lever was cold and heavier than it looked. Sable wrapped both hands around it, set his boots against the trunk's rungs, and looked down through the grate at the black water rising in the boiler room—already ankle-deep, already climbing, the flare powder's fuse hissing where Wren had lit it true.

Below and beyond, lanterns swung into the ballroom. He heard Dess Corvane's voice, much closer now, bright with triumph and thin with greed: "There! The boiler room—they're at the bulkhead—*move*, you idiots, move—"

Boots splashing. Six men, maybe seven, wading into the compartment that Sable was about to turn into the sea's front hall.

He could have warned them. Some distant, decent scrap of himself considered it. But these were men who had burned Ede's camp, who had hunted a dying prospector's map across six tides, who served a fleet that treated the coast as a larder—and Dess had held a knife to Wren's throat at the causeway and smiled while he did it. Decency had a ledger too, and theirs was long past settled.

"Sable!" Dess shouted, spotting the glow of his lamp high in the trunk. His voice went sly and pleading at once, the charm laid on thick as paint. "Whatever you're doing—don't. We can split it. Charter, gold, all of it, fifty-fifty, man to man, the way it should have been from the start—"

"You never wanted man to man," Sable called down. "You wanted man over man. Ede learned that the hard way."

"He learned it because he was *soft*—"

"He learned it because he trusted you." Sable set his weight against the lever. "I'm choosing better."

The flare powder went off below with a whump of orange light, and the bulkhead screamed—a long tearing shriek of stressed plate, the lower seam buckling inward, the water beyond it finding the flaw the way water always finds the flaw. And in that same instant Sable hauled the dogging lever down with everything his shoulders had left.

Ten riveted dogs retracted, one after another, each with a sound like a rifle shot.

The bulkhead fell.

Not toppled—*detonated* inward, twelve feet of plate driven before a fist of black water, and the sixth tide entered the *Meridian Star*'s stern the way judgment enters a courtroom. The ballroom's drowned chandeliers swung wildly. Lanterns went out. Men screamed, and the screaming lasted exactly as long as it took the compartment to fill, which was not long, because the sea does not negotiate and does not gloat and does not care whose side you think you're on.

Dess Corvane's last words were lost under the roar. Sable didn't try to catch them. Whatever they were, the sea would keep them.

The water hit the access trunk four seconds later.

It came up the shaft like a piston, and it took Sable's legs, his waist, his chest, slamming him against the trunk's upper grate with a force that emptied his lungs entirely. He got one arm through the grate, hooked it, and held—held while the flood boiled past him, held while the pressure tried to peel him loose like a label off a bottle, held while the air pocket above him shrank to a pocket's shadow, a dome of trapped breath the size of a coffin lid.

Through the grate beneath him, in the strobe of the last flare light, he saw the flooded compartment settle. Saw shapes drifting in it that had been men. Saw the gold sling—no. No, the gold was gone. Wren had it. Wren was climbing.

*Good girl,* he thought, and his vision went gray at the edges, and the water reached his chin.

* * *

The air pocket was the size of a barrel head and shrinking.

Sable pressed his face into it and breathed in shallow sips, tasting rust and brine and the chemical tang of the flare smoke. Below him the trunk was full, solid water, a column of the sea standing on end. Above him, the grate into the old dynamo room—and beyond that, forty feet of flooded corridor to the ascent line, and Wren somewhere at the top of it, alive, because he could not bring himself to imagine otherwise.

The dynamo room grate was bolted. Of course it was. He'd have bolted it too.

His free hand found the wrench still clipped to his belt—he'd stopped noticing years ago that he never went anywhere without tools, the way other men never went anywhere without knives—and he set it to the first bolt and turned until the gray closed in from all sides, and turned, and turned.

One bolt. Two. Spots of light blooming and dying behind his eyes. Three.

The fourth bolt defeated him twice before his fingers remembered they belonged to a man who had rebuilt track alone in the rain for fifteen years, and then it gave, and the grate swung, and Sable poured himself through the hole like the last of the water in a broken cask.

The corridor was flooded to within a foot of the ceiling. He swam it the way he'd done everything in his life—badly, stubbornly, with an engineer's contempt for elegance—following the guide rope Wren must have paid out, hand over hand, lungs burning, the charter tube a hard comforting bar against his ribs.

Halfway, his boot caught on a stanchion and he went under, swallowed, spinning, and for one long moment the sea had him entire, and he thought with strange detachment: *so this is what it costs.* Then his hand found the rope again, because his hand had always found the rope again, and he clawed back into the corridor's thin margin of air and kept moving.

The ascent line's base was a knot of lamplight. Wren's face appeared over the edge of the companionway hatch, haloed in surface glare, streaked with salt and soot and something worse than either.

"You're *alive*," she said, and it wasn't joy and it wasn't relief, it was fury wearing joy's clothes, and she seized his collar with both hands and hauled as though she meant to pull him up by sheer indignation. "You absolute—you *rail-rat*—you said minutes, you said—"

"Forty seconds short," Sable rasped, breaking the surface, coughing half the Salt Flats out of his lungs. "I counted."

Above them, through the companionway, the sky was the color of a bruise and getting darker—the storm wall's edge, rolling in off the deep water with the sixth tide behind it like a debt collector. And beyond the wreck site, closing fast, the black silhouette of the *Ledger*, Ilsa Vane's flagship come down from her anchorage at last, boats dropping from her davits like ripe fruit.

Wren followed his gaze and went rigid. "The crew's still out there. The longboats—"

"I know."

"And the *Ledger*—"

"I know." Sable got a hand on the ladder, tested his weight, found his legs would answer—shakily, but they'd answer. Thirty years of running had kept them lean if nothing else. He looked up at her, water streaming off his jaw, and felt the last piece of the old life fall away behind him like a coupler releasing.

"They'll take the longboats. They'll take the crew. They'll take everything we built unless somebody makes them pay for every foot of it." He started climbing. "So we're going to give them an engineering problem, Wren. The biggest one of their lives."

Below them, deep in the hull, the *Meridian Star* groaned—a long, settling sigh, a ship easing into her grave. But not yet. Not quite yet.

The sixth tide had come, and Sable Oyelaran was still breathing, and the fight, at last, was here.
