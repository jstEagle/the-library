## 1. The Debt Collector's Compass

The creditors came at dawn, which was traditional, and they came in numbers, which was not.

Rosalind Yarrow watched them from the flat roof of the wayhouse as three wagons rolled into Cinder Wells through the eastern gate, kicking up dust the color of old bone. He counted eight men and a woman with a ledger chained to her wrist. He knew what they were before anyone told him. He'd spent eleven years making sure no one could find him, and here they were anyway, finding him, because debt was the one bloodhound that never lost the scent.

He went down to meet them. No point in running. Running cost water, and water in Cinder Wells cost money he didn't have.

"You're Yarrow," said the woman with the ledger. Not a question. She had a face like a closed door and a voice that had ended better mornings than this one. "Rosamund Vetch. Acting for the Consolidated Creditors' Compact of the Border Provinces. You owe four hundred and sixty marks, plus penalties accrued over nine years of—"

"I know what I owe."

"Then you'll know we're entitled to seize against it." She snapped her fingers and her men fanned out through the wayhouse yard like it was already theirs, which legally, in a sense, it was.

They took his bedroll first. Then the spare boots. The tin of tea leaves, unopened, saved for an occasion that had never come. His good coat, the one with the oilskin lining. They worked down through his life item by item, and Rosalind stood in the yard with his hands loose at his sides and let them, because there was nothing else to do, and because he had learned long ago that a man who owned nothing could not be owned.

Except they kept coming across things.

A ranger's kit is a small religion: the long knife, the water discipline satchel, the sun hat with the brim scorched soft from ten thousand miles of horizon. When the youngest creditor reached for the kit itself—a canvas roll containing instruments Rosalind had bought used and maintained like relics—the woman Vetch raised her hand.

"Not that. A working ranger's certification is collateral of last resort. The Compact's charter forbids it." Her eyes flicked up to his face, reading him the way he read ground. "You're licensed?"

"Class two," Rosalind said. "Desert and salt pan."

"Hm." She made a note. It was the sound of a door closing on a possibility, or opening one; with ledger-keepers you never could tell. "You understand this doesn't end here. Four hundred and sixty marks doesn't evaporate. We'll be back for the rest of your earnings until it's paid."

"There won't be any earnings."

"Everyone says that." She turned to go, then paused, and something almost human crossed her face, quickly, like a lizard under a stone. "For what it's worth, Yarrow—you kept very little. Most men in your position have furniture. Photographs. Keepsakes. You have a knife and a hat. Either you're the poorest man I've ever audited, or the most careful."

"The second one."

She nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd written in her ledger years ago about men named Yarrow. Then the wagons rolled out the way they'd come, and Cinder Wells exhaled, and Rosalind stood in the empty yard among the dust their wheels had raised, owning a knife, a hat, and nothing else in the world.

It should have felt like freedom. It felt like being erased twice.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he always did when the world got too loud: he walked out onto the flats until the town was a smudge behind him and there was nothing left but salt and sky. The pan stretched white to every edge of the earth, cracked into hexagons by the dry, so that walking it felt like crossing the back of some enormous sleeping animal. Out here a man's problems shrank to their true size, which was very small, and his thirst grew to its true size, which was not.

His father had loved this country. That was the one thing Rosalind would grant him. Old Silas Yarrow had been a surveyor, a mapmaker, a man who could stand on a featureless plain and see roads where none existed yet. Rosalind had hated him for other reasons—reasons packed away so deep and so long ago that opening the box now only gave off a smell of rust and old arguments—but he had never hated the maps. Some nights, as a boy, he'd watched his father work by lamplight, drawing coastlines of country that hadn't flooded in a century, and even hating him, Rosalind had understood that the man was looking at something true.

Then Silas died, nine years back, in a salvage camp near the drowned city, and Rosalind had not gone to the funeral, and had not read the will, and had not answered the letters that came afterward, fewer each year, until they stopped.

That was the arrangement. The dead stay dead. The son stays invisible.

He was on his way back to town, thinking about which well he could beg work at, when he saw the rider coming fast along the Cinder Wells road, and knew before the rider arrived that the day wasn't finished ruining him.

* * *

The rider was Tam Osk, who ran messages between the wells and owed Rosalind a favor from the year a sandstorm ate half his herd. Tam swung down off his mule with the particular urgency of a man carrying news he'd rehearsed the whole way.

"They hit the probate office," he said. "Night before last. Broke in clean, no noise, took one thing."

Rosalind waited.

"A compass." Tam wiped his mouth. "Brass case, worn smooth, maker's mark of a meridian line. There was a description posted. It's yours, Roz. Left to you in your father's will. It sat in the probate strongbox nine years because nobody could find you to sign for it—and two nights ago somebody found it before you did."

The flats were very quiet. Somewhere far off, heat shimmered above the salt like water that wasn't there.

"That's not possible," Rosalind said. "I don't own a compass."

"You do now. Or you did." Tam dug in his shirt and produced a folded notice, cheap paper, ink still sharp. "The probate clerk's offering a tracking contract. Recovery fee of forty marks against the estate. Nobody in town will take it—they say the thief went east onto the pan, and east onto the pan is a graveyard with better manners than most."

Rosalind took the notice and read it without seeing it. *Item: brass surveyor's compass, property of the estate of S. Yarrow, deceased. Stolen from probate custody. Last seen in the possession of a buyer known as Salt.*

Salt. Everyone on the border knew the name the way sailors know a storm's name—not a person so much as a weather pattern. A broker of stolen things, a ghost with warehouses nobody had ever found, who bought rare objects from thieves all along the frontier and sold them to collectors whose names never appeared on any list either. If Salt had the compass, it was already halfway to being unfindable.

But it was his father's.

That thought arrived without permission and settled in his chest like a coal. He tried to take it back out. He reminded himself of everything: the cold house, the cold hands, the letters unanswered, the funeral unfelt. Nine years of careful erasure, and the universe had waited for exactly this moment to hand him a reason to exist again, tied with a bow of brass.

"It's just an instrument," he heard himself say.

Tam looked at him the way old friends look at liars. "Sure it is. Forty marks, though. Forty marks is most of what they took from you this morning."

It was less than a tenth of the debt. It wouldn't buy back the coat or the tea or the bedroll. But it was a number, and numbers were things a ranger could walk toward.

And underneath the number, in the place where he kept everything he didn't say out loud, another thought was forming, unwelcome and warm: *someone stole my father's compass, and I am the only person alive who can follow them.* He hated how much that sentence sounded like purpose. Purpose was a thing he'd spent a decade burying, because purpose left tracks, and tracks were how the world found you.

"Give me the contract paper," he said.

* * *

The probate clerk was a narrow man named Fettering who occupied an office that smelled of dust and sealing wax, and who looked genuinely startled when Rosalind laid the forty-mark contract on his desk and asked for the counter-signature.

"You're taking it yourself?" Fettering said. "You're the beneficiary. That's—" He consulted something. "Irregular."

"So is having your inheritance stolen out of a strongbox. Irregular seems to be the theme."

Fettering signed. His pen scratched like a beetle in a wall. While he wrote, Rosalind asked the questions that mattered, in the flat tone he used for terrain reports.

"When did the theft happen exactly?"

"Two nights past. Between the third bell and dawn."

"How did they get in? The strongbox room has a barred window and one door."

"Through the window. The bars were cut and replaced—cut so cleanly we didn't see it until daylight. Whoever it was knew locks, knew glass, knew our watch rotation." Fettering's pride was visibly wounded. "Nothing else disturbed. Just the compass. Which means they knew what they wanted before they came, which means someone told them it existed."

Someone told them. Rosalind turned that over. The will had been sealed nine years. The probate inventory was public record, technically, but nobody reads probate inventories for fun. Somebody had gone looking through a dead surveyor's estate and found one item worth stealing, in a town full of items worth more.

"What did the thief leave? Tracks? Sign?"

"Nothing on the flagstones. Outside—" Fettering shrugged helplessly. "Wind took whatever there was. Though the night watchman swears he saw someone on the roof ridge around midnight. Small build. Quick. He assumed it was a cat until it stood up."

Small build. Quick. Comfortable on rooftops. Rosalind filed it without letting his face show anything, which was its own kind of habit, older than the erasure.

"One more thing," Fettering said as Rosalind turned to go. He hesitated, then pulled open a drawer and brought out a single sheet, yellowed, folded twice. "Since you're finally here. We're required to offer the beneficiary his effects before disposal. This is yours too. It's been waiting nine years."

It was the will. Silas Yarrow's will, in his father's tight surveyor's hand, the letters ruled straight as transect lines.

Rosalind stood holding it for a long moment. Nine years of refusing this piece of paper, and now it weighed more than the seized bedroll, the coat, the tea, all of it together.

He unfolded it there in the dusty office, because if he carried it out onto the flats he might burn it, and burning it would feel good for an hour and hollow for the rest of his life.

The legal matter was short. The estate—such as it was, debts and all—passed wholly to his son Rosalind Yarrow, absent whom the assets reverted to the salvage consortium that had employed Silas at the end. One line of that made Rosalind's jaw tighten: *the Consortium of Aldous Crane holds lien against outstanding obligations.* Crane. He didn't know the name. He filed it.

Then the personal matter, appended below the seal, in handwriting that had gone slightly unsteady, as if written by a man who was tired or ill or afraid:

*Rosalind—if you are reading this, then you came after all, and I was wrong about you, and I am glad of it. The compass is not a keepsake. It is a key. What it opens, I could not finish. Do not sell it. Do not trust the fleet. And if a girl ever comes asking after me—dark hair, answers to Maren—she is your sister, whether either of us planned it or not. Forgive me the order I put these words in. Surveyors always save the hardest coordinate for last.*

Rosalind read it three times.

The office sounds drained away—the beetle-scratch of Fettering's pen, the wind worrying the shutters, the town going about its small business outside. None of it reached him. He was standing in a lamplit room twenty years ago watching his father draw a coastline, and he was standing in a salvage camp nine years ago deciding not to attend a funeral, and he was standing here, now, learning in the space of one page that his father had left him a riddle, a warning, and a sister.

*Maren.* The name meant nothing. A girl with dark hair. Whether either of us planned it or not—which meant his father had known about her and never told him, or had told him and he'd been too young, or—

He folded the will along its old creases and put it inside his shirt, against his skin, where papers stayed safe from wind.

"The compass," he said. His voice came out level, which cost him something. "Describe it again. Exactly."

Fettering described it. Brass case, worn smooth, hinged lid, a needle that didn't point north—or so the inventory noted, with the mild irritation clerks reserve for instruments that misbehave. The maker's mark was a meridian line. On the inner lid, an engraving: coordinates, six figures, in the old survey notation nobody used anymore.

Coordinates. In a compass. A key, his father had called it.

Rosalind walked out of the probate office into the white glare of late morning, and the town of Cinder Wells looked different to him—not smaller, but closer, as if he'd been viewing it through the wrong end of a glass for eleven years and someone had just turned it around. Somewhere east, across a hundred miles of salt that could kill a careless man by noon, a thief was carrying his father's compass toward a buyer named Salt, and behind the thief lay cut bars and picked locks and a night watchman's glimpse of someone small and quick on a rooftop, and behind all of it lay a dead man's steady hand writing *do not trust the fleet* in a will his son had refused to read.

Forty marks. That was the contract. That was all this had to be: a job, a recovery, a fee, and then back to the business of being no one.

He went to the well, filled every skin he had, bought hard bread and salt meat with the last coins in his pocket, and checked the long knife out of habit rather than need. At the eastern gate he paused once and looked back at Cinder Wells—the town that had stripped him to the knife and the hat—and felt the humiliation settle somewhere low and hot and useful, the way embers settle in a firepit overnight, waiting to become flame.

Then he stepped onto the pan, onto the hexagons of salt, onto the back of the sleeping animal, and began to read the ground.

The wind had been blowing from the west for two days. Anything heading east had walked against it, which meant scuffed salt healed slow, which meant there would be sign, if the thief was good, and less sign if the thief was very good.

Twenty miles out, in the long gold light of evening, he found the first print: small, quick-striding, placed with a water-discipline so precise it looked less like walking than like arithmetic.

Rosalind crouched over it for a long time without touching it.

"Well," he said, to the thief, to the compass, to his father, to no one. "Let's see who you are."

He rose, set his bearing east, and walked into the darkening salt.
