## 2. Tracks on Nothing

The salt pan began a mile east of Cinder Wells and did not end so much as surrender to the horizon. Rosalind Yarrow stood at its edge at first light with the tracking contract folded in his breast pocket and nothing else to his name but what he carried.

He had signed it himself. That was the part that still sat wrong in his chest, like a stone swallowed sideways. He had walked into the Cinder Wells assay office, put his thumbprint on the line where a hired tracker's name belonged, and taken the contract to recover property that was legally his own. The clerk—a soft man named Pell who owed him three years of small kindnesses—had looked up from the ledger with something close to pity.

"You could just file a claim," Pell had said. "It's your inheritance. The will names you."

"The will is paper," Rosalind said. "The compass is brass. Paper can wait."

What he didn't say: paper was how they found you. Every claim filed, every signature inked, every debt recorded—it all went into ledgers, and ledgers were chains with better handwriting. He had spent eleven years making himself into a man the ledgers couldn't hold. No fixed address. No credit. No debts except the ones he'd inherited against his will, which clung like burrs no matter how far he walked. And now he'd put his thumbprint in a government-adjacent document because a dead man's compass mattered more than his own erasure.

His father would have laughed at that. Or worse—his father wouldn't have reacted at all.

Rosalind shouldered his kit and walked out onto the white.

* * *

The salt pan taught you humility in the first hundred yards. There was nothing on it. No stones, no scrub, no shade, no landmark your eye could anchor to. Just crust—white, blistered, faintly pink in the hollows where brine pooled—and a sky so wide it pressed down on you like a hand. Rangers called it the Table. Things crossed it because they had to, not because they wanted to, and everything that crossed it left a mark.

This thief had left marks like a confession written in a language only three people alive could read fluently, and Rosalind was one of them.

He crouched at the pan's edge where the crust firmed up and found the first print. A boot heel, narrow, pressed deep at the ball of the foot. Long stride. The gait of someone tall, or someone in a hurry, or both.

He followed.

By midmorning he'd built the picture. The stride lengthened as the pan opened up—confident, economical, no wasted motion. The prints came down heel-first on the firm crust but rolled onto the toes where the salt turned to slush beneath, the way an experienced walker distributed weight on ground that could swallow a leg to the knee. This was someone who knew the Table. Not book-knew it. Knew it in the ankles.

At a place where the crust had collapsed into a brine hole the size of a wagon bed, the tracks detoured wide—an arc of maybe forty feet rather than testing the edge. Cautious. But the arc was smooth, made without breaking stride, which meant the walker had seen the hazard from a distance and adjusted without thinking. That wasn't caution. That was instinct.

Rosalind stood over the arc for a long moment, feeling something he didn't want to name.

He knew exactly one other person who read ground that way.

* * *

His father had taught him to track when he was seven, standing behind him with a hand on his shoulder like a yoke.

"Read it backward," Edric Yarrow said. His voice was the voice he used for instruction—flat, precise, stripped of anything warm enough to be mistaken for love. "Everyone reads forward, toward the quarry. Amateurs. You read from the sign back to the man who made it. The track tells you what he is. Then you decide if he's worth finding."

They'd been following a strayed mule across the flats outside their claim shack, forty miles north of Cinder Wells, in the dry season before the monsoons came. Rosalind remembered the heat coming off the salt in visible waves, and his father's shadow falling over him, and the crushing awareness that he was being measured and would be found insufficient.

"It's tired," young Rosalind said, studying the prints. "Left hind is dragging."

"Why?"

"It's—it stepped wrong somewhere back there. There's a hitch in the rhythm."

"And?"

"And we'll find blood ahead." He'd seen the faint rust tint in one print, half-crusted. "It cut itself."

Edric Yarrow had looked at him for three full seconds—the longest attention he ever paid anyone—and said, "Adequate," and walked on, and seven-year-old Rosalind had understood that *adequate* was the ceiling of his father's approval and that he would spend his life trying to break through it.

He never broke through. Nobody did. His mother hadn't; she'd died when Rosalind was nine, quietly, of the fever, while his father was away surveying for pay. Rosalind remembered the funeral: six people under a tarpaulin awning, wind snapping the canvas, and his father standing at the grave with his hands clasped behind him like a man waiting for a delayed coach. He didn't cry. Rosalind had spent years telling himself that grief looks different on different men, that some men mourn inward. It was a generous theory. It didn't survive contact with the rest of Edric Yarrow's life.

Because there was more than coldness. There was the ledger habit. His father wrote everything down—incomes, debts, favors owed, favors granted, each entry dated and initialed. When Rosalind turned sixteen and asked for his share of the season's wages, his father opened the ledger and showed him, line by line, how every coin Rosalind had earned had already been consumed by the cost of feeding and housing him since birth. "You are currently indebted to this household by four crowns, eleven shillings," his father said, without malice, which somehow made it worse. Malice would have meant feeling. This was arithmetic.

That was the year Rosalind started planning his disappearance. It took him two more years to execute. He left in the night with his ranger kit, took work under a name that wasn't his, let that name lapse, took another. He learned that a man with no records was a man no one could collect from, no one could conscript, no one could find unless he chose to be found. He became excellent at vanishing. It was the one inheritance he'd accepted willingly.

And now here he was, walking his father's trade across his father's kind of ground, chasing his father's last possession, having signed his name to do it.

The salt crunched under his boots. Somewhere ahead, a woman who moved like a Yarrow was carrying the past toward the horizon.

* * *

He caught her water discipline at noon.

There was a seep—a crack in the pan's skin where brackish water sweated up through the salt, bitter and barely drinkable, the kind of source rangers used only in desperation. She'd stopped there. He could see where she'd knelt: two knee prints, shallow, careful, placed on solid crust. Beside them, a small depression where something round-bottomed had rested—a waterskin or flask set down while she drank.

She'd drunk sitting back on her heels, weight off her knees, and she'd been there less than five minutes. No fire ring. No refuse. Nothing left behind, nothing disturbed beyond necessity.

But here was the detail that stopped him: she hadn't filled her flask.

The seep ran slow—a liter an hour, maybe less. Filling a flask meant kneeling longer, means soaking a cloth to filter the silt, means time. She'd drunk just enough to keep moving and given up the refill. Trading comfort now for speed.

Rosalind straightened and looked out across the white, at the smudge of her trail running dead straight toward the northeast and the distant gray line of the Vess escarpment.

Nobody went northeast. Northeast was the drowned country—the old shipping roads swallowed when the sea shifted two centuries ago, the sunken city of Vess that surfaced for a few tides each monsoon season and then went under again. Salvage fleets worked it in good years. Everyone else gave it a wide berth, because the flooded ground between here and there could open under a man like a mouth.

She was heading for Vess. With a stolen compass. At speed, rationing her water, alone.

He thought about turning back. He thought about it honestly, the way he tried to think about most things: the contract paid on recovery, and recovery meant catching her, and catching her meant either violence or conversation, and he was out of practice at both. He could report her heading to the assay office, let someone else run her down. Let the ledgers handle it. That was the smart play. That was the invisible man's play.

Instead he checked his own flask—half full, he'd have to use the seep too, damn her for reminding him—and lengthened his stride.

* * *

The second day, the trail told him things he didn't want to know.

She'd camped once, briefly, in the lee of a salt ridge—a rare formation where wind had sculpted the crust into a low frozen wave. Her fire had been tiny: a fist-sized ring of dried saltbush stems, burned down to ash and then scattered. Scattered, deliberately, after. She'd even swept her sleeping hollow with a frond, though there was no one out here to sweep it for.

You didn't do that for practical reasons. Out here, tidiness bought you nothing; the wind erased everything within a day regardless. You scattered your ashes and swept your hollow because some older voice in your head told you to. Because someone had trained you that a camp was a confession and a clean camp was silence.

Rosalind had been trained that way. By his father. Which raised a question he'd been circling since the first footprint:

Who trained her?

He sat on the salt ridge eating hardtack and dried meat, watching the light die over the Table, and let himself think about it properly for the first time.

His father had been gone four years. Heart failure, according to the coroner's note that came with the will notification Rosalind had never opened—he'd had it forwarded unopened twice before letting it sit in a Cinder Wells dead-letter drawer until Pell, damn him, finally read the gist aloud: deceased, estate minimal, one item of note, come sign or forfeit. Rosalind hadn't gone. Hadn't signed. Hadn't wanted whatever a man like Edric Yarrow would leave behind, which was surely a ledger of debts with Rosalind's name at the bottom.

Then, ten days ago, the compass vanished from the assay office strongroom where the unclaimed estates were kept, along with a handful of other items, and the theft report crossed Rosalind's desk because the assay office flagged any theft involving registered instruments and Rosalind was the nearest licensed tracker. He'd read the description—brass surveyor's compass, maker's mark E.Y., hinged lid, needle housing cracked at the north seam—and felt something turn over in his chest that he refused to call grief.

He knew that crack. He'd caused it, age twelve, dropping the instrument while cleaning it, and his father had repaired it in silence over two evenings and never mentioned it again, which was somehow heavier than any punishment. The compass had been the one object in the Yarrow household that his father touched gently.

And now a stranger with a ranger's stride and a ranger's discipline had taken it northeast toward Vess.

Unless she wasn't a stranger.

The thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do. Rosalind made himself look at it.

His father had been away a lot. Surveying contracts, salvage consultations, seasons gone. Rosalind had assumed work. Men like Edric Yarrow were always working. But there had been a year—one year, Rosalind was fourteen—when his father came back from a contract near the coast with salt-water corrosion on his boots and a new way of carrying himself, lighter, almost careless, and had stood in the doorway of the shack looking at Rosalind with an expression he couldn't parse until much later, when he'd had words for it: guilt. Or recognition. As if seeing his son reminded him of someone else.

Rosalind had filed it away and never opened it, the way he'd never opened the will.

Now he sat on a salt ridge in the middle of nowhere, looking at a stranger's tidy campsite, and the file tore itself open.

If his father had another child—if there was a daughter, raised elsewhere, trained in the same craft by the same cold hand during those long absences—then the thief wasn't stealing the compass. She was collecting it. Taking the one thing that mattered out of a strongroom full of worthless estate dross, and taking it toward Vess, where his father had clearly spent time, where something had changed him.

And she'd known the theft would bring a tracker. Any competent thief avoids the licensed man; she'd have known the assay office would flag it. Unless she wanted the flag. Unless she wanted the nearest licensed tracker—him—to come looking.

"That's paranoid," Rosalind said aloud, to the empty pan. The pan said nothing back, which he chose to take as agreement.

He banked the thought but didn't bury it. He'd buried too many thoughts in his life; they had a way of mining their way back up.

* * *

On the third morning, the Table ended.

Not gradually—the way it always did, the crust thinning over a final mile until it rang hollow underfoot and gave way to mudflats, then to channels, then to the gray-green shimmer of the flooded country. The air changed too: wetter, tasting of rot and iodine, carrying the faint mineral smell of drowned stone. Far off, the Vess escarpment stood up out of the haze like the spine of something sleeping.

Her trail ran straight into it.

Rosalind paused at the last firm crust and did the arithmetic. Two and a half days behind her, gaining slightly—she'd slowed near the flooded margins, picking her route with care, and he moved faster over broken ground than she did, or was willing to risk more. Half a day, maybe less, if she kept this pace. His water would last to the escarpment if he rationed. Past that, the drowned country had springs, foul ones, but springs.

He also noticed, with a tracker's involuntary precision, that her recent prints had changed. Deeper. Slower cadence. And beside the right boot prints, at intervals, a small round puncture mark—a walking staff, or a probe. She was testing the ground ahead of every third step now.

Careful steps. Water discipline. A woman's stride. And now caution layered over speed, which meant she knew something about this country that made even her nervous.

Rosalind worked his way along the margin, reading the flooded ground the old way—reed color, bird traffic, the smell of a channel—and found the causeway she'd taken: a submerged roadbed, ancient, crowned just below the surface, running northeast toward the escarpment. Someone local had shown her that, or she'd found an old chart. Either way, she was better informed about Vess than a random thief had any right to be.

Halfway along the causeway's visible stretch, he stopped.

There was a waypoint marker on the roadbed—a bronze survey pin, green with age, driven into the old stone. Government pattern. He'd seen thousands of them. But this one had been recently disturbed: the silt around its base churned, and scratched into the salt crust beside it, small and neat, was a symbol he recognized from his father's field notes—the Yarrow survey mark, a circle quartered by a cross, the private stamp Edric Yarrow used on his own charts.

Fresh. Days old at most.

She'd stopped here. She'd known this pin. She'd marked it—or acknowledged it—the way family marks a grave.

Rosalind stood in the ankle-deep flood with the drowned country opening around him and felt the reluctant respect curdle into something sharper. She wasn't just moving like family. She was following his father's road, marker by marker, toward Vess, and she'd stolen the compass to do it, and she might have stolen it to be followed.

Ahead, the escarpment's towers showed through the haze for the first time—gray fingers rising from the shallows, impossibly tall, impossibly far. Between him and them, the water deepened by degrees he couldn't see.

And there, on the horizon to the south, a new detail: a smudge of smoke. Not one fire. Many fires, in a line, moving.

He counted the interval between smoke columns and did the arithmetic a second time, and didn't like the answer. Something else was crossing the flooded country toward Vess. Something organized, provisioned, and large.

Rosalind pulled his kit tight and waded onto the causeway, following a dead man's markers and a living woman's prints, toward whatever his father had hidden at the bottom of the sea.
