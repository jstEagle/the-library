## 3. The Survey That Wasn't

The waystation at Kiln's Rest was the kind of place that existed only because the mapmakers had needed something to put between two emptinesses. A stone cistern, a wind-bent signal mast, a low building with a roof of salt-cured hides that had gone the color of old teeth. Rosalind had slept in a dozen like it over his years on the flats, and he knew their grammar by heart: which boards creaked under weight, where the rats ran, how long the water in the cistern would last if you rationed it like a man who expected to die thirsty.

He was half a mile out when he knew something was wrong.

It wasn't anything dramatic. It was the smoke—or rather, the absence of it. Waystations this far north kept a cookfire going through the monsoon season because wet wood was all there was and wet wood smoked, and a column of that gray smoke was as reliable as a lighthouse to anyone reading the flats. Kiln's Rest should have been smoking. It wasn't.

Rosalind stopped and let the wind move around him. He'd learned patience from the pan itself; the salt taught you that rushing got men killed. He counted the windows of the waystation, the angle of the door, the way the hide roof sagged. Nothing moved. No birds picked at the midden heap. No laundry on the line, though there were pegs for it.

The thief's trail—the woman's trail—ran straight toward the station and did not come out the other side.

He considered that for a long moment. She'd been three days ahead of him when he'd picked her spoor up off the hardpan outside Cinder Wells, and she'd lost maybe half a day since, doubling back once through a dry wash to break her line. Careful. Disciplined. She carried water like someone who'd done hard miles in the deep flats, and she walked with a stride that was familiar in a way he refused to examine too closely, because every time he examined it, it looked like his own.

Now her trail ended here, at a dead station with no smoke.

Rosalind unslung his rifle and checked it out of habit more than fear. Then he did what the pan had taught him: he went wide, circling the station in a slow arc, reading the ground.

There were tracks, all right. More than he'd expected. The flat apron of packed salt and clay in front of the station was churned with boot prints—at least six sets, maybe eight, government-issue soles with the broad heel stamp he recognized from rangers who'd mustered out into survey work. They came in from the south in a loose file and they went nowhere. That was the thing that put a cold finger against the back of his neck. Eight men could walk in anywhere. Eight men could not walk out without leaving sign, not on ground this soft after the first rains. There was no outgoing trail. Not east, not west, not back the way they'd come.

Just in. And then nothing.

He waited another ten minutes, watching the station breathe—or fail to breathe—and finally crossed the open ground fast and low, took the door with his shoulder, and went inside with the rifle up.

Nobody shot him. Nobody was there to shoot him.

* * *

The interior told its story in the quietest possible voice, and Rosalind stood in the middle of it and listened.

A survey team lived loud, even when it tried not to. He'd shared camps with them often enough to know: instruments unpacked and repacked, brass fittings clinking, the constant small argument of men deciding what to eat first. This camp hadn't even been struck. It had been left.

Four bedrolls lay along the far wall, rolled but not tied, the way a man leaves his bed when he means to come back before dark. A card game sat abandoned on an upturned crate—hands still dealt, a chit stack in the middle, one chair pushed back at the angle of a man standing up quickly. On the table beside it, a plate held half a flatbread gone green with mold. Green mold meant four days, maybe five, in this humidity. Four or five days ago, men had been eating here, and then they had stopped being here, and nobody had finished the bread.

The instruments were worse. In the corner, a theodolite sat on its tripod, still leveled, still aimed out the window at a fixed bearing. Beside it, a chronometer ticked. Rosalind leaned close and listened to it—a good marine chronometer, wound tight, keeping perfect time in an empty room. Somebody had wound it within the last day. Somebody had been here, recently, winding a clock in a station full of ghosts, and then left without disturbing the dust.

Or the dust had been disturbed. Rosalind crouched and looked at the floor properly, the way his father had taught him before he'd taught him anything else about being a disappointment. Sweep marks. Faint ones, made with a bundle of brush, erasing tracks—but only near the door. Only along the paths a stranger's eye would follow. Whoever had cleaned up had been thorough in the places that mattered and careless in the corner behind the cistern, where a single boot print pointed toward the back wall and stopped.

Not erased. Interrupted.

Rosalind looked at that print for a long time. Size nine. Government sole. And pressed into the clay at its edge, half-filled with seep water, a smear of something dark that might have been mud and might have been blood, diluted past telling.

He checked the food stores because hunger is honest and his had been talking to him since dawn. The cistern room held crates of hardtack, tins of oil-packed fish, a sack of rice—all of it untouched, seals intact, stacked with the neatness of stores that had been inventoried yesterday. Enough food for eight men for a month. Nobody had taken so much as a tin.

Eight men had walked into Kiln's Rest carrying full rations, and they had left without their food, without their beds, without their instruments, without finishing a card game or a piece of bread.

Rosalind had spent eleven years learning to read the desert's silences. Most silences meant nothing: a burrow abandoned, a well gone brackish, a caravan rerouted around weather. But some silences had weight, and this one had the specific gravity of a grave. His curiosity, which had been running hot since Cinder Wells, cooled all at once into something slower and darker. Curiosity asked *what happened here*. What was settling into his chest now didn't ask anything. It just said: *leave*.

He almost did. That was the truth of it, and he never lied to himself about it later—he stood in that doorway with the wind coming through and every instinct he owned telling him to walk away, find the woman, take the compass, collect his fee, and vanish back into the blank spaces where no records kept his name. The compass was a thief's errand. This was something else. This was the kind of silence that swallowed rangers whole and never entered it into any ledger.

Then he saw the ledger itself.

It sat on the survey table under a paperweight—a field journal, government issue, the cover stamped with the seal of the Territorial Survey Office. Open to the last written page. Rosalind told himself he was only going to check the date, confirm the timeline, and then he was reading it, because the handwriting had deteriorated down the page from a surveyor's neat hand into something ragged, and the last entry wasn't a survey entry at all.

*Day 41. Crane's tender came again. Third visit. They want our fix on the Meridian and they want it certified under fleet authority, not Survey. Voss says we certify nothing until the Commissioner countersigns. Crane's man smiled at that. I don't like how he smiled.*

*Day 42. Voss sent the pigeon. No answer yet.*

*Day 43. Tender returned at dusk. Men got off. Not the same men. Voss told everyone to stay inside. He has the pistol out. First time in nine years I've seen him—*

The entry stopped mid-word. Below it, in different ink, smaller, hurried, slanting downhill like a man writing while walking:

*They came in the night. Took Voss and the others toward the tower road. I ran. I don't know why they left me. I'm hurt. If anyone reads this, the sixth tide is real. Father Anselm's chart was right. The wreck shows on the sixth tide and Crane knows it and he will kill every man who can prove the coordinates. Do not give your name to anyone from the fleet. Do not—*

That entry also stopped. After it, the rest of the journal's pages had been torn out. Neatly. All the way to the cover.

Rosalind stood holding the journal and felt the flats tilt under him, the way they sometimes did when you'd been walking too long on glare. *Crane.* He knew the name the way everyone on the border knew it—Aldous Crane, the salvage magnate, the man whose dredge fleet worked the flooded pans each monsoon and whose company scrip was worth more than territorial currency in three towns. A name from posters and freight manifests. A name that had never had anything to do with Rosalind Yarrow, who was nobody, who had made himself nobody on purpose.

And yet here the name was, in a dying man's hand, in a station full of vanished men, sitting square across the trail of the thief who had stolen his father's compass.

His father. The thought arrived sideways, unwelcome. Edmun Yarrow had been a surveyor—had mapped half these flats for the Crown before the debts ate him, before he died broke and bitter in a Cinder Wells boarding house while his son watched from the doorway and chose, deliberately, to remember him as a cold man who loved his instruments more than his children. Rosalind had built his whole invisible life on the foundation of that memory. And now a murdered survey team's journal was whispering the word *Crane*, and somewhere in his chest an old, hated instinct stirred—the cartographer's son recognizing the shape of a map being redrawn by force.

He made himself keep moving. Grief and dread were luxuries; the light was failing.

* * *

He found the junior surveyor at dusk, in the cistern room, behind the false panel.

Rosalind hadn't been looking for hiding places, exactly. But the interrupted boot print behind the cistern had pointed at the back wall, and the back wall had a seam in the stonework that didn't match the rest, and men who build waystations build them with bolt-holes because the flats have always had bad years. The panel swung on leather hinges, silent, and beyond it was a crawlspace two feet high that smelled of dust and old rainwater and, underneath that, of blood.

The man in it was maybe twenty-five. He'd been shot through the belly—Rosalind could see the wound had been bound, badly, with strips of shirt, soaked through and re-soaked. He'd crawled in here four days ago and he'd survived four days on nothing, which meant he was either very tough or very unwilling to die, and probably both. His lips were cracked white. His eyes found Rosalind's rifle first, then his face, and something in them flickered—not hope exactly, but the animal response to any human shape after four days alone with death.

"Water," Rosalind said, and gave it to him before asking anything else. The man drank in small controlled sips, the discipline of someone trained, and Rosalind noted the uniform under the ruined shirt: Territorial Survey, junior grade, the brass buttons tarnished with salt.

"Name," Rosalind said quietly.

"Hale." A breath. "Junior surveyor Hale. Are you—fleet?"

"I'm nobody."

Something in Hale's face loosened at that. "Good," he whispered. "Be nobody. Stay nobody." He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough that bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his chin. "You read the book? The journal?"

"I read it."

"Then you know." Hale's eyes had gone glassy, fever-bright, tracking something past Rosalind's shoulder. "Voss wouldn't certify. Wouldn't sign the fix over to fleet authority without the Commissioner. Crane's men came back for it. Night of the forty-third." His hand crept along the floor, fumbling, and closed on Rosalind's sleeve with surprising strength. "They took the others down the tower road. Toward Vess. I heard—I heard the boats. Boats, in the flats, do you understand? They had boats ready. This was planned. Planned before the season opened."

"The sixth tide," Rosalind said. "What is it?"

Hale's mouth worked. For a moment Rosalind thought he'd drifted, and then the young man focused on him with terrible effort, pulling himself back from wherever the fever was taking him, because there was something he needed said and he knew he was running out of hours to say it.

"The Meridian." Each word cost him visibly. "Wrecked off Vess, eighty years back. Shows on the lowest tide of the monsoon—the sixth. Six tides a season, that's all, then she's sealed under ten feet of water till next year. Old Father Anselm charted her. Everyone thinks it's a story." A shudder went through him. "It isn't. Voss confirmed the fix. Confirmed what she carries. That's why—that's why Crane can't leave witnesses. Anyone who holds the coordinates holds—" He coughed again, longer this time, and Rosalind held the canteen to his lips and watched the water come back up pink.

"Hold on," Rosalind said. "I can get you to Cinder Wells. Two days' run. There's a medic—"

"No." Hale said it flatly, without drama, the way men say things they've already decided. "Listen. Listen to me instead." His grip tightened on Rosalind's sleeve. "The others are alive. I think they're alive. Crane doesn't kill what he can use—they'll be made to work the salvage, or made to sign papers they shouldn't sign. You have to tell the Commissioner. Tell anyone with rank. Tell them Aldous Crane silenced a Crown survey team in the field, and tell them the date, and tell them—" His voice frayed. He pulled Rosalind closer, close enough that Rosalind could smell the blood and the fever on him, and the last words came out almost gentle, almost puzzled, as if the strangeness of it had only fully landed on him now, four days late.

"He asked about names. Crane's lieutenant. Before they started shooting, he asked if any of us were Yarrow. Said there was a Yarrow on the old charts. Said if we knew a Yarrow, we should say so, and be paid for it."

The cistern room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, the wind moved across the mouth of the station and made the hide roof sigh.

"My name is Yarrow," Rosalind heard himself say.

Hale stared up at him. And then, horribly, he smiled—a cracked-lipped, feverish smile of relief, as if the answer to everything had finally arrived. "The compass man," he breathed. "He said—the compass man's son. He said the father drew the true chart, and the son would come looking, because Yarrows always come looking." His eyes were losing focus now, sliding toward whatever was waiting for him behind the ceiling of the world. "He wanted us to tell you. Wanted you drawn here. It's a trap laid with your own name, ranger. Your father's chart is worth more than the ship. Find Maren. She has the—"

The word didn't finish. His chest rose once, twice, shallow as tide pools, and then it didn't rise again, and the grip on Rosalind's sleeve went from urgent to merely heavy, and then to nothing at all.

Rosalind knelt in the dark behind the false panel for a long time, listening to the chronometer tick in the empty room, keeping perfect time for men who would never need it.

*Maren.* The name turned over in him slowly, and with it the woman's stride on the hardpan, the familiar geometry of it, the trail that had led him here—to a murdered survey, a magnate's name, and a dead boy's last instruction. She hadn't stolen the compass to sell it. She'd stolen it to bait a hook, and the hook had his family's name on it, and everything that had happened since—the trail, the station, the silence—had been arranged for him like a room prepared for a guest.

Dread had replaced curiosity somewhere in the last hour, and now dread gave way to something harder and simpler. He laid Junior Surveyor Hale out properly, closed his eyes, and covered him with his own bedroll, because the man deserved more than a crawlspace. Then Rosalind took the gutted journal, checked his water, and stepped out into the blue dark where the flats were already beginning to smell of rain.

Two days ahead of him, a woman named Maren was walking toward Vess with his father's compass.

Behind him, somewhere on the horizon, boats were already moving.

He ran.
