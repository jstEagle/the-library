## 4. Maren

He found me at the waystation, of course. I'd counted on it.

I heard him before I saw him—the particular way a ranger moves when he thinks he's being quiet, which is to say, not quiet at all if you've been raised on the same salt. A scuff of boot grit outside the shuttered window. The pause where a lesser tracker would have pushed straight through the door and mine would circle first, because our father taught us both that doors are where people die.

I sat with my back to the wall and the compass in my lap and waited for him to come through the window like a sensible paranoid man.

He did. Long legs folding over the sill, a knife already in his hand, moving low and fast into the dark room—and then stopping dead, because I'd lit the lamp an hour ago just for this moment, and there was nothing for his knife to do against a woman sitting in plain light, turning a brass compass over in her fingers like a card sharp deciding whether to fold.

"Hello, Rosalind," I said.

I want it recorded—by whom, I don't know, there's no one left to keep records of the Yarrow family except the two of us, and we are both liars—that he was better than I expected. He didn't lunge. He didn't shout. He went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and I watched three years of careful erasure fight three seconds of recognition on his face.

"You're not Salt," he said finally.

"No."

"The buyer in Cinder Wells. Gray coat, paid in script, asked after salvage routes north." He recited it flatly, the way you'd read off a ledger, but his eyes were doing something else entirely. Cataloguing me. The set of my shoulders. The shape of my jaw.

"You're staring at Father's chin," I said. "Everyone does. It's very rude of you."

* * *

Let me back up, because I know how this looks from his side—a thief, a stranger, a trap sprung shut—and Rosalind Yarrow has spent so long believing himself alone in the world that he's forgotten how families work. Families lie. Families plan. Families steal from each other and call it inheritance.

Our father had two children. He told one of them nothing, and he told the other everything, and neither arrangement was kind.

I grew up in Port Meridian—yes, named for the ship; no, that's not a coincidence either—in the surveyor's quarters above the chart house, learning to draw coastlines before I could write my own name. Father was warm there. That's the part Rosalind will never believe until I make him: he was *warm*. He carried me on his shoulders along the breakwater and taught me the names of stars by pretending they were ports—there's Vess, he'd say, pointing at Vega, drowned city, six tides a season, remember it—and he'd lift his hat to fishermen in the morning and they'd lift theirs back, because everyone loved Elias Yarrow, everyone trusted him, the government's own honest mapmaker.

And every few months he would disappear inland for weeks, and come back thinner, and drink alone with the shutters closed, and once—I was nine, hiding on the stairs—I heard him say to my mother, in a voice I'd never heard him use: *If Crane asks again, tell him I lost the pages. Tell him the sea took them.*

My mother died when I was twelve. After that it was just the two of us, and the warmth thinned out like paint stretched too far, and then the letters started coming from a border town called Cinder Wells, and Father burned them without opening them, every time, and never said why.

I opened one. Once. When he was drunk enough to sleep past dawn.

It wasn't signed. It said: *Your son has been located. The debt follows the blood. Crane's patience is not infinite.* And beneath that, an amount of money large enough that I understood, at thirteen, sitting on cold stairs with a stolen letter in my hands, that somewhere out there I had a brother, and that my father's warmth had a price tag hanging off it, and that whatever he'd done in the years before me was still walking around out there wearing his name.

So. Now you know why I stole the compass.

Not for the brass. Not for the money—it's worth maybe forty marks to a collector, less to anyone who knows its provenance is a dead surveyor nobody mourns. I stole it because it was the only object on earth that connected all four of us: Father bought it the year Rosalind was born, engraved it the year I was born, and carried it until the year he died, and when his will surfaced last month—surfaced, mind you, from a lawyer's office in Cinder Wells, addressed to a son who apparently never read it—I knew exactly one thing about Rosalind Yarrow: that he had spent years making himself unfindable, and that the only creature alive who could track a ghost was another ghost, following the scent of blood.

You don't hire a man like that. You *bait* him.

* * *

"You knew I'd come," Rosalind said. He hadn't moved from the window. The lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, and even his shadow looked like it was looking for exits. "The trail across the pan. The water discipline. You walked it like—" He stopped himself.

"Like family," I finished. "Yes. I know. I watched you find my campsite yesterday and read my fire ring, and I thought: he does it exactly the way Father taught me. Ash scattered, stones replaced, ground brushed with a bundle of sage. Where did you learn that? Because I learned it at his knee, and he never once mentioned you."

Something moved behind his face—not pain exactly. More like a door opening onto a room he'd sealed years ago and finding the furniture rearranged.

"I taught myself," he said.

"No one teaches themselves that. That's inherited. That's in the blood, like the chin." I turned the compass over. The engraving caught the lamplight: *E.Y. — R.Y. — M.Y.*, three sets of initials, three lives, one small brass disc. "Do you know what this cost him?"

"A debt," Rosalind said. His voice had gone flat and hard. "Everything he ever touched cost a debt. I grew up on the wrong side of his ledger, sister, so don't stand there polishing the brass and telling me he was some loving father who—"

"I'm not telling you he was loving." I stood up, slowly, keeping my hands visible, because I'd read enough about him in three years of digging to know that sudden movement made him jumpy and jumpy men with knives make bad decisions. "I'm telling you he was two men, and you got the cold one, and I got the warm one, and neither of us got the truth. So sit down, Rosalind, and let me show you what he actually died for. Because it wasn't debt."

That landed. I watched it land. Whatever story he'd built—deadbeat father, runaway debts, a son so ashamed he'd spent years scrubbing his own name from every register between here and the coast—that story had been load-bearing, and I'd just pulled a stone out of it.

He didn't sit. But he stopped edging toward the window.

* * *

I took the logbook page out of the oilskin packet inside my coat, where it had lived against my ribs for eleven days, and I laid it on the table between us under the lamp.

One page, torn from a government survey log, the paper stock official and watermarked. Father's handwriting—Rosalind recognized it instantly, I saw it hit him, the small involuntary flinch of a man meeting a voice he'd tried to forget. Surveyor's hand: precise, cramped, numbers marching down the margin like soldiers.

Coordinates. A set of them, triangulated three ways, cross-referenced against tide tables. And below, in the same hand, written fast, the pen pressed hard enough to tear:

*Wreck of the Meridian confirmed. Bearing 214, six fathoms at spring low. She does not carry what Crane believes. She carries the charter. Mineral rights to the entire Vess basin, crown-sealed, transferable to whoever holds the papers. If Crane gets it, he owns the flats forever—every settlement, every well, every family on the pan becomes his tenants. I have hidden the original. He knows I have it. God forgive me, I think he means to make sure I can't testify. If anyone reads this: do not sell. Do not trade. Sink it deeper than his reach. — E.Y.*

The date at the top was nine days before our father drowned.

Rosalind read it twice. I know because his lips moved the second time, barely, a habit I suspect he thinks he killed years ago. Then he looked up at me and his eyes were wet and furious at being wet, and I understood in that moment that I had miscalculated one thing about my brother: I'd planned for his anger, and his suspicion, and his pride. I had not fully planned for the fact that underneath all of it he had loved our father the whole time, hopelessly, the way you love a locked door.

"Crane," he said. "Aldous Crane. The salvage magnate."

"The salvage magnate," I agreed. "Who runs forty ships out of Port Meridian. Who funded half the government surveys on the coast, including ours, including Father's. Who came to dinner twice when I was a girl and gave me a silver hairpin and called me *little chartmaker*, and whose men dragged the harbor for Father's body for three days and then stopped asking questions very suddenly."

"And the survey team." His voice dropped. "North of here. The one that vanished. You know about that."

"I know Father's page mentions a government team resurveying the Vess basin this season. I know they stopped reporting eleven days ago. And I know that when I passed through Saltmere Junction, a Crane courier was buying up every copy of the basin charts and burning them in the yard behind the chandlery." I met his eyes. "I don't think the timing is a coincidence, Rosalind. I think Father's page is the only surviving record of what the Meridian actually carries, and I think Aldous Crane is currently erasing everything else that points at it—including, possibly, people."

Silence. Outside, the wind had changed while we talked; I felt it shift against the shutters, that wet iron smell rolling in ahead of the monsoon, months early or right on time depending on which almanac you trusted, and neither of us trusted almanacs.

"So that's your plan," Rosalind said at last. "Steal the compass. Dangle it across the salt pan like bait on a line. Wait for the ghost brother to take the hook. And then what—hand me a dead man's accusation and point me at the richest man on the coast?" He laughed, short and bitter, no humor in it anywhere. "You used me."

"Yes," I said. "I did. And I'd do it again, because you're the only person alive who moves like Father taught us, and I can't do this alone, and I have been alone with this page for eleven days and it's heavier than lead." I kept my voice level. Fury is a luxury; I'd budgeted mine for later. "Hate me for it. You should. But hate me *after* you decide what you're going to do, because the tide tables say the flats start flooding within the week, and when they do, the wreck of the Meridian opens for six tides and then seals for a year. Six tides, Rosalind. Crane's fleet will be there. The question is whether we will."

He stared at the page. At the compass in my hands. At me—at Father's chin, at Mother's mouth, at whatever else he was finding in my face that he couldn't reconcile with the story he'd told himself for twenty years.

"Why now?" he said quietly. "Why did you wait until now? Father's been dead two years."

"Because the will surfaced two months ago," I said, "and the will means someone besides us knows there's an estate to claim. And because three weeks ago, a man came to Port Meridian asking questions about Elias Yarrow's children. Both of them. By name." I let that sit. "Crane knows we exist, brother. He's known for a while. We're not hunting him anymore."

I watched him understand it—the moment the hunter feels the line go tight from the other end.

Outside, the first fat drops of the early monsoon began ticking against the shutters, soft as fingers, patient as tide.

* * *

Word count check: this scene needs to run long, and it does—but the chapter isn't done until the emotional beat lands and the hook bites. Let me give the siblings their collision properly, because fury tangled with kinship doesn't resolve in one exchange; it has to be survived.

* * *

He crossed the room in three strides and put his fist through the table.

Not at me—at the wood, beside the page, close enough that the lamp jumped and the coordinates shivered in their ink. Splinters. A long silence. His knuckles bleeding and him not caring, breathing like a man who'd been holding his breath for twenty years.

"You let me track you across open pan," he said, "for six days. Six days I ate dust reading your stride, wondering who the hell walks like that, and the whole time you were—" He shook his head. "You could have sent a letter."

"To an address?" I said. "Rosalind, you've erased yourself from every registry on the coast. I spent three years and most of my savings finding the shadow you live in. A letter would have gone in the fire with the rest of Father's mail." I stood my ground, though my heart was going like a drum. "Besides. Would you have come?"

He didn't answer. That was the answer.

"There it is," I said softly. "You wouldn't have. Not for a letter. Not for a plea. Maybe not even for Father's grave. But for a stolen compass—something taken from *you*, something you could chase—you came across sixty miles of salt in five days." I picked the compass up off the ruined table and held it out to him, flat on my palm, the way you offer your hand to a horse that wants to bite you. "So yes, brother. I manipulated you. I used the one thing you wanted as bait, because it was the only door you'd walk through. And I am not sorry, and I will not apologize, and if you want to hit me instead of the table, get in line behind everyone else Crane has ever lied to."

For a long moment he looked at my open hand and I genuinely did not know which way he'd go. This was the man, after all, who'd chosen invisibility over inheritance, who'd rather be no one than be a Yarrow, who'd built a whole life out of not-being-found—and here I was, the found thing, holding the found thing, offering him everything he'd spent years refusing.

Then he took the compass. Gently. That was the part that undid me—not the taking, but the gentleness, thumb finding the worn spot on the casing where Father's hand had polished the brass for thirty years, as if the metal remembered.

"He hid the charter," Rosalind said. His voice had gone somewhere new—quiet, stripped, the flatness burned off it. "He hid it from Crane, and Crane killed him for it, and the whole coast thinks he died running from debts." He looked up. "Two years I hated him for dying the way he lived. Running. Owing. Leaving me the bill."

"He didn't leave you a bill," I said. "He left you a map. You just never read the will."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere south, I knew—both of us knew, without saying it—forty ships were loading chain and diving bells, and a man with a silver-hairpin smile was checking tide tables.

"We'll need water," Rosalind said, sliding the compass into his coat like it had always lived there. "And we'll need to move tonight, ahead of the flood. Crane's advance boats will beat any walker to the flats unless we cut through the Needle Rocks."

I blinked. "You know the Needle Rocks?"

"I ranger these flats," he said, and for the first time since the window, something almost like a smile touched the corner of his mouth—Father's mouth, our mouth. "Try to keep up, little chartmaker."

I grabbed the oilskin packet and followed him out into the rising rain, furious at him still, furious at Father, furious at the whole drowned rotten legacy of it—and keeping pace, stride for stride, like family.
