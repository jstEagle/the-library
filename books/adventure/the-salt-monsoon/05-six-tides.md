## 5. Six Tides

The first sign was the smell. Salt and green rot riding a wind that had blown hot and dry for three days, and then, near dusk, a wall of cloud standing up over the eastern horizon like something being built by a god with no patience for scaffolding. Rosalind stopped on the crust and watched it, and felt the old ranger's arithmetic begin in the back of his skull: distance to high ground, distance to Vess, weight of water in that cloud, speed of a man on foot versus speed of a flood.

Behind him, Maren said, "That's early."

He turned. She stood with the compass case slung across her back, face tipped toward the storm, and for the first time since the waystation she looked genuinely unsettled. Not afraid, exactly. A woman who had planned this long enough to steal a compass and bait a brother across two hundred miles of salt didn't frighten easily. But the cloud had changed the terms of something she'd calculated, and it showed.

"Early by how much?" he asked.

"A week. Maybe more." She chewed her lip. "Father used to say the monsoon kept a schedule like a drunk keeps a promise. You plan around it and you pray it doesn't notice you."

"Don't call him that."

"He is that. Was." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "You can hate a man and still be his. Ask me how I know."

Rosalind didn't answer. He turned east again, toward Vess, toward the towers that would be ribs in the shallows by morning, and did the arithmetic again, and didn't like the second total any better than the first.

"We need to move," he said. "If that water comes down the basin while we're on the pan—"

"It won't. Not tonight. The first rains fall on the highlands, and it takes the water a day to find the flats." She was already walking. "But it changes the count."

"What count?"

She glanced back at him, and there was something in her face he'd seen before, in other people, at other moments—the look of someone deciding how much truth a stranger could carry. Except he wasn't a stranger. That was the whole problem.

"The tides, Rosalind. The six tides. You've walked two hundred miles after me and you don't even know what you're walking toward."

* * *

They made the edge of the pan by full dark, and there the land did something he'd only heard about from old survey men and drunk prospectors: it began to breathe.

The salt crust gave way to a shelf of dark stone, and below the stone, water. Not floodwater—tide water, pulled in from the drowned basin through channels no one had charted in a generation, filling and emptying with a rhythm you could feel through your boots. Vess hadn't drowned all at once, Maren explained, crouching at the shelf's edge with a flint and a twist of dry scrub she'd hoarded like a miser. The city had been built on a tidal shelf, its founders arrogant enough to think they could argue with the sea, and the sea had won slowly, then all at once, then—every monsoon—partially.

"The wreck sits in the old harbor channel," she said. The fire caught and threw her face into copper relief. "The *Meridian*. Three hundred tons, government-chartered, sunk with everything she carried in the last year the city was still fighting for its life. Most seasons she's under nine feet of silt and twelve of water. But when the monsoon floods the basin, the tides run strange. High water comes up over the shelf, and for a few days the channel scours itself clean. The wreck stands proud of the mud like a whale skeleton. You can walk to her at low water. You can dive her at high."

"How long?"

"Six tides. Three days. High, low, high, low, high, low—and then the flood peaks, the silt comes back down the channel, and she seals herself for another year." She held up six fingers, then folded them down one at a time, deliberate as a priest. "The first tide's tomorrow at dawn. The sixth is the night after next. After that, nothing we do, nothing anyone does, gets near her until next season."

"And Crane knows the schedule."

"Crane *wrote* the schedule. Or bought the man who did. The tide tables for the basin are government documents, Rosalind. The survey team that came out here—the one that vanished—they weren't mapping the flats. They were mapping the window. Confirming it. Timing it to the hour for the fleet."

He sat with that. The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the darkening water, something large shifted and settled, and he chose not to think about what.

"Two days behind us," he said. "You said."

"Two days, when I checked at Cinder Wells. Maybe less now. Crane doesn't send a fleet across the pan in monsoon season unless he's certain of his timing, and he's spent three years being certain." She fed the fire another twist of scrub. "He'll make the fourth tide. Maybe the fifth. He doesn't need the sixth. He needs one good low water and a hundred men with dredges."

"And we have what, exactly? You, me, a compass, and a page of a dead man's logbook."

"We have a head start and we know where we're going." She looked up, and the firelight did something complicated to her expression—half challenge, half plea. "That's more than he has. For now."

Rosalind pulled his knees up and studied the woman who was, by blood and by theft, his sister. She had their father's jaw. He'd noticed it at the waystation and hated noticing it, the way you hate finding your own handwriting in someone else's letter. She had his stubbornness too, probably. His talent for walking away from things. She'd just aimed hers in the opposite direction—toward the family instead of away from it.

"Why me?" he asked. "Out of everyone he ever wronged, everyone she ever—why steal a compass to pull *me* out of hiding?"

"Because you're the best tracker on the flats, and I needed someone who could find me before Crane did." She said it flat, honest, no flattery in it. "And because you're his son, and I thought—"

"Thought what?"

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. "I thought if anyone would understand what Crane took from us, it would be the one who spent his whole life making sure the name Yarrow couldn't hurt him anymore. I thought you'd want to finish it."

"I don't want anything from that man. Not his name, not his enemies, not his wrecks."

"No," she said softly. "You want his compass back. That's the funny part, Rosalind. You spent twenty years erasing him and you took the contract for a piece of his brass the day you heard it was gone."

He had no answer for that, because it was true, and they both sat with the truth between them like a third person at the fire, and eventually she lay down with her back to him, and eventually he slept.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet, and the water had come up in the night.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible part. The shelf where they'd made camp was simply gone, replaced by a foot of opaque green water, and their fire pit was a dark circle on the bottom of it, and the channels Maren had described were no longer channels but a single shallow sea stretching toward a horizon where the cloud wall had grown all night into a range of mountains made of weather.

"The first tide," Maren said. She was standing shin-deep, boots off, trouser legs rolled, testing the current with a stick like a woman greeting an old and unreliable horse. "Right on time, even if the season isn't. The basin fills from the south. We've got until it turns—six hours, maybe seven—before it drops again. We cross at the drop."

"Cross to what?"

She pointed north, where a line of dark shapes broke the surface of the new water, maybe four miles off. Towers. From this distance they looked like the fingers of a drowned hand, and Rosalind felt something turn over in his chest that he refused to name. He'd spent his whole life in country where the horizon was honest—salt, stone, more salt. This was different. This was a place that had been *taken*, and was being given back a few hours at a time, and there was no map in his head for how to feel about it.

"Vess," she said. "And under Vess, the harbor channel. And in the channel—" she patted the compass case on her back, "—the *Meridian*, and whatever else Father left in the mud with her."

They crossed at the turn, as she'd promised, wading through water that never came above their waists but moved with a muscle under it, a long slow pull toward the basin's heart that made every step a negotiation. Rosalind went first with the pack held overhead. Maren followed with the compass case strapped tight, and twice she stumbled, and twice he caught her elbow without thinking about it, and the second time she looked at him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to.

The towers grew as they came. By the time the water shallowed onto the drowned city's outer streets, they stood thirty and forty feet above the surface, crusted white with salt to the waterline, their upper courses still showing, here and there, the ghost of plaster and paint. Doors opened onto nothing. Staircases climbed out of the sea and stopped. A bell hung in one tower's belfry, green with age, swinging gently in the wind with a sound too soft to hear and too large to ignore.

Rosalind had erased himself from every record that would take an eraser. He had made himself a man with no debts worth collecting and no name worth stealing. And standing in the drowned streets of Vess, he understood for the first time that the city had done the same thing—had let the water take its name, its streets, its dead—and that the water gave back only what it chose, only for six tides, and kept the rest forever.

"Low water's in two hours," Maren said. "The channel's on the far side of the market quarter. We can walk it when the mud's bare." She looked at him sideways. "You still with me?"

He should have said no. Every instinct that had kept him alive and invisible for twenty years said *no, turn around, walk back onto the honest salt, let the fleet and the flood and the dead man's secrets drown together*. But the compass was on her back, and the compass was his—had been left to him in a will he'd never read, by a man he'd never mourned—and the tide was going out, and somewhere two days behind them a fleet was crawling toward this same water with a hundred men and a magnate's patience.

"I'm with you until the sixth tide," he said. "After that, we'll see."

"After that," she said, "there's nothing to see. That's the whole point." She smiled, and it was their father's smile, and he looked away from it. "Six tides, Rosalind. Then the sea closes the book for a year. Whatever we're going to do about Crane, about Father, about any of it—we do it before the water comes back for good."

They walked the drowned streets toward the harbor as the tide fell, the city emerging around them inch by inch—rooftops first, then window frames, then whole facades weeping salt water, the mud between the buildings studded with things that might have been furniture or might have been bones. And on the southern horizon, past the towers, past the flats, the storm stood and grew, and under its leading edge, far off and getting closer, Rosalind saw something that made him stop and put a hand on Maren's arm.

Smoke. Not one column—many. A dozen, maybe more, spread across the southern sky in a ragged line, burning wet coal or worse, the signal-smoke of ships under steam.

Maren followed his gaze and went very still.

"That's not possible," she said. "They're two days behind. They're *two days*—"

"Then either your count was wrong," Rosalind said, "or Crane stopped being two days behind the moment the monsoon came early. He knew the season would break. He's been running ahead of the water the whole time."

The tide was still falling. The channel was still hours from bare. And the smoke was coming on with the storm behind it, and between them and the wreck lay nothing but a drowned city, a draining harbor, and whatever their father had hidden in the mud.

"Six tides," Maren whispered. "God help us. We're down to five."
