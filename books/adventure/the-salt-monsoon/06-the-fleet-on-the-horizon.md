## 6. The Fleet on the Horizon

The water came before the ships did.

Rosalind saw it first as a change in the light—a sheen spreading across the salt pan to the south, so thin it might have been a trick of heat. Then the wind shifted and carried the smell of it: green, mineral, alive. The monsoon had turned in the high country a week early, and all that rain was coming down the dry riverbeds toward Vess, filling the flats like a cup.

"Two days," Maren said beside him. She stood with her boots off, toes in the first finger of floodwater, reading it the way he read tracks. "Maybe less. The pan tilts toward the city. Every hour we stand here, the water gains."

Rosalind didn't answer. He was looking south, past the waterline, to where the horizon had grown teeth.

At first he took them for rocks. Then one of them moved.

Ships—no, not ships, not properly. Barges and dredges and flat-bottomed salvage hulks, riding on their own puddles of pumped water or crawling on caterpillar treads where the flood hadn't reached yet, a fleet of them strung out across the pan like beetles crossing a kitchen floor. He counted nine before he stopped counting. Cranes folded flat against their hulls for travel. Smoke standing up from three of them in greasy columns. And at the head of the line, larger than the rest, a flagship with a black funnel and a name painted on its bow that even at this distance he could read without wanting to: *Meridian's Ghost*.

Crane hadn't even bothered to hide what he'd named his flagship after.

"He's early," Maren said. Her voice had gone flat, which Rosalind was learning meant afraid. "He wasn't supposed to have treads. Those are mining crawlers. That's not salvage money—that's charter money."

"You know his operation."

"I know his reputation." She pulled her boots back on, fast. "Everyone in the salvage trade knows Crane. He doesn't find wrecks. He finds *surveys* of wrecks, and then he finds the surveyors, and then there aren't any surveyors anymore." She looked at him sideways. "Sound familiar?"

It did. It sat in his chest like swallowed stone—the waystation, the untouched food, the instruments still running, the boy whispering *sixth tide* with blood in his mouth. He'd been telling himself since Cinder Wells that this was a chase. A contract. One thief, one compass, one debt settled. But fleets didn't move against thieves. Fleets moved against witnesses.

"How far behind us?" he asked.

Maren shaded her eyes, calculating. "They're making maybe four miles an hour on the wet pan, slower where it's still dry. We're faster on foot but they don't stop." She paused. "Two days. Maybe a day and a half if the flood helps them. Floating hulks move quicker than walking people."

"And us?"

She pointed north-northeast, to where a jagged line broke the horizon like a broken jaw. "Needle Rocks. Cuts two days off the route to Vess—if you go through instead of around."

"Nobody goes through."

"Nobody smart." She shouldered her pack. "But nobody smart is going to beat that fleet either. Your choice, brother. You're the tracker."

He hated that she used the word like she owned it. He hated more that she was right about the arithmetic. Nine vessels, a day and a half, and somewhere in his shirt, wrapped in oilcloth against his sternum, the brass compass that had started all of this—and the logbook page folded with it, coordinates in his father's hand, the accusation against Aldous Crane written in ink that had waited twenty years to be read aloud.

His father's legacy, crawling toward a man who killed surveyors.

"We go through," Rosalind said.

* * *

The Needle Rocks earned their name honestly. They were the bones of some ancient reef, thrust up when the world was younger and drier, and the monsoon floods had spent centuries carving channels between them—slots and corridors of stone barely wide enough for a body, some of them running with water, some of them dry until they weren't. The locals had names for the worst of them. Maren knew most of those names. She wouldn't tell him where she'd learned them, and he didn't ask, because he was starting to understand that everything about Maren Yarrow came from places she wouldn't name.

"The trick," she said, standing at the mouth of the first channel, "is to never be in a slot when the surge comes. The tide pushes water through these gaps like a fist through a sleeve. You hear it coming—there's a sound, like a train very far away. When you hear it, you climb. Doesn't matter how tired you are. Doesn't matter what you're carrying. You climb."

"What happens if you don't climb?"

"I've seen what happens if you don't climb." She stepped into the shadow between the rocks. "Keep moving."

For six hours they moved. It was the hardest tracking-terrain Rosalind had ever crossed, because there were no tracks—only stone and water and the occasional ledge of dried salt that crumbled underfoot like old bread. Maren led. She read the rock the way he read sand: a dark stain meaning seepage, a scatter of shell fragments meaning surge reach, a rope scar worn into a limestone lip meaning someone had come this way often enough to leave a mark. Twice she stopped dead and made him listen, and twice he heard nothing, and both times, thirty seconds later, the far-away-train sound came moaning up the channel and they stood on high stone while brown water boiled through the gap below them, deep enough to drown a horse.

The second time, Rosalind found himself watching her instead of the water. She crouched at the edge of the ledge with her arms wrapped around her knees, utterly calm, counting under her breath. Their father had taught her this. Their father, who had never once taken Rosalind anywhere higher than a fence line, who had kept his second family a secret so complete that Rosalind had learned his own sister's name from a stolen will. The same hands had taught them both different things—or the same things, in different rooms. Tracking. Water discipline. How to count a surge.

He didn't know what to do with a man who could be two fathers. So he did what he always did with things he couldn't hold. He put it away and watched the trail.

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf above a flooded chamber the locals called the Throat—a slot so narrow the sky was a gray ribbon overhead, water churning through it eight feet below with a sound like slow breathing. No fire. Cold rations. Maren took first watch, sitting with her back to a boulder, the compass case open in her lap.

Rosalind lay awake listening to the Throat breathe and thinking about the fleet. Nine hulls. Treads. Charter money. In his mind he ran the pursuit backward the way he'd run trails backward a hundred times: start from the kill site, work to the last sign. The survey team had died or vanished near Vess. Crane's fleet was moving toward Vess now. Which meant either Crane didn't know what had happened to his own hired instruments—which stank of a lie—or he knew exactly, and the fleet was moving in to finish a job.

And the compass. The compass kept snagging in his thoughts like a hook in wool. Of all the things to steal from a dead man's estate—a man who owned almost nothing worth stealing—Maren had taken a battered brass instrument and left the silver. Unless the theft hadn't been opportunistic. Unless she'd known exactly what the compass was, and what it carried, and who would come after it.

He'd told himself in Cinder Wells that the buyer named Salt was a stranger. He was done telling himself stories.

"Maren."

She didn't turn. "You should sleep."

"Did you steal the compass to draw me out?"

The Throat breathed. Somewhere below, water found a new channel and the sound changed pitch. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in a way that cost her something; he could hear the cost.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you'd erased yourself so thoroughly I couldn't find you any other way." Now she turned, and in the dark her eyes caught what little light the sky gave. "I wrote to the ranger registry. They'd never heard of a Rosalind Yarrow. I wrote to the creditors in Cinder Wells—they knew the name but not the man. Twenty years, Rosalind. Our father dies, and his son doesn't come to the funeral, doesn't claim the will, doesn't exist. I couldn't track a ghost. So I stole the one thing a ghost would bleed for."

He sat up slowly. The anger came first—it always came first, anger was the load-bearing wall of him—but underneath it was something worse: the reluctant respect he'd felt on the salt pan, reading her stride, recognizing the discipline. She had tracked *him*. She had laid a trail of bait across half the territory and let him do what he did best, and he had walked straight down it calling it a contract.

"You manipulated me."

"I recruited you." A ghost of a smile. "There's a difference. Manipulation is when you don't tell someone the truth afterward."

"That's not the truth. That's a rationalization."

"It's both." She closed the compass case. "Ask me again when we're not sitting over a hole that breathes. Sleep. Tomorrow's the Squeeze, and the Squeeze doesn't forgive."

* * *

The Squeeze was worse than anything the Needle Rocks had shown them yet—a descending corridor where two walls leaned together until they nearly met, forcing travelers sideways through a gap slick with spray, the floodwater running fast at the bottom of it. Maren went first, demonstrating: face to the wall, hands in old drilled footholds, weight on the balls of the feet, shuffle, breathe, shuffle. Halfway down, the gap narrowed to eighteen inches and the water rose to their thighs and the current pushed like a living thing trying to peel them off the stone.

Rosalind was three moves from the bottom when he heard the train.

Far away. Very far away. But coming.

"Faster," Maren called, and her voice had lost all its calm. "Climb, don't wade—there's a pocket, left side, twenty feet up—"

He didn't make it.

The surge hit the corridor like the whole monsoon squeezing through a keyhole. The water didn't rise so much as arrive—one moment thigh-deep and pushy, the next a brown wall that took his feet out from under him and slammed him against the downstream wall. His pack strap caught a knob of rock and held, mercifully, and he hung there in the roaring dark with water hammering his chest, unable to breathe, unable to see, feeling the strap fibers begin to creak.

Above him, dimly, Maren was screaming something he couldn't hear.

The strap tore.

The water took him.

Later, he would piece it together in fragments: the tumble, the darkness, the moment of terrible clarity underwater when the flood rolled him end over end and he saw, absurdly, a shaft of daylight through the churn. His hands went to his chest on instinct—twenty years of instinct, the ranger's reflex to inventory what you carry—and found the oilcloth bundle still there, the cord still knotted, the compass safe against his sternum.

And he understood, in that suspended half-second, that he had a choice. Let go and live, or hold on and—

No. That wasn't the choice. The choice was simpler and stupider than that. The bundle was snagged. On what, he couldn't see—something below, a root of stone, a drowned timber wedged in the corridor's throat—and the current was trying to tear him off it, and every fiber of survival sense screamed *let go*, and his hands would not.

Because it wasn't just a compass. It was the only thing his father had ever given him that he hadn't had to steal or fight for. It was the will he'd never read, the funeral he'd never attended, the apology that would never come. It was proof that somewhere inside the cold man there had been a room where Rosalind's name was written down. If he let it go into the flood, it was gone—not lost, gone—and with it the last physical evidence that he had ever mattered to anyone whose blood he shared.

The current ripped at him. His lungs burned. The world went white at the edges.

*Let go,* said the part of him that had survived twenty years by carrying nothing.

*Hold,* said the part that was a Yarrow.

He held.

Something hit his arm—hands, gripping, hauling—and the snag tore free and the flood spat them both sideways into a wider chamber where the water spread and slowed, and suddenly there was air, filthy sweet air, and he was coughing on hands and knees on a gravel bar with Maren pounding his back and shouting words he'd remember later as probably profanity.

When he could speak, the first thing he did was open the oilcloth.

The compass sat in his palm, streaming water, brass dulled but whole. The needle swung, steadied, and settled pointing north—as if nothing had happened, as if drowning was a thing that happened to other people. Beneath it, the logbook page had soaked through, the ink feathering at the edges. He lifted it with both hands, careful as a relic, and the coordinates were still legible. Barely. The accusation against Crane had blurred into a gray smear, but the name itself had been written darker, pressed harder, and it survived: **CRANE**.

"You idiot," Maren said. Her voice cracked in the middle of it. "You absolute—you should have let it go. It's a compass. It points north. There are ten thousand compasses in the world."

"Not this one."

"No." She sat down hard on the gravel beside him, dripping, shaking now that it was over. "Not this one." She looked at the page in his hands, at the smeared ink, and something moved across her face—guilt, maybe, or fear. "We need to copy it. Tonight. Before it fades. Everything your father wrote down matters more than we thought, and if Crane gets to Vess first—"

Rosalind looked up through the ribbon of sky at the Needle Rocks, at the gray light beyond them, and thought about the fleet crawling across the flooded pan. Nine hulls. A day and a half, maybe less now. Somewhere ahead, the drowned city rising out of the shallows with its towers like ribs, holding whatever his father had died protecting.

A day ago he'd wanted only his inheritance back. Standing on the gravel bar, soaked and half-strangled, he felt the want sharpen into something harder. Not restoration. Not legitimacy. Not his name in a ledger where creditors could strike it out.

*Mine.* His father's legacy, his father's truth, his father's fight—passed down whether he'd asked for it or not. And Aldous Crane, with his treads and his charter money and his murdered surveyors, was not going to take it.

"Copy it twice," Rosalind said. "One copy for each of us. And Maren—" he wound the cord back around the oilcloth, tight, "—from here on, we don't wait for the fleet. We race it."

She studied him for a long moment, water still dripping from her chin, and then she nodded slowly, and he saw in her face the same expression he'd worn in Cinder Wells: humiliation hardening into purpose. Or maybe, in her case, purpose hardening into something like hope.

"Then we'd better move," she said. "Vess rises with tomorrow's low tide. And little brother—"

"I'm older."

"You're *slower*." She pulled him to his feet. "Move."
