## 7. What Father Left

The Needle Rocks had spat them out an hour past dark, and Maren had never been so glad to see a fire in her life. Rosalind built it the way he did everything—efficiently, silently, with a flat of driftwood and a striker he kept in a waxed pouch against his ribs. The flames caught low and blue, then climbed. Beyond the firelight, the flooded pan stretched black to the horizon, and somewhere out there, two days back, Crane's fleet was burning lamps like a string of drowned stars.

She sat across from him with her boots off and her feet steaming, and she watched him dry the compass case with a rag he'd clearly carried for years. He'd nearly died for it that afternoon. She'd watched him go under in the channel between the rocks, watched the water take him whole, and then watched him surface with the case held above his head like a man raising a lantern. He hadn't even looked at her when he climbed out. He'd looked at the compass.

"You could have drowned," she said.

"I don't drown." He wrung out his sleeve. "I've had practice staying down."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered over the mountains, the monsoon still arguing with itself about when to arrive. Maren pulled her knees up and studied her brother across the flames—the sharp planes of his face, the way he sat with his back to nothing, always angled so he could see the dark. He looked like their father. That was the thing she hadn't been able to say all day, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the waystation, since the logbook, since she'd watched him track her across forty miles of salt with the patience of a man reading his own handwriting.

He looked like Elias Yarrow when Elias Yarrow forgot to be cruel.

"Tell me about him," she said.

Rosalind's hands went still on the compass case. "You know about him."

"I know what Mother told me. I want to know what you know."

"Why?"

"Because we're sitting on a salt pan waiting for a fleet of pirates to catch up to us, and I'd rather not spend the night doing arithmetic on my chances." She kept her voice level. It was a skill she'd learned early—her mother's gift, the ability to sound calm while everything underneath was moving. "And because I've been carrying his memory alone for twenty years, and now I find out there's someone else who has a piece of it, and I want to know if the pieces fit."

Rosalind was quiet for a long time. The firelight moved on his face. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flat quality she was learning to recognize as his armor.

"He taught me to read tracks when I was six. Before that, he taught me to be quiet. Before that, I don't remember." He turned the compass over in his hands. "He'd take me out on the flats before dawn. We'd walk until the sun came up, and he'd point at nothing and say, *what do you see*, and if I said *nothing* he'd make me look again until I found the beetle track or the cracked crust or the place where the wind had shifted the dust. He said the desert doesn't lie, but it doesn't tell the truth either. It just is. He said most men can't stand that."

"That sounds like him," Maren said softly.

"It shouldn't." Rosalind's eyes came up. "Because you said you were six when he left. You said your mother told you he was a surveyor, an honest man, that the fleet destroyed him. That's not the man I walked the flats with."

Maren's stomach tightened. "Then tell me who he was."

"A coward." He said it without heat, which was worse. "He owed money to half of Cinder Wells. He owed it to the salvage guild, to the ferry office, to a woman named Petra who ran the counting house and never forgave a debt in her life. And one morning he was gone. No note. My mother stood in the doorway with me on her hip and watched the road for a week. He didn't come back. He didn't send word. He let us take the debt. Do you understand what that means? I spent my whole life paying it. Every contract I took, every name I erased, every time I disappeared from a town before the creditors came—*that* was him. He taught me the shape of running by doing it first."

The fire cracked. Maren found she was gripping her own knees hard enough to hurt.

"He didn't run," she said.

"Then where was he?"

"Dead. Eight years after he left you." She made herself say it plainly, because she could see he didn't know—had never known—and the realization opened something in her that was half pity and half fury. "He died at Vess. On Crane's flagship, in the hold of the *Prosperity*. Officially it was a fever. My mother got a letter from the guild, a pension of forty marks, and a sealed letter she never opened because she said it would be full of lies. She was right."

Rosalind had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man recalculating everything, and Maren watched him do it—the debt, the running, the erasure, twenty years of a story rearranging itself in the dark behind his eyes.

"That's not possible," he said finally. "The creditors came. They took the house."

"The creditors came because he was dead and the guild didn't want to pay out what it owed him. He'd finished a survey for them. A full chart of the Vess approaches, soundings, tide tables, the wreck itself. And when he delivered it, Crane looked at it and realized what my father had found, and my father realized what Crane would do with it, and three weeks later my father was dead of a *fever*." Her voice was climbing and she let it. "He didn't abandon you. He was trying to send money home. He was working salvage seasons to clear the debt so he could come back, and the debt kept growing because the guild kept charging him for his own equipment, his own rations, his own air. He was a prisoner with paperwork."

"You weren't there."

"No. But my mother was his sister's daughter. She grew up with him. She knew him before the debts, before the fleet, before whatever it was that made him the man who walked out on a six-year-old boy." Maren leaned forward. "And she told me stories. Real ones. Do you want to hear them, or do you want to keep your version? Because you can't have both."

Rosalind looked at the fire. For a moment she thought he'd refuse—that the armor would hold, that the man who erased himself from every record would erase this too. Then he said, quietly: "Tell me."

* * *

"He used to sing," Maren said. "Badly. He had a song about a mule named Balthus who refused to die, and he'd sing it while he worked the survey instruments, and the whole crew would groan, and he'd sing it louder. My mother said you could hear him a mile off across the flats. She said the birds hated him."

Rosalind made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"He kept a garden. In the middle of the salt, at the waystation at Harrow Bend—he hauled water in barrels, a mile each way, and grew beans. Actual beans. The other surveyors thought he was mad. He said a man who can't grow something in a place has no business mapping it. He said—" she closed her eyes, reaching for the exact words her mother had given her like relics, "—he said *you don't know a country until you've made it give you something it doesn't want to give*."

"Beans," Rosalind said.

"Beans. And he gave them away. Every harvest. He'd leave sacks on doorsteps in Cinder Wells at night, no note. There were families in the salt quarter who ate that winter on Yarrow beans and never knew who to thank."

"That's not—" Rosalind stopped. Started again. "The man I knew never gave anything away. He counted everything. He counted the water in our barrels. He counted the nails in the floor. When I was seven I broke a plate and he made me work it off, three days of hauling brine for the tannery. He was—" Rosalind's jaw worked. "He was *precise*. Like the desert. Like something that just *is*, and doesn't care what it costs you."

"Or like a man who was terrified," Maren said. "A man who knew exactly how much everything cost because he'd watched debt eat his family once already, growing up. My mother said his father—our grandfather—lost everything to a ferry charter. Lost the boats, lost the house, lost his name in the ledger. Elias grew up watching a man drown in numbers. Of course he counted the nails. He thought if he could just count everything, hold everything exactly, the water wouldn't come in."

Rosalind was silent for a long time. The fire had settled to coals, and she fed it another piece of driftwood, and the light came up again between them.

"The song," he said at last. "Balthus the mule. I know it."

"You do not."

"I do." And then, in a voice low and rough and completely unguarded, Rosalind Yarrow sang three lines of a mule's ballad into the dark over a flooding salt pan, and Maren felt her eyes sting, because it was the same tune, note for note, the same ridiculous rising lilt on *Balthus*—the same song, carried down two separate childhoods like a river splitting around a rock and joining again on the far side.

"He taught us both," she said. "The same man. Two faces."

"Or one face," Rosalind said, "and two windows."

They sat with that. The thunder was closer now, and the wind had turned, coming off the water instead of the mountains, carrying the smell of the deep flood. Maren knew what was coming in the conversation, the way you know a wave is coming before it breaks. She said it anyway.

"He was a martyr, Rosalind. He died for that chart. He died so Crane couldn't own Vess."

"He was a coward who died by accident," Rosalind said, "and we've spent twenty years building a saint out of it because the truth was too heavy to carry." But his voice cracked on *heavy*, just slightly, and she saw the boy in it—the seven-year-old hauling brine for a broken plate, the boy who'd watched the road for a week.

"He left you," she said. "I know. I know what that cost, and I will never tell you it didn't. But he left to *work*, Rosalind. He left to pay. He wrote letters. My mother had them—dozens. He wrote to her because he was ashamed to write to you. He said—" Maren stopped. Her throat had closed. She pushed through it. "He said, *tell the boy I'm building him a dry house*. Every letter. Every single one. *Tell the boy I'm building him a dry house.* And then the letters stopped, and the pension came, and my mother knew."

Rosalind had put his face in his hands. She couldn't see his expression, only the shake of his shoulders, once, like a man shrugging off a blow he'd taken twenty years ago and only now felt land.

"He was a martyr," she said again, gentler now. "Say it."

"He was a man," Rosalind said into his hands. "He was a man who was small and scared and counted his nails, and he sang about a mule, and he grew beans in hell, and he left his son in a debt he couldn't fix, and he died in a hold for a piece of paper." He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and furious and alive. "He was *both*, Maren. That's what nobody tells you. That's the inheritance. Not the compass. Not the chart. *Both.*"

Maren nodded, and found she was crying too, silently, the way her mother had cried at the pension letter—no sound, just water, as if grief in their family had always known to keep quiet.

"He'd have liked you," Rosalind said. "You track like him. You plan like him. You—" he almost smiled. "You sing badly?"

"Appallingly."

"Then he'd have liked you fine."

* * *

They let the fire burn down after that, and neither of them spoke for a while, and the silence was a different silence than before—less like a wall, more like a shared room.

It was Rosalind who broke it, because of course it was; he could never let a silence stand without checking it for tracks.

"The logbook page," he said. "The coordinates. When we reach the wreck—what exactly are we taking?"

Maren looked at the coals. She had known this question was coming since the waystation, since she'd shown him the page, since the moment she'd decided to lift the compass and set him on her trail like a hound after a fox. She had rehearsed a dozen answers. She watched him across the fire, her brother, her father's son, the man who had just handed her the truth of their family like something breakable, and she thought about the other truth—the one she'd been carrying folded up in her coat since before she ever left Cinder Wells.

"The survey," she said. "The full chart. Father hid it, and Crane wants it, and it's the only thing standing between that man and everything inside Vess."

"And then what?"

"And then it's ours," she said. "Our name. Our choice. We decide what it's worth."

Rosalind studied her for a long moment, the way he'd studied the salt pan at dawn, looking for what wasn't shown. She held his gaze and kept her face still and hated herself for how good she'd gotten at it.

"You're lying," he said quietly. "Or part of you is. I can see the track but not what made it."

The thunder rolled in from the mountains, closer now, and the first fat drops of the early monsoon began to hiss in the coals between them. Out on the black water, far to the south, a lamp blinked twice—signal or accident, impossible to say.

Maren pulled her coat tighter and didn't answer.

"Get some sleep," she said. "The tide won't wait, and neither will Crane."

Rosalind held her eyes a moment longer, then lay back with the compass case under his head, and Maren sat watch alone with the rain starting and the lie sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone—knowing that by the third tide, one way or another, he was going to find out exactly what she'd been tracking him toward all along.
