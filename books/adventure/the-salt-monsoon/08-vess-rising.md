## 8. Vess Rising

The tide went out like a held breath finally released, and what it left behind was a city.

Rosalind stopped at the edge of the shallows and forgot, for a long moment, that he was being hunted and hunting in the same hour. The water slid back across the flats in braided silver channels, and where it had been there rose towers—dozens of them, hundreds maybe, breaching the flooded plain like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die an age ago and never finished. Salt crusted their flanks white as bone. Some stood only shoulder-high above the waterline, stumps wearing crowns of dried weed. Others climbed forty, fifty feet into the grey morning air, their upper stories still bearing shutters, balconies, the black sockets of windows that had watched the monsoon come and go for two hundred years.

"Vess," Maren said beside him. She said it quietly, the way you'd say a name over a grave.

"You've seen it before."

"In charts. In Father's notes." She shaded her eyes against a sun that couldn't decide whether to burn through the cloud. "Never like this."

The drowned city spread out ahead of them for better than a mile, streets running with ankle-deep water, canals that had been canals once and were canals again, bridges arcing between towers with their keystones furred green. Fish moved in the flooded intersections. Somewhere out in the middle distance, a bell hung in an open belfry, and when the wind came off the flats it stirred against the bronze and made a sound too faint to be called ringing—more a memory of ringing, a hum in the teeth.

Rosalind had spent nine years making himself unmemorable. He had burned papers, paid clerks, let debts stand in his name so that his name would be worth nothing to anyone who came looking. He had thought himself a man without a past.

The city in front of him made that look like vanity. Vess had a past so heavy it had pulled it under the sea, and the sea gave it back six tides a season, and then took it again, and no one had ever managed to make it stay forgotten or remembered. It simply was, on its own terms, indifferent to every claim ever laid against it.

He understood why his father had loved it. That understanding arrived unwelcome, like a hand on the shoulder from behind.

"We move now," he said. "Tide's already turning. We have maybe five hours before the water starts climbing the towers."

Maren was already walking. She moved through the shallows the way she moved through everything—economically, heels rolling, weight low, a woman who had learned early that splash meant sound and sound meant found. Rosalind fell in behind her and did not think about how natural it felt to fall in behind her, how the last three days had worn a groove between them that his feet fit into without argument.

They crossed the outer ring through streets named by nothing living. Doorways opened onto water. A stone lion lay face-down in a flooded plaza, its snout buried in silt, one paw raised forever toward a door it had guarded until the sea came. Rosalind waded past it and felt the flats' old arithmetic reasserting itself: distance, time, water level, the fleet two days behind them and closing. Crane's ships would be crawling up the eastern channel even now, masts visible from the high ground if anyone cared to look.

Someone would care to look soon enough.

* * *

The bell tower stood near the center of the old city, on a rise that must have been a hill before the drowning—a church or a counting house, Rosalind couldn't say which; Vess had been rich enough and pious enough that the two buildings probably shared architects. Its tower rose clean out of the flood, sixty feet of salt-white stone, the belfry open to the sky on four sides, the great bell hanging silent within.

Maren saw it first. "Smoke," she said.

He saw it a heartbeat later—the ghost of it, a dark stain fanning up the tower's seaward face, old and rain-washed but unmistakable. Someone had kept a fire in that tower, and recently. Within the season. Maybe within the month.

They approached the way they'd approach anything on the flats: wide, circling, giving the structure room to disgorge whatever it held. But the doorway at the tower's base stood open, half-drowned, and nothing came out of it but the smell of cold ash and old paper.

Inside, stairs spiraled up through gloom, the lower flights lost to black water. They climbed the dry ones single file, boots scraping on stone worn hollow by centuries of feet, and came out into the belfry.

The survey team had lived here well.

Cots lined the curved walls, six of them, bedrolls still rolled, blankets folded with government squareness. Crates sat stacked against the north wall, stenciled with the crown-and-quadrant of the Interior Survey: instruments, rations, lamp oil. A brass theodolite stood mounted on a tripod in the belfry's western arch, its lens cap off, its sights trained on the drowned rooftops to the west—as if the man who'd used it had stepped away mid-measurement and meant to come back.

Nobody had come back.

Rosalind circled the camp slowly, reading it the way he read any ground. The food stores were untouched—tins sealed, hardtack stacked, a bag of rice sitting open with its scoop standing in it. Water barrels full. A shirt hung drying on a line strung between two columns, stiff with salt, forgotten. Everything about the place said *interrupted*, not *abandoned*. Abandonment was a decision. This was a sentence cut off mid-word.

"No tracks leaving," Maren said. She was at the southern arch, looking down at the flooded street below. "I've been watching. The mud down there is undisturbed except for birds. Six men don't walk out of a tower and leave no prints."

"They didn't walk."

She turned. "You saw something."

"Come here." He crouched by the third cot. Beneath its frame, wedged where a careless searcher wouldn't look but a careful sleeper would reach, lay a knife—a survey-issue blade, cheap steel, government stamp on the flat. It was snapped off an inch below the hilt. Not dropped. Broken, in a grip, in a fight, and then hidden by a man who knew he might not get another chance to arm himself.

Maren knelt beside him. For a moment neither of them touched it.

"There was a struggle," she said. "Here. Up here, sixty feet above the water."

"And no blood," Rosalind said. "No bodies. No gear taken—they left the instruments, the food, everything worth carrying. Whoever came for them wanted the men, not the equipment." He straightened. "Or wanted what the men knew, and didn't much care about the rest."

"The junior surveyor," Maren said. "At the waystation. Before he—" She stopped. She had been there for the dying too, and her voice did something complicated around the memory. "He said the sixth tide. And Crane."

"He also said *they signed*. Remember? I've been chewing on that for two days. *We all signed.* Signed what? A petition? A report?" Rosalind looked around the belfry again, slower now, letting his eye go where it wanted instead of where he told it to. "Six government surveyors don't get taken off a bell tower in the middle of a sovereign survey without paperwork. There's always paperwork."

He found it in the fourth crate.

The documents were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was tied with survey cord, and whoever had wrapped them had done it fast—you could tell by the knots, hasty granny hitches instead of proper bends. Inside were perhaps thirty sheets: field notes, sounding records, tide tables, sketches of the drowned streets drawn in a fine confident hand that Rosalind recognized before he understood why he recognized it, and then understood, and had to stand very still for a moment with the pages trembling slightly in his grip.

His father drew the way he talked—precisely, coldly, every line accountable. Rosalind had seen that hand on debt ledgers and on the single birthday card he'd ever received, a card that read *To R., continued success* in letters like fence posts. Here the same hand had drawn the towers of Vess with something close to tenderness. Every window accounted for. Every crack annotated. In the margin of one sheet, beside a sketch of the bell tower they were standing in, a note in the same ink: *Belfry dry at all tides. Good light. J. says the acoustics are a wonder. Should sing here, if we're here long enough.*

*J. says.* His father had known these men. Had eaten with them, joked with them, marked the acoustics of their bell. And then someone had come, and the men were gone, and the drawings remained, wrapped in haste by hands that had expected to return.

Maren was watching him hold the pages. She didn't say anything. There was nothing kind to say, and both of them knew better than to spend words badly.

Rosalind set the sketches aside and went through the rest. Sounding charts. Tide logs. And then, near the bottom of the bundle, a cluster of official sheets—heavier paper, letterhead, the kind of document that existed to prove other documents true.

Every one of them had been burned.

Not destroyed—*cauterized*. The top corner of each sheet bore a scorch mark the size of a palm, deliberate, uniform, applied with a flame hot enough to char through the first layer of paper. And where the scorch had been, the seal had been. Rosalind could see the ghost of it in the fibers: a circular depression where wax or an embossed stamp had once sat, torn away or burned away with surgical care. Around the burns, the text survived—requisitions, orders, authorizations—but the authority behind them had been excised. No office. No signature line intact. No way to trace an order back to the desk that issued it.

"Someone burned the seals off," Maren said. "Why burn just the seal? Burn the whole page if you want it gone."

"Because the page is evidence and the seal is identity." Rosalind heard his own voice go flat, ranger-flat, the voice he used on bad ground. "Whoever did this wanted the orders readable. Wanted us—or anyone—to know that orders were given, that men were taken, that this happened inside the law. They just didn't want anyone knowing *whose* law. Whose desk. Which signature." He looked up at her. "This wasn't looting. This was editing."

The wind moved through the belfry and the great bell hummed above them, that sub-audible moan, and Rosalind felt the awe of the morning curdle in his chest into something colder and more familiar. Suspicion. The flats had taught him suspicion the way other places taught religion—thoroughly, and early, and with punishment for doubt.

A government survey team comes to a drowned city. A magnate with a salvage fleet wants what's under it. The surveyors sign something, or refuse to sign something, and are taken from a dry tower in the night without a track left behind. Their father's orders are preserved but stripped of origin. And a junior man, dying on a waystation floor miles from here, spends his last breath on two words: *sixth tide*, and *Crane*.

"You're thinking what I'm thinking," Maren said.

"I'm thinking we should assume the fleet isn't two days behind. I'm thinking a man who erases seals doesn't wait for slow ships." Rosalind repacked the documents, taking the sketches and the burned orders both, wrapping them in his own oilskin. "And I'm thinking that whatever your compass is supposed to unlock, it isn't treasure."

"It was never treasure." Maren paused at the arch, silhouetted against the grey water and the rib-cage towers beyond. "Father didn't think in treasure. He thought in *positions*. Coordinates. Fixes. Ask me how I know."

"I'm asking."

She didn't answer yet. Below them, out on the flooded plain, the light shifted—and Rosalind caught it, the thing Maren had already seen: a gleam on the eastern channel, brief as a knife turned over. Then another. Sunlight off glass, or off brass fittings, moving where no boat had any honest reason to be, two days ahead of any schedule Crane's fleet could keep.

The bell hummed. The tide, far out on the flats, began its long turn.

"They're early," Maren said.
