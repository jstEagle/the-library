## 9. Crane's Offer

The boat found us on the fourth morning of the flood, when the salt pan had become a shallow sea and the horizon had dissolved into a smear of gray.

I heard it before I saw it—the flat slap of an engine laboring in water too thin for its draft. Maren heard it a half-second later. She'd been sleeping sitting up against the mast step of our stolen skiff, her father's compass open in her lap like a book she couldn't stop reading, and her eyes snapped open with the particular alertness of someone who had spent years expecting to be caught.

"Engine," she said.

"I know."

"Ours doesn't have one."

"I know that too."

We'd been poling and sailing the skiff east for three days, following the drowned causeway road toward Vess, riding the monsoon's first surge across country that had been solid ground when I was born. The water came in stages—first puddles, then sheets, then channels deep enough to float a hull—and we rode the leading edge of it like surfers on a slow wave. Crane's fleet was two days behind us by Maren's estimate. We'd beaten them to the flood. What we hadn't counted on was that Crane would send his fastest boat ahead of the rest, the way a man throws a stone before he commits to crossing a river.

It came around a stand of dead tamarisks that now stood waist-deep, their black branches dripping. A long, low salvage launch, gunmetal gray, with a covered wheelhouse and a bow built for punching through chop. It was flying no colors, which told me everything. Legitimate ships fly colors. This one flew nothing because officially it didn't exist yet.

Maren stood up in the skiff, and I put a hand on her arm.

"Don't," I said.

"They're going to find us anyway."

"Then let them do the work."

She sat back down, but her hand went under her coat, where she kept a short-barreled flare gun she'd been carrying since the waystation. I kept my hands visible and my face blank. That was the old ranger discipline: when you can't win a fight, be so unremarkable that nobody starts one.

The launch slowed fifty yards out and idled. A man stood at the rail—a big man, sunburned to the color of old brick, wearing a salvage fleet jacket with the sleeves cut off at the shoulder. He had a voice that carried across water without effort, the way certain men's voices do, trained or born to it.

"Rosalind Yarrow!" he called. "And the other one. Maren, is it? Aldous Crane sends his regards."

The name landed on me like a dropped anchor. Not because I hadn't expected Crane to know we were coming—I had—but because he knew *her*. He knew her name. He'd said it easily, familiarly, the way you say the name of someone whose file you've read twice.

"How does he know my name?" Maren whispered. Her face had gone very still, which I was learning was what she did instead of going pale.

"We're about to find out," I said.

The man at the rail raised both hands, palms out, empty. "Name's Dree. First lieutenant, advance element. I'm authorized to talk, not shoot, so let's all stay comfortable." He gestured at the flooded plain around us. "Beautiful morning for it. Water's warm. Fish are already moving in off the coast. You can see them from here if the light's right—silver threads in the shallows. First ones in eleven months."

Nobody said anything. The engine idled. Somewhere behind us, thunder muttered over the interior desert, more rain coming down to fill the pan.

Dree seemed unbothered by our silence. He reached inside the wheelhouse and came out with a canvas satchel, which he held up.

"Aldous Crane is a reasonable man," he said. "People say otherwise, but those people have usually just done something unreasonable themselves. Here's what reasonable looks like." He opened the satchel and drew out papers, fanning them like a card sharp. "Item one. Full pardon, both of you, stamped and sealed. Whatever the survey commission thinks happened at their little camp out east—whatever names end up on whatever report—it won't be yours. You were never there. You were never anywhere."

He let that sit. I felt Maren go rigid beside me. Neither of us had told anyone about the waystation. Neither of us had spoken of the dying junior surveyor, or the instruments still running, or the food gone untouched. And yet here was this man, offering to erase us from an event he shouldn't have known we'd seen.

*They know because they did it,* I thought. *Or ordered it. Or paid for it.*

"Item two," Dree continued, cheerful as a market vendor. "Cash. Fifty thousand crowns, split however you like between you. Paid on delivery, no questions about what you intend to do with it. Buy a farm. Buy a ship. Buy a very large hat. Crane doesn't care."

"And item three?" Maren said. Her voice came out level. I gave her credit for that.

Dree smiled. "Legitimacy. Which is worth more than the other two combined, though most people don't realize it until they've lost it." He looked directly at me. "Rosalind Yarrow. Desert ranger, twelve years' service, exemplary record—which is remarkable, considering how hard you've worked to make sure nobody can find the record. Discharged without honors two years back. Debts registered in Cinder Wells, Saltcross, and the ferry towns along the Vess Road. Total outstanding obligation—" he consulted a paper, though I'd have bet anything he knew the number by heart— "four thousand, two hundred and sixty crowns, plus interest accruing daily. Creditors have seized your effects twice. They'll do it again. A man like you can't own anything, Ranger. You know that better than anyone. That's why you own nothing. That's why you erased yourself. Because a man with debts is a man with handles, and you didn't want to be picked up."

The water lapped against the hull. My hands were steady on the tiller. I'd spent twelve years learning to keep my hands steady while people read my life back to me.

"But Aldous Crane," Dree went on, "is prepared to buy every debt you carry. Every note, every marker, every lien. All of it, retired tomorrow morning. And beyond that—he's prepared to restore your name. Full reinstatement in the rangers, with honors backdated. Your record unsealed. Your service recognized. There are men in the capital who owe Crane favors, and one of them signs ranger commissions." He tucked the papers away. "You could walk back into Cinder Wells as a man who owns things. A man with a name people say out loud. Isn't that what you've wanted? Isn't that why you took the contract in the first place—to get your father's compass back, the one thing with your family's name engraved on it?"

I said nothing. That was the trouble with being invisible for twelve years: everyone assumed you had no words.

"It's the logbook we want," Dree said. "Just the pages. The brass compass you can keep—we know it matters to you, sentimental value, Crane respects that. But the survey logbook, and any charts derived from it. Hand those over, and items one through three are yours within the week."

"The logbook isn't mine to sell," I said.

"Whose is it?"

"It belonged to Elias Yarrow. Surveyor. Dead four years." I watched Dree's face when I said the name. Nothing moved in it, but nothing moved in it *carefully*, the way a man holds still when he's standing on ice he doesn't trust. "Crane knows who my father was."

"Everyone in the fleet knows who Elias Yarrow was," Dree said lightly. "Best hydrographer the coast ever produced. Mapped these flats so well the salvage guilds still use his soundings. Tragic business, his death. Debt, they said. A man crushed under obligations, out in the flats alone." He shrugged. "Sad story."

"That's not how he died," Maren said.

"No," Dree agreed, and smiled again, and this time the smile had edges. "No, I don't expect it was."

The silence after that was long enough to hear the fish moving in the shallows, silver threads flickering where he'd said they'd be.

"You're offering us money to hand you evidence," I said slowly, "of a murder your employer committed. And a pardon for a crime we didn't commit. Do I have that right?"

"I'm offering you a future," Dree corrected. "The past is heavy, Ranger. I've watched it drown better swimmers than you. Crane's offer is simple: put down the past, pick up the future. Most people would call that wisdom."

"And the people who don't take the offer?"

Dree looked out at the flooded plain, unhurried, a man admiring the view. "The fleet will arrive at Vess day after tomorrow," he said. "Eleven ships. Two hundred men. Cranes, dredges, diving bells, blasting powder. Mr. Crane has invested nine years and most of his fortune in the Meridian. He is not a man who tolerates complications at this stage. If the logbook comes to him willingly, everyone lives long and prospers. If it has to be taken—" he spread his brick-red hands— "well. The water's rising, friend. Things get lost in rising water. People, especially. And then the pardons wouldn't matter anyway, because dead men don't need them, and living men can't prove anything about dead ones."

"There it is," Maren said quietly.

"There it is," Dree agreed pleasantly. "I'd rather not. Honestly. I've read both your files, and I think you're interesting people, and interesting people are wasted at the bottom of a floodplain. So consider carefully. You have until the fleet makes Vess." He turned to the wheelhouse, then paused. "One more thing, free of charge. Maren Yarrow."

She stiffened.

"Your file's thinner than your brother's. Ran cargo runs out of Saltmouth for six years. Good reputation among the honest smugglers, which is a small club. No debts, no warrants—until eight days ago, when a brass compass was reported stolen from a broker's strongroom in Cinder Wells, and a woman matching your description was seen leaving through a window." He shook his head, almost fondly. "Eight days ago. Four days before the survey team stopped answering their radio. Three days before the first floodwater crossed the low pan. You moved fast, Miss Yarrow. Almost like you knew the schedule. Almost like someone told you the schedule." His eyes were pale and patient. "Crane wonders about that. Crane wonders who told you the wreck would surface this season, and when. Crane wonders whether you're working for someone else. Another guild, maybe. A ministry faction. Someone who wants the Meridian's cargo for themselves and hired a thief with the right last name to fetch it."

"I wasn't hired by anyone," Maren said.

"Then you stole a compass and ran to the exact coordinates of a wreck that surfaces six tides a year, alone, on spec, hoping to beat a nine-year salvage operation to it." Dree smiled. "Forgive me if Crane finds that a little convenient. He's been burned by coincidences before. Four years ago, mostly."

*Four years ago.* The year my father died. I turned that over and felt something cold slide along underneath it.

"What happened four years ago?" I asked.

Dree looked at me for a long moment, and something almost like pity crossed his blunt face. "Ask your sister," he said. "She was there."

*

We watched the launch recede until it was a gray smudge against the gray water, and then not even that. The engine sound faded last, a mosquito whine swallowed by wind.

Maren sat with her arms wrapped around her knees, staring at the place where the boat had been.

"He knew about the waystation," she said finally. "He knew what we saw. Which means either they're watching us closer than I thought, or—"

"Or the survey team's disappearance was theirs, and the pardon offer was a confession wearing a suit," I said. "They know we know. So they offered to make knowing worth our while."

"Fifty thousand crowns." She laughed, short and bitter. "That's what a murdered survey team costs. That's what my father's murder costs. Fifty thousand and a clean shirt."

I poled us onward, deeper into the flood, saying nothing. The truth was that the number had snagged in me like a hook. Not the fifty thousand—I'd lived poor long enough that money was just weather to me, something that happened to other people. It was the other thing. The debts retired. The record unsealed. The name restored. I had spent twelve years sanding myself down to nothing—no property, no registrations, no fixed address, no name in any ledger that mattered—and I had told myself it was freedom. Sitting in that skiff with Crane's offer still ringing in my ears, I understood for the first time that it might also have been hiding. That invisibility was a wound I'd dressed as armor. That some part of me had taken the tracking contract not for the compass but for the excuse to be *somebody* again—the man who recovered what was lost, the son who reclaimed his inheritance.

And Crane had seen it. Crane, who had never met me, had read me like a chart. He knew exactly what I wanted, because he knew exactly what I lacked, and he'd priced it to the crown: four thousand, two hundred sixty, plus interest.

"Dree said you were there," I said. "Four years ago. When Father died."

Maren didn't answer.

"Maren."

"I was fourteen," she said. "I was there when the letter came. I was there when Mother read it and burned it in the stove without telling me what it said. I was there when she packed us out of the coast house in one night and changed our name." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and furious. "Were you there for any of that? No. You were already gone. You'd been gone three years. So don't ask me what happened four years ago like I've been keeping secrets from you. Everything I know, I learned from scraps. From a burned letter and a mother who wouldn't speak and a logbook page I found sewn into a coat lining after she died." She jabbed a finger toward the fading smudge of the launch. "*He* knows what happened. Crane knows. He's known for four years, and he's been paying people to keep it quiet ever since, and now he's offering to pay *us*."

The wind shifted, bringing the smell of rain from the interior. Far off, the sky over the desert had gone the color of wet slate.

"So we don't take the offer," I said.

"We don't take the offer."

"And when the fleet arrives—"

"Then we're already inside Vess," Maren said, "with the logbook, and the archive, and whatever else Father hid in that city. And Crane can knock on the door like everyone else." She paused. "Ros. The thing he said. About someone telling me the schedule. About another buyer." Her jaw tightened. "There's no other buyer. But someone *did* tell me the wreck would surface early this season. Someone sent me an anonymous letter with tide tables and the broker's name and the compass's location, all wrapped up neat. I thought it was luck. I thought it was Father reaching out from beyond, somehow." She shook her head slowly. "It wasn't luck, was it. Somebody used me to flush you out. And somebody else knew about it before I did."

I looked east, toward where Vess waited under the rising water, its towers holding their breath below the surface. Ahead of us lay a drowned city, a murdered survey team, a father's secret, and a magnate who had just shown us he could price our souls to the penny.

Behind us, faint under the wind, I thought I could hear engines. Eleven ships, crawling across my father's maps.

"Then we move," I said. "Before the tide turns, and before whoever wrote that letter decides we've stopped being useful."

Maren shipped the oars. The skiff swung east, into the flood, into the darkening day—and somewhere behind us, patient as the water itself, the fleet kept coming.
