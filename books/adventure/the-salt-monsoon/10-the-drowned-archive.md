## 10. The Drowned Archive

The tide gave them ninety minutes. Maren counted every one of them as she stripped to her diving shirt in the shadow of the archive tower, because her father had taught her that the sea never lied about time—it only lied about everything else.

"Say it again," Rosalind said. He stood at the water's edge with his boots off and his trousers rolled, looking like a man preparing to be executed rather than rewarded.

"The vault is under the customs house," she said. "Third floor down, according to Father's notes. The door faces east, away from the current. The air pocket inside should still hold—Vess built these vaults for exactly this, so the records would survive a siege or a flood or both." She knotted her hair back. "We go down the stairwell shaft, we breathe from the pocket, we find the strongroom, we come up."

"And if the pocket's gone?"

"Then we learn something about our father's optimism."

Rosalind didn't laugh. She hadn't expected him to. Three days of salt flats and one near-drowning in the Needle Rocks had burned most of the words out of him, and what remained was a kind of watchful silence she recognized from the waystation—the same economy of motion, the same habit of reading ground before trusting it. He was their father's son too. That was the part that kept catching in her throat.

She checked the lead belt, the knife at her calf, the oilskin packet strapped flat against her ribs where the logbook page rode like a second heartbeat. The coordinates were inked there in Elias Yarrow's cramped hand: *customs house, sub-level three, east face.* Beneath them, a single line she had read forty times and still couldn't finish without her chest tightening:

*If Crane reaches this before my children do, I have failed twice.*

Children. Plural. As if he'd known about both of them all along—as if he'd sat in some lamp-lit room writing their names into the same sentence while they grew up a hundred miles apart, strangers sharing a surname like an old wound.

"Water's warm," Rosalind said. "Monsoon's turned it. Won't last past tomorrow."

"Then we dive today." She waded in. The flooded street swallowed her to the waist, then the chest, and Vess rose around her—or rather, sank beneath her—and she was swimming down a boulevard that had once carried carriages and merchants and children running races, all of it now a green cathedral of drowned stone. Light came down in slanted columns through gaps in the rooftops. Fish moved through a fountain plaza like birds through a courtyard. It was beautiful the way ruins are beautiful: honestly, and at a cost.

Rosalind swam beside her, less graceful but tireless, a man who moved through water the way he moved through everything—without complaint, without waste. He'd nearly died two days ago holding that compass case above the flood in the Needle Rocks, and he hadn't said a word about it since. She'd watched him cough half the flats out of his lungs and then stand up and start walking again as if drowning were a chore he'd crossed off a list.

*You could have let it go,* she'd wanted to say. *It's brass and glass. It's not him.*

But she knew better than anyone that it wasn't the compass at all. It was what the compass meant—that Elias Yarrow had chosen, at the end, to leave something to the son who hated him and nothing to the daughter who didn't know she existed until six months ago. Maren had stolen it precisely because of that arithmetic. Steal the inheritance, and the invisible brother comes looking. She'd told herself it was strategy. Some nights, on the flats, she admitted it was jealousy wearing strategy's coat.

The customs house loomed out of the murk ahead, its east face furred with weed, its windows dark sockets. Maren found the stairwell shaft by feel—a vertical slot in the stone, handrails still bolted to the walls, descending into blue-black. She paused at the lip, filled her lungs, and went down.

The pressure pressed on her ears like thumbs. Her fingers walked the rail rung by rung. At twenty feet the light died behind her; at thirty her palm found the vault door, and she felt along it for the recessed handle her father's sketch had shown. Iron, crusted, immovable. She braced her feet and hauled, and the hinges screamed bubbles, and the door swung inward into—

Air.

She broke the surface gasping, and the sound came back at her from four directions: a low stone chamber, maybe thirty feet square, ceiling a handspan above the waterline, a trapped lens of atmosphere shimmering overhead. An air pocket, just as the old engineers had promised. Just as her father had promised. Somewhere in the dark, water dripped with metronome patience.

A hand broke the surface beside her. Rosalind surfaced, spat, and hung onto the doorframe, breathing hard, eyes straining against the black.

"Lamp," she managed.

He pulled the waterproofed lantern from his chest harness, cracked the shutter, and lit it. Yellow light climbed the walls and stopped her heart.

Shelves. Hundreds of them, rising out of the black water in ranks, each slot packed with lead-sealed cylinders and waxed document cases, the whole archive of a city's legal memory standing in ordered rows like a regiment at attention. Vess had been a port of contracts, her father had written—every cargo, every claim, every deed filed here against the day the sea came back. And the sea had come back, and the files had waited.

"It held," Rosalind said quietly. Something almost like awe cracked his voice. "Two hundred years underwater and it *held*."

"Don't admire it yet." Maren hauled herself onto the lowest shelf tier, water streaming off her, and consulted the logbook page by lamplight. "Sub-level three is this level. Strongroom should be—" she turned, counting bays, "—there. Northwest corner. Iron door."

They waded, chests deep, between the shelf rows. The water was cold down here, fed by some deeper channel, and it carried sounds she didn't like: groans of settling stone, distant rushes, the building flexing its old joints against the tide. Twice Rosalind stopped and listened, head cocked, and twice she saw him decide the sound was nothing. She made the same decision. Neither of them believed it.

The strongroom door was iron, waist-high above the water, set into a frame of black granite. No lock—only a mechanism, a wheel of spokes crusted white with mineral bloom. Her father's note had anticipated even this: *the wheel turns counter to expectation. Left is right. He always did love his little jokes.*

Maren gripped the wheel and turned it right. Nothing. Left. The mechanism shuddered, ground, and gave with a boom that rolled through the chamber like thunder in a bottle.

Beyond lay a room no bigger than a cell, dry-walled, its floor above the waterline, its shelves holding perhaps two dozen cases. But one case sat alone on a pedestal at the center, under a glass dome gone cloudy with age, and beneath the dome lay a folio bound in grey leather, and stamped into the leather in gold that had not tarnished was the seal of the Meridian.

Maren's hands shook so badly she could barely lift the dome.

"Careful," Rosalind said. "Paper that old—"

"I know how paper works." She lifted the folio free. It was lighter than it had any right to be, dry as a sermon, preserved in whatever alchemy of wax and lead the vault engineers had devised. She set it on the pedestal, and they bent over it together, shoulder to shoulder, siblings for the first time over the same table, and opened it.

The first pages were survey documents. Her father's hand, unmistakable—the same cramped precision, the same marginal annotations in pencil. Bathymetric charts of the approaches to Vess. Sounding lines. A plot of the wreck itself, the Meridian lying on her beam ends in the silt off the harbor bar, hull breached but intact, cargo holds mapped bay by bay.

"Hold three," Rosalind read aloud. "That's where everyone thought the gold was."

"That's where Crane thinks the gold is." Maren turned the page. "Keep reading."

Hold three, according to the manifest reproduced in her father's careful copy, contained iron ingots. Ballast. Two hundred tons of dressed stone. Someone aboard the Meridian had wanted the world to believe the ship rode heavy with treasure, and someone ashore had spent years confirming the lie.

"So there's no gold," Rosalind said slowly. "There was never any gold."

"There was never any gold." Maren's voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if the chamber had taken it. "Turn to the charter."

He did. And she watched her brother's face as he read it, and she understood that whatever came next—for both of them, for all of them—began in the next sixty seconds of his expression.

The document was a royal charter, issued the year the Meridian sailed, granting exclusive mineral rights to the seabed and salt flats surrounding Vess—not to a crown, not to a company, but to *whosoever shall present this instrument*, in perpetuity, transferable by possession alone. Whoever held the papers owned the rights. Not the land. Not the ships. The papers. A fortune folded into a folio small enough to burn.

And beneath the charter, in her father's hand, a final annotation, dated eleven days before his death:

*Crane has bought the admiralty's silence and half the survey office. When the fleet finds the wreck, he will manufacture a discovery—produce a charter of his own forging, legitimate on its face, and take the flats with a signature instead of a war. This is the true instrument. I have hidden it where only the tide can guard it, because tides cannot be bribed.*

*I did not hide it to keep it safe. I hid it so that no one would ever hold it. Read this, and you will understand: whoever carries this paper becomes the thing Crane already is. There is no honest way to own it. Burn it unread if you must. I would rather my children be poor and clean than rich and complicit.*

*Burn it unread.*

The chamber dripped. The lantern flame leaned and steadied. Maren became aware, distantly, that her breath had gone shallow, that Rosalind had gone very still beside her, that the folio lay open between them like a confession neither of them had asked to hear.

"He knew," she said finally. "He knew what it was. He knew what it would do."

"He hid it from us." Rosalind's voice was flat. "From everyone. He wrote *burn it* and sealed it in a vault, and then he died for it anyway."

"Died for it?" Maren looked up sharply. "What do you mean, died for—"

"You don't know?" For a moment something raw moved behind his eyes—surprise, then pity, then a hard closing over like water over a well. "You stole his compass. You've read his pages. And you don't know how he died."

"I know he drowned. Off the survey cutter. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said debt." Rosalind straightened, and the lantern threw his shadow huge across the charter. "The creditors said it. The town said it. I built my whole life on it—on being the son of the man who drowned rather than pay what he owed. And now I'm standing in a room he sealed with his own instructions, reading his handwriting saying *burn this*, and I am starting to think everyone who ever told me who my father was was paid to say it."

The words landed in Maren like stones dropped down a shaft. She thought of her mother—of the stories she'd been given, thin and secondhand, of a brilliant surveyor lost to the sea and his debts. She thought of Crane's lieutenant on the water two days ago, offering pardons and money, knowing her name, knowing Rosalind's debts, knowing far too much about two people who were supposed to be ghosts. Knowing, she realized with a cold turning in her stomach, exactly what this folio was worth and exactly who else might be willing to carry it.

Because that was the thing about inheritance. She'd spent six months telling herself she'd stolen the compass to draw her brother out, to reunite the family, to honor the dead man's blood. But there had been another arithmetic running underneath, quiet and shameful: *find the treasure, restore the name, be a Yarrow who mattered.* And now the dead man himself had reached up out of the water and put his hand around that ambition and squeezed.

*Whoever carries this paper becomes the thing Crane already is.*

She looked at the charter. Grey leather, gold seal, the power to own a sea and all its salt. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"We should burn it here," she heard herself say.

Rosalind turned to look at her. In the lantern light his face was unreadable, ranger-smooth, and she suddenly hated how well he wore that mask—hated that it was their father's mask too, the one Elias Yarrow had worn while signing orders and hiding charters and letting his children grow up believing lies.

"Should we?" he said.

"Yes. He told us to. It's the only instruction he ever gave either of us that doesn't stink of other people's money."

"It's also the only thing standing between Crane and the flats." Rosalind picked up the folio, weighed it in his palm the way he'd weigh a stone before skipping it. "Burn it now, and he fabricates his own. His forgery becomes the truth. We'll have destroyed the real one and left the lie standing."

"Then we keep it and become the lie's bodyguard?"

"Then we keep it," Rosalind said, "and decide later. On our own terms. Not his." He tapped the folio against the pedestal. "*His* terms got a survey team disappeared and got him drowned. I'm done taking instructions from dead men, even good ones."

Something in Maren flared—anger, or grief, or the two braided so tight she couldn't split them. "You'd carry it? After what it says? After what it *does* to whoever holds it?"

"I've carried worse." He tucked the folio inside his shirt, against his skin, where the compass used to ride before he'd given it to her. "I've been nobody for nine years, Maren. Nobody owns nothing. Maybe that's the only clean hands there are. But I'm not deciding the rest of this in a flooding vault because a dead man got scared."

"He wasn't scared. He was *right*—"

The building answered before she could. A groan ran through the walls, deep and structural, and the water in the aisle shivered into rings. Then came the sound they'd both been dreading since they entered: a long, rushing exhale from somewhere below, like the chamber drawing breath.

The air pocket was failing. The waterline crept up the shelf tiers, slow but unmistakable, a finger's width in the time it took to notice it.

"Tide's turning," Rosalind said. "Early. Move."

They plunged into the aisle. Maren swam for the stairwell shaft with the logbook page crushed in her fist, and the lantern light swung wild shadows across two hundred years of drowned law sinking around them, case after case going under as the black water rose. Behind her, Rosalind's strokes were long and hard, and she knew without looking that he had one arm curled protectively over his chest, over the folio, over the fortune and the curse and the last true words of Elias Yarrow.

Her father had written *burn it unread*. They had read it. And now the paper that could buy a kingdom—or damn everyone who touched it—was riding out of the vault against her brother's heartbeat, into a sea full of Crane's ships.

At the shaft, Maren paused with her hands on the rail, lungs burning, and looked back one last time at the archive going under.

The water had reached the pedestal. The empty glass dome caught the dying lantern light and held it, a small bright circle in the dark, like a lens focusing on something none of them could see yet.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of green water, the first gun of the salvage fleet fired across the flats.
