## 11. Betrayal at Low Water

The archive vault gave up its dead slowly.

We'd spent the better part of the low-water window hauling the sealed cases up through the flooded stairwell, one at a time, Maren diving and Rosalind Yarrow hauling on a rope that bit through his gloves. The water in the vault was black and cold as a well, and every time he went under it felt like being swallowed by something patient. By the time the last case broke the surface, his hands were shaking—not from cold, though the cold was there too—and Maren was sitting on the marble steps of what had once been a counting house, wringing out her braid, laughing.

Actually laughing. Water streaming off her chin, eyes bright with salt and triumph.

"You should see your face," she said. "You look like a man who's been baptized against his will."

Rosalind set the case down on the step between them. It was lead-lined, government-stamped, and heavier than any box of gold had a right to be. He worked the corroded latches with his knife. His father's initials were scratched into the lid—*E.Y.*—in the same cramped hand that had signed the ledger pages they'd found in the bell tower, the same hand that had never once written to his son.

The charter inside was wrapped in oilcloth, three layers deep. Rosalind unwrapped it like a man defusing something.

It wasn't gold. He'd known that since the logbook page, since Maren first showed him the coordinates for the *Meridian*, but knowing and seeing were different animals. The document was vellum, still supple after decades underwater, sealed with the crown's signet and countersigned by four ministers whose names meant land grants and mineral rights and the kind of authority that turned deserts into company towns. The wreck of the *Meridian* hadn't carried treasure. It carried permission—the right to strip Vess and the flats around it down to the bedrock, granted to whoever held these papers.

Their father hadn't hidden a fortune. He'd hidden a weapon.

"Crane's been searching for this for thirty years," Maren said quietly. The laughter was gone from her voice now. "That's why the survey team disappeared. That's why Father died. It was never about salvage."

Rosalind read the signatures twice. Somewhere behind his ribs, the old arithmetic started turning: a man with no name, no property, no debts unpaid—no, one debt, always one debt—and here in his hands was the single object that could erase all of it. Legitimacy. A surname that meant something again. *Yarrow*, restored, on documents that would outlive him.

He hated how long he let himself look at it.

"We can't sell this," he said finally. "You understand that. Crane gets this, he owns everything. The city, the flats, the water rights. Every settlement from here to Cinder Wells becomes his tenancy."

"I know what it is." Maren pulled the oilcloth from his hands and rewrapped the charter with quick, practiced folds—too practiced, some distant part of him noted, the way you wrap something you've already imagined wrapping many times. "I've known longer than you have."

The tide had begun its turn. They could hear it below them, a soft knocking in the drowned stairwell, the sea asking to be let back into its rooms.

"Then we sink it," Rosalind said. "Tonight, before the fleet anchors. Crane's advance boats are already in the shallows—we saw their lights. Once the water covers the vault again, no one finds this for another year."

Maren was quiet for a moment. In the grey light coming through the broken roof, she looked suddenly very much like their father—the same narrow jaw, the same way of going still when she was deciding something. Rosalind had spent two weeks telling himself the resemblance meant nothing. Now it sat between them on the wet marble like a third person.

"There's something you should hear before you decide I'm the villain of this," she said. "And I'd rather you heard it from me than figured it out."

Rosalind said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. It was the one inheritance he'd kept clean.

"I didn't steal the compass because I needed it," Maren said. "I stole it because I needed *you*. You'd erased yourself so thoroughly that even the debt collectors couldn't find you, Ros. I tried letters. I tried intermediaries. You're a ghost—I know because I learned the trick from watching you do it. But a ghost will track anything that smells of blood, and that compass is the only thing on earth that does." She looked at him steadily. "So I made you a trail. And you came. Just like I knew you would."

"You used me."

"I *summoned* you. There's a difference, and the difference is that I needed my brother." Her voice cracked on the word, just slightly, and then steadied. "But that's not the confession. Here's the confession."

She put her hand flat on the oilcloth bundle.

"When I lifted the compass, I already had an agreement drafted with Aldous Crane. Not signed—drafted. I was going to bring him the charter in exchange for enough money to buy back the family name outright. The debts, the seized property, the record of Father's failure—all of it, expunged. A clean page. *Yarrow* restored, the way it was before Crane got his hooks into him." She swallowed. "That was the plan before I ever met you. Before I knew what you looked like. Before I knew you'd rather drown than take a coin you hadn't earned."

The knocking in the stairwell grew louder. The sea, climbing.

Rosalind stood very still. He was aware, distantly, of all the small calculations he'd been making over the past two weeks quietly rearranging themselves into a different shape. The way she'd steered them toward the fleet's route and called it a shortcut. The way she'd wanted to keep the charter dry, portable, *negotiable*. The way she'd flinched, only slightly, when he'd refused Crane's lieutenant on the open water.

"You let me believe we were running *from* him," Rosalind said.

"We were running toward the same place. I told myself the direction didn't matter if we got there first." She laughed, but there was no humor in it. "God, listen to me. I sound like him. I sound exactly like him."

"Like who?"

"Father. Who do you think taught me that a name is worth any price? You got his silences, Ros. I got his sermons. Every night at supper: *we are Yarrows, we are Yarrows, whatever it costs.*" Her eyes were wet now, and furious about it. "He died chasing that name back. He died owing Crane money, and I swore I'd be the one to pay it. Not you. Me. Because you checked out, and somebody had to stay and carry it."

"I checked out?" Something hot climbed Rosalind's throat. "I was *nine years old*, Maren. He sent me away with a debt collector and never wrote. Never once. You got the sermons? I got the silence. I got twenty years of not knowing if I had a father or a creditor."

"And I got twenty years of watching him drink himself blind over the son who vanished!" She was on her feet now, water dripping off her, pointing at him. "You think you suffered alone? He built a shrine to your absence. Every survey he ever ran, every map he ever drew, he did it half-hoping you'd see it and come home. That's why he hid the charter instead of selling it—he couldn't bear to profit from anything Crane touched, because Crane was the reason you left, and—"

"That's not true."

"It's in his own hand! The letter in the logbook—"

"He died running from debt," Rosalind said flatly. "Everyone knows it. The whole coast knows it. That's the story, and stories are the only inheritance our family has ever managed to keep."

Maren stared at him. And then, horribly, she smiled—a small, exhausted, heartbroken smile.

"You really don't know," she said softly. "You still don't know how he died."

The tide reached the top of the stairwell behind them and spilled across the marble floor, black and gleaming, touching the toe of Rosalind's boot like a hand.

"What are you talking about?"

Maren shook her head. "Not here. Not now. Because if I tell you, you'll want to burn the charter for the wrong reasons—to spite him instead of saving anyone. And I need you to decide this clearly, Ros. Decide it as the man who tracked me across forty miles of salt pan on nothing but stride-length and water discipline. Because I have watched you for two weeks now, and I know what you are. You're the most honest man I've ever met, and it's the cruelest thing about you."

"The plan," Rosalind said. "Your agreement with Crane. Is it still standing?"

She hesitated. The hesitation lasted one second too long, and they both knew it.

"If we don't sell it," she said carefully, "the name dies with us. Everything he was, everything he tried to be—it just... ends. Two orphans and a sunken paper. Is that what you want? To be nobody forever?"

Rosalind looked down at the charter in its oilcloth. He thought about the junior surveyor dying in the waystation dust, whispering *sixth tide* and *Crane* with his last breath. He thought about the burned government seals in the bell tower, the camp signs of men who'd been made to disappear because they'd seen too much. He thought about a brass compass, and a will he'd never been allowed to read, and a father who had apparently died twice—once in fact, and once in story.

"Yes," he said.

Maren closed her eyes. "Ros—"

"You asked what I want. I want Crane to never hold this. I want the surveyors' families to get answers. I want Vess to belong to the people who live on it when the water goes down." He picked up the bundle. It weighed nothing and everything. "The name was never real, Maren. It was a story he told himself while he sold maps to the man who killed him. I'm done inheriting stories."

"So you'll destroy it. Our father's last work. The only proof of what he actually—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Fine. Fine. Then do it alone."

"What?"

"You heard me." She stepped back from him, arms crossed, and the distance she put between them was measured and deliberate, the distance of a woman drawing a line on a map. "Because if you sink that charter, you're not sinking Crane—you're sinking the last chance either of us will ever have to stand in this world under our own name. And I won't help you do it. I didn't cross the salt pan, dive a drowned vault, and dig my brother out of his own grave of silence just to watch him throw away the only thing that was ever ours."

The water was at their ankles now. Through the broken roof, far out across the flooding flats, Rosalind could see the lights of Crane's advance boats, strung out in the dusk like lantern-fish. Two days early. Maybe less.

"Maren," he said. "They're coming. Whatever we decide, we decide it tonight."

"Then go decide it by yourself." Her voice was steady, which was how he knew it was costing her everything. "You're good at alone, Ros. It's the one thing you never needed tracking to find."

He stood there a moment longer, holding his father's last secret, looking at his father's other child. Two weeks ago he'd have sworn he felt nothing but duty toward her. Now the word *sister* sat in his chest like a splinter too deep to draw.

He turned and walked out into the rising tide.

The water took him shin-deep, then knee-deep, cold as judgment. Behind him he heard Maren say his name once—not calling him back, just saying it, the way you speak a word you're afraid you'll lose. He didn't turn around. Turning around was a language he'd forgotten how to read, and besides, the tide was rising fast now, and somewhere out in the dark the fleet was anchoring, and somewhere in his pocket the charter rode against his hip like a second heartbeat.

He waded until the towers of Vess rose black around him, ribs of a drowned animal, and found a ledge above the floodline where he could sit and watch the water climb. He told himself he was thinking. He told himself the burning could wait for dawn, for calm, for certainty.

What he was actually doing was waiting for her to follow him.

She didn't.

An hour past dark, he saw a light move along the tower street below—small, careful, moving *away* from him, toward the harbor side where the fleet's boats would be sending parties ashore. He watched it go with his heart going slow and heavy as a pump in a flooded hold.

Then the light stopped. And split. And became three lights, moving fast, spreading out through the drowned streets in a pattern Rosalind recognized from a lifetime of reading tracks in empty places.

A search pattern. But not Maren's. Too wide, too disciplined, too many.

Boat crews. Crane's men, ashore, working the towers street by street—and between him and the only dry route off the ledge, closing like a hand.

Rosalind pressed the charter deeper into his jacket, looked at the black water rising over the streets below, and understood that the choice he'd walked out here to make alone had just acquired witnesses.

* * *

The first light swung up the tower wall and caught him full in the face.

"Hold there!" a voice called—flat, official, the voice of a man reading from instructions. "In the name of the salvage commission, remain where you are!"

Rosalind remained exactly where he was, which was a decision, and while he made it he counted: three lights, six shadows beneath them, two peeling off to flank the tower's base. Trained. Paid. Patient. Crane didn't hire the kind of men who rushed.

"In the name of the salvage commission," he repeated under his breath, tasting it. Thirty years ago a man with that same voice had probably stood over his father's body and written *misadventure* in a report.

He could go up. The tower stairs climbed into darkness, and darkness was a country where Rosalind Yarrow had held citizenship his whole life. But the charter was a weight against his ribs, and the tide was coming in around the tower's base like a slow noose, and somewhere below, Maren was out there alone with her half-signed agreement and her sermon about names.

Or she wasn't alone anymore. That was the thought that moved him. That was the splinter working deeper.

The lights converged. Boots sounded on the flooded flagstones below, more of them than he'd counted, and a second voice joined the first—lower, unhurried, giving orders in the tone of a man who had never once been contradicted. Rosalind knew that tone. He'd heard it through a bulkhead two nights ago, aboard the advance boat, from the lieutenant who'd offered him pardons and legitimacy and a number large enough to buy a small town.

The lieutenant had known about his debts. The lieutenant had known about Maren.

Which meant Crane had never needed Maren's agreement at all. Which meant the agreement was bait, and Maren—who had baited a hook with a compass to catch a brother—was only now learning what it felt like from the other end.

"Oh, you fool," Rosalind breathed, to her, to himself, to both of them. "You absolute pair of fools."

He went up the stairs.

Behind and below, the voices reached the ledge where he'd sat, found it warm with his body, and paused. Then the low voice spoke again, closer now, carrying up the tower shaft with terrible clarity:

"Mr. Yarrow. We know the charter is on you. We also know your sister is currently walking toward our boats, quite convinced she's walking toward a negotiation." A pause, courteous as a knife laid on a table. "Come down, and she negotiates. Stay up there, and I'm afraid the commission's methods become less conversational."

Rosalind stood in the dark at the top of the tower, the sea roaring in below him, the charter burning against his ribs like a coal, and made the only calculation that had ever come naturally to him.

He started climbing.
