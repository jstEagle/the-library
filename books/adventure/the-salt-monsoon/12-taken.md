## 12. Taken

They came for her at the edge of the third tide, when the water over the flats was the color of pewter and shallow enough to show every footprint she'd ever made.

Maren had been wading the drowned causeway alone, the charter case strapped to her chest under an oilskin, testing the route Rosalind would need. That was what she told herself, anyway—that she was scouting for him. The truth was uglier and simpler: she'd wanted one hour without his eyes on her. One hour to decide whether she could actually do it. Whether she could hand Aldous Crane the papers that would buy back the Yarrow name, and live with the look that would come onto her brother's face when he found out.

She never got the hour.

The boat came out of the mist without oars touching water—a long, low hull with a muffled engine, running silent the way salvage men run when they don't want to be heard until they're already on top of you. By the time she heard it, it was too late to run; the causeway dropped off into black water on either side, and swimming in a flooded oilskin was drowning with extra steps.

Four men. No uniforms, but they moved like a crew—no wasted motion, no talk except hand signals. The first one off the bow didn't point a weapon at her. He just held out a canvas sack, open, patient, like a man collecting a debt.

"Ms. Yarrow," he said. "Mr. Crane would like a word."

*Ms. Yarrow.* Not *the woman with the compass*. Not *the thief*. They knew her name. Which meant Crane had known about her before she'd ever lifted the compass out of that pawnshop in Cinder Wells—before she'd built her whole careful plan around drawing Rosalind out of hiding.

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the water.

"I don't know who you think I am," she said, and even as she said it she heard how thin it sounded, standing hip-deep in a flooded city holding a sealed survey case against her ribs like a child holds a doll.

The man smiled without warmth. "We know exactly who you are. We've known since Salt sold us your description in Cinder Wells." He tilted his head toward the case. "You can hand it over or we can take it. Mr. Crane prefers the first way. He says it sets a better tone."

Salt. She should have known. A buyer named Salt who paid too much for a brass compass and asked no questions—who'd been a hook, not a customer, from the very beginning. Every step of her plan, every clever move she'd made to pull Rosalind into the light, had happened inside a room Crane had already furnished.

She thought about running anyway. About the black water and the weight of the oilskin and the four pairs of hands. She thought about Rosalind, somewhere behind her on the flats, tracking her trail the way their father had taught them both to track, and how long it would take him to find this spot, and what he'd find if it came to that.

She unbuckled the case and held it out.

"At least tell me this," she said. "Did you follow me, or did you let me lead you?"

The man took the case and weighed it in one hand, satisfied. "Does it matter? You walked where you were pointed, Ms. Yarrow. Most people do. They just like to believe otherwise."

They put her in the bow of the boat, between two men who smelled of bilgewater and pipe smoke, and ran her out across the flooding pan toward the fleet.

* * *

The flagship was called the *Prosperity's Daughter*, which told Maren everything she needed to know about Aldous Crane before she ever saw him.

She'd expected something brutal—a gunboat, a converted whaler, iron and menace. Instead she got opulence gone slightly to rot: gilt scrollwork flaking at the rails, a figurehead of a woman whose face had been patched with mismatched planking, lanterns burning behind salt-hazed glass. A ship built to impress, maintained by a man who'd stopped caring whether the impression landed because he'd stopped needing to impress anyone.

They brought her up through the waist of the ship, past crates stenciled with government marks that had been painted over—badly, hurriedly—and into a cabin that had clearly been a captain's great room once. Charts on the walls. A table of dark wood scarred by decades of use. And at the far end of it, a small man doing nothing at all.

That was the thing that unsettled her most, later. He wasn't reading, wasn't writing, wasn't studying charts. He was just sitting, hands folded, watching the door she came through, as if he'd been sitting there for hours specifically waiting for the pleasure of this moment.

Aldous Crane was smaller than his reputation. Grey, neat, with the soft hands of a man who hadn't hauled a line in thirty years and the eyes of a man who'd watched other people drown for profit and kept itemized records of it. He wore a plain coat. No rings, no chain of office, none of the magnate's costume. Just the eyes.

"Sit down, please," he said. "Would you like tea? It's real tea. You have no idea what I pay for real tea out here."

"I'd like my property back."

"Your property." He poured two cups anyway, unhurried. "Interesting word choice. Let's see. The charter you carry was drawn up by a government surveyor named Elias Yarrow, who was contracted—by me, incidentally—to locate and document a wreck called the *Meridian*. The documents in that case are therefore the property of the Crown, the salvage consortium, and me, in roughly that order. What they are not is yours. Unless—" he looked up, mild as milk, "—you're claiming your father's estate extends past his debts? Because I hold paper on those debts too, Ms. Yarrow. I hold paper on nearly everything out here."

She sat down. Not because he'd asked—because her legs decided the argument for her.

"You knew my father."

"I employed your father." He pushed a cup toward her. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole story. Drink. You're grey around the mouth and I need you thinking clearly, because I'm going to say some things you won't enjoy, and I'd rather you remember them accurately than through a haze of resentment."

She didn't touch the cup. He drank his own, and continued as though she had.

"Elias Yarrow was the best hydrographer I ever hired. Do you know what that means, out here? It means he could read water the way your brother reads tracks. Give him a tide chart and a week and he'd tell you things about a stretch of coast that the coast itself didn't know. I paid him more than any surveyor in the fleet earned. I did it gladly." He set the cup down. "And then he found the *Meridian*, and read the manifest, and understood what he'd actually located—and he lied to me. To my face, in this cabin, with those honest surveyor's eyes. Told me the wreck was ballast and timber. Told me the coordinates in his report were final. And then he hid the real survey somewhere only he could find it, and resigned his commission, and went home to Cinder Wells to die poor."

Maren's hands had gone cold on the table's edge. "He didn't die poor. He died—"

"He died falling from the cliff road above Sallow Point on a night with no wind and no rain," Crane said, "after meeting with two of my men. Yes. I know how it sounds. I've had eleven years to consider how it sounds."

"Say it plainly."

"No." He said it without heat, almost gently. "I'll say it precisely, which is better. I sent men to retrieve the hidden survey after he resigned. They were meant to search his effects while he was away from his lodgings. They were *not* meant to be seen. Your father surprised them on the cliff road. There was a struggle. He fell." Crane folded his hands again. "Whether he fell or was helped is a question I have never permitted myself to answer, because the answer changes nothing. The survey stayed hidden. My men panicked and told me a story. And I have spent eleven years paying for one night's incompetence—which is why, when your father's children finally surfaced, I resolved to handle matters differently."

The cabin seemed to tilt. Maren gripped the table.

"The survey team," she said. "At Vess. The ones who vanished."

Something crossed Crane's face—not guilt, exactly. Something older and tireder. Regret's bankrupt cousin.

"Yes," he said. "That was mine too. Entirely mine. No one else's to blame, so you may aim it all at me." He stood, walked to the stern windows, looked out at the grey water. "When the government announced its own survey of Vess, I knew within a day what they'd find—the same wreck, the same manifest, the same charter. A charter granting mineral rights to these flats and everything under them, valid for whoever holds the original papers. Do you understand what that means, Ms. Yarrow? Not gold. Gold is money, and money can be taxed, stolen, diluted. A *charter* is law. Whoever holds those papers owns the ground under six tides of ocean, legally, forever. Your father understood that. It's why he buried it instead of selling it—even to me. Especially to me."

"And the survey team?"

"They found the archive vault on their fourth day. I know because I had a man among them." He still didn't turn around. "I gave the order that evening. Silenced. Removed. Scattered, I told myself—I actually used that word, *scattered*, as if they'd be put down in comfortable houses in different provinces with new names. My man interpreted the order his own way. The instruments were left running because the operation was rushed. The food was left because panic does that." His reflection in the salt-hazed glass looked, for a moment, very old. "Six people, Ms. Yarrow. I can recite their names. I choose to recite their names, every morning, so that I never mistake myself for a man who does anything else. But I gave the order. Whatever my man added to it, the order was mine."

Maren realized she was shaking. Not with fear—with the specific vertigo of a floor giving way that you'd been standing on for years.

Her bargain. Her beautiful, desperate bargain. Sell the charter to Crane, buy back the name, restore the family, make her father's disappearance mean something survivable. She'd rehearsed the negotiation a hundred times on the trail—her opening price, her walk-away number, the speech about legacy and honor she'd deliver while signing. And the entire time, the man across the table had already owned the board, the pieces, the room, and her.

"You let me take the compass," she said. "Salt was yours. You wanted Rosalind tracked down as much as I wanted him found."

"I wanted *both* of you," Crane said, turning back. "Because your father hid that survey well, and dead men keep their secrets, and I gambled that he'd told someone. Blood tells blood. I merely had to wait for one Yarrow to go looking for the other." He sat back down, picked up his tea, and regarded her over the rim with those flat, patient eyes. "And here you are. Delivered. With the charter strapped to your chest, having walked every step of the path yourself, believing the whole way that you were the cleverest person in the story."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the deck; the hull groaned as the tide turned beneath them.

"Why tell me all this?" she managed. "Why confess? You could have just taken the case and locked me in a hold."

"Because I want something from you that fear can't purchase." He leaned forward. "I want you to *choose* me. Your father chose against me, and it cost him everything, and it cost you a childhood, and it cost your brother his name. Eleven years of ruin, Ms. Yarrow, all of it downstream of one stubborn man's honesty. I'm offering you the chance to end that arithmetic. Sign the transfer. Stand beside me when I present the charter to the Crown. Take your place as legitimate heir to the greatest survey ever made—and I will bury the survey team, bury the compass theft, bury your brother's debts, bury every ugly thing between here and Cinder Wells. You will be Miss Yarrow of a restored house, and no one will ever dig up what it cost."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I keep the charter, keep you comfortably aboard until the sixth tide closes, and proceed without your blessing. You are not, forgive me, load-bearing in this arrangement. You are a courtesy." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man showing you the lock on a door you'd already walked through. "But I'd rather have the daughter who chose. Sentiment is cheap, Ms. Yarrow, and I've learned to buy it wherever it's sold."

She looked at him for a long moment—at the neat coat and the soft hands and the morning litany of six names he recited to stay human—and understood, finally, the shape of the trap. It wasn't the case they'd taken from her on the causeway. It wasn't the hold waiting below. It was this chair, this tea, this offer, tailored precisely to the wound her father had left in her. Crane corrupted what he touched, and he had touched her life before she was born, and every choice she'd made since had been made inside his grip.

But he'd made one error. Small, and fatal, and she held onto it like a knife under her tongue.

He'd told her the truth. All of it. About her father, about the team, about the order. Men who confess believe they've already won—and a man who believes he's won stops watching the loser's hands.

"Give me the night," Maren said quietly. "I'll sign in the morning."

Crane's smile widened, satisfied, and he rang a small bell for the guard.

They took her down. The hold was dry, clean, even warm—a prisoner's cabin, not a cell, with a bunk and a lamp and a bolted door. A courtesy. Through the hull she could hear the sixth tide's water moving against the timbers, patient as everything else on this ship.

She lay in the dark and pressed her palm flat against the planking, feeling the sea work, and began counting hours until low water—because Rosalind was out there on the flats, tracking her, and he'd taught himself to read anything, and she had left him one thing Crane didn't know about.

She'd never given up the logbook page. Only the charter case.

And on the back of that page, in their father's hand, in ink that wouldn't show unless you held it to flame, was the route through the Needle Rocks that Crane's pilots had been avoiding for eleven years.
