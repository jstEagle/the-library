## 13. The Third Tide

The water took him the way debt takes a man—slowly at first, then all at once.

Rosalind came up coughing in a flooded lane between two drowned houses, the taste of salt and old stone in his throat. The tide had caught him in the streets of Vess with the light failing, and he had done the only thing he knew how to do when the world closed in: he had stopped fighting and started reading.

The current here ran contrary to the main flood. That meant an obstruction—a collapsed wall, a sunken cart, something breaking the flow. Obstructions meant structure. Structure meant the city's bones were still legible under the water, if a man was patient enough to read them.

He read them. He let the water carry him along a street that had once been called something, past doorways that gaped like drowned mouths, until his boots found bottom in the lee of a granary whose walls still stood two courses above the surface. He hauled himself onto the stone and lay there, shivering, listening to the flats breathe around him.

Somewhere out on the blackening water, lanterns moved. Crane's fleet, coming on with the patience of things that had never once been refused. He counted seven lights before the dark swallowed the rest.

He had walked out on Maren with nothing but the clothes he stood in and the compass riding heavy against his ribs. He had told himself it was principle. It had felt, in the moment, like freedom—the clean severance, the invisible man going invisible again, owing nothing to anyone.

Now, alone on a wet stone in a dead city, with the tide rising inch by patient inch toward his chest, it felt like what it was: a man drowning because he'd rather drown than need somebody.

His father's compass was soaked but intact. He turned it over in his hands, the brass cold, the needle swinging lazy and drunk in its housing. North, south—it didn't matter. There was no direction here that led anywhere good.

There was a sound behind him. Soft. Deliberate. The sound of someone who wanted to be heard.

Rosalind didn't turn. "If you're one of Crane's," he said, "you're wasting your time. I've got nothing worth taking."

"We know," said a woman's voice, dry as salt. "That's rather the point."

* * *

There were eleven of them, living in the upper rooms of the old customs house where the tide couldn't reach, and they had been watching him for the better part of an hour through a spyglass salvaged from their own abandoned survey kit.

Their leader was a lean, gray-templed man named Ovid Sarn, who had been second-in-command of the government survey team that had vanished into Vess three seasons ago. He poured Rosalind a cup of something hot and bitter from a dented pot over a smokeless fire—smokeless because smoke drew eyes, and eyes were the last thing they could afford—and studied him across the flames with the flat assessment of a man counting another man's teeth.

"Junior surveyor named Pell," Sarn said. "You found him at the waystation. Dying."

"I found him dying. He said 'the sixth tide' and gave me a name. Crane."

Sarn's jaw worked. Around the fire, the others had gone very still. A young woman with a bandaged arm—Rosalind would learn her name was Tessa Quill, instruments—stopped pretending to mend a net and looked at him directly for the first time.

"Pell got out," Sarn said quietly. "We didn't think anyone did. We thought—" He stopped. Started again. "Crane sent men to silence us after we found what the Meridian carried. Not pirates. Not wreckers. Fleet men, in fleet colors, with fleet orders signed by Aldous Crane himself. Eleven of us went into the water that night. Four of us made it back into the city." His mouth twisted. "Then two more left to try the flats. They never came back."

"And you stayed. In the place they tried to kill you."

"Vess is the only thing in the world Crane doesn't control," Sarn said. "Because Vess is the only thing in the world nobody believes exists nine months of the year. Underwater, it's forgotten. Above water, it's ours." He leaned forward. "But you already understand hiding, Mr. Yarrow. You've made a profession of it. Erased yourself from every record in the borderlands. We checked. There's almost nothing left of you on paper. It's rather admirable."

"It's necessary," Rosalind said.

"It's what kept you alive tonight," Sarn said. "Crane's advance boats have your description. Tall, ranger's coat, moves alone. They were told to take you alive if convenient—Crane wants to know what happened to Maren Yarrow and the logbook—but alive is negotiable." He smiled without warmth. "You see why we don't go out at night."

Rosalind drank the bitter tea and said nothing. The fire cracked. Somewhere below, the tide knocked against the customs house foundations like a polite visitor who intended to keep knocking.

"The logbook," Sarn said. "Your father's survey. Do you have it?"

"No."

"Do you know where it is?"

"I know my sister has it. And I know she means to sell it to Crane." He heard his own voice come out flat and hard, and recognized, distantly, that the hardness wasn't at Maren. It was at himself, for how much it had cost to say it.

Tessa Quill snorted softly. "Yarrows," she said. "You people are a tide all your own. Come in, wreck everything, go out again."

"Tessa," Sarn warned.

"She sold us out?" Tessa pressed, ignoring him. "Or she's playing a longer game? Because from where I sit, a woman who steals a compass to bait her brother across a hundred miles of salt doesn't strike me as someone who does anything for free."

Rosalind looked at her. She looked back, unblinking, and there was something in her face—not sympathy exactly, but recognition. The look of someone who had also watched people she trusted walk into the water and not come back.

"I don't know," he said finally. It was the honest answer, and it tasted like the tea: bitter, and probably good for him.

"Well," Sarn said, standing, "while the Yarrows sort out their arithmetic, the rest of us have a problem that won't wait for the sixth tide." He crossed to a table beneath the room's one unbroken window and unrolled a chart—hand-drawn, corrected in pencil over pencil, the work of people who had spent three seasons learning this city the way a blind man learns a room. "Crane's flagship anchors off the customs quay tomorrow night. Third tide. He'll bring the charter aboard—or send for it—and then he'll seal the site and wait out the season. After that, whatever happens in Vess happens behind his hull, and none of us will ever be believed again. Dead men make poor witnesses, and forgotten ones make poorer still."

"So we run," Rosalind said.

"We've run for three seasons," Sarn said. "Running is how Pell died alone at a waystation with a name in his mouth. No, Mr. Yarrow. Tomorrow night, while the third tide floods the streets and every boat in Crane's fleet is busy riding anchor in open water, we go out to *him*." His finger came down on the chart, on the dark shape drawn at the edge of the drowned city, labeled in careful pencil: *Meridian's Ghost — flagship.* "Eleven of us went into the water. Four of us are left. We are going to board that ship and take back every document Crane carries that proves what he did—and we are going to do it swimming."

Silence, except the tide.

"You're four," Rosalind said. "Against a crew of sixty."

"Five," Tessa said.

"Five against sixty," Rosalind corrected.

Sarn looked at him for a long moment. "You owe Crane nothing. You owe us nothing. You walked away from your own blood rather than be used, and I respect that more than you know. So I'll ask plainly, and you can refuse plainly: will you help us?"

Rosalind looked at the chart. At the flooded lanes marked in blue pencil, the causeway, the submerged arcade his father had mapped twenty years ago and that these people had relearned by nearly dying in it. At the flagship, sitting fat and certain on the paper like it owned the ink.

All his life he had subtracted himself. Every record burned, every name shortened, every contract taken under initials and paid in cash. It had seemed like wisdom. Standing here, it seemed like what it had always been: a man so afraid of being owned that he'd made himself unownable, which is another word for alone.

The needle of his father's compass swung in its housing and settled, pointing nowhere useful, pointing home, pointing at a sister on a salvage barge and a father under six feet of salt and a name he'd spent ten years trying to drown.

Invisible men don't get to choose allies, he thought. That's the whole point of being invisible.

He was tired of the whole point.

"Third tide," Rosalind said. "How deep is the arcade?"

* * *

They planned by lamplight until the moon set, and then by memory, and then by touch.

The arcade was the spine of it. An old merchant passage running from the customs house steps to within forty yards of the anchorage, roofed over in stone, flooded to within a hand's breadth of its ceiling at high tide. At slack water—twenty minutes, no more, between the third tide's rise and the fleet's anchor watch settling—they could swim it in darkness, surface inside the shadow of the flagship's hull itself, and come up the anchor chain like the ghosts the ship was named for.

Tessa knew the locks. Sarn knew the watch rotations—he'd served aboard fleet vessels before the survey, before Crane bought the service lock, stock, and barrel. The other two, a silent brothers pair named Halloway, knew explosives, or enough of them: two powder charges from the survey stores, meant originally for breaking through a sealed vault, repurposed now for cutting anchor lines and sealing the flagship's stern doors behind them.

"And when it goes wrong," Rosalind said, "because it will go wrong—"

"Then it goes wrong in the water," Sarn said, "where we've lived for three years, and where their guns can't follow." He rolled up the chart. "One condition, Mr. Yarrow. If we take documents and find your sister aboard—we get her out too. Whatever she's done. Whatever she's planning. Nobody else gets left in Crane's hold to die of Crane's arithmetic. I've buried enough of my people to know how it reads afterward."

Rosalind thought of Maren's face in the firelight, the logbook open between them, her voice steady and wrong: *I'm going to buy our name back.* He thought of walking out into the rising tide, and of how each step had felt less like leaving and more like amputation.

"Yes," he said. "That was always the condition."

Sarn nodded once, and something in the room loosened—the particular loosening of people who have decided to trust a stranger and are bracing for the cost.

Tessa threw him a waterproofed satchel. "Survey seals, spare line, a signal lamp. You swim like a ranger?"

"I swim like a man who grew up in a desert," Rosalind said, "and learned fast."

"Learn faster," she said. "Third tide comes early tomorrow. Monsoon's ahead of schedule—so is everything else."

Outside the broken window, the water lay black and breathing under the stars. Far out, the lanterns of the fleet had multiplied. Eight. Nine. Ten lights, strung across the dark like a necklace being slowly drawn tight around the drowned city's throat.

And among them, somewhere, a barge with his sister on it, holding a document that could buy a kingdom or drown one, waiting for morning and a buyer.

Rosalind checked the compass one last time. The needle swung, wavered, and pointed—not north, not the fleet, but out across the black water toward the anchorage, steady as a promise.

"Third tide," he said to the dark. "We're coming."
