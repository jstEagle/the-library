## 14. Boarding the Meridian's Ghost

The third tide came in the dark before dawn, and we went into the water with it.

I had counted the fleet from the bell tower all evening: nine hulls riding the flooded pan, lanterns strung bow to stern like a carnival that had lost its way. The flagship anchored dead center over the drowned archive quarter, her lights burning above the very streets where Aldous Crane had once sent men to silence a survey team. She was called the *Prosperity's Daughter*, which told you everything about the man who owned her.

"She'll have a watch on every deck," Ilsa Vane said beside me. The old surveyor's hands were steady as she checked the lashings on our raft of salvaged timber and tarred canvas. "But she won't be watching below."

"Nobody watches below," Maren said. "That's the whole point of drowning a city."

There were eleven of us. Six survivors of the government survey team who had spent three weeks hiding in Vess's cisterns, eating salt-cured fish and hating Crane with the patient hatred of people who had buried friends. Four of my own kind—flats rangers Rosalind Yarrow had never officially met, men and women who owed me favors from years when I'd been nothing but a rumor on the salt. And Maren, who had spent two days since her capture and escape saying almost nothing, sharpening a knife she'd taken off a dead sailor, looking at me sideways whenever she thought I wouldn't notice.

We were going to cut nine ships loose from their anchors and let the tide do the rest.

"The causeway first," I said, for the last time. "The Meridian's Ghost runs under the harbor road—old Vess built it so merchants could walk to their warehouses at low water. It's submerged now, but the roofline breaks surface at slack tide for maybe forty yards. We swim the length of it, come up inside the fleet's anchor shadow, and work ship to ship. Cut lines. No noise. By the time they understand what's happening, half the fleet will be drifting."

"And if they catch us," said a young surveyor named Pell, who was nineteen and trying not to shake.

"Then they catch us," I said. "Swim anyway."

* * *

The water of drowned Vess was warm as blood and twice as thick with silt. We went in single file along the causeway roof, hands trailing the ancient stone, heads up just enough to breathe through the gaps where the parapet had crumbled. Above us the fleet's lanterns smeared long yellow reflections across the flood, and every ripple looked like a searchlight.

I swam behind Maren. I had spent eleven days tracking this woman across the salt pan, reading her stride in crust that should have held no prints, learning her habits from the way she rationed water and chose her campsites. Now I learned her swim stroke: economical, silent, a flat sculling pull that made almost no wake. Our father's daughter. Whatever else she was—the liar, the thief, the woman who had planned to sell our family's name back to the man who'd helped bury it—she moved through water like someone who intended to survive it.

Halfway down the causeway, the roof gave out beneath Ilsa.

No warning. One moment the stone was there; the next it was powder, thirty years of monsoon rot finally surrendering, and the old surveyor dropped through into blackness. I heard the muffled *whump* of her body hitting water below, felt Pell grab my shoulder in panic, and then Ilsa surfaced in the gap behind us, treading water, calm as Sunday.

"Branch channel," she whispered up. "Runs under the harbor square. Comes up inside the fleet."

She was right. It did. It also ran directly beneath the *Prosperity's Daughter*'s anchorage, which meant it was either the best route aboard or the fastest way to die, and there was no time to argue about which. Ilsa Vane was sixty-one years old, had watched Crane's men murder four of her colleagues, and had spent those same weeks memorizing every drain and cistern in the drowned quarter because she refused to die ignorant.

"Go," she said. "I'll take the causeway party around the long way. You take the channel."

"You don't know what's down there."

"I know exactly what's down there. I mapped it." A ghost of a smile in the lantern light. "Twenty-two years ago. With your father."

I didn't ask. There wasn't time, and some doors you can't walk through twice. We split—six through the gap, five along the roof—and Ilsa's hand pressed my wrist once, brief and hard, like a signature on a contract neither of us would live to dispute.

Then she was gone into the dark, and I put my face in the water and swam.

* * *

The branch channel came up inside a collapsed customs house, its vaulted ceiling open to the sky, its floor ten feet under the flood. From the shattered window arches we could see the *Prosperity's Daughter*'s stern not sixty yards away, close enough to read the nameboard, close enough to hear the watch change—a clatter of boots, a bored voice complaining about mosquitoes.

The anchor plan worked better than plans have any right to. My flats people were artists: they slipped between hulls like eels, and one by one the fleet's cables began to part under saw and blade. The tide was rising, which meant each severed ship began to swing, slow and drunken, its crew too busy with the drift to notice the next cable going. Within an hour the *Cormorant* was aground on a submerged wall, the *Salt Wife* had drifted bows-on into her own tender, and two more had fouled each other's lines so badly that their officers were screaming across the water at one another in the dark.

It was going beautifully. That should have warned me.

The trouble came at the flagship herself. Her anchor cable was chain, not rope—half an inch of forged iron running down into black water—and no blade in Vess could part it. Which left the capstan on her forecastle, and the capstan meant boarding her openly, and boarding her openly meant the plan stopped being a knife and became a hammer.

"We go up the rudder irons," Maren said. She was dripping beside me in the customs house dark, hair plastered flat, eyes bright with something I recognized from my own mirror. "Stern gallery windows are louvered. I've been on ships like her. We come up through the gallery, take the capstan deck quiet, and let the chain run free."

"There'll be a sentry."

"One sentry." She smiled without warmth. "How many have you killed, Rosalind?"

"None."

"Good. Keep it that way as long as you can. It gets easier after the first, and that's the problem with it."

We crossed the sixty yards underwater, breathing from a shared cache of reed tubes the survivors had cut that afternoon, and came up against the flagship's stern planking in the blind spot beneath the counter. The rudder irons were slick with weed but sound. Maren went first. I followed. Behind us, Pell and two others waited in the water with the raft, ready to haul away anyone who fell.

The stern gallery was exactly as she'd promised: shuttered louvers, a sleeping cabin beyond, a single lamp burning. Maren eased a louver aside with her knife tip, listened, and slid through. I came after her into a cabin that smelled of wax and expensive tobacco—Crane's own quarters, empty, his bed turned down, a decanter of amber liquor sweating on the chart table.

On the table lay papers. I should have kept moving. I looked anyway.

Charts of Vess. Soundings of the archive quarter. And a manifest, written in a clerk's careful hand, listing cargo already recovered from the wreck site: brass fittings, coin, ship's bells—and one line item, circled twice in red ink, that turned my stomach cold.

*One (1) sealed strongbox, contents unknown, recovered from captain's cabin, Meridian. Held flag secure.*

They hadn't found the charter yet. But they'd found the box that held it, and it was somewhere on this ship, and Maren was already at the door listening for the sentry beyond.

"Capstan deck's one flight up," she breathed. "Two men on the chain, one on the capstan bar. We take them fast and quiet, knock the pawl off, and the tide does the rest."

We went up.

* * *

It went wrong in the way things go wrong—not all at once but in pieces, each small failure buying the next.

Maren took the chain sentry clean: a sap blow to the base of the skull, caught before he finished falling. I took the second. But the man on the capstan was awake, and young, and instead of freezing he did the bravest stupid thing available to him—he threw himself on the capstan bar and began to shout.

Maren reached him in three strides. What happened next took perhaps four seconds and I will see it for the rest of my life: her sap coming down, the boy twisting so the blow caught his shoulder instead of his head, his boot heel striking the pawl lever in his fall—

And the chain ran.

Eleven tons of anchor iron paying out through the hawsehole with a shriek like the world ending, the whole forecastle shuddering, every light on every deck of the *Prosperity's Daughter* suddenly alive with shouting men. The flagship swung her head away from the wind and began, majestically, to drift—which was the mission accomplished, technically, in the manner that a house fire accomplishes heating.

Boots on the companionway. Many boots.

"Gallery," I said. "Back the way we came."

"No time—they'll have the stern covered." Maren's eyes flicked across the chaos, reading it the way she read salt pan. "Forward. The hold hatch. If we get below, we're ghosts. Nobody searches a hold during a crisis."

So we ran forward, along a deck full of men running aft, two more ghosts in the crowd of their own alarm. The hold hatch stood open—cargo being shifted, crates of salvage stacked around it—and we dropped through into darkness just as the first officer's voice began organizing search parties above.

Below was the smell I will never forget: bilge, tar, brine, and under it something older, the smell of a ship that had died once already. We felt our way along the frames in absolute black, Maren's hand on my shoulder, deeper and deeper toward the bow, looking for a hiding place that would hold until the search burned itself out.

We found the brig instead.

It was luck, pure dumb luck—we blundered through a bulkhead door and there was the smell of human misery, and when I struck a match cupped in my palm there were faces. Three of them. Two men and a woman, chained to the ring bolts, lips cracked, eyes hollow. Survey team. The ones Crane hadn't bothered killing because he'd decided they were worth more as hostages—or examples.

One of them knew me. Not my face—I'd made sure of that for fifteen years—but my name.

"Yarrow," the woman whispered, staring at my match flame. "The tracker. Ilsa said—if anyone came—it would be Yarrow."

"Where is Ilsa?" I asked, and the question came out wrong, too loud, because some part of me had known since the channel split.

The woman's face answered before her mouth could. "She got the others out. Through the drains. There were guards at the last grate and she—" The survivor swallowed. "She told us to go on. She said she'd mapped this city twice and she wasn't afraid of it. She held the grate shut from the outside while we crawled through. They shot her in the water."

The match burned down to my fingers. I let it.

Cost made real. That was the arithmetic of the night: two ships crippled, one flagship adrift, one charter still hidden in a strongbox somewhere above our heads—and Ilsa Vane floating somewhere in the drowned streets of Vess, the woman who had mapped this city with my father, who had pressed my wrist in the dark and signed herself over to the tide.

Maren touched my arm. "Rosalind. We have to move. They'll sweep the hold next."

"I know."

"They'll find the brig. They'll find these three."

"I know that too."

I looked at the chained survivors, at the bolted hasps, at the heavy iron padlock holding the brig door shut from the outside. And I understood, with the cold clarity that comes when everything else has burned away, exactly where we were standing.

Directly beneath the flag secure. Directly beneath the strongbox. In the one compartment of the *Prosperity's Daughter* that Crane's own crew never entered uninvited.

Above us, boots thundered across the deck, and a voice I was beginning to hate called down the hatch: "Search the hold! He's aboard—I'd stake the whole fleet on it—Yarrow is aboard!"

Maren looked at me in the dark. "Well," she said. "He's not wrong."
