## 15. Father's Last Tide

The brig of the *Salt Meridian* was not a cell so much as a coffin with ambitions.

Maren had been aboard for two tides now, long enough to learn the ship's rhythms through the iron: the groan of the hull as the sea climbed and fell against it, the tramp of boots overhead, the way the lantern in the passage dimmed at the change of watch like something exhaling. She'd been given water twice. Food once. The bread had salt baked into it, which she supposed was Crane's idea of hospitality—eat the ocean or starve.

Her wrists were raw where the manacles bit. She'd stopped trying to work them loose around the second hour. Instead she did what Yarrows apparently did best: she waited, and she watched, and she hated quietly.

The brig held three cells. Hers. One empty, its door hanging open on a bent hinge—the survivors Rosalind had freed must have come through here, she realized, which meant her brother had been *aboard*, had fought his way into this iron belly for her, and she hadn't seen him since the flooding hold took them in different directions. And the third cell—

The third cell held papers.

They'd been left in a locked sea chest, bolted to the deck, and whoever had searched the compartment before stowing her here had gone through it carelessly: the lock hung broken, the lid askew, a corner of oilcloth protruding like a tongue. She'd noticed it her first hour and dismissed it as ballast records, manifests, the boring paper skeleton of a salvage fleet.

Boredom, it turned out, was the only key she needed.

By the third feeding—or what she judged was the third feeding, time down here moving by meals and tide-sounds rather than any honest clock—she had worked herself across the floor far enough to hook the oilcloth with her boot heel and drag the chest within reach of her bound hands. The manacles allowed maybe two feet of chain. Enough.

She opened the chest with her teeth and her chin and a patience that would have made their father proud, or furious; with him it had always been hard to tell the difference.

Charts first. Charts of Vess, beautifully drawn, annotated in a hand she didn't recognize until she found the signature block on one: *A. Crane, Surveyor's License No. 114*. So the magnate had once been a surveyor himself. Before the money, before the fleet, before he'd become the kind of man who owned ships instead of reading them. There were soundings in that hand that matched, line for line, the coordinates on the logbook page she'd shown Rosalind at the fire. Crane hadn't found the Meridian. He'd stolen the finding of it.

Then, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the charts, tied with waxed cord: letters. Private ones. Dozens of them, spanning fifteen years, and Maren read them all because there was nothing else to do but breathe and read, and because somewhere in the back of her skull a voice that sounded unpleasantly like her father's said: *read everything, girl. Paper is where men hide their sins.*

*Paper is also where they confess them.*

The early letters were business. Crane writing to investors about the drowned city, about the wreck, about the fortune in the hold. Then the letters changed. They got shorter. Colder. A different hand began appearing—her father's hand—and Maren's breath went strange in her throat, because she knew that handwriting better than she knew her own face. She'd forged it often enough as a child to forge checks against his accounts, small crimes he'd never once caught, or never once mentioned.

*Yarrow,* Crane wrote, in a letter dated eleven years back, *your survey is complete and your payment is enclosed. You will note the terms of our agreement: the location of the Meridian remains between us. If the government survey team currently operating out of Cinder Wells obtains your charts, our arrangement terminates and your debts to me become payable in full, immediately, by whatever means I deem appropriate.*

And beneath it, folded small, a reply in her father's slanted script:

*Crane — I mapped your wreck because you held my ledger over my head, same as you hold everyone's. But I've seen what's in that hold, and it isn't cargo. It's a charter. Whoever holds those papers owns every mineral claim from the flats to the coast, and you'll use it to own the people too. I didn't spend thirty years making maps so a man like you could make the world fit inside one. Find another surveyor.*

*I'm keeping my copy.*

Maren read it four times.

Then she read the rest, faster now, her heart doing something ugly and enormous against her ribs.

There were more letters. Crane threatening. Her father refusing. Crane hiring other surveyors and receiving back reports that the wreck could not be located—that the coordinates Yarrow had provided were false, that the man had salted the charts, that either the man was incompetent or the sea itself was lying. And then, dated six years ago, a letter that made her close her eyes:

*Yarrow. My divers report sabotage on the winch lines. My lead tender swears he saw a man on the flats the night before, alone, on foot, who moved like he knew the ground. You will call off whatever game you are playing or I will take everything you have left, including the boy.*

*Including the boy.*

Rosalind. Sixteen years old, already half-erased, already learning to walk without leaving prints. Their father had gone out onto the flats that night—she remembered now, remembered the argument she'd overheard through a door, her father saying *I won't let him have either of them* to someone, to Crane himself perhaps, over a wire or a wind-phone—and cut the winch lines on Crane's first salvage attempt. The attempt that failed. The attempt that cost Crane a season and a fortune and, according to the last letter in the chest, cost her father everything.

Because the final letter was not from Crane. It was a copy of an official order, stamped with a seal she recognized from the burned documents in the bell tower at Vess—a government seal, the kind that turned murder into procedure.

*Re: Yarrow, E. — subject obstructing licensed recovery operations. Resolution authorized. Discretion required. No inquiry to follow.*

No inquiry to follow. That was how they wrote it. That was how a man died on the flats with his compass in his pocket and his children believing he'd run from debt—because the debt story was the cover, the tidy lie, the *discretion required*. He hadn't fled anything. He'd walked out onto the salt knowing exactly what was waiting for him, carrying the only copy of the truth in brass and paper, and he'd died so that Aldous Crane would never own the charter.

He'd died a saboteur. A martyr. Whatever word you wanted—he'd died *for* something, and both his children had spent their lives believing he'd died running.

Maren sat back against the cell wall and the chain clinked and she didn't hear it. The lantern guttered. Somewhere above, the tide turned, and the hull spoke its long iron sentence.

She thought about Rosalind. About the way he'd said *he never came for me* at the fire, flat as a chart with nothing marked on it. About the coldness she'd defended her father against with memories of a warm man, because someone had to, because if both children believed the worst then the worst became true. They'd split him between them like scavengers splitting a carcass—she'd taken the warmth, Rosalind had taken the cold, and neither of them had taken the whole animal.

Their father had loved them. Both of them. Badly, clumsily, in the way of a man whose every letter was a threat answered and whose every silence was a shield raised—but he had spent his last years fighting Crane with the only weapons he had: false coordinates, cut cables, a hidden logbook, and finally his own life, laid on the salt like a payment he knew would be collected.

And Rosalind believed he'd abandoned them. Believed it so completely he'd built a whole self out of the belief—the erasure, the invisibility, the refusal of every record and every name. Not because he didn't want the inheritance. Because he thought the inheritance was debt, and shame, and a father who chose the sea over his son.

*Tell him.*

The thought arrived without drama. It simply stood up in her chest and refused to sit down.

*Tell him what it costs. Tell him in front of Crane if you have to. Tell him on the deck with guns pointed at you. It doesn't matter. What matters is he stops carrying a lie that was put on him by the man who killed his father.*

She looked down at the papers in her lap—her wrists aching, the oilcloth stained with rust and bilge water—and understood, with the clarity that comes only in coffins, what she was holding. Not evidence. Inheritance. The real one. Not the charter, which was power and therefore poison. This: the truth of who their father was. The one thing Crane couldn't buy back, couldn't burn, couldn't drown, because it lived now in her memory and would live in Rosalind's the moment she gave it to him.

Crane had taken the charter off her when his men grabbed her on the flats. Let him keep it. Paper burned. But this—

This she would carry out of the brig in her bare hands if she had to.

A boot scraped in the passage. The lantern brightened. Keys, and then the door, and a guard with a face like wet sand looked in at her, at the open chest, at the papers spread across her lap, and his hand moved toward the club on his belt.

"They told me to check on you," he said. "Not to find you reading the captain's mail."

"Then you found something worth reporting," Maren said, and heard her father's steadiness in her own voice, and was not ashamed of it. "Go on, then. Report it."

He hesitated. Down here, with the tide talking through the hull, hesitation was its own kind of confession. "What's in them?" he asked, despite himself. Men always asked. Curiosity was the flaw Crane's whole empire ran on.

"The truth," she said. "About your boss. About a survey team he had killed. About a ranger named Yarrow who's coming aboard with the next tide whether you're ready for him or not."

The guard stared at her. Above them, faint through the decks, came the sound of the fleet changing watch—and beneath it, farther out, almost too soft to catch, the slap of oars in flooded streets. Someone was moving through the drowned city. Someone who moved like he knew the ground.

Maren gathered the letters against her chest, chains and all, and smiled for the first time since the flats.

"Hold the tide," she whispered to the iron, to the water, to the dead man whose name she was finally proud to carry. "I've got something for my brother."

* * *

The guard reported her, of course. That was the trouble with guards—they were hired to be predictable.

Two hours later, by her count of the watch-change, the door opened again and this time it was not wet-sand-face but a lieutenant with clean cuffs and Crane's manners, and he took the letters from her without ceremony, all of them, and locked the chest properly this time, and said, "Mr. Crane wants you to know he's aware of what you read."

"Good," Maren said. "Then he knows I know. Saves us both the theater."

The lieutenant studied her the way men study weather. "He says a woman who reads that much is either dangerous or desperate, and he can price either one."

"He can't afford me." She leaned her head back against the hull. "Neither could my father. Ask him."

Something flickered in the lieutenant's face—there it was again, that flaw, curiosity, the whole rotten economy of secrets running on it—and then the door shut and the bar fell and she was alone with the iron and the tide.

Fine. Let Crane know she knew. Knowledge wasn't a weapon you could confiscate; it wasn't paper, it wasn't brass, it wasn't a charter with a wax seal. It was the one cargo that traveled light and landed hard, and she intended to deliver it personally.

She closed her eyes and listened to the ship. Third tide coming. She could feel it in the way the current dragged at the anchor chains, in the new note the hull sang, higher, tighter, the sea pulling harder toward the city. Somewhere out in those flooded streets Rosalind was swimming or wading or walking on water, because that was what her brother did—he erased himself so thoroughly that even the sea lost track of him until it was too late.

Hold on, she told him silently. Hold on, you stubborn ghost. Our father wasn't what you think. Neither am I. Neither are you.

And when the bar lifted next—if it lifted next—she would walk out of this coffin and find him, and she would say the words no matter who was listening, no matter what it cost, because a lie had ruled this family long enough.

The lantern dimmed. The tide turned.

And far off through the hull, clear as a knock, came the sound of an anchor line parting—one, then another—as if the drowned city itself had decided to set the fleet adrift.
