## 16. Reunion Under Guns

The fourth tide came in gray and hungry, and I swam into it with a knife between my teeth because my hands were busy holding me together.

The flooded causeway had nearly finished me. Twice the current took my feet and dragged me toward the open water where Crane's picket boats patrolled with lanterns swinging, and twice I clawed back to the submerged wall of some merchant's house and clung to the stone like a barnacle until my arms stopped shaking. The survivors had told me the way—old Dessa, who'd been the survey team's cook and remembered every street of Vess from the charts her team had died making. *Follow the bell tower's shadow when the lanterns swing east. The flagship's hold sits over the old customs house. There's a grate. There's always a grate.*

There was a grate.

It took me twenty minutes and most of the skin off my knuckles to work the rusted bars loose, and when I finally pulled myself up into the darkness beyond it, I lay on my back in six inches of bilgewater and breathed like a landed fish. Above me, the hull of the *Charter's Grace*—Crane's flagship, named like a joke—curved away into shadow, ribbed with iron, groaning with the tide. The whole ship shifted and settled as the water rose beneath it, a living thing, and I was inside its belly.

I'd been inside worse bellies. I'd been inside my own for thirty years.

*Move,* I told myself. *Grieve later.*

I wrung out my shirt, checked the knife, and started climbing.

The brig was amidships, two decks up, according to the plan we'd scratched into a beam of the customs house by lantern light. Dessa's plan. The survivors' plan. Mine only in the sense that I'd be the one to walk down the corridor and open the door. The others would cut the anchor lines and take the launch while Crane's crew was busy with the flooding streets and the muttering that had already begun below decks—men who'd signed on for salvage and were starting to understand they'd signed on for something else.

I climbed through the ship's throat in the dark, hand over hand, following the sound of the sea against the hull. Somewhere above me a man laughed. Somewhere below me the water rose. The *Grace* was riding high now at the fourth tide, but the sixth tide was coming, and everyone aboard knew it, and knowing it made the ship feel like a held breath.

The corridor to the brig was lit by a single lantern and guarded by a single man, which told me Crane didn't much fear escape from a woman locked in a steel room in the middle of his fleet. He should have feared more things. He feared the wrong ones.

I came up behind the guard the way my father's compass had taught me to come up behind everything—patiently, from the direction nobody watched. The needle always points somewhere. The trick is being the somewhere.

He went down without a sound. I took his keys and his truncheon and his dignity, in that order, and I opened the brig door.

* * *

Maren was sitting on the bunk with her back to the wall, knees drawn up, and when the door swung open she didn't flinch. She'd been expecting Crane's men, or worse, and she'd decided not to give them the satisfaction. That was the first thing I understood about her in that moment—not her face, though she had our father's jaw exactly, the stubborn hinge of it—but her stillness. She'd been ready to be hurt and had arranged herself around it like water around a stone.

Then she saw me, and the stillness broke.

"You," she said.

"Me."

She stood up slowly, like she wasn't sure the floor would hold, or like she wasn't sure I would. In the lantern light spilling through the doorway she looked wrecked—salt-stiff hair, a bruise blooming along her collarbone where someone had handled her roughly—but her eyes were clear and furious and alive, and I thought, with a strange lurch, *she tracks like me. She holds like me. God help us both.*

"I came to get you out," I said. Which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it.

"You came to get the logbook."

"I came to get you out," I said again. "The logbook can drown for all I care."

Something moved behind her eyes—hope, maybe, or its cautious cousin. "You mean that."

"I've had a week to learn what Yarrow things cost when you keep them."

She laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and she crossed the cell and hit me in the chest with the flat of her fist—not hard, not a blow, more like knocking on a door she'd given up on years ago. I let her do it twice. On the third one I caught her wrist, and she didn't pull away, and we stood there in the brig of our enemy's ship like two people meeting at a funeral, which in a way we were. Our father's. Again. Every time we learned something new about him, we buried him a little deeper.

"I'm sorry," she said. "For the charter. For planning to sell it before I ever met you. For using the compass to reel you in like—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Like you were a fish I'd hooked with the one bait you couldn't ignore."

"You knew I'd track it."

"I knew you'd track *anything*. I'd read the ranger reports—the ones they tried to erase. You were a ghost with a reputation, Rosalind. That's a hard thing to be." Her mouth twisted. "I figured a man who erased himself so thoroughly must have a reason, and the reason was probably standing in the same church at the same funeral, not speaking to anyone."

"You were there."

"Third pew. You were in the back, by the door, like you were measuring the distance to it." She looked down. "I've thought about that a lot. Why you stood by the door."

"Because doors are honest," I said. "They tell you where the way out is."

"And now?"

I looked at her—really looked, the way I'd looked at tracks on the salt pan, reading the whole animal from the smallest sign. The woman who'd stolen from me. The woman who'd lied to me. The woman who'd sold me out to Crane and then, by every account Dessa's people could gather, refused to tell Crane where the survivors of the survey team were hiding, even with his lieutenant's hands on her. A coward would have talked. She hadn't.

"Now," I said, "I'm measuring the distance to you."

She made a sound that was half laugh and half something else, and wiped her face with the back of her hand, quick and rough, like she was angry at the tears for existing.

"Then get me out of here," she said, "and I'll tell you what Crane told me. About Father."

* * *

We moved through the ship's lower passages with the tide talking under the deck plates, and Maren talked low and fast while I navigated by the sound of the water, the way I'd navigated the flats for a decade.

"He knew him," she said. "Not from the salvage records. From before. Crane and our father surveyed together—twenty years ago, the first government chart of the Vess basin. Crane was the money, Father was the map. And when the *Meridian* turned up in the flood silt, Crane wanted the wreck opened immediately, quietly, no government seals, no witnesses. Father refused. Filed the full survey with the provincial archive under seal, hid the working copies, and started making noise about mineral charters and crown jurisdiction." Her voice caught. "Crane didn't kill him for the compass, Rosalind. The compass was a message. *I can take what's yours whenever I want.*"

"The survey team—"

"Was finding the working copies. Crane had them silenced before they could report. Dessa's people got out because they were down in the archive vault when it happened." She grabbed my arm as we passed a junction, holding me still, listening. Boots overhead. Passing. Then gone. "He didn't die running from debt," she said, quieter. "He died standing between Crane and the one thing Crane couldn't buy. I needed you to know that. Whatever else happens tonight, I needed you to know it."

I stood in the dark corridor of a hostile ship with the sea rising under my boots, and I let it land.

All those years I'd carried the other story like a stone in my chest—the father who'd drowned chasing salvage money, who'd left his sons nothing but debt and a brass compass and a lesson about what wanting things costs. I'd built my whole invisible life on that stone. Erased myself from every record because records were how the world had judged him, and I wanted no part of being judged by the same arithmetic.

"He was a better man than I made him," I said.

"He was both men," Maren said. "The cold one and the stubborn one. You got the cold one. I got the stubborn one. That's the whole tragedy of the Yarrows, as far as I can tell—we keep meeting halves of people and calling them whole."

I wanted to argue. I didn't have time, and I didn't have a case. She was right. We were both halves, standing in the dark, and for the first time since the salt pan I was glad of it.

"The launch," I said. "Dessa's people cut the anchors at the fifth tide. We need to be off this deck before Crane knows the water's rising faster than his pumps can—"

The lights went out.

Not all of them. Just the corridor lamps, one by one, in sequence, from the stern forward—a deliberate, unhurried dark, the dark of a man turning down lamps in his own house. And through the hull came a sound I knew from the flats, from flash floods, from every trap I'd ever watched close: the sound of a door shutting that had been open the whole time, and you only heard it at the end.

Maren went rigid beside me. "He knew."

"He knew," I said. My stomach dropped through the deck plates into the rising sea. "He's known since the third tide. Maybe since the beginning."

* * *

The water was at our ankles when we reached the hold, and at our knees when we understood.

They'd sealed the lower hatches. I heard the dogs slam home one after another, iron on iron, a countdown in reverse. The bilge pumps had been shut down—I could tell by the water's behavior, rising smooth and steady instead of gulping and gouting, the tide coming in through the flooded customs-house grate below us with nothing to argue against it but time. Crane hadn't posted guards at the brig because he hadn't needed guards. He'd needed a cell with a tide in it, and he'd let us walk into it on our own feet, because men always guard the door harder than they guard the trap.

A voice came through a speaking tube in the bulkhead, brass-mouthed and unhurried, the voice of a man who had never in his life needed to raise it.

"Master Yarrow. And Yarrow. How convenient. I'd have had to introduce you eventually." A pause, and I could hear him smiling. "Your father was the same. Always two steps ahead, right up until he was one step behind. The hold floods at the sixth tide. You have, oh—three hours to reconsider my offer. The charter for the woman. The logbook for your debts. I'm told you're a man who understands what things cost."

The tube went dead.

Maren stood in the black water with her fists clenched, and for a moment I saw her ready to scream at the brass mouth, to give Crane the satisfaction of her fury. Then she looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face—calm, maybe, or the flat attention of a man reading water—she let the scream go and did something better.

She started counting.

"Grate's below us. Customs house. That's how you came in." She was moving along the bulkhead, hands out, feeling the hull the way I'd taught myself to feel the pan—reading the world through touch when sight failed. "If the grate's open, the water's coming *in*, which means there's a way *out*, which means—"

"Which means we don't swim out. We swim *down*." My heart was doing arithmetic it didn't like. "Through the grate, under the keel line, along the customs house to the old archive stairs. It's a long dive in the dark."

"How long?"

"Long enough that we do it on the fifth tide, not the sixth, when the current's still coming in instead of going out. Long enough that we need air on the way." I looked at her across the black water, and I thought about the Needle Rocks, about the compass case in my fist while the flood tried to take us both, about every time in my life I'd chosen the way out over the way through. "Maren. There's something you should know about me before we do this."

"Tell me on the way."

"I don't leave people," I said. "I spent thirty years practicing leaving. I'm done. Whatever's down there, we come up together or not at all."

The water was at our thighs now, and cold, and rising, and she reached through the dark and took my hand—my sister's hand, the first time since the funeral, maybe the first time ever—and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

"Together," she said.

Above us, muffled through three decks of iron, the ship's bell rang the change of tide.

The fifth tide had begun.
