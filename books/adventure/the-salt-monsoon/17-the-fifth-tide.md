## 17. The Fifth Tide

The water found us before the fifth tide officially began.

I heard it first—a hiss beneath the deck plates, thin as a whisper through a keyhole. Then the whisper became a rush, and the rush became a roar, and the Meridian's Ghost groaned like a living thing being opened from the inside.

"Move!" I shouted, and the word came back at me doubled and tripled off the iron walls of the hold.

We were eleven. Maren, me, and nine survivors of the survey team that Aldous Crane had tried to erase from the world. Nine people who had spent a year hiding in a drowned city because a man with a fleet decided their maps were worth more than their lives. Nine people who had followed an invisible man down a flooding causeway to cut anchor lines on a flagship—and gotten exactly half of what they came for.

Maren was free. The charter was still ash in my pocket, or what remained of it. And now the sea was collecting its toll.

The hold of the flagship was a cathedral built by men who hated cathedrals—crates stacked into walls, chain draped like garlands, and overhead, three decks of ship pressing down like a closed fist. The escape route was a maintenance shaft aft, barely wide enough for a grown person, running up through the bilge spaces to the weather deck. I'd memorized it from the survey plans Maren carried in her head, because paper could be taken from you but memory had to be killed.

"Shaft's this way," I said. "Single file. Don't stop, don't look back."

"Charming pep talk," said a woman named Odile Voss, the junior surveyor who'd once whispered about the sixth tide with a bullet still in her. She had a limp now and a knife she used like a third hand. "You always this encouraging?"

"I'm always honest. It's why nobody hires me twice."

She almost smiled. Then the water hit our ankles, cold as a verdict, and nobody was smiling anymore.

* * *

Here is what I knew about drowning holds: they don't fill evenly. They fill according to the shape of the ship, and the shape of the Meridian's Ghost was a funnel turned upside down—wide at the keel, narrow at the hatches. The water would rise fast at first, then faster, then all at once, and the air would compress into a pocket at the top like the last breath of a dying man.

Which meant we had minutes. Maybe ten. Maybe fewer.

I counted heads as we moved. Maren ahead of me, moving with the sure stride I'd tracked across forty miles of salt pan without knowing whose sister I was following. Behind her, the survivors—Voss limping, a big quiet man named Harrow carrying a boy of maybe sixteen who'd been born in Vess and had never seen dry land until this week. Behind them, five more, and bringing up the rear, an old instrument-maker named Pell who kept pausing to touch the bulkheads like he was saying goodbye to them.

"Pell," I said. "Touch them later."

"They're good bulkheads," he said. "Better than anything Crane builds new." But he walked.

The water climbed our shins. Then our knees. The ship listed to port, slow as a falling tree, and every crate in the hold shifted with a sound like teeth grinding.

* * *

The shaft hatch was where the plans said it would be, which surprised me. In my experience, plans were lies told to you in advance so the disappointment would arrive on schedule. But there it was: a wheel-lock hatch, green with corrosion, set into the aft bulkhead like a coin pressed into a wall.

Harrow reached it first and spun the wheel. It didn't move. He put his whole back into it, boots skidding on the wet plates, and the wheel gave a quarter turn and stopped like a heart giving out.

"It's jammed," he said. "Pressure differential—the water outside is pushing against—"

"I know how pressure works," I said, and immediately regretted it, because there was no time for being cruel and no reason to be cruel to a man who'd carried a boy through a flooded city.

I looked at the hatch. I looked at the water, which was at our waists now and rising in visible surges, each one taller than the last. Somewhere above us, Crane's crew was fighting its own war with the sea, sealing hatches, saving what could be saved. Nobody was coming down here. Down here was already dead; everyone aboard knew it. That was the arithmetic of sinking ships, and I'd learned it young, watching my father's boats go under one by one in Cinder Wells harbor, sold or scuttled or simply left to rot while the debt collectors circled like gulls.

A jammed hatch. Rising water. Eleven people.

There was a valve. That was the thing—I saw it as soon as I made myself look instead of panic. A pressure-relief valve set into the shaft wall just below the hatch, the kind of fitting every ship carries and no one ever thinks about until the moment thinking about it is the only thing left. Open it, and the trapped air behind the hatch would bleed off, equalizing the pressure, letting the wheel turn.

It would also mean standing in the path of the release. Water and compressed air don't negotiate. Whoever held that valve open would be holding the door for everyone else with their own body, and the sea does not say thank you.

I didn't decide. That's the truth of it, and I've thought about it since, lying awake in places with names I won't give. I didn't weigh it, didn't measure my life against theirs, didn't have some grand revelation about the man I'd become. My hands simply went to the valve, because somewhere between Cinder Wells and this iron belly, the part of me that hoarded everything—every secret, every distance, every inch of myself I'd erased from the world's records—had finally starved to death.

"Rosalind." Maren's voice. She'd seen my hands move. She knew ships too; our father had taught us both, separately, in two different lives. "Rosalind, wait—"

"No time," I said. "Get them up the shaft. When the hatch opens, go. All of you. Don't wait for me."

"You don't get to do this alone," she said. "That's not—that's not how family works."

"Family," I said, and the word cracked something in my chest I'd been keeping sealed since chapter one of my life. "Family is what you're doing right now. Go."

* * *

The valve fought me. Of course it did. Nothing in this story has come easy—not the tracking, not the truth about my father, not the sister who sold me out and then got sold out herself by the same man. The valve was crusted with salt rust, and when I threw my weight against the handle it shrieked like something being murdered.

Behind me, the water surged past my ribs. Cold bit through my shirt. The ship groaned again, deeper this time, and I heard Harrow shouting orders in a voice that had clearly commanded survey crews once and never quite stopped.

The valve gave.

Air screamed out of the shaft—a white-hot blast of pressure that hit me like a fist the size of the hold itself. It tore at my ears, my eyes, ripped the breath out of my lungs and replaced it with spray. I locked my arms around the valve stem and held it open, because if I let go, the pressure would slam shut again and the hatch would stay frozen and everyone behind me would learn what the inside of a drowned ship tastes like.

Through the roar, I heard the wheel turning. Harrow's voice, ragged: "It's moving—it's moving—light! There's light!"

Good, I thought. Light. Keep going toward the light.

The water took my waist. Then my chest. The cold went from biting to burning, the way extreme cold does, a lie your nerves tell you in the last minutes before they stop telling you anything. My arms shook. The valve trembled in my grip like a live thing trying to escape.

One by one, they went up. I couldn't see them anymore—the water was at my chin, the spray was blinding—but I heard them. Boots on rungs. Voss swearing with real artistry. Pell apologizing to the ladder. The boy, crying quietly, and Harrow's low voice telling him something I couldn't make out and didn't need to.

Then Maren's voice, close, impossibly close, right at my ear through the chaos.

"I'm not leaving."

"Maren—" The water took my mouth. I spat it out. "Go. Please."

"You held the door," she said. "Now I hold yours. That's the deal. That's what family means, in case you missed the lesson."

Her hand closed over mine on the valve stem. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be—years of hauling lines on boats I never knew about, years of a life I'd refused to watch. Together we held it, and the pressure hammered us both, and the water rose past our chins, and above us the last survivor went up the shaft into the light.

"Now both of us," she gasped.

"Not yet—" Because the hatch was still closing behind the last of them, and if the pressure spiked before it seated—

It seated. I felt it through the hull, a deep iron thunk, final as a coffin lid.

"Now," she said.

I let go of the valve.

* * *

The sea took me the way debt collectors take a house: completely, and without any interest in what happens to the furniture.

I went down. The black closed over me and the cold went through my bones like a signature on a contract. Somewhere above, faint as a memory, there was a square of grey light, and Maren's hand found my collar and hauled, and my body remembered how to move even though my mind had already started drafting its excuses.

I kicked. I clawed at the water. The shaft was narrow and full of churning white, and the rungs were slick with growth, and twice my hand slipped and the dark pulled me back down like a creditor's grip on my sleeve.

The second time, I stopped fighting.

And in that stillness—one heartbeat, maybe two, hanging in the cold—I understood something with perfect clarity. This was the tide my father died in. Not this exact water, not this exact hour, but this kind: the fifth tide, the rising one, the moment when the sea comes to collect and a man either holds the line for others or saves himself. He'd held it. Sabotaging Crane's first salvage attempt, alone, in the dark, with no one to tell the story because the only people who could have were silenced. He'd been invisible too, at the end. Invisible and unthanked and drowned for it.

I kicked again.

My head broke the surface of the shaft and Maren's arm was around my chest and she was screaming words I couldn't hear over the water roaring up the tube beneath us. We climbed. Or she climbed and dragged me, which amounts to the same thing in the ledger. The light grew. The cold loosened its fist by one finger, then another.

Then hands—many hands—reached down out of the grey daylight, and the sea gave up its claim, grudgingly, the way it gives up everything: eventually, and not for free.

* * *

I woke on the weather deck with the sky doing that monsoon thing where it can't decide between silver and violence. Rain fell straight down, soft as an apology. Around me, sprawled across the planking, were ten other people breathing hard, coughing seawater, laughing in the broken way people laugh when the alternative is screaming.

Maren knelt over me, soaked to the skin, hair plastered to her face, glaring down with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"You idiot," she said.

"Probably," I said. My voice sounded like gravel in a bucket.

"You held that valve for six people's worth of time. You nearly—"

"I know what I nearly did."

"Do you?" She grabbed my collar, and for a second I thought she was going to shake me, and for a second after that I thought she was going to cry, and then she did neither. She just held on, the way you hold a compass needle to make sure it points true. "Because I watched you do it. And I want you to know I saw. Whatever you think you are—whatever name you erased yourself into—I saw."

Around us, the flagship lay dying. The Meridian's Ghost listed hard to port, its lower decks swallowed, its upper rails crowded with Crane's crew abandoning stations, launching boats, arguing, panicking. The mutiny I'd lit with a handful of burned papers and a shouted truth had spread through the crew like fire through dry grass. Somewhere on the sterncastle, I could hear Crane's voice—still commanding, still cutting, but thinner now, a blade worn down to the tang.

And beyond the ship, across the flooded flats, the sixth tide was coming in.

I could see it from the deck: a grey line on the southern horizon, wide as the world, moving with the patience of something that has never once been late. The sixth tide. The one my father's charts warned about. The one that would seal Vess under a year of water and swallow whatever remained of Crane's fleet if it didn't clear the flats in time.

Odile Voss crouched beside me, pressed a flask into my hand. Salt spirits. It tasted like punishment and worked like mercy.

"The boats," she said. "Crane's longboats are launching. He'll make for the deep channel—he knows these tides better than anyone alive. He mapped half of them himself."

I sat up. My arms felt like borrowed equipment. Across the water, a single longboat was pulling away from the flagship's shadow, oars biting fast, a figure at the stern who even at this distance stood too straight for a man who'd just lost everything.

"He'll run," Maren said.

"He'll run," I agreed. "And in a year, when the tide drops and Vess rises again, he'll come back with a bigger fleet and a longer memory."

"Then we'd better make sure there's something here worth coming back to defend," Voss said.

I looked at her. At Harrow, lifting the boy onto the rail to show him the horizon. At Pell, already prying instruments out of a flooded crate with the tenderness of a man rescuing children. At Maren, wringing out her sleeves, watching the sixth tide come with her father's compass in her hand—brass, dented, pointing nowhere useful and everywhere true.

Eleven of us. One drowning fleet. Six hours until the sea closed the book on this season.

"First," I said, standing on legs that filed a formal complaint, "we get off this ship. Then we talk about what Vess becomes."

The sixth tide was coming, and for the first time in my life, I wasn't running from it.

I was running toward shore—with company.
