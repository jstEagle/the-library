## 18. Crane's Court

The guns found us before the sun did.

Maren heard them first—not fired, but *presented*, the heavy ratcheting sound of a dozen swivel mounts being cranked to bear, a noise like a giant clearing his throat. She came up the flagship's ladder with salt burning in her throat and Rosalind's hand locked around her wrist, and stepped onto the deck of the *Aldous Crane's* pride—the *Prosperity Grace*, three masts, iron ribs, a name painted in gold leaf that had no business on a salvage barge.

They were surrounded. That was the first thing her eyes did, the surveyor's habit, mapping the geometry of their ruin: twelve crewmen in oiled canvas, four marines in government grey that had no right to be there either, and at the center of it all, standing on his own quarterdeck like a man hosting a garden party, Aldous Crane.

He was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the second thing. All these years his name had been a weather system—Crane's fleet, Crane's money, Crane's men who didn't come back—and she'd built him tall out of fear. In person he was a compact man of sixty-odd with a white beard trimmed close as a hedge and hands folded neatly behind his back. He wore a plain coat. He looked like a schoolmaster. He looked like a man who had never once needed to raise his voice, because everyone around him had already learned what happened when he lowered it.

"Yarrow," Crane said. Not to Rosalind. To her. "And Yarrow." A small nod toward Rosalind, almost amused. "You know, I've spent eleven years looking for your father's children. He told me once, over cards, that he had two. One who wanted everything and one who wanted nothing. I confess I've been curious which was which."

"You knew him," Maren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Behind her, Rosalind hadn't moved. She could feel him breathing, slow and deliberate, the way he breathed when he was reading ground.

"I employed him," Crane said. "For nine years. The best surveyor on the coast, and the only honest one, which made him expensive in ways I didn't appreciate until too late." He turned, gesturing with one hand at the flooded horizon beyond the rail—Vess's towers breaking the water like the knuckles of a drowned fist, the sixth tide rising silver under the early light. "Do you know what he stole from me? Not papers. Not charts. *Certainty.* I had a city's worth of mineral rights sitting in a vault at the bottom of a bay, and your father took my certainty and buried it in water."

"He hid it from you," Rosalind said. His first words. Flat as slate.

"He hid it from *everyone*," Crane corrected gently. "Which is worse. A thief you can catch. A martyr—" he smiled, thin and dry, "—a martyr just leaves a mess for other people to clean up."

Maren felt Rosalind shift beside her, half a step, the way he moved when violence was becoming arithmetic. She put her hand back on his wrist. Not holding him back. Just reminding him she was there.

"The charter," Crane said. "Where is it?"

Rosalind said nothing. Maren watched him not say nothing, watched the stillness do all his talking, and understood suddenly why creditors had spent years chasing a man who owned nothing: because a man who owns nothing can't be threatened with loss. Except Crane had read deeper than the ledger. Crane had found the one thing Rosalind Yarrow wanted after all.

"Ah," Crane said softly. "There it is."

He began to walk. Slowly, along the quarterdeck rail, boots quiet on wet planking, and every gun on deck tracked with him like dogs on a lead.

"Let me tell you what I know about you, Rosalind Yarrow," Crane said. "Born in Cinder Wells. Registered ranger, border district, eleven years' service—except the registry has gaps where you should be. You've paid clerks to lose your filings. You've burned your own commendations. There's a man in Saltport who forged your discharge papers twice because you kept coming back to un-forge them." He stopped walking. "You have spent your entire adult life erasing yourself. Do you know what that costs? Not money. Money is nothing. It costs *witness*. No record, no name, no claim. You are legally a rumor." He spread his neat hands. "I can make you a fact."

The tide lapped against the hull. Somewhere below decks, water was still finding its way into places the fifth tide had opened, and Maren counted the sounds of it without meaning to—surveyor's habit again, measuring the clock they were all standing inside.

"I can restore your name to every registry on the coast," Crane went on. "I own three of them outright and the fourth owes me its harbor dredging. Your debts—four hundred and sixty marks, plus interest, held by six holders in Cinder Wells—I'll buy them tonight and burn them. Your father's will, the one you never read, the one that named you before it named the compass—I'll see it executed properly, with witnesses, with seals. You'll be Rosalind Yarrow, son of Elias Yarrow, surveyor, in ink that cannot be erased." He paused. "In exchange, I take delivery of one document. And you and your sister walk off this ship rich, free, and *real*."

Maren's chest hurt. Because she knew—that was the obscenity of it—she knew exactly how much that offer weighed. She'd stood in a flooded archive two days ago and heard herself say the same arithmetic aloud: sell it, buy the name back, be somebody again. She'd meant it. Some traitorous part of her still meant it. And now the devil himself had priced the bargain, and the price was fair, and that fairness was the most frightening thing on this whole armed deck.

"Say something," she murmured to Rosalind.

He was looking past Crane. Past the guns. She followed his gaze and saw what he saw: at the far rail, guarded but barely watched, the survivors. Tam Voss with her burned arm wrapped in sailcloth. The old hydrographer Pell, soaked to the bone, glaring at Crane with the pure hatred of a man whose colleagues had been murdered by order. Two others, young ones, from the survey team—the team Crane had sent to die in a bell tower so the drowned city would stay forgotten. They were watching Rosalind. All of them. Watching him the way people watch a scale settle.

"Your father refused you," Crane said, quieter now, and the deck seemed to lean in. "Did you know that? Nine years I offered him partnership. Legitimacy. His name on charts that would outlive empires. And every year he came back and said no, and every year I raised the number, and the last time I saw him alive he laughed at me." Something moved behind the schoolmaster's face—grief or rage or the thing that wears both as coats. "I have hated him for eleven years for that laugh. And now I'm offering his son everything I offered him, and I find myself hoping—" he stopped, and for one unguarded moment he looked simply old, "—hoping someone in this cursed family says yes."

The silence afterward was enormous. Even the sea seemed to hold its breath between swells.

Maren realized she was gripping Rosalind's sleeve. She let go. This wasn't hers to hold. Whatever he chose here would land on both of them, but the choosing was his—it had always been going to be his, since Cinder Wells, since the compass, since a man with no name tracked a stolen inheritance across a salt pan and found a sister instead.

Rosalind looked at her.

That was all. He just looked at her, and she watched his eyes move through something at terrible speed—the debt, the name, the father who'd laughed at this man instead of kneeling; the will he'd never read because reading it would have meant admitting he'd been left something worth wanting; the years of paying clerks to make him invisible because invisibility was the only freedom a poor man could afford. She watched him weigh legitimacy itself, the whole weight of it, the thing she'd betrayed him to chase.

Then he looked at the survivors. Tam Voss met his eyes and gave him nothing—no nod, no plea, just the flat patience of a woman who'd already decided what she'd do if he sold them out, and would do it without hating him. Pell spat over the rail.

Then he looked at the sea.

The sixth tide was coming in. Maren watched him watch it, and she thought: *he's not counting marks. He's counting water.* The tide that would seal Vess for another year. The tide that had taken their father. The tide that didn't care whose name was written where, because the sea keeps no registries, honors no charters, and is the one court on this coast that has never once been bought.

"Mr. Crane," Rosalind said.

Even Crane's marines leaned forward.

"My father laughed at you," Rosalind said. "I never met the man. Everything I know about him I learned from other people's lies—yours, hers, his own logbook. But I've read his charts. Every line true. Every depth exact. He drew maps for a living, and he never once drew a lie, and that's more than any name on any registry can say." He reached slowly into his coat—every gun on deck snapped tight—and brought out nothing. Just showed them his empty hand. "You're offering to write me into the world. My father spent his life keeping the world from being written over things that shouldn't belong to anyone. I think I finally understand which side of that line our family stands on."

"And which side is that?" Crane asked. His voice had gone very soft, which Maren understood now was the most dangerous register the man owned.

"The water's," Rosalind said.

Crane closed his eyes briefly, and when he opened them the schoolmaster was gone and something older looked out, something that had ordered a survey team into a bell tower and slept fine after. "So it's the martyr's answer," he said. "How tedious. How *hereditary*." He sighed, and turned to his lieutenant. "Take the document from his body, then. And the sister watches—she's shown excellent judgment before; perhaps grief will improve it."

The marines started forward.

And Maren, who had spent her whole life calculating angles, did the last calculation of her old life in half a heartbeat: the rail was four feet away, the water below was the sixth tide coming home, and the charter was sewn into Rosalind's coat, oilskin-wrapped, and there were twenty-two men on this deck and exactly one thing any of them truly wanted.

She grabbed her brother's arm and shouted, "Tam! The anchor lines!"—because Pell had told her, hoarse and grinning in the bell tower, what the survivors had been doing all night while the siblings faced Crane's court—and somewhere below their feet, deep in the flooding hull, steel began to part.

The *Prosperity Grace* shuddered.

Crane's head snapped toward the sound, and for the first time in eleven years, Aldous Crane's certainty cracked down the middle like a chart drawn wrong.

"What," he said, "have you done?"

* * *

The ship listed. Slowly, majestically, the way a dying animal lies down—three anchors cut loose in the dark by survivors who'd had nothing left to lose but their lives, and the sixth tide pouring into the harbor channel to push the *Grace* broadside onto the shallows above the Meridian's bones. Men stumbled. Guns swung wild. A marine grabbed Maren's shoulder and she drove her elbow into his throat with a technique she'd learned from a brother she'd known for five days, and then Rosalind's hand was in hers and they were running the tilting deck toward the rail.

"THE CHARTER!" Crane roared behind them, all the softness gone, the voice of a man watching his empire slide sideways into salt water. "STOP THEM AND GET ME THAT DOCUMENT!"

They hit the rail together. Below, the water of the sixth tide rose to meet them, gray-green and patient, and beyond it the towers of Vess stood out of the flood like witnesses.

Maren looked back once—at Crane shouting orders no one obeyed, at Tam Voss already moving, at the gold-painted name on the stern beginning, plank by plank, to go under—

—and jumped.
