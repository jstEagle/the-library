## 20. Aftermath of Water

The fleet broke apart the way a body breaks apart when the spine goes.

Maren watched it from the bell tower, one hand on the cold bronze of the rail, and understood that she was watching something die in real time. Not a man—a structure. An idea. The idea that Vess could be owned.

It started with the *Salt Wren*, Crane's second-largest vessel, her captain hauling anchor and swinging wide toward open water without so much as a signal lamp. Then the dredge barges, those ugly flat-bottomed things that had spent three days chewing at the drowned archive like scavengers at a carcass. They turned first, their crews having heard—everyone had heard by now—the story running deck to deck through the fleet: that Aldous Crane had ordered eleven government surveyors silenced, and that the order had come back on him in the shape of a burned charter and a mutiny he never saw coming.

The flagship held longest. The *Meridian's Ghost*, they'd named her, some boardroom joke Maren finally understood standing here—the magnate had built his empire on the bones of the original wreck, and named his pride after his own theft. Now she sat low in the water, listing slightly, her decks crowded with men who no longer took orders. Maren could see the argument happening on the quarterdeck even from this distance: knots of sailors gesturing, a boat being lowered against someone's shouted objection, cargo being carried down—not up—and stacked on the flooded causeway like offerings left for a god nobody believed in anymore.

And then the longboat.

She saw him before she was sure it was him. A figure in the stern, too still for the chaos around him, seated the way a man sits when he has stopped pretending he is steering. The longboat pulled away from the flagship with six oarsmen and one passenger, cutting west across the shallows where the water ran copper with sunset, and Aldous Crane did not look back at the city he had come to strip.

"Diminished," Rosalind said beside her.

Maren hadn't heard him climb the tower stairs. She was learning that about her brother—he arrived the way weather arrived, without announcement, and you only noticed him when he'd already changed everything.

"He's alive," she said.

"For now." Rosalind leaned on the rail. His hands were wrapped—the escape valve in the flooding hold had taken skin off both palms, and the ranger who treated him had used half her kit—but he rested them anyway, as if pain were just another thing he'd decided not to hoard. "A man like that doesn't drown. He washes up somewhere with money hidden in his boot and starts again."

"You sound almost disappointed."

"I sound tired." He said it simply, without performance, and Maren felt the strangeness of it all over again—that this was her brother, this dry-voiced ghost of a man who had spent years deleting himself from every ledger in the territory, talking to her as if they'd been doing it all their lives. "Alive is fine. Alive and diminished is better. A thread of consequence." He glanced at her. "Your phrase."

"I've been thinking in threads lately. It's what surveyors do. You pull one and see what it's attached to."

Below them, the last of the loyalists were leaving the flagship. Someone had run up a plain white cloth at the mainmast—not surrender exactly, Maren thought. More like an admission. The *Meridian's Ghost* would sit in the shallows until the tide finished what the mutiny started, and by next season she'd be a reef, and by the season after that she'd be part of Vess, another set of ribs under the water, indistinguishable from the city she'd come to plunder.

There was a justice in that that made her throat tight.

* * *

They came ashore at dusk, the survivors, in whatever would float.

Maren counted them off the boats: nine of the original survey team, four of whom had spent two years hiding in the flooded streets of Vess rather than report back to a government office that had already sold them; a dozen mutineers from the fleet who had chosen the losing side the moment they learned what their wages had been buying; the old rangers' woman, Sefa, who had patched Rosalind's hands and said nothing about either sibling while she did it; and scattered others—divers, a cook, two boys who had signed onto Crane's fleet at fourteen because the alternative had been the debt farms.

Twenty-six people standing in the shallows beneath a drowned city, holding nothing.

Sefa waded out of the surf and planted herself in front of the group like a fence post. Maren had seen her fight on the flagship's deck—an older woman with a belaying pin and no fear whatsoever—and now she saw her do something harder.

"All right," Sefa said, in the voice of someone opening a difficult meeting. "Crane's gone. The charter's gone. Which means every claim on this place just went with it. So somebody say it plain, before the arguing starts."

Nobody said anything. The tide breathed in and out around their knees. Behind them Vess rose out of the water in its evening colors, the breaching towers gilded pink and gold, windows that had been dark for two hundred years catching the last light like lamps.

Maren found herself stepping forward. She didn't decide to. Her feet decided, the way they'd decided to steal a compass in Cinder Wells, the way Yarrow feet apparently worked.

"The survey records are destroyed," she said. "I helped destroy them. My father mapped this city and hid what he found, and Crane killed people to get it, and the answer wasn't to hold it or sell it or give it to the government. The answer was the sixth tide." She looked at the water. "It comes up, it takes everything back, and then it leaves. Every season. That's the only deed to this place that ever existed. Nobody owns a thing the sea lends out."

Silence. Then one of the mutineers—a big man with salt-cracked lips, Halvard, she'd learn later—said, "So it's common ground."

"It's borrowed ground," Maren said. "We're borrowing it. All of us. Same as the fish."

"And when the fleet comes back," someone said from the back. "When Crane rebuilds. When the government sends actual soldiers instead of surveyors."

"Then we'll be here," Sefa said, "and we'll know the streets better than they do, because we'll have lived in them." She looked around at the twenty-five faces. "Anybody wants to argue ownership, argue it with the monsoon. See who wins."

Nobody argued with the monsoon.

They built fires that night in the upper rooms of the tower district, in houses whose floors had been stone for longer than any nation represented in that crowd had existed. Maren moved through the little settlements of bedrolls and cook smoke, and everywhere she went people were doing the same strange thing: touching the walls. Running their hands along carved stone, tracing doorways, sitting in window seats that had held no one since before their great-grandparents' great-grandparents were born. Not claiming. Just—meeting the place. Letting it meet them.

Rosalind sat apart, as always, on a rooftop ledge with his ruined hands resting on his knees, watching the water.

Maren climbed up and sat beside him without asking. That was theirs now, she'd decided. The not-asking.

"They voted," she said. "While you were up here brooding. Common ground. Unanimous, if you count Sefa threatening the dissenters as unanimity."

"I don't brood."

"You absolutely brood. It's the most Yarrow thing about you, and I include Father."

That landed between them the way it always did now—not a wound anymore, but a scar they'd agreed to stop picking. Their father. One man, two faces, and somewhere underneath both of them, a third face neither child had ever been allowed to see: the surveyor who died sabotaging a salvage barge rather than let Aldous Crane put a hook into Vess. A coward to Rosalind. A martyr to Maren. A man, it turned out, which was worse and better than either.

"Do you think he knew?" Maren asked quietly. "That night. On the barge. Do you think he knew what he was going to do?"

Rosalind was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"I think he packed his tools," he said finally. "The way I pack mine. Everything in its place, nothing extra. A man doesn't pack like that for a job he means to survive." He flexed his bandaged fingers. "I used to think the packing meant he was running away. Leaving us. Now I think—" He stopped. Started again. "Now I think he was packing for the only trip he had left, and he made it neat because neatness was all he had to give anybody by then."

Maren looked at her brother, this stranger with her jaw and their father's habit of finishing sentences only after long silences, and felt the exhaustion hit her all at once—the diving, the drowning, the boarding, the burning, five tides compressed into a lifetime. She leaned sideways until her shoulder touched his, and he didn't move away, and that was the whole conversation.

Below them the fires burned. Above them the first stars came out over water that had swallowed an empire's paperwork and kept it.

* * *

Sunset the next day found them both on the bell tower again, though neither had planned it. Habit, maybe. Or the simple fact that it was the highest point in Vess and they were two people who had spent their whole lives looking for high ground.

The fleet was gone. All of it. The last stragglers had cleared the horizon at noon, and now there was only the sea, running molten gold and rose toward the edge of the world, and the towers of Vess standing in it like the skeleton of something that had decided, after two centuries, to stand back up.

"We should chart it," Maren said. "Properly. The way Father started to. Not for salvage—for the record. For whoever comes next."

"Whoever comes next might be worse than Crane."

"Then they'll find honest maps and know exactly what they're walking into." She smiled. "You could help. You track things. Tracking is just surveying that runs."

"That is not what tracking is."

"It's exactly what tracking is. I'm putting it in the report."

He huffed—almost a laugh. From Rosalind Yarrow, that was a speech.

They stood together while the sun went down, and Maren thought about inheritance. She had stolen a compass to get one, and nearly sold her soul for another, and it had taken her brother sinking the truest treasure in the territory into the sixth tide to show her what actually got handed down. Not charters. Not names restored to ledgers. Just this: two orphans of the same difficult man, standing on a tower over borrowed water, alive, unowned, and—against every reasonable expectation—at peace.

The compass sat in her pocket. Brass, worn smooth, its needle settling toward north with the patient certainty of a thing that had survived everything its owners hadn't. Tomorrow she'd start the charts. Tonight she just held the pocket, and let the needle point.

The light failed slowly, the way it does over water, gold going to copper going to violet. And in the last band of brightness on the western horizon, Maren saw it—a speck, far off, moving. Too small for a ship. About the size of a longboat.

Heading not away from Vess, she noticed.

Parallel to it.

As if something out there in the darkening water wanted to stay exactly this far from the city, close enough to watch, and was very, very patient.

Rosalind saw it too. She felt him go still beside her, the ranger waking up under the tired man.

"Well," he said quietly. "Some threads don't break."

Maren kept her eyes on the distant speck until the sea folded it into darkness, and the first lamp of the new settlement flickered alight below them, small and stubborn, a candle held up against everything still coming.
