## 21. The Compass Returned

Cinder Wells smelled the same as it always had: dust, tallow, and the sour iron of the smelter that hadn't run in nine years. Rosalind Yarrow stood in the doorway of the room he'd rented before he'd left—before Vess, before the fleet, before the water—and listened to the town breathe.

It was smaller than he remembered. Or maybe he was bigger now. Hard to say which.

The room held a cot, a chair, a hook for his ranger coat, and nothing else. He'd sold the rest on the road home, piece by piece, until what remained of his worldly possessions fit inside the kit on his back and the brass compass wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of it. He set the kit down. He did not unwrap the compass. Not yet.

There was a ritual to this, and rituals deserved their hour.

* * *

They came for him on the third morning, because creditors are like vultures—they circle longer when they think you're weak, and they always know when you've landed.

Rosalind heard them coming up the stairs of the boarding house: three sets of boots, two heavy and one careful. The careful one belonged to Haskin Pell, who held paper on half the debts in Cinder Wells and wore his ledger like a bishop wears his crozier. The heavy ones were his collectors, whose names changed every season but whose knuckles never did.

Rosalind opened the door before they could knock. It saved everyone the pretense.

"Yarrow." Pell's smile was a professional instrument, maintained daily, oiled with other men's misfortune. "We heard you came back. We heard you came back *empty*."

Word traveled fast on the flats. Faster than ships, faster than monsoons. Somewhere between Vess and Cinder Wells, the story had grown legs: the Yarrow boy went out after a treasure and came back with nothing but wet boots and a sister nobody knew he had.

"That's about the size of it," Rosalind said.

Pell's smile widened by a degree. He unfolded his ledger with the tenderness of a man unveiling a relic. "Then we'll conclude our business. Your father's estate—the portion not seized under the original lien—carried certain obligations. Debts of record. You've been difficult to find, Yarrow. Years, in fact. Most men would call that evasion."

"I'd call it hygiene."

The collectors shifted. Pell didn't. He was a man who enjoyed the long game, who liked to watch debtors understand, slowly, exactly how far under they were. He ran one finger down the ledger page. "Principal, accrued interest, recovery fees, and the cost of the search itself—which, I'll note, you've been kind enough to absorb on our behalf all these years. The total comes to four hundred and eleven marks, six coppers."

Rosalind leaned against the doorframe. Behind him, sunlight fell through the window onto the bare floorboards, onto the cot, onto the single bag that contained everything he owned.

"You can look around," he said. "Take your time."

Pell's eyes flicked past him into the room, and Rosalind watched the arithmetic happen behind them—the cot, worth perhaps two marks; the coat, worn through at the elbows; the ranger kit, which Pell's men knew better than to touch, because rangers who lost their kits had a way of becoming problems, and Pell collected money, not trouble.

"That's it?" Pell said. "That's everything?"

"That's everything."

"Where's the compass?"

And there it was. Rosalind had wondered how long it would take. Word got around, but word got *distorted*, and somewhere in the retelling, the story of the brass compass had tangled itself up with the story of the treasure, so that half the town believed he'd come home rich and the other half believed he'd come home with the one thing worth more than gold—a surveyor's instrument that had mapped the wreck of the Meridian, that had pointed the way to a fortune sealed under six tides of salt water.

"The compass isn't mine," Rosalind said.

"It was willed to you. We have the document."

"You have a document. Documents aren't the thing." He straightened off the doorframe, and something in the way he moved made both collectors take a half step back without deciding to. "My father left me a debt and a needle that points north. I kept the needle. The debt—" He looked at Pell, and let the silence do its work. "The debt is settled."

Pell blinked. "Settled."

"Paid. Discharged. however you write it in your book."

"By whom? With what? You came back with nothing. Everyone says—"

"Everyone says a lot of things." Rosalind reached into his coat and produced a folded slip of paper, creased once, bearing the seal of the Free Settlement of Vess and the signatures of eleven people who, three months ago, had been strangers stranded in a drowned city with a murdered survey team and a tyrant's fleet anchored over their heads. "Four hundred and eleven marks, drawn against the common fund of the settlement. It seems when a man saves your life, and then refuses a charter worth more than every salvage fleet on the coast combined, people find ways to thank you. Eleven of them found this way. They insisted. I stopped insisting back."

Pell took the paper the way a man takes a snake he's been told is dead. He read it twice. His lips moved on the second pass, counting.

"This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is legitimate."

"It's signed by a cartographer, two ex-surveyors, a woman who used to captain Crane's own tender, and a fisherman who claims to be ninety. Judge the seal yourself."

Pell judged the seal. His face went through several small weathers on the way to acceptance. When he looked up, the professional smile was gone, and what remained was almost human: a man confronting the end of an income stream.

"You understand," he said, recovering, folding the ledger closed with a snap, "that this changes nothing about your standing. A man with no assets is still a man with no assets. No credit. No name on any register worth having. You'll die in this town exactly as obscure as you lived in it."

Rosalind considered this.

"Yes," he said. "Probably."

He meant it. That was the strange part—the part Pell couldn't price, couldn't lien, couldn't enter in any column of any ledger. Three months ago, standing on the deck of a sinking flagship with the ashes of a charter drifting down into the sixth tide, Rosalind had understood something that had taken him forty years and one drowned city to learn: that the name he'd spent his whole life erasing had only ever been a chain, and that the freedom he'd wanted wasn't invisibility. It was *unencumbrance*. To owe nothing. To own nothing. To be owed nothing.

To walk light.

Pell and his collectors descended the stairs. At the bottom, Rosalind heard him mutter to his men, "Mark the account closed. And mark the man—" a pause, the sound of a pen scratching, "—mark him *nothing*."

Rosalind smiled at the ceiling.

Nothing. After everything, the best entry anyone had ever made beside his name.

* * *

Maren arrived on the fifth day, riding a mule she'd won at cards from a salt hauler who should have known better than to play cards with a Yarrow.

She came up the stairs two at a time the way she did everything, burst through his door without knocking—kinship, apparently, dissolved all etiquette—and stopped dead in the middle of the room.

"This is where you live?" She turned a slow circle. "This is a *cell*. This is a cell with a window."

"It has a window."

"A small one."

"It has a window."

She dropped her pack on his cot without apology, which told him she intended to stay, which told him she'd already decided, which told him she was, unmistakably, their father's daughter. She'd cut her hair short since Vess—it was growing back in uneven tufts, sun-bleached, stubborn—and there was a new scar along her jaw that she hadn't explained and he hadn't asked about. Some inheritances you don't discuss. You just wear them.

"I took a position," she announced, while she was still unpacking, as if the words had been waiting behind the bedroll. "Coastal survey. Legitimate. Chartered out of the Free Settlement, countersigned by the survivors' council. I'll be mapping the flats from Cinder Wells to the Needle Rocks. Honest charts. Every shoal marked, every depth sounded, every line drawn true."

"Honest charts," Rosalind said. "From a Yarrow."

"From *this* Yarrow." She pulled the last item from her pack and set it on his table with a click: a brand-new plotting case, government issue, brass fittings, unscratched. Then she looked at him, and her voice did the thing it did when she was being serious, which was drop all its armor at once. "I spent my whole life learning to chart lies, Roz. Crane paid for beautiful maps that said whatever he needed them to say. I drew coastlines that didn't exist and depths that would drown men, and I told myself it was just work, just ink, just a living." Her hand rested flat on the plotting case. "I'm done drawing lies. Whatever else I am, whatever blood I carry—I want the lines I leave behind to be true."

Rosalind looked at his sister—at the scar, the sunburn, the ferocity she wore like their father had worn his silence—and felt the old ache shift in his chest, the one that had lived there since a bell tower in Vess, since the truth about how Emeric Yarrow had actually died. Not fleeing debt. Sabotaging Crane's first salvage run, alone, in the dark, with a hull drill and no plan for getting out. A coward to one child. A martyr to the other. Both wrong. Both right.

"Maren," he said. "There's something I've been carrying for you."

He knelt by his kit. Untied the oilcloth.

The brass compass caught the window light and threw it back in small warm coins across the ceiling. It was dented on one side—he'd nearly drowned saving it in the Needle Rocks, and the dent was the receipt—and the needle, when he lifted the lid, swung once, twice, and settled north with the calm certainty of a thing that had never once doubted itself.

Their father's compass. The one thing Emeric Yarrow had ever given either of his children freely, though he'd done it by dying, which was a complicated way to give.

Maren didn't reach for it. She looked at it the way you look at a grave marker.

"He carried that his whole life," she said quietly. "Through every survey, every lie, every year of working for Crane. I used to think it pointed at the Meridian. That's why I stole it—that first time, in Cinder Wells. I thought if I followed it, it would lead me to the wreck, and the wreck would lead me to the truth about him." She laughed, short and rough. "It led me to you instead."

"Compasses do that," Rosalind said. "Point you somewhere you weren't looking."

"Roz—"

"Take it."

Her head came up. "No."

"Yes."

"I didn't come here for—"

"I know what you came here for. You came here because your survey starts at Cinder Wells and the boarding house is the only one with rooms that don't flood." He stood, and held the compass out between them, and watched her fight herself. "But you're going to take it anyway. Listen to me. I spent years erasing myself from every record on the coast. No registry, no roll, no ledger. I got good at it. I got *expert* at it. And do you know what I learned, out there on the water, holding this thing while the tide tried to take it?" He turned the compass so the light ran along the dent. "An inheritance isn't a thing you keep. It's a thing you carry forward. Father mapped true lines once—one survey, one act of courage at the end—and it was enough to buy a city its freedom. You're going to spend the rest of your life mapping true lines. So the compass belongs with the maps. It belongs with *you*."

Maren's jaw worked. The scar whitened along its length.

"And you?" she managed. "What do you keep?"

Rosalind reached into his coat and laid the second item on the table beside the compass: a folded contract, travel-worn, countersigned, official. The tracking commission he'd taken in Cinder Wells a lifetime ago—the contract to hunt a thief named Salt, which he'd never delivered to any employer, because the thief had turned out to be his sister and the theft had turned out to be a summons.

"You kept the *contract*?" Maren stared at it. "The piece of paper? That's what you kept? Out of everything—out of a charter, out of a fortune, out of a name—you kept the job?"

"It's the only honest thing I was ever handed," Rosalind said. "Somebody lost something. Somebody asked me to find it. I found it." He shrugged. "Turns out that's the whole trade. Everything else is weather."

Maren looked at the compass. At the contract. At her brother, standing in a cell with a window, owning less than any man she'd ever met and standing easier than any of them too.

Then she picked up the compass, closed the lid, and pressed it to her chest the way you press a wound that's finally healing.

"Dinner," she said thickly, when she trusted her voice again. "I'm buying. Apparently I have a signing advance."

"Don't spend it all. The mule eats."

"The mule can eat *grass*, Roz, it's a mule—"

* * *

They ate at the Saltcellar, the tavern where this whole affair had started, where a stranger named Salt had bought a stolen compass and started a chain of events that ended with a magnate's fleet scattered, a drowned city freed, and a charter's ashes feeding the sixth tide. The tavern keeper recognized Rosalind, said nothing, and poured his drinks a finger heavier than the measure. That was Cinder Wells' version of a monument.

Halfway through the meal, Maren spread her new charts across a cleared table and started marking soundings from memory, muttering depths, and Rosalind sat across from her with his untouched drink and watched his sister work, and understood that this—this exact moment, this noise, this lamplight, this family assembled at last from wreckage—was the entire estate.

A shadow crossed the table.

He looked up. A girl, twelve maybe, dockside clothes, salt-stiff hair, holding out a folded note with both hands like it might bite.

"You're the ranger," she said. Not a question. "You're the one who sank the charter."

"News travels," Rosalind said.

"My da says you're the one who found the survey team's people. Before the soldiers did. Before anybody." She thrust the note at him. "This came on the packet boat. It's got no sender mark. The boatman said it was for 'the invisible man,' and my da said that's you, on account of—" she wrinkled her nose, "—on account of how you do things."

Rosalind took the note. The paper was good stock. Coastal postmark. No signature.

Inside, in a small, precise, expensive hand:

*The sea gives back what it is given, Mr. Yarrow. I have been given a great deal to consider. — A friend of your father's, once.*

Maren read it upside down. Her face went still.

"Crane," she said.

"Crane," Rosalind agreed.

He read it again. There was no threat in it. That was what made it worse. There was *patience* in it—the patience of a man who had lost a fleet, a fortune, and a reputation, and had walked away into a longboat alive, and had learned, as all such men learn, that ruin is just capital in another form.

Outside, far off across the darkening flats, the first thunder of the season rolled in from the coast, low and long, the monsoon sending its calling card ahead of the rain. One year, almost to the tide, since Vess.

Maren put her hand flat on the table, over her charts. "Next season," she said quietly, "the water rises again."

Rosalind folded the note into his coat, next to the contract, next to the only things he owned that mattered.

"Then we'd best finish the honest maps," he said, "before somebody starts drawing dishonest ones."

The rain reached Cinder Wells an hour after midnight, and it fell on the town like forgiveness—on the creditors and the debtors alike, on the free city and the hidden man, on the brass compass sleeping in its new owner's room and the old contract keeping its vigil in the dark.

It fell on everything equally.

That, Rosalind thought, listening to it from his cell with a window, was the whole inheritance. That was enough.
