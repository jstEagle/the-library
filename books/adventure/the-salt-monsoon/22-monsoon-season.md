## 22. Monsoon Season

**Rosalind**

The first rain of the season came at dawn, and by noon the salt pan outside Vess had turned to mirror.

Rosalind Yarrow stood on the old causeway with his collar up and his boots in six inches of warm water, watching the flats do what they always did—erase everything. A year ago he'd have called that a mercy. Now he watched the water take the tracks of forty people walking to market and felt something closer to satisfaction. Let it erase. What mattered was what stayed above the waterline.

Vess had grown since the sixth tide took Crane's charter down into the dark. The bell tower where they'd found the survey team's camp now flew no flag at all, which was its own kind of flag. Around it, three hundred souls had built a town out of salvage and stubbornness: fishing racks on the lower terraces, rain-cisterns cobbled from ship's tanks, a long hall roofed with the hull plating of a wreck nobody claimed because everybody did. They called it the Common Hall, and they meant it.

He rangers for them now. Not for a government that had burned its own seal off its own documents, not for creditors, not for a name. For the settlement, on terms written on a slate in the Common Hall: *Yarrow walks the flats. Yarrow says when the water lies.* That was the whole contract. He kept the slate himself, hanging by his door, and some mornings he read it just to remember that a man could be employed by an agreement instead of owned by one.

The monsoon was early this year. Three days early, which meant the survey windows would shift, which meant Maren would be insufferable about it.

He was smiling when he thought that. He noticed himself doing it, the way you notice a scar has stopped aching.

Down the causeway, a figure in a surveyor's oilskin was wading toward him through the shallows, carrying a tripod over one shoulder like a rifle and swearing at the water depth with every second step. His sister had never learned to love the flats the way he did. She loved them the way a cartographer loves anything: as a problem that would eventually confess.

"Rosalind!" Her voice carried flat and clear across the water. "Your tide tables are wrong!"

"They're the same tide tables as last year."

"Then last year's were wrong too." She came up beside him, breathless, pushing back her hood. Rain ran down her face and she didn't bother wiping it. "The flood's running nine inches ahead of the almanac. If we don't re-mark the channel buoys today, the supply barge grounds itself on the Needle Rocks tomorrow, and I will be blamed, and I will deserve it, because I trusted your numbers."

"My numbers are your father's numbers."

She stopped. The rain filled the silence between them, soft as static.

It still landed strangely, that sentence. *Your father's numbers.* A year ago it would have been a knife. Now it was just true, the way the water was true, and they'd both stopped bleeding around it.

"Then your father," she said finally, "was wrong too, and I'm going to tell him so at dinner."

"He'd have argued back."

"He argued with everything." She looked out across the flooding pan, and her voice went quieter. "I've decided that's my favorite thing about him."

They stood together while the water rose around their boots, and Rosalind thought about how strange inheritance was. He'd spent ten years believing he'd inherited nothing but debt and a cold man's silence. Then a brass compass, a stolen logbook page, and a drowned city had taught him otherwise—and then the truth had come again, deeper, in a flooded hold with Crane's guns sealing them in: their father hadn't fled his debts. He'd died sabotaging the first salvage attempt, alone, on purpose, so that the Meridian's charter would never reach Aldous Crane's hands. A coward by one child's accounting. A martyr by the other's. Both wrong. Both right.

The compass rode in Maren's satchel now. She'd earned it honestly, charting honest maps, and she carried it the way he used to carry his invisibility: close, and without apology.

"Come on," she said. "Buoys won't mark themselves."

"No," Rosalind said. "But they'll wait for the rain to ease."

"The rain isn't going to ease. It's the monsoon."

"It's the *first day* of the monsoon." He turned back toward the tower. "There's a whole season ahead. Learn patience from the water, Maren. It gets everywhere eventually, and it never hurries."

She threw a handful of floodwater at him. It missed. He didn't mention that it missed. Some mercies you extend even to family.

* * *

**Maren**

The letter came on the fourth tide of the season, which Maren would later decide was fitting, because fourth tides had always been where the trouble started.

She was in the map room—formerly the survey team's instrument locker, formerly a coffin of sealed government secrets, now a room with one table, two lamps, and walls papered edge to edge with her charts of Vess. The drowned city in cross-section. The channels by tide-stage. The archive vault, marked in her own hand: *flooded, stable, leave it be.* She'd drawn that last note large enough that no one could claim they hadn't seen it.

A boy brought the letter up from the landing dock—wet, salt-stained, no return mark. Just her name, in a hand she didn't know, addressed with a formality that made her skin prickle before she broke the seal.

*Mrs. Yarrow—*

*I am not Mrs. Yarrow,* she thought. *I am Miss Yarrow, unattached, armed, and busy.*

*—I write on behalf of parties who remember the events of last season with admiration rather than resentment. Certain interests in the southern ports have taken notice of the Vess settlement's... independence. Independence attracts investment. Investment attracts attention. Some of that attention is friendly. Some is not. The distinction, this year, may depend on conversations begun soon.*

*You will recall that the sea gives back what it takes, given time. Not everything sinks forever.*

*An admirer of honest maps.*

Maren read it twice. Then she set it down on the table, very carefully, the way you set down something that might be coiled.

*Not everything sinks forever.*

She knew exactly what the letter meant, and she knew exactly who it meant, because there was only one man alive whose survival would be both a threat and a boast. Crane had gone into the longboat with nothing—no fleet, no charter, no name worth the powder to print it—and the sea had swallowed him the way it swallows everything, and everyone had agreed to let that be the end of the story. Stories are comfortable that way. The sea is not.

She thought about burning the letter. She actually held it over the lamp flame, and watched the paper begin to curl, and stopped—not because she wanted to keep it, but because Rosalind deserved to see it first. That was new, this instinct. A year ago her first move in any crisis had been to manage it alone, quietly, the way she'd managed the theft of a compass: alone, quietly, betting that blood would do what trust couldn't. She'd been right, and it had nearly killed them both, and she had learned. Slowly. The way the water learns the land.

She tucked the letter inside her coat and went to find her brother.

* * *

**Rosalind**

Rosalind was on the tower top when she found him, because he was always on the tower top at flood-tide, doing the thing the settlement paid him in food and silence to do: reading the water.

From up here Vess made sense in a way it never did at street level. The towers breaching the shallows like ribs—the same image that had curdled his awe into suspicion a year ago, when he'd climbed these stairs and found the survey team's camp cold and their documents burned. Now the ribs held rope bridges and drying nets and laundry, and children raced along the parapets daring each other to look down, and the drowned city had become simply a city, half of it underwater, all of it awake.

He heard Maren's boots on the stairs and knew before she spoke that the news was bad. She climbed the way she walked into any room carrying something heavy: fast, then deliberately slower, arranging her face on the way up.

"You got a letter," he said.

Her eyebrows went up. "How?"

"You climb like you're hiding something. You've done it since Cinder Wells." He didn't turn around. "What is it?"

She handed it over without further argument, which told him how bad it was. He read it once, fast, the way he read trails—looking for what moved—and then again, slow, looking for what waited.

"'The sea gives back what it takes,'" he said aloud.

"You think it's him."

"I think it's someone who wants us to think it might be him. That's worse." He folded the letter along its original creases, precise, and gave it back. "Crane alive is a problem. Crane rumored is a weapon. Someone's testing whether we flinch."

"Do you flinch?"

Rosalind considered the horizon. The monsoon cloud stood over the southern pan like a gray mountain range that had decided to travel, and beneath it the water was coming on, patient, total, certain. A year ago he had stood in water much like this and let the tide take a burned charter out of his hands, and he remembered the exact weight of that moment—the lightness after, the strange peace of a man who had finally owned nothing and owed nothing and chosen it. He had built a life on that choice. A slate contract. A tower watch. A sister.

"No," he said. "But I'm going to walk the southern shore tomorrow and see what the storm washed up."

"Together," Maren said.

He looked at her. Rain on her face, tripod calluses on her hands, their father's compass somewhere in her coat, and her chin set at the exact angle their father's had set when he was about to be immovable about something. One man, two faces; two children, one stubbornness. It had taken them a year and a drowning to point it in the same direction.

"Together," he agreed.

* * *

**Maren**

They walked the southern shore at first light, and the storm had indeed washed things up.

Mostly it was the usual litany of the sea's indifference: weed, driftwood, the rib cage of a whale that had died dreaming of deeper water. But a mile past the Needle Rocks, wedged in the rocks where the flood current eddied, Maren spotted the glint of lacquered wood, and they dug it out together—a ship's strongbox, brass-bound, waterlogged, stamped with a crest she recognized the way you recognize a scar: the crane-and-coin of the salvage fleet.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job perfectly, lay papers. Shipping manifests. A crew list. And a chart of the Vess flats—her own survey work, copied in an unfamiliar hand, annotated in the margins with soundings she'd never taken. Soundings of the deep channels. Soundings a salvor would need.

Soundings, she realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with the wind, for bringing ships in that had no business coming in.

"He's rebuilding," Rosalind said quietly.

"Or someone is building with his name." Maren turned the chart over. On the back, in pencil, a single line of figures—a date, eighteen months out, and a tonnage estimate that made her stomach drop. Whatever was being planned, it wasn't a longboat's errand. It was a fleet.

They stood in the wind with the box open between them, and Maren thought about the letter's last line. *An admirer of honest maps.* Whoever wrote it had known she'd keep reading past the flattery. Whoever wrote it had known both of them, or wanted to seem to.

"We show the council," Rosalind said.

"We show the council," she agreed. "And then we start charting the deep channels ourselves, properly, before anyone else does it badly."

He almost smiled. "Father would approve."

"Father would have already started." She closed the strongbox. Above them the gulls wheeled over the towers of Vess, and behind them the monsoon stood on the horizon like a promise the sea intended to keep, and Maren Yarrow—surveyor, thief, daughter, sister, citizen of a town that belonged to no one—felt the year's hard-won peace settle over her like the first warm rain.

Earned. Not given. Never given.

"Come on," she said, shouldering the box. "The water's rising, and we've got maps to make."

They walked back along the shore together, two sets of prints side by side in the wet sand, and behind them the tide came in and began, patiently, to erase them—but not, this time, the ones who made them.
