## 1. Miles, Not Years

The anchor chain of the *Cormorant* had been down in the green dark for eleven days when Theo Oyelaran finally brought up the thing they'd paid her to find. She did it the way she did everything — alone, on a breath she'd stretched past the sensible limit, with her fingers reading the wreck the way other people read letters from home.

Not that she got letters from home. There was no home to send them from.

She surfaced at noon into a sky the color of hammered tin, spat her regulator into the chop, and held up the object wrapped in a mesh bag: a ship's bell, bronze gone black, crusted with twenty years of Atlantic patience. On the deck of the salvage barge, Okonkwo — who owned half the harbor concessions between here and the lighthouse and rented his enthusiasm out by the hour — threw his cap in the air like she'd raised a drowned child instead of a piece of scrap with a serial number.

"Two hundred meters off the charted position!" he shouted across the water, as if the whole coast needed to know how wrong the insurance maps had been. "Theo! You are a witch!"

"I'm a diver," she said, and hauled herself up the ladder.

That was the difference, and she'd learned it early: people wanted to call what she did magic because they didn't want to think about what it actually was. Cold water. Black water. The long arithmetic of air and depth and time, done over and over until your body did it without you. There was no witchcraft in holding your breath while your lungs screamed and your hands kept working. There was only practice, and practice was just another word for having nowhere else to be.

She stripped her gear on the barge deck and let the wind dry her while Okonkwo danced around the bell, photographing it from six angles, already composing the telegram to the insurers. The *Cormorant* had gone down in 1948 with a cargo of machinery parts and one very insured bronze bell, and for eleven days Theo had crawled through her flooded corridors mapping the decay. Somewhere below were the crew's quarters, and somewhere in the crew's quarters would be the small things — a pipe, a ring, a letter in a tin — and those were the things she never brought up. Those belonged to the water now. She had opinions about what belonged where. She'd built an entire life around them.

"You will stay for the celebration," Okonkwo said. It wasn't quite a question. "Tonight. My cousin is grilling fish. There is a woman I want you to meet — she works at the port authority, very serious, reads books—"

"How long is the job?"

"The job? The job is finished. Finished today."

"Then I'm finished today." She said it without apology, because apology implied there was something to be sorry about, and there wasn't. This was the arrangement. She came, she found, she left. Twenty years of it. She could pack her kit in nine minutes — she'd timed it once, drunk, in a bar in Takorada, on a bet — and be on a northbound truck before the fish hit the coals.

Okonkwo's face did the thing faces always did around her, the slow collapse from warmth into that puzzled hurt, as if she were a door that opened onto a wall. "You have been here three weeks, Theo. Three weeks. You do not even have a picture on your wall."

"I have a picture in my head." She tapped her temple. "It's lighter."

He laughed, because he was a good man and good men laugh at things that aren't funny, and went back to his bell.

Theo sat on the rail with her feet over the water and did the accounting she did at the end of every job, the one nobody ever saw. Distance to the nearest town with a bus depot: four kilometers. Distance to the last place anyone had expected her to still be: everywhere. Distance since she'd slept two consecutive nights in the same bed: she'd stopped counting in years and started counting in beds, which was cleaner. Forty-one beds in the last six years, if you counted hammocks and the bunk on the *Marrow*'s predecessor, a leaking scow called the *Patience* that she'd loved more than most people.

She didn't count miles anymore. Miles were for people going somewhere.

* * *

The telegram found her at dusk, at the harbormaster's office, where she'd gone to settle her berth fees and buy passage on anything moving north.

The harbormaster's clerk — a boy of maybe nineteen with a pen behind each ear — held the envelope out to her with both hands, the way you hand someone a summons. "For T. Oyelaran. It came twice. We sent a runner to the barge yesterday but you were down."

Down. Eleven days of down. She took the envelope. It was heavy paper, official, the kind telegrams came in when the sender had paid extra to make sure you understood the weight of it before you'd read a word. The return address made her stomach drop before her brain caught up, because she knew the letterhead without reading it. Everyone in her line of work knew it. The Oyelaran Institute for Riverine Archaeology. Her mother's name carved into the institution's bones like a keel.

Her mother's name. Which had also been hers, once, before she'd started introducing herself as just Theo, before she'd let the surname go soft and unremarked, before she'd learned that the fastest way to travel light was to leave your name in every town and pick up a new one at the next dock. People hired T. Oyelaran the diver without ever connecting her to Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran, and she never corrected them. Correction was a kind of unpacking, and she didn't unpack.

She stood under the office's buzzing fluorescent tube and opened it.

The message was short. They were always short; that was the cruelty of the form. She read it standing up because sitting down felt like surrender:

**EXPEDITION SILENT 19 DAYS STOP LAST POSITION KETU REACHES STOP MONSOON EARLY THIS SEASON STOP FUNDER REQUESTING IMMEDIATE ASSISTANCE ANY VESSEL CAPABLE STOP REPLY URGENT STOP**

Nineteen days. The monsoon early. And at the bottom, the funder's name, the one that meant nothing to the clerk and everything to her by the time she'd finished reading it twice: **HALVARD VOSS, REPRESENTING THE EXPEDITION'S PATRONS.**

She read it a third time. Then she folded it along its creases, walked out to the end of the pier where the harbor lights couldn't reach, and lit it with the match she used for her one cigarette a week.

The paper caught reluctantly, the way dense paper does, curling brown at the edges before the flame found its appetite. She held it until the fire touched her gloves, then dropped the last corner into the black water, where it floated for a moment — a little burning boat, going nowhere — and sank.

"That's not an answer," she told the water. "That's just noise."

But her hands had gone cold in a way the evening breeze didn't explain, and she stood on the pier a long time after the telegram was gone, watching the phosphorescence wake and die around the pilings, doing the arithmetic she'd sworn off. Nineteen days silent. Ketu Reaches was eleven hundred kilometers upriver, past the second set of rapids, past the trading towns that emptied out ahead of the rains, into country where the river stopped being a road and became a rumor. No radio contact. Early monsoon. A team of academics and hired guards, stranded above flood level with the water rising.

And Adaeze Oyelaran somewhere in the middle of it, signing her famous name to whatever it was Voss was paying for.

Because that was the part Theo's mind kept snagging on, the splinter under the skin: *funder requesting immediate assistance.* Funder. Her mother hadn't taken institutional money in a decade — not since the scandal, not since the season the journals turned on her and the profession she'd built decided, with the tidy cruelty institutions reserve for their founders, that she was finished. Adaeze Oyelaran did not take patrons. Adaeze Oyelaran *was* the patron, the name on the grants, the face on the fundraising brochures Theo had grown up hiding from her schoolmates.

So either the woman upriver wasn't her mother anymore, or her mother had changed so much in seven years of silence that she'd signed on with a man like Halvard Voss — and Theo had heard exactly enough about Voss, in dockside bars and salvage camps, to know what buying silence cost and who usually ended up paying it.

Either way, it wasn't her problem.

She said it again, walking back along the pier, out loud this time, to make it true. "Not my problem." The words bounced off the water and came back sounding like someone else's voice, younger, seventeen, standing in a university office watching her mother choose a dig site over a graduation ceremony for the third time. Some sentences you carry so long they wear grooves in you, and then they say themselves whether you want them to or not.

* * *

She packed her kit in nine minutes anyway. Habit. She told herself it was habit.

The room she'd rented for the job — three weeks, longest she'd stayed anywhere in a year — gave itself back to its owner without complaint: no nail holes, no marks, nothing on the walls because there had never been anything to put there. She'd learned young that a room you haven't decorated is a room you can leave at 3 a.m. without looking back. Her mother had decorated. Her mother had hung masks and maps and framed photographs of river confluences all over every house they'd ever lived in, filling the walls the way she filled conversations, and Theo had spent her childhood learning to see through all of it to the empty rooms underneath.

She zipped the kit. Sat on the bare mattress. Stood up again.

The bar at the end of the harbor road was loud with the sound of men celebrating someone else's money, and she drank one beer there standing at the counter because sitting down felt like staying. Okonkwo found her there around ten, flushed and generous, and pressed a wad of bills into her hand — a bonus, he insisted, for the witchcraft — and asked again about the port authority woman, the serious one who read books, and Theo said, "I'm leaving on the morning tide," and watched him absorb it, the way everyone absorbed it eventually, with that same baffled kindness.

"You run from something, Theo?" he asked. He was drunk enough to ask.

"I run toward things. It's faster."

"And what is it you are running toward tonight?"

She looked at him. He was a good man. He deserved a better answer than the one she had, which was nothing, which was the open northbound road and the first boat going up the coast and whatever job came next, because the alternative to the next job was stillness, and stillness was where the thinking happened, and the thinking was a room she'd boarded shut twenty years ago and did not intend to reopen for a telegram from a man named Voss.

"Toward the money," she said, and toasted him, and he laughed his good-man laugh, and she finished her beer and walked out into the warm dark.

* * *

Sleep did not come. That was the trouble with burning things: the smoke stays in the room.

She lay in the rented bed listening to the harbor breathe — halyards ticking against masts, the diesel throb of a late trawler, the long sigh of water against pilings — and her body, which could sleep hanging from a wreck at forty meters, lay rigid as a hull on the hard. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the telegram, whole again, unburned, the way the mind keeps duplicates of everything you destroy. **SILENT 19 DAYS.** She saw Ketu Reaches, though she'd never been there; she'd grown up on her mother's maps of it, tracing the blue lines with a finger while Adaeze narrated the currents like bedtime stories. *Here the channel splits, little bird, and the east branch lies about its depth.* Little bird. Nobody had called her that in twenty years, and she'd have cut out the tongue of anyone who tried.

She got up at two. Sat at the bare table with the lamp off and the window open and let the harbor sounds wash through her, and did the thing she never did, which was remember.

Not the big memories — those were load-bearing, she'd inspected them often enough. The small ones. Her mother kneeling in the mud of a test pit in the rain, aged forty-three, laughing at something a local farmer had said, mud to the elbows, happier than Theo had ever seen her indoors. Her mother's hands teaching hers to tie a bowline, patient, warm, smelling of pencil shavings and river water. Her mother at seventeen-year-old Theo's final school recital, in the back row, arriving forty minutes late and leaving before the applause, and the look on her face in the doorway — not boredom, that was the thing Theo had chosen to believe for twenty years, not boredom but hunger, the specific hunger of a woman whose life's work was calling to her from upstream — and how Theo had taken that hunger personally, had spent two decades converting it into evidence of abandonment, filing it away in the room she'd boarded shut.

Seven years of silence between them now. Seven years since the scandal, since the last phone call, since Adaeze had said *you don't understand what they're doing to me* and Theo had said *no, I don't, because you never let me understand anything,* and hung up, and moved, and kept moving, measuring her life in distances because distances couldn't disappoint you the way people did.

And now some clerk's typed line said her mother was nineteen days silent above floodwater, and Theo had burned the only copy, and her hands still hadn't warmed up.

She thought about the bell on Okonkwo's barge. Twenty years in the water and still legible — you could scrape the crust off and read the maker's mark, the year, the ship's name. Things kept their names longer than people kept their families. That was the whole secret of her trade, the thing the poets missed: the water doesn't erase. It preserves. It holds everything exactly where it sank and waits, patient as debt, for someone to come down and read it.

She wondered what the water above Ketu Reaches was holding right now. Radios. Crates. A woman who wept but wouldn't quit — no, that wasn't a phrase yet, that was a phrase that would find her later, downstream of everything, when it was too late to be useful. Right now there was only the arithmetic, running itself whether she authorized it or not: eleven hundred kilometers. Early monsoon. Nineteen days plus however many it took her to get there, if she went, which she wasn't going, because it wasn't her problem, because she'd burned the telegram, because the smoke was in the room and the room was boarded shut and she liked it that way.

At four in the morning she gave up on sleep, dressed, shouldered the kit, and walked down to the harbor in the gray hour when the fishing boats were waking and the water lay flat as poured metal.

There was a boat going north. There was always a boat going north, if you were willing to pay in cash and ask no questions and stand in the bow where the spray came aboard. She found the captain drinking coffee on his stern, a leathery Ewe man named Sena who'd carried her twice before and knew better than to chat.

"Ketu," she said.

He looked at her over the rim of his cup. "Ketu's a river port. I'm coastal. I don't go upriver in storm season. Nobody goes upriver in storm season."

"How far north do you go?"

"As far as the last town with a dock worth the name." He squinted at her, at the kit on her shoulder, at whatever was written on her face that she couldn't feel. "After that you're on foot or you're swimming, and both ways the river owns you."

"Sena." She counted bills into his palm, enough for the passage and enough extra to close his mouth. "How fast can you be ready?"

He looked at the money. He looked at the eastern horizon, where the sky had begun to bruise. "Storm season's coming early this year," he said. "Everyone's saying it. Birds are wrong. Water's wrong." He tucked the bills away. "An hour. And Oyelaran—" he'd known her name longer than she'd known he knew it, and she flinched at the full weight of it, the name she'd burned and buried and dragged behind her for twenty years, "—whatever you're chasing up that river, the river's chased it longer."

She stepped aboard and stood in the bow where the spray would come, facing north, kit at her feet, the harbor falling away behind her with all its celebrations and port-authority women and pictures she'd never hung on walls.

Eleven hundred kilometers. She'd measured her whole life in numbers like that, safe numbers, numbers that meant motion and therefore meant nothing. But somewhere behind her ribs, in the boarded-up room, something had heard the word *silent* and refused to stop echoing it, and Theo Oyelaran stood in the spray with her mother's name in her mouth like salt water, telling herself the only story she had left:

*I'm going for the fee. Just the fee.*

The tide turned. The engine woke. The coast began to slide away, mile by mile, the way it always did, the way it always would — except that this time, for the first time in twenty years, she was counting the miles toward something instead of away.
