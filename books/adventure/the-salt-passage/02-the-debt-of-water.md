## 2. The Debt of Water

The telegram burned fast. That was the thing about telegrams nobody told you — all that official paper and government ink, and it went up like a love letter.

Theo watched the last corner curl to black in the oil drum behind her rented room and told herself the heat on her face was just the fire. Port Harcourt in October was a furnace with a harbor attached; sweat was weather, not feeling. She wiped her palm on her thigh and went back inside to pack anyway, which was how she knew she'd already decided.

She packed the way she always did: one duffel, everything that mattered in the outer pockets where she could grab it if she had to abandon the rest. Diving mask. Knife. The waterproof case with her licenses and the photograph she never looked at. Two shirts, one pair of boots, a coil of good rope. Twenty years of practice had pared her down to a person who could be gone in nine minutes flat, and she'd once believed this was freedom. Lately it felt more like a symptom.

*Salvage fee,* she wrote in her head, the way you'd write a label on a box so future-you wouldn't open it. *Voss expedition, two months' wages for one run upriver. That's all.*

The lie had good bones. It almost stood up on its own.

* * *

Getting north took three days and every kind of vehicle that floats, rolls, or barely qualifies as either. A fish truck to Onitsha. A ferry whose captain charged her double because she was a woman traveling alone and triple because she didn't argue. Then a bus with a cracked windshield and a driver who prayed aloud at every bridge, which Theo found less reassuring than he clearly intended.

She slept sitting up, her duffel between her knees like a child, and dreamed about water the way she always did — not drowning, never drowning, just standing waist-deep in some brown river while a voice upstream called her name in her mother's accent, the one that put music even into anger. *Téo.* She woke at the border crossing with her heart going and the dream still on her skin like river silt.

The last functioning town before the flood channels was called Nkisi Junction, though the junction itself had drowned years ago and the town had kept the name out of stubbornness or grief. It was a shelf of corrugated roofs and mud-brick warehouses stacked along the last high bank, and beyond it the country simply gave up being land. Water lay over the fields like poured glass. Trees stood in it to their shoulders. Somewhere out there, forty miles past the last marker buoy, an expedition had stopped answering its radio eleven days ago, and the monsoon was coming early, and everyone with any sense was moving in the opposite direction from Theo.

Which is why the harbor office was empty except for flies and a port officer named Mr. Ezenwa who looked up from his fan when she came in and said, without greeting, "You want a boat."

"I want a boat."

"No boats." He turned back to his newspaper.

"I'll pay."

He lowered the paper an inch. Above it, his eyes did the arithmetic of her — the salt-bleached hair cut short enough to be practical, the forearms of someone who hauled anchor chain for a living, the duffel, the boots, the general absence of anything that suggested she'd take no for an answer. "Everyone wants a boat," he said. "Nobody gets a boat. Storm season came two weeks early. The channels are moving. I have lost two barges and a cousin this month, and my superior has instructed me to tell people like you" — the paper rose again — "that the river is closed."

"The river doesn't close."

"The river," said Mr. Ezenwa, "does whatever it likes. That is precisely the problem."

Theo set both hands on his desk and leaned into the fan's weak breeze. "There's a research team out there. Archaeologists. They've gone silent."

"Then they are either dead or hiding from the soldiers, and in neither case is a boat going to improve their situation." He said it without cruelty, the way you'd report the tide. "You are not the first. Three weeks ago there was another one. A woman. Also asking after the Oyelaran expedition."

Theo's hands went still on the desk.

"She also left without a boat," Ezenwa added, and something in his tone suggested he enjoyed the symmetry. "People hear *expedition* and they think romance. They think treasure. They do not think about what the water does to a hull, or what men with guns do to a hull's passengers. Go home, madam. Whatever name you're carrying, it isn't worth this."

*Whatever name you're carrying.* He couldn't have known. He was fishing, the way small-town officials fished, casting hooks into everyone who passed. But the hook went in deep anyway, because the name she wasn't carrying was the only reason she was standing in this office sweating through her second shirt, and they both knew it, and neither of them knew it.

"I need a boat," she said again, quieter now, which was somehow worse for him than shouting. Shouting he could file. This he'd have to remember.

Ezenwa sighed, folded his newspaper with the finality of a man closing a ledger, and reached under the desk. What he produced was not a boat. It was a name, written on the back of a customs form: *Baptiste — fuel dock, end of the quay. Tell him I sent you and that I am sorry.*

"Why sorry?"

"Because Baptiste will say yes," Ezenwa said. "That is his tragedy. And then it becomes mine."

* * *

Baptiste turned out to be a Cameroonian in his sixties with a face like driftwood and a fuel dock that serviced exactly one seaworthy vessel, which he owned, which leaked, and which he named the *Marrow* with the defiant pride of a man who had chosen the name himself and dared anyone to laugh. Theo laughed. Baptiste did not.

"Storm season," he said, when she made her offer. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"Two weeks early. I know."

"You know nothing." He spat expertly off the edge of the dock, into the brown water. "I ran this river thirty years. I know what she does when the monsoon comes angry. She eats boats. She eats the *good* boats, the ones with steel hulls and radios that work. You want me to take my—" he gestured at the *Marrow*, whose paint was less a coating than a memory of paint, "—into the flooded channels, past the narrows, in rising water, with a storm on our back. For money."

"For money," Theo agreed.

"How much?"

She named a figure that emptied most of her account. Baptiste looked at the sky, at the water, at her, in that order, performing reluctance like a man checking his watch before agreeing to a favor. Theo had spent enough years around boatmen to recognize theater when it was performed on her. She also recognized the moment his eyes flicked past her shoulder to the quay, and back, and understood that the performance had an audience problem: he wanted to say yes and needed a reason that wasn't greed.

"Half now," he said. "Half when we find them alive. Nothing if we find them dead — I don't take pay for corpses."

"That's not how salvage works."

"This isn't salvage," Baptiste said. "This is a rescue, and rescues are worse business. Half now."

They shook on it. His grip was dry and strong as root wood. As she counted out the advance from the canvas roll in her boot, he studied her with the frankness of the old, who have earned the right.

"The Oyelaran woman," he said. "The famous one. You have her chin."

Theo's hand stopped over the money. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Mm." He took the bills without hurry. "Thirty years on a river, girl. People come through here running from things, toward things, and they all think their faces are private. Get your gear aboard by dawn. We leave with the first light, before the wind decides whether today is a working day or a killing one."

* * *

She spent the afternoon provisioning and the evening learning why the whole town was talking about the expedition, none of it usefully. The bars along the quay were full of the usual river gossip, which traveled faster than radio and was reliable as weather. She bought beers she didn't drink and listened.

What she pieced together, glass by glass:

The expedition had come through Nkisi Junction in force six weeks back. Not the ragged academic caravans Theo remembered from childhood — canvas tents and grad students arguing about stratigraphy — but something with money in its bones. New crates. Satellite phone. And guards, which no legitimate dig needed on a stretch of river where the biggest danger was hippos. Men with rifles who didn't drink, didn't talk, and paid cash for silence the way other men paid for drinks.

"They were polite," a fuel seller told her, shrugging. "Polite like a snake is smooth."

And above them all, one name, repeated with the particular care people give names that might be listening: Voss. Halvard Voss. Theo had heard the name before, the way you hear about weather systems in other hemispheres — a European collector, a buyer of African antiquities, a man whose acquisitions appeared in museum wings with donor plaques polished to a shine that never quite explained where the objects had come from. A man who had never been photographed angry, because men like him employed people to ensure there was nothing to photograph.

"He funded the dig," the barman said, wiping a glass that was already clean. "Or he owns it. Same thing, with men like that. The professor signed papers with his seal on them. Everybody saw."

Theo's beer sat untouched and going warm. *Signed papers.* Her mother had spent forty years building a reputation so clean it squeaked, refusing private buyers so consistently that journals wrote editorials about it. Adaeze Oyelaran did not sign papers with collectors' seals on them. Which meant either the woman who raised her had changed in twenty years, or the signature hadn't been freely given, and Theo found she couldn't decide which possibility frightened her more.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the telegram: if Adaeze had sold out, then the mother Theo had spent two decades resenting was a stranger, and the resentment had been wasted on the wrong woman. And if Adaeze hadn't — if she was coerced, trapped, signing manifests at gunpoint somewhere upriver while the monsoon came down on her like a lid — then Theo was the daughter of a hostage, and she was sitting in a bar doing nothing.

Either way, the version of the story where she stayed in Port Harcourt and let the river keep its secrets had quietly stopped existing.

"Another?" the barman asked.

Theo pushed the warm beer across the counter and stood. "No. I'm leaving in the morning."

"North?" He didn't bother disguising his disbelief. Nobody went north this time of year. Going north was how you became a story told in this bar, the bad kind, the kind with a pause before the ending.

"North," she said.

* * *

The room Baptiste kept for paying passengers was above the fuel dock, a cot and a mosquito net and a window that didn't close against the smell of diesel and river rot. Theo lay on the cot listening to the water slap the pilings and rehearsed her cover story for the hundredth time.

*I'm a salvage diver hired to recover equipment from the expedition site. Insurance job. Boring. Nobody's daughter.*

It would hold until it met anyone observant, which meant it wouldn't hold long, which meant speed mattered more than subtlety. Get upriver, get eyes on the camp, get out. In and around the channels before the monsoon made the decision for everyone. She'd charted the route in her head all evening: two days to the narrows, a day through them if the water cooperated, then the flooded forest where the maps stopped meaning anything and the river started meaning everything.

Sleep came in pieces. Around midnight she gave up, sat on the cot's edge, and dug the waterproof case out of her duffel's outer pocket — the one thing she carried that wasn't gear.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: her diving certifications, her pilot's license under the name T. Okon, and a photograph taken the summer she was seventeen, the last summer before everything. Two women on the deck of a research barge. The older one mid-laugh, pointing at something off-frame, sun catching the gray coming in early at her temples. The younger one scowling, arms crossed, furious about something that had seemed, at seventeen, worth ending a family over.

Theo looked at the photograph the way she looked at wrecks: briefly, professionally, cataloguing damage she had no intention of repairing tonight.

*Two months' wages,* she reminded herself, putting it away. *One run upriver. That's all this is.*

Outside, the wind changed. She felt it before she heard it — a shift in the pressure against the window, a new note in the river's voice, deeper, more insistent. The first breath of the monsoon, arriving ahead of schedule, the way everything in this country arrived ahead of schedule except help.

Somewhere down the quay, a dog started barking at nothing, and then stopped all at once, as if it had thought better of it.

Theo lay back down and stared at the netting and admitted, to the dark, to the river, to no one: *I'm coming, Mama. God help us both, I'm coming.*

Dawn was four hours away. She was packed in nine minutes anyway.
