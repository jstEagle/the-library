## 3. A Stranger at the Dock

I had been in Port Kessu three days when I saw her argue with the boatmen, and I knew within a minute that she was chasing Voss.

You learn to read money the way doctors read symptoms. Eight years of watching Halvard Voss's men move through dig sites and dockyards taught me the gait of it — the way his people walk like they own the ground they're standing on, the way his money moves upstream against every current of sense. And here was this woman, sunburned and salt-stiff, standing on the rotted planks of the Kessu ferry dock with a telegram's worth of urgency written all over her, offering sums no sane person would offer for passage upriver in the last week before the monsoon.

"Two hundred," she said. "Two hundred now, two hundred when we make Ubeni Landing."

The boatman, an old man with hands like tree roots, didn't even look up from his net. "No one goes to Ubeni after the first wind. You want to die, drown yourself here. Cheaper."

"Three hundred."

"You're not listening, woman."

"I'm listening fine. Three hundred."

I sat on a bollard with my coffee going cold and watched her work him. She wasn't rich — you could see that in the clothes, practical canvas gone gray at the elbows, boots resoled twice. She was spending money she couldn't afford on something she couldn't afford to want. That combination has a smell, and I'd smelled it before, eight years ago, on my own skin.

She moved down the dock to the next boatman. Same numbers, same flat refusal. The third one laughed at her. She didn't laugh back. There was a moment where the whole dock went quiet and I thought she might actually hit him, and then she turned away instead, jaw working, and stood looking upriver like she could bully the water itself into cooperating.

That's when I got a good look at her face and felt the floor of my certainty tilt.

I knew that face. Not from Voss's world — from older than that. From journals, from conference photographs pinned to a departmental board when I was a resident medic fresh out of field training, from the margins of a famous career I'd studied the way you study anything you secretly resent. Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran. The daughter. The estranged one. Theo.

Which meant this wasn't just someone chasing Voss's money trail. This was someone chasing it for blood reasons, which made her either the most dangerous person on this dock or the most useful one. Possibly both.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Halvard Voss, and what nobody who still worked in archaeology would say out loud:

Eight years ago I was expedition medic on the Tel-Aruni survey. Funded through three shell foundations, all of them traceable — if you were stubborn enough to spend a year tracing them — to a private collector in Europe whose name appeared nowhere on any document. We found things at Tel-Aruni. Good things. Bronze work, funerary goods, a burial chamber the lead archaeologist wept over. And then, in the last week, crates started leaving the site that never appeared on any manifest. I counted them. Counting is what I do; it's why I made a good medic and a terrible employee. I counted eleven crates that went downriver at night on boats with no registration marks, guarded by men whose contracts listed them as "logistics consultants."

I wrote a report. I sent it to the funding body, to the national antiquities authority, to the university ethics board. I did everything right. That was my mistake — I believed there was a right.

What came back was a campaign. My field notes were "inconsistently maintained." My controlled-substance log showed discrepancies — discrepancies Voss's people had manufactured by breaking into the medical tent and doctoring records I kept locked. A patient death from the year before, one I had grieved honestly and documented thoroughly, was re-examined until it looked like negligence. The inquiry took fourteen months. At the end of it I lost my license, my standing, and every professional relationship I had, and the man behind it lost nothing, because his name had never been attached to anything. I spent two years trying to put that name on paper where the law could see it. I failed. Then I stopped trying to prove it and started trying to survive it, which is its own kind of failure, quieter and better paid.

But I kept copies. Every document, every photograph, every crate count, every forged log entry photographed beside the original. In a bank box, in duplicate, in places I won't describe even now. Because I understood something in those years that I think most honest people never have to learn: evidence doesn't rot. Evidence waits. It doesn't need me to be believed. It only needs the right day.

And now here was a woman on a dock offering money she didn't have to go upriver into a storm season, toward a silent expedition funded — I would have bet my remaining kidney on it — by the same invisible hand. Someone had finally given me the right day.

* * *

I bought another coffee I didn't drink and followed her at a distance for an hour while she worked the harbor office, the chandlery, a fuel broker who owed somebody a favor. She collected refusals the way other people collect souvenirs. By late afternoon she'd ended up at the far end of the dock, sitting on the edge with her boots hanging over the black water, eating nothing, staring at nothing.

I sat down next to her. Close enough to talk, far enough not to spook her. People who've been burned don't like their space closed.

"They'll take you upriver," I said, "for four hundred. But they'll turn back at the narrows and leave you there, and keep your money."

She looked at me sideways. Brown eyes, tired, sharp underneath the tiredness. "You've been following me since morning."

"Since the second boatman."

"Why?"

"Because you're going where I'm going, and you're going to die without help."

"I don't need—"

"A medic?" I let that sit. "A river pilot? Both, actually. You argued with five boatmen today and none of them told you the real reason nobody's running the upper channel. It's not the weather. It's that two barges went through the narrows last month and only one came back, and the one that came back had holes in it nobody wants to explain. Somebody doesn't want traffic upriver right now. You can't buy your way past that. You need someone who knows how to read water that's being watched."

She turned to face me fully then, and I watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic I'd done about her. Stranger, dock, unsolicited offer, monsoon coming: what does this person want?

"What's your name?"

"Imani."

"Imani what?"

"Just Imani. For now." I held out my hand anyway. She didn't take it. Fine. I hadn't expected her to. "I was expedition medic, eight years ago, on a survey up this same river system. Different branch. Same funder."

Something moved behind her eyes. "You know who funds these expeditions."

"I know who funds everything." I reached into my jacket — slowly, letting her watch — and pulled out a folded page I'd torn from a shipping register that morning. Voss's import house, listed as consignee on three outbound cargo declarations from this port in the past six weeks. "Halvard Voss. Collector. He buys artifacts the way other men buy silence, and mostly he's buying both. Your mother's expedition is on his ledger. So am I, in a way. He ended my career when I tried to report him. I've spent eight years waiting for a chance to hand him back the bill."

She took the page. Read it longer than the information required. When she looked up, her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when someone is holding something very heavy very still.

"My mother's team went silent nine days ago. The storm season is early. Every official in this town says wait until it passes, and by then—" She stopped herself. Started again, quieter. "Why would you spend your own money on my problem?"

"Because it isn't yours alone. If Voss is moving looted material out under cover of that expedition, he's doing it the way he always does — with paperwork, with coerced signatures, with a respected name on the manifests so customs officers wave it through. Your mother's name is the perfect instrument. Famous enough to legitimize, isolated enough to control. If she's alive up there, she's not just missing. She's being used." I paused. "And if she's dead, then whoever silenced her used her too, and I want the receipts either way."

"That's cold."

"That's survived."

We sat with that. The tide was turning below us; the dock pilings made their slow knocking sound against the current shift. Somewhere behind us a crane groaned through the evening's last loading. She folded the shipping page along its existing crease, precisely, and handed it back.

"If I say yes," she said, "what exactly are you offering?"

"Medical skills — real ones, lapsed license or not, I haven't forgotten how to keep people alive in the field. River experience — I ran supply runs on this system for two seasons before Tel-Aruni. And money." I told her the number. Half of everything I had left in the world. Her eyebrows went up despite her best effort.

"That's insane."

"It's not charity. I'm buying a seat on whatever happens upriver. I want access to that camp, those manifests, those crates. Whatever you find, I get copies. Whatever evidence exists, it comes downstream with us or it doesn't come at all. Those are my terms."

"And if what we find is just... an expedition that had bad luck?"

"Then I'll have wasted half my savings and gained a boat ride. I can live with that." I stood, brushed off my trousers, and looked down at her. "One more thing. You should know it before you decide, because you'll figure it out eventually and I'd rather you hear it from me. I'm not doing this for your mother. I barely remember her work fondly — I resented it, back when I had a career to compare it to. I'm doing this for me. If you need someone coming upriver out of love, I'm the wrong passenger."

For the first time all day, something that was almost a smile crossed her face. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes.

"Good," she said. "I'm not going upriver out of love either. I'm going because there's a salvage fee."

"Liar," I said.

"So are you."

The almost-smile again, on both our faces this time, gone as fast as it came. Two liars recognizing each other's work. There's a kind of trust that starts that way — not the warm kind, the structural kind. You look at someone else's armor and you see your own, and you understand that neither of you is going to ask the other to take it off, and somehow that's safer than intimacy ever was.

"There's a boat for sale at the south yard," I said. "Old river trader, steel-hulled, ugly as sin. Owner's asking too much because he thinks the monsoon makes it worthless, but the hull's sound and the engine's recent. I looked at it yesterday. With your salvage contacts we might get it for half."

She stood up. She was taller than I'd guessed, and steadier — the dock didn't seem to move under her the way it moved under everyone else.

"The Marrow," she read off the listing sheet I'd picked up that morning, and almost smiled again at the name. "Appropriate."

"We'll sink or float according to our honesty," I said. "Come on. We've got maybe two days of weather window, and I want to be on the water before the officials change their minds about closing the channel."

She fell into step beside me, and neither of us said anything else for a long time. Behind us the harbor lights came on one by one, and ahead of us the river ran dark toward the interior, carrying whatever it carried — my eight years, her twenty, and somewhere upstream, a camp full of crates and a woman signing her name against her will.

I had waited a long time for the right day. I just hadn't expected it to arrive arguing with boatmen, burning money she didn't have, wearing her grief like a coat two sizes too small.

The storm was coming. Good. Storms expose what's buried.

I intended to be there when it did.
