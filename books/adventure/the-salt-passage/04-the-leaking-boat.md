## 4. The Leaking Boat

The boat was called the *Marrow*, and she was dying.

Theo knew it from thirty meters away, before the broker even opened his mouth. She'd spent twenty years reading hulls the way other people read faces — the sag at the waterline, the rust weeping from seams like old scars reopened, the way a boat sits in the water when she's given up arguing. The *Marrow* sat low and sullen at the dock's end, tied with three lines because two wouldn't be trusted, and every plank of her told the same story: *I have been worked hard and loved by no one.*

"She's sound," said the broker, who was lying.

"Everything is sound until it isn't," Theo said. "How much?"

He named a figure that was insultingly high for a boat this far gone, and she named a figure that was insultingly low, and they met somewhere in the middle because neither of them had the energy for a long war. Imani stood back with her arms crossed, watching the transaction the way you watch someone buy a horse that's already lame.

"You're sure about this one," Imani said, not quite a question, as the broker went to fetch the papers.

"I'm sure about the price."

"That's not what I asked."

Theo ran her palm along the gunwale. Under the grime, the wood was decent stock — old-growth something, dense as bone, cut by builders who'd known what they were doing even if everyone since hadn't. A boat was never really rotten all the way through. It just needed someone willing to find out which parts were still alive.

"She leaks," the broker admitted, returning with a ledger and a pen that had been chewed to splinters. "Slow, though. Bailing keeps up fine in calm water."

"And in rapids?"

The broker smiled the smile of a man who would not be on this boat. "In rapids, everything leaks."

* * *

They left at first light, because Theo didn't trust the broker not to change his mind overnight, and because every hour they waited was an hour the monsoon gained on them. The river town fell away behind them in stages — the fish market smell first, then the church bell, then the last tin roof catching sun like a signal mirror — and then there was only the channel, wide and brown and moving with the patient confidence of something that had never once been in a hurry.

Imani claimed the stern seat and a bail bucket and appointed herself to both without discussion. Theo took the tiller and the engine, an ancient outboard that started on the fourth pull and settled into a rhythm like a man breathing through pain.

"So," Imani said, over the noise. "You've done this before."

"Done what."

"This. Pointed yourself at something dangerous because standing still felt worse."

Theo adjusted the throttle a fraction. The channel narrowed ahead, banks closing in, fringed with reeds that bent and whispered against each other. "I've done salvage work for nineteen years," she said. "Boats sink. Somebody pays me to bring them back up. That's all this is."

"Uh-huh." Imani dipped the bucket over the side, brought up half a gallon of river, dumped it. "And your mother being on that river is incidental."

"My mother," Theo said, "is a stranger who happens to share my surname. If she were drowning in front of me I'd throw her a rope. She's not drowning. She's silent. There's a difference, and the difference is none of my business."

Imani bailed again. Said nothing. The silence had a texture to it, though — not agreement, exactly, but the particular patience of someone who has heard a lie so many times from their own mouth that they can recognize it in others.

The first set of rapids came an hour after dawn, where the river dropped over a shelf of submerged rock and turned white and furious for two hundred meters. Theo killed the engine and steered with the current, reading the water the way her mother read strata — the V of smooth green marking the safe line, the dark boils downstream marking holes that could swallow a hull whole. The *Marrow* bucked once, twice, then committed, and for ninety seconds there was nothing but spray and the groan of old wood flexing and Imani's bucket abandoned in favor of holding onto the rail with both hands.

Then it was over, and the river smoothed out again as if nothing had happened, and Theo noticed the new sound underneath the engine.

A knock. Soft, rhythmic, from below the waterline.

"There it is," she said.

"What?"

"The leak found friends."

* * *

By noon the bilge was gaining on them. Not fast — the *Marrow* leaked like a confession, one truth at a time, each drip reluctant but relentless — but steadily enough that Imani's bailing had become a full-time occupation, her rhythm unbroken, her expression migrating from skeptical to grim.

"We should stop," Imani said. "Beach her, look at it properly."

"Beaching takes hours. Hours is distance." Theo listened to the knock under her feet. It had changed pitch since morning. Deeper. Closer to the center seam. "There's a sandbar around the next bend. We'll careen her there."

"You don't know that."

"I know this river."

"You've never been on this river."

"I've been on forty rivers that are all the same river wearing different names." Theo eased the throttle down as the bend came into view — and there, mercifully, exactly where the current's shoulder suggested it would be, lay a spit of pale sand, exposed by the dry season's low water, maybe fifteen meters of it, backed by wall-of-green forest. "See? Forty rivers."

She brought the *Marrow* in slow, letting the keel kiss the bar with a grinding shudder that Theo felt in her teeth, and shut the engine off. In the sudden quiet, the knock was unmistakable now. And beneath it, fainter, the whisper of water finding its way in through somewhere it should not be able to reach.

Imani looked over the side. "It's coming in faster than it was."

"It always does. Water's patient. It finds the second crack while you're worrying about the first." Theo was already pulling off her boots. "How are you with a hammer?"

"I'm a medic."

"That's a yes or a no."

"It's a *why do you ask*."

"Because in about four minutes you're going to be the only person on this boat who isn't underwater." Theo stripped down to her shirt and shorts, checked the knife on her belt, and began hunting through the tool locker the broker had thrown in as part of the deal — a canvas roll containing, among other treasures, a mallet, a chisel, a lump of raw oakum, and half a brick of pitch wrapped in oilcloth. Salvage divers learned to carry their own patch kit the way priests carried communion. "There's a cracked seam amidships, port side, probably running aft. I patch it from outside; you drive wedges from inside where I tell you. Can you swing a mallet without hitting your own thumb?"

"I've held a scalpel steady inside a chest cavity during a mortar attack," Imani said. "I think I can manage a wedge."

"Good answer."

Theo slung the tool roll across her back, knotted a length of rope around her waist and hitched it to the base of the mast — brown water rivers didn't forgive a diver who lost her boat — and went over the side.

* * *

The water hit her like a hand.

Not cold, exactly — the season was too far along for cold — but thick, opaque, alive. Brown water wasn't empty water. It was soup: silt and leaf-rot and the dissolved ghosts of everything the river had taken apart upstream. Visibility was a forearm's length, maybe less. Theo hung on the rope, let herself sink along the hull, and worked by touch.

Her hands found the planking. Old-growth hardwood, as she'd guessed, still dense, still honest — but the caulking between the planks had dried and shrunk through too many dry seasons moored in too much sun, and somewhere around the turn of the bilge her fingers dropped into a groove that shouldn't exist. A seam, opened. Not wide — two fingers' width at its worst point, tapering to a hairline — but running longer than she'd hoped, curving aft like a sentence trailing off.

Above her, muffled by the hull, she heard Imani's voice, distorted into nonsense, and then the flat knock of the mallet testing the planks from inside. Good. The woman followed instructions without needing them repeated. That was rarer than it should be.

Theo surfaced, blew the river out of her nose, and shouted: "Port side! Amidships, just forward of where the floorboards end! Feel for the gap!"

A pause. Then the mallet knocked — once, twice — and stopped dead on a hollow note. "Found it!" Imani's voice, thin through the wood. "It's — how bad?"

"How long?"

"Maybe a meter. Maybe more."

Of course it was more. It was always more. Theo took a breath, dove, and got to work.

Diving a hull repair in brown water was surgery performed inside a closed fist. She couldn't see the seam; she read it. Fingertips mapping the gap's width, gauging the plank edges for splinters, clearing the old caulk with the chisel in short controlled strokes while the current pushed at her like a crowd trying to move past. Every thirty seconds she came up for air, spat, reported, went back down. Between dives, Imani drove the oakum home from inside — Theo feeling each blow arrive through the hull as a deep wooden thud, adjusting her calls by feel: *harder, softer, left, there.*

"You're good at this," Imani said, on Theo's third surfacing. There was something almost annoyed in it, the way people get annoyed discovering a competence they'd decided not to credit.

"Nineteen years."

"No. I mean — most people dive a hull like this and come up swearing. You come up giving coordinates." Imani leaned on the rail, dripping sweat, forearms streaked with pitch and oakum fibers. Her hands, Theo noticed, were steady. Medic's hands. Hands that had learned steadiness the hard way, in places where shaking meant someone died. "Where did you learn to hold your breath like that?"

"Lakes. Wrecks. A reservoir in the north with a church steeple sticking out of it." Theo rolled the next length of oakum between her palms, twisting the fiber tight. "My mother used to say I was afraid of depth. Of staying anywhere deep enough to drown in." She snorted. "Turns out I just prefer drowning on my own terms."

She went back down before Imani could decide what to say to that.

* * *

An hour later the seam was caulked, pitched, and sealed, and the *Marrow* floated — not proudly, but honestly, taking no water that the bilge pump couldn't shame back out. They refloated her off the sandbar as the light went long and amber, and Theo ran the engine while Imani crouched in the bilge with her ear against the planking like a doctor listening to a chest.

"Quiet," Imani reported, straightening. "Or as quiet as this thing gets."

"She'll hold. Long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

"For whatever's at the end of this river." Theo throttled up. The channel ahead widened toward evening, the water turning to hammered copper, and somewhere beyond the next bend the monsoon was assembling itself out of heat and ocean, patient as everything else out here. "That's all a boat ever has to do. Hold long enough."

Imani settled onto the stern seat, picked up the bail bucket, considered it, and set it down — the first time she'd let it go since morning. She looked tired in the specific way of someone who had done unfamiliar physical work beside a stranger and discovered she hadn't hated it.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you drive a hull like you were born in one."

"I was born in a hospital in a city eight hundred kilometers from any water."

"Yeah, well." Imani tipped her chin at the river, the banks, the whole indifferent green world sliding past. "Somebody forgot to tell you that."

Theo didn't answer. But she found, to her irritation, that she was smiling at the water ahead of them, and she made herself stop before Imani could turn around and see it.

They made another ten kilometers before the light failed, and anchored in the lee of a bluff where the current slackened. Theo cooked rice over a spirit stove on deck while Imani cleaned and rebandaged a rope burn on her own palm, working with the brisk economy of someone who'd stitched a hundred wounds and considered her own the least interesting. Overhead the first stars came out, and the air had a weight to it it hadn't carried that morning — a pressure behind the ears, a stillness in the reeds.

Imani noticed it too. She stopped mid-bandage, head tilted, listening to a silence that had too much in it.

"Storm coming?" she asked.

"Early one, maybe. Or just the season warming up." Theo ladled rice into two bowls and handed one across. "Why?"

"Because the birds stopped." Imani nodded upstream, where the dusk sky over the distant treeline had gone a strange bruised yellow, and where — Theo saw it now, and her stomach tightened — a single fishing canoe was paddling hard *downriver*, against everything sensible, its occupant bent low and hurrying.

Nobody fled downriver at dusk unless something upstream had frightened them.

Theo ate quickly, watching the yellow deepen in the west, and began, quietly, to plan tomorrow's run in the dark.
