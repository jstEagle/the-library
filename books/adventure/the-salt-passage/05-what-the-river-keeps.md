## 5. What the River Keeps

The trading post announced itself by smell before sight — rot and river mold, the sweet-sour breath of a building that had been left to die. It crouched on the north bank where the channel narrowed, its corrugated roof peeled back like the lid of a tin someone had given up opening. Theo cut the engine two hundred meters out and let the Marrow drift.

"You feel that?" Imani said.

Theo felt it. The current here ran wrong. Not faster — *arranged*, as if something on the bank had been nudging the water's attention toward itself for years. She'd grown up around sites like this. Her mother used to say ruins didn't hide; they *waited*, patient as creditors.

"Someone's been through," Theo said. "Recently."

"The post has been dead since before I was born."

"Dead buildings don't have fresh wake patterns." She pointed with her chin at the mud shelf below the collapsed dock. "See the scuff lines? Two trips, minimum. Heavy loads going up that slope."

Imani shaded her eyes against the flat white glare off the water. "Expedition gear?"

"That's what we're about to find out."

Theo nosed the Marrow in along the downstream edge of the dock, where a stand of drowned saplings would break the wind if it picked up. She tied off with a bowline she could release one-handed — habit from twenty years of boats she hadn't trusted — and stepped onto the mud shelf with her machete already loose in its sheath. Behind her, Imani gathered the medical kit out of reflex, then set it back down, then picked it up again. Theo pretended not to notice. Everyone carried their armor differently.

The slope was worse than it looked. Sixty meters of slick red clay terraced with the stumps of an old stairway, each step holding a few centimeters of soil and a century of leaves. Halfway up, Theo found the first piece: a ration tin, expedition-stamped, pried open and licked clean by weather. Then a boot sole. Then, snagged in a sapling like a flag of surrender, a strip of green nylon — tent fabric, military grade, not the kind archaeologists bought.

"Voss's people travel heavy," Imani said behind her, breathing hard.

"Voss's people travel *armed*." Theo nodded at the nylon. "That's not survey equipment."

They came over the lip of the slope into the trading post's yard, and Theo stopped walking.

Not because of what was there. Because of how it was arranged.

The yard was a rectangle of packed earth maybe forty meters across, ringed by the post's outbuildings — a warehouse with its doors hanging open, a barracks with no roof, a cistern crusted white with old mineral scale. Scattered across it lay the contents of an expedition: crates split open, foam packing blooming out of them like fungus, a disassembled outboard motor, camp chairs folded and abandoned mid-fold. A satellite phone sat face-up in the dirt, its screen spiderwebbed.

It looked like abandonment. It was dressed up to look like abandonment, and that was the difference that raised every hair on Theo's arms. Real abandonment scattered things at angles chaos chose. This had *composition*. The crates were staged in a loose arc, close enough to read as panic, far enough apart that a search party walking the site would find everything within ten minutes and conclude there was nothing else to find. The satellite phone lay where a camera would find it. Someone had built a scene the way her mother built an excavation grid — deliberately, with an eye for what the observer would see first.

"They wanted it discovered," Theo said quietly.

"What?"

"This whole yard. It's a stage set. 'Tragic expedition lost to the river' — gear dumped, camp overwhelmed, everyone presumed dead or dispersed." She turned a slow circle. "Except whoever built it got lazy about the edges. Look at the warehouse."

The warehouse was the largest structure, a cathedral of rotting timber with light falling through gaps in the boards in dusty blades. Inside, the staging fell apart. Crates stacked three high against the far wall — but these weren't opened, weren't scattered. These were *sealed*, stenciled with shipping codes Theo didn't recognize and one mark she did: a small stamped falcon, wings folded. Voss's house mark. She'd seen it once before, years ago, on a crate being winched aboard a private barge in a port she'd promised herself she'd never return to.

She counted. Eleven sealed crates. And beside them, shoved into a corner under a tarpaulin as if someone had meant to come back and couldn't be bothered, a radio mast in three sections, two field radios, a case of signal flares, and — her chest went tight — a document tube, the kind surveyors used for maps, cracked at one end.

Theo knelt and worked the cap off with her thumbs.

Inside: a single page torn from a field journal, spiral-bound edge ragged, water-stained but legible. She knew the handwriting before she finished reading the date. She'd have known it blind, by the pressure of the pen alone — Adaeze Oyelaran wrote like she dug, every stroke committed, no hesitation marks, no second-guessing. Twenty years and an ocean of silence between them, and Theo's body still recognized her mother's hand the way it recognized rain coming.

Her eyes went to the date first. That was the mistake. That was the thing that turned her hands cold in the heat of the warehouse.

The date was nine days after the last confirmed transmission. Nine days after the telegram had said *all contact lost*. Nine days after the world had begun writing the expedition's obituary.

*Day 41. Manifests continue. R. insists the pace is unsustainable but H.V. wants volume before the rains. I have catalogued 214 items to date and remember every one. If I am to sign my name to theft, I will at least know precisely what was stolen. The soldiers rotate every six days. They do not let me near the radios anymore.*

Below that, smaller, pressed so hard the paper had nearly torn:

*If anyone finds this — the route is the narrows. Everything goes downriver through the narrows. Count the crates. Count them and make them count.*

Theo read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. The date didn't change.

"She's alive," Imani said. Not a question. She'd read it over Theo's shoulder, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a medic triaging.

"Nine days after the silence." Theo's own voice sounded foreign to her. "The official line is the team scattered when the storms came. Disbanded. Some story about a dispute — that's what the regional office told the charter pilots. But she was writing manifests. Cataloguing. Day forty-one." She looked up at the sealed crates, the folded-wing falcon stamped on every one. "This isn't a lost expedition. It's a *warehouse*."

"Then why dump the rest? Why stage all this?" Imani gestured at the yard outside, the artful ruin of it.

"To close the trail. Anyone who comes looking finds a tragedy, feels sad, goes home. The story writes itself — famous archaeologist, early monsoon, river takes another one. Poetic. Nobody asks follow-up questions about poetic." Theo stood, knees cracking, and held the page up to a blade of light. "But they missed this. Or they figured a torn journal page in a tube wasn't worth searching for."

"Or," Imani said slowly, "they left it."

Theo looked at her.

"Somebody on the inside," Imani said. "Somebody who wanted a breadcrumb to survive. You don't tear a page out of your own journal and hide it in a map tube unless you're planting evidence. Your mother spent her whole career teaching students how artifacts speak. Maybe she made sure this one would."

The thought landed in Theo's chest like a dropped anchor. Her mother — coerced, guarded, signing manifests at some armed man's insistence — had still been working. Still thinking in layers, in records, in the long game of documentation. Even hostage, even weeping, if the priest on the shrine island had it right, she had done the one thing she'd always done better than anyone alive: she had *catalogued the truth* and hidden it where the truth-tellers would find it.

"We should take everything," Imani was saying, moving through the warehouse with new urgency, checking the radios. "These are expedition units — same frequency band as their field sets. If I can get one powered—"

"Batteries will be dead."

"I brought spares. I told you I was useful." For the first time since the dock, Imani smiled, and it had teeth in it. "Give me an hour."

While Imani worked, Theo walked the yard again, slower now, reading it as a crime scene instead of a grave. The dread that had ridden her since the telegram — the low, tidal dread of *too late, too late* — was changing state. She could feel it crystallizing, dread becoming something with edges. Purpose, maybe. Or fury wearing purpose's clothes. Hard to tell the difference yet. Both kept her hands steady.

She found more at the edges, exactly where the staging got lazy. A cigarette butt, filtered, foreign brand, tucked under a stone — days old, not months. Boot prints in the dried clay at the rear fence line, size twelve, deep-heeled, military pattern, heading not toward the dock but *upstream*, along a game trail. And nailed to the inside of the barracks' last standing wall, half-hidden behind a warped plank, a strip of paper with a schedule penciled on it: rotations, dates, a word repeated every sixth day — *NARROWS* — followed by a tally mark system she didn't understand yet but suspected meant shipments.

Count the crates. Count them and make them count.

She counted the sealed ones again. Eleven. If the manifest numbers in Adaeze's note held — 214 items catalogued — then most of the collection had already moved. Downriver. Through the narrows. Ahead of the storm, exactly as *H.V.* wanted.

Which meant the storm was the deadline. Which meant the monsoon everyone feared was, for once, on her side: whatever hadn't shipped yet was still up there, waiting on weather, guarded by men whose rotation put them at their most careless on day six.

"Imani." Her voice came out rougher than intended. "How fast can you have that radio listening?"

"Why?"

"Because I want to know if anyone upstream is still transmitting. And because—" She stopped. Made herself say it plainly, which was harder. "Because the woman who wrote this page is my mother, and she is alive, and she is a prisoner doing arithmetic with stolen objects, and I have spent twenty years measuring my life so I'd never have to measure the distance back to her. So. How fast."

Imani looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Theo's face seemed to settle something.

"Forty minutes," she said. "Less, if you get me that antenna mast."

They carried the mast down to the boat together, piece by piece, and rigged it lashed to the Marrow's stern rail while the light went copper and long across the water. Imani worked with a soldering iron off the battery bank, tongue between her teeth, cursing gently at corroded contacts. Theo watched the river.

The river didn't care about any of it. It moved brown and enormous and indifferent, carrying its silt, its fish, its drowned history, its secrets — keeping all of them, giving none back except when it chose. Her whole life she'd treated the river the same way she treated her mother: something vast you navigated carefully and never asked to explain itself.

No more. The river had kept a page of her mother's handwriting, and now Theo was going to take it personally.

The radio crackled alive at dusk.

Static first — the long empty hiss of an empty band. Imani swept frequencies with the patience of someone who had done this in worse places. Theo stood over her shoulder, arms crossed, telling herself the tightness in her throat was the smoke from Imani's soldering.

Then, threaded through the static like a vein of ore: a voice.

Low. Controlled. Reading numbers.

Theo's whole body went still.

"—seven, four, one, one. Confirm receipt. Repeat: seven, four, one, one. Manifest continuation follows—"

Adaeze's voice. Older, rougher, flattened by distance and compression and fear — but underneath all of it, unmistakable, the same voice that had read bedtime stories like field reports and excavation notes like prayers. Reading coordinates. Reading *manifests*. Broadcasting into the dark on a band nobody official monitored, in a code Theo didn't understand yet but recognized instantly as a message designed to survive interception — numbers that meant nothing to a guard, everything to whoever held the key.

Holding the key. Holding the *page*. The journal fragment, the route, the counts — her mother had seeded the river with pieces of herself, waiting for someone to assemble them.

Waiting for *her*.

The transmission cut. Not faded — cut, mid-number, the way a hand comes down on a switch. In the sudden static, Theo heard, faint and clipped, a man's voice saying something short and irritated, and then nothing.

Imani lowered the handset slowly. Neither of them spoke. On the bank, a night bird started up its one-note question, and the river answered the only way rivers do.

"She's broadcasting," Theo finally said. "Coordinates. Counts. She's turning herself into a record."

"And someone up there doesn't like it." Imani set the handset down with exaggerated care, as if it might bruise. "That cut wasn't atmospheric. That was a person."

Theo looked upstream, where the channel bent away into a darkness already thickening with the first smell of rain. Somewhere up there: her mother, eleven crates, a garrison of soldiers, a collector's falcon stamped on stolen history, and a countdown measured in storms.

The dread was gone. What replaced it had a direction.

"We run the narrows tomorrow," Theo said. "Before day six."

* * *

They loaded the Marrow by lantern light, working without discussion, the division of labor arriving naturally as if they'd done it for years — Imani sealing the radios in waterproof bags, Theo breaking down the mast and lashing it flat. The journal page Theo kept on her own body, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked inside her shirt against her skin. It felt warm there. It felt like a hand.

Just before she cast off the lantern for the night watch, Imani paused at the gunwale, looking back at the dark shape of the trading post on its hill.

"You know what bothers me," she said. "Whoever staged that yard — they were good. Deliberate. Professional. People like that don't miss a document tube."

Theo had been turning the same thought over. "So either they missed it—"

"Or they wanted it found. By someone specific." Imani's eyes caught the last of the lantern light. "Your mother planted the page. Fine. But someone had to leave the tube where a searcher would look, and someone had to keep the radios behind instead of smashing them. Radios are the first thing you destroy if you're cutting a team off." She tapped the waterproof bag. "These still work, Theo. Somebody left us a working radio in a staged grave site."

The river slid past, black and patient.

"Then somebody up there," Theo said slowly, "is playing both sides."

Imani nodded once, and said nothing more, and the Marrow rode the current through the dark with its small cargo of living voices — a mother's numbers, a stranger's loyalty, a daughter's new and terrible purpose — toward the narrows, and the storm, and whatever waited where the river kept the rest.

Ahead, thunder muttered beyond the hills like something clearing its throat.

Day five was almost over.
