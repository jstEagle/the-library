## 6. Imani's Ledger

The Marrow sat at anchor in a backwater the color of steeped tea, and Imani counted her breaths the way she used to count a patient's pulse — not because anything was wrong, but because counting was how she kept her hands steady.

Theo was asleep in the bow, or pretending to be. She slept like a cat that had learned the world punished deep sleep: one ear open, one hand near the knife she kept under the thwart. Eight days upriver and Imani still didn't know what to make of her. The woman navigated flooded channels by reading the water the way other people read faces, and she talked about her mother the way you'd talk about a war you'd survived — briefly, flatly, with your eyes somewhere else.

Imani opened the oilskin packet for the hundredth time and did not take out its contents. That was the discipline of it. The packet stayed shut until she decided to trust someone, and she had not decided anything in eight years except to keep moving and keep carrying.

But tonight the river was quiet, and Theo Oyelaran had asked her one question too many.

*Why does a disgraced medic know so much about Halvard Voss?*

She'd deflected then. Now, with the mosquitoes singing their thin hymn against the netting and the water slapping the hull like something patient waiting to be let in, she thought maybe it was time to stop deflecting. Not because Theo had earned it. Because Imani was tired of carrying it alone, and tiredness, she'd learned, was how trust actually happened — not as a choice but as a surrender.

* * *

Eight years ago there had been a dig at Kessuma, on a terrace above a river not unlike this one, and Imani Okonkwo had been twenty-nine years old and certain of everything.

She remembered the light of that last morning: white and merciless, the kind of light that made even dust look guilty. She'd been the expedition medic for a consortium-funded survey — Voss money, though at the time the name Halvard Voss meant nothing more to her than a signature on the funding documents, elegant and cold, like a fish spine. Her job was malaria prophylaxis, tetanus boosters, and pulling splinters out of grad students who thought gloves were optional. She was good at it. She liked being needed in small, concrete ways. Medicine was honest work: you saw a wound, you cleaned it, you closed it. Nobody argued with a wound.

The finds started disappearing in the third week.

Not dramatically. Nothing so crude as crates vanishing overnight. It was subtler than that — a catalog discrepancy here, a context number reassigned there. A bronze weight from Trench Four logged as "pending analysis" and never seen again. Two terracotta heads that existed in photographs but not in the finds hut. Imani noticed because she was the one who treated the local laborers, and the laborers talked, and what they said was this: men came at night in a boat with no lights, and Dr. Essien, the site director, signed things he wasn't supposed to sign, and everyone knew and no one said, because the men paid in a currency everyone understood.

She might have let it go. That was the thing she could never forgive herself for considering, later — that some part of her had done the arithmetic and found silence cheaper. But then a boy named Ekon, sixteen, who carried water for the sieves, cut his palm badly on a crate edge while loading that night boat, and came to her tent to be stitched rather than report it, because reporting it meant explaining what he'd been doing.

She stitched his hand by lamplight. He wouldn't meet her eyes. And when she asked him gently whose boat, he said, "Doctor, please. My mother needs the work."

That was the moment. Not outrage — she'd felt plenty of that already and it had gone nowhere. It was the boy's shame. Shame was a wound too, and it doesn't close on its own.

So Imani did what she knew how to do. She documented.

For eleven nights she sat where she could see the landing without being seen, and she wrote down times, boat descriptions, the names of the men she recognized — two of them wore the same gray jackets with a corporate crest she photographed through the tent flap, a stylized wave inside a circle. She copied catalog pages. She photographed the empty slots in the finds registry beside the entries that described objects now missing. She recorded Essien's signature on three manifests that listed destinations no museum would use — a freight forwarding address in a port city, a private villa district, a customs agent's name that appeared on paperwork connected to nothing else.

And then she took it to Dr. Essien himself, because she was twenty-nine and believed institutions worked, and she laid her folder on his desk and said, "We need to talk about what leaves this site at night."

Essien looked at the folder for a long time. Then he looked at her with something worse than anger — with pity, almost tenderness, the way you look at someone standing on ice you know is thin.

"Imani," he said. "Do you know who funds this expedition?"

* * *

In the present, the lamp guttered and Imani turned the wick down. Theo shifted in the bow but didn't wake. Or didn't show that she was awake. With that woman it was impossible to say, and Imani found she didn't much care anymore which it was. Some things you said to the dark, and if the dark happened to have ears, so be it.

The discrediting had been surgical. That was what she wanted Theo to understand, someday, if she ever told her — that they hadn't screamed at her or threatened her. They'd simply made her unbelievable.

First, Essien filed an incident report: Medic I. Okonkwo had been observed self-administering controlled substances from the medical stores. It was a lie built on a truth, which is the strongest kind — she *had* taken stock once without logging it, a bottle of codeine tablets she'd moved between kits during a reorganization, meaning to update the ledger and forgetting. There was a witness. There was always a witness; they'd bought one for less than the cost of the bronzes leaving the site.

Then the consortium's compliance office conducted its review, and the review found that Medic Okonkwo's documentation of "irregularities" coincided suspiciously with her own disciplinary issues, suggesting fabrication motivated by self-protection. Her photographs were dismissed as unverifiable — she'd shot them on her own camera, and the camera's date stamp had been off by a year, a detail she'd never noticed and couldn't defend. Her manifest copies were called forgeries. Her testimony was called unstable. One of the reviewers noted, in language that would follow her through every subsequent job application, concerns about the medic's judgment and emotional regulation.

They didn't fire her loudly. They just stopped calling. Licenses lapsed because the jobs dried up. References went unanswered. Within eighteen months, Imani Okonkwo — who had graduated top of her cohort, who had stitched a boy's hand by lamplight because it was the only decent thing within reach — was working ferry clinics and fishing camps, patching nets and pulling teeth, her name a small bad smell in the rooms where expeditions were staffed.

Ekon's mother lost the work. Imani had checked, years later, through a cousin of a cousin. The family had moved inland. She didn't know if the boy's hand had healed well. She thought about it more often than she admitted, usually at night, usually when she was counting something to keep her hands steady.

And Halvard Voss? She'd looked him up afterward, once she understood whose crest rode on those gray jackets. A collector. A philanthropist, the newspapers said. A man whose foundation funded cultural preservation across half the continent, whose acquisitions were celebrated in capitals she'd never see, whose name opened doors and closed mouths. He had never heard of her. That was the most humiliating part. She had burned her life down against him and she was not even a smudge on his windshield. When she finally got close enough to matter — a hearing, an inquiry, anything — she'd learn otherwise. But that was later. That was the ledger's second volume.

* * *

Theo was awake. Imani knew it the way you know weather has changed — not by any single sign but by the quality of the silence.

"You breathe different when you're deciding whether to say something," Theo said to the darkness. "Faster."

"I'm deciding whether to trust you."

"Don't." Flat, immediate. "I'm not worth it. I burn things, remember?"

"The telegram." Everyone in the river town had known about the woman who burned her mother's telegram on the dock and then stood there staring at the ash like it might rearrange itself into better news. Small towns metabolize grief fast and gossip faster.

"That was one telegram."

"And you've been running since you were seventeen. You think I can't see it? You navigate like someone being chased." Imani sat up. The netting brushed her forehead. "I'm not asking you to be trustworthy, Theo. I'm telling you what I have, and then we'll both know what we are."

She reached under her bunk roll and brought out the oilskin packet. Her hands were steady. Eight years of practice had bought her that much, at least.

"Eight years ago I worked a dig at Kessuma," she said. "Voss money. I documented his people stripping the site at night — bronzes, terracotta, gold weights. Catalogued it, photographed it, copied the manifests. Took it to the site director, who was already bought. Within a month I was a drug thief and a fabricator. My career ended in a committee room I wasn't invited to." She heard her own voice stay level and was grateful to it. "Everyone told me to let it go. Move on. Rebuild. And I did rebuild — I'm here, aren't I, bailing out your leaking boat. But I never gave them back the originals."

She untied the packet's cord. Inside: a fat bundle wrapped in waxed cloth, and beneath it, flat and square-cornered, a metal document box the size of a thick book. She set the box on the deck between them without opening it. Let it sit there in the lamplight like a heart on a table.

"Copies of everything," she said. "Photographs. Manifests in Essien's hand. Shipping routes. Names of the middlemen — two of whom are still in the trade, still running river cargo for the same buyer. And this—" she touched the box "—is eight years of follow-up. Every shipment I could trace. Every auction lot that surfaced with no provenance and matched my photographs. Every shell company between the river and the showroom. I'm not a medic anymore, Theo. I'm a bookkeeper of crimes nobody would read."

Theo had come upright at some point without seeming to move, the way she did everything. In the low light her face was all planes and shadow, and her eyes were fixed on the box the way she'd looked at the drowned wreck outside the harbor — assessing, hungry, careful.

"Why tell me now?" Theo asked. Not hostile. Genuinely asking, which was rarer and more dangerous.

"Because tomorrow we pass the narrows where Voss's guards turn boats back, and after that everything we do could get us killed. You deserve to know what you're carrying upriver besides yourself." Imani turned the box slowly, the way you'd turn a key. "And because I need to know if you're the kind of person who, when handed the only evidence that can destroy a powerful man, thinks first about what it's worth."

"To whom?"

"To anyone. To Voss himself. To the people downstream who'd pay to make it disappear." She held Theo's gaze. "I watched a whole expedition sell itself for less than this box weighs. I'm not going to assume you're different just because you're angry at your mother. Anger's a currency too. I've seen what it buys."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere across the water a night bird made its one note and quit.

Theo reached out — and Imani's pulse jumped, absurdly, like a girl's — but Theo didn't touch the box. She put her hand flat on the deck beside it, an inch away, touching nothing.

"It's yours," Theo said. "Whatever happens, it stays yours. If it comes down to the boat or the box, I throw the boat over." A pause, and then, drier: "It leaks anyway."

Something loosened in Imani's chest that she refused to call relief. Trust extended as a test, she'd told herself — and here was the result, unglamorous and unexpected: the salvage diver didn't want her evidence. She wanted Imani to keep it. Which meant either honesty or the best acting Imani had ever seen, and she'd spent eight years learning to spot the difference.

"There's more," Imani said. "The reason I followed Voss's trail to this river isn't just Kessuma. Three months ago, a lot surfaced at a private sale in Europe. A bronze leopard, Edo work, documented provenance going back to a dig that officially never recovered it. My photographs match it. Same casting flaw, same patina pattern. And the sale catalogue thanked one consulting scholar for authentication." She watched Theo's face do the thing it did when it didn't want to do anything at all. "Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran."

The silence that followed had weight, like water overhead.

"No," Theo said.

"The catalogue's public. I can show you—"

"*No.*" But it wasn't denial, Imani realized. It was the sound of a person taking a blow and refusing to fall in front of witnesses. Theo stared at the lamp flame, jaw working. "She spent thirty years screaming about provenance. She ruined friendships over sloppy chain-of-custody. She—" The voice caught and was dragged back under control. "You're saying her name is on stolen work."

"I'm saying her name is on a document. Whether she signed it free is the question we're rowing toward."

Theo was quiet a long time. Then she reached past the box, past Imani, and pulled the anchor line taut, checking it — a useless gesture, the anchor had held for hours, but her hands needed work the way other people's needed words.

"Then we go get her answer," she said. "And Voss's guards at the narrows can try to stop us."

Imani lay back down and pulled the cloth over the box, and above the netting the sky had begun, very faintly, in the east, to change color — not toward dawn but away from it, a bruised green-gray gathering at the horizon like something rising from under the skin of the world.

The monsoon was coming early. And for the first time in eight years, Imani Okonkwo was exactly where she intended to be.
