## 7. The First Monsoon Wind

The sky turned the color of bruised fruit an hour before the wind arrived, and I knew what it meant the way a dog knows a beating is coming — not through thought, but through the body's older intelligence.

I was at the Marrow's tiller when it started, watching the western horizon go the color of a wound. The river had been running high for two days, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past us like the water was clearing its throat before it spoke. Imani sat amidships with her back against the fuel crate, cataloging what we'd found at the trading post: the radios, the crates stamped with the expedition's mark, the single page of Adaeze's handwriting dated nine days after the world was told she'd gone silent.

*Someone wanted the world to think we vanished.* She'd written it in the journal's margin, in the looping, impatient script I'd spent my childhood watching form over dinner tables and dig sites and the margins of other people's maps.

"Storm's early," Imani said. It wasn't a question. She'd been a field medic long enough to read weather the way she read symptoms.

"Monsoon's not due for another ten days."

"Tell the sky that."

I told the sky nothing. I put the Marrow's nose toward the eastern bank, where the chart showed a cluster of fishing stilt-villages — seasonal camps, mostly, but this far upriver the charts were suggestions more than promises. The wind arrived while I was still choosing my line, and it arrived sideways.

The first squall didn't hit like weather. It hit like intent. Rain came down in sheets you could lean against, and the river — which had been running high and mean all morning — stood up in whitecaps the size of dogs. I killed the engine and let us drift rather than fight it, because fighting brown water in a wind like that is how boats die, and I'd already buried one boat's worth of money in this hull.

"Take the bailer," I shouted over the roar. "Both hands."

Imani bailed. I steered by feel and by the shape of the treeline, which I could just make out through the rain — a black serrated edge against a sky gone the color of a fresh bruise. The Marrow took water over the gunwale twice, and both times I felt the river testing us, the way it tests everything, patient and impersonal, asking: *are you worth keeping?*

We found the village by smell before we found it by sight — woodsmoke and fish guts and the particular green rot of flooded reeds. Then the stilts resolved out of the rain, a dozen houses on poles above the water, connected by walkways, and a long covered platform at the center that might have been a drying rack or a meeting place. Dogs started barking before we were within a hundred meters, and by the time I brought the Marrow alongside the lowest walkway, half the village had come out to look at us.

They looked at us the way river people look at strangers in storm season: without hostility, without welcome. Assessment. Two old men with machetes. A woman with a baby slung on her hip. Children hanging off the walkway rails like monkeys, thrilled by anything that interrupted the boredom.

I held up both hands, empty. "Storm shelter," I said in the trade tongue, the rough pidgin that runs the length of this river like a second current. "We pay for the platform. We leave when the water drops."

The older of the two men looked at the Marrow — at the patched seam, at the water still sloshing in the bilge — and made a sound that might have been a laugh. He said something to the woman with the baby, and she went up the walkway without hurrying, because nobody hurries in a fishing village, and came back with a coil of rope.

We tied off. We climbed. We were, for the moment, dry.

* * *

The platform turned out to be the village's everything — fish-drying racks at one end, a cold hearth at the other, nets in various states of repair hung from the crossbeams like the shed skins of enormous animals. The rain hammered the corrugated roof hard enough to make conversation a shouting exercise, so for the first hour we didn't talk. Imani helped the women rehang nets that had blown down. I helped the men drag a half-sunk canoe out of the flood and turn it to drain. Nobody asked our names. In storm season, names are a luxury. Labor is the currency, and we paid it gladly, because labor was warmer than sitting still.

The old woman came out when the light started going.

She was small the way river elders are small — compacted, like driftwood, all the softness weathered off years ago. She moved with a stick but didn't seem to need it. She sat down on the platform's edge with her feet hanging over the floodwater, watched me coil rope for a while, and then said, in the trade tongue, with an accent so old it predated the trade itself:

"You run a boat like a woman who learned from a man who hated the river."

I looked at her. She looked back. Her eyes had the particular opacity of eyes that have decided you're not worth lying to, only telling the truth to.

"I learned from a woman," I said. "She hated the river too."

"Hm." She nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd suspected about the whole species. "And who taught her?"

The question sat in the air between us, and I felt the old reflex — the flinch, the deflection, the invented name. Twenty years of giving the wrong name at docks and border posts and salvage offices, twenty years of being nobody's daughter on paper, and here was a woman the size of a child asking me the one question I'd spent two decades learning not to answer.

"Oyelaran," I said. "Adaeze Oyelaran. She's my mother."

The stick tapped the platform twice. The old woman's face didn't change, but something behind it did — a door opening somewhere deep in the house of her attention.

"Oyelaran," she repeated. "The one who reads the ground."

"You know her."

"Everyone on this river knows the one who reads the ground." She said it without admiration or contempt, the way you'd say *everyone knows the flood*. "She came through here. Five weeks ago, maybe six. The big boat with the metal roof. The one with the generators."

My hands went still on the rope. "She stopped here?"

"She asked to stop here. Her soldiers wanted to keep moving." The old woman said *soldiers* the way Imani said *Voss* — flat, careful, a word handled with gloves. "They had guns. Not army guns. The other kind. Clean, new, and men who carried them like they were carrying someone else's temper."

The rain filled the silence for a moment. Down the platform, Imani had stopped working. She was standing very still with a net in her hands, and I knew she'd heard every word, because Imani missed nothing; it was the occupational habit of a woman who'd once been destroyed by what she failed to document in time.

"Adaeze Oyelaran came to this platform," the old woman continued, "and she sat where you're sitting, and she asked about the old trade routes. The salt passage. Where the river narrows and the old people used to carry their goods overland to avoid the rapids." She tapped her own chest with the stick. "I told her. I'm the last one who knows the whole of it. She wrote everything down. She wrote like a woman writing a letter she expected someone to steal."

"Did she say why she needed the routes?"

The old woman looked at me for a long time. In that look I felt her deciding something — weighing me against some scale calibrated over eighty years of floods.

"She said she needed to know where the river could be avoided." A pause. "She said it like a woman who was planning an escape she hadn't told anyone about. And then the soldier with the scarred face came and stood behind her, and she stopped talking like that and started talking like a teacher. Like she was giving a lecture. And the light went out of her face the way you blow out a lamp."

I heard my own pulse. The rain on the roof. Somewhere below, the river grinding past with its cargo of trees.

"Soldiers," I said. "How many?"

"Eight. Maybe ten. They rotated — some went downriver, some came up. They treated this village like a tollgate. Anything we caught, we sold to them at their price. They bought fish, fuel, silence." She smiled without warmth. "We sold them the silence at a fair price. We're not stupid people."

"And the expedition — the scientists, the students. Did you see them?"

"Some. Young ones. Scared ones. They looked at the soldiers the way goats look at the knife." She shrugged. "The woman who reads the ground kept them close. She counted them every morning, I saw her do it. Like a woman counting her own heartbeats."

Imani had drifted closer without seeming to move. She lowered herself onto the platform edge on the old woman's other side, and the three of us sat in a row above the floodwater like the world's grimmest fishing crew.

"The manifest crates," Imani said. "The ones stamped with the expedition mark. Did they go downriver?"

"Every week. The metal-roof boat runs them down at night, before the storms." The old woman spat into the flood, a small dark courtesy to the river. "They told the village it was equipment. But equipment doesn't need four men with guns to guard it, and equipment doesn't get wrapped in cloth the way the dead are wrapped." She looked at me sideways. "You're the daughter. You have her eyes when she was angry, which I saw once. What do you want to know, daughter-of-the-ground-reader?"

I wanted to know everything. I wanted to know the scarred soldier's name, the schedule of the metal-roof boat, the exact words Adaeze had used, whether she'd looked well, whether she'd looked afraid, whether she'd looked like a woman who remembered she had a daughter somewhere downriver. I wanted to know if she'd asked about me, and I knew she wouldn't have, and I knew that not-knowing was the worst part — worse than any answer, because it left room for both hope and its opposite.

"The salt passage," I said instead, because that was the question that kept us alive. "You told her the routes. Can you tell me?"

The old woman was quiet for a long moment. The rain slackened, the way squalls do, into a steady grey drizzle, and the light on the water went from bruise to pewter.

"I'll tell you what I told her," she said finally. "But you should know something first." She turned and looked at me full-on, and her eyes, for all their age, were not cloudy at all. "The woman who reads the ground cried when I showed her the old shrine island on my map. Right there on the paper. She cried and she didn't wipe her face, and she said, *my daughter would know this place, she'd have loved this place.* And then the scarred man came, and she folded the map, and she was a professor again."

She reached over and gripped my wrist with a hand like a root. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"This is not a robbery you're chasing," she said. "Soldiers don't guard fish. Soldiers guard the thing that ends careers, ends villages, ends bloodlines. The men who run this river now, they don't fear the police. They don't fear the government. The government eats from their hand. You understand? Whatever you're going upriver to do, you're not going to do it with a patched boat and a brave face."

"Then what do I do it with?"

She released my wrist. She looked out at the river, at the pewter light dying on it, at the far bank where the drowned forest stood up to its shoulders in the flood.

"You do it the way the river does," she said. "You don't fight the bank. You find the channel. And you wait for the water to do the heavy work."

* * *

They gave us the platform for the night, and the storm gave us its full attention until well past dark — a proper monsoon preview, the kind of rain that makes you understand why river gods were invented. I lay on a fish-drying rack with a tarp over me and listened to the water rise. I could hear it rising. That was the thing nobody who hadn't lived on a river could understand: you could hear the flood coming up the banks, a sound like slow applause.

Imani appeared out of the dark and sat down against the rack's leg, close enough to talk without shouting now that the rain had settled into its long grey rhythm.

"Ten soldiers," she said. "Rotating shifts. Nightly cargo runs. A coerced signature on the manifests." She was laying it out the way she'd lay out symptoms, clinical, building a diagnosis. "That's not an expedition that went rogue. That's infrastructure."

"I know."

"Voss doesn't hire soldiers for artifacts he's buying legally. He hires them for artifacts he's stealing, and for the people who have to sign the papers that make the stealing look like science." She paused. "Your mother's signature is on those manifests. I saw the copies in the trading-post crates. Every shipment, signed and dated. Nine weeks of them."

"Coerced."

"Coerced still moves cargo." Imani's voice was gentle in the way that only truly unsentimental people can be gentle. "I'm not saying it to hurt you. I'm saying it because if we're going to do what I think you're going to do, we need to know exactly what we're walking into. She's not a prisoner in a cell, Theo. She's a prisoner in a paperwork. They've made her the expedition. Her name is the license. As long as she signs, the whole machine runs, and as long as the machine runs, she stays alive. The day she stops signing—"

"I know what happens the day she stops signing."

Imani was quiet. The river rose. Somewhere out in the dark, a hippo blew air through the floodwater, a sound like a bellows the size of a house.

"She asked about the salt passage," I said, after a while. "The overland routes. Where the river can be avoided."

"You think she was planning a run for it."

"I think she was counting her people every morning and memorizing escape routes and writing everything down like she expected it to be stolen." I stared up at the underside of the tarp, at the rain-shadow moving across it. "That's what she does. That's what she's always done. She doesn't panic. She catalogs. She's probably got every crate, every artifact, every guard rotation written down somewhere in that camp, in that handwriting, waiting for the day she can hand it to someone." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She built a twenty-year career on being the person who knows everything about a site. She's doing it from inside a cage."

"And she cried," Imani said quietly. "When she saw the shrine island on the map. And said her daughter would have loved it."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. Twenty years of distance, and my mother had sat on this same platform, held by the same root-handed old woman, and spoken my name to a stranger in a voice that — I made myself not imagine the voice. There would be time for the voice later, if there was a later, if the river gave us one.

"The old woman's right," Imani said. "This is bigger than looting. Armed men, government silence, a collector who buys whole rivers. We're not chasing a salvage fee anymore, Theo. We're — " she searched for the word, and I watched her find it and decide to say it anyway — "we're in it. Whatever it is."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've watched you since the trading post. You're steering like a woman planning a rescue. Rescues get people killed. What we might actually be able to do is smaller and harder: get her out, get the evidence out, and let the big machine break on its own weight. Not a rescue. An extraction of one woman and a box of paper."

"And the artifacts?"

Imani was silent for a moment. "The artifacts are already gone, Theo. Nine weeks of shipments. Whatever's left at that camp is the tail end of it. You can't save the collection. You can save the woman who can testify to every piece of it."

Out in the dark, the river kept rising. I lay there and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the telegram: the metal-roof boat, the night runs, the storm season closing like a door. If the monsoon came early — and it had just announced that it intended to — then the shipments would get rushed. And rushed shipments meant mistakes, and mistakes meant opportunity, and opportunity on this river came wrapped in the same water that kills you.

"Imani," I said. "The old woman's map. The salt passage. Where the river can be avoided."

"Yes."

"If the soldiers are running cargo at night, ahead of the storm, they're using the main channel. Deep water, fast runs." I turned my head to look at her in the dark. "Which means nobody's watching the old routes."

Imani went very still, the way she went still when a diagnosis came together.

"You want to take the Marrow up the passage the old woman described."

"I want to know it before we need it. Every channel, every carry-over, every place the river can be avoided." I looked back up at the tarp. "My mother asked about those routes five weeks ago because she was planning something. I intend to know them better than she does."

The rain drummed. The river rose. And somewhere upriver, in a camp I couldn't yet see, my mother was counting her people in the morning light, writing everything down, waiting for water to do the heavy work.

Sleep didn't come. Around what I guessed was the third hour, the old woman came out onto the platform with two cups of something hot and bitter and sat between us without being asked, and the three of us drank in silence like women keeping the same vigil.

When she left, she paused at the platform's edge and said, without turning around:

"The scarred one comes back through here in three days. To buy fuel and silence. If you're going to be ghosts, be gone before then."

I was awake for the rest of it, listening to the river rise, doing the arithmetic of three days.

Three days to learn a drowned passage, patch a leaking boat, and become ghosts — before the man with the scarred face came looking for anything upriver that shouldn't be.
