## 8. Soldiers of the Collector

The narrows announced themselves before we saw them: a smell of woodsmoke where there should have been only water and wet green, and then a thread of gray smoke rising off the far bank, thin and deliberate, the kind of signal fire that isn't signaling anything except *we are here*.

I cut the engine to half and let the Marrow drift.

Ahead, the river pinched between two ridges into a channel maybe sixty meters wide — the only deep-water route north for the next forty kilometers. Everything else was floodplain forest and oxbow graves, water that looked passable and wasn't. I'd run this stretch twice in my twenties, hauling diesel drums for a mission station that had since washed away, and I remembered it as a throat: fast in the middle, dead water along the edges, one honest line through.

Now there was a boat anchored mid-channel. A patrol skiff, military-green, with a mounted gun that didn't need to be manned to make its point. Two more men stood on the near bank under a tarp shelter, and as I watched, a dugout canoe loaded with fish turned away from the narrows, its owner paddling back downstream without argument. Turned around at a hundred meters out, like he'd done it before. Like everyone did.

"Checkpoint," Imani said from under the canopy. She'd been sleeping sitting up, her malaria medication making her dreams strange, and she came awake the way she did everything now — quietly, completely, no wasted motion. "That's not a government post."

"No."

"Government posts have flags." She crawled forward on her elbows to look over the gunwale. "And they don't turn people away. They tax them and wave them through. Whoever's running that wants the river empty."

"Voss," I said.

"Voss," she agreed. "He doesn't want witnesses going upstream. He wants the road behind him swept clean before the storm season closes everything anyway. Smart. Ugly, but smart."

I studied the water instead of answering. That was the discipline of piloting: when your chest gets loud, look at the river. The river never lies about what it is.

The main channel was out. Even if I could bluff past the skiff — and I couldn't; we had no cargo worth declaring, no papers worth showing, and a woman aboard whose face was still known in certain provincial offices from eight years ago — even then, they'd log us. A name, a direction, a time. And somewhere ahead, men who answered to Halvard Voss would hear that a leaky salvage boat with two women aboard had come asking questions with its nose pointed at his camp.

There was another way. There is almost always another way; it's just worse.

* * *

"Tell me no," I said, "and I won't do it."

We were anchored in a backwater two kilometers downstream, hidden under overhanging fig branches while I spread my father's old survey charts across the deck boards and weighted the corners with spark plugs. The paper was soft as cloth from twenty years of folding. My father had drawn these channels himself, soundings taken by hand from a canoe, back when he still worked rivers instead of just dreaming about them. Adaeze Oyelaran's husband, the cartographer who married up and knew it. He'd been dead eleven years and his ink was still more reliable than any government hydrographic survey printed since.

Imani leaned over my shoulder. Her finger found the left bank, upstream of the narrows, where the chart showed a confusion of dashed lines threading through what the legend called *seasonal swamp — unreliable*.

"The Kiri bypass," she said.

"You know it?"

"I ferried supplies through it once, for a clinic. In the dry season." She paused. "It was barely a channel then. Knee-deep in places. Now, with the rains starting early—" She looked up at me. "It'll be deeper. It'll also be full of things that were dry land three weeks ago. Trees. Fences. Stakes."

"Fishing stakes," I said. "That's what I'm afraid of. Hardwood, driven into the mud years ago, invisible under floodwater until they come up through your hull like a spear." I tapped the chart. "Every village along here plants them. And nobody's pulled the ones in a swamp nobody navigates."

"So we go at night."

"At night. Slow. Pole and paddle, engine off past the first bend — the sound carries too far, and if there are stakes, I need to hear them before I hit them." I traced the dashed line with my thumbnail. "Six kilometers of this. Then it dumps back into the main river above the checkpoint, and we're through."

"And if we're not through. If we open the hull in the middle of a flooded swamp at night."

I folded the chart. "Then we swim, and the Marrow becomes a reef, and you tell me honestly whether your ledger would've been better off with a faster boat."

She didn't laugh. She looked at me for a long moment with an expression I was starting to recognize — the one where she decided something about me and filed it away without saying what. Then she said, "I'll bail. You steer. And Theo — if I go in the water, I can't swim well with this fever. Don't be noble about it. Be fast."

"Noted," I said, and meant it, and hated how much I meant it.

* * *

We waited for full dark, which this close to the equator means waiting for the sky to fall shut like a lid rather than fade. No twilight to speak of. One hour the clouds held the last bruised light of the squall season; the next, black, total, the river a sound instead of a sight.

I went in with the pole first, standing at the bow, reading the water by feel and by ear. This was the oldest kind of piloting, older than engines, older than charts — the kind my father taught me before he taught me anything else, before the marriage soured and the distance grew. *Listen for the change*, he used to say, poling me down some drowned creek when I was small enough to stand between his knees. *Flat water sounds flat. Water over a snag sounds like it's chewing.* I was thirty-eight years old and I could still hear him saying it, and I still checked every current twice because somewhere in my chest a seven-year-old wanted him to say *good girl* one more time.

You carry your parents in the strangest places.

The first kilometer was merciful. Flooded grassland, the channel wide and slow, our wake whispering through stems that should have been a field. Imani sat aft with a paddle and a flashlight she was forbidden to use, and the Marrow moved like a ghost through a drowned world where frogs sang from every side at once.

The second kilometer lied to us.

The channel narrowed without warning — not gradually, but all at once, as if the swamp had made a decision. Trees closed overhead. The water went from brown glass to moving shadow, and I felt the current pick up against the pole, pushing us sideways toward a wall of submerged brush, and I understood that the flood had rearranged everything. My father's chart described a channel that no longer existed. The river had redrawn it in the three weeks since the early rains, the way it always does, the way it punishes anyone who trusts paper over instinct.

"Paddle," I breathed. "Hard left. Follow my voice."

She paddled. She was good — better than I expected, a steady deep stroke with no splash, and I filed that away too: wherever Imani had spent her eight lost years, boats had been part of it.

The third kilometer nearly killed us.

I heard it before I saw it, which is the only reason we lived. A sound in the current ahead — not the chewing of a snag, but a hiss, a thin organized tearing, like water being combed. I jammed the pole down and hit bottom sooner than I wanted, which told me the channel was shallowing, and the pole tip rang against something that was not mud.

"Back water," I whispered. "Back. Back."

Imani reversed stroke. The Marrow slid to a stop in water that was combing itself around us, and I leaned out over the bow into the dark and put my hand flat on the surface and felt it: a line of vibration running crosswise through the current, the signature of water parting around vertical objects. Stakes. A whole rank of them, driven in a row across the old channel — someone's fish fence, abandoned or active, hardwood tops just under the flood line, sharpened or broken to points.

If we'd been motoring, we'd have hit them at six knots and gone down in ninety seconds.

"Feel that?" I breathed.

"I feel the boat shaking."

"That's the fence. There's a gap somewhere — there's always a gap, the fishermen leave a gate to run their own nets through." I poled us sideways, inch by inch, my hand trailing in the water, reading vibrations like a blind woman reads a page. The hiss ran on. Ran on. Then, near the right bank, it stuttered — a break in the texture, a slack pocket where the current eddied instead of tore.

"There. Paddle easy. Trust me."

She trusted me. I felt the boat answer her strokes, felt the gap take us — a passage barely wider than the Marrow's beam, walled by stakes I could sense but not see, close enough that their points trailed the hull planks with a sound like fingernails. Imani held her breath. I held mine. For ten seconds we moved through a corridor built by strangers for fish, guided by nothing but my hand in the black water and whatever stubborn inheritance lives in my wrists.

Then the gap released us, and the channel opened, and we were through.

* * *

We didn't speak for a long time after that. The adrenaline has to burn off before language works again; anyone who tells you different has never been afraid properly.

At kilometer five the trees opened onto flooded savanna and, briefly, a sky. Clouds had shredded enough to let a few stars through, and the swamp lay silvered and endless around us, and I realized my hands were shaking on the pole — not from fear anymore, but from the sheer animal expenditure of the last hour, every muscle I owned spent and invoiced.

Imani shipped her paddle. "Your mother," she said quietly.

"What about her?"

"She ran sites like this. Off-map. Everyone said she was reckless — that she took routes the careful expeditions wouldn't touch." Imani's voice was careful, the way it got when she was handling something fragile. "But watching you tonight. That wasn't recklessness. That was craft. You read that water like it was talking to you personally."

"Don't."

"I'm not flattering you. I'm telling you what I saw."

"Well, stop seeing it." The words came out harder than I intended, and the frogs went quiet around us, offended. I stood with the pole planted in the mud and stared at the stars and tried to find a version of this conversation I could survive. "Everyone spent thirty years telling me I was her. Every teacher, every journalist, every drunk at every dock from here to the coast — *you must be Adaeze's daughter, do you dig too?* Like I was a sequel. Like the only interesting thing about me was whose womb I came out of. So I became the opposite. Salvage. Wrecks. Dead men's cargo. Nothing that anyone could compare to her life's work." I pulled the pole free, dripping. "And tonight I'm running flooded swamps at night to chase her down a river, using her husband's charts, and I'm good at it, and I hate that I'm good at it, because it means they were right. It means I was always just her, wearing a different job."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, a hippo blew air through the dark like a bellows.

"For what it's worth," Imani said finally, "I spent eight years being the woman who cried wolf. One report, one career, finished. So I stopped reporting anything. Stopped testifying, stopped publishing, stopped saying what I knew to anyone who could use it against me. I became very good at silence." She looked at me across the dark deck. "And now I'm poling through a swamp to hand my evidence to a stranger so she can burn down the man who made me quiet. We're both doing the thing that ruined us. Because it's also the thing we're best at."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't true, and the truth was too big to fit through my mouth yet.

But I thought about my father's hands over mine on the pole, and my mother's hands — I hadn't seen them in nine years, but I remembered them, ink-stained, quick, turning a potsherd in the light like it was telling her a joke — and I thought: *maybe competence isn't a cage. Maybe it's a compass.* Maybe the thing they gave me, the thing I'd spent two decades treating like a hereditary disease, was just the family tool kit, passed down whether I signed for it or not.

Adaeze Oyelaran read ground the way I read water. That was all. That was everything.

"Kilometer six," I said. "Main river ahead. Sleep if you can. I'll take us out."

* * *

The bypass dumped into the narrows' upper reach exactly where the chart promised, and the checkpoint fires glowed downstream around the bend, guarding a door we'd already walked through. The patrol skiff sat anchored and bored, its crew smoking, watching an empty channel for travelers who had all sensibly turned back hours ago.

We drifted past in the last of the starlight, engine cold, paddle silent, two ghosts and a leaking boat.

It was Imani who saw it first. She touched my arm and pointed upstream, to the far bank, where the ridge dropped to the water — and where, moored in a cut in the mangroves with its lights doused, sat a barge. Not a fishing barge. A working barge, steel-hulled, with crated cargo stacked amidships under tarps lashed down against weather, and guards on the deck with rifles slung across their chests, and a generator humming somewhere below the sound of the current.

Crates. Dozens of them. The shape and size of artifact crates — I'd grown up around that shape; I knew it the way I knew my own hands.

"They're already moving it downriver," Imani whispered. "Ahead of the storm. Voss isn't waiting for the monsoon to finish the job — he's shipping early, in batches, under cover of night."

The barge sat dark and patient in the mangroves, loaded with everything her mother had spent her life protecting, and above it, on the ridge road, headlights swung — a truck, descending toward the water, carrying more.

"We're through the checkpoint," I said softly, poling us into the shadow of the bank, my heart hammering a pilot's rhythm — *current, cargo, current, cargo*. "Now we follow the crates."
