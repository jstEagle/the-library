## 9. The Shrine Island

The soldiers' lanterns were still moving along the far bank when Imani spotted the island.

She said nothing at first. She'd learned that skill in hospitals and in Voss's camps — the ability to see a thing and hold it behind her teeth until the moment was right. But Theo was already looking at it too, her jaw tight, hands steady on the Marrow's wheel the way they only got steady when she was deciding something dangerous.

"That's a shrine island," Theo said.

"You can tell from here?"

"The egrets." Theo nodded at the white birds standing in the shallows on the upstream side, unbothered, dozens of them. "Nobody hunts near a shrine. Birds know where they're safe. So do people who need to be."

Imani looked at the woman she'd known for six days — a woman who measured trust in nautical miles and paid for everything in cash so no name would follow her — and thought: *you know this river the way I used to know triage.* Instinct first. Justification later, if there was time.

"They'll have seen us pass the narrows," Imani said. "Even if they didn't stop us, someone will report a boat running dark."

"Then we don't stop anywhere with a reportable name." Theo eased the throttle down until the engine dropped to a mutter. "No villages. No trading posts. Just—" she tipped her chin at the island, "—that."

"It's sacred ground. We can't just land on sacred ground."

"We can ask."

*We can ask.* As if the whole world operated on the same terms Theo did: knock, state your business plainly, accept the answer. Imani had spent eight years learning that the world did not work that way — that the world worked on ledgers and leverage and men like Halvard Voss who never knocked at all. And yet here was this salvage diver, proposing courtesy as a survival strategy against armed patrols.

It shouldn't have made sense. It did.

Theo cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the current carry them, silent, around the island's downstream shoulder. The Marrow slid through water the color of steeped tea, past mangrove roots like knuckles gripping the mud, and into a narrow channel where the canopy closed overhead and the air went cool and green-smelling. Imani kept one hand on their single bag — the radio wrapped in oilcloth, her ledger copies sealed in wax inside a tin that had once held sardines, everything they owned that mattered weighing less than a child.

The shrine announced itself before they saw it: carved posts rising from the water at the channel's mouth, each one crowned with a bowl of clay, some holding offerings gone soft with rain. Then the trees opened onto a clearing of packed earth, and there it was — not a temple, exactly, but a long low house with a thatched roof and walls of woven palm, facing the water the way old buildings do when they've watched centuries go by. A fire pit. Drying racks. A canoe pulled up under a lean-to.

And a man standing at the waterline, waiting for them, as if he'd been expecting them all day.

He was old — older than he moved, which is its own kind of frightening — with a close-cropped white beard and a fisherman's build gone lean with age. He wore a plain shirt rolled to the elbows and nothing that marked him as clergy or caretaker or anything else. He watched the Marrow drift in without alarm, without curiosity even, the way you watch weather arrive.

Theo killed the engine and let the boat bump gently against the mud. For a moment nobody spoke. The egrets called somewhere behind them, thin and metallic.

"You're running from the boats with guns," the man said. Not a question.

"Yes," Theo said.

"You're not soldiers. You're not smugglers. Smugglers don't look tired." He considered them both. "You eat fish?"

"I—" Theo blinked. "Yes?"

"Then come up. You can pull your boat into the reeds where the patrol boats won't see it. They don't come past the offering posts anyway." He turned and walked toward the fire pit, conversation apparently concluded. "The fish are small this season. The river is angry early."

Imani caught Theo's eye. Theo shrugged — the universal gesture of a woman who has stopped being surprised by anything — and they hauled the Marrow into the reeds, shouldered their bag, and followed an old priest into sanctuary they hadn't asked for and couldn't explain.

His name was Ekwueme. He would not say how long he had kept the island. When Imani asked, he said, "Longer than the road, shorter than the river," and declined to elaborate, and something about his tone suggested the elaboration existed and was enormous.

* * *

They ate smoked fish and pounded yam with a pepper sauce that made Imani's eyes water and her chest feel briefly, absurdly happy. Theo ate like a person refueling machinery — fast, efficient, grateful in a wordless way — while Ekwueme watched them over the fire with the patience of someone whose days were measured in tides.

"You're going upriver," he said eventually. "Past the narrows."

"How do you know?" Theo asked.

"Everyone who comes here now is going upriver or hiding from whoever is upriver. There is no other direction anymore." He poked the coals. Sparks rose and died among the smoke. "The soldiers came through three weeks ago. Before them, the expedition. Before the expedition, surveyors drawing maps of channels that were mapped two hundred years ago. Everyone suddenly interested in my quiet stretch of water." His voice stayed level, but Imani heard the thing underneath it, the thing she'd heard in displaced villagers and burned-out medics: grief wearing the mask of calm. "They ask questions. They take answers. They leave."

"What did the expedition want?" Imani asked carefully.

Ekwueme looked at her for a long moment. Firelight moved in the deep lines of his face.

"You're not with them," he said. It wasn't a question either. This man, Imani realized, dealt almost entirely in statements shaped like questions, because he already knew the answers. "You came asking about them, though. The woman. Your face changed when I said the word expedition."

Imani felt Theo go still beside her — not visibly, but she'd spent enough hours pressed against this woman in a leaking boat to feel it, the sudden absence of motion, the held breath.

"There was a woman," Ekwueme continued, "who came by canoe six weeks ago. Older. Straight back like she was carrying a load on her head even empty-handed. She sat where you're sitting and ate my fish and asked about the old trade routes."

Imani heard Theo's breath go out slowly through her nose.

"She wanted to know which passages the salt traders used in the dry years," Ekwueme said. "When the river drops and the main channel dies, how did the old ones move their goods? She wrote down everything I told her. In a book. With a pencil she kept sharpening with a knife, always sharpening, like the pencil might escape her." He smiled faintly at some private memory. "She talked to me for two days. Most visitors talk for ten minutes and want me to confirm what they already decided. This one listened. She argued, too — politely. She thought my grandfather's route through the Sorrow Channel was a story we told to keep treasure hunters off the real one. I told her the Sorrow Channel is real and it is exactly as sorrowful as it sounds. She laughed. A real laugh. She said, 'My daughter would like you. She doesn't believe anything anyone says either.'"

Beside Imani, Theo made a sound — very small, quickly swallowed, the kind of sound a person makes when a door they'd bricked over turns out to be open.

Ekwueme either didn't notice or was kind enough to pretend not to. "She stayed two nights. On the second night the rains came early, the first squall, and she stood out in it at the waterline for an hour. Soaked through. Watching the river."

"Why?" Theo asked. Her voice came out rough.

"I asked her the same thing." Ekwueme fed a stick to the fire. "She said — I remember exactly, because it was a strange thing to say — she said, 'I'm trying to decide if water is a wall or a door.' And then she said, 'It depends which side you're standing on,' and went inside and slept like a stone."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, the river moved with its low continuous voice, the sound Imani had begun to hear even in her sleep, the sound that had replaced eight years of hospital corridors.

"What happened to her?" Theo asked. "After she left here."

Ekwueme looked at her then — really looked, the way doctors are trained to look, and Imani recognized the examination because she performed it herself: pupils, posture, the tremor a person thinks they're hiding. Whatever he saw made him answer honestly instead of kindly.

"She cried," he said. "Before she left. Standing right there at the posts. She wept but would not leave — she stood there crying and arguing with herself, and finally she wiped her face and got in the canoe and told the men waiting for her that she'd gotten everything she needed." He paused. "There were men waiting. They didn't come onto the island. She wouldn't allow it. She said if they stepped past the posts she would put down everything she knew about every hidden channel between here and the delta, and none of it would be true, and they'd spend the monsoon drowning in lies. They believed her. Smart men, to believe her."

*She wept but would not leave.* Imani turned the phrase over and understood it the way you understand a diagnosis — all at once, from the inside. A hostage who negotiated. A prisoner who drew lines. A mother who stood in the rain trying to decide whether water was a wall or a door.

She glanced sideways at Theo.

Theo was staring into the fire, and her face — the face that had been armor-plated since the dock at Kalabar, since the telegram, since probably since she was seventeen — had come apart. Quietly. Without drama. Just the slow collapse of a levee, the features settling into something young and wrecked and human. Imani had read the file, such as it was — the scandal, the name lost, the twenty years of distance compressed into a single sentence Theo had offered on the second night: *My mother chose the work. I just stopped competing for second place.*

But this was different. This was a stranger describing Adaeze Oyelaran not as the celebrated archaeologist, not as the disgraced one, not as the headline or the wound — but as a woman who sharpened her pencil too much, who laughed at jokes about the Sorrow Channel, who stood in the rain doing arithmetic with rivers, who cried at a shrine and still walked back to the men holding her leash because walking away would have cost something she'd decided was worth more than her freedom.

A person. That was all. A person, described by someone who'd shared fish with her, and it undid Theo more than twenty years of silence ever had.

"Ekwueme," Theo said finally. Her voice was steady again, mostly. "The routes she asked about. Did you tell her the truth?"

"I told her the truth about everything except one thing." He met her eyes. "I left out the shrine passage. The one that runs behind this island, under the ridge, usable only in high water. My grandmother's secret, and now mine." He tapped the earth beneath them once, twice, like knocking on a door. "Because I could see she was going to be made to use whatever I gave her. And I thought — this family should keep one door of its own."

*This family.* Imani watched Theo absorb those two words, watched them land somewhere deep and unguarded.

"You knew," Theo said quietly. "You knew they'd coerce her."

"I'm old, not stupid." Ekwueme shrugged. "Soldiers don't fund expeditions. Collectors fund expeditions. Collectors don't ask about hidden channels unless they intend to move things through them that can't be seen." He looked at Imani. "And you. The medic. You didn't come upriver for the fee. Nobody risks the narrows ahead of the monsoon for a fee."

Imani's hand went, without her deciding it, to the bag. To the tin with the wax seal. Eight years of keeping her mouth shut, and this old man on an island smaller than a hospital ward had read her in an evening.

"No," she said. "I didn't."

He nodded as if she'd confirmed a diagnosis he'd already written in the chart. "Then you'll want to rest. Both of you. The shrine passage floods in four days, maybe five, when the monsoon proper arrives. If you mean to go further up, you'll want to be through the narrows before the soldiers fortify them, and you'll want to sleep tonight while sleeping is still a thing bodies let you do." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered the bowls. "There are mats in the long house. The egrets will wake you at dawn. They wake everyone. It's why I keep them."

He shuffled toward the house, then stopped, half-turned.

"The woman who wept," he said, to the dark more than to them. "If you find her — and I think you will, because stubbornness recognizes stubbornness across any distance — tell her the Sorrow Channel argument isn't settled. Tell her I'm still right and she owes me a rematch."

Behind Imani, Theo made that small swallowed sound again, and this time it broke in the middle.

* * *

Later — much later, when Theo's breathing had finally gone long and even on the mat beside hers, when the egrets had settled and the river ran on with its endless commentary — Imani lay awake and took stock the way she used to take stock at 3 a.m. in field clinics: who's stable, who's bleeding, what do we have, what do we need.

Theo: sleeping harder than she had in six days, one hand curled loosely near Imani's mat, close enough to touch, not touching. Some knot loosened, temporarily, by the strange medicine of hearing her mother described as a whole person. It wouldn't hold. Grief like that re-knots. But tonight it held.

Supplies: adequate. Radio: repaired, tested, receiving only — transmitting meant batteries they didn't have. Evidence: dry, sealed, intact. Eight years of proof against Halvard Voss, wrapped in oilcloth ten feet from where she lay, heavier than lead and lighter than paper.

Risk: considerable and climbing. Soldiers in the narrows. Monsoon four days out, maybe five. And somewhere upriver, a woman who wept but would not leave, sharpening a pencil, cataloguing God knew what, buying her days by being useful to men who would discard her the moment her usefulness ended.

Imani stared at the woven ceiling and admitted the thing she'd been not-admitting since the dock: she hadn't come for Voss alone. Or not only. Somewhere between the leaking hull and the brown water and the night Theo dove under the boat without hesitation, the mission had grown a second organ, an unplanned one — the fierce, inconvenient wish to see this specific stubborn woman get her mother back, because Imani knew what it was to lose people to Voss's machinery and never get the body back, metaphorically or otherwise.

*This family should keep one door of its own,* the old man had said.

Fine, Imani thought at the ceiling. One door. But doors swing both ways, and tomorrow they'd walk through theirs straight toward the men with guns.

She was nearly asleep when Theo spoke into the dark, so quietly Imani almost pretended not to hear.

"She laughed," Theo said. "At your joke about the channel. She told him I don't believe anything anyone says." A pause. The river filled it. "She talks about me. After twenty years, she still talks about me."

Imani reached across the gap between the mats and found Theo's wrist — pulse steady, alive, grieving — and held it, saying nothing, because some patients don't need treatment, they need witness.

Theo didn't pull away.

Outside, far downstream toward the narrows, a searchlight swept the water once, twice, and went dark. Four days, maybe five. And in the morning, Ekwueme said, he would draw them the shrine passage — the door the family would keep — and after that there was nothing between them and the camp except the storm, the soldiers, and whatever was left of twenty years.
