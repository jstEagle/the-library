## 10. Radio Silence, Broken

The caretaker priest's name was Ekwueme, and he had the hands of a man who had spent seventy years doing small things carefully.

He showed them the path to the island's high point on their first morning, walking ahead with a machete he mostly didn't need, cutting back a frond here and there out of what seemed like courtesy rather than necessity. The shrine island rose maybe forty meters above the river at its crown — nothing by coastal standards, but on this drowned stretch of the middle passage it was the only high ground for thirty kilometers, a green knuckle of granite and old-growth fig trees with a mud-brick compound at its summit that had outlasted three empires and every flood the river had ever thrown at it.

"The antenna," Ekwueme said, and pointed with the flat of the machete.

There was no antenna. There was a rusted stub of pipe bolted to the compound's highest wall, and Theo understood immediately why Imani had gone quiet beside her.

"You said there was a radio."

"I said there was a radio," Ekwueme agreed. He had the mild, unbothered tone of a man describing weather. "The mission left it in sixty-three. The aerial was taken by the flood of seventy-one. The set itself, I keep dry." He paused. "Mostly dry."

* * *

The set was a Hallicrafters S-38, and it was a miracle it existed at all. Bakelite case gone amber with age, dial clouded, the whole thing smelling of dust and camphor and the particular sweetness of old capacitors that had been waiting decades for someone foolish enough to apply current. Imani laid it on the compound's central table like a surgeon exposing a wound, and Theo watched her face do something complicated.

"This is a museum piece."

"This is a working museum piece." Imani had her kit open — the small one, the one she kept under her bunk, the one Theo had never been allowed to inventory. Soldering iron, spools of wire, a leather roll of tools worn silver at the handles. "The question is whether it's a working museum piece *today*."

"The question," Theo said, "is whether it's worth the power. We've got the generator running four hours a night as it is. Ekwueme's fuel won't last the season."

Imani didn't look up. She was already inside the case, and her hands had changed — Theo had watched them for two weeks now, steady enough on the tiller, careful with the bail bucket, competent in the way of someone who'd learned medicine and never quite stopped moving like a medic. But this was different. This was the thing her hands had been made for.

"Voss's men control the narrows. They control the channels below the narrows. They control the town, the fuel depot, the ferry schedules." She stripped a wire with her teeth because the strippers had gone soft. "They don't control the sky. Radio doesn't need their permission."

"Radio also doesn't need to find anything. There's a lot of sky."

"Which is why we climb." Imani finally looked up, and there was something in her face Theo hadn't seen before — not the guardedness, not the ledger-keeper's caution she'd shown on the Marrow, not even the raw fury of the night she'd laid out her evidence of Voss. This was older. Hungrier. "Eight years I've been carrying paper, Theo. Documents, photographs, copies of manifests. Paper is evidence. Paper convicts. But paper has to be *carried* out, and we're forty miles from anywhere with a post office, and the monsoon is coming, and every day Voss's operation moves crates downriver that I can't stop." She turned back to the set. "A transmission is different. A transmission is *out*. The moment it's out, it's out forever. He can't drown it."

Theo thought about the field journal page in Adaeze's handwriting, dated nine days after the world was told she'd vanished. Someone wanted the expedition to be a ghost story. Ghost stories don't need rescue.

"Fine," she said. "The roof. Tonight."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the light went strange and heavy — the pre-monsoon light Theo had grown up dreading, the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Ekwueme's boys — two grand-nephews, twelve and fourteen, who had adopted the Marrow and its leaks as a personal project — helped haul the antenna wire up the compound wall, and Theo spliced it to the rusted stub of pipe with the particular bitterness of a woman who had once owned a mast worth more than this entire island.

The generator coughed awake at dusk. Imani sat before the Hallicrafters with her headphones on — actual headphones, foam perished to crumbs, one ear bare — and began the slow sweep of the bands, and Theo stood at the window and watched the river turn to pewter and then to nothing, and tried not to hope.

Hope was a current. You didn't fight it and you didn't ride it; you respected it and you kept your knife loose in its sheath. That was the first thing the river had ever taught her, before her mother's name had taught her anything else.

Static. The long hiss of the empty bands, the sound of a world not talking. Imani worked down through frequencies with the patience of someone counting beads, and Theo listened to the generator knock, and the frogs, and the fig trees moving in a wind that had started to carry the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Then Imani's hand stopped.

Not gradually. It stopped the way a heart stops — all at once, mid-motion, the tuning knob frozen under her fingers.

"Theo."

Theo was across the room before the word finished. She didn't take the headphones. She leaned in close, cheek almost touching Imani's, and listened.

Static. But static with a spine. Underneath the hiss there was a rhythm — a carrier wave, weak, wandering, the sound of a transmitter being powered by something that wasn't a proper generator. A field set. A hand-crank or a battery rig, something struggling.

And then a voice.

It was a woman's voice, and it was reading numbers.

"Four — seven — dash — nine — two —" A pause, filled with breath that was trying very hard not to be a sob. "— dash — one — one —"

Theo's whole body went cold and hot at once. She knew that voice. She had spent twenty years not knowing that voice — not answering its letters, not returning its calls, not hearing it across dinner tables or hospital corridors or the one disastrous afternoon in a courthouse corridor when they had both said things that could not be unsaid. She knew it the way you know a scar. You don't look at it. You always know where it is.

"That's her," Theo said. Her own voice sounded like it belonged to someone drowning. "That's my mother."

Imani's hand closed over hers on the table — not comfort; restraint. *Don't touch the dial. Don't breathe on it.*

The voice continued. Numbers, and between the numbers, words — clipped, careful, the diction of a woman who had spent her life lecturing to halls of two hundred and was now lecturing to a transmitter that might be her last act on earth. Coordinates. Theo caught fragments: *the north channel... the third island... the old customs house...* And then, threaded through the numbers like wire through beads, a word repeated at intervals that was not a coordinate at all:

"*Marrow.* *Marrow.* *Marrow.*"

"She's calling the boat," Imani breathed. "She doesn't know if you're alive. She's calling the boat."

"She can't know we —"

"She's guessing. She's gambling. She's a scientist with no data and one hypothesis." Imani's eyes were shining and wet. "She's betting her life that you're stubborn enough to have come upriver."

Theo wanted to laugh. It came out as something else, something that hurt her throat on the way through. Twenty years of silence, of returned letters, of a name she'd refused to answer to — and her mother's first message across the dark was an accurate assessment of her character.

The numbers kept coming. Imani wrote them down as fast as they arrived, her pencil flying across the back of an old supply manifest, and Theo forced herself to do the same — to be useful, to be a salvage diver and a river pilot and not a daughter, because the daughter was going to come apart and the pilot was the only one who could keep listening.

Coordinates. She recognized the shape of them even through the code. The north channel above the narrows. The old customs house — she knew it, a derelict colonial pile on a bluff where the river bent hard east, a place she'd sheltered behind once, years ago, running a cargo of outboard engines ahead of a customs launch. The third island. The numbers were a map, and the map was a location, and the location was where Adaeze Oyelaran was sitting in the dark reading numbers into a transmitter powered by hope and hand-crank.

And then the voice changed.

It dropped the careful diction. For three seconds — Theo would count them later, over and over, three seconds that she would carry for the rest of her life — the voice stopped being a broadcast and became a person.

"Theo. If it's you. I know what I —"

A door, or something like a door. A voice that wasn't Adaeze's, male, flat, with the bored authority of a man who had done violence often enough that it no longer interested him. The numbers stopped. There was a scuffle, the crackle of a knocked microphone, a woman's voice saying something fast and low that the set couldn't resolve into words —

— and then the carrier wave died, mid-breath, and the band was just static again, and the generator knocked, and the frogs sang, and Theo Oyelaran stood in a mud-brick compound on a shrine island with her mother's last three seconds burning a hole through her chest.

* * *

Imani copied the last legible numbers for another ten minutes, sweeping the band, hunting the carrier wave like a dog hunting a scent. It didn't come back. Eventually she took off the headphones and set them down with the exaggerated care of someone who needed something to do with her hands.

"She's alive."

"I heard."

"The coordinates —"

"I heard them too, Imani."

Imani was quiet for a moment. Then, gently, in the voice she probably used on patients: "Say the rest of it."

Theo looked at the dead radio, at the amber Bakelite and the clouded dial, at the stub of antenna pipe against a sky that had finally, finally begun to rain — fat, warm, preliminary drops that hit the compound roof like fingers testing a drum.

"The rest of it is that she said my name." Theo's voice came out level, which surprised her. "The rest of it is that she was mid-sentence, Imani. *I know what I —* What? What does she know? What did she —" She stopped. Breathed. The pilot, keeping the daughter lashed to the deck. "The rest of it is that a man cut her off, and men like that don't cut off transmissions and leave the transmitter working. They find it. And when they find it, they ask who was listening."

"So we move. Tonight."

"So we *don't* move tonight." Theo turned from the window. Rain now, properly, the first real fall of the season, silvering the compound yard. "The coordinates are above the narrows. The narrows are patrolled. We go up in the dark, in the rain, on a boat with a patched seam, and if they catch us, we're two women in a leaky hull with a radio and a ledger, and Voss's men don't ask questions, they just —"

She stopped again. Because she'd heard it. The thing under the fear, the thing the fear had been standing in front of like a curtain.

*Captive by choice or force.*

That was the question, wasn't it. The numbers were a map, but a map to what? A prisoner signaling for rescue? Or a woman in over her head, hiding, running her own operation inside Voss's, using the only frequency she had left? *The woman who wept but would not leave,* Ekwueme had said. Adaeze had come through here weeks ago, asking about old trade routes, and she had wept, and she had not left. You didn't weep and refuse to leave if you were a willing partner. But you also didn't read coordinates into the dark unless you wanted to be found — and if she wanted to be found, she had wanted it for weeks, and she had said *Theo's* name, not the authorities', not the expedition's, not even Imani's.

*If it's you.*

Her mother had spent the last of her signal not on a mayday. On an apology she couldn't finish.

"We go at first light," Theo said. "Before the storm builds. The narrows guards won't expect traffic running east in rain — they'll be under cover, lazy, watching the main channel. I take the Marrow through the side cut, the one past the drowned customs post. I've run worse in worse."

"*We* take the Marrow," Imani said.

"Theo." Ekwueme's voice, from the doorway, and both women turned. The old priest stood with rain running off his white skullcap, unhurried, and in his hands he held a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. "You will need this. The river is rising, and the river remembers who feeds it and who cheats it. Go to the north channel. Go to the third island." He set the bundle on the table beside the dead radio and the pages of numbers, and he looked at Theo with eyes that had watched this river for seventy years and did not seem afraid of it, which frightened her more than anything Voss's men could do.

"The woman who wept but would not leave," Ekwueme said, "left something with me. She said that if a boat called the Marrow ever came asking, I was to give it to her daughter."

He unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside, on a fold of expedition map, was a page torn from a field journal — Adaeze's handwriting, dense and urgent — and beneath it, wrapped in a scrap of silk, a small bronze figure no bigger than Theo's hand. An ichthyic thing, riverine, old beyond guessing, the kind of find that ended careers and started wars.

And on the map, in Adaeze's hand, a single line that was not coordinates at all:

*They are counting the crates. I am counting everything. Find me before the water does.*
