## 11. The Middle Passage

The river past the shrine island had no name on any chart Theo owned. Ekwueme called it the Middle Passage, and when she asked why, he only said, "Because that is where you find out what you are carrying," and went back to sweeping his courtyard as if he hadn't just handed her a bronze god and a sentence that would keep her awake for a week.

She left at first light because leaving was what she did. Imani came aboard without being asked, which Theo noted and did not comment on. The priest stood at the water's edge with his broom and did not wave, and Theo found she couldn't look back at him for longer than a second, the way you can't look too long at someone who has seen through you.

The map page rode in her breast pocket, folded to the size of a cigarette packet. *Find me before the water does.* She'd read it forty times in the night while rain ticked on the Marrow's deck overhead. Her mother's handwriting hadn't changed in twenty years — the same hard downstrokes, the same impatient crossings of the t. It was like hearing a voice from a locked room.

"Current's wrong," Imani said from the bow. She'd been quiet all morning, watching the banks with the flat attention of someone cataloguing symptoms. "Faster than it should be this early. The monsoon's pushing water down from the highlands ahead of schedule."

"The whole season is early."

"The whole season is early," Imani agreed, "which means everything downstream of us is going to be early too. Including the flood." She turned. "How far to the narrows where you think the camp is?"

Theo checked the map, then didn't check it, because she'd memorized it. "Three days if the engine holds. Five if we fight the current the whole way."

"And if the engine doesn't hold?"

Theo looked at her over the tiller. Imani's skin had gone greyish under its brown, and there was a fine tremor in her hands she kept trying to hide by gripping things — the rail, the gunwale, the bail line.

"When did you last sleep?" Theo asked.

"I'm fine."

"That's not what I asked."

Imani held her gaze for a moment, then let it go, which from her was practically a confession. "I take suppressants. Quinine base. I ran out two days ago. I was going to mention it."

"You were going to mention it."

"At an appropriate time."

"There is no appropriate time for malaria, Imani."

"There's no appropriate time for anything on this river," Imani said, with such weary precision that Theo almost laughed. Almost. "I've carried it dormant since I was twenty-three. It comes up when I'm exhausted or frightened. I am currently both, so let's just say the odds are not in my favor and leave it there."

Theo said nothing. She trimmed the throttle and put the Marrow's nose into the current, and thought about how every person on this boat was running from something, and how the river didn't care, and how three days was suddenly not a distance but a countdown.

* * *

The engine died at noon on the second day, in the worst possible place: a twelve-kilometer stretch where the channel braided into a dozen threads between sandbars and drowned forest, the kind of water that ate boats with patience rather than violence.

One moment the Marrow was pushing upstream against a current gone the color of strong tea; the next, the engine coughed twice, made a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something final, and stopped.

Theo drifted them toward the nearest sandbar with the sweep oar, heart hammering, mind already running diagnostics. Fuel line — clear. Spark — present. She pulled the head off right there on the deck with the boat grinding softly against sand, and found the gasket blown and the block cracked along the water jacket, a hairline seam weeping a thread of steam even now.

"It's done," she said.

"How done?"

"Done done. I can patch it. It'll get us maybe a day, maybe less, and it will never run again after that." She sat back on her heels. "There's a trading post marked about eight kilometers up. Kofar Bend. If it still exists, they might have a replacement block. If it doesn't exist—"

"Then we row."

"We row," Theo confirmed. "Twelve kilometers of braided channel, upstream, against the monsoon push. With two oars and whatever we can cut for poles."

Imani looked at the water, then at the sky, which had begun assembling its afternoon thunderheads with the unhurried confidence of a stage crew. Then she picked up the spare oar and said, "Well. We'd better start before it rains."

They patched the block with epoxy and a strip of tin from the fuel drum, and it ran for four hours — long enough to clear half the braid — and then it died again, permanently, with a clank that Theo felt in her teeth. By then the light was going and the first drops were coming down fat and warm as coins, and the nearest bank was a wall of drowned trees with black water standing between the trunks.

They tied off to a tree that looked older than the country and spent the night in the hull, bailing by turns, listening to the rain work the canopy above them like fingers on a drum. Neither slept much. Around two in the morning, Theo heard Imani breathing wrong — fast and shallow, a whistling catch on each inhale — and crawled aft to find her curled against the transom, shivering so hard the boat rocked.

"I'm fine," Imani said, before Theo could speak.

"You're not."

"I know I'm not." A pause. The rain filled it. "It's starting, isn't it. The relapse."

"Probably."

"Then tomorrow you should leave me here."

Theo stared at her. "What?"

"This is the middle passage," Imani said, and even shaking, even feverish, her voice had that dry edge that Theo was beginning to rely on more than she wanted to admit. "Ekwueme told me the same thing he told you, before we left. He said everyone meets themselves out here. So meet yourself, Theo. Ask the question now, in the dark, where it's cheap. How far would you go for a stranger? Because I am a stranger. Eight days ago you didn't know my name. And if the honest answer is 'not this far,' that's not shameful. That's arithmetic."

Theo sat in the dark and listened to the rain and did the arithmetic.

Twenty years of measuring her life in distances traveled. Twenty years of leaving first, always first, before anyone could leave her — before her mother could choose a dig site over a daughter's graduation, before lovers could wake up and see her clearly, before any place could become a place she needed. Leaving was the one skill she'd mastered completely, the one inheritance she'd taken from Adaeze Oyelaran without ever admitting it.

And here was a feverish woman offering her the exit, gift-wrapped, no blame attached.

"No," Theo said.

"No?"

"No. I'm not leaving you on a drowned tree in the flood path. That's the answer. You can stop asking."

Imani was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gone strange and small. "Nobody's ever stayed without being asked to."

"Well," Theo said, pulling the tarp tighter over both of them, "don't make it weird."

But in the dark, with the rain coming down and the fever heat radiating off Imani's shoulder against hers, Theo understood that something had shifted in the hull of her own life, some seam opened that wouldn't close again. She had stayed. Without being asked. Without calculating the salvage fee or the exit route or the distance to the next horizon.

She had stayed, and the world had not ended, and morning was going to come, and she was going to have to carry a grown woman eight kilometers through flooded forest, and somehow none of it felt like drowning.

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping. Imani couldn't stand.

That was the plain fact of it. Theo got her upright and her knees folded like wet cardboard, and her eyes had rolled back into that glassy unfocused stare that Theo recognized from a bad year on the coast when a crewmate's appendix went. Fever, 103 if she had to guess, maybe higher. The quinine was gone. The nearest medicine was at Kofar Bend, if Kofar Bend existed, and Kofar Bend was eight kilometers away through drowned forest, and the boat was dead in twelve kilometers of braided channel.

So Theo rigged a harness from the mooring lines and the canvas tarp, slung Imani across her back — the woman weighed nothing, which frightened her more than weight would have — and stepped off the sandbar into chest-deep water.

The forest took them slowly. Every step was a negotiation with mud that wanted the boots, roots that wanted the ankles, branches that wanted the eyes. The water rose and fell without logic — waist-deep in one stretch, neck-deep where a creek had backed up, thigh-deep again where the ground remembered itself. Twice Theo went down fully, Imani and all, and twice she got them up by thinking of nothing at all, emptying her mind the way she did before deep dives, becoming just a body doing the next thing, and the next thing, and the next thing.

Imani talked in her fever. That was the worst part.

Not words, mostly — fragments, names, numbers. "...ledger says forty-one crates... he signed for thirty..." And once, clearly, terribly: "I reported it. I reported it and they took my license instead." And once, in a voice so young it broke Theo's heart: "Mama, I passed the exams. Tell him I passed."

Theo climbed.

Somewhere in the fifth kilometer the trees thinned and the ground firmed and she allowed herself, for the first time since dawn, to think about the question Imani had asked in the dark. Not the version Imani had offered — the polite one, the arithmetic one. The real one underneath.

How far would she go?

For a stranger: apparently eight kilometers through a flood. For a friend: she didn't know yet, because she'd never had one long enough to find out. For her mother—

Her mother was the variable that broke every equation. Twenty years of silence, and one scrap of silk with a bronze fish in it, and one line of handwriting that read like a hand reaching out of a locked room. *Find me before the water does.* Adaeze Oyelaran had spent her whole life choosing the work over the child, and Theo had spent her whole life making sure she'd never have the chance to do it again. And now the work had become a cage and the child was the only one who knew the channels, and the old wound and the new purpose had fused into something Theo couldn't name and couldn't put down.

She crested the last levee at midafternoon and saw, below her, smoke.

Kofar Bend existed. A dozen houses on stilts, a drying shed, a dock with two pirogues, and — Theo's legs nearly gave out at the sight — a fuel drum outside the largest structure with a hand-painted sign: MECHANIC. BOATS. BATTERIES.

She walked down into the village carrying Imani, and the people came out of their houses to watch, and nobody spoke until an old man with a mechanic's forearms stepped forward and said, in careful trade-accent:

"What happened to her?"

"Malaria. Relapse. She needs quinine, IV fluids if you have them, and rest."

"We have quinine. The nurse comes Thursdays." He looked at the sky, gauging. "Today is Tuesday."

"Then I need your coldest room, your cleanest water, and every blanket you own," Theo said. "And I need your help with an engine block."

The old man smiled for the first time. "Now that," he said, "is a woman after my own heart. Bring her inside first. Then we'll look at your boat."

* * *

Two days. That was the price.

Two days while Imani burned and shivered and burned again, while the nurse — who arrived Wednesday after all, having heard the river was rising and come early — hung a bag of saline from a roof beam and pushed quinine through Imani's veins and pronounced her "past the worst, if she rests." Two days while Theo and the old man, whose name was Danjuma, hauled the Marrow's dead block ashore and machined a patch, welded a brace, and coaxed the engine back to something between life and memory.

Two days, while downstream the water rose, and somewhere beyond the narrows her mother counted crates in a camp that would soon be underwater.

Theo sat by Imani's cot on the second evening, cleaning the salt crust off her dive knife because her hands needed occupation, when Imani's eyes opened — clear, for the first time in three days.

"How long?"

"Two days. You missed a very exciting welding session."

Imani tried to sit up, failed, settled for propping herself on one elbow. Her voice was a rasp. "You carried me eight kilometers."

"Nine. There was a detour around a snake."

"A snake."

"Green mamba. Very rude. Didn't even apologize."

Something moved across Imani's face — not quite a smile, something older and more battered than a smile. "I told you to leave me."

"You did."

"And you did the math anyway."

"I did the math." Theo set the knife down. "Here's what the math said. It said that eight days ago I was a woman with a boat and no destination, and now I'm a woman with a boat, a destination, a partner who keeps copies of everything, and a mother who counts crates. The ledger improved. I don't walk away from improving ledgers."

Imani studied her for a long moment. "That's the most dishonest sentiment I've ever heard," she said, "and I once worked for a man who falsified death certificates."

"Then we understand each other perfectly."

Imani laughed — a real laugh, cracked and rusty, the first Theo had ever heard from her — and then winced and lay back, and the laugh dissolved into something quieter. "Theo. The things I said. In the fever. Did I—"

"You said numbers. Crates. Signatures." Theo paused. "You said forty-one crates, and someone signed for thirty."

Imani went very still. Outside, the river muttered against the pilings, and somewhere a dog barked twice and quit.

"Eight years ago," Imani said finally, "at the Telu dig, Voss's men pulled eleven artifacts out of the finds tent and logged them as field losses. Eleven pieces, written off as damaged in transit. I documented every serial number. I photographed the crates. I sent the file to the ministry, and three weeks later my medical license was revoked for 'unsubstantiated claims endangering an approved expedition,' and the eleven pieces surfaced two years later in a private gallery in Zurich under different names." Her eyes were open, fixed on the roof beams. "I kept copies of everything. I told you that. What I didn't tell you is that I've spent eight years waiting for a second set of numbers to compare against. Forty-one and thirty." She turned her head and looked at Theo, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "If those are real numbers, then Voss isn't smuggling artifacts anymore. He's laundering a museum. And your mother isn't signing manifests under threat."

"She's signing them under something worse," Theo said. "She's signing them so they stay accurate. She's keeping the count honest so someday the count can prove what happened."

The silence that followed was the kind that rearranges a room.

"So we go," Imani said.

"So we go."

Danjuma refused payment for the engine work — "You brought me a cracked block and a dying woman; the block was the interesting part" — but took Theo's dive knife in trade, which she regretted immediately and forever. They loaded the Marrow at dawn on the third day, Imani walking aboard under her own power but leaning on the rail like a woman much older, and the rebuilt engine caught on the third pull with a sound like a smoker's laugh.

The river had risen another meter overnight. Debris ran past them in ribbons — branches, a drowned goat, half a canoe. The monsoon was no longer approaching. It had arrived, and it was pushing everything ahead of it toward the sea, including, if the charts and the map page and the fever-numbers were right, a camp full of crates and a woman who wept but would not leave.

Theo opened the throttle. The Middle Passage narrowed ahead of them into the first true gorge of the upper river, walls closing in, water going from brown to black, and pinned to a stilt-pole at the gorge mouth — fresh-carved, the wood still pale — was a marker she hadn't seen on any chart.

A red cloth. Tied in a knot she recognized from the fishing village, from the old woman who had said *with soldiers.*

Someone was marking the way in.

Or marking it closed.
