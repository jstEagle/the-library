## 12. The Smuggler's Camp

We came at the last hour of light, when the ridge above the river went gold and the shadows below it turned to ink, because Imani said that was when eyes were kindest — when the world half-hid itself and you could look without being fully seen. She was right, the way she was usually right about things she'd learned the hard way.

The climb took two hours through wet forest that grabbed at us like it had opinions. Vines thick as my wrist. Ground that sucked at our boots and gave back a smell of rot so sweet it was almost floral. I went first, cutting a line with my machete in strokes I kept low and slow, because sound carries over water and we were close enough now to hear the river working — a long, muscular mutter somewhere below the trees, the voice of a channel that had swallowed better boats than the Marrow.

Imani climbed behind me with her medical bag strapped tight and her radio wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like something newborn. Every twenty minutes she stopped, pressed two fingers to her own wrist, and counted. I pretended not to notice. Her malaria had come back on us in the Middle Passage like a debt collector, and though the fever had broken three days ago, it had left her thinner, quieter, moving like a woman who no longer entirely trusted her own body to keep its appointments.

"Rest," I said, at a fallen trunk wide as a road.

"We're close."

"That's why."

She sat. I crouched where I could see downslope through the trees, and for a while there was nothing but the forest breathing and the far-off argument of water, and I let myself think about what we were about to see, and what I would do if it was what I feared.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about hope: it's heavier than dread. Dread you can carry alone. Hope you have to carry carefully, both hands, watching every step, because if you drop it, it doesn't just break — it cuts.

For eleven days I'd been carrying the possibility that my mother was dead. It had fit into my pack easily enough, rode between the spare fuel can and the tinned fish, weighed almost nothing after the first hundred miles. Dead was simple. Dead meant grief, and grief I knew how to do; I'd been rehearsing it since I was seventeen years old, when Adaeze Oyelaran chose a dig site over my graduation and I chose a life of leaving over a life of waiting.

But then the radio fragment from the shrine island. Her voice, thin through static, reading numbers — coordinates, a date code, the old field shorthand she'd taught me herself before I could ride a bicycle. *Grid seven-niner. Manifest sequence. Confirm receipt.* And then a man's voice cutting across hers, flat and bored, and the carrier wave dying like a throat being closed.

Alive, then. Alive and transmitting in code under someone else's supervision. Which meant alive and watched. Which meant everything ahead of me got heavier.

"Ready?" Imani said.

"No," I said, and stood up anyway.

* * *

We broke from the treeline onto the ridge shoulder an hour before dusk, bellies down in ferns gone brown with the season, and looked down at what the world had been told was a lost archaeological expedition.

It was a loading dock.

That was my first thought, absurdly domestic: *it's a loading dock.* A long timber platform built out over a cut bank of the river, maybe eighty meters of planked staging, roofed in corrugated tin gone orange with rust. Under the roof, stacked in ranks like ammunition, were crates. Dozens of them. Some small enough for one man to carry, some the size of coffins, all of them stenciled with the same marking — a stylized bird over crossed lines, the logo of the Voss Foundation for Cultural Preservation, which I had seen once in a newspaper photograph beside a man whose smile looked bolted on.

A crane — diesel, portable, the kind salvage outfits use for engine blocks — stood at the platform's end. Beyond it, moored to pilings driven into the bank, sat a barge. Not a river barge, exactly; too clean, too new, its paint still holding a factory shine, its hold open like a mouth. Two outboard skiffs patrolled lazy circles downstream of it, each carrying four men with slung rifles who had the posture of people being paid rather than people believing in anything.

And at a table set at the center of the platform, under a hanging lamp already lit against the coming dark, sat my mother.

I want to tell you I felt something cinematic. That the sight of her hit me like a wave. But what actually happened was stranger and colder: the world went very quiet and very detailed, the way it does when a diver drops below the thermocline and everything sharpens. I saw the table. I saw the ledger on it, bound in green canvas, fat as a Bible. I saw the stack of manifests beside it, each sheet weighted at the corner with a stone. I saw the man standing behind her right shoulder — tall, gray-shirted, sidearm holstered but hand resting near it in a way that wasn't casual — and I saw the second man at the foot of the ramp, and the third walking the crate lines with a clipboard.

And I saw my mother write.

Her pen moved down the manifest line by line. Crate number. Contents. Weight. Provenance notation. She wrote the way she used to write at our kitchen table when I was small — left-handed, slightly hunched, the paper angled toward her body as if she were keeping it warm. When she finished a sheet she lifted it, blew on the ink, and held it out without looking up, and the gray-shirted man took it from her fingers and passed it down the line, and another crate went up the ramp into the barge's mouth.

"She's cataloguing," Imani breathed beside me. "Look at her hands. She's not just signing — she's recording. Full provenance strings on every item."

"So they can sell them clean," I said. "A signature from Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran turns grave goods into inventory. Every museum broker on three continents will take her word over carbon dating."

"No." Imani's voice had gone strange. "Look again. She's writing more than they need. Serial positions. Find-spot detail. She's building a record *against* them. Every manifest she signs is also evidence — if anyone ever gets the originals."

I lay in the ferns and watched my mother commit treason against her captors one careful line at a time, and understood her completely for the first time in twenty years. This was what she had always done. This was the thing I had hated her for and never named it: she did not run, she did not fight, she *stayed* — stayed inside the burning house, stayed inside the bad marriage to my father's memory, stayed inside the scandal that ate her name — because staying meant you could document. Staying meant you could be useful, and usefulness meant survival, and survival meant someday, someone, would read what you wrote.

She was keeping herself alive by being indispensable, and she was keeping the truth alive by making herself its hostage clerk.

Something in my chest that had been grieving for eleven days quietly changed state. Grief drained out of me like water through a cracked seam, and what filled in behind it was not relief. It was fury — cold, patient, structural, the kind of fury that measures twice and cuts once. Not hot rage at the men with rifles. Something older and more architectural. Fury at the whole machine of it: the foundation letterhead, the bolted-on smile, the barge painted new, the ledger bound in green canvas, the entire apparatus built to convert a woman's name into a bill of sale.

They hadn't kidnapped her, exactly. They'd done something worse. They'd made her complicit on paper, in her own handwriting, and every day she signed, the world's proof that she was a victim got weaker and their leverage got stronger. Hostage of paperwork. You couldn't ransom your way out of that. You couldn't even prove you were in it.

"They'll move the rest before the monsoon," Imani said. "Voss won't risk the high water. There'll be a final shipment — the valuable pieces, probably flown out. Seaplane or—"

She stopped. Because below us, upstream around the riverbend, we heard it: engines. Not the skiffs. Something bigger, flatter, laboring against the current.

A second barge came around the bend, riding deep, escorted by a third patrol boat. And behind it, tied off at the camp's upstream mooring, sat a floatplane under canvas, its wings folded for storage, guarded by two men who didn't walk their post so much as own it.

"There's your final shipment," Imani whispered.

* * *

We stayed until full dark, because leaving information on the table is a sin I don't commit twice. We mapped it. Guard rotations — forty minutes on the platform perimeter, sloppy on the upstream side where the terrain was steepest, because steep ground reads as security to men who've never climbed anything harder than stairs. The generator hut, aft of the sleeping quarters, humming loud enough to cover small sounds. The radio mast behind the command tent, wire-guyed, a single bulb burning at its base. The cook fire downwind, meaning the evening watch would smell nothing but dinner. The number of crates remaining on the platform — I counted three times, and got thirty-one, thirty-one, thirty-one.

Thirty-one crates. One barge load short of everything.

And my mother at the table, signing, signing, while the lamp swung slightly on its hook and her shadow moved on the planks like a woman pacing a room she couldn't leave.

Twice, she paused. Both times she pressed the heel of her hand against her eye — a gesture I knew, a gesture that used to mean a headache coming on during a long translation session, a gesture I had inherited along with her left-handedness and her stubbornness and her habit of measuring everything. Both times, the gray-shirted man shifted his weight, and she picked the pen back up.

At full dark, the lamp over the table was doused and replaced by floodlights on poles, and the platform became a stage lit for a play nobody in the audience wanted to see. My mother was walked to a tent at the camp's edge — walked, not escorted at gunpoint, but flanked in a way that made the distinction decorative. Before the flap closed behind her she stopped, once, and looked out at the river.

I was a kilometer away, on a ridge, in the dark, invisible. She could not have seen me. There was no possible way.

She looked straight at me anyway. Or at the darkness where I was. Held it for three seconds. Then went inside.

"Tell me you saw that," Imani said.

"I saw it."

"What does it mean?"

"It means she knows the ridge is the only place worth looking from," I said. "It means she's telling us she's alive, and she's telling us to be smart about it." I rolled back from the ridgeline, into the black under the trees, and felt the cold fury settle into something with edges and a plan. "And it means she's been counting guards and timing rotations too. She's not waiting to be rescued, Imani. She's waiting for a partner."

We climbed down through the wet dark, machete work by feel, and made the Marrow two hours past midnight, tied in the flooded side channel where no patrol boat had any reason to look. Imani got the little spirit stove going behind the tarp and heated water for the tablets she took now like a religion. I sat in the stern with the map board on my knees and drew the camp from memory — every guard post, every crate stack, the barge, the floatplane, the generator hut, the tent where my mother slept under guard of paperwork and proximity.

"The final shipment goes by air," I said. "Voss won't trust the river with the best pieces, not with the monsoon coming. Floatplane out, transfer to a freighter downcoast. Everything else moves on that barge within days."

"So we stop the barge."

"No." I tapped the drawn rectangle of the platform. "Stopping the barge gets us shot and gets her moved somewhere we'll never find. We don't break the machine. We drown it." I looked at the map, at the river running through it like a vein, at the storm symbol Imani had penciled in the corner weeks ago and re-inked every morning since, marching up from the sea like something summoned. "The monsoon hits in three days, maybe four. When it does, this camp floods — the platform, the records, everything. Voss will have an evacuation plan. Men first, cargo second, paper last."

"And the paper is the case," Imani said slowly. "The manifests. In her handwriting. With your mother's provenance strings underneath."

"The paper is the case," I agreed. "And the paper burns in a flood unless somebody carries it out first."

Imani was quiet for a moment. Downriver, faint, the patrol skiffs droned their circles. Above us the stars were going out one by one behind incoming cloud, and the air had begun to taste the way it does before weather — metallic, expectant, like the inside of a struck bell.

"You're talking about going into that camp," she said.

"I'm talking about getting those manifests off that table before the river takes them," I said. "And I'm going to do it the one way nobody down there will expect."

"How?"

I folded the map. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat, testing the acoustics.

"I'm going to walk in the front way," I said. "As a courier. Somebody's buyer sends a girl with sealed orders and a verification code — Voss runs collectors' errands all the time, he'll have protocols. Nobody questions money's paperwork." I looked at Imani across the small wet dark of the cabin. "I need you to build me a courier. Papers, seals, a reason to exist. Can you do that?"

Imani smiled for the first time in days, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent eight years keeping receipts.

"I can do better than that," she said. "I can build you a courier Voss's own office sent."
