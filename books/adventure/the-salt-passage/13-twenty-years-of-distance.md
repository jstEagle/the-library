## 13. Twenty Years of Distance

Adaeze Oyelaran had spent forty years teaching students that an object was a sentence, and a collection was a paragraph, and a site was the whole ruined book of a people's thinking. She had taught them that context was everything — that a bronze head pulled from its soil was a corpse without a story, a beautiful thing made meaningless by the act of taking it.

Now she sat in a canvas field tent at the edge of a river that was rising by the hour, and she catalogued, from memory, the contents of forty-one crates of stolen history.

She did it in her head because they had taken her notebooks. She did it precisely because precision was the only weapon left to her. Every evening, after the guards finished the evening meal and the generator coughed to life and the floodlights buzzed on over the loading dock, she sat on her cot with her hands folded in her lap like a woman praying, and she walked the collection again, crate by crate, object by object, and she memorized her own testimony.

*Crate nine. Fourteen pieces, Ife terracotta, heads and torsos, one with original pigment traces at the hairline — that one is irreplaceable, that one must be photographed before it moves. Crate twelve. Benin plaques, six of them, and whoever packed them used newspaper directly against the surface, which means the ink has already begun to transfer, which means Voss is destroying the very things he is stealing, the ignorant man, the thief who doesn't even know what he steals.*

She kept the list in the architecture of her mind the way she had once kept stratigraphy — in layers, in order, in the certainty that anything buried carefully enough could be dug up again. When the day came — and she had decided, in the third week, that the day would come — she would recite it. Every object. Every provenance. Every name of every site Voss's men had stripped. She would stand in a courtroom or a consulate or, if it came to it, a ditch, and she would speak the collection back into existence as evidence.

It was, she understood, a small thing. A ledger kept in a dying woman's head. But Adaeze Oyelaran had built a career on the conviction that memory was a form of resistance, and she was not going to stop believing it now, at the end, when it was the only thing she had left that was hers.

They kept her alive because she was useful. She knew that. She was not naive; she had been negotiating with powerful men since before some of these guards were born. Voss needed her signature on the manifests — the great Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran, whose name turned stolen goods into legitimate salvage in the eyes of every customs officer and insurance broker between here and the coast. Her signature was the forgery-proof seal on the whole operation. So they fed her, and they let her walk to the river in the mornings under guard, and they did not beat her, because a beaten woman's hands shake, and shaking hands sign badly.

In exchange, she made herself indispensable in smaller ways. She read the manifests aloud for the men who couldn't parse the English. She identified the fragile pieces and told them — truthfully — which packing methods would destroy value, because every piece that arrived undamaged was a piece she could later testify to. She smiled at Voss's site manager, a soft-bellied Dutchman called Brandt who believed himself a scholar, and she corrected his attributions gently, the way she would correct a promising student, and she watched his vanity swell like a sail, and she thought: *every day you trust me is a day closer to the day I bury you.*

She was seventy-one years old. She was not afraid of dying. She was afraid of dying useless — of the collection scattering into a hundred private vaults, unnamed, unprovenanced, un-mourned, and her testimony dying with her in this tent on this river that had already begun to smell of the coming flood.

So she catalogued. Every night. Crate by crate.

It was on the forty-first night that the guard told her about the woman.

* * *

His name was Ekon, and he was the youngest of them, maybe twenty-three, with the loose-shouldered boredom of a boy who had taken a soldier's pay because there was nothing else in his district and discovered that standing watch over a loading dock was even less than nothing. He was the only one who talked to her. She had decided early that he was the crack in the wall she would work at, patiently, the way water works at limestone.

She didn't flatter him. That was the first rule. She asked him real questions — about his mother's village, about the fish in the river here versus the river where he grew up — and she listened to the answers, and she remembered them, and after three weeks he had begun to bring her things. A better pillow. A scrap of dried fish. Once, an orange, which she ate slowly, letting the juice run down her wrists, and he watched her with an expression she pretended not to see, because it was the expression of a boy watching his grandmother eat, and she could not afford to be anyone's grandmother. Not yet. Sentiment was a door and doors opened from both sides.

It was Ekon who mentioned the storm first, in the way of people who mention a thing they've been told not to mention.

"The water's coming early this year," he said, squatting at the edge of her tent in the blue hour before the floodlights came on. He turned a stick over in his fingers. "My uncle fishes up past the narrows. He says the birds went south two weeks ago. Birds don't lie."

"The birds are smarter than the men who built this dock," Adaeze said. "It'll be underwater inside the month."

"Brandt says we'll be done by then."

"Brandt says a great many things." She let the silence sit. Ekon was easy to let silence sit with; he filled it the way young men do, with whatever was pressing on him.

"There was a boat came through the narrows two nights ago," he said. "Small one. Ran the side channel at night — Osaze's patrol heard the engine and went out and found nothing but a cut line where somebody'd paid a fisherman to move the marker stakes. Osaze is furious. He says it's a runner, a smuggler beating us to the coast with something good." He glanced at her, quick, checking her face. "But Musa saw it, before it went into the channel. He says the pilot was a woman."

Adaeze kept her hands still in her lap. Forty years of fieldwork had taught her stillness — the stillness you need when a snake crosses the path, when a village elder is deciding whether to trust you, when the thing you most want in the world is standing at the far edge of a trench and one wrong movement will send it back into the earth.

"A woman," she said. Level. Interested the way a scholar is interested. Nothing more.

"Light-skinned. Oyinbo-mixed, maybe, or just river-country. Short hair. Young, but not a girl." Ekon shrugged. "Musa says she handled the boat like she was born in it. Came up the narrows against the current at night. Nobody does that."

"Nobody sensible," Adaeze said, and Ekon laughed, and the laugh was a small warm thing she took and hid away with the rest of her hoard.

But when he had gone, when the floodlights buzzed on and the generator settled into its grind and the camp settled into its evening noise of dice and radio static, Adaeze sat on her cot with her hands still folded, and she let herself do the thing she had forbidden herself for forty-one nights.

She let herself think about her daughter.

* * *

The last time she had seen Theo, the girl was seventeen and standing in the doorway of the house in Lagos with a duffel bag over her shoulder, and she had said, in a voice Adaeze had never heard from her before — flat, final, adult — "You chose the dig. You always choose the dig. I'm just the first thing you leave behind."

And Adaeze, who could reconstruct the trade routes of a dead civilization from three potsherds and a line in a Portuguese sailor's log, who could read a landscape the way other women read letters from home, had stood in her own doorway and said — what? What had she said? She had spent twenty years not remembering, and twenty years remembering, and the two versions had long since fused into one unbearable sentence: *I said something about the work being important. I said something about the work.*

She had not said *stay.* She had not said *you are the dig, you are the only excavation that ever mattered and I failed at it, I failed at you, I left you in boarding schools and field camps and your aunt's house in Enugu while I chased the approval of men who gave out grants.* She had said something about the work. And Theo had shouldered the bag and walked down the drive and gotten into a taxi, and Adaeze had watched her go with the terrible composure that was her inheritance from her own mother, the composure that had won her every honor she'd ever been given and cost her everything that hadn't come with a certificate.

Twenty years. Theo had made a life in the water — salvage, piloting, the kind of work that kept a body moving and a heart unexamined — and Adaeze had followed it from a distance the way you follow a bird you once banded: reports, rumors, a photograph in a diving magazine eight years ago, a woman in a wetsuit holding up a recovered ship's bell, squinting against the sun, and Adaeze had cut the photograph out and kept it in the back of a monograph on Niger Delta trade ceramics where no one would ever find it, and she had looked at it perhaps a thousand times.

*She handled the boat like she was born in it.*

Of course she did. Adaeze had put her in a canoe before she could walk; there were photographs of that too, somewhere, a toddler standing in the bow of a pirogue with her fists out for balance, laughing, fearless, on the river at Aboh where Adaeze had done her first fieldwork and where Theo had spent the happiest eight months of her childhood before the boarding school took her. The river had been the one place the two of them had ever been good together. And then Adaeze had gone and made a career of digging the past out of the earth, and her daughter had gone and made a career of the water that covered it, and neither of them had ever once said the obvious thing, which was that they were doing the same work from opposite ends.

And now, if Musa's eyes were good and Ekon's tongue was loose and the gods who had abandoned her twenty years ago had chosen this of all moments to look down —

Now a woman had run the narrows at night, against the current, in the teeth of an early monsoon, heading upriver toward a camp that did not officially exist, in the one week of the year when no sane pilot moved.

Coming for her. Or coming for the collection. And Adaeze found, examining her own heart with the same cold instrument she applied to everything, that she could not tell which possibility frightened her more. If the woman was a stranger — a rival salvager, a Voss competitor — then Adaeze was alone, and had always been going to be alone, and that was survivable, that was the plan, that was the ledger in her head and the testimony and the ditch if it came to it.

But if it was Theo —

If it was Theo, then her daughter was steaming toward a camp full of armed men who answered to a man who had already, once, in the first week, when Adaeze had asked too many questions about where the crates went, put a pistol on the manifest table and said, in the pleasant tone of a man discussing weather, "Your name is worth more alive, Doctor. Don't make me prove the other direction."

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay on her cot and listened to the river.

It had a new voice. She had noticed it two days ago and said nothing, and now, in the dark, she gave it her full attention the way she would give a test pit her full attention — every sound a datum. Under the usual long exhale of the current there was a thicker note, a dragging note, the sound of a river carrying more than water. Silt from the highlands. The monsoon was not coming. It was already here, in the headwaters, in the hills where the rain had begun to fall on ground that would send every drop of it down to this camp, to this dock, to these forty-one crates and the seaplane ramp Brandt had had dredged in September against all local advice.

Voss knew, of course. Voss had a schedule, and the schedule said the final shipment would move before the peak, and Voss had never in his life let a schedule be argued with by something as negotiable-seeming as weather. Adaeze had met his kind in forty years of this work — the men who believed the world was a warehouse and everything in it, ground, river, people, history, was inventory. They were always astonished at the end. The river was the one party that never signed a manifest.

She got up in the dark, quietly, and went to the tent flap, and looked out at the water.

The floodlights made a yellow island of the dock. Beyond it the river ran black and wide and patient, and on the far bank the fishing village's cooking fires were fewer than last week — the villagers were leaving, moving their boats and their goats inland, the way people had moved before floods on this river for four thousand years. They knew. Everyone knew except the man with the schedule.

And Adaeze stood there in her thin cotton wrapper in the warm night, seventy-one years old, a hostage of paperwork in a camp of thieves, and she let herself hope.

It was a physical act, hope. She felt it in her chest like the first pressure of water behind a seam. She had spent twenty years telling herself she had no right to it — that she had spent her daughter's childhood the way Voss's men spent this river valley, extracting what was valuable and leaving the scar — and she had believed it, mostly, the way you believe a thing you repeat often enough. But hope did not care about her accounting. Hope stood at the far side of a black river and handled a boat like she was born in it and came anyway, at night, against the current, in the storm season, twenty years too late and exactly on time.

*If it's her,* Adaeze thought, *and they touch her, I will burn this whole operation down from inside it. I will sign nothing. I will let them prove the other direction. I will die on this dock and my last act will be to make my signature worthless.*

And then, because she was who she was, she took the thought and made it a plan, and the plan into layers, and the layers into order. If the woman was Theo, she would need to know the camp — the guard rotations, the generator schedule, the mud that swallowed boots past the ankle on the north side where the dock lights didn't reach. Adaeze had had forty-one nights to learn all of it. She began, that night, to organize what she knew the way she organized everything: for someone coming after her.

* * *

In the morning, Ekon brought her tea, and she drank it, and she said, casually, the way one remarks on weather, "Your friend Musa — the woman on the boat. Was there anyone with her?"

He shrugged. "He says he saw two shapes. Hard to tell. Dark, no running lights." He hesitated, and then, because he was twenty-three and she had spent three weeks being the only person in this camp who asked about his mother's village: "Osaze put it on the radio to the coast, to Voss's people. If she's somebody's runner, they'll know by now."

Adaeze set down the cup. Her hands, she noted with a kind of distant professional interest, did not shake.

"Then they'll come for her," she said.

"Osaze says the narrows will handle her," Ekon said, and grinned, boyish, wanting to share the camp's contempt for the anonymous intruder. "Stakes and the current and the dark. River takes care of its own."

"Yes," Adaeze said. She looked past him, out the tent flap, at the brown and rising water. "It does. It always has."

And she began, silently, in the architecture of her mind, in the layer beneath the crates and the provenances and the testimony, to build a second list — shorter, more urgent, written in a hand that shook after all:

*Guard rotation, north side, two men until midnight. Generator off at four. The mud. The mud on the north side. If she comes — when she comes — the mud.*
