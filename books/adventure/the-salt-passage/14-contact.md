## 14. Contact

The courier's shirt was too clean.

That was the first thing I noticed in the cracked mirror of the Marrow's head, the morning we drifted into the narrows below the camp. The shirt was white linen, pressed by Imani with a heated stone and borrowed from a crate of trade goods we'd salvaged at the shrine island — goods meant for sale to people who never got their hands dirty. On me it looked like what it was: a costume worn by a woman who hadn't owned an ironed thing in twenty years.

"You look like a man who delivers invoices," Imani said behind me.

"That's the idea."

"You look like a man who delivers invoices and hates his life."

"Even better. Nobody talks to that man."

She didn't laugh. She'd barely slept, her malaria still smoldering somewhere in her blood like a banked fire, and she'd spent the night assembling my cover out of nothing: a forged waybill on expedition letterhead she'd copied from the dumped crates at the trading post, a leather satchel stuffed with blank paper, a buyer's seal pressed into soft wax from a stamp she'd carved herself with a fish knife. Voss's operation ran on paper — Adaeze's signature on manifests, guards checking seals, men who trusted documents because documents were how they told themselves they weren't thieves.

"They'll check you against a list," Imani said. "There's always a list."

"Then I'll be on it."

"Theo."

I turned. She was holding the radio handset like something living, and her eyes had the flat shine of someone running calculations they don't want finished. "You come back downriver by noon tomorrow or I bring the storm to them early. I don't care if the plan's ready. I don't care what I burn. You understand me?"

It was the closest thing to love either of us had said out loud. I nodded once, because my voice would have betrayed me.

* * *

The camp sat where the river bent around a shoulder of rock, and it was wrong in every way a place could be wrong.

From the ridge two days ago I'd mapped it: forty tents under canvas flyways, a loading dock built over the floodplain like the river wouldn't notice, crates stacked in ranks with the patience of a military depot. Generator lines. A radio mast. Men with rifles walking the perimeter in pairs, unhurried, bored, which is the most dangerous kind of guard — the kind that shoots first because thinking is effort.

Now, drifting toward the dock in a stolen pirogue with my hands loose on the paddle, I let myself see it as a courier would see it. Just another stop. Just another manifest. The river here ran green and slow, carrying leaves and one drowned antelope past the loading cranes as if the water itself were delivering inventory.

A guard flagged me in with two fingers. Young. Sweat-dark collar. He looked at my face longer than he looked at my papers.

"Courier for Meridian," I said, and gave the name Imani had lifted off a real waybill — a buyer three countries downstream whose seal she'd replicated well enough to fool a bored man. "Manifest corrections. My principal wants the revised weights countersigned before the last shipment."

"Last shipment's not for days."

"My principal believes otherwise." I let a flicker of irritation cross my face, the small arrogance of a man whose time is money he doesn't own. "Storm season came early. Everyone downstream is nervous. Check your list."

He checked his list. Somewhere in those laminated pages there was a Meridian, because after a long minute he jerked his chin toward the big tent at the center of the camp — the operations tent, where the paperwork lived, where I'd watched through field glasses a woman with gray-threaded braids sit at a table signing page after page while a man stood behind her shoulder reading each one before she touched it.

"Tent's open. Don't touch anything. Okafor handles the manifests."

My own name, spoken by a stranger with a rifle, in a place where my mother was a hostage. The river seemed very loud all of a sudden.

* * *

Inside the tent the air was generator heat and ink and dust, and she was there.

Twenty years is a strange distance. It's long enough to forget the exact pitch of a voice and short enough that the sound of a pen scratching paper can put you back at seventeen, standing in a doorway in Lagos, watching your mother work instead of watching you graduate. Adaeze Oyelaran sat at the manifest table exactly the way she used to sit at excavation tables — spine straight, left hand flat on the surface, right hand moving with the economy of someone who wastes nothing, not even motion. Her hair was shorter than I remembered. There was a new scar over her eyebrow. Her wrists were thinner.

And she was cataloging. Even now, even coerced, even signing her name to theft, she was doing it precisely — I could see it in the way she handled each sheet, the way her fingers paused on certain entries a half-second longer. She wasn't just signing. She was memorizing. Building a record inside herself that no one could confiscate, because I knew that habit, that discipline, that stubbornness dressed up as professionalism. I'd inherited it and called it a flaw.

The guard announced me. "Courier. Meridian corrections."

She looked up.

I had rehearsed this moment on the river for two days. I had rehearsed being nobody — a flat-eyed functionary, a man who delivered invoices and hated his life. I had rehearsed the nod, the satchel, the bored recitation of numbers.

I had not rehearsed surviving her eyes.

They went through me like sonar. One pass, two passes, and I watched something move behind them — not recognition, not yet, but the animal unease of a woman who has learned that every stranger might be a trap. Then the second look, slower, and I saw her take in the shape of my jaw, which is her jaw; the set of my shoulders, which is her father's; the way I stood with my weight back, ready to leave, which she taught me without ever meaning to.

"Corrections," I said. My voice held. Some part of me was astonished by that.

"Put them on the table." Her voice was lower than I remembered. Rougher. A voice that had been shouting into radios that no one answered.

I opened the satchel and laid out Imani's forgery — revised tonnage figures, crate counts, a request for countersignature. My hands were steady. Under the table, out of sight, my knee was not.

She drew the pages toward her and read them the way she read everything: completely, once, top to bottom. And then she did the thing that nearly broke me open in front of six armed men and a canvas roof.

She picked up her pen, and instead of signing, she began to correct. Small marks in the margins. A crossed figure here, an amended weight there. Professional. Unremarkable. And in the margin of the third page, in handwriting so small and so casual that only someone who had grown up watching that hand write grocery lists and birthday cards and apology letters never sent — in that margin she wrote four words:

*Your boat leaks worse.*

The pen didn't pause. Her face didn't change. She initialed the correction and slid the pages back across the table, and the guard behind her shifted his rifle and looked at the ceiling fan, and I stood in the heat of that tent with my mother's handwriting burning a hole through the forgery and twenty years collapsing into the space between one breath and the next.

She knew. Not suspected — knew. Knew me from a walk, from a stance, from whatever ghost of my father lives in my face. Knew me, and chose, in front of guns, to say the only thing she could safely say: a joke about the Marrow, about the boat I'd patched underwater in brown water while a stranger bailed beside me. A joke that meant *I know you're here, I know why you're here, be careful, go carefully, I see you.*

I looked down at the pages. I made my face do its job.

"These amendments will need re-certification downstream," I said. "My principal won't accept initial-only."

"He'll accept what the river allows," she said. And then, folding the pages, tapping them square against the table, handing them back to me with her eyes fixed on mine for exactly one second too long: "Tell him the weights are honest. Everything else is negotiable."

Three sentences. That was all we got, all we allowed ourselves, there at the manifest table playing strangers while guards breathed behind us and the monsoon stacked itself black over the western hills. Three sentences that carried a graduation ceremony missed, a scandal survived, a name lost, a telegram burned, twenty years of miles measured instead of years. *Your boat leaks worse.* *He'll accept what the river allows.* *Everything else is negotiable.*

I took the pages. I turned. At the tent flap I stopped, because some part of me refused to leave without one more second, and I said — to the air, to the guard, to her — "The old woman on the island sends regards. She says the river remembers everyone who wept on it."

Behind me, the pen scratching stopped. Just for a beat. Just long enough.

Then it resumed, steady as a heartbeat, and I walked out into the light.

* * *

Imani met me at the pirogue with a pole disguised as a fishing rod and murder in her expression, which was her version of relief.

"Well?"

I handed her the amended manifest. She read it fast, then read it again, slower, and stopped at the margin. Her head came up.

"She wrote this? In front of them?"

"In front of them."

Imani stared at the four small words like they were scripture. "She's telling us the plan holds. The weights are real — the shipment schedule is real. She's confirming our timing against theirs." She tapped the page. "And this — 'everything else is negotiable' — that's her saying the signatures mean nothing. She's building her testimony into the paperwork itself. God. Twenty years and she's still the sharpest person on this river."

"She's a hostage with a pen," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the word hostage, and hated it, and let it crack.

We pushed off. The current took us around the bend and the camp disappeared behind the rock shoulder, and I kept my face toward downriver until Imani quietly took the tiller line out of my hands because my grip had gone white and useless.

"She saw you," Imani said. It wasn't a question.

"She saw me."

"And she let you walk out."

"She let me walk out." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Twenty years, Imani. Twenty years of not speaking, and when we finally do, it's shipping logistics. We talked like strangers. We *were* strangers. And it was—" I stopped, because the true sentence was dangerous. "It was the best conversation I've had in years."

Imani said nothing for a long time. The river carried us. Ahead, the sky over the western hills had gone the color of a bruise going bad, and the air had that pressure-cooker stillness that every fisherman on this water fears.

"She corrected the weights," Imani said finally, almost to herself. "Which means she trusts us to act on them. Which means the last shipment moves when the storm does — Voss wants it out ahead of the floods, same window we planned for." She folded the manifest into the waterproof sleeve with the rest of our evidence, her ledger, eight years of grudge and proof. "Tomorrow night, then. Maybe the night after. We won't get a better one."

"We won't need one," I said.

But that night, anchored in the mangrove shallows with the radio murmuring weather warnings neither of us wanted to hear, I lay awake listening to the rain start — fat, scattered drops hitting the canopy like slow applause — and I thought about the pen stopping. One beat. One heartbeat of hesitation in a woman who had survived this camp by never hesitating.

She'd heard the old woman's island in my words. She'd known I'd been there, known about the caretaker, known I'd been asking after her the way you ask after the dead.

And she'd gone back to signing.

Because that was Adaeze Oyelaran. Because usefulness was how she stayed alive, and precision was how she stayed sane, and love — I understood this now, lying in the dark with the storm coming down the valley like a debt finally being collected — love was the thing she did in the margins. Four words in small handwriting. A joke about a leaking boat. Everything essential written where only family would think to look.

Tomorrow night the river would rise, and Imani would broadcast eight years of truth to anyone downstream with a receiver, and my mother would walk out of that camp or die trying, and I would be there with a crowbar and a stolen pirogue and no plan worth the name.

I fell asleep to the rain, and dreamed of a manifest table, and woke with salt on my lips that had come from nowhere near the sea.
