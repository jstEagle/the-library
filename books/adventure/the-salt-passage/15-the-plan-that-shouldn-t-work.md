## 15. The Plan That Shouldn't Work

The priest's name was Brother Ekwueme, and he had one rule: nothing spoken inside the shrine walls left them. Imani thought it was the first honest institution she'd encountered in eight years.

She sat in the back of the mud-brick chapel with the radio open in her lap like a patient on a table, its guts exposed, while rain tested the roof overhead. Theo was out at the tree line watching the river. Adaeze Oyelaran had come to them an hour after dark, soaked to the collar, having walked out of the smugglers' camp on the pretext of checking a fish trap — a lie so thin that Imani had marveled the guards let her live on it. But Voss wanted his manifests signed and his signature woman healthy, and a woman who checked traps was a woman who could be watched from a distance without effort.

So here they were: three liars and a priest, planning a theft.

Imani had spent her career around plans. Field medicine was nothing but triage dressed up as strategy — you decided who bled loudest and worked backward. She looked at the plan taking shape between these two women and recognized the shape immediately. It was a triage plan. It assumed losses.

"The manifests are carbon-papered," Adaeze was saying. She sat very straight on a bench built for children, hands folded like she was lecturing a seminar. "Three copies. One stays in the camp strongbox, one goes downriver with each shipment, one gets couriered to Voss's office in the delta. If we destroy only what's in the camp, Voss still holds proof of every piece that ever moved."

"So we don't destroy them," Imani said. "We drown them."

Theo turned from the doorway. Rain had gotten into her hair; she didn't bother with it. "Explain."

"Water ruins ink. Water ruins paper. But water also *moves* things." Imani tapped the radio casing. "I've been listening to their channel for two days. The camp runs on a schedule — supply runs before the storm closes the narrows, then they pull everyone to high ground at the old mission upstream. Voss's men aren't stupid. They know the flood is coming. They've already moved the fuel drums and the small valuables. What they *haven't* moved is the paperwork, because paper doesn't float away if you put it in a steel box, and because men like Voss's lieutenant think records are someone else's problem."

"You want the river to take the strongbox," Adaeze said slowly.

"I want the river to take everything, and I want us to have already taken photographs." She pulled the ledger from the oilcloth wrap where it lived against her ribs. Eight years of copies — requisition forms, medical logs, a dig-site inventory in her own handwriting with dates that predated every official record Voss ever filed. "This is half of it. The manifests are the other half. Together they're not evidence anymore. They're a confession with a timeline."

* * *

They argued until midnight. Not loudly — Brother Ekwueme slept twenty feet away, and sound carried over water — but with the low, grinding intensity of people who had all survived by never agreeing to anything quickly.

Theo wanted to go in herself. Of course she did. Imani had watched her for three weeks now, long enough to map the reflexes: Theo Oyelaran met every problem with her own two hands, as if the world were a hull breach and she were the patch. It would be funny if it weren't going to get her killed.

"You can't swim into a guarded camp and photograph a strongbox," Imani said.

"I can dive under the dock at night. I've done worse in worse water."

"And when the flood comes? You'll be underwater when the river decides to rearrange the landscape. I've seen what a monsoon does to a channel system. It doesn't flood, it *edits*. Islands vanish. Channels become fields. Fields become channels. You want to be holding your breath under a dock when God starts moving furniture?"

Theo's jaw worked. Imani recognized the expression from hospital corridors — the face of a person being asked to trust other people with something they'd only ever trusted themselves to carry.

"Then who goes in?" Theo said.

"Your mother."

Silence. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

Adaeze didn't flinch. Imani gave her credit for that. The older woman looked at her daughter across the chapel and said, "She's right. I'm the only one who can open the strongbox without a key. The combination is my birthday. Voss chose it himself — he thinks it's clever, keeping me bound to my own name." A dry, terrible smile. "He has never understood that I have been cataloging him since the day I arrived. Every crate. Every guard rotation. Every time the seaplane comes. It's all in my head, and heads don't flood."

"And if you're caught?" Theo's voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and Imani looked away to give them privacy neither of them would ask for.

"Then I'm caught," Adaeze said simply. "Theodora. I have been dead to you for twenty years and a hostage for four months. At some point you must allow me to be useful instead."

Imani studied the floorboards. Some conversations you witnessed like weather — you took shelter and waited for it to pass.

* * *

The plan, when they finally nailed it down, had eleven steps, and Imani hated every one of them for different reasons.

Step one: tomorrow night, during the guard change, Adaeze would open the strongbox and pass the manifest books to Theo through the gap beneath the loading dock — a gap Theo had measured by eye on the ridge and sworn was wide enough, which meant it was exactly the width of a book plus hope.

Step two: Imani would photograph what couldn't be carried — the wall charts, the crate stencils, the seaplane schedule chalked inside the shed — using the camera she'd bought in the river town with money she'd sworn she'd never spend on anything but a new life. Then the originals went into a waterproof satchel, and the satchel went into the river current at the narrows, timed to reach the fisheries post downstream where a man named Odili owed Imani a debt from another life and owned the only working radio transmitter between here and the coast.

Step three: the storm. This was the part Imani kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"We're betting on timing," she said, drawing lines in the chapel dust with a stick. "Adaeze opens the box at the guard change — forty minutes, maybe less if the lieutenant drinks tonight, which he will, because men drink before storms. Theo takes the books and runs the Marrow—" she stopped, corrected herself, said it anyway— "runs the boat through the side channel to the narrows drop point. I trigger the evacuation call on their own radio frequency — a false report of a breach downstream, something that makes them pull everyone off the docks and up to the mission. And then the river does the rest. By dawn there's no camp, no manifests, no route. Just a flooded valley and a story nobody will believe except the people holding my ledger."

"And if the storm comes early?" Theo asked.

"Then we die wet instead of dry."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about plans made in chapels — they either got laughs or they got silence, and silence meant everyone had already done the arithmetic on their own death and found it acceptable. Imani had done hers years ago, in a courthouse corridor, watching a man in a good suit read out the findings that ended her career. She had walked out of that building knowing exactly what she was willing to lose. It made planning easier. It made living harder.

"There's one more variable," Adaeze said quietly. "Voss arrives before the shipment. He always inspects the final load personally. If he's on the ground when this happens—"

"Then he's on the ground," Theo said. "And he watches his whole operation drown."

"He won't drown," Adaeze said. "Men like Halvard Voss have boats we haven't seen yet."

Imani wrote that down in her head and underlined it twice. It was the first truly pessimistic thing the archaeologist had said, and therefore the most valuable.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which Imani distrusted on principle. Storms that arrived politely were saving their manners for later.

She found Theo at the shrine's high point, coiling rope with the automatic precision of someone whose hands needed work the way other people's lungs needed air. Imani sat down beside her and said nothing for a while. That was another thing she'd learned in eight years of exile: silence offered freely was worth more than comfort demanded.

"The satchel," Theo said finally. "If I make the drop and the current takes it wrong—"

"It won't. I've timed the drift from three points on the ridge. Odili's post sits below the confluence; everything in this river ends up there eventually. That's why the smuggling works. That's why the plan works. Same river, same physics." She paused. "You're asking me if I trust the river."

"I'm asking if you trust me."

Imani looked at her. Twenty-some days of leaking boats and bad water and malaria shivers, and this was the first time Theo Oyelaran had asked anyone for anything resembling reassurance.

"I trusted you with my life in the Middle Passage," Imani said. "You carried me two miles through reeds with a fever cooking your brain. So no — I don't trust you with a satchel. I trust you with considerably more than that, and it terrifies me, because the last person I trusted with my work was a man named Dr. Emeka Nwosu, who smiled at me across a dig site and sold my reports to Voss within the week." She picked up the end of Theo's rope and began coiling it alongside her, matching the rhythm. "Trust isn't a feeling, Theo. It's a decision you keep making after the evidence turns bad. I'm making it again. That's all any of us are doing."

Theo was quiet a moment. "My mother used to say something like that. About excavation. 'You keep digging past the point where it stops making sense, because the sense is always deeper than the doubt.'"

"She sounds like she was worth waiting twenty years for."

"She still is. That's the problem." Theo tied off the coil and stood. "If this works, she testifies. She comes back to the world. And I go back to being the daughter of Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran instead of just some diver nobody's heard of. I spent my whole life building a name that isn't attached to hers."

"And now?"

"Now I think maybe I've been salvaging the wrong wreck."

* * *

They spent the day preparing in the particular quiet of people about to do something irreversible. Brother Ekwueme brought them food and asked no questions, which Imani suspected was its own form of prayer. Adaeze memorized the sequence of steps with her eyes closed, reciting it back once, perfectly, like scripture. Imani cleaned the camera lens nine times and told herself the tenth was superstition.

At dusk the barometer fell so fast her ears popped. The western sky had gone the color of an old bruise, green-black at the seam of the horizon, and the air smelled the way a struck match smells — the whole atmosphere waiting for a spark.

Imani set up the radio on the chapel's flat roof, antenna strung through the bell ropes. She tuned to the camp frequency and listened to the guards' chatter: bored, nervous, complaining about mosquitoes and the lieutenant's mood. Ordinary voices. Men who thought they were the weather's masters because they had a tin roof and a schedule.

At 19:40, Adaeze slipped out along the water path toward the camp, wearing the caretaker's fishing poncho, carrying nothing but the combination in her head and twenty years of practice at being underestimated.

At 20:15, Theo launched the Marrow into the darkening channel, alone, with a waterproof satchel and a diver's knife and the expression of a woman who had finally stopped arguing with herself.

And Imani Okafor sat down at a stolen radio, keyed the transmit switch, and prepared to lie to armed men in a voice she hadn't used since medical school — crisp, official, faintly bored, the voice of authority that assumes obedience.

"This is Narrows Station Six," she said, and heard her own heartbeat in the pause. "We have a structural breach on the lower levee. Repeat, breach on the lower levee. Recommend immediate evacuation of riverside positions. You have perhaps ninety minutes before the secondary channel overtops. Acknowledge."

Static. Then, distantly, a man's voice calling for the lieutenant.

Imani exhaled and looked west, where the sky had begun, at last, to move.

Ninety minutes. Eleven steps. Three people who had no business trusting each other, betting everything on a storm that had never once, in the history of water, negotiated.

Downriver, faint as a memory, she thought she heard thunder — or a seaplane engine, arriving early.

She couldn't tell which.
