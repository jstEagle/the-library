## 16. Voss Arrives

The seaplane came in low over the eastern bend at midmorning, when the light was flat and the river gave nothing away. Adaeze heard it before she saw it — a sound like tearing cloth, growing into a roar — and she set down her catalog pen with the deliberate care of a woman who had spent six weeks teaching herself not to flinch.

She was in the manifest tent, as always. Crate 114, a bronze ceremonial staff, provenance: shrine complex, upper narrows, estimated fourteenth century. Her handwriting on the inventory sheet was neat, unhurried, the handwriting of a professional. That was the trick of it. She had learned that her competence was the only currency Voss's men respected, and so she spent it lavishly, every day, signing manifests with a steady hand while inside her something screamed and screamed.

The plane touched down on the wide water below the loading dock, threw up a white fan of spray, and taxied to the mooring pontoon with the arrogance of a thing that had never once been told no.

Adaeze stood. Through the tent's open flap she could see the guards straightening along the dock, could see Okafor — Voss's site manager, a soft-voiced man with hard eyes — hurrying down the steps to receive him. The whole camp reorganized itself around that descending figure the way iron filings obey a magnet.

*He's early.* The thought arrived cold and precise. The final shipment wasn't due for three days. The manifests weren't finished. And Halvard Voss did not come anywhere ahead of schedule unless he suspected something.

She smoothed her shirt, tucked the pen behind her ear out of habit, and walked out into the heat to meet him.

---

Voss climbed the dock ladder himself, refusing Okafor's hand. He was smaller than his reputation — a compact man in his sixties, silver-haired, dressed in field khaki that fit too well to be anything but tailored. He wore expensive boots caked with authentic river mud, and Adaeze understood immediately that even his mud was curated.

"Dr. Oyelaran." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the worst part. "You look well. The river agrees with you."

"The river disagrees with everyone," she said. "It simply hasn't gotten around to you yet."

His laugh was genuine, warm, appalling. He took her elbow as if they were old colleagues at a conference, steering her along the dock, away from the guards, away from Okafor's hovering. His grip was light. It didn't need to be heavy. They both knew what he held.

"I'll be direct," he said, "because I respect you, and because we haven't much time. The storm is coming early this year. My pilots say forty-eight hours, perhaps less. So I want the final shipment loaded today, and I want your signature on the complete manifest by nightfall."

"The conservation protocol requires—"

"Dr. Oyelaran." He stopped walking. Turned to face her fully. And the warmth drained out of his face like water through sand, leaving something patient and bottomless. "I flew eleven hours to be here. Let's not insult each other with protocols."

She looked past him at the crates stacked on the dock — her crates, her finds, or rather the finds her team had been forced to log as *salvage recovery, export authorized*, the lies accumulating in her handwriting like sediment. Somewhere in that stack was the ivory flute from the shrine island. Somewhere was the terracotta head her own graduate students had lifted from the earth three weeks ago, believing, because she had told them to believe, that every paper was legitimate.

Her students. That was how he'd done it. Not guns pointed at her — though there were plenty of those within reach — but guns implied at everyone she was responsible for. Daniel with his bad knee. Priya who had a baby at home. The two local guides whose families lived downstream, reachable, touchable. Sign, Dr. Oyelaran, or people begin to have accidents. Sign, and your team goes home safe and employed.

She had signed. Six weeks of signatures. She told herself she was keeping them alive, and some mornings she even believed it.

"You're worried about something," Voss said now, watching her face with the attention of a man who had made a fortune reading people. "You've been worried for days. Your crew says you've been checking the radio shed twice a night." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "And yesterday one of my men found footprints on the north ridge. Fresh ones. Someone has been watching this camp."

Adaeze kept her breathing level. Six weeks of practice. "Fishermen use the ridge."

"Fishermen," Voss repeated pleasantly. "Of course."

---

He inspected the camp for an hour, and it was the most frightening hour of her captivity, because he did it like an archaeologist. He read the site. He crouched at the edge of the loading ramp and studied the water stains on the planks. He counted the crates twice against Okafor's list and found the discrepancy — crate 109, logged but not yet moved from the storage pit — and said nothing about it, which meant he had filed it somewhere in that meticulous mind where discrepancies went to become leverage.

Then he asked for her. Alone. In the communications tent, with the door flap tied shut and one guard outside and the radio sitting on its shelf between them like a witness neither would acknowledge.

"Sit," he said, and she sat, because sitting cost nothing.

He poured two cups of coffee from the camp's battered thermos and handed her one, and she drank it, because refusing also cost something. Outside, she could hear the crew beginning to move crates under Okafor's direction. Ahead of schedule. Just as he wanted.

"Do you know why I built all this?" Voss asked, settling across from her. "The expedition, the permits, the whole apparatus. Everyone assumes it's greed. Greed is simple, and I am not a simple man." He sipped. "I built it because objects like yours are *dying*, Dr. Oyelaran. Every year, more of them rot in humid ground, get smashed by looters who don't know what they're holding, drown when the rivers rise. I rescue them. Private hands preserve what nations neglect. In two hundred years, no one will thank your government's museum committee. They will thank me."

"You've rehearsed that," she said. "It's very good. You almost believe it."

Something flickered behind his eyes — annoyance, quickly banked. "Your daughter doesn't believe it either, I imagine."

The tent went very quiet. The river's sound came through the canvas, vast and indifferent.

Adaeze set down her cup. Carefully. The way she'd set down the pen that morning. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Voss agreed softly. "You wouldn't." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and regarded her with something that in another man might have been kindness. "A woman came through the fishing villages upriver asking after us. Then a boat ran the narrows at night — my sentries fired on a channel runner, missed, and the description they gave was interesting. Female pilot, forties, traveling with a woman carrying medical supplies. And then, three days ago, my man in the last river town wired me a name off a charter ledger. He said the handwriting caught his eye." Voss smiled again, and this time it did not reach anywhere at all. "*Oyelaran.*"

The word hung between them. Twenty years of distance compressed into eight letters.

"She's a salvage diver," Adaeze heard herself say, and her voice was steady, God help her, it was steady. "We haven't spoken since she was seventeen. Whatever she's doing on this river has nothing to do with me."

"That's what makes it useful," Voss said. "Nothing to do with you — and everything." He rose, walked to the radio, and rested his hand on it lightly, proprietarily. "Here is what happens now. The shipment loads tonight. You sign. My plane lifts tomorrow morning ahead of the storm, and I am gone, and your contract is fulfilled, and your people go home. Clean ending. Everyone prefers clean endings."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then I will assume the woman on the ridge is connected to you, and I will treat her accordingly." He said it without raising his voice, without emphasis, the way a man states the price of rice. "My guards shoot first at strangers on this river, Doctor. You know that. You've seen their discipline. All that stands between your daughter and a very regrettable accident is my continued belief that this transaction remains... uncomplicated."

Adaeze stood. Her legs felt like someone else's legs, borrowed, barely obedient. But her voice, when it came, was her mother's voice, the one that had commanded dig sites and lecture halls and a household once, long ago, before everything broke.

"If you touch her, I will burn every artifact on this dock myself, and then I will spend the rest of my life testifying against you in every court that will have me. You think your money protects you. Money didn't protect the last man who made an enemy of me, and he only stole *potsherds*."

Silence. Voss studied her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it — the threat, the woman, the mathematics of risk. She had seen that weighing before, in committees, in funding boards, in the face of her ex-husband deciding whether she was worth the trouble. Men always thought the calculation was hidden. It never was.

"There she is," Voss said finally, wonderingly. "The famous Adaeze Oyelaran. Do you know, I paid a great deal of money for your reputation? Your name on these manifests is worth more than half the cargo. A coerced signature is worthless; a *willing* one is priceless. That's why you're still alive, Doctor. That's why your daughter is still alive." He gathered his papers, unhurried. "So let's keep both of you valuable. Sign tonight. Keep your crew close and your curiosity closer. And if the woman on the ridge is who I think she is—" he paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the glare, "—then tell her, when you next speak, that family should stay together. It saves so much negotiation."

The flap fell shut behind him.

Adaeze stood alone in the communications tent with her cold coffee and the radio she was no longer permitted to approach. Her hands, when she finally looked at them, were shaking — fine tremors, the kind that come after the adrenaline spends itself.

*Theo is here.* On this river. Close enough that Voss knew, close enough that the knowledge was already a weapon being forged around her daughter's throat.

Six weeks ago, Adaeze had made herself a promise in the dark of her sleeping tent: survive, catalog, testify. Stay alive by being useful, and one day walk into a hearing room and bury Halvard Voss under the weight of everything she remembered. It had been a good plan. Patient. Archaeological.

But plans assumed time, and Voss had just taken time off the table. Loading tonight. Departure tomorrow. And somewhere out there, watching from a ridge or running a channel in the dark, Theo was moving toward this camp with no idea that her arrival had already been priced into a madman's arithmetic.

Adaeze pressed her palms flat against the manifest table until the trembling stopped. Then she picked up her pen, turned to crate 115, and began to write — small, exact, memorizable. If tonight was all the time she had left, then every hour would be documented, dated, and carried out of here one way or another.

Outside, the first wind of the monsoon moved through the camp, lifting dust off the loading dock, bending the river's surface into long gray ridges.

The storm was coming early. So, she understood now, was everything else.
