## 17. The Storm Makes Landfall

The wind arrived before the rain did.

I felt it through the soles of my boots first — a low tremor in the ridge stone, like the river clearing its throat — and then it came up the valley in a wall, flattening the reeds along the bank and tearing a strip of corrugated tin off the loading shed below us. The tin cartwheeled into the water and was gone. Just like that. Gone.

"Imani." I didn't have to say more. She was already at the radio, her hands moving with a steadiness I'd only seen from her once before, when she'd stitched a gash on my forearm in the dark of the Marrow's cabin while I steered one-handed through the narrows. Eight years of exile had taught her to be calm about things that should not be calmed about.

"Frequency's set," she said. "Antenna's grounded to the shrine stone like the priest showed us. If this works, it works. If it doesn't—" She shrugged, and lightning lit her face white for half a second. "Then we'll have other problems."

Below us, Voss's camp was coming apart in slow motion. Men ran between the crates with tarps, shouting over the wind. Flood lamps swung on their poles. And there, at the center of it all, under a canopy lashed down with what looked like ship's cable, Adaeze Oyelaran stood at the manifest table with a clipboard in her hands, signing.

Even from here, even through the rain starting to come down in ropes, I could see the way she held herself. Straight-backed. Deliberate. A woman doing arithmetic with every stroke of the pen — how long she could stay useful, how long being useful would keep her breathing.

*Twenty years,* I thought. *Twenty years of distance, and I still know your posture better than my own.*

"Theo." Imani's voice cut through. "It's time. Get to the generator cutoff."

The plan — Imani's plan, the plan that shouldn't work — had exactly four moving parts and no margin for any of them. One: kill the camp's main generator so the flood lamps died and the radios went to static. Two: Imani broadcast the ledger — eight years of documentation, photographs, shipping manifests, names, dates, everything Voss had buried her career to keep quiet — on the emergency band, where any listening post downstream would catch it. Three: the guards, blind and deaf and watching a river rise toward their knees, would do what frightened men always do. Scatter. Four: Adaeze walked out in the confusion, and we took her downstream ahead of the flood crest.

Four parts. Any one of them failing meant all of them failing.

I slid down the ridge trail in the dark, mud to my shins, rain needling my face like thrown gravel. The generator shed sat behind the loading dock, padlocked, humming. I put the bolt cutters I'd taken off a guard's boat two nights ago through the hasp and killed the main breaker.

The lights went out across the whole camp, and the world became lightning and noise.

* * *

For eleven seconds, nothing happened. Long enough for me to think: *it's failed, they have a backup line, Voss planned for this, he plans for everything—*

Then Imani's voice came out of the darkness everywhere at once.

She'd rigged the camp's own PA horn to the radio transmitter — the priest's idea, actually, an old man who tended a shrine island and knew more about improvisation than any engineer I'd ever met — and now her voice rolled across the flooded compound, flat and clear and merciless:

*"This is a priority transmission on the international emergency band. I am broadcasting evidence of artifact smuggling under the authority of Halvard Voss, financier of record for the Oyelaran expedition. Manifests, photographic records, and correspondence are being relayed now. This is not a warning. It is already done."*

And then she read names. Guard commanders. Downstream brokers. Port officials on three rivers whose signatures appeared on transit documents dated months before the expedition ever reached them. She read them the way she did everything — without hurry, without mercy — and I stood in the rain beside a dead generator and listened to eight years of a ruined career turn itself inside out and become a weapon.

Men started running. Not toward the crates — away from them. Away from the names. A man carrying a rifle dropped it in the mud rather than be found holding it. Two others fought over the keys to the last working skiff until neither got them.

The river did the rest.

I'd been watching the water gauge on the dock piling since midnight, and it had climbed a hand's width every twenty minutes — faster than any seasonal rise I'd seen in twenty years of piloting these channels. This wasn't the monsoon arriving politely. This was the whole sky upstream letting go at once, weeks early, and sending its bill downriver to collect. By the time Imani finished reading, the water was over the lower dock boards, black and muscular, lifting empty crates and spinning them like toys.

*Now,* I thought. *Get her now.*

I crossed the compound low and fast, using the crates as cover, and came up behind the manifest canopy from the side Voss's men never watched because it faced the rising river — because who comes from the water?

Adaeze was still at the table. Of course she was. In the strobing dark, with her guards gone and her jailer's empire audibly collapsing around her, Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran was calmly stacking her manifests, squaring their edges, weighting them under a stone.

"You're staying to testify," I said, and my voice didn't sound like mine. It sounded like a girl's.

She turned. Rain blew under the canopy and hit her face and she didn't flinch. For a moment neither of us moved, and twenty years stood in the space between us wearing the shape of a storm.

"I know," she said. "You're late."

* * *

Later — much later, on the island, in the dry — I would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. But some pieces stay fixed.

I remember telling her the plan. I remember her shaking her head once, sharp as a scalpel, and saying, "Not downstream. The records vault. Everything Voss can't afford to lose is in the steel cabinet in his quarters, and if the flood takes it, the physical originals die with it and Imani's copies become claims instead of proof." I remember arguing — *the water's at the door, Mother, there is no vault anymore* — and I remember her looking past me at the black river and saying, with the terrible calm of a woman who has spent six weeks cataloguing her own hostage situation, "Then we have perhaps four minutes."

I remember running for the quarters together, our shoulders banging in the dark, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that she ran like me. Same stride length. Same lean into the wind. Twenty years of distance and our bodies had never gotten the memo.

I remember the vault: a steel cabinet bolted to the floor of Voss's stateroom, and Adaeze working the combination with wet fingers, missing it twice, and me standing in the doorway watching the water climb the steps behind us — one step, two — and hearing her whisper the numbers under her breath like a prayer in a language I almost recognized.

I remember the cabinet opening. I remember the weight of the document case against my chest, oilcloth-wrapped, heavier than any salvage I'd ever carried up from depth.

And I remember the seaplane.

* * *

It came in low over the ridge through the rain, engines screaming against the wind, and touched down on the flooded channel beyond the loading docks with a violence that threw spray thirty feet in the air. Even in the chaos — even with his guards scattered and his manifests drowning and his name going out over the emergency band to every listening post on the coast — Halvard Voss had come back for his cargo.

Or for something else. I didn't know yet which.

"He'll take the master collection," Adaeze said. We were flattened against the stateroom wall, the document case between us, water at our ankles now and climbing. Through the blown-open window I could see the plane's running lights swinging toward the crated artifacts — the pieces that mattered, the ones too valuable to trust to a boat. "The bronze heads. The ivory. He'll load them himself if he has to. He won't leave without them."

"Then he drowns waiting for a crane crew that's already run."

"No." Her voice changed, and I heard something in it I'd spent my whole childhood trying not to hear: fear. Real fear, from the most fearless person I knew. "Theo. He knows you're here."

The words landed like cold water down my collar.

"A guard came to me yesterday," she said, fast, low. "Told me a woman matching your description bought passage at the last town. Voss questioned him for an hour. He let me hear him do it. He wanted me to know." Her hand closed on my wrist, hard, the grip of a woman who had held onto things her whole life by refusing to let go. "He will use you. Whatever happens next — whatever he offers, whatever he threatens — understand that it will be shaped around you. That's how he works. That's how he worked me."

"Mother—"

"Listen to me." Lightning filled the room, and in its light her face was older than I'd ever allowed it to be, and more afraid, and more human. "Whatever happens next, you get that case downstream. You don't come back for me. Do you understand? I have spent six weeks being useful enough to stay alive, and I can be useful for six more. You cannot."

The water took another step.

And somewhere out on the flooded channel, a seaplane's engines changed pitch — idling down, settling, patient — and a searchlight swept across the compound and stopped on the stateroom window where we stood.

* * *

We ran.

Out the back, through the storage shed, into the rain proper — real rain now, the kind that hits like thrown stones, the kind that makes the air itself something you have to push through. The document case banged against my ribs with every stride. Behind us the searchlight played over the shed roof, unhurried, certain. Ahead, the ridge trail rose toward the tree line and the shrine path and Imani, and I aimed us at it like a heading.

Halfway up the slope, Adaeze stumbled.

I caught her arm and felt the heat of her through the soaked sleeve — fever-high, or close to it, six weeks of coerced labor and bad rations finally presenting their invoice — and for one stride she leaned on me, actually leaned, this woman who had never in my living memory accepted weight from anyone.

"The case," she gasped.

"Is with me."

"Give it to me. Take the ridge alone. I'm slowing you—"

"You're not." I hauled her up another terrace of the trail. "And I'm not giving you the case, because if we're separated, they'll search you first and me second, and I know how to hide things on my body that divers can't find. So we stay together, and we move, and you yell at me about my technique later. Deal?"

Something happened to her face then — something quick and complicated that the lightning kept stealing before I could read it — and she said, "Deal," and we climbed.

Behind us, the searchlight found the stateroom. Found the open, empty vault. And stopped.

The engines changed again — that new note, the ugly one, the sound of a decision being made — and began to spool up.

"They're leaving," Adaeze said. "Without the cargo?"

"No." I looked back over my shoulder, down through the rain at the flooded compound, at the crated bronzes lashed to the dock that was now more water than wood, at the seaplane sitting fat and patient on the channel with its lights burning. "They're not leaving. They're waiting."

"For what?"

I didn't answer, because I already knew, and the knowing sat in my chest like swallowed ice.

Voss hadn't come for the cargo. Or not only for the cargo. His vault was empty, his manifests were drowning, his name was on the airwaves, and there was exactly one thing left in this valley that could still be used as leverage — one thing that could buy him silence, or time, or a bargaining chip against whatever authorities were already turning their boats downstream.

A guard had told him a woman matching my description was on the river.

He'd flown into a hurricane to collect it.

The engines idled. The searchlight swept the ridge line, slow and methodical, working its way toward us terrace by terrace. And the river, black and rising, ate another step of the trail below.

Imani's voice crackled faintly from somewhere above us — still broadcasting, still reading names into the teeth of the storm, stubborn as a heartbeat — and I pulled my mother higher up the slope, into the trees, into the dark, and understood with total clarity that the plan had just ended and the night had just begun.

The searchlight found the tree line.

And stopped there. Waiting.
