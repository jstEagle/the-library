## 18. The Choice of the River

The river came for the camp the way a debt collector comes — not all at once, but with the patience of something that had already done the arithmetic.

Theo stood at the tree line above the loading docks and watched brown water climb the pilings in lurches, each surge swallowing another rung of ladder. The monsoon had made landfall an hour before dawn and hadn't paused to breathe since. Rain came sideways, hard as flung gravel, and the sky was the color of a bruise going bad. Somewhere behind her, Imani's voice crackled through the stolen camp radio, broadcasting the ledger into the storm — coordinates, dates, manifests, eight years of proof — to any authority downstream with a receiver and the will to listen.

*Let it land,* Theo thought. *Let it land somewhere.*

Below her, the camp was coming apart exactly as planned. Guards ran between collapsing tents, hauling crates toward higher ground that didn't exist. The flood channel Imani had mapped from Adaeze's coded coordinates was doing its work: the river had found the old trade cut behind the storage sheds and poured through it like it remembered the way home. Docks groaned. A fuel drum bobbed past, then another, then a whole raft of them, spinning.

And at the center of it, tied off to the last standing mooring post, sat Voss's seaplane.

It had come in two days ago on floats, white and gleaming and obscene against the mud, and now it rode the rising water like it had been born to it. Men were loading it even now — four of them, slick with rain, passing crates up into the cabin through the open door. Theo could see the stenciling on the nearest crate even at this distance. She'd spent twenty years around recovered goods; she knew museum crating when she saw it. Those were the pieces. The best of what Adaeze had cataloged under threat, the irreplaceables, the ones Voss would sell quietly to men who never asked where beauty comes from.

The engine cover was up. Someone was trying to keep it dry.

Theo slid down the slope through the wet brush, boots finding the channels she'd scouted yesterday, and came out behind the boathouse where she'd left the pirogue. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Everything else in the world was shaking — the trees, the water, the air itself — and her hands were calm as ballast.

She thought about Imani's face this morning, fever-bright, holding the radio mic like a knife. *Go,* Imani had said. *Get your mother. I'll make sure the world hears it.* And Theo had gone, because that was the deal they'd never spoken aloud: you hold the line, I cross it.

The pirogue took the flooded yard like it was nothing, which it wasn't. Theo poled through what had been the mess tent, past a floating table, past a guard's rifle case spinning slowly with the current. The current here was already strong enough to argue. She let it argue and steered anyway, angling for the seaplane's blind side, the side away from the loading crew.

Adaeze stood at the foot of the plane's boarding ladder, manifest board strapped across her chest, pointing men toward crates. Even drenched, even sixty, she stood like a woman conducting an orchestra that happened to be drowning. Theo's chest did something complicated and painful.

*Mama.*

Twenty years of not saying it, and here it was, useless, shouted inside her own skull over the roar of rain.

A guard spotted the pirogue. He shouted — the words torn apart by wind — and grabbed for a slung rifle. Theo did the math in half a second: forty meters, rising water, one rifle, one woman poling a log through a flood. She changed course directly at him.

He expected her to flee. People always did. She came straight on instead, fast, and at ten meters he was still fumbling the sling over his head when the pirogue's bow caught him at the hip and put him into the water. Theo was already past, already swinging wide, and the rifle went under with a gurgle the storm swallowed whole.

Then the seaplane loomed over her, white flank streaked with river mud, and Adaeze turned at the sound of the hull scraping float.

For one full second, mother and daughter looked at each other across three feet of black water and twenty years of silence, and neither of them said anything, because there was no sentence long enough and the storm wouldn't have allowed it anyway.

Then Adaeze said, "You're supposed to be downstream."

"Good to see you too," Theo said.

Voss's voice came from the cabin doorway, and the temperature of the world dropped. "Miss Oyelaran." Halvard Voss stood braced in the frame, gray-haired, immaculate even soaked, holding a pistol the way other men held umbrellas — casually, like weather was a personal inconvenience. "I wondered which of us the river would bring first."

"The river brings everybody eventually," Theo said. "That's kind of its whole thing."

"Your mother signed my manifests for eleven weeks because I told her what would happen to her team if she didn't." He said it pleasantly, conversationally, a man explaining terms. "Her team is currently on a barge two days south, alive, because she has been useful. You are making her less useful by existing here. Do you understand the transaction you're interrupting?"

"I understand you're loading stolen heritage onto a plane in a hurricane," Theo said. "I understand my mother has been signing those manifests at gunpoint-adjacent coercion for three months. And I understand that everything you're doing right now is already recorded, timestamped, and broadcasting downstream on a frequency your radio man failed to jam." She smiled, and it felt good, felt like biting. "Imani sends her regards. Eight years late, but she keeps excellent records."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — not fear, not yet, but the first cold draft of it. He glanced at the sky, at the water climbing the floats, recalculating. Men like him always recalculated. It was their only honest instinct.

"Load faster," he told his men. Then, to Adaeze: "Board."

"Mama, don't—" Theo started.

"He'll drown them," Adaeze said simply. Not dramatically. The way you'd state the water level. She put her boot on the ladder rung.

And Theo understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the lungs, that the plane lifting off was the end of everything. Not just the artifacts — though God, the artifacts, three thousand years of somebody's hands in those crates — but the testimony. Adaeze alive and free and able to stand in front of authorities and say *here is every piece, here is every date, here is the man who bought me*. Once Voss had her in the air, she was leverage again, forever, a signature on legs. And once the plane cleared the storm cell heading east—

No.

Theo drove the pirogue's bow hard into the gap between the seaplane's port float and the mooring post, and grabbed the mooring line.

The plan assembled itself in her head the way river plans did — fast, ugly, complete. The plane was tied to a post that was going under. If the post went, the plane drifted, and Voss would lift off and be gone. But if the line were cut while the plane was still swamped, if it shipped water before it could get on step, if the storm got its hands on it—

Floats weren't built for open water in a monsoon. Neither was she. But she'd spent twenty years learning exactly how much punishment a hull could take, and she knew — bone-deep, salt-deep — that this plane couldn't take much.

"Cut the line," she said to Adaeze, "and we take the crates back."

Adaeze looked at her daughter. Rain sheeted off her jaw. Something crossed her face that Theo had no name for yet — pride maybe, or grief, or the specific terror of a mother watching her child choose the harder right.

"You'll drown," Adaeze said.

"Probably not," Theo said. "I'm very buoyant. Ask anyone I've ever salvaged."

Voss raised the pistol. "Enough."

What happened next happened in the space of a single wave.

The surge hit the mooring post and the post *moved* — tilted, roots of it groaning in the drowned mud — and in that lurch Theo hauled the pirogue alongside the float and went over the side with her knife already out. The water took her like a fist. Cold, brown, violent, full of debris. She surfaced gasping against the float's hull, spat river, and started cutting.

Three strokes of the knife. That's all she needed. The line was wet nylon, swollen, miserable, and the blade bit through it stroke by stroke while bullets — she registered distantly that Voss was shooting, that the shots were going wide because the plane was rolling and Voss was rolling with it — slapped the water around her like fat raindrops with intentions.

Above her, muffled through hull and storm, she heard Adaeze's voice rise into the register Theo had only ever heard in lecture halls — the voice that made rooms go quiet. She was shouting at the loading crew. Telling them the dock was failing. Telling them to save themselves. And they believed her, because Adaeze Oyelaran had spent forty years being the most credible person in any room she entered, and men who loot antiquities are still men, and men run.

The fourth stroke went through. The line parted.

The seaplane swung free.

For one beautiful, catastrophic moment, it simply floated — dead in the water, shipping spray, engine coughing as some brave idiot tried to start it. Theo hauled herself onto the float, knife between her teeth, and got one hand on the strut.

Then the river decided.

The current that had been building all morning found the untethered plane and took it sideways, and the world became motion. Theo clung to the strut as the float dug, as water came over the top of her in a solid green wall, as the plane yawed beam-on to the waves and began, with terrible dignity, to ship water through the door someone had left open. Inside, she heard screaming. Crates shifting. Voss shouting numbers — *weight, weight, we're overweight* — a man discovering that greed has mass.

The plane rolled further. Theo's grip tore loose.

The river folded her under.

Down there it was quiet, which was the worst part. Brown murk, a crate sinking past her trailing packing straw, her own hair a black rope in the water. Her lungs lit up. She kicked for the light, found it wrong — sideways, moving — corrected, kicked again, and broke surface thirty meters downstream of the plane with her arms already burning.

The seaplane was on its side. One float pointed at the sky like a reproach. Waves were breaking over the exposed hull, and through the open door she could see crates sliding out one by one, sinking, the river accepting its inheritance. Two of Voss's men were clinging to the upward float. A third surfaced near Theo, thrashing; she grabbed his collar on reflex, got his head up, and shoved him toward a floating fuel drum without slowing down.

Where was Adaeze.

*Mama—*

There. On the high float, wedged against the strut, manifest board still strapped to her chest like a shield, one arm hooked through the mooring cleat. Alive. Holding on with the stubbornness of a woman who had survived worse men than rivers.

And Voss — Voss was in the water too, or part of him was; Theo caught a flash of gray hair and white shirt upstream, clawing toward the drifting wreck of his own ambition, and then the rain closed over him like a curtain and he was gone into the storm. Not dead, she'd have bet anything. Men like Voss didn't die. They resurfaced somewhere with dry socks and a new story. But he was empty-handed, and that was its own kind of drowning.

The evidence was downstream. Imani's voice, Imani's ledger, eight years of receipts riding the same flood that was eating Voss's empire. Whatever the river kept, it kept for a reason.

Theo swam.

It took her four tries to reach the float. The current fought her the whole way, and on the third try a wave picked her up and threw her against the hull hard enough to ring her skull, and she went under again tasting blood and diesel. On the fourth try she got a hand on Adaeze's ankle, then her wrist, then the cleat, and dragged herself up beside her mother into the hammering rain.

They lay there together on the side of a dying seaplane in the middle of a flooding valley, gasping like landed fish.

"You," Adaeze managed, "cut my plane loose."

"It was never your plane."

"I signed for it."

"You signed for everything he put in front of you," Theo said. "That's over now."

Adaeze turned her head. Rain ran off her nose. Up close, older, soaked, furious, terrified, she was still unmistakably the woman from the photographs on Theo's childhood shelf — the ones Theo had taken down at seventeen and never put back up.

"You came," Adaeze said. "After everything. You came upriver into a monsoon for me."

"I came for the salvage fee," Theo said.

"Liar."

"Yeah," Theo said. "Liar."

The float shuddered beneath them. The plane was going down in increments, settling into the drowned camp, and the water around it was thick with drifting crates and packing foam and one slowly rotating museum stencil. Theo watched a crate sink — watched three thousand years of somebody's hands go under — and felt the loss like a physical wound, and then, underneath the wound, something harder and cleaner.

Some things you save. Some things you spend. The river would keep what it kept, and the pieces that survived would surface someday for people who asked better questions. What mattered was what stayed above water: a mother on a float, a ledger downstream, a financier with nothing left to sell.

"Hold on to me," Theo said. "The boathouse roof is still dry-ish, and I know the way."

"There's a current between here and there."

"There's a current everywhere, Mama. That's the job."

Adaeze laughed — a cracked, disbelieving, drowned-sounding laugh, the first one Theo had heard from her in twenty years — and hooked her arm through her daughter's.

They went into the water together.

* * *

The boathouse roof held. Of course it held; it had been built by river people, and river people built things that argued with floods and won on points. Theo hauled Adaeze up the last stretch of ladder and collapsed beside her on the wet tin, both of them flat on their backs under a sky that had finally, finally begun to spend itself instead of rage.

In the distance, the seaplane's tail fin slipped under with a sound like a sigh.

Somewhere south, a radio was still transmitting.

And somewhere closer — a whistle, thin and defiant, cutting through the rain from the ridge above the camp. Three notes. Their signal. Imani, feverish, furious, alive, calling them home.

Theo lifted one arm off the roof and waved it until her shoulder screamed.

"She's going to say I told you so," Adaeze said.

"She's going to say it for the rest of our lives," Theo said. "We should probably get used to hearing it together."

Adaeze looked at her — really looked, the way archaeologists look at something they've waited their whole careers to find — and said nothing at all, which from her had always been the loudest sentence there was.

The rain thinned. The river kept rising. And downstream, in the dark, the evidence was already arriving.
