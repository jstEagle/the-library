## 19. Aftermath of Water

I woke to the sound of someone crying, and it took me a long time to understand that it was the river.

Not a person. Not Adaeze, though she had earned the right. The river itself — swollen past its banks, dragging whole trees downstream like a man hauling furniture out of a flooded house, muttering and groaning against the shrine island's stone skirt. It had gotten what it wanted. It had eaten the camp, the docks, the crates, the Marrow, and now it was digesting, and the sound it made was grief or satisfaction or both.

I lay on the priest's floor with my malaria shaking loose in my bones and took inventory, because inventory was what I did when I couldn't do anything else. One caretaker priest named Ekwueme, who had given us his back room without asking a single question he didn't need answered. One archaeologist, sleeping in the corner with her hands still curled as if around a manifest pen. One salvage diver, not asleep at all — I could tell by her breathing, which was the breathing of a woman watching the ceiling and losing arguments to it. One radio, cracked housing, repaired twice by my own hands, silent since dawn. One ledger's worth of evidence, broadcast into the storm two nights ago and gone wherever broadcasts go.

Zero boats.

The Marrow. I kept reaching for the thought of her the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore. Eight days we'd had her. She leaked like a confession — Theo's phrase, delivered with something almost like affection — and she'd carried three people up a river that didn't want them, through rapids and soldiers and a storm that came early specifically, I believed, to spite us. And in the end we'd cut her loose at the loading dock and let the flood take her, ballast tanks open, hull filling, going down alongside Voss's seaplane moorings like a deliberate act of sabotage, which is exactly what she was.

She'd been a bad boat. I missed her like a limb.

* * *

Ekwueme brought cassava and smoked fish at midmorning, setting the bowls down with the unhurried precision of a man who had fed refugees before and expected to again. He was seventy if he was fifty, with white hair cropped close and hands that moved like they'd done everything twice.

"The water will hold high for nine days," he said. "Maybe twelve. After that, the launches come from the mission station." He looked at me. "Your transmission. It was heard?"

"I don't know."

"You know."

I ate instead of answering. Across the room, Theo sat up slowly, and I watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing all night: no boat, no radio confirmation, a financier with money enough to buy silence running loose somewhere downstream with nothing but a seaplane and his life.

"He got away," Theo said. It wasn't a question either.

"Into the storm," I said. "With empty hands. That's not nothing, Theo. That's a man who flew out of a war with nothing but the clothes he was wearing and the knowledge that a woman he'd never taken seriously just ended him."

"He doesn't know it's ended."

"He knows the manifests are gone. He knows the ledger exists. He knows the woman who kept copies for eight years has a voice that carries." I turned the fish over in my bowl. "Men like Voss survive on the assumption that nobody keeps receipts. We kept receipts."

Adaeze stirred in her corner. She'd been awake longer than any of us; I'd heard her turn toward every sound in the night, the way mothers sleep even when they aren't sleeping. When she spoke, her voice was rough, unused.

"The shipments," she said. "The ones already sent downriver before the storm. Three consignments left the camp in the last month. If he has buyers waiting—"

"Then those buyers are about to have company." I reached under the floor mat where I'd wedged it the night we arrived, soaked and half-drowned, and pulled out the notebook. My real one. Not the copy I'd broadcast — the original, eight years of it: dates, site numbers, crate counts, names of Voss's men, the medical records I'd falsified nothing in and been accused of falsifying everything in. Water-stained now. Still legible. "The mission station at Akassa has a shortwave set. The coastal authority has an office there. And the consignments have to come through the salt passage to reach any buyer worth selling to."

Adaeze looked at the notebook the way she must once have looked at artifacts — reading strata, reading history, reading what a thing cost the person who carried it.

"Eight years," she said quietly. "You carried this for eight years."

"Somebody had to. Everyone else was busy being discredited."

Theo made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been too expensive this week.

* * *

The rain came back in the afternoon, softer, almost apologetic, and Ekwueme showed us the island while the light lasted. It was smaller than it had seemed in the dark — a shrine to a river goddess whose name he said only when facing the water, a courtyard of worn stones, a single canoe pulled above the flood line, older than any of us and tarred so many times it was more pitch than wood.

"This canoe has carried four generations of caretakers across in flood season," he said, patting the gunwale. "It does not go fast. But it does not sink."

"Neither do I," said Theo, "as a general rule," and I saw Adaeze watch her daughter say it and file it away somewhere behind her eyes, some account being reopened after twenty years of closure.

We stood at the island's edge where the water ran brown and enormous, carrying the monsoon's harvest: branches, roofing tin, a child's doll turning slowly, a dead antelope bloated and dignified. Somewhere under all of it was the camp. Somewhere under all of it were the crates we hadn't saved — the pieces that had gone down with the docks, Benin bronzes maybe, or older, things Adaeze could have catalogued by memory and grief.

"I signed for all of it," Adaeze said to the water. "Every crate. My signature is on the paperwork of their theft."

"And your testimony is on the record of their recovery," I said. "That's how this ends. Not with the river deciding. With you standing in a courtroom saying exactly what happened, in your name, which they stole and you're taking back."

She didn't answer. But she straightened, slightly, the way a flagpole straightens when someone finally runs the flag up.

* * *

On the third day, the radio spoke.

I'd been nursing it along on Ekwueme's battery ration, listening to static shaped like voices, and then at 0600 the static opened and a voice read numbers — coordinates first, then a string I recognized because I'd written it myself as a verification code years ago, back when I still believed institutions worked. A confirmation ping from the coastal authority at Akassa.

Then the operator's voice, flat and official and beautiful:

"Two consignments intercepted at the estuary checkpoint. Cargo seized pending provenance review. Be advised: warrant issued for one H. Voss, residence abroad, charges pending. Repeat: warrant issued."

Theo was standing behind me before I finished writing it down. She read the page upside down, which was a habit of hers I'd noticed and decided not to tease her about, because teasing required a surplus of something none of us had.

"They got them," she said. "The shipments. They actually—"

"Two of three." I set the pencil down. My hands were doing the malaria tremor again, or possibly not the malaria. "The third went out ahead of the intercepts. Before your mother ever signed it. Before we ever knew."

Silence. Then Adaeze, from the doorway, wrapped in Ekwueme's spare cloth, her voice steady in the way of a woman who had spent weeks practicing steadiness at gunpoint:

"The Olokun head. It went out in the first shipment. I catalogued it myself." She closed her eyes. "I told myself it would be recovered someday. That documentation was survival. That if I recorded everything precisely enough, the record itself would be a kind of rescue."

"It will be," I said. "Records are patient. Heads are heavy. Somebody, somewhere, is going to try to sell that piece, and when they do, your catalogue will meet it like a fingerprint meets a hand."

Theo looked between us — the disgraced medic and the coerced archaeologist, planning the long game over cassava porridge on a drowned island — and shook her head slowly.

"You're both impossible," she said. "You know that? The boat's gone, the storm's still here, and you're talking about fingerprints."

"What else would we talk about?" I asked.

And she laughed — really laughed, short and cracked and surprised out of her, the first one I'd heard from her in eleven days of knowing her — and Adaeze smiled like a woman hearing a language she used to speak.

* * *

That night I sat with Ekwueme on his step while the others slept, and he asked me the question nobody had asked yet.

"Why did you come? Truly. The diver had her mother. You had—" he gestured at the notebook under my arm "—paper."

I thought about lying. He'd earned better.

"Eight years ago I watched men carry history out of the ground in crates marked as medical supplies, and I reported it, and the world chose to believe a rich man over a field medic because rich men have lawyers and medics have notebooks." The river muttered below us. "So when I saw a woman arguing with boatmen about a river nobody would sail, I knew she was sailing it anyway, and I thought: here is someone the world has also chosen not to believe. Maybe two unbelievables make a believable."

"And did they?"

"Not yet. Ask me after the trial."

He nodded as if trials were weather, which on this river they nearly were.

"The goddess takes and gives back," he said. "Not always the same things. Not always in the same condition." He tapped my knee with one finger, gentle as a doctor. "But she keeps accounts too. Your notebook and her ledger are the same book, I think."

I looked out at the black water moving endlessly past the island, carrying its stolen cargo of trees and roofs and old griefs toward the sea, and I let myself feel it — all of it, the exhaustion sitting in me like silt, the Marrow somewhere downstream as a reef for fish, the eight years, the three consignments, the two recovered and the one lost, the warrant with Voss's name on it that might or might not ever become handcuffs.

Quiet triumph. That was the honest shape of it. Nothing gleaming. Just paper beating money, for once, in a small way, on a big river.

Inside, Theo's breathing had finally slowed into actual sleep. Adaeze's hadn't. Some accounts take longer.

Nine days, maybe twelve, until the launches came. Nine days of cassava and rain and a mother and daughter learning each other's voices again in a room barely bigger than a grave.

I opened the notebook to a fresh page and wrote the date, and under it, the first entry of whatever this was now:

*Day one. All hands accounted for. Boat lost, cause: sacrifice. Cargo lost, cause: same. Evidence intact.*

*Awaiting river's decision.*
