## 20. Days, Not Miles

The rain stopped on the ninth day, and neither of us knew what to do with the silence.

I had spent my whole life filling silence — with lectures, with field reports, with the careful recitation of facts that proved I was useful, that I deserved my place at a table. On the shrine island there was no table. There was the caretaker's hut with its leaking palm roof, the fire pit under an overhang of woven fronds, and the long gray hours where the river breathed and nothing needed to be catalogued, defended, or explained.

Theo sat across the fire from me, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen. She had been doing it for twenty minutes. The blade had been sharp since the second day.

"You'll wear it to a sliver," I said.

"It passes time."

"There's no time to pass here. That's rather the point."

She looked up at me then, and I braced for the old flint in her face, the look she'd worn at seventeen when she told me she was leaving and not coming back, the look that said *you taught me this*. But the rain had been falling on both of us for nine days, and flint softens. What I saw instead was exhaustion, and beneath it something almost like curiosity — as if she were seeing me for the first time without the frame of my reputation around me. Without the frame of her anger, either.

"Say what you're thinking," she said. "You always used to do that. Lecture mode."

"I'm not lecturing."

"You are. You just haven't gotten to the part with dates."

I laughed. It came out of me unexpectedly, a rusty sound, and Theo's mouth twitched despite herself. The caretaker, old Ekon, glanced over from where he was mending a fishing net by the waterline, saw us laughing, and returned to his work with the studied indifference of a man who understood that some repairs should be left alone to hold themselves.

* * *

The days had no numbers on them anymore. That was the strangest gift of the monsoon's tail. For twenty years I had lived by schedules — grant deadlines, excavation seasons, flight manifests, the countdown clocks of other people's expectations. And for the last months before the flood, I had lived by Voss's schedule, which was worse: the hourly arithmetic of survival, of how much longer I could be useful before usefulness expired.

Here there was only weather. Rain, then less rain, then the river rising or falling, then hunger, then food if Ekon's traps were lucky. We measured life in small units now. A pot of smoked fish stewed with island peppers. A dry hour in which to spread our soaked clothing on rocks. The particular quality of light through cloud that meant evening was coming whether we wanted it or not.

Theo hated it at first. I could see it in her hands — they were never still. She checked the Marrow's remains washed up on our shore, though there was nothing left of the boat but splinters and a length of chain. She paced the island's circumference twice a day, reading the water like a pilot, calculating distances to towns that might as well have been on the moon until the rivers dropped.

"You're measuring again," I told her on the fourth day after the flood.

"I'm assessing."

"You're counting miles."

"And you're counting artifacts." She said it without heat, which somehow made it land harder. "You spent three weeks in that camp memorizing every piece that went into those crates. Don't tell me you weren't."

"I was," I admitted. "It kept me alive. If I could name a thing — its period, its provenance, its people — then it wasn't just loot. It was evidence. Evidence meant testimony. Testimony meant someday."

"Someday." She turned the word over like a stone she didn't trust. "You always believed in someday. Someday you'd be home. Someday we'd talk. Someday the work would slow down."

"And you believed in leaving first," I said, "so no one could ever leave you."

The words were out before I could weigh them, and I watched them strike. Theo went very still, the way water goes still before it takes the shape of whatever holds it. For a moment I thought she would stand and walk to the far side of the island, the way she had walked out of my house at seventeen with one bag and no backward glance.

Instead she sat down.

"That's fair," she said quietly. "That's fair, and it's cruel, and you learned cruelty from somewhere too."

* * *

We talked that night until the fire burned down to coals and Ekon pretended to sleep in his corner of the hut while listening to every word.

Not all at once. Not the great accounting either of us had perhaps imagined — no single conversation where twenty years were reconciled line by line, debts entered and forgiven. It came in pieces, the way silt comes down a river, unglamorous and constant, settling into layers.

She asked about my last expedition — the real one, the scandal, the thing that had cost her her name before it cost me mine. I told her the truth I had never told a journalist: that I had signed the report I knew was wrong because two of my students were still in the field and Voss's men had made it clear what happened to people who obstructed shipments. I had chosen their safety over my integrity and called it pragmatism, and I had let the world call it fraud, and I had never once corrected the record because correcting it would have meant naming Voss, and naming Voss would have ended everything — my career, my students' careers, possibly their lives.

"You could have told me," Theo said. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated that it cracked; I saw her jaw set against it. "You could have told *me*. I was a kid. I didn't need the whole truth. I needed to know my mother wasn't—"

"Wasn't what the papers said."

"Wasn't a liar." She stared into the fire. "Do you know what I did with your name? Every town I worked, every dock, every salvage contract — someone eventually figured out who I was. Oyelaran. The fraud's daughter. So I stopped using it. Twenty years, Mama. I gave away my own name so yours couldn't hurt me, and it hurt more, not less, because then nobody knew me at all."

I reached across the fire pit. I did not touch her — I had learned long ago that Theo received touch the way stone receives rain, slowly, on her own terms — but I laid my hand on the warm earth between us, palm up, an offering.

"Theo," I said. "Your name was never mine to damage. I'm sorry is too small a word. But it's the truest one I have, and I've had three weeks in a cage to learn how heavy small true words are."

She looked at my hand. She did not take it. But she did not move away from it either, and that night, when we lay down under the leaking roof, she slept closer to me than she had since she was a child — close enough that I could hear her breathing change as she fell asleep, the held breath of a diver finally surfacing.

* * *

On the sixth day the sun came properly out, and the island steamed, and Theo found me sitting at the shrine itself — the old stone at the island's heart, worn smooth by centuries of hands, ringed with offerings Ekon tended: cowries, river clay figures, a child's sandal bleached white.

"Do you believe in it?" she asked, nodding at the stone. "The shrine. The passage. All of it?"

"I believe in what people carry to it," I said. "That's the archaeologist's answer. The object tells you about the hand that held it."

"That's a dodge."

"That's a profession." I smiled. "But no. I don't know what I believe anymore. I believed in the record once — that if we wrote things down truly, the truth would protect itself. Then I watched a man buy silence for thirty years and learned the record can be purchased like anything else. Now I think belief is less like a monument and more like a boat. You patch it. You bail it. You keep going because stopping means sinking."

Theo sat beside me on the flat stone. Up close I could see how thin she'd gotten, the tendons standing in her forearms, the salt-and-river weariness carved around her eyes. My daughter, who had become a stranger and then a comrade and then, somewhere in the flooding dark of that camp, my rescuer — and whom I still did not entirely know.

"Why did you come?" I asked. It was the question I had carried since the night she walked into the loading yard wearing a courier's jacket, playing a stranger, playing it so well it broke something in me. "Not for the fee. Not really. Why?"

She was quiet a long time. The river moved past us, brown and swollen and indifferent, carrying the monsoon's debris toward the sea.

"A telegram burned badly," she said finally. "I tried to burn it and it wouldn't go. Kept curling back up off the plate. Like it knew." A breath. "I told myself it was the salvage fee. I told myself a lot of things. But Imani says—" she huffed, almost a laugh, "Imani says I argue with boatmen the way you argue with review boards. Same stubbornness. Same refusal to lose. I came because I couldn't not come. Because the wound was never healed, I just stopped looking at it. And because—" her voice dropped, roughened, "because I found out you can hate someone for twenty years and it doesn't survive contact with them drowning."

I let that sit between us. Some truths you don't answer; you witness them.

"I measured my life in miles," she went on. "Every job, every port. Never unpacked a bag. Never stayed. Staying meant being known, and being known meant being compared to you. Out here—" she gestured at the small green prison of the island, the drowned world beyond it, "out here there are no miles. Just days. And I don't know how to live in days, Mama. I don't know what I am if I'm not leaving."

"Then learn," I said. "You learned the river. You learned diving, piloting, surviving men with guns. This is smaller than any of those."

"Is it?"

"It's harder," I said honestly. "But it's smaller. It's just showing up tomorrow. And the day after. No navigation required."

* * *

Ekon taught us to fish his way that week — or taught Theo, rather, while I sat on the bank and offered commentary that Theo informed me was unnecessary, and I informed her was maternal, and we discovered, both at once, that we were joking with each other. Teasing. The muscle had atrophied for twenty years and yet it remembered.

He also talked, in his slow way, in the evenings. About the woman who had come upriver weeks before us, asking after trade routes, weeping at this same shrine and refusing to leave it. He described her without knowing he was describing me — the stubbornness, the grief, the way she touched the stone like it owed her an answer. Theo listened to him tell it and then looked at me across the fire, and I watched her revise me in real time: not the monument, not the fraud, not the distant brilliant mother whose shadow she'd fled. Just a woman, tired and frightened, asking stones for help.

"That's you," she said. "That's actually you."

"That's actually me."

"Huh," she said, and it was the most forgiving sound I had heard from her in two decades.

* * *

On the eighth day, Imani's fever broke for good.

She had been drifting in and out since the flood — malaria chewing through her in waves, Theo nursing her with a competence that surprised no one who knew her and would have astonished everyone who thought they did. When Imani finally sat up, hollow-eyed and clear-headed, she looked at the two of us — me mending net with Ekon, Theo re-lashing the shelter's frame — and said, "You two have been talking."

"We have," I said.

"About the important things?"

"Some of them."

"Good." She accepted the cup of broth Theo handed her and drank it like a woman taking an oath. "Because the rivers will drop soon. Maybe a week. Maybe ten days. And when they drop, a launch comes, and the world comes back, and the world has opinions about us. Voss's lawyers. The authorities who'll want statements. Journalists." She fixed us both with the level gaze of a woman who had been discredited once and had spent eight years sharpening herself against it. "So enjoy the days. Because the miles are coming back."

Theo and I looked at each other over Imani's head, and something passed between us — not forgiveness complete, nothing so finished as that, but a truce with a future in it. An agreement to keep talking after the talking became difficult again.

"We'll be ready," Theo said.

"No, we won't," I said. "But we'll be together, and that's better preparation than ready."

Imani snorted into her broth. "You two are going to be insufferable."

And Theo laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound I had not heard since she was sixteen and still lived under my roof — and the sound went out over the water like something released, and Ekon looked up from his nets and smiled at the sky as if the river itself had finally agreed to something.

* * *

That night I woke near dawn to find Theo already awake, sitting at the edge of the water, watching the east go pale.

I sat beside her. Neither of us spoke for a long time. The river had begun its slow retreat; new mudbanks showed along the shores like the exposed bones of the world, and somewhere downstream, unseen, the current was carrying our evidence toward justice, and somewhere upstream, the wreckage of Voss's operation was being inventoried by men with clipboards, and none of it concerned us just now. For these few minutes, nothing needed doing.

"Mama," she said at last. The word still fit strangely in her mouth, like a tool recovered from deep water, unfamiliar with use. "When the launch comes. What happens to us?"

"We go home. Whichever home we choose."

"I don't have one."

"Then choose one," I said. "Or build one. You're good with your hands. You patched a hull underwater in brown floodwater while a medic bailed with a cooking pot. Building a life is easier than that, and drier."

She almost smiled. Then her eyes went past me, upstream, and sharpened.

"Mama," she said, and her voice changed — the pilot's voice now, the voice that read water. "Look."

Far up the channel, where the flooded river narrowed toward the narrows, a shape was moving against the current. Small. Deliberate. Too early for the rescue launch, too steady for driftwood.

A boat. Coming toward the shrine island.

And standing in its bow, even at this distance, unmistakable — a tall figure in a pale suit, one hand raised against the gray morning light, watching us watch him.

Theo rose slowly to her feet beside me, and I felt the days end and the miles begin again.
