## 21. What We Carry Out

The river dropped the way a fever breaks — not all at once, but in visible increments, each morning revealing another arm's length of mud bank, another drowned crate washed clean and empty on the shoals, another stretch of the Marrow's final resting place. She lay on her side in the shallows off the shrine island's eastern point, half-buried in silt, her hull split along the seam that had nearly killed us in the narrows. The storm had finished what the stakes started.

I went out to her every morning anyway. I told myself it was to salvage what was worth salvaging — the brass fittings, the anchor chain, the engine block if I could get it free before the silt took it. But the truth was simpler and less useful: I couldn't stop looking at her. Twenty years of boats, and I'd never once watched one die. I'd always been gone by then, already upriver or downcoast, already measuring the next distance.

Now I had nowhere to be, and the boat I'd learned to love was drowning in slow motion, and I stood knee-deep in brown water with my hands on her gunwale like a man at a bedside.

"You're going to rust," Imani said from behind me. She'd come down the path with two mugs of the caretaker's bitter tea, moving carefully. The malaria had left her thinner, slower, her hands still unsteady some mornings. But she was walking. She was here. Eight days ago she'd been delirious against my shoulder while I carried her through flooded reeds, and now she was handing me tea and judging my life choices, which felt about right.

"She's already rusting."

"Then maybe stop holding a vigil." She looked at the Marrow the way you look at a photograph of someone who can't hear you anymore. "She got us here. That's more than most boats manage."

"That's more than most people manage."

We drank our tea standing in the shallows while the water slid past us toward the sea, carrying whatever it carried. Somewhere downstream — hours down, days down, past the narrows and the fishing village and the last functioning river town — there were authorities who had Imani's ledger now. Copies of copies of documents she'd kept hidden for eight years inside a false-bottomed medical kit, through disgrace and unemployment and a monsoon. Voss's signatures. Voss's men. Voss's smuggling routes mapped in his own courier's hand. The evidence had traveled faster than any of us could have, because evidence travels light.

The rest of us were heavier.

* * *

The caretaker priest's name was Ekwueme, and he had decided without asking anyone's permission that we were his responsibility until the water let us go. He fed us fish and cassava from the island's small gardens, taught Imani which leaves brought fevers down, and never once asked us what we'd seen at the camp upstream. He knew. Of course he knew. The river tells him things, Adaeze said. He'd known about the woman who wept but would not leave; he'd known about soldiers and crates and a seaplane that left in the teeth of a storm and did not come back.

On the ninth morning, Ekwueme climbed the hill to where I sat sharpening the diving knife against a stone, and said, "There is a launch coming up the channel. Government paint. They will want names."

"How far?"

"Two hours, if the channel is kind. It is not kind."

I put the knife down. Two hours. I'd spent twenty years making sure I always had more than two hours' warning before anything caught up with me — a lease ending, a harbor closing, a phone call I didn't want to take. Two hours was nothing. Two hours was the time it took to pack a bag and become someone else.

Except I didn't have a bag. Everything I owned had gone down with the Marrow or burned in the camp flood or was currently drying on a line outside Ekwueme's hut, smelling of woodsmoke and river. My whole life fit on a clothesline. I found this funny in a way I couldn't explain to anyone, so I just sat there grinning at the knife until Ekwueme gave me the look priests give you when they've decided your soul is a salvage job they're not qualified for.

Adaeze was in the garden. Imani was sleeping off the tail end of the fever. And in two hours a government launch would arrive, and someone would ask me where I wanted to go, and for the first time in my adult life I would have no answer prepared.

* * *

I found my mother kneeling in the wet earth with her sleeves rolled up, transplanting seedlings with the same focused precision she'd once used on Bronze Age burial sites. Watching her hands work, I thought about manifests. About her signature, coerced onto documents that moved looted history downriver in crates marked as scientific equipment. About how she'd catalogued every stolen piece from memory, planning to testify, keeping herself alive by being useful — and how I'd spent three weeks hating myself for ever believing, even for one telegram's length of time, that she might have signed those papers freely.

"I heard the launch," she said without looking up. "Ekwueme sent word an hour ago. You two argue loudly when you think I'm asleep."

"We weren't arguing."

"You were arguing with yourself. It carries."

I crouched beside her in the dirt. The seedlings were something green and stubborn, the kind of plant that grows back after floods. "They'll want statements. From both of us. There'll be officials, and press probably, and—" I stopped. "There'll be a boat going downstream, is the point. Going anywhere."

"And you've already chosen somewhere." It wasn't a question. She pressed soil around a seedling's roots with her thumbs, firm and gentle at once. "You always know the exit before you've entered the room. You get that from me. I'm sorry."

"I don't have anywhere."

"Then that's new." She finally looked up. Her face was thinner than it had been in the camp, the coercion and the fear and then the rescue carved out of it, but her eyes were the same eyes that had measured me across kitchen tables and airport lounges for twenty years, always finding me slightly wanting, always loving me anyway in a language I refused to learn. "Theo. When they ask you where you want to go — don't answer right away. Sit with not knowing for a minute. It won't kill you. I checked."

"When did you check?"

"The night the seaplane cut loose." She went back to the seedlings. "I thought we were going to drown, and my last thought wasn't regret about the artifacts. It was that I'd wasted twenty years being right instead of being present. So. Consider me checked."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that the last eight days hadn't already said — the long nights on the island talking, actually talking, for the first time since I was seventeen, working through the scandal and the silence and the name I'd lost and the expedition she'd lost and everything the river had taken and given back. We'd excavated twenty years of sediment between us, layer by layer, and neither of us had finished. But we'd started. That was more than either of us had managed since I walked out of her house with a duffel bag and a chip on my shoulder the size of her reputation.

"I'll come find you," I said, standing. "After the launch. After the statements. Wherever you land."

"I know." She smiled without looking up. "You always do. You just usually leave first so you don't have to watch yourself do it."

* * *

The launch came around the island's northern point at midday, riding low with supplies, government grey with a flag snapping in the post-storm wind. Three men aboard: a district officer with a clipboard, a police sergeant who looked at our faces like he was memorizing them for later, and a young radio operator who couldn't stop staring at Adaeze, because apparently even after everything, Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran was still famous enough to be recognized by a boy who listened to frequencies for a living.

The district officer asked for names. We gave them. He asked what happened at the camp, and Imani told it — flat, precise, devastating — in the voice of a woman who had rehearsed testimony for eight years without knowing it. Voss's operation. The coerced signatures. The crates. The ledger, already in their hands downstream, corroborated by manifests recovered from the flooded records office. The officer's pen stopped moving twice. The sergeant whistled once, low.

Then the officer looked up and said the sentence I'd been bracing against for two hours:

"We can take you downstream today. Where would you like to be dropped?"

And I opened my mouth to give the automatic answer — the coast, the nearest port, anywhere with a dock and a departure — and stopped. Because Adaeze was watching me, and Imani was watching me, and the river was sliding past the hull carrying everything it carried, and for the first time in twenty years I let the silence sit there. Not knowing. It didn't kill me. It turned out to be survivable, this not knowing. Almost comfortable, even. Like a boat that leaks but floats.

"I haven't decided," I said.

The officer blinked. People who ask that question expect an answer the way rivers expect downhill. "Take your time," he said, uncertainly. "We're here until tomorrow's tide."

* * *

Imani found me at sunset on the eastern point, sitting on the Marrow's exposed keel like a mourner on a headstone. The water had dropped another handspan. By tomorrow she'd be high and dry, a skeleton in the mud, and there was nothing left in her worth taking except the lesson.

"There you are," Imani said. She lowered herself onto the mud beside me, not caring about her trousers, which told me the fever was truly gone. The old Imani would have found a log. "The officer's offering bunks on the launch. Real beds. Hot food. I said we'd think about it."

"'We'?"

"Don't make it weird." She pulled her knees up. Out on the channel, egrets were coming down to roost in the mangroves, white shapes folding into green. "Eight years ago I lost a career because I told the truth about Halvard Voss and nobody with power wanted to hear it. Last week, everybody with power heard it. Do you understand what that does to a person? I keep waiting to feel finished. Vindicated. Whatever the word is. And instead I just feel—" She waved a hand at the river. "Light. Unanchored. Like I handed off a weight I'd been carrying so long I forgot it was heavy, and now I don't know what shape I am without it."

"I know the feeling."

"You would." She glanced sideways at me. "Twenty years of running. What shape are you without it?"

"I was going to say the coast. When he asked. Coast, port, next job, next distance." I picked up a piece of dried mud and broke it. "It came out of my mouth before the question finished. Twenty years of reflex. And then I heard myself, and I thought — why? Why the coast? Because it's away from here? Away from what?"

"Your mother's on this island."

"My mother's on this island," I agreed. "And for the first time since I was seventeen, leaving her feels like losing something instead of escaping it. Which is its own kind of terrifying, honestly. I built a whole personality on the escaping."

"I noticed. It was very annoying."

I laughed. It came out rough, half-swallowed, the way laughter does when it hasn't had much practice lately. Downstream, somewhere past the darkening bends, the authorities were intercepting Voss's remaining shipments using a dead woman's careful handwriting — no, not dead, I corrected myself, a woman who'd refused to stay buried — and Halvard Voss himself was somewhere ahead of the net, having escaped into the storm with nothing but his plane and his name, and his name was worth nothing now. Justice, served cold and slow and outside the law's usual pace, but served. Imani had done that. With paper. With patience. With eight years of refusing to let go.

"What happens to you?" I asked. "When the tide comes. Medically, you're cleared to work again, you know. The board will want you back. Disgraced-then-vindicated reads well in the journals."

"The board," Imani said, "can write me a letter." She was quiet a moment. Then: "I've been thinking about boats."

"Have you."

"A person with my particular skill set — field medicine, documentation, an allergy to institutional authority — could be useful on the water. River work. Coastal work. Salvage operations, say, that occasionally stumble into criminal conspiracies and require someone to patch bullet holes and forge alibis."

"That was one time."

"It was one time *so far*." She turned to face me fully, and in the last light her expression was the one she'd worn at the dock in that river town, the day she'd offered her skills and half her savings to a stranger arguing with boatmen — guarded, testing, hopeful underneath the armor. Except the armor was mostly gone now. The river had taken that too. "I'm not asking to crew your boat, Theo. I'm asking whether you're going to have one. Because if the answer is yes, I'd like my name on it. Co-signed. Equal shares. If the answer is no, tell me now, and I'll take the launch tomorrow and we'll part as friends, and that'll be fine too."

I looked at her for a long moment. Found family, I thought. That was the phrase for it, the one people used, though nobody ever told you it wouldn't feel like finding anything. It felt like building. Like driving stakes into a riverbed and hoping the current didn't pull them loose. Like choosing, deliberately, in full knowledge of the flooding, to moor yourself to another person's hull.

"I'm going to need a medic," I said. "Someone has to talk me out of diving under hulls in brown water."

"Someone absolutely does."

"And a co-signer who keeps better records than I do."

"Not a high bar."

"And—" I stopped. Looked out at the channel, at the dark coming down over the water, at the place where the river widened toward the sea and the salt began. "And someone who stays. That's the real qualification. Everyone else in my life has been temporary by design. I need one person whose whole plan is to still be there next season."

Imani was quiet. Then she reached over and took the piece of dried mud out of my hand and set it down, and gripped my forearm the way divers grip each other — firm, deliberate, a signal that means *I have you*.

"Next season," she said. "And the one after. Write it into the contract."

* * *

At dawn the launch's horn sounded across the channel, long and low, and Ekwueme walked us down to the water's edge carrying a small bundle — dried fish, the fever leaves, a carved wooden fish he pressed into Adaeze's hands without explanation, because some gifts don't survive explanation.

Adaeze embraced Imani first. Then she turned to me, and for a moment we stood there in the awkward geometry of twenty years, two people who'd forgotten how the other one hugged. Then she solved it the way she solved everything — directly, without ceremony — and held on, and after a second I held on back.

"Coastal dig, three months from now," she said into my shoulder. "I consult. Honestly, this time. There's a site near the delta mouth, right where the fresh water meets the salt."

"I know the passage," I said. "I've run it a hundred times."

"Then run it a hundred and one." She stepped back, and her eyes were bright and dry, because Oyelarans cry privately or not at all. "Don't leave first this time. Let's see how it feels."

The district officer held the rail. Imani climbed aboard, then turned and offered me her hand — unnecessary, theatrical, exactly right. I took it. Behind us the shrine island receded, Ekwueme raising one hand in blessing or farewell, the Marrow's keel gleaming white in the mud like a bone the river had decided to return.

Ahead, the channel bent south, and the water tasted different already. I could feel it through the hull — the faint resistance, the old argument between current and tide, fresh meeting salt where neither wins and neither loses.

For the first time in twenty years, I wasn't measuring the distance to the next departure.

I was measuring the distance home.
