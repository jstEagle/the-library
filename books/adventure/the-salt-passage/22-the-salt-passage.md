## 22. The Salt Passage

The new boat doesn't have a name yet, and I've decided that's not a problem.

Imani disagrees. She's been keeping a list in the back of her ledger — the same ledger that brought down Halvard Voss — of names she thinks are candidates. *Persistence.* *Second Tide.* Something called *The Reckoning*, which I vetoed immediately because a boat named that is a boat that's asking for trouble. She writes them down anyway, in her small precise hand, and every morning there's a new one at the bottom of the page like she's leaving me notes I didn't ask for.

"You can't pilot an unnamed vessel," she says now, from the bow, where she's been sitting for an hour with her knees drawn up, watching the coastline resolve out of the haze.

"Sure you can. People did it for thousands of years."

"People also died of infected teeth for thousands of years. That's not an argument."

I let the engine answer for me. It's a good engine — secondhand but honest, the kind that tells you what it needs instead of sulking about it. The hull is fiberglass over wood, shallow-drafted enough for the delta channels and seaworthy enough for the coastal runs, which was the whole point. A boat that could live in both worlds. It took us four months to find her, three weeks to refit her, and one afternoon in a harbormaster's office to make it official: two signatures on the registration. Oyelaran. Sesay. Co-owners, co-signers, co-conspirators, if you asked anyone who'd followed the story, though nobody asks anymore. Eight months is a long time for a scandal to stay interesting, even one involving a collector with a seaplane, a forged archaeologist, and a river that ate a smuggling operation whole.

Voss is in custody in the capital, awaiting trial that keeps getting postponed. His lawyers are good. His money is better. But Imani's ledger is in a government vault now, forty pages of dated photographs and transcribed testimony from eight years ago, cross-referenced against manifests recovered from the flooded camp, and paper is patient in a way that lawyers aren't. Adaeze says trials like this take years. She says it without bitterness, which took practice. She spent six weeks after the rescue giving testimony — days of it, sitting in rooms with fans turning slowly overhead, cataloguing aloud every artifact she'd memorized under guard so the recovery teams would know what to look for on the riverbed where the crates went down. They recovered most of them. Some pieces are gone into private hands forever, sold before the net closed, and those losses sit in her like stones she's decided to carry rather than set down. But most of them came home. And Voss's signature is on documents that will follow him longer than his money will.

Justice outside the law's slow hands — that was Imani's phrase, back on the shrine island, when we were still wringing river water out of everything we owned. She'd earned the right to say it. Eight years of being told she was hysterical, unreliable, finished; eight years of clinics in towns too small to gossip, all because she'd written down what she saw. In the end the writing was the whole weapon. She just had to survive long enough to aim it.

Ahead of us, the coast opens like a held breath finally released.

* * *

We're running south along the shoreline toward Tano Point, where the university has a dig site — a real one this time, permitted, documented, staffed by graduate students who complain about sandflies the way students have complained since students existed. Adaeze consults there twice a week. Not directing. Consulting. She'd fought for that distinction herself, insisted on it in the contract negotiations, and when I asked her why she said, "Because I spent two years being the name on other people's paperwork. I want to be the advice, not the authority." Then she'd looked at me sideways, the way she does when she's said something true and wants to see if I caught it.

I caught it. I catch more of her than I used to.

Eight months ago I couldn't have told you my mother's laugh sounded like. Now I know it starts in her shoulders. I know she takes her tea bitter enough to strip paint and pretends it's a discipline rather than a preference. I know she hums when she catalogs — old highlife songs, half-remembered, always losing the melody at the same bar and starting over. I know she cried at the harbor the day the Marrow's replacement was delivered, not because of the boat but because Imani and I had argued about paint colors for twenty minutes in front of the seller like sisters, and Adaeze stood there watching us with her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if something in there had finally come loose.

We don't talk about the camp much. We don't need to. There's a language between us now that was built in floodwater, and some things don't bear repeating in ordinary air. What we talk about instead is smaller and stranger: whether the mangroves at Tano Point are older than the ones upriver. Whether Imani's malaria is truly done with her or just biding. Whether a person can love a place they never intended to stay.

That last one comes up a lot lately. Because I stayed. That's the thing nobody who knew me before would believe. Theo Oyelaran, who measured her life in miles, who kept her belongings packed in waterproof duffels for two decades so she could be on a boat within the hour — Theo Oyelaran signed a lease. Six months, unfurnished, in a town with a fish market and a post office and a harbormaster who already knows my coffee order. There are hooks in the walls. There is a shelf. My dive gear lives in a corner instead of a bag, and every morning I see it sitting there, settled, and every morning it looks less like surrender and more like furniture.

The telegram I burned in chapter one of this whole disaster — I think about it sometimes. How I fed it to the stove flame and watched my mother's name curl black, and how I lay awake afterward telling myself the ache in my chest was indigestion. It wasn't indigestion. It was twenty years of distance collapsing all at once, the way a levee goes: slowly, then catastrophically, then — if you're lucky, if someone is holding your hand in the water — rebuilt on new ground.

* * *

"Tano Point," Imani calls, pointing. "There. You can see the tarps."

I can. Pale rectangles strung between poles above the beach, and beyond them the low green shoulder of the headland where the midden trenches are cut into the earth like stitches. The dig sits right at the mouth of the passage — that stretch of water where the river finishes its long argument with the sea. Fresh meets salt and neither wins. The current runs both directions depending on the tide, and the color changes hour by hour, brown to green to that impossible bruised silver, and the fish come there to spawn because the confusion suits them. Local pilots call it the salt passage. It's famous for eating propellers and delivering enormous catches, sometimes in the same afternoon.

It's also, I've decided, mine.

Not owned. Occupied. There's a difference, and it took me forty years and one drowned boat to learn it. Ownership is what Voss wanted — title, provenance, the right to put his name on things that were never his. Occupation is quieter. You live somewhere until it lives in you, and then the question of whose it is stops mattering.

I bring us through the passage on the ebb, riding the outgoing fresh water against the incoming salt swell, and the hull does its little shudder where the layers shear past each other, and I feel it in my palms through the wheel like a pulse. Imani braces against the console beside me, grinning. She loves this part. She'd never admit it — she claims to be a person of laboratories and ledgers — but I've seen her face when the boat bucks through the seam between waters. It's the face of someone coming home to a place they've never been.

"Your mother's on the beach," she says. "Third tarp. Arguing with somebody."

"She's consulting. Arguing is the service she provides."

"No, I mean really arguing." Imani squints. "There's a man in a suit."

A man in a suit, at a coastal dig in the wet season, is never good news. I bring the boat around the point and into the lee of the breakwater, and I'm reaching for the mooring line when I see the second detail that matters: the suit isn't alone. There's a woman with him holding a leather portfolio, and both of them stand the particular way people stand when they're delivering news they believe is important.

Adaeze sees our boat and lifts one hand — not waving, exactly. Signaling. The way she used to signal across excavation sites: *come here, carefully.*

I tie off fast.

* * *

The man introduces himself as an attorney representing the Ministry of Antiquities, which turns out to mean he represents the committee overseeing the restitution of the Voss collection. The woman is a registrar. They have come to Tano Point because Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran is listed as primary consultant on the recovery inventory, and there is a matter requiring her consultation, and — the attorney hesitates, choosing words the way you choose footing on submerged stakes — a matter that may also concern her daughter.

I fold my arms. "Concern me how?"

"The salvage crews working the riverbed near the old camp site," the registrar says, opening the portfolio, "have located additional material. Below the silt line. Material that predates the expedition."

She lays photographs on the folding table beneath the tarp, weighting the corners with shells. Riverbed shots, grainy, taken in bad light by divers with silt-churned masks. And in them — I lean closer, and Imani leans closer, and Adaeze goes very still in the way I've learned means her mind is moving faster than her voice —

In them is timber. Carved timber, waterlogged, black as bog oak, in patterns no expedition crate ever carried. A prow fragment. A panel with figures in relief, their faces worn smooth by decades or centuries of current. And a bronze, small in the frame, crusted green, shaped like nothing any catalogued tradition in the region produces.

"We believe there's a wreck," the registrar says. "An old one. Possibly very old. The camp's flooding scoured the overburden off it — the storm that destroyed the smuggling operation exposed it." She allows herself the smallest pause, the professional's version of a held breath. "The ministry would like to mount a proper excavation. And they'll need pilots who know that stretch of river in all its moods. Pilots who can work the salt passage at low water." Her eyes move to me. "They asked us to extend the invitation personally."

Silence, except for the sea doing its work against the breakwater.

Adaeze picks up the photograph of the bronze and holds it at arm's length, tilting it against the light, and I watch my mother become entirely, radiantly herself — the scholar, the woman who reads objects like letters from the dead. "This alloy patina," she murmurs. "If this is what it appears to be, it's pre-contact trade goods. It would rewrite the coastal routes." She looks up, and her eyes find mine over the top of the photograph, and there's something in them that isn't scholarly at all. "Theo. This is a season-long project. Maybe longer."

"I heard."

"It would mean staying."

"I heard that too."

Imani makes a sound beside me that I recognize as her trying not to laugh. When I glance at her, she's looking at the boat — our unnamed, unnameable boat, riding easy at the mooring in the seam between waters — and then at me, and she shrugs with elaborate innocence. "I'm just thinking," she says, "that we haven't chosen a name yet. And that seems suddenly relevant."

The attorney clears his throat, sensing — correctly — that the decision is happening in a language he doesn't speak. "There's no obligation today. The ministry expects—"

"They expect an answer by the end of the week," Adaeze says, still watching me. "But I suspect we'll give one sooner."

I turn and look down the beach, past the tarps and the complaining students, to where the water runs both ways. The tide is turning. I can read it in the surface texture, in the way the current knots itself against the headland — the salt pushing in, the river refusing to yield, the two of them meeting in the middle and making something neither could make alone. For twenty years I ran from water like this, from every place that demanded I pick a shore. I thought staying meant drowning.

It doesn't. Staying means learning the tides.

I pick up the photograph of the carved prow and study the worn faces of the figures, people who loaded their goods onto a boat centuries ago and set out into a passage just like this one, and whatever happened to them, the river kept them safe until someone came who knew how to listen.

"Yeah," I say. "We'll take it."

"*We*," Imani repeats softly, tasting it.

"We." I hand the photograph back to the registrar. "But first — somebody help me name the boat. She's not answering to *hey, you* anymore, and I have a feeling she's going to be carrying all three of us for a while."

Adaeze smiles — the full one, the one that starts in her shoulders — and looks out at the water where fresh meets salt and neither wins, and says:

"Well. Let's begin there."
