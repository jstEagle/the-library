## 1. Miles, Not Years

The storm came off the flats like a debt collector—no warning, no grace period, just a wall of bruised cloud walking across the saltbush on legs of rain.

Ulyana Okonkwo drove toward it.

That was the whole trick of her life, if you wanted to be honest about it, and she was trying lately to be honest in the way that didn't cost anything. Most people ran from weather. She ran *toward* it, because a storm was the only thing in the world that moved faster than her problems and didn't ask questions while it passed over you. You parked the truck on the leading edge, let the hail hammer the roof like applause, and for forty minutes or four hours there was nothing in your head but pressure and wind speed and the animal joy of being exactly where the sky was angriest.

Her rig was a twenty-year-old chase truck named Bessie with a cracked windshield and a lightning rod welded to the rack by someone who'd loved her once. The anemometer on the mast read sixty knots and climbing. The flats unrolled gray-green to every horizon, stitched with the dry channels of rivers that had forgotten their own names.

She should have been thinking about the number. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve marks. That was what she owed the Optimists, give or take the interest that accrued while she slept, which they calculated to the minute because of course they did. The Optimists called themselves a lending syndicate, but nobody lent money out of cheerfulness. Their logo was a rising sun. Their collections division smiled the way crocodiles smile—with everything forward-facing and nothing behind the eyes.

Forty-one thousand marks. She'd done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth, like river stones. Storm data sold to the agricultural co-ops: eleven hundred a season, maybe fourteen hundred if the monsoon was mean. Salvage work after floods: sporadic, underpaid, dangerous. She was losing ground at roughly two hundred marks a month, which meant she would die owing them money, and they had made it very clear—in their pleasant, sunlit way—that dying indebted was not an exit strategy they honored.

So instead of thinking about it, she watched the storm.

It was a good one. A right-turner, the kind that spun up out of the heat sink where the flats met the foothills and then hooked northeast like it had remembered an appointment. Ulyana had been reading storms since she was nine years old, since before her mother's name became a thing she said only in past tense, and this one had a signature she liked: tight rotation, clean inflow, a tail cloud dragging like a hem. If she positioned on the southeast flank she could ride its shoulder for an hour, log the wind field, sell the data—

The radio crackled.

Not channel nine, which was hers, the open storm-chaser band, mostly static and other lunatics calling hail sizes. This was channel three. Nobody used channel three except people who already knew who they were talking to.

"Okonkwo." A man's voice, warm as fresh bread. "Lovely afternoon for it."

Ulyana's hands went still on the wheel. She knew the voice without knowing the name, the way you know a smell from childhood—the Optimists' regional enforcer had called her twice before, both times about payment schedules, both times smiling through the speaker hard enough that she'd checked her mirrors afterward.

"You're supposed to be on hold," she said. "I put you on hold eight months ago."

"And yet here we are." Static breathed. "You're two kilometers off his northeastern flank. I can see your mast. May I?"

"You may not."

"I'm going to anyway."

* * *

He pulled alongside ten minutes later in a white utility vehicle so clean it looked like it had never seen dirt, which on these flats was either a miracle or a threat. He got out when she stopped, and she stayed in the truck with the engine running, window down two fingers' width, because that was protocol and also because he was the kind of man who took an open door as an invitation.

He was broad and silvering and handsome in the way of well-kept tools, and he wore a pale suit that the wind immediately began bullying. His smile arrived before the rest of him.

"Ulyana Okonkwo." He said her full name slowly, tasting it. "Storm chaser. Orphan of the Kessel flood. Debtor." He spread his hands. "Forgive me. I'm Aurel. We've spoken."

"We've transacted."

"Even better." Behind him, the storm dragged its shadow across the flats, close enough now that the light went the color of old tin. Thunder rolled out flat and long, a sound like furniture moving in a room above the world. "May I say something honest? You're the best reader of weather in three districts, and you're using that gift to make eleven hundred marks a season."

"Fourteen in a bad year."

Aurel laughed—a real laugh, which was worse. "There she is." He glanced at the sky with professional appreciation. "She's a beauty. Right-turner. You'll lose her over the gorge line in forty minutes, and then you'll drive home to whatever you drive home to, and the number will be—" he paused, delicately, "—slightly larger than yesterday."

"What do you want, Aurel."

"A job. One job." He reached into his jacket and she tensed, hand drifting toward the door lock, but he produced only a folded paper, which he held up rather than offering, the way you'd show a treat to a dog you hadn't earned the trust of yet. "The Meridian Expedition. Nine scientists and support, licensed survey, gone silent eleven weeks ago. Last transmission from the upper river, above the gorge, above the last marked settlement. The family that funded them wants them found. Resupply, confirm status, report back. Clean slate, Okonkwo. Every mark. Gone. One job."

The wind shoved against the truck, rocking it on its springs. Hail ticked once against the roof, twice, like fingers testing a drum.

"Nobody goes above the gorge," Ulyana said. "The current runs wrong up there. Ask any boatman."

"I'm asking you."

"Because I'm desperate."

"Because you're *good*," Aurel said, and the correction was gentle and total, the way he probably corrected all his ledgers. "Desperate is most of our portfolio. Good is rare. And here's the part I suspect you'll pretend not to care about: the Meridian Expedition carried a woman named Dr. Imara Voss. Hydrologist. Before your time, perhaps, but there are stories about her, and stories about why she went upriver looking at water nobody's allowed to look at."

The storm's edge reached them. Rain hit the windshield in fat, deliberate drops, then all at once, a white roar, and the world beyond the glass smeared into gray. Ulyana sat with both hands on the wheel and let the noise fill her up the way it always did, that blessed pressure that left no room for thinking.

But thinking got in anyway. It always did.

Because here was the thing Aurel couldn't know—or maybe could, maybe that was the whole design of him—and here was the thing she kept locked below the waterline: she was tired. Not broke-tired, though she was that too. Tired in the place where tired becomes structural. Twelve years of outrunning, first a town, then a name, then a debt, and every year the horizon got shorter and the running got slower, and some mornings she woke up in Bessie's cab and could not remember a single day that had been about anything except distance.

Running costs more than staying. She'd learned that the way you learn fire.

And the Optimists never offered clean slates. That was the part that itched. They forgave nothing; forgiveness wasn't in their theology. Which meant this job wasn't a rescue mission. It was a hook, baited with her own neck, and somewhere upriver at the end of it was whatever the sun-logo people wanted badly enough to erase forty-one thousand marks.

"If I find them," she said, "and they don't want to be found?"

Aurel's smile did something small and complicated. "Then you'll have a fascinating report."

Rain hammered. The anemometer screamed past seventy knots. Somewhere behind the curtain of water, the storm turned northeast without her, keeping its appointment, and she felt it go the way you feel a train leave a station you decided not to board.

"Paper," she said.

Aurel passed the contract through the gap in the window. She read it by the dome light with rain crawling down the windshield like the flats were crying on her behalf, which they were not; the flats didn't cry for anyone. Clean slate upon confirmed contact. Debt dissolved. Her eyes snagged on the clause about discretion—*findings to be reported exclusively to the undersigned*—and she made herself keep reading, made herself sign anyway, because the alternative was another twelve years of miles.

She pressed her thumb to the pad he held out. It beeped, satisfied.

"Pleasure," Aurel said, stepping back into the rain, spreading his arms to it like a man being baptized. "Oh—one more thing. You'll need a boat, and boats need crews, and crews need feeding. There's a river town called Lowmark, two days south. Start there." His smile widened, warm as ever, and for just a second something moved behind it, quick and cold, like a fish turning under ice. "And Ulyana—if you see Voss, do be careful. Some discoveries prefer to stay buried."

He walked back to his white truck and was gone into the rain, taillights dissolving like embers.

Ulyana sat alone in the roaring dark, engine idling, contract glowing on the seat beside her. Above the gorge, eleven weeks of dead air. A hydrologist with buried secrets. A debt erased by people who never erased anything.

She put Bessie in gear and turned south, away from the storm, toward Lowmark and the river and whatever was waiting at the top of the water.

The rain followed her the whole way, like it knew.
