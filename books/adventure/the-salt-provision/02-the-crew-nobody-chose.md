## 2. The Crew Nobody Chose

Lowmark smelled like every river town Ulyana had ever hated: diesel, fish rot, and the sweet-rotten promise of easy money changing hands too fast. The town clung to the last navigable bend before the gorge country began, a smear of tin roofs and rope bridges strung along the north bank like something the flood hadn't gotten around to eating yet.

She'd been in town four hours and had already been offered three boats, two of them seaworthy, one of them a lie. She'd bought the lie, because the liar—old man Ketch with his cataract eyes and hands like braided rope—had knocked forty percent off for cash, and cash was the only language she had left that didn't come with interest.

The boat was thirty feet of scarred aluminum with an outboard older than she was and a name painted over so many times the hull read like a palimpsest: *MERCY* under *LUCKY PENNY* under whatever she'd been before that. Ulyana stood on the dock looking at her and thought: *good enough to die in.*

"Sentimental already?" said a voice behind her. "That's how it starts."

He was maybe fifty, sitting on a mooring post like he'd grown there, sketching on a waterproof pad with a pencil worn down to a stub. Lean, sunburned, a canvas hat with the brim chewed by salt air. His shirt pocket held three more pencils and a folded map that had been folded so many times the creases were going soft.

"I'm not sentimental," Ulyana said. "I'm broke. Different disease."

"Same symptoms." He didn't look up from the pad. He was drawing her boat—or her boat's future, anyway, some corrected version of it with a reinforced transom and a fuel rack where the rotten bench seat was. "You'll want to move the weight forward. She's stern-heavy. In the gorge current she'll squat and take water over the transom, and you'll drown about six miles upstream of here, which is a waste of a perfectly good debt."

Ulyana went still. "Who told you I have debts?"

"Nobody. You have the look." Now he looked up, and his eyes were quick and gray and entirely without pity, which she found restful. "Everyone running from something has the same walk. Weight on the balls of the feet. Like the ground owes them money." A small smile. "Which, in your case—"

"Who *are* you?"

"Teodoro Vasquez. Teo." He tapped the pad. "Cartographer. Formerly of the Meridian Survey, formerly of the Royal Cartographic Institute, formerly employed at all. Currently a drunk with excellent penmanship." He said it lightly, the way men say terrible things when they've made peace with them or given up trying to. "You're going upriver. Past the gorge. Everyone who goes past the gorge goes with bad maps, because the good ones are mine and they burned me out of the trade. So you can buy my maps secondhand and wrong, or you can buy me, cheap, and have the man who drew the river."

"The Meridian Expedition," Ulyana said slowly. "You know them."

"I drew their route." Something flickered across his face—the first honest thing he'd shown her. Not guilt exactly. Its cousin. "They went silent eleven days ago. I've been sitting on this dock for eleven days waiting for someone stupid enough to go find them, because nobody local will, and I can't afford a boat." He shrugged. "And then you walked past with your ground-owes-me-money walk, and here we are."

*Here we are.* Ulyana looked at the river, brown and patient, sliding toward the sea with everything it had taken. Somewhere up there was a missing expedition, a payday, and a clean slate with her name on it if she could just get the ledger closed. Behind her was a storm season and a syndicate that smiled while it collected.

"Payment's split of whatever the Optimists pay on delivery," she said. "Minus boat costs. And you stay sober above the gorge."

"I'm always sober above the gorge," Teo said. "It's below it that gets complicated." He stuck out a hand, pencil calluses and all. "Welcome aboard, Captain. What do I call you?"

"Okonkwo. Ulyana Okonkwo."

"Storm chaser," he said, and grinned. "I heard about you even down here. The woman who races weather for money." He shook his head. "Everybody in this business is running from something. At least yours is visible from a distance."

*

Sefa found her that evening at the dockside cantina, which was less a building than a roof with opinions, and sat down across from her without being asked. That alone should have warned Ulyana off. People who asked permission wanted something small. People who didn't ask wanted something enormous.

"You're hiring for the upriver run," Sefa said. She was compact, forties, hair cut short and practical, wearing clothes that had been expensive once and were now merely durable. On the table between them she set a leather folio, squared its corners precisely, and left her hand flat on top of it like a paperweight made of intent. "Before you say no—I know what the Optimists pay. I know what they deduct. I know the penalty clauses in your contract better than you do, because I probably wrote the version they're using."

Ulyana kept her face neutral and her hand near her knife. "You're Optimist."

"*Former* Optimist. Bookkeeper, collections division, nine years." Sefa's voice was level as a ledger line. "I reconciled accounts for people like you. I calculated what a person's remaining working years were worth against their outstanding balance, and I filed the difference under 'acceptable loss.'" Her jaw tightened, just once. "Then I reconciled my own sister's account. She drowned in a fishery collapse the syndicate insured against and then denied. They wrote her off in eleven minutes. It took me nine years to work up the courage to resign, and they took my pension, my references, and my name out of their books—which is the only reason I'm alive to sit here."

"And why would they let a bookkeeper walk?"

"They don't. Which is why I'm offering you something worth more than a deckhand." She opened the folio. Inside were documents, copied in a neat, merciless hand: contract templates, penalty schedules, collection protocols, and—a page that made Ulyana's stomach drop—a routing sheet for Optimist assets on this river, including patrol schedules and the registry number of a fast boat currently stationed two bends downstream. "They know about the job. They always know about the jobs they arrange. This is how they keep their investments safe. I can tell you where their people will be, and when, and what they'll be carrying."

"Why help me? You don't know me."

"No." Sefa closed the folio but didn't withdraw it. "But I've read your file. All two hundred pages of it. Interest accruals, collateral seizures, the storm-chasing income projections they ran on you for six years." For the first time something moved behind her composure—not quite warmth. Recognition. "You're not a client to me, Ms. Okonkwo. You're a case study I never got to finish. I want to see how you end."

"That's a terrible reason to risk your life."

"It's the only honest one I have." She slid the folio across. "Wages same as anyone. And I cook. Badly, but I cook."

Ulyana stared at the routing sheet, at the neat columns of a machine that had been chewing on her life for six years, laid open by someone who used to feed it. It felt like theft. It felt like relief. She hated how much she wanted both.

"Deck watch is midnight to four," she said finally. "And you don't touch the radio."

"Deal," said Sefa, and squared the folio's corners again, and Ulyana understood she'd just hired the most dangerous person on the boat and couldn't have said why that felt safer than safer would.

*

Pirro wasn't hired. Pirro was discovered at dawn, folded into the hold behind a crate of fuel drums like a comma in a sentence nobody meant to write.

Ulyana found him when she went to check the fuel lines—sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and hollow cheeks, dark curls matted with bilge dust, watching her with the flat defiance of someone who'd already decided how the next five minutes were going to go and accepted it might end badly.

"Off," she said.

"No."

"This isn't a ferry. We're going into gorge country. People die up there."

"People die *here*," the kid said. "My brother signed onto a dredge crew in March. The Optimists own the dredge. He sends money home until he doesn't. I'm not doing that." He unfolded himself, joints cracking, and stood as tall as he could, which wasn't tall. "I can run lines. I can patch aluminum. I can cook better than whoever else you got, guaranteed. And I already ate half your stores, so firing me is just throwing away food twice."

From the dock, Teo called down, lazily: "Captain, tide turns in twenty minutes and I've charted us a window through the shallows that closes with it. Your call, but the river won't wait on your parenting decisions."

Ulyana looked at the kid. The kid looked back, chin up, terrified and immovable, and something in Ulyana's chest did a thing she refused to name—some old hinge swinging on rusted pins toward a door she'd nailed shut twelve years ago.

"Midnight watch with Sefa," she said. "You touch my charts, you swim home."

"Yes, Captain." The grin that broke across his face was so sudden and so young it hurt to look at directly, like sun off water.

*Don't learn their names,* she told herself, casting off as the tide turned and Lowmark slid away astern. *Don't learn anything you'll have to lose.*

*

The first night they anchored in the lee of a bluff, engine cold, river muttering against the hull. Sefa produced a stew from stores and lies—it was, as promised, bad—and Pirro improved it with a tin of dried chilies he produced from somewhere on his person with the shamelessness of a born smuggler. Teo spread his old charts on the deck planks and weighted the corners with cups, and for an hour he talked the river aloud: the gorge narrows, the false channels, the reach above the falls where the survey teams had reported currents that behaved like nothing he'd ever drawn.

"Singing water," Pirro said, mouth full. "That's what the upriver people call it. My gran said the source of the river sings."

"Your gran also said eels were government spies," Teo said.

"She was right about the eels."

And they laughed—all three of them, easy as breathing—and Ulyana sat at the edge of the lamplight with her cup of bad stew and felt the laugh land somewhere in her that had no armor left over it. She looked at their faces in the yellow light: the disgraced man drawing his redemption one careful line at a time, the exiled accountant guarding her stolen pages like a reliquary, the stowaway kid already halfway to belonging somewhere he'd never been invited.

*This is how it happens,* she thought. *This is exactly how it happens. You hire strangers and one night the lantern comes out and suddenly they're yours, and everything you carry gets heavier instead of lighter.*

"Captain." Sefa was holding out the ladle. "You eat. You gave away your portion to the boy."

"He's growing."

"So are you. Sideways." But there was no edge in it, and Ulyana took the ladle, and the stew was terrible, and she ate every bite.

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls and the lamp was down to a blue whisper, Ulyana took the midnight watch herself, though it wasn't hers. She sat with the rifle across her knees and watched the river run black and silver toward the gorge, and behind her she could hear them breathing—three strangers, asleep, trusting her with their unconscious bodies the way you can only trust someone you've decided not to fear.

Near dawn, Sefa came up quietly and stood beside her at the rail.

"Channel Nine," she said softly. "Meridian Expedition's assigned frequency. I checked their filing before I resigned. If they're alive, and if they still have power, they'll be calling in on Nine." She paused. "We should start listening."

Ulyana nodded once, eyes on the water. Ahead, the bluffs were closing in, and the river was narrowing, and somewhere far upstream—past the gorge, past the maps—something waited that the Optimists wanted found or wanted buried, and she couldn't yet tell which frightened her more.

Behind her, in his sleep, Pirro murmured something to his brother, and turned over, and was still.

*Don't learn their names,* Ulyana reminded herself, uselessly, one last time.

She already knew all three.
