## 3. Dead Air on Channel Nine

The coordinates put them at the mouth of a side channel where the river split around a gravel bar shaped like a sleeping animal. Ulyana cut the engine and let the boat drift the last hundred meters, because there was a way a camp sounded when it was alive—generators, voices, the clatter of someone doing dishes badly—and this camp had none of it. This camp had the sound of a place that had been left in a hurry or left forever, and from the water those two sounds were identical: nothing.

"Channel Nine," Sefa said. She had the radio handset in her lap, had been cycling through frequencies since dawn. "Nothing. Same as Lowmark. Same as yesterday."

"Try it again anyway."

Sefa tried it again anyway. Static, flat and gray as the sky. Ulyana watched Teo in the bow with the survey map spread across his knees, his finger tracing the channel's bend, and she watched Pirro not watching anything except the treeline, the way kids watch treelines when they've decided something lives in them.

"Meridian Expedition, Meridian Expedition," Sefa said into the handset, one more time, like a prayer she didn't believe in. "This is the resupply run out of Lowmark. Anyone receiving, come back."

Static. Then, so faint Ulyana thought she'd imagined it, a sound that wasn't static—a tone, high and wavering, almost musical, gone before she could decide what it was.

"You hear that?"

Sefa lowered the handset slowly. "Hear what?"

Ulyana let it go. The wind was doing things to the reeds; the river did things to itself. Everything up here sang if you listened long enough.

They grounded the boat on the gravel bar and walked in.

* * *

The camp sat on a low terrace above the channel, four expedition-grade tents in a neat row, guy-lines taut, rain flies still rigged. That was the first wrong thing. Nobody rigs rain flies for a camp they're abandoning. Nobody stakes tents that well and walks away. The second wrong thing was the fire pit: ash raked flat, wood stacked under a tarp against weather, a kettle sitting on a flat stone like someone had just stepped off to piss and would be back any minute to boil water for tea.

Except the kettle had rust blooming on its lid.

"How long?" Teo asked quietly. He'd stopped at the edge of camp the way he stopped at the edge of everything, cataloguing before entering, a man who trusted maps more than ground until he'd made the ground into a map.

Ulyana crouched by the fire pit and touched the ash. Dry all the way down. "Weeks. Maybe three." She stood, wiped her hand on her thigh. "Long enough that whoever left isn't coming back for their kettle."

Pirro had already drifted toward the nearest tent, and Ulyana let him, because he moved quiet and because sending an adult would mean admitting she wanted an adult to go first. He unzipped the flap and looked inside and said, "Uh," in a voice that made her cross the camp in six strides.

Inside the tent: bedrolls rolled, gear stowed, everything packed for departure that never happened. And on the folding table in the center, arranged with deliberate care, a radio.

Or what had been a radio. It was an expedition transceiver, the good kind, the kind that cost more than Ulyana's boat, and someone had destroyed it—not smashed in rage but taken apart with intent. The casing was intact. The chassis was open. Every component that could carry a signal had been removed: the transmitter board, the antenna coupler, the power amplifier, gone, extracted cleanly, leaving cavities in the electronics like pulled teeth. The remaining parts were laid out beside the carcass in a tidy row, labeled with masking tape in small, precise handwriting.

*Removed. Removed. Removed.*

"They didn't break it," Pirro said. "They *unmade* it."

"They didn't want anyone calling out," Sefa said from the tent door. Her voice had gone flat, bookkeeper-flat, the voice she used when numbers told her something she didn't like. "Or they didn't want anyone calling *in*. You gut a radio when you don't want to be found—or when you don't want to be overheard."

"Expeditions don't do this," Teo said. "Expeditions check in. That's the whole architecture of an expedition—the schedule, the call window, the check-in. Voss filed her call windows with the Lowmark harbor office like clockwork. I've seen the logs."

"So where is she?"

Nobody answered. Ulyana moved to the next tent, then the next. In the third she found the food stores—crates of ration packs, still sealed, stacked three high. Half-starved people don't leave food behind either. Whatever had happened here hadn't been desperation. It had been *decision*.

In the fourth tent, wedged under the corner pole where it wouldn't blow away, she found the journal.

It was a standard expedition logbook, waterproof pages, half filled with Dr. Imara Voss's tight, slanted script—hydrological readings, flow rates, mineral assays, the dry music of a scientist doing her job. Ulyana flipped backward through weeks of it, Teo and Sefa reading over her shoulders, Pirro hanging off the tent frame. The entries climbed the river with them: tributary surveys, sediment samples, a note about a fish species that shouldn't exist this far inland. Then, eleven days before the last entry, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. Pressing hard enough to emboss the next page.

*Day 41. The gorge readings are impossible. Salinity rising upstream. Every textbook says it should fall. We ran the assay three times. Either our instruments are lying or the river is. Found a spring seep on the north bank—water tastes of salt, clear as glass. Pirro would love it. K. tasted it first and laughed and said it was singing. We thought he was being poetic.*

*Day 43. Followed the seep up the side valley. The springs are real. All of them. Dozens, maybe hundreds, feeding the headwaters through limestone channels. The concentration is—there is no commercial precedent. If this water reaches the lower basin, every desalination contract in the region becomes worthless overnight. Every reservoir monopoly. Every license. I understand now why no survey has ever come back from above the gorge. Not because the river is impassable. Because someone pays for it to stay unknown.*

*K. keeps saying the water sings. Ran a frequency analysis on the spring outflow today. There is a resonance. A literal acoustic signature in the flow, harmonic, stable. It's not poetry. The limestone acts as a resonator. The water sings. God help me, the water sings, and I think I'm the first person in a century to hear it and I have never felt less like celebrating.*

*Day 44. Boat on the water below us today. Didn't hail us. Kept pace for an hour, then turned back downstream. Too far to read the name. M. says we're paranoid. M. has never owed anyone money.*

*Day 45. Decision made. We are breaking camp tonight, moving the equipment up-valley. If they want this silent, we will be quieter. To whoever reads this: the coordinates in the log are true. The singing water is real. Do not trust the river to be empty. Do not trust the smiling men. We are not lost. We are hiding.*

Ulyana read the last line twice. Around her the tent was very quiet.

"We are not lost," Sefa repeated softly. "She signed off her own missing-persons case."

"She went dark on purpose." Teo had gone pale in the way he did when the data contradicted the map. "She found something the Optimists would bury, and instead of broadcasting it she *buried herself* with it."

"Because broadcasting gets you bought," Sefa said. "Or buried. I processed those contracts, Ulyana. Water rights, survey licenses, 'environmental remediation' clauses. When the Optimists find water, people downstream start paying for rain."

"And the boat," Pirro said. "Day forty-four. Someone was following them."

Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, defensive. "I read it. Someone was following them."

Ulyana folded the journal page along its crease and put it inside her jacket, against her ribs, where the debt ledger used to sit before Sefa had talked her into leaving it locked in the boat's dry box. Old habit, keeping the dangerous paper close. New habit, apparently, keeping other people's dangerous paper close.

"We should go back," Teo said. Not arguing—offering it, gently, the way you offer a rope to someone in current. "We report the camp, collect the fee, let the Optimists mount their own recovery. That was the job. Find the expedition. We found evidence of the expedition."

"The job was find the *expedition*," Ulyana said. "Not find their laundry."

"Ulyana." Sefa's turn now, careful. "A gutted radio means someone controls the silence up here. Whoever emptied that transmitter knew exactly which parts to pull. That's syndicate work. That's *our* work—" she caught herself, jaw tightening, "—that's their work. If Voss is hiding from the Optimists, and we're flying the Optimists' money, then to her we're the wolves."

"I know what we look like."

"Then say what we're all thinking. Turn around."

Ulyana stepped out of the tent into the gray afternoon light. The camp sat there being wrong at her—kettle, ash, taut guy-lines, the whole stage set of a life paused mid-sentence. Three weeks ago these people had eaten breakfast here. They'd laughed about singing water. And somewhere above the gorge they were alive, starving or freezing or both, hiding from smiling men, while she stood in their empty kitchen holding a contract that said she worked for the smiling men.

She thought about running. She was good at running. She'd been outrunning a storm across the flats the day the Optimists found her, and before that she'd been outrunning other things for twelve years, and the running had a rhythm to it, almost comfortable, like a bad mattress you'd learned to sleep on.

But Voss had written *do not trust the smiling men*, and Ulyana had cashed their advance, and there was no version of turning around now that didn't end with her handing these pages to the exact people they warned about.

"We're not turning around," she said.

Teo exhaled. Sefa closed her eyes briefly, a woman recalculating.

"Not for the Optimists," Ulyana went on. "Not anymore. We go upriver, we find Voss, and we tell her the truth—that I came paid, and that I'm choosing what I do with what I know. After that it's her call. But nobody hands these pages to a smiling man. Nobody."

"That's not the job," Teo said.

"It's the job now."

* * *

They broke camp at dusk, stripping anything useful—fuel tablets, the medical kit Voss had left (left, not abandoned; Ulyana noted the difference), a coil of climbing line. Pirro carried the kettle, because he'd decided it was theirs now, some kid-logic salvage right, and nobody argued him out of it.

Ulyana took the last watch before dawn. She sat on the gravel bar with the journal in her lap and the river talking to itself in the dark, and she thought about the tone she'd heard on Channel Nine that morning, that high wavering note under the static. Singing water. Resonant limestone. She'd assumed wind.

Now, listening, she wasn't sure. The sound seemed to come from everywhere—from the water, from the banks, from under the world—and it rose and fell like breathing.

Movement, downstream. Just for a second: a light among the trees on the far bank, low and shielded, the kind of light you use when you don't want to be seen moving at night. It vanished. She counted to a hundred. It did not come back.

She woke Sefa without a word and showed her the stretch of dark shoreline.

"Lamp discipline," Sefa murmured. "That's how they taught us to move at night. Shielded low, shuttered between steps." Her face was unreadable in the starlight. "Someone's following us upstream, Ulyana. One day back, maybe less."

Ulyana looked upriver, into the black throat of the gorge where a woman who refused to be lost was hiding with her singing water.

"Then we don't sleep deep," she said, "and we don't stop moving. First light—we race."
