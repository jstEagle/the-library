## 4. What the River Keeps

Teo had drawn coastlines for ministries. He had charted harbors for men who owned fleets, and he had once, in a former life that felt like it belonged to someone else entirely, been trusted with the only complete survey of the Meridian Delta. Every map he'd ever made had one thing in common: someone paid him to make the world look knowable.

The river above Lowmark was not knowable.

He sat in the bow of the *Salt Lark* with his drafting board strapped to his knees and a grease pencil behind each ear, watching the water do things water was not supposed to do. It eddied against the current. It pooled where it should have poured. Twice in the last hour he'd marked a backflow—a tongue of river running upstream against its own body, quiet as a lie.

"You're frowning at your paper," Pirro said from beside him. The kid had appointed himself Teo's assistant on day two, mostly by refusing to leave.

"I'm frowning at the river."

"Same thing." Pirro leaned over the board, dripping river water off his elbows because he refused to understand what a towel was for. "What's wrong with it?"

"Nothing's wrong with it. That's what's wrong with it." Teo tapped the grease pencil against a stretch of bank they'd passed an hour ago. "Rivers are honest. Gravity is honest. This one keeps changing its mind."

Above them the gorge walls rose in red terraces, and the channel narrowed, and the sound of the water changed—that deepening, guttural note every boatman learned to fear before he learned its name. Rapids ahead. Big ones.

Ulyana stood at the wheel, and Teo watched her read the water the way he read maps: whole, all at once, no line-by-line hesitation. She hadn't spoken in twenty minutes. That was normal. What wasn't normal was the set of her shoulders—coiled, like she was bracing for something the river hadn't shown her yet.

"Sefa," she called. "Kill the cargo lashings' slack. Everything tight or nothing."

Sefa moved without argument. She'd been like that since Lowmark—efficient, silent, watching Ulyana with an expression Teo couldn't classify and had decided not to try. An ex-Optimist bookkeeper carried her own weather.

"Teo." Ulyana's eyes never left the channel. "Your map. Where does it widen?"

"It doesn't. Not for six kilometers."

"Then we run it."

"We could portage—"

"With what? We have no road, no cart, and a boat full of supplies the Optimists want delivered more than they want us alive." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "We run it."

Pirro made a sound of pure delight.

"No," Teo said.

"Yes," said Ulyana.

"I'm a cartographer, not a—"

"Hold onto your board, then."

* * *

The rapids took the *Salt Lark* like a dog shaking a rat.

Teo would remember it afterward in fragments, the way he remembered all disasters: the wheel fighting Ulyana's hands; Sefa shouting depths from the stern; the first rock appearing out of the churn like a whale's back; the second one not appearing at all until the hull kissed it with a grinding shriek that went up through Teo's boots and into his teeth.

They came through the worst of it sideways. Then the river dropped them into a chute between two boulders, and the chute spat them into standing waves, and somewhere in the white chaos Pirro stopped being on the boat.

Teo saw it happen and didn't see it happen—one moment the kid was hauling the jib line, the next the deck was bare and there was a small orange life vest bobbing forty meters astern, going under, coming up, going under.

"Pirro!" Sefa's voice cracked across the water.

Ulyana did the thing Teo would think about for years afterward. She didn't turn the boat. She killed the engine, grabbed the coil of rope off the stern cleat, tied it around her own waist in three seconds flat, and said, "Sefa. Take the wheel. Teo—haul."

Then she dove into rapids.

"Are you insane—" Teo was already paying out rope, hands working on instinct while his mind screamed. The river swallowed her whole. He saw her surface twice, swimming hard at an angle, cutting across the current toward the orange vest.

The vest went down and stayed down.

Three seconds. Five. Teo's arms burned with the rope's pull; the current was trying to take all three of them. Then Ulyana surfaced with an arm locked around something small and thrashing, and Sefa brought the boat around in a wide screaming arc, and together—rope, engine, current, luck—they dragged both of them over the gunwale and onto the deck.

Pirro vomited half the river onto the planks. Ulyana lay on her back beside him, chest heaving, staring straight up at the gorge walls, saying nothing.

"You jumped into class-four water," Teo said, when he could speak. "Tied to a boat. In a flood current."

"He's fourteen."

"He's a stowaway who snuck aboard!"

"He's ours," Ulyana said, and then closed her eyes as if the word had cost her something.

Nobody corrected her. That was the strange part. Sefa just put a blanket over the boy and started checking his pulse with brisk, furious competence, and Teo found himself sitting down hard on the cargo crates, holding a rope he no longer needed, feeling something rearrange itself quietly in his chest.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar below the gorge mouth, a fire going, everything they owned spread out to dry. Pirro slept like the dead, wrapped in two blankets, alive and insufferable about it when conscious ("I've decided I'm allowed to fall in, it builds character"). Sefa took first watch without being asked.

Teo worked by firelight. He pinned fresh paper to his board and began drawing the day properly—the gorge, the chute, the standing waves—and it was there, in the clean geometry of ink, that he finally saw what his instincts had been nagging him about since Lowmark.

"Ulyana." His voice came out strange. She looked up from sharpening a knife that didn't need it. "Come look at this."

She crouched beside him. Firelight moved on her face.

"The current flows wrong." He traced the channel with his pencil. "Below the third bend, the main flow hugs the south bank. Above the gorge, it should continue south—it's the same gradient, same volume. But it doesn't. Above the gorge, the deep channel shifts north. And these—" he tapped the marks he'd made all day, the backflows, the impossible pools, "—these aren't eddies. Eddies spin. These *reverse*. There's water moving upstream along the bottom, Ulyana. A lot of it. Something below the gorge is pushing back."

"Springs?" Sefa said quietly, from the edge of the firelight. She'd come to stand watch and stayed to listen.

"Upwelling, maybe. Some kind of pressure from underneath." Teo shook his head. "I've surveyed thirty-one rivers. Rivers don't push back. They don't run backward along their own beds. Whatever's feeding this thing isn't behaving like groundwater. It's behaving like—" He stopped, because the next word felt too large for the firelit dark. "*Like a source.*"

Silence. The river muttered against the gravel bar, patient, ancient, keeping whatever it kept.

Ulyana stared at the map for a long time. When she spoke, it wasn't about the river at all.

"I had a brother," she said.

Teo went very still. In four days he'd learned her habits, her silences, her storm-chaser's grammar of wind and pressure—not this.

"Twelve years ago. We grew up on the flats, downstream people, salt-poor, buying our drinking water by the liter from Optimist pumps at prices that kept us poor forever. My brother figured out there was water under the flats—clean, free, ours. He organized the neighbors. Dug wells. For about eight months it worked." Her knife-hand flexed. "Then the Optimists came with lawyers and enforcers and a smile you could feel from across a room. They filled the wells with concrete. My brother argued with them. Publicly. Loudly." A pause. "There was an accident. Their words. He drowned in a flooded quarry the week after the last well was sealed. Nobody was ever charged. Nobody ever is."

The fire popped. Upstream, the gorge breathed its low guttural note.

"So when I chase storms," Ulyana said, "it's because storms are honest. They tell you exactly what they're going to do to you. And when I took this job, it was because running costs more than staying, and I told myself it was just debt, just miles, just a delivery." She looked at Teo's map—at the reversed current, the pushing water, the source that shouldn't exist. "But I've known since Lowmark what the Optimists want found upriver. And I've been pretending I don't know why they want it."

Teo understood then, looking at her—the woman who'd jumped into rapids for a stranger's kid without hesitating for one heartbeat—that the armor wasn't coldness. It had never been coldness. It was a person who had already lost everyone worth losing, doing arithmetic about whether she could afford to lose anyone again.

And losing anyway, apparently. Sitting right here on a gravel bar, letting herself be crewed, be followed, be needed.

"Well," Teo said, and picked up his pencil, because someone had to say something ordinary or the night would crack open. "If the current flows wrong, my map has to flow wrong with it. Which means redrawing everything above the gorge from scratch. Which means—" he glanced at the sleeping boy, the silent bookkeeper, the storm chaser who saved people, "—we go up and see what the river's hiding."

Ulyana almost smiled. Almost.

"First light," she said.

It was Sefa, still at the edge of the firelight, who spoke last. She was facing upstream, toward the dark throat of the gorge, and her voice was careful in a way Teo had learned to recognize as fear wearing professionalism.

"There's a boat behind us," she said. "I've been watching its lamp for two hours. It's not gaining. It's not falling back. It's keeping pace." She turned, and the firelight showed her face gone pale. "I know that pattern. That's how the Optimists follow someone they haven't decided to kill yet."

Above the gravel bar, the monsoon clouds that had been gathering for days finally blotted out the stars.
