## 5. The Smiling Wake

Sefa counted the wake before she saw the boat.

That was the old training talking. Three years in the Optimists' regional office at Corren's Landing, and they'd taught her to read water the way other people read ledgers: every disturbance is a transaction, every ripple a debt being called in. The river behind them had been behaving all morning—clean V-wakes from their own engine, the occasional crocodile slide, nothing else. Then, an hour past noon, a second wake. Wider. Slower. Holding exactly their speed, exactly their distance, patient as interest accruing.

She didn't say anything right away. She stood at the stern with a coil of rope in her hands like she was doing something useful, and she watched the smudge of a boat sit on the horizon behind them, and she did the arithmetic she'd sworn off doing when she'd burned her ledger and walked out of Corren's Landing with nothing but her name.

*They found us. Of course they found us.*

"Sefa." Ulyana's voice, low, from the wheel. "You see it too."

It wasn't a question. Ulyana Okonkwo read storms for a living; she could read a following boat in her sleep.

"Been there since the bend," Sefa said. "Not closing. Not falling back. They want us to know."

"Optimists?"

"The wake says patience. Patience says Optimists." She set down the rope. "Pirates run you down. Smugglers hide. Only the Optimists follow you politely and let you sweat."

Teo looked up from his charts, face going gray. Pirro, who was supposed to be below deck mending nets and was instead perched on the rail like a heron, said, "Is that bad? That sounds bad."

"It's bad," Ulyana said. "Sefa. How long before they overtake us if they want to?"

"At that tonnage?" Sefa calculated without wanting to. Old habits were load-bearing; you couldn't pull them out without the whole wall coming down. "Half a day. Sooner if they've got the fuel we don't."

Ulyana's jaw tightened. Fuel was the wound they were all pretending not to look at. They'd burned through Lowmark's cheap diesel faster than planned—Teo's route above the gorge added three days nobody had budgeted—and now they were running on fumes and optimism, which was ironic enough to be funny if any of them had the energy to laugh.

"There's a trading post ahead," Teo said, tapping the chart with a blunt finger. "Ganji Bend. Fuel depot, mooring, maybe forty people. Neutral ground, mostly. The Optimists keep an office there but it's small."

"Then we stop there first," Ulyana said. "Buy what fuel they'll sell us, and meet our new friends on ground where there are witnesses." She glanced at Sefa, and her eyes did the thing they did—measured her, weighed her, decided something. "You okay with that?"

The question underneath the question: *Are you going to betray us?*

"I trained half the people the Optimists send out into the world," Sefa said. "If I wanted to hand you over, I'd have done it while you slept. I'm expensive."

Something that wasn't quite a smile crossed Ulyana's face. "Good. I couldn't afford you anyway."

* * *

Ganji Bend smelled of fish smoke and engine oil, a shelf of stilted buildings clinging to the outside of the river's curve like barnacles. The trading post's fuel depot sat at the downstream end, its tanks rust-striped, its dock crowded with pirogues and one fat barge flying no colors at all, because neutrality was cheaper than loyalty out here.

Sefa went to the office while Ulyana negotiated with the depot man—a leathery woman named Bintou who quoted prices like she was defending a siege. Sefa knew the type. She'd been the type, once, behind a desk in Corren's Landing, quoting numbers to desperate people and calling it help.

She was halfway through counting out their remaining cash when she felt it: the particular silence of a room adjusting itself around someone important.

"You're a long way from your desk, Bookkeeper."

She knew the voice before she turned around. Of course she did. There were perhaps six voices in the world that could reach into her chest and squeeze, and this one belonged to Master Anselm Roke, who had recruited her at nineteen, trained her at twenty, and taught her everything she knew about how cheerful men do terrible math.

He hadn't changed. Silver at the temples now, but the same easy posture, the same linen suit somehow unwrinkled despite the river, the same smile—the Optimists' signature smile, warm as a lamp, empty as a lampshade. Behind him in the doorway stood two of the syndicate's river men, big and polite and armed the way furniture is heavy: you stop noticing until you trip over it.

"Master Roke." Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "I left. You signed the paperwork yourself."

"And yet here you are, three hundred miles upriver, on a boat chartered by a debtor, headed toward an expedition my employers have spent considerable money worrying about." He drifted closer, examining the office's dusty inventory like a buyer. "I trained you, Sefa. Do you remember your first lesson?"

"*Every number has an owner.*"

"Every number has an owner," he agreed warmly. "And every owner comes to collect. Miss Okonkwo owes the Optimists a great deal. You owe them—" he paused, savoring it, "—well. Nothing, legally. But you owe *me*, girl, and I collect differently."

Outside, she heard the second boat tie up at the far end of the dock. Heard Ulyana's voice go carefully pleasant. Heard Pirro's laugh, pitched wrong, the way kids laughed when they were scared and performing.

"What does the syndicate want?" she asked.

"To talk. We're very reasonable people." Roke picked up a brass weight from the counter, turned it over. "The Meridian Expedition went silent. My employers invested heavily in Dr. Voss's survey. They simply want to know what happened to their investment. If Miss Okonkwo locates the expedition and reports back—accurately, completely—her ledger closes. Clean slate. Everyone smiles."

"That's not what she was told."

"It rarely is, at the start. Terms improve when people are cooperative." He set the weight down and finally looked at her, and for one unguarded instant the smile dropped and she saw the thing beneath it, the thing she'd always known was there: a man doing arithmetic on human beings and finding them profitable. "You could come home, Sefa. Your talent was wasted on conscience. Conscience doesn't compound."

"I manage fine."

"You're crewing a debt-runner's boat with a disgraced mapmaker and a stowaway child, burning fuel you can't replace, chasing a story up a river that flows backward." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "Fine is a generous audit."

* * *

The parley happened on the dock at dusk, because Ulyana insisted on witnesses and Roke, amused, allowed it. Bintou leaned in her office doorway with her arms folded. A handful of traders drifted close, pretending to mend nets.

Roke made his offer twice—once smiling, once with the smile set aside—and both times it amounted to the same thing: report back, or the syndicate would stop being patient. Ulyana listened with her arms crossed and her storm-chaser's stillness, that flat calm Sefa had learned meant weather coming.

"We'll report," Ulyana said. "When there's something to report. To whoever pays us. That's the job."

"The job," Roke repeated gently, "is whatever we decide it is. But I admire your spirit. Truly. It will make the eventual disappointment more instructive." He clicked his fingers at his river men. "We'll take our leave. Long day tomorrow."

They didn't take their leave. One of them walked to the depot's main tank, produced a key from somewhere, and began uncoupling their hose from the pump.

Bintou straightened. "That's paid-for stock."

"It's *our* stock," Roke said pleasantly. "We hold the depot's supply contract. Check your paperwork, madam—you'll find every liter in that tank is already owned, on paper, by the Optimist Provision Society. We're not stealing it. We're exercising an option." He turned to Ulyana, and the lamp-smile came back full wattage. "Consider it a lesson in ownership, Miss Okonkwo. Everything on this river has an owner. The sooner you learn whose things you've been borrowing, the gentler the collection."

They pumped forty minutes. Nobody stopped them. Bintou checked her contract and went pale and quiet, because the paperwork was real—that was the horror of the Optimists, Sefa thought, watching the fuel gauge of their lives drain away; the paperwork was *always* real. They didn't break rules. They wrote them, years in advance, for people who hadn't been born yet.

Pirro started forward and Ulyana caught his collar without looking. Teo stared at the tank with the expression of a man watching a library burn. And Sefa stood beside Roke, close enough to smell his cologne—cedar, always cedar, the scent of every meeting where someone's life got restructured—and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't cost more than silence.

At the last minute, though, Roke paused beside her.

"You didn't argue," he said softly. "Good girl. Some part of you is still mine."

"No," Sefa said. "Some part of me is still *yours*—the part that watched you do this and memorized every step. Contract numbers, supply codes, the name of the clerk who countersigned Bintou's lease. I kept the lesson, Master Roke. I just changed what I use it for."

For the first time, the smile flickered. Just at the corner. Just for a heartbeat.

Then he was walking down the dock, and the Optimists' boat pulled away into the dark with their fuel, and the river closed over its wake like a page turning.

* * *

They sat around the boat's little stove that night, eating rice that tasted of diesel fumes and defeat, and nobody spoke for a long time.

"They'll be ahead of us now," Ulyana said finally. "Faster boat, full tanks. They'll wait for us above the gorge."

"Then we go around them," Teo said. "There's a channel. Barely mapped. My maps are disgraceful, but disgraceful isn't the same as useless."

"How much fuel do we have?" Pirro asked.

Nobody answered, which was answer enough.

Sefa turned her cup in her hands. She should have felt broken—she'd just stood next to the man who built her and watched him rob her crew, and she'd done nothing. Instead she felt something colder and cleaner settling into place, the feeling she used to get when a ledger finally balanced after weeks of hunting a discrepancy.

Because she *had* memorized it. All of it. Roke's supply contract with Ganji Bend, clause and countersignature. The depot's legal recourse—thin, but real, if anyone upstream ever cared about paper justice. And more than that: three years of Optimist files, copied in her own careful hand before she burned her ledger and ran. Debts. Leverage. Names. Including, filed under *Meridian Expedition — Special Account*, a payment authorization dated eleven days before the expedition went silent.

A bribe. Offered, or offered and refused—she'd never known which. But authorized. Which meant somebody at the top had known the expedition would need silencing before it ever needed rescuing.

"Ulyana," she said quietly, across the stove. "When we find Voss—it's worse than a missing expedition. Somebody paid to make it go quiet before it ever went dark. I have the receipt."

Ulyana looked at her across the flame, and Sefa watched the storm-chaser recalibrate, the way she did when the barometer dropped.

"Then we'd better get there first," Ulyana said.

Above the black line of the gorge, unseen, the monsoon's first thunder cleared its throat.
