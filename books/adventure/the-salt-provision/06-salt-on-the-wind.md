## 6. Salt on the Wind

Above the gorge, the river stopped behaving.

Teo had been saying it for two days—*the current runs wrong, Ulyana, I'm telling you, it runs wrong*—and now she could feel it through the deck, a slow backward pull, like the water was being drunk from underneath. The *Meridian* sat high in the current, riding a river that seemed to hesitate, and the morning fog came off the surface tasting of salt.

Ulyana licked her lips. Licked them again.

"Sefa," she said. "Taste the air."

Sefa leaned over the rail, and Pirro scrambled up beside her, all elbows and impatience. The boy stuck out his tongue like he was catching snow.

"It's salty," he said. "The fog is *salty*."

"Rivers don't do that." Teo stood at the bow with his chart, the one he'd been redrawing since the gorge, lines crossing out lines. "Freshwater doesn't do that. Unless—"

"Unless there's a source." Ulyana cut the engine and let them drift. The silence up here was enormous. No birds. No Optimist boats behind them—Sefa swore they'd lost the second boat in the gorge, though Ulyana had stopped trusting *lost*. Just the river, breathing salt.

The journal page from the dead camp was folded in her breast pocket, soft as cloth now from handling. *The water sings before it speaks.* She'd read it so many times the words had stopped meaning anything. She took it out anyway.

"Keep drifting," she said. "Eyes open."

They came around a bend of red clay cliffs and the valley opened like a held breath finally let go.

Terraces climbed the far slopes—green, planted, *tended*—cut into hillsides in steps that had to be generations old. A stream threaded down through them, branching and branching again, silver in the morning light. Smoke rose from stone chimneys among the trees. And at the head of the valley, where the cliffs closed together, a spring emerged from the rock face itself, wide as a house, water welling up in a slow boil and spilling over a lip of white stone.

The stone was white because it was crusted in salt. It gleamed. The whole lip of the spring gleamed, and the terraces below it were edged in the same white, like the land itself had been frosted.

Nobody spoke. Pirro's mouth was open. Teo had lowered his chart and was staring like a man watching his life's work become obsolete in real time.

"That's not possible," Sefa said quietly. "The hydrology surveys—the Optimists bought every survey of this basin for thirty years. There's no spring on any of them. The headwaters are *mapped*, Ulyana. They're mapped and they're dry."

"Then the maps are wrong," Ulyana said, "or the surveys were never done." She thought of the Optimists' cheerful letters, the water tariffs that climbed every dry season, the towns downstream buying their own river back by the liter. She thought of the enforcer's smile. *Clean slate, Ulyana. One job.*

Every river below. Every town downstream. It all came from here.

"Someone's coming," Pirro said.

Figures on the near terrace—five of them, six, moving down through the green with the unhurried confidence of people on their own ground. Two carried machetes, not raised, just carried. A woman in front, older, gray-braided, with a shotgun broken open over her arm like a courtesy.

Ulyana killed the engine fully and raised both hands, which felt absurd on her own boat.

"We're not armed," she called. "We're looking for the Meridian Expedition."

The gray-braided woman studied them for a long moment. Then she said something over her shoulder in a language Ulyana didn't know, and a boy broke away at a run up the terraces.

"You'll tie off," the woman said. "And you'll walk from here."

* * *

They walked. The woman—her name was Odile, and she offered nothing else—led them up a path between the terraces, past crops Ulyana didn't recognize growing in soil crusted white at the edges. Salt-farmers, she realized. Or salt-*farmers*, growing food in ground that bled salt, which shouldn't have worked, and looking around at the green terraces, clearly did.

The expedition's camp sat on a flat above the spring: four tents, patched, sun-bleached, and a radio mast lashed to a dead tree. People moved among the tents—thin, sunburned, moving slowly, but moving. Alive. Ulyana counted seven before she stopped counting.

A woman came out of the largest tent, and Ulyana knew her before anyone said the name, because the face had been on the news feeds for years: Dr. Imara Voss, the basin's most famous hydrologist, the woman whose expedition had gone silent four months ago. She was gaunt. Her hands were cracked and salt-burned. Her eyes swept over Ulyana's crew the way Ulyana's own eyes swept over weather—cataloguing, measuring, deciding.

"You're the resupply," Voss said. It wasn't a question. "The Optimists sent you."

Ulyana felt the whole valley tilt a degree. "We were hired to find you. Resupply and report. That's the job."

"And who do you report to?"

"The Optimists. I owe them." She said it flatly, because lying to this woman seemed both impossible and suicidal. "Debt. It's not complicated. I chase storms, they hold paper on my life, and finding you clears it."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. "Then you should know what you're reporting." She turned and walked toward the spring, and after a glance at Odile, who gave the smallest shrug, Ulyana followed. Her crew trailed her; nobody stopped them.

Up close the spring was louder than she'd expected—a deep, resonant upwelling sound, a note more than a noise, and she understood the journal page at last. *The water sings before it speaks.* It wasn't poetry. It was acoustics. The spring hummed.

"The headwaters of every river in this basin," Voss said, over the sound. "Not one river. All of them. The gorge splits the flow six ways below us. Every town from here to the coast drinks this." She crouched, touched the water, tasted her fingers. "Saline at the source. The salt precipitates out over the first fifty kilometers. By Lowmark it's fresh. Nobody downstream has ever known why the basin never goes dry in drought years. I knew. I measured it. And then I went silent."

"Why?"

"Because a man from the Optimists came to my camp in the night with a very polite offer." Voss stood. Her cracked hands were steady. "Enough money to endow my institute for a century, if the survey data showed the headwaters as seasonal seeps. Unreliable. Unworth developing." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing Ulyana had seen in years, colder than the enforcer's smile because it wasn't selling anything. "I refused. So they stopped answering my radio, and I understood that the next visitors would not be polite. We stayed. Half my team wanted to go home. I couldn't ask them to carry this secret down through a basin where the Optimists have ears in every trading post."

"So you starved quietly," Ulyana said, "on a mountain, guarding a spring."

"I guarded a *fact*, Ms. Okonkwo. The spring was here long before me." Voss looked at her, and the measuring in her gaze sharpened. "The question is what you'll do. Your debt is real. Your report is real. You go downstream and tell them where we are, and within a season there's a pumping station on that white stone, and within two seasons the valley community that fled the water monopoly is dispersed, and within five the Optimists own the only reliable water source in the basin outright, and every tariff they've ever dreamed of becomes law. That's the arithmetic. I've done it. I'm very good at arithmetic."

Ulyana looked at the spring. The white stone. The terraces climbing green and salt-frosted up the hillsides, worked by people who had run from the Optimists and built a life on the one thing the Optimists didn't know existed.

She thought about the number. Her number. The one that had chased her across the flats for twelve years, the one she'd taken a job to erase.

It had never looked this small.

"I came upriver to clear a debt," she said. "Not to sell a valley."

Voss's expression didn't change. "I've heard that sentence before," she said. "From a man who took the money."

* * *

They camped that night above the spring, the crew and the expedition together, and Ulyana sat apart on a salt-white rock with the journal page in her hand, not reading it. Pirro found her anyway. The boy always found her.

"Do we stay?" he asked.

"We haven't decided."

"Teo's mapping the split. Six rivers, he keeps saying. *Six*, Ulyana." Pirro sat down beside her, knees to his chest. "Sefa's not talking. She's been writing numbers in her book since sundown and she won't show anyone."

That snagged. Sefa, the ex-bookkeeper who'd fled the Optimists with nothing but a grudge and a ledger, writing numbers and hiding them. Ulyana filed it away in the drawer where she kept everything she didn't want to look at yet.

Below them, the spring hummed its one long note. Downstream, six rivers carried it to a hundred thousand people who didn't know its name.

"Get some sleep," she told Pirro.

She didn't sleep. Near midnight, Odile's runner boy came through camp at a jog again, waking the gray-braided woman, and Ulyana heard the words passed tent to tent in that unknown language, and one word in it she did know, because Sefa had taught it to them all as a joke weeks ago, an Optimist trade-tongue word that meant *boat*.

Two words, actually. *Boat. Coming.*

Ulyana stood up on her cold white rock and looked down the valley, toward the gorge, toward the dark water running wrong.

The Optimists hadn't lost them in the gorge after all.
