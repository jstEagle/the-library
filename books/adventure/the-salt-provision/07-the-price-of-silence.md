## 7. The Price of Silence

Imara Voss had spent thirty years learning to read water, and the first thing the water taught her was that everything lies.

Rivers lie about their age. Rain lies about where it came from. And the Meridian Expedition, six months upstream of anywhere that mattered, had been lying to itself since the day it left Lowmark with a charter that said *hydrographic survey* when everyone aboard knew the word underneath the word was *claim*.

She stood at the rim of the spring basin in the hour before dawn and watched the water breathe.

That was the only word for it. The springs rose in terraces of white stone, and each terrace filled, trembled, and released in a slow rhythm—*inhale, hold, exhale*—so regular it might have been engineered, except nothing human had engineered it. The overflow ran down the terraces in threads that caught the last of the moonlight and turned to wire. And beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the singing.

The others heard it in their sleep now. Halima dreamed in it. Okafor had stopped wearing his earplugs and started wearing a smile he didn't seem to know was there. Imara still woke at three each morning with the sound in her teeth, a note below the floor of hearing, the sound of a very large thing being very patient.

*Salt springs feeding every river below.* That was what the samples said, and the samples didn't lie, or rather they lied in only one direction, which was the direction of the truth. Every tributary in the lower basin carried trace salinity that no one had ever explained, because no one had ever come this far. The Optimists' water monopoly was built on the assumption that water came from the sky and belonged to whoever owned the pipes. It had never occurred to them that water could come from the ground, carry a signature, and belong to a valley of two hundred people who had fled the pipes.

Behind her, gravel shifted. Ulyana Okonkwo came up the trail the way she did everything—quietly, like someone practicing for a smaller life.

"You're up early," Imara said.

"Someone's up early." Ulyana stopped beside her and looked out at the terraces. She'd been at the valley four days and still hadn't gotten used to the sight; Imara could see it in her, the way her eyes went to the springs and then away, like looking at a fire. "The clouds. East ridge. They're stacked up like plates."

"Monsoon's early."

"Monsoon's *here*. Three days, maybe four." Ulyana's jaw worked. "Your people need to be off the low terraces when it breaks. The overflow channel won't hold that volume."

"The overflow channel has held it for longer than either of us has been alive."

"Has it." Ulyana said it flat, not a question. She was doing that more—flat statements that were questions, questions that were accusations. "You've had six months to tell someone where you were. Six months of dead air. I came up here expecting corpses, Dr. Voss. I'd like to hear why the corpses were so healthy."

Imara had rehearsed this conversation on the trail down from the ridge, and again at the fire, and again in the dark of the tent while the singing worked through the canvas. She'd rehearsed it because she was a scientist and scientists believed that if you could say a thing precisely enough, you could survive saying it.

"Sit down," she said.

Ulyana didn't sit. Imara sat anyway, on the cold stone at the basin's rim, and told it from the beginning, because there was no other place to begin.

* * *

They had found the springs on the forty-first day, and the finding had been an accident, and Imara had known within an hour that it was the most dangerous accident of her life.

The charter was Optimist money. She'd told herself it was laundered through the Meridian Institute, that the Institute's name on the letterhead meant the science belonged to science, that money didn't remember whose hands it came from. She'd told herself a lot of things in the lower gorge, when the current started running wrong and Okafor's instruments started disagreeing with the maps—water flowing uphill against every gradient they'd charted, the river braiding and rebraiding as though it couldn't decide where it was going, as though something below were pulling it.

They portaged the falls into a valley no cartographer had bothered to name, because no cartographer had come back from it, and found the terraces, and found the people—two hundred souls in stone houses with slate roofs, growing salt-tolerant grain on the high fields, running their lives on water that arrived from the ground with a purity no filtration plant in the lowlands could match.

The valley's elder was a woman named Sena with hands like tree bark and the unhurried eyes of someone who had been waiting for visitors her whole life.

"We knew you'd come," Sena said, on the first evening, pouring tea that tasted faintly of stone. "The Optimists bought the pipes. They never bought the source. Sooner or later, someone thirsty enough would walk upstream."

"We're not with the Optimists," Okafor said.

Sena looked at him the way you look at a child explaining the family business. "Your fuel has their mark on it. Your radio has their frequency in it. It doesn't matter what you are. It matters what you found."

And that was the sentence Imara carried back to camp, and turned over all night, and could not put down: *it matters what you found.*

Because she'd already run the numbers. Every scientist runs the numbers; it's how we pray. Two hundred people in a valley. Four million downstream, drinking water the Optimists metered and sold back to them at prices that broke families. The springs could end the monopoly in a season. The springs could also end the valley in a season—surveyors, drillers, security contractors, a road cut up the gorge, a company town on the high fields, and the people who had fled the pipes finding the pipes arriving anyway, wearing the mask of rescue.

She had six months to decide. She used all of them.

* * *

The offer came on day ninety, by radio, in a voice like warm oil.

Imara had been transmitting on the expedition's schedule—weekly check-ins, position reports, nothing of substance—and on day ninety the check-in was answered by a different voice. It introduced itself as a facilitator. It congratulated her on her progress. It did not say how it knew her progress, and it did not need to; the radio was an Optimist radio, the fuel was Optimist fuel, and she understood, with a lurch, that the expedition had never been a survey at all. It had been a probe. She was the probe's needle, and the hand holding the needle had felt something at the tip.

"Dr. Voss. The Institute's funding is secure for another decade, and your personal position—"

"Go on."

"Continue your survey. Report the gorge as impassable. Report the upper valley as geologically unstable, saline contamination, unusable aquifer. There is a number attached. It is not a small number. And there is a second number, a larger one, attached to the phrase *permanent closure*."

She stood in the radio tent with the handset against her ear and the singing in her teeth and did the thing she would think about every day for the rest of her life: she said, "I'll need time to consider."

"Take all the time you need," the warm voice said, and she heard in it the thing she'd spent thirty years studying water to understand, which is that the gentlest currents are often the deepest ones.

She told the expedition. That was the one thing she would not compromise on—she told them all, that night, around the fire, and watched the offer land on each of them like weather. Halima wanted to take it. Halima had debts. Okafor wanted to refuse it and transmit everything, immediately, consequences be damned. And the youngest of them, a surveyor named Denna who'd grown up in an Optimist water town, said the sentence that decided it:

"If we transmit, we don't control what happens. If we stay silent, at least we're the ones standing here."

So they went silent. They smashed the radio themselves, which was a lie of its own—a staged death, a manufactured mystery, because a dead expedition would draw one rescue boat and a missing expedition would draw a hundred searchers, and they needed the searchers to be the right kind or none at all. They moved into the valley. They learned the grain and the slate roofs and the rhythm of the terraces, and they waited to find out what kind of people they were.

Six months. And the answer, Imara had concluded, was: the kind who couldn't decide. The kind who watched the eastern ridge stack up with monsoon clouds and knew the water was coming either way.

* * *

"—and that's why you're dead," Ulyana said, when Imara finished. "Officially."

"Officially."

Ulyana paced the basin rim, three steps out, three steps back, a woman wearing a groove into stone. "You know what I was doing six months ago? Getting threatened by a man with a beautiful smile because I owed his employers money. And you're up here sitting on the biggest secret in the basin, and your plan was—what? Wait it out? Wait for *what*?"

"For the monsoon," Imara said. "For the season to close the gorge. For another year of learning how to be the kind of people who—"

"Who die on schedule?" Ulyana stopped pacing. "There's a second boat behind us, Dr. Voss. Optimist hull, Optimist fuel, and a man on it who smiles the way you'd smile at a number you liked. They followed my resupply run straight to you. Whatever you decided up here, the decision's already been made for you. It's coming up the river at eight knots."

Imara had known. Of course she'd known—the moment Okonkwo's boat rounded the bend with strangers aboard and a story about a resupply contract, she'd known the valley's six months of silence had been a held breath, and breaths get released. But hearing it said plainly, in the cold before dawn, with the terraces breathing their slow rhythm below—there was a difference between knowing a flood is coming and watching the water rise over your boots.

"Then I'll tell you what I want," Imara said. "And you can tell me I'm a fool, and then we can both go back to not sleeping."

Ulyana crossed her arms. "Go."

"I want to seal it. Not sell it, not broadcast it—*seal* it. The access gorge has one choke point above the falls. Sena's people have kept it passable for a century with a rope ladder and a prayer. Collapse it—properly, with the expedition's charges, the ones we were going to use for seismic survey—and the valley is a legend again for another hundred years. The springs stay unclaimed. The monopoly stays intact, and four million people keep drinking from Optimist pipes, but two hundred people keep living." She heard her own voice go quiet. "That's the offer I refused, turned inside out. They wanted me to bury it for money. I'm asking you to bury it for free."

The singing rose and fell. Somewhere below, the first terrace exhaled.

Ulyana stood very still for a long moment, and Imara watched her do the arithmetic she'd seen storm chasers do—distance, speed, shelter, the odds of the thing—and watched the arithmetic come back wrong, watched her refuse to say it was wrong.

"You're asking me to help you erase the only thing that could break them," Ulyana said. "The thing I came up here to sell them."

"I'm asking you to decide who you are before they arrive and decide for you."

Ulyana looked east. The clouds on the ridge had come down a thousand feet while they talked, stacked and bruise-colored, and the wind had turned, and it carried something new in it—salt on the wind, and under the salt, faint and patient, the first smell of rain.

"Three days," Ulyana said. "Two boats. One valley." She turned back, and her eyes had the flat bright look of someone standing in open ground watching a storm cell decide. "You'd better show me these charges, Dr. Voss. And then you'd better pray your elder woman has a faster answer than either of us—because I don't think the river's going to give us time to be good people."
