## 8. Two Boats, One Truth

Pirro heard the engines before anyone else. That was the thing nobody understood about him yet—he wasn't just a deckhand, he was ears. Twelve years old and half-starved when Ulyana fished him off the dock at Lowmark, and his whole life he'd learned to listen for the sounds that meant trouble: boots on stairs, a father's key in the lock, the particular idle of an Optimist patrol boat with its engine tuned too rich.

He was up on the ridge above the springs, hauling water for Imara Voss's half-dead expedition survivors, when the sound came threading up the gorge. Two strokes. Deep. Wrong for this river.

He dropped the yoke and ran.

* * *

By the time he skidded into the valley camp, everyone had heard it too. The whole settlement was gathered at the downstream bend where the river narrowed—the valley folk with their salt-crusted hands and their quiet eyes, the Meridian survivors leaning on each other like furniture, Ulyana standing out front with her arms crossed like she could stop a boat by glaring at it.

Teo had the spyglass. He passed it to Ulyana without a word.

"Well?" Sefa said.

"Two boats," Ulyana said. "Optimist hulls. The big one's got a mounted winch." She lowered the glass. "They're not even hurrying."

"They don't have to," Sefa said. Her voice did something Pirro had never heard it do—went flat and small, like a coin dropped on stone. "This is how they always come. Slow. Like the outcome's already decided and they're just being polite about it."

"You know their playbook," Ulyana said, watching her.

"I wrote part of it." Sefa's jaw worked. "There'll be a parley first. They always offer terms before force. It's cheaper."

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less if the water's high."

Ulyana turned to the group. Pirro pressed himself against a supply crate so he wouldn't be sent away, because he knew what came next: the adults deciding things, the kid getting shuffled behind the tents. Imara Voss stepped forward, sunburned and stubborn, and the valley elders clustered around her, and the argument started—the same argument they'd been having for three days, sealed valley or shared truth, except now it had engines attached to it.

Pirro didn't listen. He watched Sefa.

She'd drifted to the edge of the circle, and she was doing something with her hands. Counting, almost. Fingers moving against her thumb like she was running numbers nobody else could see. He'd seen bookkeepers do that in Lowmark—his mother had been one, before the debt took everything—and there was a rhythm to it, a private arithmetic.

Then she looked up, caught him looking, and stopped.

"Pirro," she said quietly. "Come here."

He went, because you went when Sefa called; she was the one who made sure he ate.

"When they land," she said, "some of what happens next is going to look a certain way. I need you to remember one thing." She crouched to his eye level, and her hands were steady but her eyes weren't. "Whatever you see me do—remember that I kept the original survey data. The real coordinates. The full chemical assay. It's not on any boat. It's not in any ledger they can burn."

"Where is it?"

"That doesn't matter yet." She glanced back at the circle of adults, still arguing. "What matters is that it exists, and it can't exist only in my head, because heads are fragile things around the Optimists. So I need you to be my backup. Can you do that?"

"I don't understand what you're—"

"No," she said gently. "You don't. Yet. Just remember: I kept the originals. Say it back."

"You kept the originals."

"Good boy." She stood, and something in her face closed like a ledger snapping shut. "Now go help Teo with the lines. And Pirro—" she caught his shoulder, "—stay near the boats. Whatever happens, stay near the boats."

* * *

The Optimists landed forty minutes later, and they landed smiling.

Pirro had seen the enforcer once before, from a distance, through Teo's spyglass on the river—the man called Brother Cassian, though he wasn't anybody's brother. Up close he was worse. Big without being heavy, gray at the temples, wearing a pale linen suit that somehow stayed clean on a wild river, and a smile like a door left open in a warm house. Behind him came eight crewmen in matching vests, cheerful as a rowing club.

"My friends!" Cassian spread his arms on the shingle. "What a journey you've had. The Optimists' Concern for Human Welfare extends its compliments to Dr. Voss, to Captain Okonkwo, and to all of you. We've come to bring you home."

"There's nothing here worth your concern," Imara said.

"Oh, I disagree most warmly." He gestured at the terraced springs above them, the white mineral shelves steaming faintly in the morning cold. "Salt. Unmediated, unlicensed, unmonetized salt, feeding every drop of water between here and the sea. Do you know what that does to our balance sheets, Doctor? It gives us hives."

"So you've come to destroy it."

"I've come to *discuss* it." The smile widened a fraction. "The Concern offers generous terms. Relocation stipends. New identities. The valley folk walk away wealthy. The expedition walks away employed. And Captain Okonkwo—" he turned, and the smile found her like a searchlight, "—walks away free. Every mark of debt, expunged. Signed today, witnessed by God and the river. All we ask in return is discretion. A sealed valley. A story that ends here."

Pirro saw Ulyana's hands. They were fists at her sides, and then they weren't, and then they were again. He saw her look at the valley people—at the kids, and there were kids here, small ones, staring out from behind their mothers—and he saw her do the arithmetic she must have been doing since Lowmark. One signature against all of this.

"Don't answer yet," Cassian said pleasantly. "Deliberation is a virtue. We'll wait on the boats until dusk."

That was when Sefa stepped forward.

"Sefa Okonkwo-Ndiaye," Cassian said, and his smile changed—not warmer, but more real, the way a knife is more real than a handshake. "My first student. My best student. I grieved when you ran."

"You celebrated," Sefa said. "I read the internal memo."

"I celebrate efficiency." He opened his arms. "Come home. You know how this ends. You can make it end kindly."

And Pirro watched her go to him.

Watched her cross the shingle, past Ulyana, who said her name once—just once, cracked down the middle—and watched Sefa take Cassian's hand and turn to face the camp and say, in her flat bookkeeper's voice:

"The terms are acceptable. The Concern will want the survey ledgers as proof of good faith. Captain, fetch them."

Ulyana stared at her. "Sefa."

"Now, Captain. Please." And there was something under the words, some frequency only dogs and twelve-year-olds could hear, and Pirro's skin prickled, because he remembered: *I kept the originals.*

Ulyana hesitated one second longer. Then she went to the supply tent, and came back with the false ledgers—the decoys Teo had spent two nights doctoring, wrong elevations, diluted assays, a spring location off by six kilometers—and put them in Sefa's arms.

Sefa carried them to Cassian like a bride carrying flowers.

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way bad things do.

Cassian's men relaxed—that was the mistake, the beautiful human mistake, because they thought it was over. The winch crew stood down. Two of them actually sat down and opened a flask. Cassian weighed the fake ledgers in his hand, flipping pages, and his smile started to curdle at whatever he was reading, because Teo's forgery was good but the chemistry was subtly wrong and Cassian had been trained by Sefa, which meant he knew numbers.

"You said the main vent was at elevation nine hundred," he said slowly.

"It is," Sefa said.

"This says eight-forty."

"Then the document is correct and your memory isn't."

Cassian looked up. And Pirro, watching from beside the boats exactly where Sefa told him to stay, saw the exact moment the smile died—saw the man understand, all at once, that he'd been played by his own student, in front of his own crew, and that the cost of letting that stand was higher than the cost of anything else.

"Bind them," Cassian said. "All of them. Gently—we're the Concern for Human Welfare—but bind them."

The valley erupted. Ulyana moved first, driving her shoulder into the nearest vest-man; Imara snatched up a survey pole and swung it like a pike; the valley folk came off the terraces with stones and fury. It became a scrum, ugly and close, dust and shouting, and Pirro was supposed to stay by the boats, and he did, he stayed—

—which is why he was ten feet from the Optimists' lead boat when he saw it.

The radio.

Mounted on the stern rail, brass-cased, antenna up, a transmit key sitting right there. Channel Nine crackled faintly from its speaker—river traffic chatter, Lowmark harbor control, the whole chattering world downstream. He'd spent a hundred nights listening to that box while the adults slept. He knew its voice better than his own father's.

*Sefa's voice in his head:* heads are fragile things. *Her fingers counting.* I kept the originals—it exists, and it can't exist only in my head.

But it also couldn't exist only in hers. Not if she was about to be bound, or worse. Not if the only copy of the truth was inside a woman standing next to the man who trained her to lie.

Somebody had to put it somewhere no fist could reach.

Pirro climbed over the transom.

His hands shook so badly he missed the transmit key twice. Behind him the fight rolled and crashed—someone screamed, a body hit water—but he kept his eyes on the dial, the way Ulyana had taught him to keep his eyes on the horizon when the flats storm hit: *the panic lives in your stomach, Pirro, never in your hands.* He found the band. Harbor control, Lowmark. Then the relay repeater at Milestone Bend that rebroadcast to every town on the river, because Sefa had explained it to him once, bored, on a long slow stretch, and he'd memorized it the way he memorized everything because knowing things was how a small person survived.

He keyed the mic.

"Mayday, mayday, mayday, this is—this is Pirro, deckhand of the vessel *Charity's Wake*, calling all stations, all towns, all listeners on the river." His voice cracked and he hated it and he kept going. "The source of the river is salt springs. Real ones. Free ones. Above the gorge, in the hidden valley, elevation nine hundred meters, and the Optimists' Concern has known about them for years and buried them, and they're here right now trying to seal the valley forever. The water monopoly is a lie. The springs belong to everybody downstream. Come and see. Come and—"

A hand closed on his collar and hauled him backward off the crate.

"—come and *see*—" he shrieked into the open air, and then he was spinning, and Cassian's face filled his whole sky, and the smile was gone now, gone completely, replaced by something older and colder than any ledger.

"You're the stowaway," Cassian said. "The one from the manifest. Children are expensive, little one. Did you know that? There's always a price."

"He's already paid it," said a voice behind them both.

Sefa stood at the rail, breathing hard, a split lip bleeding onto her chin, holding the false ledgers in one hand—and in the other, held up where the whole river could witness it, a slim oilskin packet.

The originals.

"You broadcast it?" she asked Pirro.

"Every station," he gasped. "Every town."

Sefa smiled—a real one, fierce and ruined—and pitched the packet high across the boat toward Ulyana on the shingle, toward safety, toward the future.

Cassian moved faster than a man that size should move. He lunged, not for the packet—for Sefa—and the two of them went over the rail together into the brown flood of the river, and upstream, black as a bruise, the monsoon finally came over the mountains.
