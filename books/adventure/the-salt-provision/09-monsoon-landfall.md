## 9. Monsoon Landfall

The storm did not arrive. It landed.

I'd chased weather for twelve years and I had never heard anything like the sound the monsoon made coming down the valley—a freight-train roar with no tracks, a wall of gray that ate the ridgeline whole. One moment I could see the far cliffs. The next, the world was water falling sideways.

"Inside!" Imara's voice, barely audible over it. "The survey hut—go!"

We ran. The valley community scattered toward the stone shelters they'd built against exactly this, generations of them, and I grabbed Pirro by the collar because he was standing in the open with his face turned up to the sky like he was being blessed.

"Pirro!"

"I'm coming!" But he wasn't looking at the rain. He was looking at the river.

The river was climbing.

Not rising—*climbing*, fast as a man walking, then faster, brown and muscular, tearing at its banks, and above us the salt springs' terraces were already vanishing under white water. Everything the Optimists wanted, everything they'd kill for, was drowning in front of their eyes, and there was a black kind of joy in that I didn't have time to feel.

The enforcer—Rask, Sefa had finally given me his name, Rask who smiled like a door closing—was not running for shelter either. He was running for the expedition's supply boat, where the evidence ledger sat lashed under canvas: every reading, every assay, every proof that the springs fed every river downstream and the monopoly was built on a lie. The Optimists had come upriver for one thing, and when the flood took the valley, the ledger would be the only thing left floating.

He reached the boat before I did.

* * *

Water hit my knees halfway down the bank. The rain came so hard it bounced, a foot of standing spray on solid ground, and the river had swallowed the landing entirely. The boat strained at its lines like an animal that had decided, finally, to be free.

Rask was aboard already, sawing at the mooring line with a knife, and the ledger case was open at his feet—he'd cut the canvas first, priorities straight as always, cheerful even now. I could see him laughing. I couldn't hear him over the storm but I knew the shape of his mouth.

There was no plan. There was no clever Ulyana Okonkwo move, no calculated risk, no debt arithmetic. There was just cold water and a man with a knife and the only true document in three hundred miles of lying country.

I swam.

The current took me sideways immediately, slammed me against the hull, and I got an arm over the gunwale and hauled. My shoulder screamed. The boat bucked under both of us now, shipping water, the mooring line half-cut and groaning. Rask straightened up and looked at me with genuine, unfeigned delight, the way he'd looked at me on the flats outside Lowmark when he'd offered me my clean slate.

"Ulyana! You're a terrible swimmer!"

"You're a terrible person!" It came out as a cough. Half the river was in my lungs.

He picked up the ledger case and held it over the side, over the flood, dangling it. That was the thing about Rask—they never trained him to fight, they trained him to negotiate. Everything was leverage. Even now, drowning-adjacent, his instinct was to make the world an offer.

"Here's the deal," he shouted. "You let go, I keep the case dry, everybody walks away rich. Or we all find out what's downstream of here together."

"The towns already know," I screamed back. "Pirro transmitted the coordinates last night. Every radio downstream. You're holding a dead man's receipts, Rask!"

Something flickered across his face—the first crack in the smile—and I understood, watching it break, that he hadn't known. He'd been so busy chasing the paper truth that he'd missed the broadcast one. The Optimists' whole empire was built on controlling information, and a fourteen-year-old with stolen equipment had just made control obsolete.

"That's not—" He stopped. Recalculated. Smiled again, wider, and I realized with horror that the smile meant he'd found a new angle. "Then the case doesn't matter. What matters is you. Dead chaser, drowned in a flood, tragic. And the syndicate keeps its option to say the springs are a rumor."

He dropped the case into the water.

I went after it without deciding to. That's the honest version—I didn't choose, my body chose, twelve years of chasing storms having taught me that you move toward the thing that matters or you spend the rest of your life explaining why you didn't. The water closed over my head. The case was sinking, heavy with paper and proof, and I caught the handle as the current ripped us both downstream, past the boat, past the bank, into the brown roaring dark.

* * *

I have drowned before. Not all the way—but enough to know the grammar of it: the cold punching your lungs shut, the panic, then the strange calm when the body understands it is losing. This time the calm never came, because the river kept slamming me into things, and every impact said *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

I got a hand on a submerged fence post. The valley community's old fencing, driven deep decades ago by people who planned for floods. I wrapped the ledger strap around my wrist and held on while the water tried to peel me off like bark.

And through the sheeting rain I saw the second boat.

Sefa stood at the helm of the Optimists' vessel, alone at the wheel, driving it straight through floodwater no sane pilot would enter. Behind her, lashed to the deck, were the fuel drums they'd stolen from us at the trading post—and she was cutting them loose. All of them. Drums bobbed away downstream, and the boat swung around in a wide, deliberate arc, putting herself between me and the worst of the current, breaking the water for me the way a hull breaks a wave.

She threw a line. It landed close. Second throw landed closer.

"Sefa!" I could barely hear myself. "You went back to them!"

"I know!" She was hauling the line in hand over hand, teeth gritted, and she was crying or the rain was, impossible to tell. "I know what it looked like! Get in the boat, Ulyana!"

I got in the boat.

I lay on the deck with the ledger case under me like a child with a stuffed animal, shaking, alive, furious. Sefa knelt over me, and from inside her coat she pulled out a second bundle, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with cord, bone-dry.

The original survey data. Voss's own field notebooks. Every page the ledger was copied from, plus the pages the ledger didn't have—the ones Voss had hidden from her own team, the ones with the spring locations marked in detail, the ones worth more than everything else combined.

"You—" I couldn't finish sentences. "When?"

"The night before the parley." She sat back on her heels, drenched, exhausted, and for once she didn't look like a bookkeeper. She looked like a woman who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had finally set it down. "Rask trained me, Ulyana. Do you know what he taught me first? Never carry the real numbers where they can be taken. Keep a copy. Keep two." She almost laughed. "I've been keeping copies since I was nineteen years old. They think I defected back. Let them. By the time they figure out what I actually did, it won't matter."

Downstream, the fuel drums she'd jettisoned were drifting toward the Optimists' own moored launch. Toward Rask, who was clinging to the wrecked supply boat, still smiling, still calculating, reaching for the nearest floating object—

The drum hit him broadside.

It shouldn't have been enough. A drum of fuel, even a full one, even moving at flood speed—it shouldn't have been enough to tear a grown man off a boat. But the river had been waiting for him all day, patient as debt, and when the drum struck, the current took him the way it takes everything it has decided belongs to it.

He didn't scream. That was the worst part. He kept smiling, even in the water, even going under—even, I swear, in the instant the flood folded over him—as if he'd finally found an offer he could accept.

The supply boat broke apart behind him. The ledger case was still strapped to my wrist. The original data was still dry inside Sefa's coat. And somewhere downstream, in a hundred towns that had heard a teenager's voice on channel nine saying *the source is real, the springs are real, come and see*—the truth was already loose, traveling faster than any flood.

* * *

We grounded the boat on the high terrace below the survey hut, and the crew came down through the rain to pull us out—Teo with a rope, Pirro with his arms out like he was catching me personally, Imara shouting questions nobody could answer over the wind. The valley community emerged from their stone shelters and stood in the deluge watching the river eat the low fields, and nobody panicked, because they had built for this, because they had always known the water would sometimes take and always return.

I sat on the terrace with the ledger in my lap and shook until the shaking stopped.

Pirro crouched next to me. He was soaked to the skin and grinning like the storm was a parade thrown in his honor.

"You transmitted," I said. "Last night. While everyone was sleeping."

"You weren't going to do it." No apology anywhere in him. "You were going to negotiate, or wait, or find some way where nobody got hurt. Somebody had to just *say it*." He shrugged. "You taught me that. On the flats. You said running costs more than staying. So I stayed up and I said it."

I pulled him against my side, and he let me, which he hadn't a month ago, and the rain hammered down on both of us like applause.

Above us, the clouds were thinning at the western edge—not clearing, monsoons don't clear, but shifting, the storm spending itself the way storms do. Teo stood at the terrace edge with his ruined map case held over his head like a trophy, watching the flood reshape the valley he'd spent weeks failing to map correctly.

"It flows right now," he called back to us, wonder in his voice. "The current—above the gorge it always ran wrong, upstream, recirculating. The flood's overwhelmed it. For the first time since I started charting, the river runs the direction rivers are supposed to run."

Toward the sea. Toward the towns. Toward everyone waiting downstream with radios turned up loud.

Sefa sat down beside me and put the oilcloth bundle between us, and neither of us touched it. It didn't need guarding anymore. It needed *delivering*—to every town, every council, every well that had ever run dry while someone upstream grew rich on the lie.

"When the water drops," she said quietly, "we go downriver. All of us. We read them the numbers ourselves."

I looked at the ledger in my lap—at the contract tucked inside it, my own debt, the Optimists' neat handwriting promising me a clean slate I no longer needed anyone to grant.

The river was dropping already. Faster than any flood I'd ever seen, as if it had somewhere to be.

As if it knew the way home.
