## 10. The Source Is Downstream

The rain stopped on a Tuesday, and the world did not end.

Ulyana stood in the doorway of the survey hut with a tin cup of tea going cold in her hands and watched the valley steam. Water ran everywhere—off the eaves, down the terraces, through the channels the valley folk had cut generations before anyone downstream thought to ask where their rivers began. It ran loud and constant and free, and for the first time in her adult life, Ulyana Okonkwo did not calculate what it was costing her.

Pirro found her there. He'd grown two centimeters since Lowmark, or claimed to, and he wore Dr. Voss's spare field coat with the sleeves rolled four times.

"Radio's doing it again," he said. "Every channel. Kessington's tapped the north fork. Marrow's dug a well right off the survey coordinates. There's a woman in Marrow who says her grandmother used to sing to the water." He grinned. "Singing water. It was true the whole time."

"It usually is," Ulyana said. "The lies are just louder."

Downstream, the Optimists were dissolving the way salt dissolves—slowly at first, then all at once. Their lawyers had turned on each other within days of Pirro's broadcast, every branch office suddenly an independent franchise of deniability. The enforcer who had trained Sefa, who had smiled his way upriver behind them like a patient shark, had been swept out of the gorge and fished out three days later by eel fishermen, alive, minus one ledger and most of his certainty. He'd sent no threats. That was how Ulyana knew it was over. Men like him only went quiet when the arithmetic stopped working.

Her own contract sat on the table inside, where she'd left it after the flood, water-stained and warped, the Optimists' cheerful gold seal bloomed with mildew. She touched it every morning the way you touch a bruise—to confirm it still hurt, then to confirm it hurt less.

* * *

They came for the tearing on a Thursday, because Teo insisted history needed scheduling.

The cartographer had spent the weeks since landfall drafting something unprecedented: not a map of the river, but a map *for* it. Every spring, every seep, every tributary the monopoly had buried under paperwork and pipe, rendered in his precise, disgraced, redeemed hand. He'd titled it simply THE SOURCE IS DOWNSTREAM and printed forty copies on Voss's ancient press, and now towns were running them off by the hundreds, pinning them to market walls from Lowmark to the coast.

The camera crew arrived by mule—two young journalists from Kessington with mud on their boots and the hungry look of people who'd been told *no* their whole careers. They set up in front of the survey hut, and Ulyana stood in the frame with the contract in her hands, and found she didn't know what to say.

"Just tell it true," Pirro whispered from off-frame. "That's all anybody wants."

So she did.

"My name is Ulyana Okonkwo," she said. "I owed these people eleven years of my life. I chased storms because running was cheaper than staying, and I took this job because staying cost more than I had." She held the contract up so the seal showed, soft and rotten as old fruit. "This is what they called a clean slate. It was never clean. It was a leash with good handwriting."

She tore it once, lengthwise. The paper resisted, then gave. She tore it again, crosswise, and again, until she was holding confetti of her own servitude, and the wind off the springs took a handful of it and carried it toward the water.

"The debt is scattered," she said. "Like everything else they tried to keep."

The journalists asked questions afterward—about the expedition, about Voss, about the man in the flood—but Ulyana gave them the short versions. Some stories you ration. The long ones belonged to the valley now.

* * *

Sefa found her that evening on the ridge above the springs, where the whole watershed lay visible: silver threads braiding into the river, the river braiding into tomorrow.

"I got a letter," Sefa said. She handed it over without ceremony. "From the litigation committee. They want me back. Bookkeeper emeritus. They think if they can get one of us to come home, it proves nothing really changed."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them I'd consider it." Sefa's mouth twitched. "I'm letting them sweat. It's the first time in my life I've ever made an Optimist wait, and I have to say, the view is excellent."

Ulyana laughed—a real one, surprising her own chest. Below them, the evening shift of valley workers moved along the channels, checking gates, singing half a song. An old woman waved up at the ridge, unhurried.

"They'll dissolve eventually," Sefa said. "Or shrink into something small enough to drown in a teacup. Either way." She looked out at the water. "You know what I keep thinking? Eleven years I balanced their books, and I never once audited myself. Up here, everything reconciles."

"That's the most bookkeeper thing you've ever said."

"I contain multitudes. Also ledgers."

They stood a while longer. Then Sefa said, quietly, "Teo's drawing a house. Did you know? Into the new map, bottom corner, little sketch. A house with room for four. He thinks nobody's noticed."

Ulyana said nothing. Her throat had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with grief.

* * *

The next storm came on schedule, because storms always do.

She felt it first as pressure behind her teeth, an old familiar ache, and by dusk the horizon south of the valley had gone the color of a bruise and the wind had that low, gathering note she'd spent twelve years chasing across flats and floodplains. Every nerve she owned lit up like a switchboard. *Go*, said the oldest part of her. *Get ahead of it. Get the readings. Get the data. Be somewhere else when it lands.*

She walked out to the boat landing instead.

The *Meridian's Wake* rode at anchor, patched and scarred, hull still bearing the gouge from the gorge rocks. Pirro was aboard, coiling lines with the exaggerated competence of a boy who'd decided he was crew forever. Teo sat on the rail with the map proofs, annotating. Sefa was arguing with him about scale. Down at the springs, Imara Voss knelt at the water's edge taking samples she no longer needed to hide, and looked up, and nodded—not a summons, just a greeting between people who expected to see each other tomorrow.

The storm built in the south, magnificent and indifferent.

"You could make it to the flats by midnight," Pirro said, watching her face. He knew exactly what she was looking at. "You'd get a full read on it. Best cell of the season, probably."

"Probably," Ulyana agreed.

"And you'd be gone a week. Maybe two. Maybe—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Everyone on that landing knew the shape of the thing she'd spent her whole life outrunning: that the chasing was also the leaving, that every storm was a permission slip written in lightning.

Ulyana looked at the sky a long moment. Then she did the thing she had never once done in twelve years of professional storm-chasing.

She sat down.

Right there on the dock planks, boots dangling over the black water, and patted the boards beside her. Pirro hesitated, then dropped down next to her. One by one the others followed—Sefa, Teo rolling his maps carefully first—and even Voss climbed the path from the springs, drying her hands on her coat, and joined the row of them like it was the most natural thing in the world, five people sitting on a dock watching weather come.

"We could measure it," Teo offered. "From here. Barometric drop, wind onset, rainfall total. All the data, none of the driving."

"We could," Ulyana said.

"It won't be the same," she added, and was surprised to find she wasn't sad about it. "But nothing that matters is."

The first drops hit the river fat and warm, raising rings. The wind came up the valley carrying salt, and the water below them began its faint, impossible hum—the singing the old woman's grandmother had known, the sound the whole region would grow up believing in now, because a teenage deckhand had refused to let it stay quiet.

Pirro leaned against her shoulder. "How long you staying?"

Ulyana considered the question the way she'd once considered cloud formations: seriously, professionally, with her whole attention.

She thought in miles. She had always thought in miles—miles to the debt, miles to the storm, miles between herself and anywhere that might ask her to be known. But miles were a runner's arithmetic, and she was done running.

"Ask me in a year," she said. "And then ask me again after that. And again after that."

Pirro frowned, working it. "So... years?"

"Years," Ulyana said.

The storm broke over the valley in earnest then, rain hammering the channels full, the springs answering with their bright salt song, and nobody on the dock moved to chase it. They just sat together in the roaring dark, five people who had chosen each other by accident and kept choosing on purpose, while the water rose and sang and ran, as it always had, toward everyone.

Far downstream, in towns that were already digging, someone was surely nailing a copy of Teo's map to a wall. And far upstream, at the head of the valley where the springs rose white and steady out of the dark earth, the source did what sources do.

It stayed.
