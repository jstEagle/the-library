## 2. Marguerite at the Fence

The rain came in an hour behind him, which was how he knew it had been waiting.

Wendell put the plane away first—chocks, tie-downs, the canvas over the cowl, each motion worn smooth as river stone from seven years of repetition. The hangar at Ishikawa Field wasn't much: one corrugated bay, a workbench with a vice that had come with the property, a cot in the corner he'd stopped apologizing for. He hung his wet jacket on the nail by the door and stood a moment listening to the roof tick and drum, letting the day's cargo settle out of him. Vaccines to Kessuma Landing. A crate of schoolbooks. Two hundred letters, most of them love letters, all of them more honest than anything he'd written since Elio died.

Then he walked up to the house, and saw her sitting on his porch.

She'd made herself comfortable the way weather makes itself comfortable—not asking permission, simply arriving and rearranging everything around her presence. Marguerite Voss sat in the one chair that faced the valley, legs crossed, sunglasses pushed up into dark hair cut short and severe, a leather document case balanced on her knees like something she'd been born holding. She wore field clothes that cost more than his annual fuel budget and wore them like they were embarrassed to be seen on her. No umbrella. She'd let the rain hit her on the walk up from the strip, and either she hadn't minded or she'd wanted him to see that she didn't.

"You're late," she said.

"Flight schedule's posted at the co-op." He climbed the two steps and did not sit. "You're not on it."

"I don't fly scheduled." She tilted her head back against the post and looked at him through the screen of rain coming off the eaves. "You look terrible, Wendell."

"You look expensive."

A smile touched her mouth and left again, quick as a fish rising. "Seven years. You used to be funnier."

"Seven years ago I had a co-pilot who laughed at my material."

Something moved behind her eyes at that—fast, controlled, gone—and Wendell felt the old reflexive satisfaction of having landed a blow, followed immediately by the older, duller ache underneath it. He turned away, opened the door, and jerked his chin at the case. "Inside. Rain's getting in."

* * *

His kitchen was a table, two chairs, a stove, and a window that looked down the slope at his airplane under its canvas, small and safe as a kept promise. Marguerite took the chair facing the window without being offered it. He put water on to boil because his hands needed a job, and while it heated she laid three documents on the table in a neat fan, squared their edges with one finger, and waited until he sat down before touching any of them.

He recognized the ritual. She'd always been like this—the presentation before the pitch, the theater of order before the sale. At the survey firm, before the Salt Rivergate, she'd brief a room full of grown men with maps and slide rules and leave them feeling like children who'd wandered into a lecture. Elio used to say she could sell a drowning man the river.

"Wendell Ishikawa," she read, though she obviously knew it already, "holder of aircraft registration 4-Bravo-Kilo, operator of a mail-and-medical route serving eleven villages between here and the Kessuma headwaters, debtor to Meridian Credit & Trade in the amount of—" she glanced at the top sheet, though she didn't need to— "a figure we both know you can't clear in eleven weeks."

"The balloon payment's private."

"It was. It's mine now." She slid the first document across. "As of nine days ago, Meridian sold your paper. All of it. Principal, interest, the balloon, the works. To me."

The kettle began its low climb toward a whistle. Wendell didn't get up. He looked at the signature block on the transfer notice—at the confident, downward-slanting hand he'd have known anywhere, the hand that had signed off on half the surveys that opened the interior—and felt the floor of the last seven years tilt very slightly beneath him.

"You bought my debt."

"I bought your debt," she agreed.

"That's not a loan, Marguerite. That's a leash."

"It's a contract." She tapped the second document. "And contracts have terms. Here are yours." She pulled the middle sheet free and turned it so he could read it upside down, then rotated it, unhurried, watching him do the arithmetic of humiliation. "One flight. Charter rate, double standard, hazard bonus on top. Your balloon payment cleared in full at wheels-down. Debt discharged. Title to the aircraft transfers clean, no liens, no Meridian, no more eleven-week clocks ticking in your chest every morning."

"And in exchange?"

"One flight to the headwaters. My crew, my cargo, my schedule. You fly, I navigate, nobody asks anybody questions about why."

The kettle screamed. He got up, poured water over the grounds in the dented pot, and came back to find her watching him with an expression he remembered too well—the patient look of a woman holding a winning hand and enjoying, genuinely enjoying, the other player's discomfort.

"Nobody asks questions," he said. "That's the part that should worry me."

"That's the part that pays you."

He sat. He wrapped both hands around his cup because they wanted to shake and he refused to let them. Outside, the rain thickened against the window, and somewhere down the slope the canvas over the cowling snapped once in a gust and went still.

"Headwaters is closed country," he said. "The range permits were revoked after—" He stopped. Rerouted. "After the survey. Nobody flies up there. There's nothing up there but weather and rock and a river nobody owns."

"Nothing," Marguerite said softly, "is a very specific word, Wendell. Nothing is what people say when they've decided to stop looking." She took off her sunglasses entirely now, folded them, set them on the table between them like a small black insect. Her eyes were the same as he remembered—pale, level, and absolutely without mercy for anyone including herself. "I'm asking you to fly me someplace you swore never to go again. I know that. I've done the math on what that costs you, and I've priced it into the offer. What I haven't done is pretend it isn't exactly what it is."

"Which is?"

"Leverage." She said it plainly, no apology. "I own your debt. I could call the balloon tomorrow and take the plane, and you'd be grounded, broke, and back to being nobody in eleven weeks instead of debt-free in one flight. I'm not going to do that. But we both know I could, and pretending otherwise would insult us both. So." She spread her hands. "Ask me the real question."

Wendell looked at her across the steam rising off his cup. Seven years of careful smallness—small routes, small cargoes, small talk with small-town people who asked nothing of him but punctuality—all of it suddenly weightless, suddenly provisional, because the past had landed on his airstrip and started rearranging his furniture.

"The real question," he said slowly, "is why you need me. You've got money. You could charter anyone in the territory."

"No," Marguerite said, "I couldn't."

And there it was—the first crack in the performance, small as a seam in ice. She looked down at her folded glasses, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped its boardroom polish and become something quieter and far more frightening: sincere.

"There are maybe four pilots alive who can put a load into the Kessuma headwaters before the snows, and two of them are dead, one is blind, and the fourth is sitting in front of me drinking bad coffee and refusing to look at me. I don't need a pilot, Wendell. I need *you*. The one who flew that corridor when it was new. The one who knows what the weather does in the throat of the range better than the forecasters do. The one who—" she paused, and chose the words like footing on scree— "the one who was there."

"I wasn't there when it mattered."

"You were there longer than anyone." She leaned forward. "Wendell. I'm not the enemy. I know you've spent seven years deciding I am, and I understand why, and I'm not going to waste our time arguing with a ghost. But whatever you think happened up there, whatever you've been carrying—it's not finished. The rivergate isn't finished. And somebody else is going to go looking whether I do or not, and I would rather it be me, with you, than them, without you."

"Who's them?"

"Ask me after wheels-up."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that doesn't end this conversation." She held his gaze, and for a moment—just a moment—he saw past the armor to the thing underneath, the thing he'd glimpsed only twice in all the years he'd known her: fear. Real fear, disciplined and buried and running the whole operation from below decks. "Eleven weeks, Wendell. One flight. Snow closes the passes in six. Do the math faster than I did."

He did the math. He'd been doing it all morning, actually, since he'd seen her standing at the fence line at Kessuma Landing watching him taxi in, and understood that the empty sky behind him had been temporary. The math said: lose the plane, lose the route, lose the eleven villages that expected him the way they expected the rain—unreliably, occasionally, but always eventually. Lose the only version of himself he'd managed to build out of the wreckage.

The math also said: fly north into the corridor where Elio burned, with Marguerite Voss in the right seat and questions in the cargo bay he didn't want answered.

"Who's your crew?" he said finally.

"Four of us besides you. Surveyor, mechanic, and a man who handles complications."

"A fixer."

"A logistics specialist."

"Marguerite."

"Yes," she said. "A fixer."

He looked out the window. The rain was easing, the way it did here, spending itself all at once and leaving the air scrubbed and shining. Down the slope, light broke across the wet canvas of the cowling, and for a second his plane gleamed like something worth saving—which, he thought, was exactly the point. It was always exactly the point with her.

"If I say no," he said, "you'll call the balloon."

"I'll give you until morning," Marguerite said, "and then yes."

* * *

She slept in the hangar office—her insistence, her sleeping bag, her arrangement, everything hers—and Wendell lay awake in the loft above the bay listening to the building settle and the last drips come off the roof, doing the arithmetic he'd promised himself he was done with.

Elio would have taken the flight. That was the trouble. Elio would have taken it laughing, already halfway to loving her again, already composing the joke he'd tell about it later, because Elio trusted the world the way Wendell had never learned to and it had killed him for it. And Wendell had spent seven years becoming the opposite of that trust—checking tie-downs twice, filing flight plans nobody required, flying the long way around weather instead of through it—and now the world had sent Marguerite Voss to his kitchen table to explain, politely, with paperwork, that avoidance was a luxury he could no longer afford.

Below him, faint through the floorboards, he heard her moving. Not sleeping. Walking the hangar bay slow, stopping—by the sound of it—at the workbench, at the cot, at the nose of the covered plane. Inspecting. Taking inventory of what seven years had made of him.

He thought about the way the old-timers at Kessuma had looked at him that afternoon, before she arrived. Not hostile. Worse. *Recognizing.* Like men reading a name off a list they'd memorized years ago and wondering when it would come up again.

He thought about the balloon payment. Eleven weeks. He'd carried it so long it had become less a number than an organ—something he lived around, the way you live around a bad tooth.

And he thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that Marguerite Voss had not come all this way, bought all that debt, and flown herself into the middle of nowhere to sit on his porch in the rain for salvage. Salvage could be hired. Whatever was at the headwaters, she believed it was worth more than money, and feared something enough to want the one pilot alive who'd sworn never to go back.

Dawn came gray and thin. He dressed, went down, and found her already outside, standing at the fence line with her hands in her pockets, looking north up the valley toward the range. The peaks were out—rare, this time of year, before the monsoon clouded everything—and they stood white and enormous against the brightening sky, beautiful and closed, the way they'd been in every dream he'd had for seven years.

"Cleared weather till noon," she said, without turning. "Good flying."

"You're assuming I said yes."

"You're standing next to me instead of locking your door." She glanced sideways at him. "In my experience, that's a yes with extra steps."

Wendell looked north a long moment. Somewhere up there was a rivergate he'd helped find and sworn to bury, a wreck he'd never visited, a friend whose last week nobody had ever explained to him. Somewhere up there was the answer to the question he'd been not-asking since the night the sky over the Salt Rivergate caught fire.

"One condition," he said.

Marguerite turned fully toward him, and he watched her brace—watched the boardroom come up like a shield.

"Name it."

"When we're done—whatever we find, whatever you're paying me for—you tell me the truth about that night. All of it. Even the parts that cost you."

Something flickered across her face too fast to read. Then she put out her hand, and her grip was dry and strong and final as a stamp on a contract.

"Deal," she said. "Wheels up at eight. Pack for cold."

She walked back toward the hangar to fetch her case, boots ringing on the boards, and Wendell stayed at the fence alone, looking north, doing the last piece of arithmetic—the one that had no numbers in it.

Behind him, inside the hangar, he heard her case click open, and heard, beneath it, a sound he almost missed: the soft, deliberate tear of an envelope.

By the time he turned, she was closing the case, smiling at the mountains, and whatever she'd just read—or reread—was already folded away somewhere behind her pale, level eyes.

Wendell went to pack.
