## 3. The Crew Assembles

They came at dawn in a chartered Twin Otter that touched down on my strip like it owned the deed to it, which, given the paperwork Voss was carrying, maybe it did.

I'd spent the night not sleeping, which is a skill I've refined. Seven years of practice. I lay in the bunk above my office and listened to the river doing its patient work below the bluff, and I made lists of reasons to say no. The list ran long. It ended every time at the same item: she knows how Elio died. I didn't know yet if that was true or bait, and I hated her for making me want to find out.

By first light I had the coffee going and the Otter's engines ticking as they cooled. Four people climbed out into my yard.

Voss first, of course. She'd changed out of yesterday's city clothes into field kit that probably cost more than my annual fuel budget and looked like she'd been wearing it all her life. Behind her came three strangers, and I did what I always do with new cargo: I weighed them.

You spend enough years loading aircraft by feel—judging whether a crate will balance before you put it on the scale—you start weighing everything. People especially. Every passenger is ballast, and ballast has a center of gravity, and most folks don't know where theirs sits until the turbulence finds it.

The tall man came first. Dark-skinned, silver at the temples, glasses that fogged instantly in the river air. He carried a hard case the way a priest carries scripture—close to the body, one hand never leaving it. Surveyor's build, or academic's. Soft hands under the field gloves. But his eyes went everywhere: the strip, the wind sock, the tree line, the hangar door hinges. Cataloguing. That's not a soft man's habit. That's a careful one.

The woman behind him was built like she'd been assembled from spare aircraft parts. Short, wide through the shoulders, forearms that could turn a stubborn prop nut by hand. She carried a toolbox in each fist and didn't set either down until she'd walked the full perimeter of my hangar, and when she finally did set them down, it was with the particular gentleness of someone who respects tools more than furniture. She looked at my old Heron sitting in the bay—the *Kestrel*, nineteen years of patches and prayers—and I watched her face do something complicated. Hunger, mostly. A mechanic looks at an old airframe the way a doctor looks at a patient everyone else has written off.

The third one I couldn't read, and that bothered me more than anything else about the morning.

He was medium in every dimension. Medium height, medium build, clothes chosen so you wouldn't remember them. He carried one duffel and no case, and he stood slightly outside the group—not apart, exactly, but offset, the way a man stands when he's used to watching doors. His eyes met mine once, level and unbothered, and then moved on to cataloguing exits. No fog on his lenses because he wasn't wearing any. No fidgeting. Hands quiet. In my experience, hands are where people leak their nerves, and this man's hands had nothing to say.

Voss introduced them on the walk up from the strip, names tossed over her shoulder like she was dealing cards.

"Adaeze Okafor. Surveyor. Best cartographer working the interior, and the only one who'll admit half the official maps are fiction." Okafor inclined her head a precise five degrees. "Delia Marsh. She kept a mining operation flying in the Cordillera for six years on parts she fabricated herself. Your aircraft is now the best-maintained machine in this hemisphere." Delia grunted, already drifting toward the hangar. "And Bram."

Just Bram. No surname offered, none volunteered. "Bram handles logistics. Permits, contacts, complications."

"Complications," I repeated.

"Every expedition has them," Voss said. "I prefer to bring my own rather than meet yours in the field."

* * *

We sat in my office, which had never held four people and barely held two. Voss took the chair; the rest stood or leaned. Okafor opened her hard case on my desk and began laying out maps, and I'll confess something: for about ninety seconds I forgot to be suspicious, because those were beautiful sheets.

Not government stock. Hand-drafted, linen-backed, inked in a draftsman's hand so fine it looked printed. Contour intervals tight as wood grain. And they covered country that the national survey shows as blank—white space above the last triangulation points, the kind of blankness that gets stamped *UNSURVEYED — PERMANENT ICE* and forgotten.

"You drew these," I said.

"I corrected them," Okafor said. Her voice was low and exact, each word placed like a foot on wet rock. "The official record for the upper Salt watershed was compiled in 1931 from a single traverse that turned back at the gorge. Everything since has been copies of copies. I've spent eleven years rebuilding it from ground observation." She tapped a contour cluster north of the white space. "Which means I know precisely how much of what your government publishes is wishful thinking."

"And what does that have to do with me?"

Voss answered before Okafor could. "Your routes. Seven years of medicine runs, mail runs, freight runs. You've flown more hours over this range than any pilot alive, including the ones who died learning it. You know things about that country that aren't on any map because you've never written them down." She smiled thinly. "Don't look surprised. I've read your flight logs. Or rather, I own the bank that holds the paper they're collateral against, and banks can read anything."

Delia, who'd come back in wiping grease off her hands, snorted. "Charming company you keep, Voss."

"I keep effective company. Charm is for people who can't afford results." Voss turned back to me. "Here's the shape of it. One charter. My crew, your aircraft, your skills. We go upriver to the headwaters country, we survey, we come back before the snows. You're paid enough to clear your debt twice over, and the Kestrel gets a full overhaul at Delia's hands before we leave—her choice of parts, my money."

"Why?" I asked. "Surveyors get hired by ministries. Mining companies. Why buy a bush pilot's debt to reach blank spots on a map?"

Okafor's pen stopped moving. Just for a second—a hitch in the scratch of ink, like a heartbeat skipping. Then it resumed.

"That's between you and Ms. Voss," she said quietly.

"It's between all of us if we're sharing an aircraft over glaciated terrain," I said. "I've buried friends who flew with crews that didn't ask enough questions."

Silence. Delia looked at the floor. Bram looked at nothing. Voss looked at me with something that might have been respect, or appetite.

"Fair," she said. "Partial answer now, full answer when we're committed past the point where you can quit gracefully. That's not cruelty, Ishikawa. It's sequencing. Some information changes hands better after a handshake than before."

"That's not sequencing. That's leverage."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "It is."

* * *

I walked the strip while they got settled, because walking is what I do instead of thinking, though it never works.

The morning was doing its usual performance—mist burning off the river, the far ridge coming up pink, swallows working the grass along the runway edges. Ordinary. I tried to hold onto ordinary the way you hold water.

Okafor found me at the wind sock. She came without her case, hands empty, which I noted. People approach you differently depending on what they're carrying.

"The 1931 traverse," she said. "You knew it turned back at the gorge."

"Everyone in river country knows that story. The surveyor wrote a book about it. Very dramatic. Mules drowned."

"Mm. Did you also know that his turning-back point doesn't match his own barometric readings? The numbers say he got at least forty kilometers further. There's a gap in the record—one night, one camp—that he never explains in the book." She adjusted her fogged glasses, gave up, took them off. Without them her eyes were older than the rest of her face. "I've spent eleven years wondering what a Victorian gentleman saw in one night that made him lie in print for the rest of his career."

"And you think it's still there to find."

"I think geography keeps secrets badly. Water goes where water must. Stone breaks along old lines. Whatever he saw, the land will show us again." She said it without romance, which somehow made it worse. "Ms. Voss believes the same thing, only she thinks in terms of assets. I think in terms of truth. We tolerate each other."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you asked questions and she deflected, and I wanted you to know that at least one person on this expedition answers honestly when asked directly." She put her glasses back on, and the surveyor returned, the woman retreating behind the glass. "Also because I read your file. Not the bank's version. The real one. You were on the Salt Rivergate expedition, Mr. Ishikawa. The one that isn't in anyone's records either."

The wind sock snapped overhead. Somewhere downriver a boat engine started, small and domestic, and I envied whoever was driving it.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said.

"No," she said. "Of course not." And she left me alone with the swallows and the lie.

* * *

Delia had the Kestrel's cowling off by midmorning. I stood in the hangar doorway watching her work, because there are few honest pleasures left to me and one of them is watching someone competent love a machine.

She talked while she worked, which mechanics do, filling the silence with diagnosis.

"When'd you last pull the number-three cylinder?"

"Forty hours ago."

"When'd you actually pull it, versus log it?"

I laughed despite myself. "There's a difference?"

"There's always a difference." She wiped her hands and patted the wing root like a horse's neck. "She's tired but she's straight. Nobody's butchered her. Whoever patched that spar section knew what they were doing—field repair, no shop, done under pressure. Yours?"

"A friend's."

Something in my voice closed the subject, and to her credit she let it stay closed. She just nodded and went back in with her borescope, and after a while she said, conversationally, "Whoever's paying for this overhaul, take the money. Take double. Because whatever we're flying into, these wings are going to need to forgive us, and right now they'd forgive us once and bill us for it."

"You've been told what we're flying into?"

"I've been told nothing. That's how I know it's bad. Good jobs come with briefings." She slid out on her creeper, upside down under the fuselage, and her voice floated up, muffled. "Six years in the Cordillera taught me one rule, Ishikawa. When the money gets vague, the destination gets ugly."

* * *

Bram I found at dusk, and finding him was itself informative, because I hadn't seen him since the introductions. He was at the edge of the strip, near the fuel drums, doing nothing visible. Standing. Watching the ridgeline the way a man watches weather.

"Bram," I said.

"Wendell Ishikawa." He said my name correctly, no hesitation, which meant he'd studied it. Most people stumble on the syllables or flatten them into something comfortable. He hadn't. "Nice strip. Good sightlines."

"Sightlines."

"For landing." A pause, exactly long enough to let me decide whether he'd meant it. "Voss says wheels up day after tomorrow, weather permitting."

"She says a lot of things."

"She pays for them too." He turned to look at me fully for the first time. His face was the kind you forget while looking at it—unremarkable in the specific way of men who've practiced being unremarkable. "You want to ask me something. Everyone does eventually. So: I'm not a soldier, I'm not a spy, and I'm not going to tell you what I am, because the answer would make you more nervous, not less. What I am is useful. Ask anybody I've worked for."

"Anybody I'd know?"

"Nobody you'd want to." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to honesty I'd get from him, and I suspect he knew it. "One thing, though. Free of charge." He nodded toward the office window, where lamplight showed Voss bent over Okafor's maps. "Whatever she told you about why she needs you—whatever story, whatever leverage—it's true that she needs you. That part's real. I've watched her negotiate for a week now, and she doesn't spend money on things she can take. She bought your debt because she couldn't take you any other way."

"And that's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to inform you." He picked up his duffel, weightless-looking, everything he owned in one bag. "Comfort's not in my line."

* * *

That night I sat at my desk with the ledger open—the debt ledger, columns of red I've memorized down to the decimal—and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Four strangers. One liar with money, one honest woman with a secret, one mechanic with good instincts, and one man whose whole self fit in a duffel bag and whose eyes counted my exits. Destination: the one place on earth I'd sworn off. Timeline: ahead of the snows, which meant fast, which meant heavy loads, thin margins, weather I could predict and weather I couldn't.

Trust as a currency: I checked my reserves. Nearly empty. Seven years of spending it nowhere, hoarding nothing, and the account had gone to dust. I had skill—that was still minted, still legal tender. I had the country in my bones. What I didn't have was a single soul on this expedition whose interest I could verify matched mine.

Except maybe Okafor, and hers matched mine only as far as the truth, and the truth and survival are frequently different destinations.

I closed the ledger. Outside, the river kept on with its patient work, hauling the mountains toward the sea one grain at a time, asking nothing, owning nothing, belonging to everybody and nobody downstream.

I thought: that's the job description I wanted. Small cargo. Clean ledger. Invisible.

Then I thought about Elio, and about whatever Voss knew, and about the gap in a dead surveyor's record—one night, one camp—and I lay down in the dark and listened to the water, and sleep came late and shallow, and when it finally took me, it took me downriver, toward the headwaters, against the current, the way dreams go when the past has bought your debt.

Day after tomorrow, wheels up. Between now and then, I had fuel to order, lies to sort from weather, and one question I hadn't yet found the nerve to ask Marguerite Voss:

What exactly did she mean when she said she knew how Elio died?

Because there were only two people alive who'd been on that mountain, and I was one of them—and I had never told anyone the whole truth of that night. Not the inquiry board. Not the newspapers. Not myself, some nights.

So either she was guessing.

Or somebody had talked.

And either way, I needed to know which—before I strapped four strangers' lives to my wings and flew them into the throat of the range.
