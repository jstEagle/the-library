## 4. Fuel, Lies, and Weather

The manifest Voss handed me was a lie on good paper.

I read it twice in the hangar light while her crew loaded drums behind me and the smell of avgas cut through the cold. Medical supplies, survey equipment, mail contracts—everything itemized for a run to Fort Calloway and the mining camps beyond. Clean. Legal. The kind of paperwork a man could show a customs officer without his hands sweating.

"Fort Calloway," I said.

"That's what it says."

"You wrote it."

"I did." She watched Delia cinch a strap over the second fuel drum with the flat attention of a woman checking someone else's arithmetic. "Paperwork is a courtesy, Wendell. It tells people what you want them to know."

I set the manifest down on the wing strut, careful, the way I set down everything when my hands wanted to do something else. "Then tell me what I'm supposed to know."

Voss smiled. She had a smile like a customs stamp—official, impersonal, applied where it hurt. "You'll know by tomorrow."

* * *

We spent the day loading, and loading told me more than she would.

Seven drums of fuel. Not five, which is what Fort Calloway and back needed with margin. Seven meant range past the mining camps, into country where there were no strips worth the name unless you counted gravel bars and old logging cuts. Delia worked the tie-downs like she'd been born in a hangar, testing each strap twice, and Okafor stood at the open cargo door with a leather case chained to his wrist—I noticed that, the chain—and watched the mountains instead of the work.

Bram I didn't see until midday. He came out of nowhere the way quiet men do, carrying a crate marked SPARE PARTS that he set down soft as an egg. I've hauled enough crates to know that spare parts don't get packed in foam. Spare parts rattle.

I said nothing. Seven years of flying alone teaches you that most of what you notice is better kept in the logbook behind your eyes.

By late afternoon the Skywagon sat heavy on its gear, nose low, every pound accounted for except the ones nobody had itemized. I walked the plane one last time, palm on the cowling, feeling for anything wrong the way you'd check a horse's leg. She was fine. She was always fine. That was the one clean thing left in my life, and tomorrow somebody was going to use her for something, and I still didn't know what.

Voss found me at the fuel bowser.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we fly north-northwest from Calloway. Two hundred forty miles up the Salt corridor."

The wrench in my hand stopped being a tool and became something I was holding onto.

"The Salt corridor is closed."

"It's closed on maps."

"It's closed because the last expedition that flew it put four men into a canyon." My voice came out level, which surprised me. Underneath the level there was a sound like ice cracking on a lake, far off, getting closer. "Three bodies recovered. One never found. The official report says weather. I was the pilot who filed it."

"I've read the report."

"Then you know better than to ask."

"I know," Voss said, "that reports are written by survivors." She stepped closer, close enough that I could smell jet fuel and some expensive soap underneath it, and lowered her voice though there was no one within fifty meters but Delia, who was very carefully not listening. "The destination is the headwaters, Wendell. The Salt Rivergate. And before you say the word impossible, understand that I bought your debt knowing exactly what I was buying. I didn't purchase a pilot. I purchased the only man alive who has flown that corridor twice and lived."

Once, I almost said. Once alive, once not.

What I said was: "No."

* * *

I got as far as the fence.

That sounds like nothing. It wasn't nothing. Walking away from a contract with Marguerite Voss means walking away from the debt she holds, means the bank calls in the note on the Skywagon by Friday, means seven years of small cargo and small kindnesses end with a repossession agent and a phone call I'd have to make to the clinic at Two Rivers explaining why the vaccines stop coming. I knew all that while I walked. I walked anyway, because there are things a man has to do with his legs even when his head has already lost the argument.

The evening fog was coming off the river in ropes. At the fence I stopped and put both hands on the wire and looked north, where the range was doing what it always does at dusk—turning from rock into rumor, the peaks going blue-black, the canyons between them filling up with shadow like ink poured into water.

Somewhere up there was a canyon with no name on the map anymore. They'd taken the name off after the crash, which is a thing governments do instead of grieving. Elio had loved that canyon. He'd stood next to me on final approach two days before he died and said, *Wendy, look at it, the river just disappears into the rock like the earth swallowed it whole,* and he'd laughed, because Elio laughed at everything, especially the things that should have frightened him.

Especially me. He laughed at how careful I was. *You fly like a man paying off a mortgage,* he said once, delighted, and I said, *somebody has to land this thing tomorrow too,* and he said, *no, Wendy, that's the beautiful part—nobody has to. We get to.*

We get to. That was Elio's whole philosophy in three words. I have spent seven years trying to forget them and have failed completely.

"Wendell."

Voss had followed me. Of course she had. She stood a few feet back with her hands in her coat pockets and the fog beading on her collar, and she let the silence sit between us long enough that I turned around first. She'd counted on that too.

"No," I said again.

"You haven't heard the price of yes yet."

"There isn't one. Whatever it is, I've already paid worse."

"Have you?" Something moved behind her face—not cruelty, exactly. Precision. A surgeon finding the spot. "Do you actually know how Elio Marchetti died? Not what the report says. What happened."

The fog was cold on the back of my neck. The river talked to itself below the bank, patient, endless.

"Weather," I said. "Downdraft off the ridge line pushed him into terrain he couldn't see. I reconstructed the flight path from the wreckage. I did it myself. I spent four months—"

"You reconstructed it from wreckage and guilt," Voss said, "which are different materials. The wreckage tells you where the plane hit. It doesn't tell you why he was there at all, in that canyon, at that hour, off the filed route, with a full load of fuel he shouldn't have taken on." She paused. "Doesn't that ever keep you up at night? The fuel?"

It did. Every night for about two years, and then less often, and then never out loud. Elio had topped off against my advice. I'd signed the fuel order anyway because he was Elio and signing things for Elio was half my job. A full tank meant he planned to go somewhere far, or stay somewhere long, or both. A man sightseeing doesn't fill his wings to the caps.

"He was surveying," I said. "He went wide to photograph the gorge."

"At dusk. In building weather. With no camera aboard—the recovery team inventoried the aircraft, Wendell. There was no camera. There was no survey equipment. There was a duffel bag with forty pounds of something dense in the baggage compartment, and the manifest for that flight listed the compartment as empty."

I hadn't known that. I had read the inventory a hundred times and I had not known that, or I had known it and buried it so deep that learning it again felt like hearing it fresh, and either way the ground under my feet did something unreliable.

"What was in the bag?"

"Now that," Voss said, "is the question. Because whatever it was, he took it out of the gate site the night before he died, and he didn't file a flight plan to any survey grid. He filed a plan to a village called Senngat—a place so small it isn't on your charts—and then he never arrived anywhere at all." She watched me the way Okafor watched mountains. "Elio wasn't mapping the Salt Rivergate, Wendell. He was stealing from it. Or protecting it. I've spent six years and a great deal of money trying to determine which, and everyone who might have told me is dead or hiding, and here you are at the end of my list, standing at a fence, looking north."

"You're lying," I said. "Or guessing."

"I'm neither. I have the tower logs from that night. I have the fuel chit with your signature on it." She let that land—she'd earned the right to let it land; I felt it go in under the ribs like a splinter of ice. "And I have a page of Elio's handwriting, obtained from a source I won't name, describing something he saw inside the gate works that made him afraid of the people who funded our expedition. Our, Wendell. Yours and his and mine. I signed the financing on the original survey. You've forgotten that. I haven't."

I had forgotten it. Or I'd folded it up small and put it away with everything else from that year. Voss had been the money. Voss had been the voice on the satellite phone, crisp and impatient, asking when the data would flow. And when the data stopped flowing and the planes started burning, Voss had vanished into her holdings like water into sand, and I had never once wondered where she'd gone, because wondering required looking, and I had sworn off looking.

"Elio was afraid," I said slowly.

"Elio was terrified. His own words. He wrote that if anything happened to him, the material had to reach—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, a woman catching a door before it swung too far open. "That's ahead of ourselves. The point is this: I am going to the headwaters to find what Elio found, and I need a pilot who can fly the corridor, and you need your airplane and your life back. Those facts fit together whether you like their shape or not."

"And if I say no again?"

"Then I charter someone else, they'll likely die, the buyer I'm racing will send worse people, and you'll spend the rest of your life at this fence wondering what was in the bag." She turned to go, then didn't. "One more thing. The night Elio died, the tower logged a second aircraft holding at the edge of the corridor. Unidentified. It stayed on station for forty minutes after his last transmission and then departed south. The report makes no mention of it. I've seen the raw tape."

The fog had come all the way up the bank now. The range was gone entirely, and I was looking at gray where mountains should be, which felt accurate.

"Forty minutes," I said.

"Someone was watching him go down, Wendell. Someone with an aircraft and a reason. So when you reconstruct that night—and I know you do, men like you always do—reconstruct it with company."

She walked back toward the hangar lights, and I stayed at the fence a long time, and the river kept saying the same thing over and over the way rivers do, which is: *keep going, keep going, keep going,* and for seven years I had mistaken it for comfort.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the hangar office with the bad coffee and the good logbooks, and I pulled the one I swore I'd retired—the summer of the expedition, dog-eared, coffee-ringed, Elio's initials in the margin of every other entry because he used to annotate my flight plans like a schoolmaster grading homework. *Wendy: watch the shear line past the saddle, it bites after 1400.* *Wendy: you compute reserves like a miser counts coins, bless you.*

On the page dated two days before the end, in my own handwriting: fuel order, full tanks, co-pilot E. Marchetti, purpose: survey photography, sector nine. Sector nine was the gorge. But the tower tapes—if Voss was telling the truth, and the terrible thing was how much I wanted her to be telling the truth—said he'd filed onward to Senngat. A village. Forty pounds of dense something. No camera.

A man doesn't fly to a village with forty pounds of mystery unless he's delivering it or taking it away.

I closed the book. Delia was standing in the doorway with a flashlight held low, polite about the light.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said.

"Something like that."

She came in and leaned against the filing cabinet and studied me with the frankness of mechanics, who spend their lives looking for cracks. "You know I've done jobs for Voss before. Cargo runs, mostly honest. She pays on time and she doesn't ask you to carry anything that breathes." A pause. "This one's different. Okafor's maps aren't mining surveys. Bram's crates aren't parts. And you—" she nodded at the logbook under my hand, "—look like a man reading his own autopsy."

"Is there a question in there?"

"Not yet." She straightened. "Just thought you should know the crew's noticed too. Whatever we're flying into, we're flying into it with our eyes at least half open. Figured that mattered to you."

It did matter. It mattered more than she could know, and I told her so in the fewest words possible, and she took the hint and left, and I sat alone with the coffee going cold and the fog pressing at the windows.

At some hour that had no name, I made the calculation I'd been avoiding. Voss had the tower logs, the fuel chit, a page of Elio's hand. Whether she was honest or monstrous—and those were not mutually exclusive—she was the only door open onto that night. Behind every other door was the same gray room I'd lived in for seven years. And somewhere north, in a canyon with no name, there was a duffel bag's worth of answer, and possibly a second aircraft's worth of witness, and Elio's last transmission had been cut off mid-word, and I had spent seven years pretending the word didn't matter.

It mattered. It had always mattered. *Mayday, mayday, Wendy, they're—*

They're. They're what?

I got up, refilled the coffee, and wrote the flight plan for the morning. North-northwest from Fort Calloway, two hundred forty miles up the Salt corridor, into the throat of the thing that ate my best friend, carrying seven drums of fuel, three liars, one surveyor with a chained case, and whatever was in Bram's foam-packed crates.

My hand was steady. That was the worst part. Rage and grief had finished their argument somewhere around midnight and reached the only conclusion they ever reach, which is each other, and what they make together is a kind of calm that has nothing to do with peace.

First light came up gray over the range. I heard Voss's car on the gravel and went out to meet the morning, and the first thing she said, before hello, before good morning, was:

"Change of plans. The monsoon's tracking early—we fly today, and we fly the corridor direct. Calloway's off the route."

Off the route. Which meant the manifest was already dead, which meant the lies had started shedding layers before the wheels were even wet, and I stood there in the cold with the flight plan in my hand and understood that whatever I'd agreed to in the dark, the real job had been waiting for me all along, patient as weather.

"Then we'd better preflight," I said.
