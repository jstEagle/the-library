## 5. Last Town Before the Range

Kettle Falls wasn't on any map worth trusting. It existed the way weather existed—something you planned around, not something you could predict. A gravel strip bent along the river like a man leaning into wind, a fuel cache in a Quonset hut that smelled of decades, and a town of maybe two hundred souls strung along the bank in houses built to survive everything except boredom.

Wendell had been coming here for seven years. He knew which boards on the fuel dock were rotten, which of the two hand pumps actually worked, and that if you wanted coffee before nine you went to Marta's and poured it yourself because Marta believed service was a form of weakness.

He also knew, as they came down through the last layer of cloud with the range filling the northern horizon like a wall someone had forgotten to finish building, that Kettle Falls remembered him.

Not the way the river villages remembered him—gratefully, simply, by the name he'd given them. Kettle Falls remembered him from *before*. From the other name. From the expedition.

"Strip looks good," Delia said beside him, already running her checklist though they were still five hundred feet up. She'd been doing that all morning, touching things, confirming things. He'd decided to read it as professional thoroughness rather than nerves. "Wind's quartering off the ridge."

"I see it." He flared the Heron over the tree line, felt the crosswind shove at the tail, corrected with more rudder than the maneuver strictly required, and set down smooth enough that Okafor, in back, didn't stop writing. That was the standard now. If Okafor didn't look up, the landing hadn't happened.

Bram had looked up. Bram always looked up. He was watching the tree line when they rolled out, watching the strip's far end, watching the way a man watches when he's being paid to watch.

Voss sat in the cockpit doorway, uninvited, arms crossed. "You've landed here before."

"A few times."

"You flew mail runs through here for years and you're telling me nobody knows you?"

"Nobody knows me," Wendell said, "is the entire point of my career."

But even as he said it, he saw old Sefu Okonkwo standing at the edge of the strip with his hands in his coat pockets, not waving, just standing, the way men stand when they've been waiting since dawn and won't admit it. Sefu had run the fuel cache for thirty years. Sefu had refueled the expedition's aircraft in another life, in the summer the Salt Rivergate made headlines and then made graves.

Sefu looked at Wendell the way you look at a man you buried.

* * *

The Quonset hut was cool inside, dim, organized with the fierce precision of someone who'd been left alone too long. Sefu worked the pump handle while Wendell signed the fuel log, and neither of them spoke until the tank gauge climbed past half.

"You look older," Sefu said.

"Flying does that."

"Not flying." Sefu's eyes came up, brown and unhurried. "Guilt does that. Flying just adds gray."

Wendell kept his pen moving. The signature came out steady, which surprised him. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Sefu agreed. "You wouldn't. That's how it works." He shut the valve, wiped his hands on a rag that had outlived several governments. "Your friend's mother came through here. Four years ago. Maybe five."

The pen stopped.

"She was looking for the valley where they say he walked out. Before the fire. She'd heard a story from a trader who heard it from a boatman." Sefu folded the rag with great care, corner to corner. "I told her I couldn't help her. Because I couldn't. But she stood where you're standing and asked me if I knew anyone who'd been on the expedition, and I said no, and we both let it be true."

"Sefu—"

"I'm not asking you anything." He held up one palm. "I'm telling you that this town has a long memory and short patience, and you've spent seven years trading on both. Now you come back with a woman whose boots cost more than my roof, and four strangers, and a surveyor drawing maps of country that isn't hers to draw." He nodded toward the strip, where Voss stood talking to Okafor while Delia circled the Heron on her knees, checking the gear. "And somebody else has noticed you too."

"What do you mean?"

"Man came in yesterday. Chartered flight out of the coast, hired pilot, dropped him and left. Said he was a birdwatcher." Sefu smiled without any humor in it. "Birdwatcher with a camera worth more than your airplane. Been walking the perimeter since first light. Took pictures of your tail number twice. Once from up close, once from across the river with a lens long enough to count your rivets."

Wendell turned toward the window. At the far end of the strip, near the tree line, a figure in a green jacket stood with a camera raised. Even at this distance there was something wrong about the posture—not the loose stance of a hobbyist waiting for a heron, but the planted, patient stillness of a man doing a job.

"He photograph anyone else?" Wendell asked.

"Photographed the strip. Photographed the fuel hut. Photographed me, which I did not care for." Sefu hung the rag on its nail. "Then he bought coffee at Marta's and asked her, very friendly, how often planes come through heading north. She told him every day, all day, whatever he wanted to hear. Marta lies beautifully when she doesn't like someone. You should hear her describe the fishing."

"Why are you telling me this?"

Sefu looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the light shifted as cloud moved over the ridge, and the whole town seemed to dim and recover.

"Because seven years ago I fueled an aircraft full of young men who thought they were going to find treasure," he said, "and I watched them come back as cargo, and I have never once been able to forget the sound the crates made when we unloaded them. And because you were the one who carried the last of them out, alone, in a plane with half its instruments shot, and you landed here at midnight and slept on my floor and never told me what happened up there, and I never asked." He picked up the fuel log, closed it. "So we're even, you and me. But whoever that man is working for—he isn't even with anybody yet. And that makes him dangerous in a way you should understand better than most."

* * *

Marta's was four tables, a counter, and a wood stove that had been burning continuously since before the war that ended the last war. The crew took two tables and the room rearranged itself around them the way small rooms do—conversation dropping, eyes lifting, the particular silence of a place deciding whether you belong.

Okafor spread his maps between the salt and the sugar and started annotating. Delia ate like a woman refueling machinery, efficient and total. Bram sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, and drank tea he didn't touch.

Voss leaned across to Wendell and lowered her voice. "The old man at the fuel hut. He knows you."

"Everyone here knows everyone. It's a small town."

"He looked at you like he was reading an obituary."

"Marta!" Wendell called, grateful for the interruption. "Five coffees and whatever's hot."

"There's stew," Marta said from the stove, not turning around. "There's always stew. It's been stew since Tuesday and it'll be stew until it's gone." She ladled five bowls, brought them over, set them down hard enough to make a point, and then—this was the part Wendell braced for—she stayed. She put her hands flat on the table and looked at him with the frank appraisal of a woman who had watched this valley eat people for forty years and considered herself its bookkeeper.

"Wendell Ishikawa," she said.

"Just Wendell here."

"That's what you think." She glanced at Voss, at the maps, back. "You flying north?"

"Passing through."

"To the headwaters." It wasn't a question. News traveled upriver faster than boats; it always had. "My cousin's boy guides hunters up the Salt fork. Says there's been strangers in the high country all month. Quiet ones. Buying supplies, paying cash, asking about the old expedition route like it's a hiking trail." She straightened. "You know what's funny? Seven years, you come through here, you drink my coffee, you fix my neighbor's generator without being asked, you never once talk about that summer. Whole town's respected that. Whole town's decided you get to be whoever you say you are." Her eyes went to Voss. "Then she walks in with paperwork and a crew and a destination, and suddenly everybody remembers you're the one who *came back*."

"Marta," Wendell said quietly.

"Eat your stew." She pushed the bowl an inch toward him. "It's better than it sounds. Everything is."

She left. The table was silent. Delia looked at Wendell with an expression he couldn't file—curiosity, maybe, or the beginning of a question she was polite enough to hold. Bram ate his stew without appearing to notice any of it, which was its own kind of performance.

Voss broke the silence. "She's right, you know. About the strangers in the high country."

"You knew that already."

"I suspected. Now it's confirmed by a woman who lies beautifully, apparently." Voss pulled the top map toward her. "Someone's staging ahead of us. Which means someone knows our timeline, which means either you talked, or I have a leak, or the buyer's network is better than I estimated." She tapped the map. "We stage tonight, cross the first pass at first light, beat whatever's ahead of us to the corridor."

"And the birdwatcher?"

"Bram's handling it."

Everyone looked at Bram. Bram looked mildly back, spoon resting in his bowl. "I'm going to have a conversation," he said. "Conversations are underrated. Nobody ever shoots anybody during a conversation."

"That's not true," Okafor said, not looking up from his maps.

"It's rare," Bram conceded.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like water finding its level. Wendell walked the strip alone, as he always did in strange towns—a habit from the early years, checking tie-downs, chocks, the Heron's fabric skin for the small wounds weather inflicts. The light went amber, then copper, then gone. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing with great conviction.

The birdwatcher was gone from the tree line. Had been since mid-afternoon. Bram had gone to have his conversation and returned ninety minutes later with dirt on his knees, a scratch on his jaw, and no explanation beyond "we reached an understanding." When Wendell pressed, Bram only said: "He won't be photographing anything tonight. Whether tomorrow's a different story depends on who he reports to, and I couldn't make him tell me that. Which bothers me. Men like that always know exactly who they report to. Unless they've recently stopped knowing."

Wendell crouched at the Heron's tail wheel and checked the cotter pin by feel. Behind him, footsteps on gravel—Delia, hands in pockets, breath fogging.

"Fuel line's good," she said. "I checked your work. No offense."

"Some taken. Accurately."

She crouched beside him. For a while they just listened to the dog.

"The old lady at the restaurant," Delia said finally. "And the fuel guy. They weren't looking at you like a stranger, Wendell. They were looking at you like—" She stopped, restarted. "Like a letter that got returned to sender seven years ago and just showed up."

"They're old. Old people look at everyone like that."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make yourself small so nobody asks questions." She said it without heat, almost gently, which made it worse. "I've watched you do it since the coast. You answer direct questions with directions. Somebody says your name like they *know* it and you go find a task. I fix engines for a living. I know what it looks like when a machine's running fine because the operator won't let it idle long enough to make noise." She stood. "Anyway. Not my business. Just—if we're flying into whatever you're flying into, I'd rather know the shape of it than be surprised by it."

She walked back toward the town lights. Wendell stayed crouched at the tail wheel a moment longer, the cold coming up through the gravel, and admitted to himself that she was right, and hated how easily she'd seen it, and hated more that some traitor part of him wanted to tell her everything.

* * *

He found Sefu locking up the fuel hut, lantern in hand, and fell into step beside him without asking. Sefu didn't object. They walked the length of the strip in silence, and at the far end, where the gravel gave way to grass and the grass gave way to the dark shoulder of the world, Sefu stopped and looked north at the range. The peaks caught the last starlight and held it.

"My grandfather paddled freight up the Salt fork," Sefu said. "Before the airstrip, before the town had a name. He used to say the headwaters weren't a place, they were a *decision*—that the mountain decides every year how much water to give the lowlands, and everything downstream lives or dies by that decision. Rice, fish, the flood plains, all of it." He turned. "When those young men came through seven years ago, they talked about the gate like it was a vault. Something to open. Something to own. My grandfather would have told them they had it backwards."

"Backwards how?"

"The gate isn't locked so treasure can't get out." Sefu lifted the lantern; the light moved across his face and settled. "It's locked so the wrong hands can't reach the lever. There's a difference, and it's the size of the ocean." He started walking back. "You knew that once. I watched you know it, that night on my floor, when you couldn't sleep and kept saying one thing over and over."

Wendell's mouth went dry. "What did I say?"

"'It was supposed to stay closed.'" Sefu didn't look at him. "You said it like a man reciting a debt. So whatever she's paying you, whatever she's holding over you—it isn't money, is it. It's that night. And I'd bet this whole town you're going north to settle it, one way or the other." He paused at the hut door. "Get some sleep, Wendell. First light comes early, and the mountain's already deciding."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the Heron's cockpit with a flashlight shielded under his coat, reading the weight-and-balance sheet he didn't need to read, and at some hour past midnight he heard footsteps on the gravel again—light, deliberate—and looked up to see the birdwatcher standing ten meters off the wing, camera hanging unused at his side, hands visible, empty.

"Mr. Ishikawa," the man said. His accent was coastal, educated, worn thin. "I was hoping you'd still be awake."

"You have until I decide to shout."

"Fair." The man didn't come closer. "I'm not going to waste your time pretending to be anything. I was hired to document your aircraft and your route. That's all. Photographs, position reports, nothing else—I want to be very clear that sabotage was never in my contract, because I understand how this looks."

"Who hired you?"

"A brokerage firm. Coastal address. Three layers of shell companies deep—that's not unusual in my line." The man shifted his weight. "Here's why I'm breaking character, Mr. Ishikawa. This afternoon I received amended instructions. New payment schedule. And a new deliverable." He reached slowly into his jacket—Wendell's hand found the flashlight, the only weapon aboard—and produced instead a folded paper, which he set on the gravel and stepped back from. "They're not paying me to track you anymore. They're paying me to confirm arrival. Yours *and* the woman's. And the amendment includes a clause I've never seen in seven years of this work." He backed toward the dark. "A bonus, payable on confirmation, for ensuring there are no witnesses to the confirmation."

The paper lay pale on the gravel between them. The man melted into the tree line without hurrying, like a man leaving a bar, and Wendell sat alone in the cold cockpit with the flashlight dying in his fist and the words arranging themselves into the shape he'd been dreading since the coast:

Somebody upstream didn't want the gate found.

Somebody wanted the finders buried with it.

In the morning, at first light, they'd fly into the throat of the range—and somewhere ahead, quiet men were already buying supplies along the old expedition route, cash only, asking after the trail like hikers.

Wendell got out of the plane and went to wake the others.
