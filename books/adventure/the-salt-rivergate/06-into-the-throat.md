## 6. Into the Throat

The weather came down off the range like something with intent.

Wendell saw it from forty miles out—a gray wall stacked over the eastern peaks, boiling slowly, the kind of build-up that looked lazy and wasn't. He'd flown into enough of them to know the difference between a storm that wanted to kill you and a storm that merely would, if you gave it the chance. This one was patient. That made it worse.

"Is that normal?" Marguerite Voss stood behind his right shoulder, one hand on the back of his seat. She'd been there for an hour, watching him fly the way people watch a card sharp—looking for the trick.

"It's monsoon season arriving early," Wendell said. "Or it's a squall line that will spend itself by dark. Either way we cross before it decides for us."

"And if it decides now?"

"Then we land in a meadow and wait, and you get to explain to your schedule why the mountains won."

He felt her smile more than saw it. She had that quality—the ability to be amused at exactly the moments that should have infuriated her. Seven years ago he'd have called it charm. Now he filed it under hazard.

The Cessna Caravan bucked through the first gusts as they crossed the last river valley. Behind him, cargo shifted on its straps: fuel drums, Okafor's survey crates, Delia's tool chest, three weeks of rations packed by someone who clearly believed they were provisioning a siege. Bram sat in the jump seat with his eyes closed, which Wendell had learned meant he was listening—to the engines, to the airframe, to everything.

Delia was up front in the copilot's seat she'd claimed on day one, headset half-off one ear, hands busy with nothing. "She's running hot on number two," she said. "Cylinder head temps. Nothing scary."

"Nothing scary yet."

"That's what I said. Yet." She grinned at him. Delia grinned the way other people breathed; it cost her nothing and told you nothing. But her hands were good. He'd watched her re-torque the prop governor at dawn without being asked, and he'd decided then that whatever else she was carrying into these mountains, it wasn't incompetence.

The valley floor fell away beneath them. The river that had walked beside them all morning turned narrow, then braided, then vanished into a gorge so deep the water in it was rumor. Ahead, the first wall of the range rose out of its own foothills like a ship's bow coming up out of black water.

Wendell set his jaw and did the thing he always did at this point: he stopped thinking about where he was going and started thinking about only the next ninety seconds. Ninety seconds at a time. It was how he'd survived seven years of small flights and small cargoes, and it was how he intended to survive this.

Except the route didn't allow it. The route was Voss's route, plotted by Okafor off maps predating the official record, and it threaded the northern passes—the passes that ran close. Too close. Close enough that somewhere off his left wingtip, hidden behind a shoulder of granite, lay a corridor of broken country he had spent seven years routing around, paying in fuel and hours, never once crossing.

The crash corridor. Where Elio's plane had come apart against a ridge in weather much like this.

"You're quiet," Voss said.

"I'm flying."

"You're flying *and* quiet. There's a difference. I've watched you talk your way through turbulence for two days like a man narrating his own radio show."

He adjusted the mixture. "There's weather ahead. I'm conserving words."

"Liar," Delia said pleasantly, not looking up from her gauges.

* * *

They climbed through the first pass in cloud.

It was instrument work, pure and blind—no horizon, no ground reference, just the artificial horizon's little painted wings and Wendell's hands keeping them level while the aircraft porpoised through rising air. Turbulence hit them in slabs. Loose cargo groaned against its straps. In the back, Okafor said something clipped and calm to Bram, and Bram answered in a voice too low to catch, and Wendell noted that even now, being shaken like dice in a cup, neither of them sounded afraid. He didn't know yet what to make of that.

"Deviation one-eight-zero for ten miles puts us in clearer air," Delia offered.

"Deviating south adds forty minutes and puts us over the Kesh valley in this wind. We stay on course."

"On course is north," she said. "On course runs near—" She stopped herself. She knew. Of course she knew; Voss would have briefed them. Everyone on this airplane probably knew about Elio Marchetti and the Salt Rivergate expedition and the pilot who'd walked away from the wreckage of his own name. Everyone except, possibly, Wendell himself, who sometimes suspected he knew least of anyone.

"I know where on course runs," he said.

Delia put her headset back on properly and said nothing else. Which was its own kind of courtesy.

The pass opened around them like a throat indeed—gray rock walls streaked white with old avalanche chutes, cloud shredding through the col at their altitude, snow flurries ticking against the windshield even though it was high summer below. Wendell flew the center of the gap by memory of the map and instinct refined over hundreds of mountain crossings, feeling for the smoothest air the way a swimmer feels for current. There was a rhythm to it. There was always a rhythm. Find the lift, ride the lift, never fight the air that's trying to help you, respect the air that isn't.

His hands knew this work the way his hands knew nothing else anymore. That was the strange comfort of competence: no matter what else had burned down in his life, the airplane still responded to him honestly. It did not care about his debt or his dead friend or the name he'd buried. It cared about angle of attack and airspeed and whether he respected it. Everything else in his life lied. The airplane never had.

They cleared the col by four hundred feet, which was three hundred more than he'd promised himself, and dropped into the long northern valley beyond.

And there it was. Off the left wing. The corridor.

* * *

From this altitude it looked almost gentle—a wide glacial trough running east toward the heart of the range, floored with scrub and scree and the silver threads of meltwater streams. Beautiful country. Killing country. The walls funneled wind from the interior like a blowgun, and the weather that built over the high peaks spilled into it and stayed, swirling, until it either exhausted itself or exhausted whatever flew into it.

Seven years ago, a plane very much like this one had gone in there with six souls aboard and come apart against the north wall in conditions the inquiry report described, in its bloodless way, as *convective activity exceeding the aircraft's performance envelope.*

Wendell had read that sentence maybe four thousand times. He could recite it. He could also recite the alternative version, the one that lived in him at three a.m.: *Elio pushed it. Elio always pushed it. And I wasn't there to pull the yoke back.*

Because that was the thing nobody said out loud, the thing the inquiry never touched because it wasn't in any logbook: Wendell was supposed to be on that flight. He'd traded legs with Elio at the last minute—Elio had wanted the southern villages, the longer day, the bigger load of goodwill, and Wendell had wanted his bunk and a bottle and had said yes too fast. So Elio flew the corridor run alone, in building weather, with a cargo hold full of expedition equipment that officially didn't exist, and the mountains closed their hand.

If Wendell had been in the right seat, he'd have called it off. He knew that the way he knew his own heartbeat. He'd have seen the build-up over the peaks and said *not today* and Elio would have grumbled and landed and bought the drinks and lived. One decision. One ordinary cowardly comfortable human decision, and a man would still be alive who sang badly and laughed easily and believed—this was the part that had hollowed Wendell out for years—that believed the discovery they'd made together should belong to everyone downstream.

Instead Wendell had slept. And the corridor had kept the secret, along with Elio.

"Turn five degrees left," Voss said quietly beside him.

He blinked. He'd drifted. Three degrees off course, drifting left, drifting toward the trough mouth like iron filings toward a magnet. His body had done it without asking him. Muscle memory of fear, running the wrong direction.

"Five degrees left," he repeated, and corrected, and the corridor slid aft off the wingtip, and he realized his shirt was wet at the small of his back.

Voss was looking at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere. Not triumph. Not pity. Something almost gentle, which from Marguerite Voss was more frightening than either.

"You've never crossed it," she said. Not a question.

"No."

"In seven years."

"The long way costs fuel. Fuel costs money. I've been short on both."

"Wendell." She said his name like she was setting something down on a table between them. "We're going to have to cross it eventually. You know that. The headwaters are past it. Everything is past it."

"I know where the headwaters are."

"Then say it plainly. Say you won't fly over where he died."

The cabin was very quiet. Even the turbulence seemed to lean in. Delia had gone statue-still at the copilot's seat, eyes fixed forward, listening with her whole spine.

Wendell flew for a full minute before answering. Below them the valley scrolled past—villages he knew, fields he'd buzzed greetings over, the whole living country that had taken him in when he'd had nowhere else to be. Then he banked gently southeast, onto the heading that would take them across the trough's upper end rather than around it, and felt the aircraft settle onto the new line like a decision being made by someone calmer than he was.

"We'll cross at the top," he said. "Highest point, widest margin, best escape lines. If the build-up reaches the col before we do, we turn back and you can fire me."

"I don't want to fire you."

"Everybody wants something they can't afford, Ms. Voss."

Behind them, Bram opened his eyes for the first time in an hour. Wendell caught it in the reflection off the windshield glass—just a flicker, just the quiet man doing quiet arithmetic. Watching the corridor. Watching Wendell. Cataloguing something.

* * *

They crossed at fourteen thousand two hundred feet.

The trough's upper end passed beneath the left wing at a respectful distance, wrapped in cloud, anonymous, just another gray fold in the gray country. Wendell flew it clean and flat and fast, hands light, voice steady, calling out altitudes and headings in the flat professional cadence that had gotten him through a thousand hard crossings. If his heart was slamming, none of it reached the controls. That was the deal he'd struck with himself years ago: the fear could live in him, but it didn't get to fly the airplane.

Halfway across, the storm ahead announced itself.

It came off the main range as a single consolidated mass now, no longer patient, lightning strobing inside it like a thought trying to form. The build-up he'd measured at forty miles had eaten twenty of them while they climbed. The window over the pass ahead—the second pass, the high one, the real gate into the interior—was closing at a rate he could see with his own eyes.

"Delia. Fuel state."

"Two-forty usable. We burn thirty getting home if we turn now."

Okafor leaned forward between the seats. He had a map on his knee despite everything, pencil already moving. "There is a strip," he said. "Meadow strip, marked on my base map, eleven miles short of the pass. Grazing country. The herders use it."

"I know it," Wendell said. "Kilometer-long grass, soft in the middle after rain, slope to the east. Landable today. Takeoff tomorrow morning, early, before the thermals wake up." He looked at the storm. Looked at his watch. Did the arithmetic every pilot does and hates. "We're not beating that over the pass. We're spending the night at the meadow."

Voss exhaled through her nose. "That costs us a day."

"The storm costs us the airplane. Pick one."

For a moment he thought she'd argue. He could see it assembling behind her eyes—the schedule, the buyer's clock, whatever engine was driving her that she hadn't shown them yet. Then she looked out the windshield at the lightning, and something in her face went carefully blank, and she said, "The meadow."

"Good choice," Delia said, and began securing the cabin.

They came down through the last layer of cloud onto the strip at six in the evening, Wendell flying a low pass first to read the grass and the wind sock—a tattered orange thing that told him the surface wind was quartering fifteen and gusty—and then setting the Caravan down on the uphill third with a touch so soft that Okafor, watching from behind, actually said, "Huh," in a tone of genuine surprise.

Wendell taxied to the far end, into the wind, and shut down, and the silence rang.

They made camp in the lea of the fuselage. Delia got a stove going. Okafor spread his maps on a groundsheet and weighted the corners with rocks, and Voss crouched over them with him, the two of them murmuring, planning tomorrow's leg. Bram stood apart, facing the storm, arms folded, watching the lightning walk along the ridgelines like a sentry counting patrols.

Wendell drank tea that tasted of smoke and altitude and let his hands stop shaking where no one could see.

The storm spent itself against the pass all night, thunder rolling down the valley in long diminishing volleys, and near midnight the clouds tore open above them and showed stars, cold and enormous and indifferent. Wendell stood his watch by the wing, as he always did, checking tie-downs, checking fuel caps, checking the sky.

Off to the east, in the torn edge of the clearing cloud, moonlight found the north wall of the corridor. Just for a moment. A pale scar of exposed rock high on the face, visible even at this distance, even after seven years.

The inquiry had published aerial photographs. He'd memorized them despite himself. He knew that scar. He knew exactly where Elio had hit.

He stood there a long time, tea going cold in his hands, until Delia came out to relieve the watch and found him looking and, wisely, asked him nothing at all.

"Get some sleep," she said instead. "Early start. Thermals."

"Thermals," he agreed.

But as he crawled into his bag behind the wheel fairing, he heard it—faint, carried on the dying wind from the direction of the pass, a sound that didn't belong to storms or herders or anything natural.

An engine. High and thin and wrong. An aircraft, flying at night, in weather that had grounded everything with wings, coming from the east.

Coming from behind them.

Wendell lay still in the dark and listened to it fade, and did not sleep again.
