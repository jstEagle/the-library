## 7. Okafor's Maps

Adaeze Okafor had been lying to Wendell Ishikawa for six days, and it was starting to itch.

Not the way a lie usually itched—she'd carried professional lies before, the small cartographic fictions every surveyor learned to live with: the smoothed contour, the omitted ravine, the boundary drawn where the client wanted it rather than where the water said it was. Those were tolerable. They were the cost of doing business in a world that preferred its maps tidy.

This was different. This was a man flying them into weather that could kill them all, and she was carrying a folder under her bunk that would tell him why.

She told herself it wasn't her decision to make. Voss had been explicit: *Nobody sees those maps until we're committed, Adaeze. Not the pilot, not the mechanic, not Bram. Committed means past the last turnback point.* And Okafor had signed on knowing Voss's methods. Had admired them, once, from a comfortable distance—the way you admire a hawk from a blind.

But they were four days out of the frontier town now, camped on a gravel bar beside a river the charts called the Upper Seng but which Wendell, without looking up, had called by another name entirely, and the distance between admiring a hawk and riding inside its talons had gotten very short.

She spread her instruments on the tailgate of the supply sled while Delia argued with the generator two hundred meters off. Wendell was at the plane, running his evening ritual: walking the airframe with a flashlight, hand flat against the skin of it, like a doctor palpating something he loved and didn't trust. He did it every night, same order, left wingtip to tail. She'd watched him do it six evenings now. It was the only time his face went soft.

*Committed,* she thought. Were they? The pass was still two days ahead. A man like Ishikawa could turn this aircraft around on a sentence.

She closed the instrument case and went to find out what kind of man he was.

* * *

He was re-tying a cargo strap when she reached the plane, and he didn't stop working, just angled his head enough to acknowledge her. "Surveyor."

"Pilot." She leaned against the gear strut, close enough to feel the cold coming off the aluminum. "Question. Tomorrow's leg—you said we'd follow the Seng gorge to the confluence, then climb the western tributary."

"That's the route."

"The official survey of that tributary dates to 1911," she said. "A colonial triangulation party. Two of their cairns are still standing—I've seen the photographs. Their map puts the gorge wall at nine hundred meters."

Wendell pulled the strap tight. "And?"

"And I've flown that corridor twice on contract work for the mining ministry. The wall is closer to fourteen hundred." She watched his hands. "Your route notes say we'll clear it with four hundred meters to spare in the narrows. Your route notes are wrong, pilot. Or they're not yours."

For the first time he stopped moving. He straightened up slowly and looked at her, and she saw the thing she'd been trying to read all week finally surface: not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been waiting for someone to say the true thing so he could decide what to do about it.

"Show me your numbers," he said.

She showed him. He checked them himself, taking her notebook without asking, working the arithmetic with a grease pencil on the plane's cowling, and when he finished he stood there a long moment with the pencil motionless in his fist.

"Fourteen hundred," he said quietly. "Yeah. That's about right." He looked up. "You've known since the town?"

"Since before the town."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Something wearier. "Voss's numbers?"

"Voss's numbers come from me," Okafor said. "That's my job. What's *her* number, Wendell—that's the question I can't answer, because there's a source she won't show either of us, and everything she's routing us toward matches it to within fifty meters. Nobody routes that clean off a 1911 triangulation. Somebody has modern data. Somebody has *exact* data." She paused. "And I think you know whose."

The wind came down the valley then, carrying river-smell and the first real bite of altitude, and Wendell looked north toward the range the way a man looks at a dog that bit him once.

"I know the country," he said. "I don't know her sources."

"Then let me make us even." She heard herself say it and was surprised—she'd planned a careful approach, incremental, deniable, and instead here was the whole truth walking out into the open like a fool. "You showed me your route is honest. Let me show you hers isn't. Come to my tent. Bring nothing. Tell no one."

* * *

The maps lived in a waterproof tube lashed inside her duffel beneath a false layer of clothing, and she'd slept on top of them for six nights, which meant she'd also slept on the knowledge of them, which was maybe why the itch had gotten so bad. She unrolled them across her cot by lamplight, weighting the corners with her boots and the lamp base and a tin of pencils.

Three sheets. Wendell bent over them and went very still.

They were beautiful. That was Okafor's own confession, the one she'd never said aloud to anyone: whatever else these were, they were the finest work she'd ever seen. Base layers in faded sepia—old, older than the colonial survey, older than anything official—and over them, in a newer hand, corrections in red ink so precise they looked printed. Elevations corrected. River courses corrected. And threading through the upper watershed, in that confident red hand, a route: not the route Voss had filed with the charter authority, not the tourist line down the main channel, but a working route into the headwaters, annotated in a cramped, slanted script.

Wendell touched the script with one fingertip, not quite resting on it.

"That's Elio's hand," Okafor said.

"I know," Wendell said.

His voice didn't change at all, and that was how she knew. She'd lost a brother; she knew what a voice sounded like when it refused to change because changing would let everything behind it out.

"When—" he started. Stopped. Started again. "These base sheets. Where did she get pre-colonial surveys of a watershed the government pretends doesn't exist?"

"There's an archive in the south. Missionary records, mostly—orders that came upriver a century ago and mapped what they found before anyone told them not to." Okafor sat back on her heels. "I authenticated them myself. They're genuine. But the red ink, the corrections, the route—that's field work. Recent field work. Whoever drew that walked the headwaters with a theodolite and a barometer, and did it *well*, and did it recently enough that the ink isn't even foxed."

"Elio died seven years ago."

"Then either these are seven years old," Okafor said, "or somebody has been back since. Look at the third sheet. Bottom corner."

He turned to it. She watched him find it: a small date notation in the margin, in the same red ink, in the same slanted hand. Not seven years old. Four.

Wendell sat down on the duffel as if his knees had been disconnected from the rest of him.

"Four years ago," he said. "Somebody was in the headwaters four years ago, mapping with Elio's hand." He laughed once, a sound with no humor in it whatsoever. "Or Elio wasn't where I buried him."

"Careful," Okafor said quietly. "That road goes somewhere bad, and I've watched you circling it all week."

"You don't know what I circle."

"No," she admitted. "But I know what I'm looking at. A dead man's handwriting, dated after his death, in the possession of a woman who bought your debt to get you to fly her to the exact coordinates it describes." She held his gaze. "I'm a surveyor, Wendell. My entire profession is the discipline of not fooling yourself. And I am telling you that the evidence in front of us does not support the story Marguerite Voss told us in her office, and it does not support the story she told us at the staging field, and every day she doesn't explain these maps is a day the story gets worse."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the generator coughed and caught—Delia winning her argument—and the light through the tent canvas flickered with it. Somewhere downslope, Bram's silhouette crossed between the fire and the river, unhurried, checking the perimeter the way he did every night at the same hour, and Okafor noted it the way she noted everything now: automatically, professionally, filing it against a day she might need it.

"Here's what I can't get past," Wendell said finally. He hadn't looked away from the maps. "Elio hated maps like these. Not the craft—he loved the craft. He hated what they were *for*. We found something up there, Adaeze. Something that made both of us agree, drunk, sober, terrified, all of it—we agreed nobody gets coordinates. You don't put a place like that on paper, because paper travels. Paper gets bought." His jaw worked. "So either he broke the only promise that ever mattered to him, or—"

"Or someone made these from his notes after he died," Okafor finished. "Which requires access to his notes. Which requires someone he trusted, or someone who took them off his body."

The lamp hissed. Wendell looked at her then, and she saw the calculation finish behind his eyes, the moment the private grief got converted into operational fact, and she understood suddenly why Voss had wanted this man: not for his flying. For this. For the way he could take a wound and turn it into a bearing.

"Tomorrow," he said, "when we hit the narrows, I'm going to fly the corridor my way, not hers. Fourteen-hundred-meter walls get fourteen-hundred-meter respect. If she objects, she can explain why in front of the crew."

"She won't object. Objecting reveals she knows better, and she's spent a week pretending her data is generic."

"Exactly." He stood. "And the day after the pass, when we're committed—"

"We're not using her word," Okafor said. "*Committed* is a leash word. I've heard her use it three times, always to people she's leashing."

That almost got a smile out of him, the ghost of one, gone fast. "When we're past turnback, then. You and I are going to put these maps on the crate table in front of everyone, and she's going to tell us where a dead man's handwriting came from. Delia deserves it. Even Bram deserves it."

"Bram already knows."

Wendell stopped in the tent flap. "What?"

"He asked to see my instrument calibrations on day two. Standard paranoia. Except he asked about the *barometric* calibration specifically, and he asked whether I'd cross-checked against any historical surveys." She shrugged. "Fixers don't care about barometry, Wendell. Someone briefed him. Maybe not fully—but briefed."

Wendell stood there processing it, and she watched him reorder the crew in his head, reshuffling loyalties like cargo weights, and she felt the itch sharpen into something with an edge on it. Because here was the thing she hadn't said yet, the thing sitting under the maps like groundwater:

She liked him. Six days of watching a man palpate his airplane and call rivers by their right names, and she liked him, and liking people in this profession was how you got used. Voss had used her admiration once already. She wasn't going to stand in the rain and watch it happen to someone else.

"One more thing," she said. "You should know why I'm showing you this. It's not conscience. Well—it's not only conscience." She folded the third sheet along its original creases, careful, archival, the way her hands did when her mind was elsewhere. "There's a notation on the second sheet. Red ink, upper watershed, near the coordinates Voss actually wants. It's not a survey note. It's a warning. Same hand, same ink, four years old or seven, I can't date it finer than that."

"What does it say?"

Okafor handed him the sheet and pointed.

In the cramped slanted script, beside a bend in the river where the walls narrowed to a throat, Elio's handwriting—or the handwriting wearing Elio's name—had written a single line:

***Whoever reads this: they will come with money and maps. Burn both.***

Wendell read it twice. When he looked up, the lamplight caught his face from below, and Okafor saw a man arriving at a conclusion he'd been fleeing for seven years.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we fly the narrows low and slow, and everybody keeps their eyes on the walls." He rolled the maps with a care that surprised her, handing them back like something alive. "Because if Elio wrote that, then whoever sent us up this river isn't chasing salvage."

Outside, the fire had burned to coals, and Bram was no longer visible anywhere along the perimeter.

"They're making sure," Wendell said, "that nobody ever reads it twice."
