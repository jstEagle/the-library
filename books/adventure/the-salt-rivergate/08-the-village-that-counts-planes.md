## 8. The Village That Counts Planes

Delia noticed the smoke first.

Not engine smoke—cooking smoke, a dozen thin columns rising out of the green where the river bent wide and slow. She'd been riding in the back of the Otter for two hours with her boots braced against a crate of fuel drums, watching the mountains chew the horizon into smaller and smaller pieces, and she'd learned by now that Wendell Ishikawa flew the way other men read: silently, completely, turning pages nobody else could see.

"Smoke," she said, leaning forward between the seats.

"Kamari village," Wendell said, without looking. "We're burning more fuel than I like on this leg. We stop."

Voss glanced up from her maps. "Is there an airstrip?"

"There's a meadow," Wendell said. "The airstrip is a courtesy we extend to each other."

Delia filed that away—the way he said *we*, casual as breathing, as if the whole river valley were a single household and he simply one of its errands. Seven years, Okafor had told her, flying medicine into country like this. She'd assumed it was penance. Watching his hands on the yoke, easy and exact, she was starting to think it might be something else entirely.

The meadow appeared out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen—a long green shelf above the river, grass to the hubs, a wind sock that was really just a pole with faded cloth someone had tied to it out of optimism. Wendell set the Otter down so gently Delia felt the wheels touch before she heard them.

And then the children came.

They came out of the tree line at a dead run, maybe fifteen of them, shrieking something in the river tongue, and Delia's hand went instinctively toward the tool roll under her seat—until she saw Wendell's face.

He'd gone still. Not afraid. Something worse than afraid. He looked like a man who has walked into a room and heard his own name spoken by strangers.

"They know you," Voss said quietly.

"That's not possible," Wendell said. But his voice had gone strange.

He killed the engines and the sound of the valley rushed in—river, rain on leaves, and the children's voices resolving, as they got closer, into a word they were shouting over and over, a word Delia didn't understand but whose shape she recognized immediately, because it was being aimed at the pilot's seat like an arrow.

Wendell opened his door and the smallest child reached him first, a girl of six or seven, and grabbed his sleeve and said the word again, right up at him, insistent, and then laughed when he didn't answer fast enough.

"What are they saying?" Voss asked.

Okafor, who had been quiet the whole flight, leaned forward. He'd been listening too, and there was an odd expression on his face—the expression of a surveyor finding a feature on the ground that his maps said shouldn't exist.

"It's a name," Okafor said. "It's *his* name. Or close enough. They're saying—" he worked through it, careful, "—*the plane-man*. But it's not generic. It's got his name folded inside it. Like a title." He looked at Wendell. "How long have you been coming here?"

"I've never—" Wendell stopped. Started again. "I fly this corridor twice a year. Vaccines, mostly. Sometimes a woman named Sefu radios ahead for antibiotics for the kids' ears. I drop crates. I've never landed here. I've never met anyone."

"You've never landed," Okafor repeated, gently, like a man testing ice.

"I drop at the crossroads marker. They come get it."

Delia watched it land on him, all of it, in stages. The children were swarming the plane now, touching the struts with proprietary familiarity, and one boy—older, maybe twelve—climbed up on the wheel strut and patted the cowling the way you'd pat a horse, and said something to the others that made them laugh, and Delia would have bet anything she knew what it was. Some joke about the plane. An old joke. A joke with history in it.

They had names for his quirks. These children who had never spoken to him had names for how he flew.

* * *

The village came down to meet them properly—an elder first, a small wiry man with a white beard and a walking stick topped with river-polished stone, flanked by two women carrying woven mats. Behind them, half the village, it seemed, moving at the unhurried pace of people who have decided something is going to happen and see no reason to rush it.

The elder stopped ten feet from Wendell and looked at him for a long time. Then he spoke—not the river tongue, but trade speech, accented but clear.

"Seven years," the elder said. "Twice a year. Fourteen times the sky made its noise over the crossroads, and fourteen times my granddaughter ran to fetch the crate, and fourteen times the crate held exactly what we needed, though you did not know what we needed." He tapped his stick once against the earth, a full stop. "You thought you were hiding, plane-man. You hid very loudly."

Wendell's jaw worked. "I don't understand."

"No," the elder agreed. "That is common among the young." He turned to the women. "They will want food. The tall one looks like she argues with engines; feed her most. And the drums must be moved off the wet grass before the rain comes back and takes them downhill."

It was, Delia realized, an invitation phrased as logistics, delivered with the absolute authority of a man who had never once in his life been disobeyed by anyone whose opinion mattered.

They carried the drums up to the village themselves—Wendell refused help, of course, shouldering two drums at a time while children trailed him like ducklings, and Delia watched him pretend not to notice and fail completely, because one of the smaller boys had taken to holding onto his trouser leg and he couldn't walk a straight line without dragging the kid along like an anchor.

The village sat on a bluff above the river: stilt houses, drying racks hung with fish, a long open-sided shelter at the center where a fire burned even in the rain. Someone brought cassava and smoked fish and a dark sweet tea, and Delia ate sitting on a mat with her back against a post, listening to Bram answer the children's questions about his knife in flat monosyllables that somehow delighted them more than stories would have.

Voss stood apart, maps under her arm, wearing the expression of a woman recalculating. Delia drifted over to her.

"Trouble?" she asked, low.

"Information," Voss said. "This man has been supplying this entire watershed for seven years and doesn't know he's known. Which means every village on this river knows our route, our schedule, and our aircraft." Her eyes tracked the elder, who was speaking quietly with Okafor near the fire. "I spent three months and a great deal of money acquiring intelligence on this region. It was apparently available for the price of a crate of vaccines."

"Maybe that's the point," Delia said.

Voss looked at her.

"Maybe that's why he does it," Delia said. "Fly small. Be nobody. And the whole time he's the most known man on the river." She shrugged. "You can't buy that, Ms. Voss. I'm guessing you tried."

Something flickered across Voss's face—annoyance, yes, but underneath it something that might have been envy, quickly buried. "Eat your fish," she said, and walked away.

* * *

Delia found Wendell at the edge of the bluff at dusk, because that was where the twelve-year-old said he'd gone, in the way of children who keep better track of adults than adults keep track of themselves.

He was standing looking down the river, and beside him, uninvited, was the old man's granddaughter—the same small girl from the landing, now armed with a stick she was using to draw in the mud. She drew a shape. Delia, coming closer, saw it was an airplane. Crude, but recognizable—and recognizable specifically as *his* airplane, high wing, twin blades, the little stub nose. The girl drew a line from it to a circle. Then another circle further along. Then a third.

"She's drawing your route," Delia said.

Wendell didn't startle. He'd heard her coming; she was sure of it. "Every stop I make," he said quietly. "Crossroads. Kamari. Two more upstream. There's a fourth circle." He crouched down. "What's this one, little one?"

The girl said something rapid and proud, pointing at the fourth circle, which sat apart from the others, higher up, near a squiggle that could only be the headwaters. The mountains.

Wendell went very still.

"What did she say?" Delia asked.

Okafor answered from behind them—he seemed to materialize whenever maps or languages were involved, and his voice had a carefulness in it that hadn't been there before. "She says that's where the plane-man's friend lives. The one who waits."

"The one who—" Wendell straightened slowly. "There is no friend. There's no stop there. I've never flown past the second crossing."

"Children hear things," Okafor said. "Elders say things. This whole valley talks to itself, Wendell. It's been talking about you for seven years." He paused. "And apparently about someone else. Someone connected to you, in their telling, who is associated with the headwaters. With the gate country."

The rain started again, soft on the leaves. Down in the mud, the girl finished her map with a flourish—a fourth circle, and around it she drew a ring of small marks, and Delia didn't need a translator to read those. Mountains. Or teeth.

"We leave at first light," Voss called from the shelter, crisp, businesslike, the moment already converted into schedule. "Weather window won't hold."

But Wendell wasn't listening. He was crouched again in front of the girl, and he asked her something in careful trade speech, and she answered, and whatever she said made him close his eyes for a moment like a man taking a punch he'd been expecting for seven years.

"What is it?" Delia asked. "What did she say?"

Wendell opened his eyes. When he spoke, his voice was level in the way Delia had learned meant the opposite of level.

"She says the waiting friend left something behind," he said. "At the last village before the gate. And that the elders have kept it safe." He looked down the darkening river, toward the headwaters he'd been flying away from for seven years. "She says it's for me."

Behind them, at the fire, the elder was watching. He tapped his stick once against the earth—that same full stop—and said nothing at all.

And Delia, who fixed engines for a living and trusted nothing she couldn't put her hands on, understood suddenly that they were no longer flying toward the Salt Rivergate.

It had been flying toward them the whole time.
