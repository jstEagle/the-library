## 9. Bram in the Night

Delia Reyes had a rule about engines: they never lied. A man could lie all day with his mouth, but an engine told you everything—what it had been fed, what it had been asked to carry, what it was about to do to you. She'd built her whole reputation on listening to machines tell the truth.

Which was why she couldn't sleep in the hangar at Drowned Meadow's lower strip, even after Wendell finally killed the work lights and the others rolled into their bedrolls. The plane wasn't right. Not broken—nothing she could put a wrench on—but *not right*, the way a room isn't right when someone has moved your tools two inches to the left. She'd felt it since the fuel stop at the village that counted planes, some low-grade wrongness humming under her sternum like a bearing running dry.

So she lay there in the dark listening to the rain start against the corrugated roof, and she did what she always did when a machine bothered her. She got up to check it.

* * *

The hangar was old army surplus, half of it open to the weather, and the Cessna sat in the lit half where Wendell could keep an eye on it from the cot he didn't use. Delia moved through the dark by feel and habit, past the stacked cargo crates Voss had insisted on bringing—survey gear, supposedly, though half of it weighed wrong for survey gear—and stopped dead.

The radio panel was glowing.

Not the standby glow. The full glow. Backlight on, frequency window lit, and a thin thread of sound coming off it that she knew the way she knew her own heartbeat: the carrier hum of an active transmission.

Somebody was talking on the radio at one in the morning, and it wasn't Wendell, because Wendell was asleep ten meters away with his boots still on, and it wasn't Okafor, who'd gone down hard after two cups of the frontier town's coffee and hadn't stirred since, and it wasn't Marguerite Voss, because Marguerite Voss would have made a speech about it first.

That left Bram.

Delia stood in the shadow of the crates and watched him. He was crouched at the panel with a small light clipped to his collar turned down to almost nothing, and he had the access cover off the transceiver—the cover she herself had torqued down that afternoon, because that was her job, because she was the mechanic, because this was *her* airplane as much as it was anyone's. His hands were moving inside it, quick and sure, and they were not the hands of a man doing maintenance. Maintenance hands were slow. Maintenance hands checked twice. These hands were doing something specific, something rehearsed, and when he lifted out a component no bigger than a matchbox and held it up to his little light, turning it, Delia felt the tourniquet go on around her chest—one clean turn, ratcheted tight.

She knew what that was. She'd seen them in the ports down south, in the hands of customs men who weren't really customs men. A beacon. A tracker. Something that squawked your position to whoever was paying for the squawking.

Bram turned it over once more, then reached back into the radio's guts—

"Careful," Delia said. "You bend that antenna lead, we're deaf for the rest of the trip."

* * *

To his credit—and Delia noted the credit the way you note a debt, coldly, to be collected later—he didn't jump. He went very still instead, which was worse, and then he finished seating the component back into its bracket with two precise movements before he turned around. Even caught, he made the motion look like routine. That took discipline. She gave him that much.

"Maintenance," he said.

"At one in the morning."

"You were snoring."

"I don't snore." She came forward into the dim spill of the panel light, arms crossed, and looked pointedly at the access cover lying on the floor beside his knee. "And maintenance doesn't happen with the aircraft owner asleep and the actual mechanic uninvited. You want to try again?"

Bram looked at her. In the low light his face was all planes and shadows, unreadable as a closed ledger, but his eyes did something she hadn't seen them do before—they weighed her. Not the way men usually weighed her, with the quick dismissive flick of a man deciding whether she mattered. This was slower. This was a man calculating how much trouble she was going to be, and what it would cost to make her not be trouble, and whether the math worked.

Delia had grown up around that look. Her father ran a salvage yard outside Cebu where the customers sometimes weren't customers, and she'd learned before she was twelve that the dangerous ones were never the loud ones. The dangerous ones did math.

"Squawk box was loose," Bram said. "Wouldn't want it rattling loose over the pass."

"The squawk box is riveted to the frame. I riveted it."

"Then maybe I was checking your rivets."

"Maybe you were." She crouched down across from him, close enough to see the fine work inside the panel, and let the silence sit between them like a third person. Rain drummed the roof. Somewhere out in the dark a dog was losing an argument with another dog. "Here's the thing about me, Bram. I don't care much what people's secrets are. Everybody's got a suitcase full of them, and most suitcases weigh about the same. But I care what goes into my airplane. Every gram of it. Because my airplane is the only thing standing between six people and a mountain range that eats airplanes for breakfast, and if there's hardware in that radio that I didn't put there, then I can't vouch for my airplane anymore, and the second I can't vouch for my airplane, I'm just a woman sitting in a tin can waiting for physics to finish its paperwork."

Bram said nothing. His stillness had a quality she recognized now—it wasn't calm. It was load-bearing. A man holding still so nothing else shifted.

"So here's what happens," she went on, keeping her voice level, almost friendly. "Tomorrow, in daylight, with Wendell watching, you're going to hand me whatever you've got stashed in that radio, or you're going to convince me—actually convince me—that there's nothing to hand over. And if neither of those things happens, I'm going to tell Wendell, and Okafor, and Marguerite, in that order, and then it stops being your secret and becomes everybody's problem. I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you the schedule."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear exactly. Recalculation. The math changing.

"And tonight?" he said.

"Tonight, you put the cover back on, you go to sleep, and I stay up and watch you do it."

He held her gaze a moment longer. Then, unhurried, he picked up the access cover, fitted it back into place, and drove the four screws home with the little driver from his breast pocket—each one snug, each one even, workmanlike to the last turn. It was, Delia thought, the most honest thing he'd done all night. A man who lied with his hands too wouldn't have bothered making the repair look good.

"Goodnight, Delia," he said, and rose, and walked back toward his bedroll with that unhurried walk of his, and lay down, and within minutes his breathing slowed into something that sounded convincingly like sleep.

Delia stayed where she was. She pulled the crate of spare parts over, sat on it facing the panel, and settled in.

* * *

The rain thickened toward dawn, coming down in ropes, and the hangar roof began to leak in three places she catalogued for later patching. She kept watch. Not because she expected Bram to move again—a man like him had gotten what he needed, whatever it was, and wouldn't risk a second pass—but because she wanted to think, and thinking was easier with something to guard.

Here was what bothered her, laid out in the order she'd assembled it:

One. Bram had been in the radio at night, alone, working fast and rehearsed. Two. Whatever he'd handled, he'd put it *back*. If he were planting a bug, he'd have planted it and left. If he were removing one, he'd have removed it and pocketed it. Instead he'd inspected it, adjusted it, and reinstalled it—which meant the device was supposed to be there, already installed, already working, and he'd come to check on it. Service, not sabotage. Someone was servicing a transmitter hidden in Wendell Ishikawa's radio, and Bram was the service tech.

Three—and this was the one that made her stomach drop through the hangar floor—she remembered the stranger in the frontier town. The one photographing the aircraft. She'd clocked him at the time and filed him under "frontier weirdo," same drawer as the man who'd tried to sell Okafor a stuffed monitor lizard. But frontier weirdos photographed sunsets. They didn't shoot forty frames of a parked Cessna from three angles, including one long lens shot of the tail number, and then get into a car with government plates that nobody in a town like that had any business driving.

Four. Which meant somebody knew where they were going. Not guessed—knew. And was following along from a distance, patient as a leech, letting Bram do the walking.

Delia turned it over and over, looking for the version where it was innocent. Maybe Bram was ex-military signals and this was habit. Maybe the beacon was a locator for insurance purposes that Voss had installed without telling anyone, and Bram, discreet professional that he was, was checking it on his employer's behalf. Maybe. She built that story carefully, brick by brick, and then she looked at it honestly and watched it fall down. Insurance beacons didn't get serviced at 0100 by the quiet fixer while everyone slept. Employers who installed trackers told their crews, because crews talked, and secrets that leaked got people sued. No—the arrangement she was looking at had the shape of something that needed to stay secret from the crew specifically. From *her* specifically, maybe, since she was the one who'd find anything wired into the airframe.

And underneath all of it, quieter and colder than the rest: the village. The children chanting Wendell's name. The way the old women had counted the plane in like a returning son. Delia had watched Wendell stand in that crowd of kids and not know what to do with his face, and she'd thought, *there it is, that's the real man, buried under seven years of flying small and meaning less.* She liked him. Against every rule she had about employers and clients and men generally, she liked him—liked the way he flew like the plane was part of his nervous system, liked the way he'd spent the whole evening teaching a nine-year-old girl to read a compass rather than make small talk with Voss.

And somebody was using his airplane as a homing pigeon.

* * *

Gray light came up through the rain around five. Delia heard the others stir—Okafor coughing himself awake, Voss's crisp footsteps on concrete—and she made her decision the way she made all her decisions, by torque spec: enough force to hold, not enough to strip the threads.

She wasn't going to say anything yet.

It wasn't loyalty to Bram, obviously. It was arithmetic. If she raised the alarm now, on evidence this thin—"I saw the fixer near the radio at night"—one of two things happened. Either Bram denied it smoothly, and then she was the paranoid mechanic who'd accused a crewmate on vibes, and the trust in this little expedition cracked along a seam nobody could see but everybody would feel for the next thousand miles. Or Bram didn't deny it, and then it became a confrontation, here, now, at a wet airstrip with one plane and no alternatives, and a cornered man with Bram's skill set might decide the situation called for more than words. She'd seen the knife he carried. She'd also seen how he carried it—low on the hip, angled for a cross draw, the rigging of a man who'd drawn it before. You didn't push a man like that onto his back foot in a confined space unless you were ready for what came off his front foot.

No. What she needed was proof, and proof required patience, and patience required her to spend a day flying beside a man she now understood to be a wiretap with legs, smiling, passing him tools, letting him believe the night had gone unnoticed.

She could do that. Her father hadn't taught her engines only.

Voss appeared at the hangar mouth with her hair already disciplined and her voice already loaded. "Weather window closes at noon. We lift at ten. Delia, I want the preflight done by eight."

"You'll have it by seven," Delia said.

Wendell came in last, rolling his shoulders, glancing automatically at the plane the way he always did—first the wings, then the cowling, then the tail, a man greeting an old friend for signs of illness. His eyes passed over the radio panel, over the access cover sitting square and true, and moved on.

Delia watched him check, and felt the tourniquet give one more slow turn.

Because now she knew something else, and it sat in her chest like a stone swallowed whole: whatever Bram was servicing in that radio, it reported to someone, and that someone had photographed this plane in a town full of old-timers who looked at Wendell like a ghost with a flight plan. And when the truth came out—and it would come out, machines never lied, and neither did she for long—it was going to land on Wendell Ishikawa like bad weather, and he had enough bad weather in his history to last a lifetime.

She picked up her tool roll and started the preflight, and she smiled at Bram when he brought her the oil sample, and she hated how easily it came.

At seven-forty, while the others loaded cargo, she found herself alone at the tail of the plane for ninety seconds, and she used them. She knelt at the radio's external antenna fairing, the one spot on the whole airframe where a device could reach the transceiver bus without touching anything she'd logged, and she looked—really looked—with her thumbnail and a flashlight and seven years of knowing where things hide.

There was a seam in the fairing that shouldn't have been there.

Fresh solder shine under the sealant. A wire no thicker than a hair, running inward.

Delia sat back on her heels in the rain-smell and the fuel-smell, and somewhere far off, thunder rolled down out of the range they were about to fly into, and she thought: *I have to tell him. Not today. But soon.*

Behind her, gravel crunched—Bram, carrying the next crate, watching her with those ledger-keeper's eyes.

"Problem with the tail?" he asked pleasantly.

"No," said Delia, standing, closing the tool roll, smiling her father's smile. "Just admiring the workmanship."
