## 10. Storm Gate

The storm came up the valley like something with intent.

Wendell saw it first as a bruise on the western ridgeline, a color the sky had no business wearing at two in the afternoon. By three it had eaten the sun. By four he had the Heron down on the meadow strip at Kettle Gap with forty minutes of daylight left and his hands shaking in a way he hadn't let them shake since the year Elio died.

"Textbook," Delia said, climbing out onto the wet grass, admiring the landing the way she admired anything mechanical—without sentiment and with real respect.

"It wasn't." He killed the engine. "I've landed here maybe thirty times. I've never seen that wind shear off the eastern spur. The gap's acting like a chimney."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the storm found a way through the range it isn't supposed to use. Meaning we're not going anywhere tomorrow either."

Okafor stood at the edge of the strip with her survey case hugged to her chest, watching the cloud wall come down the valley like a tide of gray wool. "How long?"

"Two days if we're lucky. Three if we're not."

Bram said nothing. He'd said almost nothing since the last town, and Wendell had stopped trying to read him because Bram was the kind of man who'd been built to be unreadable—deliberately, professionally, the way some men were built to be forgotten. But he helped stake the tie-downs without being asked, working the ropes with quick economical hands, and when the first rain came sideways across the meadow he was already under the wing with the rest of them.

There was a shelter at Kettle Gap. That was the one mercy of the place—a warden's hut from the old grazing days, corrugated roof, stone walls, a stove that smoked but burned. Somebody had left split wood under an oilskin tarp years ago and somebody else had kept adding to it, which told Wendell everything he needed to know about how often pilots got pinned here. Every pilot who flew this country knew about Kettle Gap the way sailors knew about harbors.

They got a fire going. They ate rice and tinned fish and Okafor produced a bar of dark chocolate from somewhere in her kit and divided it into five exact portions with a ruler-straight cut, which told Wendell she was the kind of person who measured even generosity. Delia talked about her father's machine shop in a voice that made it clear she wasn't looking for conversation, just making noise to fill the space where fear would otherwise live. Bram cleaned a knife that didn't need cleaning.

And Marguerite Voss sat closest to the fire with her boots off and her stockinged feet stretched toward the heat, and watched Wendell with an expression he couldn't name until much later, when he understood it had been pity all along.

"You're quiet," she said finally.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a man doing arithmetic." She turned her hands toward the flames. "So let's do some arithmetic together. You've been flying this route for seven years. You know every strip, every village, every weather pattern between here and the headwaters. And you have never once—not once in seven years—flown the corridor where your own expedition went down. I checked. I asked around for months before I ever came to your fence."

The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof like gravel thrown by a giant hand.

"That's not arithmetic," Wendell said. "That's just my business."

"No. Your business is medicine and mail." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Your business is being small. The corridor is your *past*, and you route around it the way water routes around a stone. I've watched you do it for two weeks now. You don't even look at that side of the sky."

Delia glanced up from the stove. Okafor went very still, the way surveyors go still when they sense the ground shifting under them.

"So tell me," Voss said, "what actually happened out there. Since you're the only living pilot who was on the expedition, and since you clearly can't bear to say it—I'll tell you what *I* know. What I was told. By people who were there."

"There was nobody else there."

"That's what you believe." She leaned forward. "Elio Marchetti took the Salt Rivergate contract in April, seven years ago. Not for salvage—for survey. A mineral assessment company hired him to map the upper watershed. He subcontracted you as his second pilot. You flew eleven supply runs into the headwaters over six weeks. On the twelfth run, flying out heavy with core samples, his aircraft went into the canyon wall in weather he should never have launched in—and you were the one who cleared him to launch."

The words went through Wendell like ice water through a crack.

"I didn't clear him to launch. Nobody cleared anybody. We didn't work that way—we were—" His voice caught on the word. "*Partners.*"

"Were you?" Voss tilted her head. "Because the story I heard has a different shape. It says Elio was carrying samples worth more than the whole rest of the contract combined. It says he was under pressure from the client to get them out before the season closed. And it says his second pilot—the careful one, the one who did all the weather calls because Elio trusted him absolutely—gave him a window that didn't exist. Because the second pilot wanted the job done too. Wanted his name on the discovery. Wanted to stop being somebody's *second*."

"I never—"

"And then," she said, relentless, gentle, terrible, "when the plane went in, the second pilot walked away from the wreckage, buried the samples' existence, spent seven years buying small airplanes and delivering vaccines, and never once told anyone what he'd done. Because telling would mean admitting it."

The hut was very quiet except for the rain. Delia had stopped pretending to be busy. Bram's knife lay flat and forgotten on his knee. Okafor was looking at Wendell with an expression he refused to translate.

"That's not what happened," Wendell said. His voice sounded far away to himself, like radio traffic from another aircraft. "That's not—Marguerite, that's not what happened at all."

"Then what did?"

And he opened his mouth to say it—the true version, the version he'd carried sealed inside him for seven years like a splinter worked so deep into the flesh it had become part of the bone—and found that he couldn't make the words come out in order. Because the memory had edges now that hadn't been there before. Because her version fit against his own in places where it shouldn't have. Because there *had* been pressure from the client. There *had* been a window he'd called. He remembered standing at the strip at dawn with the barometer falling and saying *you can make it if you go now*—

Hadn't he?

Or had Elio decided alone? Had Wendell simply failed to stop him? Was there a difference, at seven years' distance, between those two things? The memory was a room he'd furnished carefully over a long time, and now Voss had walked through it moving furniture, and nothing was where he'd left it, and he could no longer prove to himself which arrangement was original.

"We found something in the gorge," he said instead. Slowly. Testing each word like ice on a wing. "Not minerals. A structure. Old—older than any map in the country. Elio saw what it was within a day. A sluice system. Engineered. Someone built the rivergate, thousands of years ago, to move water for everyone downstream. And the client didn't want a survey anymore—they wanted to buy it, dismantle it, sell the stonework and the water rights. Elio told them no. He told them no on the radio, in writing, in person. He was going to burn the report and file a false location. That's why they needed the samples out fast—that's why he flew that night—to get the evidence away from them before they could—"

"Before they could what?" Voss asked softly.

*Before they could kill him.* The words rose in him and stopped. Because he had never once, in seven years, allowed himself to think that sentence all the way through. He had filed Elio's death under weather. Weather was survivable grief. Weather was a thing a pilot could accept. Murder was not.

But Voss was watching him, and her face had changed—there was something almost like alarm in it now, quickly mastered—and Wendell realized with a lurch that he had just shown her something she hadn't known. Or hadn't expected him to know.

"The client pulled out after the crash," she said. "Contract voided. Records sealed. That's public knowledge."

"People don't pull out of contracts worth that kind of money because their pilot died. They pull out because the pilot wouldn't sell." He was speaking faster now, the room tilting. "You said you heard this story from people who were there. Who? Who was there, Marguerite? Name them."

She held his gaze for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"You're better company than your reputation suggests," she said at last, and reached for her boots. "Get some sleep, Wendell. Tomorrow's a long day of not flying."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed, lying back on her bedroll with her back to him. "It isn't."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat by the banked fire and did what he'd spent seven years refusing to do: he walked the room of that night, deliberately, touching every piece of furniture.

Dawn, the twelfth of October. Barometer falling fast, the kind of fast that meant the front was ahead of schedule. Elio at the strip loading sample cases, humming, alive in the way only Elio was ever alive—all surface light and hidden weight. And Wendell with the forecast in his hand saying the window closes by ten.

*You can make it if you go now.*

He had said that. He was nearly sure he had said that. But had he said it as a warning or as a clearance? Had Elio asked *should I go?* and Wendell answered *yes*, or had Elio simply gone and Wendell's sentence was something he'd added afterward, in the long years of rewriting, to give his guilt a clean shape to hold? Guilt wanted a task. Guilt would rather be the murderer than the bystander. Maybe that was the truth underneath both versions: not that he'd killed his friend, and not that the weather had—but that he'd stood at the edge of a decision that belonged to someone else and never learned, in seven years, whether his voice had tipped it.

And the other thing. The thing Voss's version had no room for at all.

The radio call. Forty minutes into Elio's flight, a transmission, broken by static, that Wendell had never reported and never repeated aloud to another living soul: *"Wendell, there's a second aircraft on this frequency. It's been shadowing me since the pass. If I go down, it wasn't the weather—"*

And then the sound. And then nothing.

He had told himself for seven years that the static had invented the second aircraft. Grief inventing enemies. But Voss had just described a story about samples and pressure and a careless launch, a story with no shadowing aircraft in it anywhere—and she'd told it smoothly, rehearsed, like a document. Like a record someone had prepared. Like a cover story.

Which meant someone had written a cover story. Which meant there was something to cover.

Across the dark hut, Bram's eyes were open. Watching him. Not pretending otherwise.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Bram closed his eyes again, unhurried, a man with nothing to declare, and Wendell lay back on his bedroll and stared at the roof and listened to the storm lean on the mountains.

Tomorrow the weather would keep them pinned. Tomorrow Voss would smile and Okafor would measure and Bram would sharpen his unnecessary knife. But something had shifted tonight, some load had redistributed in the airframe of his life, and Wendell Ishikawa understood that he was no longer flying away from the crash corridor.

Something in that corridor was flying toward him.
