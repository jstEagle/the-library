## 11. Elio's Last Flight

Seven years before the monsoon, three years before Marguerite Voss bought his debt, Wendell Ishikawa still loved flying.

That was the thing he'd have to explain to people later, if he ever explained anything at all—that the love came first, and the ruin came after, and the two had nothing to do with each other until the night they did. In those days the Cessna was new to him, or new enough, and Elio Marchetti rode in the right seat with his boots on the dash and his hands behind his head like a man being chauffeured through paradise.

"Bank left," Elio said, though there was no reason to bank left.

"There's a valley down there full of people who need their mail."

"The mail can wait ninety seconds. Bank left."

Wendell banked left. The wing dipped toward the river, which ran silver and braided below them, splitting and rejoining like something alive trying on different shapes. Elio whooped—not a word, just sound—and Wendell felt it in his chest the way you feel a struck bell. That was Elio's whole talent, really. He made other people's bodies ring.

They were twenty-six and twenty-seven years old, and they had a contract from the Meridian Geographic Society to survey the upper Salt watershed, and between them they owned one aircraft, one camera, one battered theodolite, and a friendship so complete that neither of them had ever needed to describe it out loud.

"Meridian thinks we're mapping tributaries," Elio said, when the wings leveled again.

"Meridian thinks we're mapping tributaries because that's what we told them we're mapping."

"And what are we actually doing?"

Wendell looked at him. Elio was grinning, sunburned, half-mad with altitude and appetite. He had a way of asking questions he already knew the answers to, not to test you but to make you say the true thing aloud, because saying things aloud was how Elio believed the world got made.

"We're following the old surveyor's notes," Wendell said. "The ones from 1911 that nobody followed up on."

"Because?"

"Because the 1911 man wrote that the river comes out of the mountain *organized*."

Elio laughed and slapped the instrument panel hard enough to jolt the compass. "Organized. I love it. The river has a work ethic." He leaned forward then, all at once serious, and this too was an Elio trick—the whiplash from clown to prophet, delivered so fast you never learned to brace for it. "Wendell. Think about it. If those terraces are real—if somebody built waterworks up there before anyone was supposed to be building anything—then every map downstream is wrong. Every flood model. Every irrigation scheme some ministry is drawing up right now in a comfortable office. We wouldn't be finding a ruin. We'd be finding the instruction manual."

"And we give it to whom, exactly?"

"That," Elio said, settling back, "is the question of our lives. Isn't it great?"

* * *

The expedition proper took eleven weeks, and Wendell remembered it afterward in fragments, the way you remember any season when you were happy without knowing it: the smell of canvas drying; Okafor—no, not Okafor, that was later—a young cartographer named Pell who cried at night from homesickness and mapped like an angel by day; the village at the river's knee where an elder named Sefu fed them bitter greens and asked no questions, which in that country was its own kind of generosity.

And Elio, always Elio. Elio learning forty words of the trade language and deploying all forty wrong, daily, to general delight. Elio trading his spare shirt for a carved stone he couldn't read and wearing the shirtlessness like a medal. Elio sitting up with the porters around the fire while Wendell checked the tie-downs, because Elio understood—without ever having been taught, it seemed—that an expedition lived or died on whether the men carrying your gear believed you'd carry them.

"You do the machines," Elio told him once, "and I'll do the people. That's the division of labor. That's the whole company."

"What happens when the machines and the people disagree?"

"Then one of us gets educated." He said it lightly. He would stop saying it lightly within a month.

They found the gate on day sixty-three.

* * *

Later, when journalists and lawyers and men from ministries used the word *discovery*, Wendell would flinch at it, because discovery implied surprise, and there had been none. The 1911 surveyor had told the truth. The river came out of the mountain organized.

It came out of the mountain through the gate.

They stood on the ridge above it for a long time before anyone spoke. Below them the valley opened like a held breath, and across its throat ran the works: sluice channels cut into living rock, terraced spillways descending in clean geometric steps, intake galleries whose mouths were faced with fitted stones so old the lichen had made them look grown rather than built. Water moved through all of it—had always moved through all of it—in quantities that made the modern diversions downstream look like children digging at the edge of a pond.

"It's regulating the whole watershed," Pell said finally, voice cracking. "Do you understand? This isn't a dam. It's a *distribution system*. Somebody engineered the entire river."

"Somebody," Elio said quietly, "who is not in any history book."

They spent nine days documenting it. Nine days of photographs and measurements and Elio climbing into intake galleries with a lamp clenched in his teeth, coming out gray-faced with dust and lit up like a lamp himself. On the fifth day they found the inscription—hundreds of characters, a script related to nothing in any archive—and Elio copied every one by hand, lying on his stomach on wet stone for six hours, refusing help, refusing rest.

On the ninth day, at the fire, he said the thing that ended everything.

"We don't report it."

Wendell looked up from the fuel ledger. "What?"

"Not yet. Not to Meridian, not to anybody." Elio turned his hands over, palms up, as if weighing something invisible. "Think about who finds out first if we file. Meridian publishes. Then the ministry. Then whatever consortium pays the ministry's consulting fees. Within five years there's a concession here, and within ten there are engineers pulling the intake stones apart to see how much flow they can sell, and the villages downstream get whatever's left over. You've seen the diversion works on the lower river, Wendell. You've seen who gets the water when there's a contract involved."

"So we sit on it forever?"

"We sit on it until we know how to protect it. There are ways. Heritage designations, international registries—we find the right people, the ones who can't be bought, and we bring it to them first. Quietly. Properly." He was leaning forward now, firelight in his eyes, and Wendell could see he'd been building this speech for days. "We didn't come here to sell a river. We came here to understand one. Let's not be the men who drew the map for the auction."

Wendell thought about it for exactly as long as it took to remember the braided silver river from the air, and the elder Sefu's face, and the children who had run alongside their column for miles just to see the airplane.

"All right," he said. "We bury it."

Elio grinned, and for a moment it was simple again, the two of them against the world, keepers of a secret instead of sellers of one.

Neither of them knew that Pell had already written to Meridian. Neither of them would know for another nineteen days, and by then it would not matter.

* * *

The letter arrived at the last supply strip, forwarded through three hands, and Wendell watched Elio read it and understood, from the stillness of him, that the world had changed shape while their backs were turned.

"They want a follow-up expedition," Elio said. "Jointly funded. Meridian and—" he stopped, and read the name again as if it might rearrange itself. "The Corvane Development Group."

"I've never heard of them."

"You have. They're the people who did the lower-river concessions. They're the reason the delta villages buy drinking water." He folded the letter along its original creases, very precisely, over and over until it was a small hard rectangle. "Pell."

"Pell wouldn't—"

"Pell wrote home in March. His sister works for a firm that consults." Elio closed his eyes. When he opened them, the anger was already gone, converted, as it always was, into motion. "It doesn't matter. It doesn't matter how they found out. What matters is that in about four months, there will be men with contracts standing where we stood. So we have four months to make this place unfindable, unbuyable, or both."

"How do you make a valley unfindable?"

"You move the coordinates." Elio was already pacing, already somewhere three steps ahead. "Our filed survey has errors—I put them there deliberately, months ago, small ones, in case. If we resubmit corrected data pointing at the wrong tributary system, and we do it fast, before anyone cross-checks—"

"Elio."

"—and meanwhile we take the inscription copies to the university in the capital, the old professor, Ferreira, he hates Corvane, he'd move heaven and—"

"*Elio.*"

He stopped. And Wendell saw, for the first time in seven years of friendship, fear in Elio Marchetti's face—not fear of the valley or the work, but fear of the machinery they'd accidentally fed. Fear of having been, without meaning to be, the first domino.

"We should never have photographed it," Elio said.

"No," Wendell agreed. "Probably not."

But they had. And the photographs existed. And the photographs, Wendell would learn much later, were the actual cargo that everyone upstream wanted—not the gate itself, which could not be moved, but the proof of it, the documentation, the thing that turned a rumor into a concession bid.

They flew out two days later, heavy with fuel and dread, and the weather over the range was already turning.

* * *

The storm was not supposed to be there. Every forecast said three more days of clear windows. But mountains make their own weather, and the pass had filled with cloud by midafternoon, and they went in anyway because Elio wanted the last load of inscription copies out of the country before anyone thought to search their gear, and because Wendell was twenty-six and believed, with the whole faithless fervor of youth, that skill was a substitute for luck.

They crossed the first range fine. The second range ate them.

Ice first—he remembered the ice, the way the controls went thick and mute, the way the stall warning sang its one flat note like a struck glass. Then the downdraft, a fist of falling air off the lee slope that took a thousand feet from them in under a minute, and Wendell flying it, actually flying it, engine at full climb and the ground still rising, and Elio in the right seat doing the one thing Elio had never done in all their years together.

He was quiet.

Not calm-quiet. Praying-quiet. And somehow that was worse than the ice, worse than the altimeter unwinding, because Elio Marchetti did not go quiet, and if Elio had gone quiet then the world had gone quiet, and—

They cleared the second ridge by what Wendell estimated, in the years afterward, at eighty feet. Maybe less. Maybe it was imagination that added the treetops whipping beneath them. The valley beyond opened gray and rain-hammered, and there was the strip—the mission strip, the long grass runway at Drowned Meadow—and Wendell put the plane down through weather that had no business permitting a landing, and they sat on the ground shaking, and Elio started to laugh, huge and helpless, and hit Wendell on the shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"You beautiful bastard," Elio said. "You beautiful, impossible bastard. We're alive."

"We're alive," Wendell said, and laughed too, and that was the last time either of them laughed for a long while.

Because the strip was not empty.

There was a truck at the far end, government-marked, and four men beside it in rain gear, and one of them held a document wallet under his coat the way you hold a thing you've been sent to deliver and would rather not. They walked over through the wet grass. The tallest one did the talking. He was polite. That was the detail Wendell's memory kept returning to, all the years after, the politeness, the courtesy, the way the man apologized for the hour and the weather before he said a single substantive word.

"Gentlemen. Thank you for landing safely. Mr. Marchetti, Mr. Ishikawa—we represent parties with an interest in your recent survey work. There's a purchase offer. For the documentation. All of it—photographs, inscriptions, coordinates. Generous terms. Alternatively—" and here the man smiled, apologetic, reasonable, a mirror held up to a future Wendell would one day meet again in person, "—there are administrative complications with your operating permits that could become genuinely inconvenient."

Elio stepped forward. Wendell would replay this too, endlessly, the geometry of it: Elio stepping slightly in front, shoulders squared, hands loose at his sides, and saying, in his best conference-room voice, the voice that had talked porters and ministers and customs officers into goodwill for years:

"We don't know what you're referring to. Our survey is public record. Take it up with the Society."

The tall man's smile didn't change. "Mr. Marchetti. Everyone knows what you found. The only variable is who holds the paper. Right now, you hold the paper. We'd like you to consider how long that remains an advantage." He set an envelope on the wing strut, weighted it with a stone, and tipped an imaginary hat. "There's a number in there. Think it over tonight. We'll come back tomorrow."

They drove away. The rain kept falling. Elio picked up the envelope, weighed it once in his hand, and threw it unopened into the flooded ditch, and Wendell loved him so much in that moment that the love felt like a physical injury, like a broken rib he'd carry forever.

"He'll be back," Wendell said.

"Then we fly tonight."

"In this?"

"Especially in this. Nobody follows a plane into this." Elio was already walking toward the aircraft, already loading the document case, the inscription copies, the film—the paper, always the paper, the paper that was the whole war. "We go over the third range low, we put down at Ferreira's station, and by morning this stuff is in a university vault with witnesses. Let them buy permits. They can't buy a vault."

Wendell looked at the sky, which was lowering and black and moving like something with intent, and every instrument of judgment he possessed said *no*, and he looked at Elio, who was looking back at him with total confidence—not in the weather, but in him—and Wendell heard himself say:

"Okay. Pre-flight's done. Get in."

* * *

He remembered the flight in pieces, and the pieces never assembled into a whole no matter how many nights he lay awake trying. That was the cruelty of it. Trauma does not edit; it only shuffles.

The ice on the second crossing, worse than before, the de-icing boots thumping uselessly. The radio dying mid-sentence—an electrical failure, clean and total, so that the world contracted to wind and engine and the shape of Elio's head in the seat beside him. The third range appearing out of the murk closer than it had any right to be, because the downdrafts were eating their altitude faster than the gauge admitted.

And then the fire.

He never established—never, in seven years of interrogating the memory—whether it was the carburetor or something else, something introduced, something the polite men had arranged while the plane sat unguarded on the meadow strip. The investigators' report, when it finally came, said electrical. The report was paid for by people who paid for reports. Wendell knew what he smelled: fuel, burning where fuel should not burn, aft of the firewall, and Elio unbuckling and turning around in his seat, shouting over the roar, and the words Wendell caught were *the case*—get the case—because even now, even burning, Elio's mind was on the paper, on keeping the gate's secrets out of the buyers' hands.

The case was wedged. Of course it was wedged. Everything was wedged, the documents, the film, the copies of a dead language, all of it jammed behind the cargo net like a heart clotting.

"Go!" Elio shouted—or Wendell decided afterward he'd shouted; the cockpit was infernal noise. "Get out, I've got it—"

And Wendell, twenty-six years old, with the fire coming aft and the ridgeline coming up, did the arithmetic that would define every remaining year of his life. Seconds to the ridge. Seconds to unbuckle. Seconds to fight the wedged case. Not enough. Not enough for both. Elio was nearer the fire and farther from the door, and the plane was going down regardless, and Wendell chose the door.

He chose the door.

He hit the trees at the edge of the crash corridor and broke his collarbone and his ankle and three ribs, and he crawled clear, and he lay in the fern brake and watched the aircraft burn on the slope above him with Elio inside it, and the fire was enormous and orange and utterly silent, because his ears had stopped working, everything had stopped working except the watching.

The case did not burn. He learned that later. The buyers' men found it in the wreckage two days before the rescue party did, and that was the final joke, the cosmic punchline: Elio died holding the paper and the paper was taken anyway, and the gate's location went into a Corvane filing cabinet in the capital, and the follow-up expedition was postponed, and postponed, and postponed—not abandoned, never abandoned, because men like that do not abandon—until such time as the political weather favored it.

Seven years, as it turned out.

Wendell testified. He told the truth as he had it, which was almost no truth at all: ice, downdraft, fire, jump, alone. The report said electrical fault. The Society sent condolences and a small check. The newspapers gave him a paragraph—*pilot sole survivor of survey crash*—and he took the paragraph and used it to disappear. He sold the Cessna's twin, bought a battered older model, and began flying medicine and mail into villages that had known Elio's laugh, and he never once told any of them why he flinched at the upper river, why he routed around the crash corridor, why he had made smallness his armor and smallness his penance.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud, not to Voss, not to anyone: the arithmetic had been correct. There had not been enough seconds. He had run the numbers in a burning cockpit at twenty-six and the numbers had been right, and knowing they were right had never once, not for a single night, made him forgive himself for choosing the door.

Love and guilt, braided together, indistinguishable, permanent. That was the wreckage he actually carried. The plane was just where he kept it.

* * *

Now, seven years later, in a high meadow with rain coming in sideways and Marguerite Voss's fire smoking in the wet, Wendell finished telling himself the story he had just spent this whole night retelling, and understood suddenly, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with altitude, that Voss's version of these same events—which she had given around the flames an hour ago, smooth and certain—did not match his own in a single particular.

In her version, Elio had sold the papers willingly.

In her version, there had been no envelope on the wing strut, no ultimatum, no burning takeoff into a storm.

In her version, Elio Marchetti had died a collaborator—and she had said it looking directly at Wendell, watching his face, the way you watch a lock to see if the key turns.

Across the fire, Voss met his eyes, and did not look away.
