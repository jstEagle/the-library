## 12. The Monsoon Makes Landfall

The radio woke him at 4:17 in the morning, and Wendell knew before he touched it.

He'd slept in the aircraft's shadow, as he always did at high strips—under the wing where the aluminum held a little of the day's warmth and the wind had to work harder to find him. The meadow strip sat at nine thousand feet, a green scar between two ridgelines that the surveyors of an earlier century had named and then abandoned. They'd been there three days now, pinned by cloud on the pass, waiting for weather that kept promising to break and never quite did. Three days of Bram checking tie-downs, Delia arguing with the fuel pump, Okafor drawing maps by lamplight, Voss standing at the tree line looking north like she could stare a hole through the range.

The radio crackled twice—the old village set at Drowned Meadow's lower camp, forty minutes' walk down through the pines—and Wendell was moving before the third crackle finished, because in seven years of flying this country he had learned that radios didn't crackle at four in the morning to say anything good.

He keyed the handset. "Go ahead, Meadow."

Static, long enough to make his chest tighten. Then the voice of old Sefa, the camp keeper, thin and flattened by distance: "Wendell. Weather wire came up from the coast relay. Monsoon made landfall last night. Early."

"How early?"

"Nine days." A pause, the sound of a man choosing his words with care because he knew what they'd weigh. "They're saying the front is moving faster than anything in the record books. It'll be over the headwaters inside thirty-six hours. Maybe less."

Wendell looked east, where the pass would be if the clouds weren't sitting on it like a lid. He couldn't see it. That was the whole problem. He couldn't see any of it, and wouldn't be able to until they were already committed.

"Copy," he said. "Anything else on the wire?"

"One more thing." Sefa's voice dropped, went careful. "There's a charter out of Kalimpong registered to a mineral concern. Filed a flight plan yesterday for the eastern valleys. Nobody here knows why a mineral concern needs to fly the eastern valleys in monsoon season."

Wendell closed his eyes. *The buyer's shadow.* Chapter fourteen hadn't happened yet, but its shape was already pressing against them from behind, and now the sky itself was about to close the door in front.

"I'll come down after first light," he said. "Keep the kettle warm."

He sat under the wing for a long time after the radio went quiet, listening to the wind change. It had been blowing out of the west for three days, dry and steady, the kind of wind you could trust. Now it was stuttering, coming in gusts from the south, carrying a smell he knew too well—wet stone, cold iron, the green rot of a country about to drown. The monsoon didn't arrive in this country so much as it *took* it, all at once, like a hand closing.

And they were on the wrong side of the range when it closed.

* * *

By six, the whole crew was awake and gathered around the fire pit, and Wendell laid it out for them the way he'd learned to lay things out in briefing rooms a lifetime ago: plainly, completely, without decoration.

"The window's gone. What we thought we had—five days, maybe six—is now measured in hours. The front hits the headwaters sometime tomorrow night. After that, nothing crosses this range for four months. Nothing flies, nothing walks, nothing crawls. The passes fill with snow above eleven thousand feet and the rivers below go from waist-deep to unrunnable in about six hours. We are standing on the last dry ground on either side of those mountains."

Voss stood with her arms crossed, her face doing what it always did—which was nothing. But Wendell had spent a week reading her now, and he saw the flicker behind the stillness. Not fear exactly. Recalculation. She was a woman who had built her life on the assumption that time could be bought, and someone had just told her the market was closed.

"Then we go now," she said.

"It's not that simple."

"It never is with you, Ishikawa. Make it simple."

Wendell crouched and drew in the dirt with a stick, because some arguments were better made with lines than words. "Here's us. Here's the pass—eighteen miles northeast, top of it at twelve-four. In clear air, in a decent aircraft, it's a forty-minute hop. I've done it in worse than this. But look at the ceiling." He pointed his stick at the ridgeline, invisible above the low cloud. "That cloud deck isn't going to lift. It's going to *lower*. By noon it'll be down to ten thousand. By afternoon, eight. The pass will be inside the cloud, inside the rock, inside the ice. And the monsoon front comes in behind it like a wall."

"So we beat the wall," Voss said.

"We try to beat the wall. And here's what beating it costs." He drew a second line, crossing the first. "If I take off at dawn, I'm climbing into cloud that's already building vertically. Thunderstorms by mid-morning, scattered at first, then not scattered. Ice above eleven. Downdrafts on the lee side that can put a plane into a ridge before the pilot knows he's falling. I have flown this pass in bad weather. I have never flown it in *this*."

Delia spoke from beside the fire, arms wrapped around a tin mug. "What are the odds? Actual numbers. You're the one who does actual numbers."

Wendell hated her a little for asking, and loved her a little for asking it straight, and gave her the truth because she'd earned it.

"Fifty-fifty," he said. "Maybe sixty-forty if the front slows down. Maybe forty-sixty if it doesn't. Those aren't odds I'd bet anyone else's life on. Mine, fine. I've been spending that currency for years without noticing. But there are five of us, and one aircraft, and if we go and it goes wrong, there is no rescue. No one flies into this after the monsoon lands. Whatever happens on that crossing, we handle alone or we don't handle it."

Silence around the fire. Okafor had stopped drawing. Bram stood at the edge of the light with his hands in his pockets, wearing the expression of a man calculating something other than survival odds—or so Wendell thought, and filed away, because there was no time right now to pull that thread.

"We could wait out the winter here," Okafor said quietly. "Drowned Meadow took people in before. The villagers—"

"No." Voss said it flatly, and for once didn't dress it. "We don't winter over. We don't sit in a meadow for four months while—" She stopped herself. Wendell watched her rebuild the wall, brick by brick, the composure sliding back into place like armor being buckled. "While everything we came for sits unguarded on the other side of that range. We go today. We go *now*, while there's still a gap in the cloud, and we stop discussing odds like this is a committee."

"It is a committee," Wendell said. "Six lives ride on my judgment, and I don't spend other people's lives to save time. That's the one thing I've never done and I'll die before I do it."

Something moved across Voss's face then—something old and quick, gone before he could name it. Later he would wonder whether it was memory. Whether she'd heard those same words once before, from a different mouth, on a different night that ended in fire.

"Then tell me your judgment," she said, quieter. "Not your rules. Your judgment."

Wendell looked at the sky. The cloud was already lower than it had been an hour ago; he could see it eating the ridgeline to the south, patient as water rising in a hold. He ran the calculation he'd been running since 4:17, the one he'd hoped would change if he refused to look at it, and it didn't change.

There was a gap. There would be a gap for perhaps three hours after dawn—a slot of merely-bad weather between the dying night storms and the arriving front, a corridor of survivable air threading up the valley and over the shoulder of the pass. He knew the route by heart, every turn and every escape line, because he had flown it in dreams for seven years and in daylight had refused to fly it at all. His hands already knew the control pressures. His memory already held the sound the wind made off the western buttress when a downdraft was waiting on the far side.

And behind them, somewhere over the eastern valleys, a charter with a mineral concern's paperwork was flying a flight plan that meant men with guns and questions, closing at whatever speed money bought.

Stay, and be caught between the winter and the wolves. Go, and gamble five lives against a fifty-fifty sky.

"The answer's yes," he heard himself say, and hated how calm it sounded. "We cross today. First light tomorrow—we stage tonight at the lower camp, fuel full, weight minimum, everything not nailed down left here for the winter. And I want it understood by everyone, once, plainly: I am not promising we get through. I am promising that if we get through, it's because I flew the best crossing of my life, and if we don't, it won't be because anyone in this crew failed. The sky doesn't grade effort."

Delia nodded slowly. Okafor wrote something in his notebook—Wendell didn't ask what. Bram just looked at him, and for a half second the fixer's mask slipped and Wendell saw underneath it something that might have been fear, or might have been the particular loneliness of a man who has just realized which side of a bet he's standing on.

Only Voss smiled. Small, sharp, gone immediately.

"There he is," she said softly. "The man who flew the Salt Rivergate expedition in. I wondered where you'd been hiding."

"I wasn't hiding," Wendell said, turning toward the aircraft. "I was burying something. It's different."

But even as he said it, walking to the plane in the grey pre-dawn with the southern wind smelling of drowned stone, he understood that the burial was over. The monsoon had taken the choice from him the way it took everything else in this country—all at once, like a hand closing. Tomorrow morning he would point his aircraft at the crash corridor he'd avoided for seven years, at the pass where the weather was stacking up like a debt coming due, and he would fly it or die trying, and either way he would finally stop running.

Behind him, the radio at the lower camp crackled once more in the dark—Sefa's voice, urgent now, calling his name.

"Wendell. Come down. Come down now. There's a plane circling the valley."
