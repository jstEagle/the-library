## 13. Delia Confides

Delia had rebuilt forty engines in her life and every one of them had taught her the same lesson: a lie sounds exactly like a loose bearing. It still runs. It still gets you where you're going. But there's a rasp underneath the hum, a note that doesn't belong, and if you're the kind of person who listens—and Delia had been that kind since she was nine years old, lying under her uncle's truck with her ear pressed to the transmission—you can't unhear it.

Bram's lie had a rasp.

She'd caught it three nights ago, at the meadow strip, when the storm had pinned them and everyone else had gone to their bedrolls. She'd come back for her torque wrench, forgotten by the fire ring, and found Bram crouched at the radio with the back panel off and a small screwdriver in his hand, moving with the particular economy of a man doing something he'd practiced. When he'd heard her, he hadn't startled. That was the part that had kept her awake since. A guilty man startles. Bram had simply closed the panel, set down the screwdriver, and said, "Moisture in the contacts. Storm season," in a voice as level as a spirit bubble, and gone back to his bedroll.

She'd checked the radio the next morning. The contacts were clean. Drier than the rest of the aircraft. Someone had been in there and put everything back with care.

She hadn't said anything. Not yet. Because saying something without knowing the shape of the thing you were saying it about—that was how you got people hurt, in her experience, and she'd had enough of that to last the rest of her life. So she'd watched. Bram didn't sleep much, she'd noticed. He walked the perimeter at odd hours. He'd taken to carrying a small canvas pouch she'd never seen before the storm. And twice, when the clouds had thinned enough to show sky, he'd stood at the meadow's eastern edge with his face tipped up, not admiring the view but reading it, the way a man reads a clock.

Tonight the storm had finally broken and the sky over the high valley was rinsed clean, cold enough that their breath hung. Wendell was down at the aircraft doing his evening ritual—Delia had catalogued it by now, the way he walked the plane's length with his palm trailing the fuselage like a man touching the flank of a horse he wasn't sure he trusted—and she decided there would never be a better hour than this one.

She came down the slope with her hands in her pockets and her heart going harder than it should have.

"Wendell."

He looked up. In the last of the light his face was all planes and shadow, a face like terrain, and she thought—not for the first time—that he was a man who had crashed somewhere inside himself years ago and never filed the report.

"Got a minute?"

"For you? I've got an hour." He said it lightly, but his eyes did the thing they did, the quick read, the instrument sweep. He'd noticed her tone. Of course he had. "What's wrong?"

"I need to show you something. And I need you to hear all of it before you do anything."

His palm came off the fuselage. "That's a hell of an opening, Delia."

"Yeah," she said. "It is."

* * *

She told him standing between the aircraft's wing and the dark, her voice low, the words coming out in the order she'd rehearsed them and then, halfway through, abandoning the rehearsal entirely and just telling it straight, the way she'd tell a customer their engine was worse than they'd hoped.

"Bram. Three nights ago. Back panel of the radio off, working on it with a screwdriver at eleven at night. Said it was moisture." She watched Wendell's face as she said it, and watched the light change there—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. That frightened her more than surprise would have.

"You saw it yourself."

"Saw it myself. Checked the panel the next morning. Contacts clean. Cleaner than clean." She paused. "You don't look shocked."

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere down the meadow, water ran in the ditch the storm had cut, a thin constant voice in the dark.

"I don't sleep much either," he said finally. "And I've been watching him since the last town. The way he stands when he thinks no one's looking. The photograph." He rubbed his jaw. "I told myself I was being paranoid. Seven years of flying alone does that to you. You start reading ghosts into every shadow."

"It's not ghosts."

"No," he said. "It's not ghosts." He turned and looked back up the slope toward the fire ring, where Bram's bedroll was a pale shape in the gloom, empty—of course empty; Bram was out there somewhere in the dark doing whatever it was he did. "Okay. You were right to come to me. And you were right not to shout it from the ridgeline first. What do you want to do?"

"That's why I came to you and not Voss."

Something moved through his face at that—fast, quickly buried, but she caught it. He didn't trust Voss either. Good. Neither did she, though for different reasons. Delia trusted her hands and her eyes and, increasingly, this quiet ruined pilot who checked his landing gear twice and thanked his airplane under his breath.

"Here's what's eating me," she said. "If Bram's reporting to somebody, the radio's the wrong tool. I've been over that set twice since the storm. It's clean now—whatever he did, he undid it. But a radio transmission from here, we'd hear the traffic, we'd see the power draw on the battery log, and I keep the log. There's nothing." She let that sit. "Which means either he's very good—"

"Or there's something else."

"Or there's something else. And if there's something else, it's not on the plane, because I've been through the plane like a lice comb. Which leaves—"

"The cargo," Wendell said.

They looked at each other. The cargo crates sat in a row under the wing tarp, seven of them, lashed and tarped and untouched since the last town, because Voss's manifest said survey equipment and survey equipment was heavy and nobody's business. Okafor's theodolite cases. Voss's sample boxes. Bram's own duffel.

"His duffel's not on the manifest," Delia said. "It's listed as personal effects. Nobody checks personal effects."

"Nobody checks personal effects," Wendell agreed.

They stood there a moment longer, both of them listening to the ditch water, both of them aware that what they were about to do was a line, and that once you crossed a line in company like this you didn't get to step back over it.

"Voss?" Delia asked. "Okafor?"

"Not yet." Wendell said it slowly, weighing each word like ballast. "If Bram's working alone, we spook him and lose the thread. If he's not working alone—if somebody in that camp is his contact—then the moment we say the word transmitter out loud, we're two people with a secret and a short future. We look first. Quietly. Tonight. Then we decide who else needs to know."

"Okafor's straight," Delia said. "I'd bet my tools on it."

"Okafor's straight," Wendell agreed, "and Okafor is also the worst liar on this expedition. If he knows, it'll be written on him in letters a meter tall." He pulled the flashlight from his jacket, checked the lens with his thumb. "You and me. Right now, while he's out walking. We go through those crates, and if we find nothing, we say nothing and I start sleeping with a wrench."

"And if we find something?"

Wendell looked at her, and in the dark his face was hard to read, but his voice wasn't.

"Then fear shared is fear halved," he said. "My mother used to say that. She was wrong about most things, but she was right about that."

* * *

They worked by feel and by the flashlight's hooded spill, Delia's hands and Wendell's, kneeling in the wet grass under the wing with the tarp lifted just enough. The first two crates were what they claimed to be: Okafor's theodolite, wrapped in oilcloth; brass fittings; boxes of plotting paper gone soft with damp. The third held Voss's sample cases, empty, waiting for rock that hadn't been collected yet. Delia found herself relaxing by degrees, hating herself for it, knowing better. The fourth crate was heavier than it should have been.

She felt it before she saw it—the wrongness in the way it resisted when they shifted it, the balance off. She and Wendell exchanged a look. He worked the lid latches. Inside, on top of a false layer of packing straw, sat six crates within the crate: squat metal boxes, olive-drab, military in the way that nothing on a survey expedition had any business being military. Stenciled markings, mostly worn. Delia's hands knew hardware the way a priest knows scripture, and her hands were telling her what these were before her mind caught up.

"Those are transmitter housings," she whispered. "Old ones. Pre-war field sets, stripped down. Somebody's built something out of these."

Wendell lifted one. It was lighter than it looked—gutted. He turned it over, found the panel seam, and looked at her. She nodded. He opened it.

Inside: not a radio. Not exactly. A transmitter, yes, but small, modern, married to the old housing like a new heart sewn into an old chest. A battery pack. An antenna socket with a whip antenna coiled beside it, greased and wrapped. And a clock—a small mechanical timer, the kind that could be set to wake the set up for thirty seconds every six hours and put it back to sleep before anyone's ears caught it.

Delia felt the cold go through her all the way to the spine.

"Six-hour bursts," she breathed. "Short enough to miss. Timed. Somebody's been logging our position every six hours since—" She stopped. Did the arithmetic. "Since the last town. Maybe since before."

"Since before," Wendell said quietly. He was very still, the flashlight cupped in his palm, and she watched him do his own arithmetic, the longer sum. "Every airstrip. Every pass. Every place we've slept." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it got quieter. "Someone knows this route better than I do. Someone's been reading it off a map that updates every six hours."

Delia looked at the five remaining housings. Five more sets. Spares—or something else. She reached for the second one, and Wendell's hand closed on her wrist.

"Don't," he said. "We don't touch anything else. We put this back exactly how it sits. If he checks—and a man like that checks—we want him to see nothing."

"Then what do we—"

"We take the knowledge and leave the hardware." He was already lowering the housing back into its straw, aligning it by the marks his fingers had memorized. "Delia. Listen to me. This changes the shape of everything. This isn't a rival outfit trying to beat us to a salvage claim. You don't spend hardware like this on a rival. Six-hour position bursts, military housings, a man on the inside who doesn't startle when you catch him—" He set the lid down without a sound. "Somebody out there isn't racing us. Somebody's herding us."

The word landed in Delia's chest like a dropped wrench.

"Herding us where?"

"I don't know. But I know where we're going, and I know we were never the ones choosing the route." He sat back on his heels, and for a moment the flashlight's hooded glow caught his face, and she saw something in it she hadn't seen in seven days of flying with him—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has heard a sound he heard once before, a long time ago, on the worst night of his life.

"Voss," Delia said. "Does Voss know?"

"That," Wendell said, "is the question, isn't it. Either she's the buyer's instrument and doesn't know it, or she knows exactly what she's carrying and we're all cargo." He pressed the tarp back down, weighted the edge with a stone, and for a moment his hand stayed on it, flat, as if holding something down that wanted to rise. "Either way, we say nothing tonight. Not to Voss. Not to Okafor. Not yet. Tomorrow I'll find a reason to put us down at the fuel cache a day early, and we'll get a message out on a set Bram's never touched—my old backup, the one I keep in the tail locker. There's a man in the valley at Rivergate Mouth who owes me and doesn't ask questions."

"And Bram?"

Wendell stood. Down the meadow, a shape moved along the tree line—Bram, walking his perimeter, unhurried, patient as a bearing.

"Bram," Wendell said, "keeps walking. We let him think the night's as blind as it's always been. Because here's the thing, Delia—" He turned to her, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, and she had to lean in to catch it. "Whoever's on the other end of that transmitter thinks they can see us. And for the first time since the last town, that means we can see them. We know what they know. We can feed a man who's watching."

Delia looked at the tarp, at the crates sleeping under it with their sewn-in hearts, at the dark where Bram walked.

"You want to lie to a liar."

"I want to fly into weather with a working compass while he flies blind thinking he's got ours." Wendell almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Get some sleep. You did the hard part tonight. You listened."

Delia walked back up the slope alone, and the fear was still there—it would be there tomorrow, and the day after, a cold wire running through everything—but it was different now, the way he'd promised. Halved. She lay in her bedroll looking up at the rinsed stars and found herself thinking about the village where the children had known Wendell's name, and about the way he'd touched the fuselage tonight before she'd called to him, and about the strange arithmetic of trust: that she'd bet her life tonight on a man she'd known a week, because she'd watched his hands, and hands don't lie the way voices do.

Across the dead fire, Bram's bedroll stayed empty a long time.

And in the last hour before dawn, far to the east, past the broken teeth of the range, an aircraft that had no business flying in monsoon season lifted out of a valley none of them had ever seen—and turned toward the mountains.
