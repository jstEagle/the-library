## 14. The Buyer's Shadow

Bram had a rule about being followed: you never looked directly at the tail. You let it live in your peripheral vision, in the reflection off Okafor's compass case, in the way Wendell's eyes kept flicking to the mirror on the port side and then deliberately away. Looking confirmed. Not looking gave you time.

They were an hour out of Drowned Meadow, climbing through broken cloud toward the second pass, when the shadow came back.

It had been there at dawn too—a glint above the cloud deck, gone before Delia finished her preflight. Bram had filed it under coincidence then. Charter traffic, a mail plane, a survey bird. The interior was full of aircraft that meant nothing.

But now it held position four miles back and two thousand up, matching their heading, matching their airspeed, and it did not close and it did not fall away. That was not curiosity. That was work.

Bram sat in the jump seat behind the pilot seats with his hands folded, doing what he did best, which was nothing at all. He'd spent eleven years being unremarkable in rooms where remarkability got people killed. He knew how to be furniture. Right now he was very good furniture, and inside the furniture his mind was running arithmetic it didn't like.

The radio. The crates. The frequency he'd found wedged into the cargo netting three nights ago, transmitting on a schedule he hadn't set—or rather, that he *had* set, weeks ago, in a hotel room in the capital, before any of this had faces attached to it.

He'd been hired to track. That was all. A man named Ferro—soft voice, rimless glasses, the kind of face you forgot while shaking your hand—had laid out photographs of the crew on a table: Voss, Okafor, Delia, the pilot Ishikawa. Daily position reports by shortwave, encrypted, thirty seconds each. No interference with the expedition. No contact. "You're a lighthouse," Ferro had said, "not a hand on the tiller." And the money had been the kind of money that made a man stop asking why a lighthouse needed building.

Bram had done this kind of work before. Asset tracking, mostly—mining concerns wanting to know where their geologists really went, insurance syndicates auditing claims in places without auditors. Clean work, as such things went. Nobody bled. You watched, you reported, you collected, and somewhere far away men in good suits made decisions about information you'd never see whole.

So why, then, had Ferro's instructions included a dead-drop protocol? Why the secondary frequency, the one Bram was supposed to switch to only if the primary went silent for more than forty-eight hours? Lighthouses didn't need fail-safes. Fail-safes meant someone expected the light to go out.

And why, when Bram had checked the transmitter's log that morning—while Delia was arguing with Wendell about fuel burn—had he found entries he hadn't sent?

* * *

"Contact again," Delia said quietly. She didn't point. She'd learned that much already, or she'd known it coming in. "Two o'clock high, in the gap between decks."

Wendell's hands stayed easy on the yoke. Bram watched him work the problem the way a man works a familiar lock—by feel, no wasted motion. "Same altitude band as this morning?"

"Maybe five hundred lower. Closing to three miles." Delia paused. "It's matching our turns. We rolled left for weather and it rolled left."

"Could be anyone going anywhere."

"It could," Delia said, in a voice that meant it wasn't.

Voss leaned forward from the second row, one hand braced on the seatback. Up here, with the wind hammering the fuselage and the mountains stacking white and gray ahead of them, her certainty looked thinner than it had in the lowlands. "Can you identify it?"

"Not without getting closer, and I'm not getting closer." Wendell's eyes flicked to the mirror again. "Single-engine, high wing, long-range tanks by the way it sits in the air. Somebody paid for endurance, not speed. Somebody who wanted to stay with us all day."

"Somebody," Voss said, "who knows where we're going."

Nobody answered that, which was answer enough.

Bram felt the sentence land on him like a hand on the back of the neck. *Somebody who knows.* Because somebody did know—not the destination, maybe, not yet, but the route, the timing, the fact that a debt-ridden pilot and a woman buying redemption were threading the range ahead of the monsoon. Somebody had known since the capital. Since the table with the photographs. Since Ferro's soft voice saying *daily reports, Mr. Bram, and nothing else.*

He'd told himself the sabotage suspicion was paranoia—Delia's, mostly, though he'd seen it growing in Okafor's careful silences too. He'd told himself tracking was passive, harmless, a lighthouse. But sitting in the jump seat with a chartered plane holding station behind them like a promise, Bram understood something with the cold clarity of a man reading his own autopsy: he had never been the watcher. He had been the instrument. And instruments get read by whoever holds them—and discarded, or worse, when they've given up their readings.

Ferro had said *no interference*. But Ferro worked for someone, and Bram had never met that someone, and the chain of custody on his own orders had always ended in fog. Now the fog was flying formation off their wing.

* * *

They put down at a shelf strip above a river gorge to wait out a cell building over the pass—a gravel bench barely long enough, wind quartering across it, the kind of landing Wendell made look boring, which Bram understood was the highest compliment flying had. The plane behind them overflew the field once, high, unhurried, and slid away east without landing.

"That's worse," Delia said, watching it go. "If it lands, it's a rival. If it flies off, it's reporting."

"You've thought about this," Wendell said.

"I've been thinking about it since the radio started lying to us."

Bram kept his face still. Delia hadn't said *since Bram touched the radio*. She'd said *since the radio started lying*, which was a kindness, or a trap, or both. He couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing was starting to cost him sleep he wasn't getting anyway.

They took shelter under the wing with tea from a thermos, because Voss insisted on ritual even now—said ritual was what separated expeditions from flights of fancy. Okafor spread his maps on a crate and weighted the corners with stones. The wind gusted. Somewhere below the shelf, the river ran brown and swollen with early melt.

"The monsoon's early," Okafor said. "Radio this morning. Landfall south of the delta, moving north faster than the models. We have a window measured in hours now, not days."

"Then we fly through it," Voss said.

"We fly *around* it or we don't fly," Wendell said. "There's a difference between courage and suicide, and I've buried enough friends to know the line."

Bram watched Voss take that. Watched her jaw set and then, surprisingly, loosen. "Around, then. Show me around."

While they argued routes—Okafor tracing contour lines with a blunt finger, Wendell overriding half of them from memory, the two of them discovering a grudging grammar of shared expertise—Bram drifted to the tail of the aircraft on the pretext of checking the tie-downs. The pretext was even true; he did check them. But then he crouched by the cargo hatch, out of the sightline of the others, and took out the little receiver he'd built himself, the one tuned to his own primary frequency, and listened to the last scheduled transmission he'd sent.

Except it wasn't the one he'd sent.

His report had been terse, per protocol: position, heading, condition of party, weather. What played back through his earpiece—recorded in the transmitter's buffer, timestamped after his transmission, sent onward under his authentication code—was longer. It included details he had never reported. The bent gear at Drowned Meadow. The state of Okafor's maps. A phrase that turned Bram's blood to ice water:

*"Subject pilot demonstrates familiarity with crash corridor. Recommend assessment of prior association with original expedition."*

Someone else was writing his reports. Someone with access to the equipment, the codes, the channel. Someone who had been feeding a picture of this expedition to a buyer—enriching it, editing it, steering it—and letting Bram believe he was the author of his own surveillance.

He was not a lighthouse. He was a decoy lamp, burning bright so nobody would notice the real navigation happening in the dark.

Bram closed his fist around the receiver until the casing bit his palm. Eleven years of clean work. Eleven years of *nobody bleeds*. And the whole time, apparently, he'd been the bleed-valve—the expendable asset whose transmissions could be forged, whose cover could be burned, whose presence in the crew could be revealed at exactly the moment it became useful to reveal it. If the buyer's people moved against this expedition, the first thing they'd do was expose him. Turn the crew against the traitor in their midst. Watch the alliance shatter from inside while the charter plane circled overhead.

He'd seen the technique used on other people. He'd never imagined standing inside it.

"Bram."

Delia's voice. He pocketed the receiver and stood, arranging his face into its usual mild blankness. She stood ten feet away with her arms crossed, tea steaming in one hand, wind pulling at her jacket.

"Tie-downs are fine," he said.

"I didn't ask about tie-downs." She came closer, lowered her voice beneath the wind. "I asked your name, and you gave me a sentence instead. I've watched you check that cargo bay every morning like a priest counting relics. I watched you flinch when the charter overflew—not at the plane, at *yourself*. So here's what I think." Her eyes were steady and not unkind, which was somehow worse than anger. "I think you're not working against us. I think you're working for someone who is, and you've just figured out they don't need you anymore."

Bram said nothing. There was nothing true enough to say that wouldn't end with him walking off this mountain alone, and possibly not walking at all.

"I'm not telling Wendell," Delia said. "Not yet. Because right now you're more useful scared than exiled. But understand me, Bram." She stepped in, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her eyes, the engine grease ground permanently into her knuckles. "Whatever leash you're on—I'd find out who's holding it. Because leashes run both directions, and somebody's about to pull."

She walked back to the map crate. Bram stood a moment longer by the tail, palm stinging where the casing had bitten it, and admitted the thing he'd been circling for three days:

He didn't know who held the other end anymore. Ferro had been a voice, a middleman, a glove over someone's hand. The forged reports proved the hand reached past him, around him, through him. Every assumption he'd built his contract on—clean work, passive work, *lighthouse* work—was rubble. Which meant the money in his account wasn't payment. It was bait. He was the fish already hooked, and the rod was bending.

* * *

They flew the northern detour late in the day, skirting the storm cell along a valley Wendell clearly knew the way a man knows the lines of an old lover's face—calling out rock spires and blind corners before they appeared, easing the load around turbulence he smelled coming. Behind them, at the edge of radar range and the edge of eyesight, the charter kept its distance and kept its schedule.

At dusk they landed at a mission strip on a river terrace, a grass field with a windsock faded to the color of old bone, and a lean missionary with a rifle he carried like a garden tool waved them in. While Delia fueled from drums and Okafor argued coordinates with the missionary's wall chart, Bram walked to the edge of the terrace under the pretense of stretching his legs.

The radio shack was a shed with a corrugated roof. The missionary's assistant, a boy of perhaps sixteen, let him in for the price of a story about the outside world. Bram waited until the boy was busy with the generator, then keyed the secondary frequency—the fail-safe, the one for emergencies, the one Ferro had sworn would never be needed.

Static. Then a voice, synthesized, flat, wrong in a way that raised the hair on his arms.

*"Asset Bram. Primary channel compromised by third-party injection. All previous directives rescinded. New directive: confirm salvage status at destination. Do not return. Do not attempt extraction. Further instructions will follow on this frequency only."*

A pause, long enough for Bram to start breathing again.

*"Note: your compensation account has been frozen pending completion. Non-completion will be interpreted accordingly."*

The channel went dead.

Bram stood in the dimness of the shack, the generator thudding outside, and felt the last thread of his old profession snap quietly in his chest. Frozen accounts. Rescinded extraction. *Interpreted accordingly.* He had heard those words used about other men, men who then stopped appearing in photographs, and he had collected his fees and told himself the trade was clean.

Now the trade had turned its attention on him, and it was not clean at all. He was stranded eight days' travel from anywhere, embedded in a crew that had every reason to hate him, employed by a buyer who had just erased the exit, and shadowed by a charter plane whose pilot had orders Bram could guess but not prove.

There was exactly one move left on the board, and it was the one he'd spent eleven years avoiding: telling the truth to people who might use it against him.

He walked out onto the terrace. The evening light lay gold along the river. At the aircraft, Wendell was showing the missionary's boy how the propeller feathered, patient, hands moving with a gentleness Bram had never bothered to earn from anyone. Delia was laughing at something. Even Voss looked almost human in the last light.

Bram crossed the grass toward them, and with every step the old arithmetic—self-preservation first, truth as currency, loyalty as a line item—lost another decimal place.

"Wendell," he said, when he was close enough. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "We need to talk. All of you. And you're not going to like any of it."

Behind them, far to the east, above the darkening ridgeline, a single navigation light blinked twice—acknowledgment, or warning, or simply a hand testing the leash—and slid away into the night.
