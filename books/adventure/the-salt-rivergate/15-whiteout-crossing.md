## 15. Whiteout Crossing

They left the meadow strip at first light, before the storm could finish deciding to kill them, and before the charter plane's shadow could find them on the ground.

Wendell had been awake since three, standing at the edge of the meadow in the blue-black cold, watching the stars over the pass. Stars meant clear air above. Clear air above meant a window, and windows in this country were doors that slammed. He'd counted the hours the way he counted everything now—against fuel, against weather, against the chartered aircraft that had been pacing them for two days like something patient and hungry.

By four he'd woken Delia. By five they'd pulled the covers off the engine and he'd run the preheat while she worked by lamplight, her breath smoking, her hands sure on the cowling latches. By five-thirty the others were up, silent, folding camp with the economy of people who understood that some departures were arguments you won by not having them.

Voss came last, as always, her pack already shouldered, her face unreadable in the gray light.

"You're going early," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going before the storm closes the pass and before whoever's flying that charter decides today is the day." Wendell checked the tie-downs a final time. "We beat both or we don't fly."

"And if we don't fly?"

"Then we walk two hundred kilometers through what's coming down out of the north." He looked at her. "I've done it. I don't recommend it."

She didn't argue. That was new, and he didn't trust it.

* * *

The loading was a ritual he knew by heart: Okafor's maps wrapped in oilcloth and strapped flat; the water cans braced; Bram riding shotgun with the radio on his knees because he was the only one besides Wendell who could work it and because Wendell wanted him where he could see him. Delia took the jump seat behind the cargo net, one hand resting on the frame the way she did, as if the airplane were an animal that might startle.

"Weight and balance," Wendell said, more to himself than anyone. He'd done the math twice. The strip was short, soft from yesterday's rain, and the climb-out would take them straight at the wall of the range. Everything about the morning was arithmetic with no good answers, only less-bad ones.

He ran up the engine. It caught on the third blade, coughed, settled into its old uneven heartbeat. Seven years of that sound. Seven years of small cargoes and careful routines, all of it funneling toward this: a loaded airplane, a closing sky, and a canyon he'd sworn never to fly again.

"Everyone strapped?" Heads nodded. He turned forward. "Then we go."

The takeoff was ugly and perfect. The wheels left the meadow grass heavy and reluctant, and for three long seconds the airplane simply refused to climb, wallowing in the cold dense air with the ridge growing in the windshield—and then the lift came, sudden as a hand under the tail, and they were up, skimming the tree line, the meadow shrinking behind them into a green scar in the wilderness.

Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the engine noise wouldn't drown anyway.

Wendell climbed in a wide spiral, trading distance for altitude, watching the western sky where the storm was building. It didn't look like weather yet. It looked like a rumor of weather—a bruise along the horizon, purple-gray, organized. Storms like that one moved like armies. You didn't outrun them. You slipped past their flank and hoped they hadn't posted sentries in the pass.

"Charter?" he asked over his shoulder.

Bram had the receiver pressed to his ear, one hand turning the dial in slow increments. Static, static, then a burst of voice—thin, distorted, gone. He shook his head. "Can't raise anything. Either they're below us or they're quiet."

"Quiet's worse," Delia said.

"Quiet's worse," Wendell agreed.

* * *

The pass opened ahead of them an hour later, and it was worse than he remembered, which was saying something, because he remembered it every night.

Two granite walls, close enough to spit across in places, rising so steeply that the glacier between them looked like a river of frozen milk poured down a throat. At the head of the valley, the notch itself—a saddle between two peaks, maybe nine hundred meters of clearance on a generous day, and today was not shaping up to be generous. Cloud already sagged over the saddle like wet wool laid across a doorway.

"We go through that?" Okafor leaned forward between the seats. His voice was carefully neutral, the voice of a man reading instruments rather than looking at mountains.

"We go through that."

"The ceiling looks—"

"I know what it looks like." Wendell kept his eyes moving: walls, saddle, cloud, walls. "It looks worse from here than it is. The wind funnels through the notch and scours the cloud off the saddle most mornings. We get there in the next forty minutes, we thread it. After that—" He shrugged, deliberately casual, and hated how much effort the shrug cost. "After that we're committed."

"Committed to what?"

"To the other side."

Delia laughed once, short and sharp, without humor. "That's not commitment, that's a coin toss."

"It's a calculation," Wendell said. "There's a difference. Coin tosses don't have escape routes. This does. If the saddle closes, I turn us around inside the canyon—we've got the width for it until the narrows, and after the narrows there's a bench at kilometer eleven where I can put us down if I have to."

"You've landed there?"

"No. Elio surveyed it." The name came out of him before he could stop it, and the cockpit went very quiet. He felt the crew exchange glances behind him like heat off a stove. "He said it would hold a light aircraft in an emergency. I'm choosing to believe him, because the alternative is choosing not to believe him, and I've spent seven years doing that and it hasn't helped."

No one answered that. Ahead, the canyon mouth swallowed the horizon.

* * *

The first hour was flying as craft, and Wendell loved it the way he always loved it—the way a surgeon loves a clean incision, privately, professionally, without needing anyone to see. The canyon demanded everything. Wind poured off the walls in sheets and fell back in downdrafts that hit the wings like dropped stones; he flew the corrections with his hands and his feet and the backs of his eyes, the airplane an extension of a nervous system seven years in the making. Behind him the crew rode in silence broken only by Okafor's occasional, involuntary exhale when a wingtip seemed to graze stone.

It didn't. Nothing grazed. That was the point. That was the whole art: to make death look like near-misses and near-misses look like routine.

At the narrows the walls pinched to less than two hundred meters and the wind turned chaotic, bouncing between the rock faces, and Wendell slowed the airplane until it hung on the edge of a stall, riding the turbulence like a man walking a log across a river—one foot, weight, the next foot. Ice began to form on the leading edges. He saw it first as a dulling of the wing's shine, then as ridges, tiny white teeth growing backward into the airflow. The stall warning chirped once, a small electronic opinion he did not ask for.

"Ice," Delia said quietly. She'd seen it too.

"I see it."

"How bad?"

"Manageable." It was true, mostly. The carburetor heat was on, the prop was throwing slush in yellow arcs off the spinner, and the wings were shedding more than they caught—for now. But the air temperature was falling as they climbed toward the saddle, and ice was a debt too. Everything in this country was a debt. "Ask me again in ten minutes."

At minute six the downdraft found them.

It came off the eastern wall without warning—a column of sinking air the size of a cathedral, cold and solid as water, and the airplane simply began to fall. Not the floating drop of turbulence but a driven descent, hundreds of feet per minute, then a thousand, the altimeter unwinding like a madman telling a story. The floor of the canyon came up. The glacier came up. Wendell shoved the nose down to keep flying speed, pushed the throttle through its gate, and the engine roared and the airplane kept falling.

"Floor!" Okafor shouted, though Wendell had already seen it—rock and ice streaming beneath them, close enough to count crevasses.

He rolled toward the center of the canyon where the sink would be weakest, dove to trade altitude for airspeed, and pulled the airplane out of the dive thirty meters above the ice with the stall horn singing and everyone's stomach somewhere around their boots. Then he held it there, low and fast, skimming the glacier in the canyon's cold core, where the air was densest and the wings bit hardest.

For ten seconds nobody breathed.

"Again," Voss said finally, from the back. Her voice was steady. Too steady. "Is that going to happen again?"

"Yes." Wendell leveled off, climbing gently, feeling the ice on the wings change the airplane's voice, making it sluggish, sullen. "Probably worse higher up. The sink accelerates near the saddle. So we stay low, we stay in the middle, and we don't stop flying. Ever. Nobody stops flying in this canyon."

"You're the only one flying," Bram said.

"Then nobody distract the only one flying."

* * *

The saddle appeared at last through the ragged cloud, and it was half open—half closed, depending on how you priced hope. A river of cloud poured over the notch from the north, thickening even as they watched, but beneath it, hugging the saddle itself, was a corridor of scoured air perhaps two hundred meters deep. Through it, distantly, he could see the far side: descending valleys, brown and gold, the watershed opening like a hand.

Through it. Or nothing.

"Storm's maybe ninety minutes behind us," Wendell said. "Charter's somewhere behind that. This window doesn't reopen. If we turn back now we land, we wait out the monsoon's advance guard, and they find us on the ground with all the time in the world." He looked at the corridor of air, at the cloud boiling slowly over it, eating it meter by meter. "Or we go now."

"Your airplane," Voss said. "Your call."

And there it was—the whole strange architecture of the last two weeks compressed into four words. His airplane. His debt. His dead friend's canyon, his dead friend's bench at kilometer eleven, his dead friend's ghost sitting beside him in the right seat where he'd sat a hundred times, saying *you've got it, Wendell, you've got it, just fly the airplane.*

"All right," Wendell said. "Everybody breathe. When I say so, nobody talk."

He turned into the saddle's approach, lined up on the corridor of scoured air, and flew at it the way you'd swim at a wave—not fighting it, not fleeing it, meeting it exactly where it broke.

The cloud took them.

The world went white—not gray, not dim, but absolute, featureless white, the kind of white that erases up and down and left and right and leaves only the instruments and your own heartbeat. Ice built on the wings in earnest now, and the airplane grew heavier, drunker, answering the controls a half-beat late. The downdraft grabbed them twice more and Wendell fought it with power and attitude and pure stubbornness, holding the climb profile he'd drawn in his head, the numbers ticking past—altitude, altitude, altitude—while the white screamed silently on every side.

Thirty seconds. Forty. In a whiteout, time stops being time and becomes endurance.

Then the cloud thinned, tore, and the far side of the range unrolled beneath them—valleys running gold and brown with the first stain of autumn, rivers braiding silver through the floors, and ahead, far ahead, catching the low sun, a line of glitter that could only be the upper watershed.

The Salt River country. He was looking at it for the first time in seven years.

Behind him someone made a sound—not quite a cheer, not quite a sob. Delia, maybe. Or all of them at once.

Wendell flew on, hands steady, heart hammering, and let himself feel it for exactly three seconds: the terror transmuted, the fear burned clean down to something almost like joy. The finest flying of his life, and no one would ever read about it, and that was fine. That had always been fine.

Then he saw the smoke.

A thin thread of it, rising from the valley floor twenty kilometers ahead, right where the trail to the headwaters crossed the river—too deliberate to be a village cooking fire, too fresh to be anything old. Someone was down there. Someone had come over the range ahead of them, or around it, and was waiting on the route they had no choice but to take.

"Bram," Wendell said quietly. "Get me that radio working. I want to know who owns that fire."

Behind him, in the silence, he heard Bram pick up the handset—and hesitate.
