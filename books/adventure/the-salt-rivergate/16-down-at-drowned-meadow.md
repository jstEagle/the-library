## 16. Down at Drowned Meadow

The first thing Adaeze Okafor learned about flying with Wendell Ishikawa was that he talked to his aircraft. Not the way pilots did in the survey corps—checklists murmured like prayers—but conversationally, as if the plane were an old colleague who'd earned the right to hear his complaints.

"Come on, girl," he said now, hands light on the yoke, "don't do this to me today."

The second thing she learned was that when he stopped talking, they were all going to die.

He'd been talking for two hours. Through the whiteout crossing—through ice feathering across the windscreen and a downdraft that had pressed her stomach into her spine while the canyon walls closed around them like a book—he had narrated steadily: *trim's fighting me, we're heavy on the left tank, Delia watch that gauge.* The words had been a rope thrown to all of them, something to hold while the world outside the glass went gray and vertical.

Now, descending through a last layer of cloud toward a green smear that the map called nothing at all, Wendell had gone quiet.

Okafor leaned forward between the seats. Below them lay a meadow the color of wet felt, ringed by scree slopes still holding winter in their shadows. A river braided through it in silver threads. It looked, from two thousand feet, almost gentle.

"It's soft ground," she said. "Saturated."

"I know."

"The drainage pattern suggests—" She caught herself. "It's a marsh. That whole flat is a marsh wearing a meadow's clothes."

"Also know." Wendell's eyes moved constantly: airspeed, altimeter, the scree, the river, the clouds stacking up behind them like furniture being carried upstairs. "Question is whether it's a marsh I can land on or a marsh I can drown in. There's a gravel bar along the east braid. See it? Where the water splits."

She saw it. A pale ribbon, maybe three hundred meters long, maybe less. Maybe wide enough. Maybe.

"Wind's quartering off the glacier," Delia said from behind him. Her voice was flat, professional, but her knuckles were white where she gripped the seat frame. "You'll be landing crosswind on gravel the width of a runway someone forgot to finish."

"Story of my life." Wendell banked, lined up. "Bram, get everything loose strapped down. Voss, sit on the journal case. If we go over, it stays under you, not under you."

In the back, Marguerite Voss said nothing. She had said very little since the pass—since the whiteout, since Wendell had flown them through a canyon by memory alone, threading stone with a confidence that had frightened Okafor more than the ice had, because it meant he'd done it before, or dreamed it, or both. Voss sat with the journal case clamped between her boots and her hands folded on top of it, and her face wore the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

Okafor knew that expression. She saw it in mirrors, in colleagues, in the government men who came to review her surveys and found the maps didn't say what the ministry wanted them to say. It was the face of someone whose numbers had stopped adding up.

The meadow rose to meet them.

* * *

Later, Okafor would try to reconstruct the landing from the fragments her body kept: the lurch of the flare, the first kiss of wheels on gravel—a sound like a struck match—and then the sound after, which no one should ever have to hear beneath them, the deep wet crunch of the left main gear punching through the crust of the bar into the marrow of the marsh.

The world tilted. The propeller bit gravel and screamed a note she felt in her teeth. Something in the fuselage let go with a bang, and then they were slewing, the horizon spinning past the windows in great lazy arcs, grass and water and sky trading places, until the wing dropped, kissed the earth, and plowed a furrow that brought them all to a stop so hard her harness printed itself into her collarbones.

Silence. Real silence, engine-silence, the kind that rang.

"Everyone," Wendell said, already unbuckling, "out. Now. Slowly, but now."

They climbed down onto ground that trembled faintly, like a sleeping animal's flank. The aircraft sat canted hard to port, left gear buried to the axle in black muck, propeller bent into a shape like a question mark. Fuel smell hung thin over the meadow—no leak yet, but Okafor's nose promised one eventually.

Delia circled the wreck of the landing gear twice, then crouched and put her hand flat on the mud as if taking its pulse. When she stood, she wiped her hand on her thigh with great deliberation.

"Axle housing's bent. Strut's driven back into the well." She looked at Wendell. "I could straighten it. Given a machine shop, a hydraulic press, and about a week."

"We don't have a week."

"No," Delia said. "We don't."

Okafor turned slowly, surveyor's habit, reading the land the way she read any land: contours first, water second, escape routes third. The meadow sat in a shallow bowl at perhaps eleven thousand feet, above the treeline, which here meant above firewood, above shelter, above most things that lived. The braided river ran north-northwest, toward—she oriented herself against the sun's smothered disk—the headwaters. Toward the Salt Rivergate. Perhaps five days' walk in good weather.

Behind them, to the south, the weather they'd crossed ahead of was arriving with the patience of a creditor. The cloud deck had lowered while they landed; the scree slopes were already losing their summits into gray.

"How far to the gate?" Voss asked.

"Five days," Okafor said. "Four if the river braids favor us. Seven if the monsoon catches us in the open."

"And the monsoon?"

Okafor looked at the southern sky, at the wall of it building there, purple-black and organized, the kind of weather that had intent. "Is catching us."

* * *

They salvaged what they could in the next hour, working in the particular silence of people who have just understood their situation. Tents, rations for nine days if they stretched them, the medical kit, ropes, the fuel remaining in the wing tanks siphoned into jerry cans—for the stove, Delia said, not for any engine, because there would be no engine. Bram carried crates without being asked, moving with an economy that made Okafor watch him longer than she meant to. He handled the radio crate differently than the others. Lighter, she thought. Like something already emptied.

She filed that away with the other things she'd filed: the maps predating the official record, Voss's knowledge of the gate's location, the stranger photographing their aircraft in the last town. Her conscience had been itching for days now, a low-grade fever of doubt. Surveyors were trained to notice anomalies—to ask why the contour lines didn't match the ground. Lately the entire expedition refused to match its own map.

Wendell stood apart, at the aircraft's nose, one palm resting on the cold cowling. He stayed there a long time. Okafor drifted close enough to hear him.

"You got us down," he told the plane quietly. "That's what matters. You got us down."

She looked away. Some griefs you weren't supposed to witness.

Voss joined her at the edge of the gravel bar, arms crossed, watching the southern sky eat the peaks one ridge at a time.

"Your assessment," Voss said. It wasn't quite a request.

"We walk. Tonight, before the rain makes the river crossings lethal. We follow the east braid north to where the valley narrows, then take the high bench on the left side—less flood exposure, better footing. Five days." Okafor paused. "Assuming the monsoon behaves like the historical record says it should."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then the record is wrong, and I'll want to know why, because I wrote part of it."

Something flickered across Voss's face—not amusement exactly, but the ghost of respect, the way one professional acknowledges another's stubbornness. "You really do love the data."

"The data is the only thing that doesn't lie to you," Okafor said. "People lie constantly. Maps lie occasionally. Data lies only when someone has edited it, and then it lies in a direction, and you can follow the lie back to the editor." She nodded south, at the storm. "That is lying in a direction."

Voss followed her gaze. For a moment her armor slipped and Okafor saw underneath: not ambition, not the cool command she wore like tailoring, but something older and more tired. Fear, maybe. Or the memory of fear.

"Mr. Ishikawa," Voss said, raising her voice. "How long have you known this meadow?"

Wendell turned. Rain had begun—not the monsoon yet, just the vanguard, fat cold drops that dotted the mud like smallpox scars.

"Known it?" He frowned. "Never landed here before, if that's what you mean."

"That's not what I asked."

A pause. Wind moved the wet grass in long silver strokes.

"When I was flying mail runs," Wendell said finally, "there was a strip on the far side of the range. Drowned Meadow, the old-timers called it. Flooded every spring, dry every fall, good gravel in between. I used it maybe a dozen times." He looked out at the water threading through the grass. "This isn't it. But it's the same kind of place. Same name, probably, in whatever language named it. The country repeats itself up here. Like handwriting."

"Drowned Meadow," Okafor repeated softly. She looked at the marsh, at the buried gear, at the sky. "Well. The country has a sense of humor."

"It has a sense of irony," Wendell said. "Humor would be funnier."

* * *

They made camp on the highest hummock of the bar, four tents in a defensive crescent, and ate cold rations under a tarp while the rain found its rhythm. Okafor spread her maps on a crate and weighted the corners with stones, and one by one they gathered—first Delia, then Bram, then Wendell, and last Voss, who stood at the edge of the lamplight as though attending a lecture she resented needing.

"Here." Okafor traced the route with a grease pencil. "East braid north, twelve kilometers to the narrows. Then the left-bank bench—here, see the contour interval tightens, that's the old terrace, glacial, stable footing. Two days on the bench. Then we drop to the river again at the confluence—" her pencil stopped, hovered, "—here."

"What's there?" Delia asked.

"A village. Small. Seasonal, possibly abandoned." Okafor hesitated. She had been turning something over since the pass, weighing it, and the weight hadn't gotten lighter. "There's something else. On my earlier sheets—the ones predating the official record—this valley appears. This exact valley. And the village appears. And there's a notation."

She pulled the old sheet from its waterproof sleeve and unrolled it beside the new one. Lamplight fell yellow across forty-year-old ink.

The notation, in faded pencil, in a hand she now recognized as Elio Marchetti's, read simply: *They count the planes.*

Nobody spoke. Rain drummed the tarp.

"Count the planes," Delia repeated. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know," Okafor said. "But Wendell does."

Every head turned. Wendell was staring at the old map, and his face had gone through surprise into something deeper, some sediment of memory being disturbed.

"The village," he said slowly. "On the confluence. There's a woman—Kesang, her name is Kesang—who flags down every aircraft that passes. Every single one, for years. I always assumed she was waving. Waving hello." His jaw worked. "She's counting. She's been counting every plane that flies this valley, and writing them down somewhere, and I never once stopped to ask why."

"Because you never stopped," Voss said quietly.

"Because I never stopped," Wendell agreed. His voice had an edge now, self-directed. "Seven years of flying over these people. Delivering medicine, picking up mail, being the friendly pilot. And I never landed. Never asked. Never once wondered what a woman standing in a field with her arm raised might actually be trying to tell me."

"Maybe she was just waving," Bram said. His first words in hours. They came out smooth and unreadable.

"Maybe." Wendell rolled up the map with sudden force. "But Elio thought otherwise, seven years ago, and Elio was standing right about where we're going to be standing in four days." He looked around the lamplight, and Okafor saw the decision arrive in him like weather. "We leave at first light. Earlier if the rain lets up. We're going to that village."

* * *

Okafor took first watch, because she slept worst and knew it. She sat under the tarp's edge with her back against a supply crate, the old map sheet across her knees, and watched the storm come down the valley like a slow animal.

By midnight the rain had thickened into the true monsoon arrival—not a shower anymore but a condition, a climate change, the sky relocating itself permanently to a lower altitude. Water sheeted off the tarp. Somewhere in the dark, the river changed its voice, rising, gathering argument.

She heard footsteps and tensed—then relaxed, barely, when Bram materialized at the camp's edge. He stood looking south for a while, rain running off his hood, and Okafor pretended to study her map and watched him instead, sideways, the way she watched anomalies.

He moved to the supply line. Checked knots. Normal. Then his hand lingered on one crate—the third one, the light one—and did something small and quick that might have been adjusting a strap and might not have been.

When he turned back toward the tents, he found her looking directly at him.

Neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence, patient as water finding a level.

"Can't sleep either?" Bram said at last, pleasant, empty.

"Too much data," Okafor said. "Unreconciled."

"Surveyors." He shook his head, smiling a little. "Everything's a discrepancy."

"Everything unresolved is," she said. "Goodnight, Bram."

He went to his tent. She watched the flap fall shut, and then she sat for a long time with the rain roaring and the old map on her knees, and she made herself a promise in the manner of her profession: she would reconcile the ledger. Every anomaly, every discrepancy, every number that pointed in a direction. Before this valley was done with them, she would know who had edited the map.

Toward dawn, the rain slackened just enough to show her the southern passes: white with new snow already, and closing.

The sky, which had carried them this far, was finished carrying anyone.

From tomorrow, they walked.
