## 17. What Voss Carries

Okafor found her at the edge of the camp, where the meadow dropped away into cloud and the wind came up the valley in long, cold breaths. She was sitting on a rock with her pack open at her feet, and she was not looking at the view. She was looking down into the pack, and her hands were moving inside it with the particular care of a woman checking that something was still there.

He had watched her do it three times now. Once in the aircraft, before the landing that bent the gear. Once in the first hour of the overland march, when she'd stopped to retie a bootlace that did not need retying. And now, on the second night, when she thought the firelight and the distance made her invisible.

Okafor had spent nineteen years reading terrain for a living—reading slope, reading water, reading the lie of land under forest canopy where nothing announced itself honestly. People were not so different. A person carrying a secret moved differently than a person carrying weight. Weight slowed you. A secret made you *check*.

"Voss," he said.

She closed the pack fast, too fast, and turned with the easy smile she kept holstered for exactly these moments. "Okafor. Shouldn't you be sleeping? You drew first watch."

"I traded with Delia." He came down the slope toward her, boots quiet on the wet grass. "I wanted to talk."

"At midnight."

"Some conversations keep."

Marguerite Voss studied him the way she studied everything—as an asset to be valued or written off. Okafor had known her for six years, since the survey contract in the northern basin, and he had never once mistaken her charm for warmth. But he had believed, all this time, in the thing underneath it: that she was ruthless about objectives and honest about everything else. That her word, once given, held.

Lately he had begun to feel like a man standing on a bridge and hearing, underneath his feet, a crack he couldn't locate.

"Talk, then," she said.

He sat on the rock beside her, close enough that courtesy required her to stay. Below them the cloud floor glowed faintly with the last heat of the day. Somewhere upslope, Wendell's voice murmured to Delia by the fire—a low exchange, two people learning to trust each other, which was more than Okafor could say for himself tonight.

"The maps," he said. "The ones you showed me in the office. The ones predating the official survey."

"What about them?"

"You told me they came from a colonial archive in the coast capital. Uncatalogued. You said you'd paid an archivist to find them."

"I did pay an archivist."

"Yes. And I've been turning that story over for two hundred kilometers of mud, and it doesn't hold." He looked at her. "Archives don't produce maps with field annotations in a living hand. Those margins weren't archival, Marguerite. They were working notes. Someone had walked that country and written what they found on the paper while the finding was still wet."

Voss said nothing. The wind moved through the dwarf pines above them, and somewhere far off, thunder muttered along the range like a man grumbling in his sleep.

"And there's a second thing," Okafor went on. "A smaller thing. The kind of thing a surveyor notices because noticing is the whole job." He paused. "You know the elevation of the headwaters to within four meters. Not from any map I've seen—not from the ones you showed me. When we planned the crossing, you corrected my figures. Twice. From memory."

"Good memory is a professional qualification."

"Not for numbers you were never given."

The silence between them stretched until it sang. Then Marguerite Voss exhaled, and something in her posture changed—the charm going back into its holster, the real woman stepping forward into the cold. He had seen her do this once before, in a negotiation that ended badly for the other party. It was, he reflected, not entirely reassuring that she trusted him enough to show him. Or perhaps it wasn't trust. Perhaps it was triage.

"You're right," she said. "The archive story was a convenience. The maps came from somewhere else."

"From where?"

She reached into the pack.

Okafor had expected many things in six years of working for Marguerite Voss. He had expected her to lie longer. He had expected her to buy his silence, because that was cheaper than truth and she respected cheap. What he had not expected was the object she lifted out and set on her knee, wrapped in oilcloth, handled the way you handle a thing that is both worthless and priceless.

A field journal. Leather covers gone soft and dark with years of weather, the spine repaired once with copper wire in a neat, practiced stitch. Water-stained. Smelled even at arm's length of old smoke.

He knew whose it was. There was only one person it could be.

"Elio Marchetti's," he said quietly.

"Elio Marchetti's," she agreed.

Below them, by the fire, Wendell laughed at something Delia said—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering how to move. Okafor felt it go through him strangely, that laugh, heard from above and outside, while the dead man's journal sat warm under Voss's hand.

"How," he said, "do you have that?"

* * *

She told it plainly, which was itself a warning sign; Marguerite Voss was never plain unless plainness served her.

"The expedition had a support contract. My firm handled logistics from the coast—resupply scheduling, insurance, the boring architecture that keeps romantic people alive. I met Elio twice. Three times. He was—" she paused, choosing, "—he was the kind of man who made you believe the next valley was worth crossing to. I understand now why Wendell followed him."

"He didn't follow him," Okafor said. "They went together. There's a difference, and Wendell knows it, and it eats him."

"Yes," she said. "I've gathered that. Keep gathering it; it matters." She turned the journal over in her hands. "The third time I saw Elio was eleven days before the fire. He flew out alone, unannounced, against the expedition's own protocol, and he came to my office unshaved and half-wild, and he put this on my desk and said: *If anything happens to me, this goes to no one. Not the company. Not the government. No one.*"

"And then?"

"Then I asked him what was in it, and he wouldn't say. He asked me instead whether I understood what a rivergate was. I said no. He said, *good*, like that was the answer he'd hoped for, and he left. Eleven days later the camp burned and he was dead, and every official record of the expedition described a fuel explosion caused by negligence." Her jaw tightened. "I read those reports forty times. Negligence. As if a man like that was careless."

"So you kept it."

"I kept it. For seven years I kept it in a safe and told myself I was honoring a dead man's wish. And then—" she stopped.

"And then someone offered you money."

"And then I needed the money," she said, with a flash of the old steel, "and then someone offered it, and the two facts arrived in the same week, and I told myself the rest was research." She looked out at the cloud floor. "I'm not going to insult you by pretending the order of events was more noble than that."

Okafor sat with it. The journal gleamed dully in the starlight, and he found he could not look away from it, the way you cannot look away from a wound on someone else's body.

"Who's the buyer, Marguerite?"

"I don't have a name. I have a broker, a bank routing number, and a set of instructions detailed enough to frighten me, which takes some doing." She wrapped the journal again, slowly. "They want confirmation the gate exists and is recoverable. That's all I was contracted to provide. Confirmation. You don't spend that kind of money on confirmation unless removal is already planned."

"And Wendell doesn't know."

"No."

"He thinks you're chasing salvage on speculation. He thinks you dragged him out here on a rumor and a debt."

"Yes."

"He loved that man." Okafor heard his own voice go flat and hard, the voice he used on junior surveyors who fudged readings. "Seven years he's carried that fire around like a stone in his chest. And you've been sitting on the one object in the world that might tell him what actually happened that night—and you brought it out here as *inventory*."

Voss flinched. It was small, and she killed it fast, but Okafor had spent his career watching landscapes for the tremor that precedes the slide, and he caught it.

"You think I haven't done that arithmetic?" she said. "Every night? You think I packed this thing without knowing exactly what it would cost me to open it in front of him?" She stood, hugging the wrapped journal to her chest like a child, or a shield. "I'm going to tell him. That's why I'm awake. That's why I'm out here. Because I've been trying to find the version of the telling that doesn't destroy him, and I'm starting to understand there isn't one."

"There isn't," Okafor said. "But there's a version where he hears it from you instead of finding out. Those are different catastrophes."

"Is that a surveyor's wisdom?"

"It's a friend's. Don't get used to it."

For a moment—just a moment—she almost smiled. Then she tucked the journal deep into the pack, and the moment was gone, and she was Marguerite Voss again, and Okafor felt the crack under his feet widen by another hand's width.

Because here was the thing he hadn't said yet. Here was the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He believed her story. That was the problem. Every detail rang true—the timing, the protocol violation, Elio flying out alone against orders. It fit the maps, the elevations, the margins in a living hand. Marguerite Voss was many things, but she was not a good enough liar to fabricate grief that precise.

Which meant the journal was real, and her account of receiving it was real, and therefore the question was not whether she was lying about *how* she got it.

The question was why, seven years later, she had opened a dead man's sealed wish—and who else knew it existed.

* * *

He did not sleep. He walked the perimeter instead, the way he always did when the ground under his certainties started moving, checking anchor lines, checking the lashed cargo, checking the sky for the storm light that was building in the west like a slow bruise.

It was near the coldest hour when he passed the supply sledge and saw Wendell crouched beside it.

The pilot had a crate open—one of the ones they'd repacked at Drowned Meadow, cargo shifted after the gear collapsed. His headlamp threw a hard white circle on the contents, and his hands had gone still inside it, and his whole body had the quality of a man listening to something very far away.

"Wendell?"

Wendell didn't answer. Okafor came closer and looked past his shoulder into the crate.

Beneath the spare parts and the ration tins, wedged where it had no reason to be, lay a radio. Not their radio—that was racked by the fire, Delia fussing over its antenna. A second unit. Compact, military-surplus, its casing freshly scratched as if recently installed, its power lead spliced into the sledge's battery bus with workmanlike competence.

And clipped to its side, a frequency card. Handwritten.

Okafor read it upside down. So, clearly, did Wendell. Neither of them spoke for a long moment. The storm light in the west brightened a degree, and the dwarf pines whispered, and somewhere below the cloud floor the rivers they could not see ran on toward country neither of them had ever mapped.

"That's not ours," Okafor said finally. Stupidly. But someone had to say it.

"No," Wendell said. His voice was very quiet and very level, the voice Okafor had learned, over these terrible days, meant the opposite of calm. "It isn't."

Wendell lifted the unit out with both hands, carefully, the way you lift a snake you intend to study rather than kill. He turned it over. He looked at the splice, at the solder, at the handwriting on the frequency card—and Okafor watched him arrive, visibly, at the same conclusion Okafor had already reached about the crew member capable of that particular quality of work.

Then Wendell looked up, and his eyes went past Okafor, up the slope, to where Marguerite Voss stood at the edge of the firelight with her pack on her shoulder and her face drained white in the dying glow.

"You knew," Wendell said. It wasn't a question.

"I know something," Voss said. "Not this."

"Then you'd better start talking," Wendell said, "because I just found a transmitter in our cargo reporting our position to somebody—and I am done, Marguerite, I am *finished*, being the last person in this crew to learn what everyone else knows about me."

Behind him, at the fire, Bram's silhouette rose slowly to its feet.
