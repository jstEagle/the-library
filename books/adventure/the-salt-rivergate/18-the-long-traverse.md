## 18. The Long Traverse

The rain came in on the second morning and never really left.

It wasn't the dramatic kind of rain—not the monsoon's theatrical downpour, all thunder and announcement. This was the patient kind, the kind that seeped into everything: boot leather, canvas packs, the seams of jackets, the spaces between people. By the third day Wendell stopped trying to stay dry. He'd learned years ago that above the treeline you didn't fight the weather. You negotiated with it. You gave it your comfort and kept your judgment.

"Tell me again why we didn't wait at Drowned Meadow for the gear to dry out," Delia said, picking her way along a shelf of wet granite. She had her mechanic's hands wrapped in strips of torn shirt to keep the rope burns from going septic.

"Because the gear isn't going to dry out," Wendell said. "Not until the monsoon breaks, and it won't break for weeks. And because every day we sit, the buyer's men get one day closer with dry socks and a radio."

Behind them, Okafor moved like a man made of stubbornness and fever. He'd refused to be left at the meadow with the wrecked plane, and Voss hadn't argued—Wendell suspected she understood Okafor better than any of them did. A surveyor who let other people carry his maps was a surveyor who'd already quit. So he walked, pale under the rain hood, one hand always resting on the oilcloth bundle where his charts lived, as if touching them proved he still existed.

Bram brought up the rear. He'd been there since the ford, since the ambush, since the bullet—and he walked like a man recalculating something constantly, some private ledger whose columns wouldn't balance. Wendell noticed it the way he noticed weather changes: not with alarm, just with attention.

They made camp that night in the lee of a rock spine that cut the wind. Rations were down to rice, a strip of dried fish each, and tea made from pine needles Delia gathered without being asked. The fire was small and smokeless—Wendell built it that way, in a hollow lined with flat stones, because he knew this country remembered fires longer than it forgave them.

"We're three days behind my projection," Voss said. She sat with her back against her pack, boots off, feet wrapped in bandages that were more mud than cloth. Her voice was level, but Wendell could hear the arithmetic grinding underneath it. "At this rate we reach the headwaters after the first hard snow."

"At this rate we reach the headwaters alive," Wendell said. "Those aren't the same thing, and I only promise one of them."

"You've said that every night for four nights."

"And I'll keep saying it until it stops being true."

Voss looked at him across the fire. Something passed between them—not warmth, not yet, but an acknowledgment that they were both doing the same math and arriving at different answers for different reasons. She wanted the gate. He wanted them breathing. The route had to serve both or neither.

* * *

On the fifth day the map failed them.

Not Okafor's map—the old ones, the colonial surveys everyone flew by, the ones that showed a pass through the range at a place called Kettle Saddle. The trail up to it existed, faint but real, worn into the scree by generations of feet that predated any government's interest in these mountains. But when they crested the shoulder below the saddle, Wendell stood looking at a wall of broken ice where the map promised a walkable col.

"The glacier advanced," Okafor said quietly, beside him. He was breathing hard, and not just from the climb. "Or the survey was wrong. Or both."

"The survey was wrong." Wendell crouched, touched the ice, rubbed the meltwater between his fingers. "These maps were drawn by men who came up the river in summer and drew what they saw from a distance. Nobody wintered here. Nobody asked the people who actually crossed." He straightened. "There's another way. It's lower and longer, and it goes through country the maps don't even pretend to cover."

"How do you know it exists?" Voss asked.

"Because I've flown over it for seven years and landed in it twice, and because an old woman in the river villages told me about it once when I was waiting out weather and she got tired of watching me sulk." He shouldered his pack. "She called it the Weeping Stair. I thought she was being poetic. She wasn't."

Delia snorted. "That sounds encouraging."

"It's a series of ledges," Wendell said. "Water runs down all of them, all year. In the monsoon it's a waterfall in pieces. We cross it in the early morning, before the melt builds, and we move fast and we don't stop on the stone."

Nobody argued. That was new, Wendell realized—the absence of argument. Four days ago Voss would have demanded alternatives, weighed options aloud, asserted command because command was her native language. Now she just nodded and started walking. Not surrender. Recalculation. She'd begun treating his knowledge the way she treated Okafor's maps: as data that outranked her preferences.

He wasn't sure how he felt about that. Seven years of making himself small, and now people were following him through fog because he happened to know where the ground dropped away. It fit strangely, like a coat tailored for someone slightly larger.

* * *

The Weeping Stair nearly killed them anyway.

They started before dawn, moving by starlight through the trees, and reached the ledges as the sky went the color of dirty pewter. The water was manageable then—a cold film over black stone, trickling rather than pouring. Wendell went first, reading the rock the way he read instruments: this slab is sound, that one rings hollow, hold here, never there. Behind him came Voss, sure-footed and silent, then Delia hauling the shared rope, then Bram with Okafor tethered to him, the surveyor's face gray but set.

Two-thirds of the way up, the sun cleared the ridge behind them and the mountain began to wake.

It happened fast, the way it always did. Meltwater gathered in the high gullies and came down the stair in sudden pulses, each one louder than the last. Wendell heard the third pulse before he saw it—a deep throaty rush somewhere above—and pressed himself into a crack in the rock and shouted for the others to do the same. The water hit like a living thing, waist-deep and furious, dragging at their legs, and for ten seconds Wendell felt the whole mountain trying to peel him off its face and hand him to the valley far below.

Then it passed, and the water thinned, and they climbed.

Okafor was shaking when they pulled him onto the grass bench at the top. He sat down hard in the wet turf and laughed—a short, cracked sound, half hysteria, half wonder.

"I have spent thirty years mapping terrain," he said, wringing water from his sleeves, "and I have never once been inside a map. This is—" He shook his head. "This is humbling."

"Maps are rumors," Wendell said. "Terrain is gossip. You have to hear it from the source."

Voss stood a little apart, looking back down the stair they'd climbed, the water already running silver and innocent in the full light. When she turned, her expression was one Wendell had never seen on her—not command, not calculation. Gratitude, offered plainly, without leverage attached.

"That was the finest route-finding I've ever seen," she said. "And I'm including the survey teams I've funded."

"Don't get used to it."

"I intend to get exactly used to it." She picked up her pack. "Lead on, Ishikawa."

Something in his chest shifted a quarter-inch. He ignored it and walked.

* * *

They descended for two days through country no outsider had named. Wendell navigated by things that would have meant nothing to anyone else: a lightning-split cedar he'd photographed from the air so many times he thought of it as a colleague; a bend in a streambed where the gravel ran red with iron; a cliff face shaped, if you squinted, like a sleeping man's profile, which the villagers called Grandfather and which Wendell had used as a checkpoint on mail runs for years without ever standing beneath it.

Standing beneath it now, craning his neck at the stone face streaming with rain, he felt oddly exposed—as if the mountain had finally gotten a good look at him after seven years of him looking down.

"You named your landmarks," Delia said, coming up beside him. She'd stopped wrapping her hands; the rope burns had scabbed clean, and she flexed her fingers as she talked, a mechanic's habit. "The split cedar. Grandfather. The red gravel. You talk about them like coworkers."

"They're the most reliable colleagues I've ever had."

"That's sad."

"That's aviation."

But she was smiling, and he found, to his mild annoyance, that he was too. Somewhere in the last week the crew had stopped being cargo he was responsible for and become something else—something he didn't have a word for yet, because he'd spent seven years avoiding words like *crew* and *team* and especially *friends*. Friends had a cost structure he knew by heart.

That evening, camped in a hollow of dwarf pine, the rations situation announced itself formally. Delia turned the rice bag upside down and shook it. What fell out wouldn't have fed two people, and there were five of them.

"So," she said. "Options."

"Hunting," Bram said.

"In this rain? Everything with legs is under a rock."

"Fishing," Wendell said. "There's a stream half a mile east that comes off snowfield. Cold water, dumb fish. And there's a village—" he hesitated, calculating distances, elevations, the angle of the ridgeline— "a day south, maybe less if the trail holds. Small place. They know me."

Voss's eyes sharpened. "Will they help us?"

"They'll feed us whether they want to or not. That's how hospitality works here. The question is whether they'll tell anyone we passed through, and the answer depends on how we arrive." He looked around the fire. "We arrive hungry and polite and we ask permission before we ask for anything. We don't flash money. Voss, you keep the ledger-talk in your pocket. Bram—" he caught the fixer's eye— "you keep whatever you're carrying behind your eyes behind your eyes. These people will read you in about nine seconds."

Bram held the look without flinching. "Understood."

And the strange thing, Wendell thought later, was that he believed him. Somewhere between the ford and the stair, between the ambush and the fever, Bram had stopped performing and started belonging—or at least stopped actively working against it. Wendell couldn't say when it happened. Trust arrived the way the rain did: gradually, then all at once, and impossible to pinpoint afterward.

* * *

The village was called Tsering's Bend by the maps, which was wrong, and something untranslatable by the people who lived there, which was right. Eleven houses, terraced gardens clinging to the slope like green stitches, smoke rising straight despite the drizzle because the hearths burned dry pine hoarded all summer.

An old man met them at the edge of the terraces—not armed, not wary, simply present, the way a gatepost is present. He looked at Wendell for a long moment.

"You are the plane that brings medicine," he said. His accent was thick, his trade language slow and careful. "Every month. Even in the bad months."

"When I can."

"You came in the bad months." He stepped aside, gesturing up the slope. "Come. There is soup. There is always soup, because my wife assumes travelers exist even when they don't."

They ate in the largest house, around a iron stove older than anyone present, and the soup was barley and nettle and a little dried yak meat, and it was the best thing Wendell had ever tasted, and he watched his crew discover the same thing in five different ways. Okafor ate slowly, reverently, like a man taking notes. Delia had seconds before she finished her firsts. Voss sat very straight and thanked the old man's wife in careful, rehearsed phrases, and the wife patted her arm the way you pat someone who has clearly never been patted enough.

Afterward, the old man took Wendell aside, out under the eaves where the rain made a curtain between them and the others.

"The men you are running from," he said. No preamble. "They came through the low pass eight days ago. Six of them. Good boots, bad manners. They asked about a plane crash and paid for information with money that smelled of the city."

Wendell's stomach tightened. "What did you tell them?"

"I told them nothing, because they asked nothing of me. They asked the river traders." He shrugged. "The river traders told them what river traders tell city men: yes, yes, this way, a little farther. The mountains will handle the rest." A thin smile. "Six men in good boots. The Weeping Stair in monsoon. Perhaps two remain."

Wendell exhaled slowly. "You knew we'd come."

"I knew the medicine plane would come eventually, walking instead of flying, because the mountain decides these things and the mountain likes you." The old man studied him with eyes gone pale as river ice. "Your friend came through here too. Long ago. Before the burning."

Wendell went still. "Elio?"

"He stayed three days. He helped mend the millrace. He laughed easily." The old man's gaze drifted toward the darkening valley, downstream, toward the headwaters none of them would name. "He asked many questions about the old stones. About who built them and why. My grandfather's grandfather answered as much as was ours to answer." He turned back. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow I will send my grandson with you as far as the high trail. After that, the country belongs to you and to it."

Inside, the fire cracked and the crew slept in a heap of borrowed blankets, and Wendell stood alone under the eaves a long time, listening to the rain come down on a roof that had sheltered Elio too, seven years ago, in the last week of his life.

Fellowship, he thought. That was the word he'd been avoiding. Not trust, not respect—those he could ration, control, spend carefully. Fellowship meant your fates were braided, meant that Delia's frostbitten fingers and Okafor's fever and Voss's dangerous secrets and even Bram's unreadable silences were now, somehow, his business in a way that couldn't be undone by landing anywhere.

He'd sworn off braid. Braid was how you lost people.

But standing there in the smell of woodsmoke and barley soup, watching the rain fall on the roofs of people who counted his deliveries the way farmers counted seasons, he found the oath had quietly expired sometime during the Weeping Stair, and nobody had informed him.

Tomorrow they'd take the high trail with the old man's grandson, into country the maps refused to admit existed, two days—maybe three—from the headwaters, with six hunters somewhere behind them and the monsoon thickening overhead.

He went inside, found a space near the stove, and slept the first unguarded sleep he'd had in seven years.
