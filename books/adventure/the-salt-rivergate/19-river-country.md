## 19. River Country

The trail gave out at the top of the last ridge, and what lay below it stopped even Okafor's fevered muttering.

A valley folded into the mountains like a letter into an envelope. Terraced slopes stitched with water channels, the green of them so deliberate it looked drawn rather than grown. The river ran through the middle of it in a braid of silver channels, and along its banks stood a village of maybe forty houses—thatch and timber, smoke rising straight up in the windless morning, dogs already barking because dogs always know when strangers are coming before the strangers know it themselves.

"That's not on any map I've ever seen," Delia said.

"It's on mine," Wendell said.

He had been here before. Not to this village—never to this village—but to this kind of place, a dozen times over seven years of flying small cargo into country that didn't want to be found. He knew the grammar of it: the way the terraces followed contours no surveyor would bother with, the way the paths connected houses to water instead of house to house, the way everything was built to shed rain and hold soil and stay quiet.

"Will they take us in?" Voss asked. She had gone very still beside him, the way she did when she was calculating.

"They'll feed us whether they like us or not," Wendell said. "That's how it works here. Whether they let us leave is another question."

Bram said nothing. He had said almost nothing for two days, since the ambush at the ford, since Okafor took the bullet meant for the journal. Okafor walked now between Delia and the elder's grandson—a boy of perhaps fourteen named Sena who moved through the trailless forest like water finding its level—with his arm bound against his chest and his skin gray under its brown. Every hour that passed made the wound worse. Every hour also brought them closer to people, and Wendell had been praying to no one in particular that the two equations would balance.

They started down. The descent took most of the morning, switchbacking through cloud forest where the air itself seemed to drink, moss hanging from branches like laundry left out too long. Twice they crossed channels of running water channeled into stone troughs so old the stone had gone soft-edged, worn smooth by centuries of the same water following the same path. Okafor, half-delirious, kept trying to stop and look at them.

"Those aren't natural," he'd say, and then sway, and then walk again.

By early afternoon the dogs found them first—a pack of five or six, circling at a distance, announcing their arrival to the whole valley with the tireless authority of professionals. By the time Wendell's boots touched the packed earth of the village path, there were people waiting. Not many. A dozen, maybe. Standing in doorways and at the edges of terraces, watching with the particular patience of people who have seen every kind of stranger arrive and knows that waiting costs nothing.

An older man stepped forward. He was small and square-built, white-haired, with a face like a river stone—something you could look at for years without ever seeing all of it. He wore a woven vest and carried a walking stick he clearly didn't need, which told Wendell he was a man whose authority came from somewhere other than his legs.

Wendell stopped ten paces away and did what he had learned to do in seven years of arriving: he waited. Let the silence do the introductions.

The old man looked at each of them in turn—the crew, mud-caked and hollow-eyed; Okafor swaying between Delia and the boy; Voss standing with her chin up as if she were the one doing the welcoming. Then his eyes came back to Wendell and stayed there.

"You are the pilot," he said. His accent was thick but his words were careful, chosen like stones for a wall.

"I am."

"The one who brings the medicine." It wasn't quite a question. "The white plane. You fly low over the ridge in the dry season, and the children count."

Something went loose in Wendell's chest. "You know me?"

"We know your engine," the old man said, and something that was almost a smile moved across his face. "I am Tamu. This is Kesh village. Come. Your friend needs to lie down, and everyone needs to eat. We will talk after."

* * *

They laid Okafor in a house near the center of the village, on a platform bed above the floor, and the women of Kesh went to work on him with a competence that made Delia stop asking questions and start taking notes. They cleaned the wound with boiled water and something bitter-smelling that stung even to stand near. They packed it with a paste of herbs and honey. They fed him willow bark tea until he slept, really slept, for the first time in two days.

"Is that going to work?" Voss asked quietly, watching from the doorway.

"It worked on me once," Wendell said. "Fever, eight years ago. I crashed short of the strip at Tolo and walked in here half-dead." He paused. "Not here. Another village. But the same medicine."

"You never mentioned that."

"There's a lot I never mention."

Delia came out wiping her hands. "His pulse is steadier. Whatever's in that paste, it's doing something." She hesitated. "Wendell. There's something else. The women asked me questions while they worked. About you. Where you'd been, why you came back, whether—" she frowned, trying to get it right, "—whether you were finished with whatever you were carrying. That's how they put it. *Finished with what you were carrying.*"

"What did you tell them?"

"That I didn't know."

Tamu found them at dusk, at the fire where the whole crew sat eating a stew of river fish and greens that tasted like the valley itself, green and mineral and alive. The old man lowered himself onto a bench with the care of a much larger man and studied Wendell across the flames for a long moment.

"You have come down from the high country," he said. "From the gate road."

Voss went still. Wendell felt Bram shift at the edge of the firelight.

"We're travelers," Wendell said carefully. "Our plane is down above the treeline. We're making for the headwaters, and then out."

"No one makes for the headwaters," Tamu said. "People make for the gate. Some to open it. Some to close it. Some to own it." He turned his stick over in his hands. "Which are you?"

Wendell met his eyes. Seven years of practice told him how to deflect this question. Something older than practice made him answer it straight.

"To close it," he said. "Or to keep it closed. Whatever keeps it out of certain hands."

The old man nodded slowly, as if Wendell had confirmed something he'd suspected since the dogs started barking. Then he said, in the tone of a man setting a heavy object down on a table:

"Elio spent his last week in this village."

The name landed in Wendell like a dropped anchor. The fire cracked. Somewhere a dog barked twice and went quiet.

"He—" Wendell's voice failed on the first try. He tried again. "Elio Marchetti. You knew Elio?"

"He stayed in the house where your friend sleeps now. He ate at this fire. He fished the channel with the boys, badly." Tamu's eyes crinkled. "He talked too much, that one. Asked too many questions. But the children loved him, and the women decided he was harmless, and so we let him stay."

"How long ago?"

"Seven years. When the rains ended late." Tamu watched Wendell's face do whatever it was doing. "You did not know he came here."

"No." Wendell heard his own voice from far away. "No one knew where he was that week. The expedition log has a gap. Nine days, no entries. I flew search patterns for—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I flew search patterns."

"We wondered if anyone would come asking, someday. He said someone might. He said, *if a pilot named Wendell Ishikawa ever comes down into this valley, tell him I'm sorry about the radio, and give him what I left.*"

The world tilted slightly. Wendell set his bowl down very carefully, because his hands had become unreliable instruments.

"What did he leave?"

Tamu rose, unhurried, joints cracking like green wood. "Come. It is kept in the spirit house. It should not sit in the dark talking to itself any longer."

* * *

The spirit house stood apart from the others, small and windowless, raised on posts, its door hung with dried flowers gone brown as paper. Tamu lifted the latch and gestured Wendell inside ahead of him. It was dim inside, smelling of smoke and old cloth and time. On a shelf, wrapped in oilcloth tied with cord gone brittle, sat a bundle the size of a book.

Tamu untied it with fingers that took their time. Inside was a notebook—water-stained, swollen, its cover scarred—and beneath it, folded into a square gone soft with handling, three pages torn from a legal pad, covered in Elio's handwriting, the big looping scrawl that Wendell had watched fill a thousand flight plans and one suicide note of an expedition report.

"He gave these to my wife's mother the night before he went upriver," Tamu said. "He said, *keep these where they can be found by the right person, and lost by the wrong ones.* For seven years, they have done exactly that."

Wendell took the pages. His hands were shaking. He recognized the ink—one of those cheap pens Elio bought in bulk because he lost them constantly. He recognized the pressure of the writing, hard enough to emboss the page beneath.

The first lines hit him like downdraft:

*Wendell—if you're reading this, then either you finally came looking, or the river sent you, and honestly either one sounds like you.*

Below that, the handwriting got smaller, tighter, the words of a man writing fast because he had limited light and unlimited things to say:

*They lied to us. All of it. The gate isn't a ruin and it isn't a treasure—it's infrastructure. It's a machine that decides who drinks and who drowns, and the people who built it understood something about water that our whole civilization hasn't figured out yet. I've spent nine days with the elders here learning what the channels actually DO. The sluice system feeds every watershed for two hundred miles downstream. Sell it piece by piece like Harrow wants and you don't get rich—you get a genocide with a delay on it.*

*I'm going up tomorrow with Tamu's brother-in-law to see the upper works myself. If I can prove the flow numbers, nobody can sell this thing as salvage—it becomes a public utility or it becomes a crime scene. Either way it stops being merchandise.*

*If I don't come back, it's because somebody up there decided merchandise was safer. Check the flow data against the rainfall records. The math doesn't lie. People lie. Math just waits.*

*Tell Marguerite she was right about Harrow. Tell her I'm sorry I wouldn't listen.*

*And Wendell—whatever you blame yourself for, put it down. You couldn't have known. Neither could I. That's the whole story. That's the only version.*

*Fly low. Count the children.*

*—E*

Wendell read it twice. Three times. The fire outside threw moving light through the doorway. Tamu waited with the patience of stone.

"Marguerite," Wendell said finally, and his voice came out scraped raw. "He says to tell Marguerite she was right about Harrow."

Behind him, at the edge of the doorway, Voss stood perfectly still. How long she'd been there, Wendell couldn't say. Her face in the firelight was doing something complicated—grief and vindication and horror braided together, none of them winning.

"He wrote to me," she said quietly.

"He wrote to you."

"In the journal. The one I carry. There are pages I never showed anyone—pages where he argues with himself about whether to trust Harrow's financing. I thought..." She stopped. Started again. "I thought I understood what happened that week. I don't think I understand anything."

Wendell looked down at the pages in his hands. Grief, which for seven years had been a sealed room in him, a room he'd bricked over and flown around and refused to enter—grief had found a door. It hadn't kicked the door down. It had simply opened it, the way Elio used to open doors, without knocking, expecting to be welcome.

"Tamu," Wendell said. "The notebook. The one underneath. What's in it?"

"Flow measurements," the old man said. "Numbers. He was very excited about numbers. And drawings of the gate works, and names—he wrote down the names of every family in this valley and the villages below, saying someone should know who lives downstream of all that water." Tamu's eyes were steady on Wendell's. "He said the math doesn't lie. He said whoever controls the gate controls the rain for everyone under it. He said a man named Harrow wanted it to be a secret forever."

Outside, the monsoon arrived properly at last—not the distant thunder they'd been hearing for days, but the real thing, rain hitting the roof of the spirit house like applause from an enormous indifferent crowd.

Wendell folded Elio's pages along their old creases and put them inside his jacket, against his chest, where the weight of them felt less like cargo and more like ballast.

"Tomorrow," he said to Voss, to Tamu, to the rain, "we go up the gate road. All the way."

Voss nodded slowly. "Harrow's men will already be moving."

"Then we'd better move faster." Wendell looked at the rain hammering down, at the fire hissing in the wet, at the faces of the crew gathered in the doorway behind Voss—Delia's grim set jaw, Bram's unreadable stillness, Sena watching everything with the solemn attention of a boy about to inherit a story. "Because I just got instructions from a dead man. And Elio Marchetti never once in his life accepted *no* for an answer."

The rain fell harder. Somewhere up the valley, in the dark, the river ran free toward the gate—and toward everything that wanted to own it.
