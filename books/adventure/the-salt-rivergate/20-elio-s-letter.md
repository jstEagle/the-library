## 20. Elio's Letter

The elder's name was Suri, and she was smaller than the bundle she carried.

Wendell watched her come down the longhouse steps with the thing held against her chest in both hands, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age and folded so many times the creases had worn to threads. The rain had eased to a fine drifting mist that hung in the courtyard like breath. Behind him, Delia was arguing gently with a boy about whether the plane's cowling could be left off overnight—she'd already lost that argument twice—and Okafor slept under a mosquito net with his maps spread over him like a second blanket, and none of it mattered very much just then, because Suri was walking toward Wendell with seven years of his life in her hands and he found he couldn't move.

She stopped an arm's length away. She was old the way river stones are old—not worn down but rounded, made smooth by everything that had ever passed over her. Her eyes were bright and unhurried.

"You knew him," Wendell said. His voice came out wrong, scraped.

"I fed him." She said it simply, as if that settled everything, and maybe it did. "For six days he ate at my fire. He helped mend the fish weir. He was bad at it." A smile moved through the wrinkles like current under ice. "He talked too much. He asked too many questions. He wrote at night by a lamp until I told him the lamp oil was not free, and then he wrote faster."

Wendell's hands were shaking. He put them in his pockets like a boy.

"He said someone would come," Suri continued. "Not you specifically. He said *if a pilot comes, one who flies small and quiet, give him this.* He described your airplane. He described how you land—how you always land into the wind even when the wind is crossways and everyone else lands with it because they are lazy or stupid." She tilted her head. "I have watched you for years, Wendell Ishikawa. You always land into the wind."

"I didn't know anyone was watching."

"That is what watching is for." She held out the bundle. "Take it. It has been heavy long enough, and I am too old to carry other people's dead."

He took it. The oilcloth was lighter than he expected, which felt like a lie, like the whole thing should have weighed as much as an engine block. His fingers wouldn't work right on the knots. Suri watched him fumble for a moment, then reached up—she barely came to his shoulder—and undid them herself with the patience of a woman who had untied a thousand difficult things.

Inside was a field journal. Not Elio's official log—that would have been recovered with the wreckage, catalogued, sealed away in some corporate archive—but a cheap bound notebook of the kind sold in frontier towns for pocket change, its cover soft with handling, its spine cracked white. And beneath it, folded around it like a shell, three pages torn from something else, covered in Elio's slanting hand, dense as code.

"When did he write this?" Wendell asked.

"The last night. He did not sleep. In the morning he gave me the bundle and took the boat upriver with the others." Suri's face did something complicated—not grief exactly, more the expression of a woman who has seen many men leave for the headwaters and kept a private tally of returns. "You know how that ended."

"I know how it ended."

"Then read what he said before it." She turned and walked back toward the longhouse, leaving him alone in the mist with the notebook, and that was its own kind of mercy, because Wendell did not think he could have opened it with anyone watching.

* * *

He didn't open it right away. That was the strange part, looking back. He carried it to the edge of the village where the ground rose toward the airstrip, and sat on a fallen trunk beneath a breadfruit tree, and held it, and listened to the river doing what rivers do, which is never stop talking.

Seven years he had built his life on a silence about that night. Seven years of small cargo and careful routines, of landing into the wind, of never once saying out loud what he believed had happened—because saying it would make it real, and real things demanded answers, and answers demanded that he go back to the headwaters, and he had sworn he never would. The silence had been armor. It had also been a room he locked himself inside, and now an old woman had just handed him the key and walked away.

He opened the notebook.

Elio's handwriting filled the first pages in his usual register—the expedition log voice, brisk and wry, full of measurements and jokes. *Day 9. Voss's survey crew finally admits my elevation figures are better than theirs, which I will be insufferable about for the foreseeable future. W. sulks beautifully when proven wrong. Should bottle it.* Wendell huffed a laugh that hurt his throat. He'd forgotten that voice. He'd spent seven years remembering only the last hour of it, and here was all the rest, alive on the page, insufferable and brilliant and twenty-nine years old forever.

Then, four pages in, the register changed. The handwriting tightened. The jokes stopped.

*Found it today. Or rather, stopped pretending we hadn't.*

*It's not a natural formation. I don't know why we kept telling ourselves that—the terraces were visible from the air, the cut stones are everywhere once you look. The gate is a machine. An enormous, ancient, deliberate machine for moving water, and it is still working. Still balancing the whole watershed. Every village downstream, every rice terrace, every dry-season well—this thing has been keeping them alive longer than any of our nations have existed.*

*Voss wants to report it as salvage. The consortium wants it in sections. They've already got buyers lined up—I saw the correspondence. Meridian Consolidated Holdings. They call themselves an infrastructure group. What they are is a scalpel with a letterhead.*

Wendell went cold. He read the name again. *Meridian Consolidated Holdings.* He'd never heard it spoken aloud, but he had heard it, hadn't he—in fragments, in Marguerite's careful phrasings, in the shape of the job itself. A buyer who wanted the rivergate's secrets buried. A name on paperwork he'd signed without reading closely enough, because debt makes people skim.

He kept reading.

*I told Voss we bury it. Falsify the survey. Report a gorge, rapids, nothing. She looked at me like I'd suggested drowning a puppy. She says discovery wants to be announced, that hiding it is theft from history. Maybe she believes that. Maybe she believes whatever lets her sleep. But I've met the Meridian people and she's met them too, and neither of us actually thinks they want to study the gate.*

*They want to own it. And owning it means controlling the water. And controlling the water means every farm downstream holds its breath every dry season waiting to learn what it costs. That's not infrastructure. That's a leash.*

*W. agrees with me. Of course he does—he's spent more time in these villages than the rest of us combined. He knows what the river means to them. We swore on it, the two of us, drunk on terrible rum, which is the only proper way to swear anything. Bury the find. Keep the gate closed. Let the water run free.*

*But Voss has already transmitted a preliminary report. I checked the radio log while she slept. She sent coordinates.*

The page blurred. Wendell pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and waited, breathing, until the letters reassembled.

*So that's where we are. Tomorrow we go up to the gate itself to take final readings, and I'm going to falsify everything I can reach, and I'm going to hope she doesn't check my math. If she does—*

*If she does, I need whoever reads this to understand one thing plainly, because I may not get the chance to say it slowly:*

*Meridian Consolidated does not send scientists. They send men with contracts and men with guns, and sometimes the same man wears both faces. If the gate's existence is ever confirmed publicly, they will come for it, and they will not care what stands downstream. Villages. Families. Children who wave at airplanes. All of it is, to them, a rounding error against the value of owning a river.*

*The gate must stay closed. Not hidden—closed. Sealed against them. Whatever it takes.*

*And if it's Wendell reading this—you stubborn, careful, windward-landing son of a bitch—I'm sorry about the fight. I'm sorry about all of it. You were right about the water and I should have said so sooner and louder. Take care of my sister's kid if you ever get south. And forgive yourself for whatever happens tomorrow, because I already know you, and I know you'll spend ten years doing penance for something that wasn't yours to carry.*

*It was mine. I sent the coordinates' correction too late. Whatever burns tonight burns because of me.*

*—E.*

Wendell sat under the breadfruit tree for a long time.

The mist thickened, beaded on the notebook's cover, and he shifted it inside his jacket without noticing he was doing it, the way you shield a flame. Somewhere behind him Delia had won or lost the cowling argument; somewhere the boy was laughing. The river talked and talked.

Because here was the thing—the thing that broke something loose in his chest and rearranged it: Elio had known. Had known about Meridian, had named them, had understood exactly what kind of animal was coming, and had written it down and given it to a river woman for safekeeping, addressed to no one and to the one person most likely to come asking. And Wendell had spent seven years believing the fire was an accident. Believing it was weather, or fuel vapor, or fate, or his own incompetence wearing the mask of chance. He had built a whole life on top of that belief the way you build a house on a fault line, and the house had stood, and he had called the standing peace.

It wasn't an accident.

He didn't have proof yet. He had a dead man's suspicion in ink, and a rival who claimed to carry Elio's journal, and a buyer whose name matched the name in these pages, and a fixer with a second transmitter in the cargo. But suspicion with weight like this wasn't suspicion anymore. It was direction. It was north.

Sorrow had been his co-pilot for seven years, riding quiet, asking nothing. It was still there. But underneath it now, unfamiliar, warming him like a stove in a cold room, was something harder and cleaner.

Purpose.

* * *

He found Marguerite Voss at the far end of the longhouse, apart from the others, standing in the doorway looking out at the rain. She heard his footsteps and didn't turn. She'd changed since the crash of her composure at Drowned Meadow—wore the wilderness looser now, hair tied back, boots ruined. But her spine was the same spine. He'd have known it anywhere.

"You've been reading," she said.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you walk differently when you're angry, and you've been walking angry since you came down off that hill." She turned then. Her eyes went to his jacket, to the corner of oilcloth showing at his collar, and something flickered across her face—not guilt exactly. Recognition. "So. He told you."

"Told me what, Marguerite." He kept his voice level. "Say it plainly. For once in your life."

She looked past him, down the length of the longhouse, checking for ears. Then she stepped out into the mist beside him, close, and spoke low.

"Elio found out I'd transmitted the preliminary coordinates. We fought. He threatened to radio Meridian directly and burn the whole contract. That night the camp generator exploded, and the fuel store with it, and Elio went up to the gate with two of the porters to secure the site, and their boat never came back." Her jaw worked. "I have spent seven years telling myself the explosion was mechanical failure. I am a thorough woman, Wendell. I have never once believed it."

The rain ticked on the longhouse roof. Downstream, faint, the river ran on toward all those villages, all that green, all the lives balanced on an arrangement of stones older than memory.

"Meridian Consolidated Holdings," Wendell said.

Voss went very still. "Where did you hear that name?"

"The same place you're going to tell me you heard it first." He pulled the oilcloth bundle from his jacket and held it between them, dripping mist. "In his handwriting. The night before he died. He knew who bought the survey, Marguerite. He knew what they were. He warned that they'd come for the valley and bury everyone in it. And you flew me up here under contract—" his voice cracked, and he let it, "—under *your* contract, to confirm the salvage still exists. For them."

"That isn't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, and he heard for the first time how tired she was, how long she'd been carrying her own version of the fault line. "That isn't the whole of what I was hired to do."

"Then it's time it became the whole of what you tell me."

From up the slope, near the airstrip, Delia's voice rang out sharp across the courtyard—calling his name, calling *Wendell*, with an edge he'd learned to hear as trouble. And beyond her, far off through the rain, thin and wrong and getting closer, came a sound he knew from another lifetime:

An engine. More than one. Coming up the valley.
