## 21. Voss Breaks

Marguerite Voss had rehearsed this conversation a hundred times in her head, and in none of the rehearsals did she lose.

She stood at the edge of the elder's courtyard with her arms crossed, watching Wendell Ishikawa read Elio Marchetti's letter by lamplight, and she understood—slowly, the way floodwater understands a levee—that the conversation was already over. She just hadn't been told yet.

The letter was four pages. He read them slowly. His lips moved on certain lines, which meant he was hearing Elio's voice, which meant the words were landing somewhere below the ribs where arguments couldn't reach. She knew that place. She'd been living there for eleven months.

*They will not come to buy it,* Elio had written, if Okafor's shaky translation could be trusted. *They will come to erase it. A gate that controls water for two hundred miles is worth more destroyed than owned—if you're the kind of man who thinks in insurance and leverage. Confirm it exists. That's all they asked me to confirm. I said yes because I wanted to believe they'd stop there.*

Wendell looked up. The lamplight made his face older than seven years should have allowed.

"You knew," he said. "You knew what they wanted before you ever came to my airstrip."

"I knew what I was told," Voss said. "Which is not the same thing, and I have spent this entire expedition learning the difference."

"Read it to me again." He held out the last page. "The part about the valley."

She didn't take the page. Her hands stayed crossed over her chest like armor plates, and she heard herself say, in the flat boardroom voice she'd used on three continents: "I don't need to read it. I wrote the contract summary myself. Meridian Consolidated, resource division, engagement code Saltgate-9. Objective: verify existence and structural integrity of pre-colonial hydraulic works at the headwaters of the Salt River system. Deliverable: photographic confirmation and an engineering assessment of salvage feasibility."

"And?"

"And nothing. That was the deliverable." She paused. The pause cost her something. "There was a second page. Contingency language. If verification failed—if the site proved inaccessible or degraded—the engagement terminated quietly. But if verification succeeded—" She stopped.

"If it succeeded," Wendell said.

"Then the assessment wasn't about salvage feasibility anymore. It was about how much of the structure would need to fail before the water rights downstream became legally chaotic enough to acquire cheaply." She heard the words come out of her mouth and was surprised, still, after all these years in rooms full of men who talked exactly this way, at how clean they sounded. How reasonable. "Two hundred miles of river, Wendell. Sixty villages. Every one of them farming on land titles that only hold value while the water behaves. You destabilize the gate, the floods come, the land sells for nothing, Meridian buys the watershed for the price of a parking lot. Nobody has to fire a shot. It's not even illegal. It's arithmetic."

The courtyard was quiet except for the rain starting up again on the palm thatch, soft as static.

"That's what you were bringing them," Wendell said. "Not a map. A confirmation."

"Yes."

"And when you found out what the confirmation was for—"

"When I found out?" She laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and thin. "I signed the contract eleven months ago, Wendell. I've known since the day I signed. I told myself it was due diligence. I told myself I'd renegotiate the contingency clause once I saw the site. I told myself a great many things in very good lighting." She uncrossed her arms finally, because they were shaking, and put her hands flat against her thighs to steady them. "I am not going to stand here and tell you I didn't know. I knew. I flew your debt into my portfolio specifically so I could own you, because you were the only pilot alive who could get me here, and I did it knowing what the photographs in my camera were going to be used for."

Okafor, propped against the granary wall with his bandaged shoulder and his fever-bright eyes, said nothing. He'd been saying nothing for two days, which from Okafor was a scream.

Delia stood near the fire pit with her arms wrapped around herself, watching Voss the way you watch a machine you suspect is about to let go of its load.

"So why are you telling us now?" Wendell asked. "Why not wait until we reach the gate? Take your pictures, make your call on the satellite phone, fly home rich."

"Because of the second page," Voss said.

"There is no second page. You just said—"

"The second page isn't paper." She made herself look at him. Made herself look at Delia, at Okafor, at the dark doorway behind them where the elder's grandson sat listening without pretending otherwise. "It's a name. The name of the man who runs Meridian's special assets group. Aldous Renner. And there's a clause in my contract—I reread it forty times on the flight south, after Bram's radio, after the charter plane, after everything—that says if the engagement fails, the principal reserves the right to pursue alternative resolution of the asset question." She swallowed. "'Alternative resolution.' That's the phrase. I thought it was boilerplate. Lawyers write boilerplate like that into everything; it's how they justify their fees. But then Renner sent a message through the charter frequency, three nights ago, addressed not to me but to the advance team. And the message said: *if the asset cannot be confirmed as recoverable, confirm instead that no witnesses remain.*"

Delia's breath went out of her audibly.

"No witnesses," Wendell repeated.

"To the valley. To the gate. To the fact that any of this ever existed." Voss's voice stayed level, which took everything, absolutely everything, the way holding a door shut against wind takes everything. "Sixty villages downstream, Wendell. And everyone upstream of them who knows why the water might start misbehaving. The elder. His grandson. The people who fed us last night. You. Me. Okafor bleeding in a stranger's house. All of it filed under 'alternative resolution.'"

"You're lying," Delia said—but softly, testing it, the way you test ice.

"I wish to God I were lying. I have never in my life wanted so badly to be wrong." Voss heard her own voice crack on the last word and despised it, despised the crack, despised the wet heat behind her eyes, despised the eleven months of good lighting and better lies that had carried her here. She turned away from the lamp so they couldn't see her face. It didn't help. They could all hear it anyway. "I built my whole life on being the smartest person in every room. Do you know what that costs? It costs you ever admitting the room was wrong. I read that contract and I understood it perfectly and I signed anyway, because understanding felt like control. It always does. Until the day you're standing in a flooded valley reading the fine print out loud to the people it's written on."

Wendell set the letter down on his knee. He didn't look angry. That was worse. He looked tired, in the specific way of a man who has been tired for seven years and has just been told the tiredness was justified.

"Elio wrote this the night before he died," he said. "He knew. He tried to tell me and I wouldn't hear it. I thought he was being paranoid. I thought the burn was ambition talking." He rubbed his face with both hands. "And you—you walked onto my strip with his death in your briefcase and offered me a job."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you're the best pilot in these mountains and I needed one." She said it fast, reflexively, and then the reflex ran out of fuel and the truth came up behind it like groundwater. "And because I told myself that if anyone could look at that gate and find a way to make the salvage story true—to make it benign, to make the arithmetic work differently—you would be the one. I needed you to be the version of this that didn't end in a body count. That's not a plan, Wendell. That's a prayer wearing a business suit. But it's the truth."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the slope, the river spoke in its sleep.

Okafor shifted against the wall and winced. "Marguerite." His voice was gravel and fever. "The message to the advance team. Did it specify a timeline?"

"It gave a date." She closed her eyes. "Day after tomorrow. If the confirmation hasn't reached Renner by then—or if what reaches him suggests the site can't be quietly acquired—the team proceeds to 'resolution' on their own authority. They're already on the ridge above the headwaters, Okafor. They've probably been there since the ambush. They weren't trying to kill us at the ford. They were trying to slow us down long enough to get into position first."

"They lost the radio," Delia said slowly. "At the ford. We took their radio."

"And they took ours. Which means they can't report up, and neither can we—which means right now, tonight, nobody in this valley knows what's coming except the six people in this courtyard." Voss opened her eyes and made herself face Wendell fully, no lamp between them, no contract language left to hide inside. "So here is what I know how to do. I know contracts. I know Renner's legal exposure—he built the entire operation on deniability, which means every order he's sent is documented somewhere he thinks is private, and I have spent eleven months making copies. I can burn his deniability down to the studs. But paperwork takes weeks, and we have a day and a half, and I can't fly, and I can't fight, and I can't fix engines." She spread her empty hands. "I'm telling you this because it's the only thing I have left that isn't a lie, and because if we do nothing, in thirty-six hours the men on that ridge start earning their pay."

Wendell was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, it wasn't to her.

"She's telling the truth," he said to Delia. "I watched her lie for two weeks straight. This isn't it."

"How can you tell?"

"Because she hates every word." He picked up Elio's letter and folded it along its old creases, careful, the way you fold something that has already survived one fire. Then he looked at Voss, and whatever was in his face, she couldn't read it, and for the first time in her adult life Marguerite Voss discovered she didn't want to be the one holding the unreadable expression. She wanted, desperately and humiliatingly, to be forgiven by a bush pilot with mud on his boots.

"Renner's men are on the ridge," Wendell said. "Above the gate. Between us and it."

"Yes."

"And we have a day and a half, a wounded surveyor, no radio, and one working rifle."

"Yes."

He stood. He tucked the letter inside his jacket, against his chest, where she supposed a man kept the things that had cost him most.

"Then we'd better decide," Wendell Ishikawa said, "which of us Renner hears from first."

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the fire had burned to orange bones and the others slept in shifts—Voss sat awake under the eaves of the granary and took out the copies.

Eleven months of them. Contract pages photographed in hotel bathrooms, memos transcribed from memory in airport bars, Renner's voice memorized off a compromised line until she could recite his cadence in her sleep. She'd told herself it was leverage. Leverage for the renegotiation. Leverage for the exit. She'd never once admitted, until this courtyard, until this rain, that what she'd actually been building was a confession with footnotes.

She heard footsteps and didn't startle. Delia lowered herself onto the packing crate beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to keep her own counsel.

"You copied all of this before you ever met him," Delia said. Not a question.

"Before I met any of you."

"Then somewhere in eleven months, you decided which side you were on. You just didn't tell yourself yet." Delia nodded at the papers. "People keep evidence like this for one of two reasons. To sell it, or to spend it. You could have sold it to Renner's competitors years ago. You didn't."

Voss looked at the top sheet—her own handwriting, *Saltgate-9, contingency clause, verbatim*—and felt the strange lightness of a woman setting down weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"No," she said. "I didn't."

Downslope, toward the headwaters, thunder rolled along the ridge like something heavy being dragged into position. Day and a half. She folded the papers back into their oilskin and held them against her chest, and for the first time since she'd signed her name to Saltgate-9, Marguerite Voss was afraid in the useful way—afraid forward, afraid toward, afraid like a woman with somewhere to be.

Above the valley, on the black ridge, someone struck a match.
