## 22. The Turncoat

Bram had always been able to sleep anywhere. That was the first thing they taught you in the trade, before weapons, before languages, before the careful art of being forgettable: sleep when you can, eat when you can, because the next chance might be a long time coming. He'd slept on loading docks in Mombasa, in the wheel well of a grounded freighter off Zamboanga, once for eleven hours in a drainage culvert outside Tbilisi while men with dogs walked past close enough that he could smell their cigarettes. Sleep was a tool. He kept it sharp.

So it was its own kind of alarm when he lay awake in the village longhouse and could not.

The rain came down on the palm-thatch roof like applause from an enormous indifferent crowd. Somewhere across the room Delia breathed slow and even, Okafor's fever-labored breathing less so, Wendell not at all—Bram could tell by the quality of the silence which of them was awake, a skill he'd never had reason to be proud of until lately, when he'd started using it to feel ashamed.

Three days since the ford. Two since the villagers had carried Okafor up from the river on a door torn off its hinges, and the old woman with the silver earrings had boiled willow bark and something sharper over the fire and packed the wound with a poultice that smelled like crushed green things and old earth. The bullet had gone through the meat below his collarbone, clean in, messier out. Infection was the enemy now, and infection did not negotiate.

Neither, Bram thought, did the man on the other end of the transmitter.

He'd buried the unit under the floor slats near the fire pit the night they arrived, before anyone thought to search him again. Standard practice: cache your comms separate from your kit, because kit gets searched and floors don't. He'd told himself it was habit. Habit was what he told himself everything was.

Now, past midnight, with the house breathing around him and the rain working its rhythm, he eased the slat up and lifted the transmitter into his lap like something fragile and venomous.

It was a small thing. A hand-length of matte composite, no bigger than a paperback, with a folding antenna and a single channel that opened twice a day at hours set by someone else. He'd used units like this on four continents. They were designed to be forgotten about. That was the whole philosophy of them—you didn't think about the person on the other end, you thought about the task, and the task was small, and small tasks were how you kept from thinking at all.

The schedule window opened at 0140 local. His watch said 0137.

He should have reported the ambush already. That was procedure too: contact lost or contact made, you filed it either way, because silence read as compromise and compromise got people sent after you. He hadn't filed. He'd let two windows close like doors down a corridor, and each one he'd let shut had made the next one heavier.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't stop running. If he reported the ambush, the response would come fast—the charter, the men who wouldn't negotiate, the whole machine spinning up to protect an investment. And the machine's first question would be why a professional crew had been ambushed at a river crossing by an advance team that shouldn't have known their route. The machine did not tolerate loose threads. Bram knew exactly which thread he was.

And if he didn't report—

If he didn't report, the silence itself would trigger the same response, just slower and angrier, and somewhere in the protocol there would be a line item for assets gone dark, and the line item would not be gentle.

Either way the plane came. The only variable was whether Bram was standing in the open waving it in or lying in the brush watching it land.

Unless.

He turned the transmitter over in his hands. Around the fire that evening, Wendell had sat with Okafor's head in his lap, feeding the surveyor sips of broth with a patience Bram had never seen in a man who flew airplanes for a living. Delia had rewrapped the wound by lamplight, her hands steady, her mouth pressed into a line that meant she was scared and refusing to spend anything on showing it. And Voss—Voss had stood apart, as she always did, but her eyes had stayed on Okafor's face the way you watch a fire you're afraid is going out.

Nobody had asked Bram to help. That was the part that lodged in him like a splinter. After the ford, after he'd put two rounds downrange and dragged Okafor behind the deadfall with his own hands, nobody had said thank you and nobody had said *we know*, and the not-knowing-which was worse than either. They fed him from the same pot. The old woman had given him the largest piece of fish without being asked, the way you feed a man who looks hungry, and Bram had stood there holding it and felt something shift in his chest that he recognized, distantly, from a very long time ago—a time before the trade, when he'd been a boy in a port town eating whatever the boats brought in, and a stranger had bought him a meal and never asked his name.

He'd spent thirty years being paid to remember nothing. It was galling, at fifty-one, to discover he remembered everything.

The watch ticked to 0140.

He keyed the transmit switch. Old reflex; his thumb moved before his conscience finished convening.

"Bram." Static, then the voice—flat, filtered through whatever relay chain ran between here and wherever the buyer's world actually lived. Not the handler he'd been given at contract signing, some smooth corporate type named Aldous who'd talked about "deliverables." This was the other voice, the one underneath, the one that had checked in exactly twice in five months and never identified itself. "You missed windows."

"Comms discipline," Bram said. "Hostile territory."

"Hostile territory is why we have windows." A pause, the sound of wind on someone else's mountain. "Status."

"Advance team made contact at the eastern ford. We lost the radio, took a casualty, held the crossing. They withdrew." He heard himself reporting it straight, clean, the way he'd report weather. Thirty years of craft. "Voss has the journal. Ishikawa has the letter. They're moving for the gate with a local guide."

"The letter." The word came down the line like a dropped stone. "Describe it."

"Paper. Handwritten. I haven't seen the contents."

"You were hired to see contents."

"I was hired to track," Bram said. "That was the contract. Track, report, confirm the salvage exists. Nobody said anything about—" He stopped himself. About what? About old men bleeding out on doors? About villages that fed strangers? About a pilot who looked at him sometimes with eyes that seemed to be doing arithmetic of their own?

"Nobody said anything about what, Bram."

Silence. Rain. His own heartbeat, annoyingly loud.

"Here's where we are," the voice said, patient as frost. "The journal confirms the gate's mechanism. The letter confirms the gate's history. Together they're worth more than every other asset in this operation combined, and if either reaches a ranger post, a court, or a newspaper, then a great many expensive arrangements come apart, and the people who made those arrangements do not have temperaments suited to disappointment. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you."

"The charter is fueled. Twelve men, current generation, no negotiation protocols. They arrive on the ridge within seventy-two hours regardless of your position. Your task is simple: the journal and the letter are in your hands before they land, or you are listed as compromised, and compromised assets are handled by the same team handling everything else." A breath. "You've been useful for a long time, Bram. Don't make this the year that changes."

The channel clicked closed. Not even a goodbye. They never bothered, at the end.

Bram sat in the dark with the transmitter cooling in his hands and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the decision point has passed, that he had just been given a choice dressed as an order—and that the order was impossible, and the choice was real.

Seventy-two hours. Get the documents or be erased alongside everyone holding them.

* * *

He didn't sleep. At some gray hour the rain thinned and the longhouse filled with the sounds of morning in the interior—roosters, water being carried, the old woman scolding a grandchild in a language Bram had begun, unbidden, to pick words out of. He watched the ceiling beams and ran scenarios the way other men prayed.

Option one: take the journal and letter, walk into the hills, meet the charter, hand them over, collect the balance of his fee and a ride out. Clean. Professional. And it required him to steal from sleeping people who had fed him fish and packed a wounded man's wound and never once treated him like what he was. Also it required him to believe that twelve men with no negotiation protocols would let a witness walk away knowing faces, routes, and the location of a gate they intended to own. He'd run that math before, for other employers. The answer never changed. Assets who saw the endgame got folded into it.

Option two: warn them. Tell Wendell and Voss and Delia that the plane was coming, that the clock was running, that the man who'd eaten their food had been the leak all along. And then watch them look at him the way people look at a thing they've stepped in. Then maybe they'd use him anyway, because they'd need every body, and maybe they wouldn't, and either way he'd have traded thirty years of being no one in particular for being a specific kind of traitor to a specific set of people, one of whom might shoot him on principle and be right to.

Option three, which he examined last because it was the largest: stay. Fight beside them. Try to turn the arrival of the charter from a death sentence into an event with a shape they could survive. It assumed they'd have him. It assumed he was worth having. It assumed that a life built entirely out of leaving things could still, at the end, contain one act of staying.

Delia found him at the fire.

She crouched across the coals with a tin cup, poured something hot, and handed it across without a word. She'd done that every morning since the ford. She did it now looking at him with those mechanic's eyes that catalogued wear and stress fractures, and Bram felt catalogued down to the rivets.

"You were up most of the night," she said.

"Old men sleep badly."

"Old men snore. You listened." She sipped her own cup. "I'm not asking, Bram. I'm telling you I noticed. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, because you're the one who taught half this crew the difference between noticing and asking."

He held the cup. Steam climbed between them.

"If a man wanted to say something," Bram said carefully, "to this crew. Something that would change how they looked at him. When would be the least bad time?"

Delia considered him for a long moment. Outside, the rain had stopped altogether, and the light coming through the smoke-hole was the pale washed color of a sky deciding something.

"When the saying it helps more than it hurts," she said. "And before the people who need to hear it hear it some worse way." She stood, joints cracking, and paused. "Wendell wants to move at first light tomorrow. Says the guide knows a ridge trail the charter can't land on. You might mention that to whoever it is you talk to at night, if there is such a person." Her face didn't change at all. "Or don't. But decide today, Bram. Whatever you are, be it all the way. The crew can carry almost anything except a man split down the middle."

She walked away toward the aircraft parts laid out on oilcloth, and Bram sat alone with his cup going cold, and for the first time in thirty years of small tasks and clean exits, he found his hands were not steady.

By noon he had made the decision three times and unmade it twice. By late afternoon, watching Wendell stand at the village edge with Elio's letter in a waterproof sleeve, reading it yet again with the expression of a man taking orders from the dead, Bram understood that the decision had actually been made days ago—at the ford, probably, when he'd dragged Okafor to cover instead of using the chaos to vanish, which any professional would have done, which the old Bram would have done without breaking stride.

The new Bram keyed the transmitter one last time at dusk, on an unscheduled window he'd have to answer for later, and spoke six words into the static:

"Charter won't find them on the ridge."

Then he pulled the antenna flat, killed the unit, and went to find Wendell, because there was one more thing a man split down the middle could still do: he could tell the truth to the one person on this crew who had the least reason to forgive him—and ask, for the first time in his career, for nothing except the chance to be believed.
