## 23. Ambush at the Ford

Delia heard the river before she saw it—a low, constant argument of water somewhere below the trail, getting louder as the morning wore on. She'd been listening to it for two hours, the way you listen to a engine knock you can't yet diagnose: with respect and dread in equal measure.

"Ford's ahead," Wendell said, dropping back to walk beside her. He'd taken point since dawn, and he moved through the wet forest like a man reading a familiar letter—quick, but pausing at certain lines. "Wide, shallow, fast. We cross single file, poles first, packs unbuckled. If anyone goes down, don't grab for them. Throw the rope."

"Charming," Delia said.

"It's honest, at least." He glanced at her. His face had changed in the last two days—harder to read, but also more settled, like a mechanism that had finally been seated on its mounts. "You sleep?"

"An hour. Maybe two."

"Same." He didn't say anything else about it. He didn't have to. They'd all spent the night listening for the charter plane, and the plane hadn't come, and somehow that was worse than if it had.

Behind them, Voss was arguing quietly with Bram about the pace. Okafor walked between them with his rifle slung and his maps wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and when he caught Delia looking, he gave her the small, dry smile that had become his answer to everything the mountains threw at them.

"Surveyor," she said, falling back a step, "how's the ankle?"

"Structurally sound. Aesthetically a disgrace." He'd turned it two days ago on the scree, and he hadn't complained once, which in Delia's experience meant it was worse than he said. "The river will be cold."

"The river will be *freezing*."

"Then we will be efficient."

The trail bent through a stand of hemlock, black trunks dripping, and then the trees opened and there it was: the ford. The river came down out of the high country in a broad, braided sheet, gray-green with glacier milk, splitting around gravel bars and rejoining with a sound like tearing canvas. On the far side the trail climbed again into cloud.

Wendell stood at the edge and did not move.

Delia watched him read the water the way he read weather—the set of the current, the color of the deep channels, the way the surface broke over the far gravel bar. She'd spent enough time around pilots to know when a man was checking his instruments. Wendell's instruments were the whole landscape.

"Water's up," he said finally. "Melt. It was knee-deep when I came through seven years ago. Now—" He pointed with his chin at a boulder midstream, its top just barely showing, water combing over it in a white fan. "That's mid-thigh at the shallow line. Doable. But we cross now, while it's still morning melt and not afternoon melt. And we cross *fast*."

Voss came up beside him. "Any other crossing?"

"Fifteen kilometers downstream there's a bridge. It's a three-day detour and the bridge is rotten, or was." He looked at her. "You want to argue, argue fast."

"I'm not arguing." Voss was already unbuckling her pack's sternum strap, her movements brisk and precise, and Delia thought, not for the first time, that the woman treated everything—weather, rivers, betrayal—like a logistics problem she intended to win. "Okafor first. He's steadiest in current."

"I am not," Okafor said.

"You are. Go."

They roped together in pairs, thirty feet of line between them, and Wendell went first alone, testing the bottom with a pole, reading the ford foot by foot. The water hit Delia at mid-thigh when her turn came and the cold went through her like a live wire, her boots suddenly full of the mountain's whole winter. She locked her jaw and leaned into the current and thought about gearboxes, which was what she thought about when she needed her hands to stay calm.

Okafor crossed third, roped to Bram. He was halfway across, angled against the current, the oilcloth bundle strapped tight to his chest, when Delia—safely on the far bank, wringing out her sleeves—heard the sound.

It took her a second to place it. Not a bird. Not the river.

An engine. Idling. Somewhere upstream, on the near side, hidden by the hemlock bend.

"Down," she said, and her voice came out flat and wrong, and then she was shouting it. "*Down! Get down!*"

Wendell was already moving. He was still in midstream, and instead of finishing the crossing he turned *into* the current and dropped, going low behind a gravel bar, and Delia understood in one cold flash that he'd heard it too, that some part of him had been listening for it for days.

The first shot cracked off the rocks above the ford and echoed up the valley like the sky breaking.

* * *

Later—much later, when she had time to take the morning apart bolt by bolt—Delia would reconstruct it in sequence, because sequence was how she kept panic in its box.

The engine. The shot. Wendell dropping low in the current, bellowing "BACK—BACK TO COVER—" at the top of his lungs. Voss pulling Okafor down behind a fallen hemlock on the near bank. Bram simply vanishing into the brush with a smoothness that told Delia, again, exactly what kind of work the man had done before this.

And the far bank—her bank, the bank she'd thought was safety—erupting.

Three men. Maybe four. They'd come down the trail from the ridge in the night, and they'd let the crew get into the water, and now they stood up out of the ferns with rifles and spread along the bank in a loose, practiced line. Not soldiers exactly. Something adjacent to soldiers—the way they moved had that economy, that lack of wasted motion, but there was a looseness in it too, an appetite, that made Delia's stomach drop lower than fear alone could.

The nearest one fired again, deliberately wide, the round slapping into a gravel bar two meters from Wendell's head.

"That's a warning!" the man called. His voice carried over the water, easy, almost friendly. "Nobody has to get hurt! We just want the papers and the woman's documents! Send them across and walk away!"

Delia was flat on her belly in wet gravel, twenty meters from the nearest rifle, and she did the arithmetic she always did: assets, liabilities, options. Assets: Wendell's rifle, slung on his back, soaked. Okafor's rifle, on the near bank. Bram, somewhere in the brush, armed, position unknown. The rope still stretched across the river, linking the two halves of her crew like a nerve.

Liabilities: everything else.

"Wendell!" Voss's voice, sharp, from behind the hemlock. "The journal—"

"I know what they want!" He'd gotten his rifle around, and he was working the action with hands that shook—from cold, Delia told herself, from the cold. "They want it because it's the only proof! Voss, listen to me—if they take it, there's no case, no charges, nothing! Your records mean nothing without the original!"

"Then we don't give it to them!"

"That's easy to say from behind a tree!"

The man on the bank laughed. Delia hated the laugh more than the rifle. "Ishikawa!" the man called. "We know who you are! The pilot! The buyer says you're a reasonable man—you flew away once already! Fly away again! There's no shame in it!"

Delia saw it hit Wendell. She saw it land, physically, like a round she could trace—the small collapse in his shoulders, the half-second where the man behind the rifle became the man from seven years ago, the one who'd flown away from the fire. The current pushed at him. The water combed white over the gravel bar.

Then Okafor stood up.

Not all the way—just enough to put his shoulder over the fallen log, his rifle up, his voice pitched in that dry, precise tone he used for correcting other people's maps.

"The gentleman is mistaken," Okafor called out, conversationally, to the armed men. "Mr. Ishikawa is not a reasonable man. I have traveled with him for three weeks. He is a *principled* man, which is considerably more inconvenient."

And he stepped out from cover—out, into the open gravel, into the sightlines of four rifles—and Delia understood what he was doing one full second before it finished happening. He was making himself the loudest thing on the riverbank. He was buying Wendell the half-second to move.

The rifle cracked from the treeline.

Delia never saw which man fired. She saw Okafor stagger, saw the oilcloth bundle jump against his chest, saw him sit down in the gravel with an expression of genuine scholarly surprise, as if the riverbank had made an error he intended to correct in the margin.

Then the world came apart.

* * *

What happened next took ninety seconds. Delia knew because she counted, because counting was the only thing her hands could hold.

Wendell fired from the gravel bar—three rounds, fast, not at the men but at the rocks just above their heads, and the ricochets screamed off the cliff face and did what ricochets do to men who are brave behind rifles. Bram came out of the brush on the near flank like a piece of the forest deciding to be a man, and his first shot took the rifle out of the nearest attacker's hands, and his second took the man off his feet entirely, and Delia would think about that later, the way Bram moved, the way he didn't hesitate, and she would decide not to ask.

"DELIA!" Wendell's voice, raw. "The rope! Cut them loose or pull them—your side—*go!*"

She went. She crawled to the rope anchor, a hemlock root they'd wrapped it around, and her hands were shaking so badly she couldn't find the knot, and she heard herself make a sound—low, furious, mechanical—and she made her hands work the way she made them work on a seized fitting. Find the problem. Apply the tool. Do not think about the fire.

The rope came free. On the near bank, Voss had Okafor under the arms and was hauling him back into the hemlock shadow, and there was blood on her hands, a lot of it, and Voss was not screaming, which frightened Delia more than screaming would have.

The attackers regrouped. Delia could see them conferring behind a boulder, could hear fragments—the buyer's name, *the charter, two hours out*—and she understood the shape of the thing with terrible clarity: this wasn't a raid. It was a first move. There were more of them coming, and the men on this bank were the expendable part of the plan.

Wendell saw it too. She watched him see it—the flick of his eyes upstream, toward the sound of the river, toward the treeline, doing the pilot's math he couldn't stop doing even on his belly in the gravel.

"Bram!" he shouted. "Left flank, the big deadfall—can you make it?"

"I can make it."

"Then make it *loud*."

What Bram did with the deadfall, Delia never fully reconstructed. She heard it: a sustained, disciplined fusillade from a position that hadn't existed thirty seconds earlier, rounds cracking through the ferns from an angle that made the attackers' cover worthless. She heard one of them shout the word *withdraw*, and she heard the particular note in it—not fear exactly, but the sound of men whose contract had just been repriced.

They went. Not running—falling back, in that economical way, dragging their wounded man, and the last thing the leader did was fire one more round into the gravel near Wendell's head, a signature, a promise, and then the forest swallowed them and the engine noise upstream rose and faded and was gone.

The river kept arguing. The birds, after a long, appalled silence, started up again.

Delia lay in the wet gravel with the rope in her hands and counted to ninety, and then she got up and went to Okafor.

* * *

The bullet had gone through the muscle of his left shoulder, high, missing the joint by what Wendell later said was maybe two fingers' width. Okafor lay on a spread of oilcloth with his head propped on his own pack, gray-faced, teeth locked, while Voss cut his shirt away with the efficiency of a woman who had done field medicine before and had hated every minute of it.

"Hold this," Voss said to Delia, pressing a folded cloth against the entry wound. "Hard. Don't be gentle."

Delia held it. Okafor's blood was shockingly warm against the morning's cold still in her hands.

"Surveyor," she said. "Hey. Eyes open."

"They are open." His voice was thin but intact, and his eyes, when they found hers, were dry and dark and entirely his own. "I would like it noted in the record that I said I was not steadiest in current."

"You weren't in the current. You were on the bank being a *idiot*."

"A calculated idiot." He winced as Voss packed the wound. "They were shooting to warn, until they weren't. Someone had to move the arithmetic." He breathed through the next part, the pressure, the packing, and when it passed he said, quietly, "The maps are dry. Check the maps."

"The maps are fine," Delia said. "You're the one leaking."

"Then I am the one who requires maintenance." He almost smiled. "You would be the one to appreciate that."

They got him stabilized with the kit Voss carried and the knowledge Wendell had—more field medicine than Delia expected from a man who lived alone, and when she raised an eyebrow at him he said only, "Seven years of bush flying. You land where the doctor isn't," and left it there.

But the wound wasn't the loss. The wound could be walked through, could probably even be flown out of, eventually. The loss was what the attackers took with them, because they'd been thorough, because they'd been *sent*: the radio, crushed under a rifle butt with a deliberateness that had felt almost ceremonial. The spare antenna. The signal kit.

"No contact with anyone until we're out," Wendell said, standing at the edge of the ford, looking at the wreckage of the set. "No rangers. No Voss's people. Nobody knows where we are, and nobody's coming."

"Delia's decoy work—" Voss started.

"Was on the aircraft radio. Which is a bent prop and two days' walk from here." He turned. "They didn't take the radio because they wanted it. They took it because they wanted us *silent*. That's not a raid. That's a schedule. They've got a window and they're clearing the road through it."

Delia watched him say it, and she watched Voss hear it, and she saw the same thing land in both of them at the same moment: the adventure was over. Whatever this had been—a debt, a chase, a reckoning, a salvage job dressed up as an expedition—it was something else now. It was a fight, and the other side had guns and a timetable and no reason at all to leave witnesses in the valley.

Okafor, feverish already, his shoulder bound and his arm in a sling made from a pack strap, said it best, from the ground, in his dry and precise way:

"Gentlemen. And ladies. I believe the survey phase has concluded." He closed his eyes. "We are now in the resistance phase."

* * *

They lost two days. There was no way around it—Okafor couldn't march, not with the wound, not in the heat of the afternoon, and Delia wouldn't leave him and discovered, watching the others' faces, that none of them would either. They made a camp off the trail, back in the hemlock, off any sightline, and they took turns watching the ford and the ridgeline, and the monsoon rain came in on the second evening like a curtain dropping on the whole world.

Delia sat a watch with Bram on the second night, the rain drumming the tarps, Okafor's breathing shallow but steady ten feet away.

"You shot to wound," she said, after a long time. "The first one. You could've killed him."

Bram was quiet for so long she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Did," he said finally. "Could've. Yeah." The rain filled the silence. "They were just hired, most of 'em. Same as me." He turned his head, and in the dark his face was unreadable, but his voice wasn't. "Somebody's paying all of us, Delia. Difference is I'm starting to look at the invoice."

She didn't know what to say to that, so she said nothing, and they watched the black rain fall on the river, and somewhere upstream, in the storm, the men with the timetable were moving.

On the morning of the third day, Okafor stood up. It took him three tries and Voss's shoulder and a sound he made through his teeth that Delia never forgot, but he stood, and he shouldered his pack with his good arm, and he said, "The trail is that way, I believe. I drew the map."

They went. Slow, in rain, a wounded man setting the pace—and faster than they should have been able to, because Wendell had stopped leading like a man hiding and started leading like a man with somewhere to be.

Behind them, far upstream, Delia heard it once, faint under the rain: an engine. Not a boat. Not a truck.

A helicopter, coming up the valley.

She looked at Wendell. He was already looking at the sky.
