## 24. Fever Watch

The fever came at dusk, the way fevers do—quietly, then all at once.

Okafor had been sleeping in the elder's house since the ford, on a woven mat near the fire, his leg propped on a rolled blanket. The bullet had gone through the meat of his thigh, clean in and out, and for two days he'd been making jokes about it, calling it a souvenir, asking Delia to sign his cast even though there was no cast. Wendell had let himself believe the jokes. That was the mistake he kept making with wounds: believing the good hours.

By the time the rain thickened from drizzle to something with intent behind it, Okafor's skin was hot as a stone left out in the sun. His jokes had stopped. He lay with his eyes half open, tracking things that weren't in the room, and when Wendell put a hand on his forehead the man flinched as if touched by a brand.

"Septic," Delia said. She'd said it an hour ago, quietly, to Wendell alone, and now she said it again because saying it twice made it more real. "The wound's angry. Red streaks going up toward his hip. He needs antibiotics, real ones, IV if we can get them, and he needs them yesterday."

"We don't have any."

"I know what we have." She held up the med kit, the one they'd carried overland, down to gauze, tape, a blister pack of painkillers with four pills left, and a bottle of iodine she was rationing like it was made of gold. "I'm telling you what we need."

Wendell looked at Okafor. The surveyor's breathing had gone shallow and fast, and every few breaths there was a small catch in it, like a motor missing.

"The nearest clinic is three days' walk downstream," Wendell said. "Four, in this rain."

"Then we've got a problem."

"No." He stood up. "We've got a village."

Delia frowned at him. "What?"

"Stay with him. Keep the wound open and draining, keep him drinking water—I mean it, make him drink, even if you have to argue him into it. I'll be back before the fire burns down twice."

"Back from where?"

But he was already moving, pulling his jacket off its peg, and the question followed him out into the rain unanswered, which was answer enough.

* * *

The elder's name was Maren, though everyone in the valley called her Grandmother, including people who were not her grandchildren, including, apparently, Wendell, who caught himself doing it and didn't correct it. She was small and straight-backed and she sat by her own fire like a woman who had been sitting by fires since before the fire had opinions about her.

When he told her what he needed, she didn't ask questions. She listened the way old women listen, all the way to the end of the sentence, and then she said, "You want the white capsules. In the brown bottles. From the plane."

Wendell blinked. "You know the medicine?"

"You brought it." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the river rose* or *the rains came*. "Every cold season, seven years. The white capsules in the brown bottles, for the lungs. And the yellow powder for the children's fevers. You never stayed long enough to see who took it."

He hadn't. That was the whole architecture of those years: land, unload, nod, lift off. He had made a discipline of not seeing, because seeing accumulated, and accumulation was weight, and weight was the thing that grounded men like him. He had delivered medicine to forty villages along the watershed and told himself it was cargo. Just cargo. Small cargo, light cargo, the kind that didn't ask anything of you.

"Where are the bottles now?" he asked.

Maren smiled, and there was something in the smile that made him feel like a student who has just been asked a question he should already know the answer to.

"They are where we keep things that matter," she said. "Come."

* * *

They walked through the rain to the longhouse at the center of the village, Maren moving over the wet ground with a sureness that mocked his slipping boots. Inside, the smell was woodsmoke and dried fish and something medicinal underneath, bitter and green. Lamplight. A dozen faces turned toward them—not surprised, Wendell realized, not even curious. As if they'd been expecting this visit the way you expect weather.

A young man climbed a ladder to a shelf built high under the roof beam, above the reach of damp and mice, and came down with a wooden box. When he opened it, Wendell's chest did something complicated and unhelpful.

Brown bottles. Dozens of them, packed in straw, their labels soft with age but still legible. Amoxicillin. Some tins of the yellow fever powder. A jar of the multivitamins he used to hand out to mothers with underweight babies, back when he could still pretend he wasn't counting anyone as his own.

"These are seven years old," he said. "Maybe eight. Antibiotics degrade—"

"Some are newer." Maren lifted one bottle, turned it so the lamplight caught the date stamp. "Your last flight before the snows, two years ago. You remember it? The engine ran rough and you left fast."

He remembered. He remembered being embarrassed by the rough start, by the whole visit, by the way the children had run alongside the plane waving until he was too high to see them. He had told himself they were waving at the airplane. Any airplane. Children liked airplanes.

"You kept them," he said. "All this time. Why didn't you use them?"

"For sickness," Maren said, as if the answer were obvious, because it was. "For a bad sickness, when there is no time to go downstream. We do not eat our seed corn in a thin year."

"And now?"

"Now there is a bad sickness." She nodded toward the door, toward the elder's house, toward the man burning on his mat. "Your friend carries it in his blood. So we spend the corn."

She pressed the bottles into his hands, both of them, and then a tin of the yellow powder, and then, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of dried leaves whose purpose she explained in three sentences that Wendell understood maybe half of, involving boiling and timing and a bitterness that "pulls the heat out through the skin."

"Maren." He stopped at the doorway, the bottles heavy against his ribs. "Why do you have these? Why keep them for me—for anyone—like this?"

The old woman looked at him for a long moment. Rain drummed on the longhouse roof. Somewhere outside a dog shook itself and thought better of barking.

"You think we did not know who you were?" she said. "The pilot who would not look at us. Seven years, the same routes, the same loads, always leaving before the food was ready. We knew a man running from something. We did not need to know what." She shrugged, a small motion like a bird settling its feathers. "A man running still flies straight when he carries medicine. We counted the flights. Forty-one, before the last two winters. My grandson counted. He counts everything."

Forty-one. Wendell stood there holding eight years of his own life in a wooden box and found he couldn't speak.

"You thought you were hiding," Maren said, not unkindly. "But a man cannot hide from people who feed him. He can only hide from himself. That part we left to you."

* * *

Delia met him at the door of the elder's house with the look of someone who has been fighting a war on two fronts and losing on both. Okafor was worse. The red streaks had reached his hip. He was talking now, but to people who weren't there—to colleagues at some institute, defending a map, arguing about contour lines with a dead man named Halloran.

They worked by lamplight while the rain came down like a verdict. Delia crushed the antibiotic tablets into powder and mixed them with boiled water and got them into Okafor a spoonful at a time, which was slow, and crude, and the only thing they had. Wendell boiled the leaves per Maren's instructions and bathed the wound, and the smell of it filled the room, bitter and green and ancient, older than any pharmacology he'd ever trusted.

Voss sat in the corner and did not offer to help, and after a while she did offer, and was given the job of keeping Okafor's forehead cool with wet cloth, and she did it for six hours without complaint, her sleeves rolled, her precise hands gentle, and Wendell watched her do it and revised something inside himself that he'd been carrying since the airstrip.

Bram sat by the door with his arms around his knees, watching, saying nothing. Once, near midnight, he got up without a word, went out into the rain, and came back twenty minutes later with an armload of dry wood and a haunch of smoked fish someone had given him. He fed the fire all night. Nobody asked him to. He never mentioned it afterward.

Toward dawn, the fever broke.

It didn't break dramatically. There was no moment when the battle turned. Okafor simply stopped talking to Halloran, and his breathing slowed and deepened, and the terrible heat began to drain out of him like water finding its level. By full light he was asleep—real sleep, the plain human kind—and the red streaks, when Delia checked, had not advanced.

"He'll live," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she covered it by yawning enormously. "Assuming the pills aren't too degraded. Assuming the infection doesn't rally. Assuming—" She stopped herself and laughed, tiredly. "Assuming. That's the whole job description out here, isn't it? Assuming."

"That's the whole job description everywhere," Wendell said. "Some places just admit it."

Delia looked at him across the sleeping man, across the empty bottles and the bowl of leaf-bitter water, and her face did something complicated.

"You knew," she said. "You knew they had medicine here. All these years you flew this route, and you knew."

"I didn't know. I delivered and left. That was the deal I made with myself."

"And they kept it. They kept your medicine like treasure, for seven years, waiting for a night exactly like this one." She shook her head slowly. "Wendell. You spent seven years thinking you were paying nothing to anybody. Running tabs you didn't know existed. And the whole time—" she gestured at the bottles, the box, the village, the rain, everything— "the whole time, the ledger was running the other way. You were never invisible. You were *invested*."

He wanted to say something to that. He opened his mouth and closed it again, because there was nothing in him equal to it, and Delia, mercifully, didn't wait for him to find anything. She curled up on the floor beside Okafor and was asleep between one breath and the next.

* * *

Wendell stepped outside into the gray beginning of morning. The rain had softened to mist. The river below the village ran high and brown and loud, carrying the monsoon's news downstream to other villages, other rivers, the sea.

Maren was on her porch, wrapped in a blanket, watching the water the way she seemed to watch everything: patiently, as if it would eventually tell her something worth hearing.

"He'll live," Wendell said.

"Yes." She didn't look surprised. "The corn was planted well."

He sat down on the step beside her, uninvited, and she allowed it, which felt like a promotion.

"Maren, why tell me now? About the counting. The forty-one flights. Why not years ago?"

"Because years ago you would have flown away faster." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd note that a door is locked. "A man must arrive at his own debts. We could not pay them for you." She turned her head and looked at him directly, and her eyes in the early light were pale and clear and completely without sentiment. "But listen to me now, pilot, because the water is rising and my patience for riddles is finished. The men who shot your friend at the ford—they are not done. They lost two days and they are angry, and angry men with guns move faster than sick men with maps. And there is another thing."

"What thing?"

"Two nights past, my grandson saw lights on the far ridge. Not fires—a light that moves in a line, like a lantern carried by a walking man. It crossed the ridge twice, north to south, and once it stopped for a long time, looking down into this valley." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Someone is counting *you*, Wendell Ishikawa. The way we counted your planes. And their arithmetic is almost finished."

Downriver, around the bend, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong to the monsoon or the mountains or anything natural in this country at all.

An engine. Aircraft. Getting closer.
