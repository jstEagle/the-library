## 25. The Gate Road

The fever came in waves, and Okafor learned to ride them the way he had once learned to read contour lines—by surrendering to their logic instead of fighting it.

In the mornings he was mostly himself. He could walk, leaning on Delia's shoulder or on a staff Bram cut for him from some pale hardwood, and he could count things, which was how he knew he was still a surveyor. He counted his steps between rests. He counted the switchbacks as they climbed out of the river country. He counted the times Wendell looked back at him with that expression Okafor was beginning to hate—the one that measured him like a load that might have to be abandoned.

But by afternoon the fever rose like water behind a dam, and then the world went strange and generous, and the counting stopped meaning anything.

* * *

The grandson's name was Teo, though it took Okafor two days to learn it because at first he thought the boy was his own nephew, Daniel, dead six years in a car accident outside Kaduna, walking beside him through the rain with that same patient half-smile.

"You're not Daniel," Okafor told him on the second morning, when the fever had burned down low enough for honesty.

"No," Teo said. He was maybe nineteen, wiry, barefoot in sandals, carrying a machete he used like an extension of his arm to open the trail ahead of them. "I'm nobody's ghost."

"That's what a ghost would say."

Teo laughed. It was a good sound, unguarded, and Okafor decided that if he was going to hallucinate a dead relative, he could do worse than this boy who wasn't one.

They were nine now. Wendell in front with Teo, reading the ground the way other men read print. Voss behind him, carrying Elio's journal against her chest like something she'd stolen from a church and couldn't decide whether to return. Delia and Bram flanking Okafor, trading the weight of him between them without ever quite saying they were doing it. And behind them all, three villagers from the elder's house who had volunteered to carry supplies as far as the terraces, and who sang sometimes, low work songs that Okafor's fever translated into hymns from his grandmother's church until he had to stop and stand very still with his eyes closed until the world agreed to be only itself again.

"You should ride the litter," Delia said, when he opened his eyes.

"I should keep walking. If I lie down I'll start seeing the map."

"You already see the map."

"Yes," Okafor said. "But lying down I'd see it wrong."

* * *

The trail, when there was trail, was old. That was the first thing the fever taught him clearly, on the third day, when the sun stood high and hot above the canopy and his mind sharpened into a kind of terrible clarity it hadn't had since before the ford.

Old trails are made by feet, and feet make trails in certain ways. They cut switchbacks where the grade steepens. They braid around obstacles. They erode into gullies that heal over decades and reopen. This trail did none of those things. It ran straight when straightness cost effort. Its drainage cuts were too clean, too regular, spaced like fence posts. And every few hundred meters, a stone.

Not marker stones exactly—or not only markers. They were waist-high blocks of a gray-green rock that didn't match the local geology, and each one was carved. Okafor knelt at the first one they passed after his clarity came, ignoring Delia's protest, and put his fingers in the grooves.

Channels. The carving was channels—interlocking grooves that ran across the face of the stone and down its sides and into the earth, and at the top of the block, a basin worn smooth as if water had run through these channels for centuries.

"It's a model," he said. His voice came out cracked. "Or a diagram. This is a hydraulic schematic. Someone carved the whole system into—" He looked up the trail. Through the trees, dimly, another stone stood like a sentry. "How many of these are there?"

"Many," Teo said. "My grandfather says the road remembers itself."

"The road remembers itself," Okafor repeated. He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and Delia put a hand flat against his back and left it there.

"Fever's climbing," she said to no one in particular.

"I'm fine," Okafor said. "I've never been finer. Do you understand what this is? Everyone downstream thinks the rivergate is a ruin, a curiosity, a thing to be found. But look at the spacing of these stones. Look at the drainage. This isn't a path to the gate." He tapped the carved channels with one trembling finger. "This is part of the gate. The approach is engineered. We've been inside the machine since the ford and didn't know it."

Behind him, he heard Voss stop walking.

He didn't turn around. He watched her shadow lengthen past him on the trail and hold still, and he thought about the maps she'd shown him in the first week, the ones predating the official record, the ones that had proven she'd known the location all along—and he wondered, with the fever's terrible evenness, whether her maps had shown stones like these, and whether she had understood them, and whether she had chosen not to say.

Then the shadow moved on, and the column moved on, and Okafor let himself be moved with it.

* * *

That night the fever took him properly.

He remembered fragments. A fire ringed with wet stone. Delia forcing broth into him, spoon by patient spoon, saying numbers under her breath—pulse counts, probably, or the words of a prayer she'd learned somewhere and repurposed. Bram sitting awake at the edge of the firelight with his rifle across his knees, watching the dark the way a man watches a door he expects someone to knock on.

And in the middle of it, the dream that wasn't a dream:

He was standing on a terrace. Not the small terraces of the hill farms they'd passed—these were vast, curved, engineered, cut into the mountainside in arcs so wide that each one held its own weather. Water moved along them in silence, sheeting from level to level through slots cut in the retaining walls, and the sound it made was not the sound of falling water but of breathing. The whole mountainside breathed. In and out. Level to level.

Someone stood beside him. He understood, in the way of dreams, that it was the man who had built this—that the builder had been waiting a very long time for someone to stand here and see it correctly.

*You think we found this,* the builder said. *Everyone thinks it was found. Rivers are found. This was decided.*

"How long?" Okafor asked. His voice in the dream was his grandfather's voice, which confused him.

*Longer than your longest map,* the builder said. *We did not build it to keep the water. We built it to keep the argument. Anyone can take water. Only patience can hold an agreement.*

"What agreement?"

But the builder was already turning away, becoming the terrace, becoming the water, and Okafor woke with his hand pressed flat against the wet earth as if checking it for a pulse.

Delia crouched over him. "You were talking," she said. "Terraces. Levels. You kept saying *level to level.*"

"There are terraces ahead," Okafor said. "Big ones. Ask the boy."

"We know," Delia said quietly. "Teo told us. We're crossing the first one tomorrow." She hesitated. "Wendell says the official survey shows natural terrain. No terraces. Nothing."

"The official survey never walked this road." Okafor closed his eyes. "Most truths don't survive being written down by people who didn't go and look."

* * *

The fourth day was the worst and the best.

The worst because the fever peaked near noon and he lost an hour somewhere on a climb, and came back to himself to find Bram carrying him piggyback up a slope, breathing hard but steady, saying nothing, having apparently simply decided that this was happening and Okafor's dignity was a casualty the mission could absorb.

The best because of what waited at the top of that slope.

The forest ended—not gradually, not thinning, but ending, the way a sentence ends—and beyond it the mountain opened into the first of the great terraces.

Okafor had seen it in the fever. Seeing it clear-eyed was worse. Better. Both.

It curved away below them for nearly a kilometer, a floor of ancient masonry softened by centuries of moss and grass, and along its outer edge ran the channel—two meters wide, cut with geometric precision, still carrying water after however many hundreds of years, running silver-bright in the afternoon light toward the headwaters. Beyond it, half-hidden in cloud, the next terrace rose. And beyond that, faint as a rumor, another.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Voss stepped forward to the edge of the channel and looked down at the water moving in it, and Okafor watched her face do something complicated—watched the ambition and the awe fight each other openly, like two dogs on a rope.

"They weren't lying," she said finally. "Elio's journal. The drawings I thought were exaggerations. They weren't lying." Her voice caught on something. "He tried to tell me. Seven years ago he tried to tell me what this was, and I thought he'd gone native, gone soft, lost his nerve." She touched the journal through her jacket. "I owe a dead man an apology I can't deliver."

"He knew you'd come back," Okafor said. The fever was rising again, softening the edges of everything, making him brave. "People like Elio always know. That's why he left the letter where he did. Not hidden. *Placed.* For whoever followed."

Voss looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face—the sweat, the shaking hands, the fever-brightness—made her nod slowly, once, as if accepting a verdict.

Then Wendell called from the head of the column, and they moved onto the terrace, following the channel upstream, and Okafor walked between Delia and Bram and let the wonder and the mortality share him equally, like two porters carrying a load between them.

* * *

He began keeping notes that evening, because that was the last professional thing he could think to do. If the fever took him all the way, someone should have the record.

*Day four. Terraces confirmed artificial. Construction continuous with approach road; single integrated system spanning minimum twelve kilometers. Carved stones function as schematics or ritual documentation—possibly both; distinction may not have existed for builders. Channel flow rate suggests management of seasonal volumes far exceeding local catchment. Conclusion: the Salt Rivergate is not a structure on the river. It is the river's administration.*

He read it back to himself by firelight and thought: *administration.* What a dry word for the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. But it was the right word, and right words were all he had ever had to offer anyone, and he found he was crying a little, quietly, and let himself do that too, because the fever had stripped away everything except what was true, and what was true was that he might die here among wonders, and that would not be the worst trade a surveyor ever made.

Delia sat down beside him. She didn't ask about the tears. She handed him tea and said, "Tell me about the stones again. The slow version. I want to get it right when I write it up someday."

"Someday," Okafor said. "Listen to you."

"Shut up and drink your tea."

So he told her, slowly, and she listened the way good students listen, asking questions that sharpened rather than dulled, and somewhere in the middle of it the fever receded, and the night became ordinary and enormous, full of stars and the sound of the channel running its ancient errand below them.

* * *

On the fifth day they reached the place where the terraces climbed to meet the headwater gorge, and the trail narrowed onto a shelf of cut stone above a drop that made even Wendell go quiet, and that was where Teo stopped them with a raised hand.

He was looking up. They all looked up.

On the ridgeline opposite—two hundred meters above, sharp against the sky—a figure stood. Watching. Then a second figure joined it, and a glint of glass: binoculars, or a scope.

"Those aren't rangers," Bram said softly. He'd gone very still, in the way Okafor had learned meant the fixer was doing fast arithmetic. "Rangers don't post sentries on a ridge they haven't hiked to. That's a relay. Somebody's been expecting us."

"The charter plane," Delia whispered.

Below them, the channel ran on, indifferent, carrying its water toward the gate as it had for five hundred years. Okafor gripped his staff and felt the fever lift one last time into a clarity that felt almost like a gift, and heard himself say:

"Then we'd better hurry. The machine is almost done remembering us."
