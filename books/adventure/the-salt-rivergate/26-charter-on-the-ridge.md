## 26. Charter on the Ridge

Delia heard the plane before she saw it, and by then it was already too late to pretend they hadn't.

The sound came down the valley like a dropped wrench—twin engines, heavy, flying a pattern no mail run or medevac would ever fly. She was on point, breaking trail through the alder with Okafor's grandson Toma leading them on paths only his feet seemed to know, when she stopped and held up a fist the way her father had taught her, back when she'd been a girl working oil rigs in a country that no longer existed.

Toma stopped too. He was maybe sixteen, all elbows and patience, and he cocked his head at the sky the way Wendell did, listening with his whole body.

"Charter," he said. "Big one."

"Everyone off the trail," Delia said. "Trees. Now."

They melted into the green without argument—that was the thing about this crew, she'd learned; whatever else they were, they moved like people who had been hunted before—and from under the canopy she watched the plane come over the western ridge. It was a twin-otter conversion, stripped for cargo, and it came in low along the ridgeline with its flaps hanging like a bird deciding whether to land or die. It touched down on the long meadow shelf above them, bounced twice, rolled out, and turned.

Doors opened. Men got out.

Delia counted eight before the fuselage blocked her view. They moved well—not soldiers exactly, but men who'd been paid to move like them. Crates came off behind them, long crates, the kind that made her stomach drop because she knew what fit in long crates.

She looked at Toma. The boy's face had gone very still, very old.

"You knew about this place," she said quietly. "The ridge strip. Who uses it?"

"No one," he said. "It is not a place. It is a scar." He paused. "My grandfather says the rivergate must never be owned. He says men have come before."

"How long ago?"

"Before I was born." Toma shouldered his pack. "They went up to the gate. They did not come back down. The river took them, my grandfather says. But my grandfather also says the river does not kill. People do."

* * *

They found Wendell and Voss fifty meters back along the trail, crouched over the map case while rain dripped off everything in the world. Delia gave them thirty seconds to absorb the news, watching their faces do different things—Wendell going flat and calm, the pilot-face, the face he wore when the downdraft hit; Voss going sharp and bright, like a blade being told it finally had something to cut.

"Eight men, maybe more," Delia said. "Long crates. They're between us and the gate road, but below the old terraces. If we keep moving we can be at the gate before they sort out their approach."

"And if they don't sort out an approach," Voss said, "they'll simply walk down and take it. Eight against five, and one of our five is a boy."

"I'm not asking anyone to fight them," Delia said. Then she stopped, because she realized she was, actually, about to ask exactly that—or something adjacent to it, something worse and better. She'd been turning it over since the ridge, since the sound of those engines, and now that it was out of her mouth there was no taking it back. "I'm asking some of us not to go to the gate at all. Not yet. I'm asking for a decoy."

Wendell looked up. Rain ran off his jaw. "Say it."

"They think they're chasing five people and a journal. Fine. So we give them five people and a journal—moving loud, moving visible, on the low trail toward the ford, where any sane person would go if they wanted to leave this valley. Bram knows how to make a party look bigger than it is. Noise discipline is just noise management in reverse. Meanwhile you two take Toma and the high route—the gate road, the carved steps, whatever the hell your elder's map calls it. You get there first, you get inside, and you find out what we're actually defending."

"And the decoy party?" Voss asked. Her voice was even. Too even. "What happens to them when eight armed men catch up?"

"That's the part I'm volunteering for," Delia said.

Silence, except the rain.

"You don't have to—" Wendell started.

"Yes, I do." She said it plainly, without drama, because drama would have been a lie and she was tired of lies; she'd spent three weeks lying by omission about Bram, about the transmitter, about how much fear she carried, and she was done. "Listen to me. Nobody on this crew has a single good reason to be here. You're flying a debt. She's flying a threat. Okafor's shot and feverish back at the village. Bram was paid to betray us and picked us instead, which means he's got the most to lose and the least to gain. And I'm here because seven years ago I walked away from a job I should have stayed on, and I've been walking away from things ever since." She wiped rain out of her eyes. "Every excuse not to be brave. All of us. So I figure courage is the only thing left worth choosing freely."

Wendell stared at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not gratitude exactly, something older and more startled than that, the look of a man realizing he'd been seen.

"Bram goes with you," he said finally. "He knows their playbook—he was almost part of it. And he owes you." A ghost of a smile. "He told me. In his way. Which is to say he fixed my radio without being asked and pretended it was routine maintenance."

"He did mention that," Delia said dryly.

Voss hadn't spoken. She was looking at the ridge, at the gray shape of the charter plane, and her hands were steady but her voice, when it came, wasn't quite. "I've spent this whole expedition spending other people," she said. "Their money, their loyalty, their lives. Okafor took a bullet for my cargo." She turned. "I won't spend you, Delia. I'm ordering you to refuse."

"You can't order me to refuse," Delia said. "You bought my contract fair and square. Best money you ever spent." She stuck out her hand, and after a moment Voss took it, and Delia felt the fine tremor in the other woman's grip and understood, suddenly and completely, everything Marguerite Voss was afraid of. It wasn't the buyer. It had never been the buyer.

"Get to the gate," Delia said. "Write it down, photograph it, memorize it—I don't care. Just make sure that when this is over, there's something left worth having defended."

* * *

They split at the fork below the terraces, where the trail divided into the low river path and the steep climb toward the gate road. There was no ceremony. Wendell gripped Delia's shoulder once, hard, the way pilots say things they can't say out loud. Bram checked the loads on both decoy radios, slung his pack, and gave Voss a look that contained an entire unsaid sentence—which, knowing Bram, probably did.

Then it was four: Delia, Bram, and two of Toma's cousins who'd caught up with them at dawn, river boys with slingshots and an unshakable belief in their own invincibility, which Delia decided to treat as an asset rather than a liability.

The plan was simple, as plans made in rain always are. Make noise. Be seen. Move toward the ford like people running. Let the eight men commit to the chase. And then, at the right moment, vanish into country that Delia didn't know and Bram didn't know and the pursuers definitely didn't know—but that two river boys knew like their own teeth.

Bram worked the first hour like an artist. He rigged a radio on a delay timer, propped in the crook of a tree playing looped static-and-voice traffic, so that anyone sweeping frequencies would hear a party coordinating. He kicked over stones on the trail. He let himself be glimpsed—a figure crossing a slide slope, gone again. By midday they'd been "seen" three times, and Delia could feel the pursuit behind them like weather, a pressure in the trees, men spreading out into a line because that's what trained men did when they thought their quarry was fleeing.

"They're committed," Bram murmured, dropping back beside her. He was breathing hard but his voice was level. "Line formation, pushing downhill. They'll reach the ford by dark if we let them."

"We're not letting them," Delia said. "We're letting them think they're winning."

One of Toma's cousins—Kesh, the older one, with a laugh like a rockslide—grinned at her from uptrail. "You are enjoying this," he said.

"No," Delia said honestly. "But I'm not hating it, either. That's new."

* * *

The turn came at midafternoon, at a place where the low trail crossed a scree field beneath a cliff the color of wet iron. Beyond it, the river roared through a gorge, and the ford—their supposed destination—waited half a kilometer downstream, wide and shallow and obvious.

That was where Delia stopped being prey.

"Here," she said. Kesh and his cousin peeled off uphill without being told, taking the decoy radio with them, climbing toward the rimrock where a boy with a sling and a transmitter could become an army. Bram set the second radio in a rock cleft, timer running, volume up. Then he and Delia stood together in the scree and watched the line of men come down through the trees below them.

Seven visible now. One holding back near the ridge, probably the leader, probably carrying the satellite phone that mattered.

"Now we hurt them," Bram said quietly. "Not kill. Hurt. Enough that they stop believing this is easy."

"What did you have in mind?"

"Their radios tell them we're ahead of them and splitting up. Their eyes tell them the same. So we make their ears lie." He nodded uphill. "Your boys start throwing rocks. Big ones, from the rim. Every man will turn toward the sound, and every man will report movement uphill, and within ten minutes they'll believe there are a dozen of us above them instead of two below them."

"And while they're looking up?"

"While they're looking up," Bram said, "we take their leader's phone."

Delia looked at him. "That's not a decoy plan. That's a raid."

"Yes," Bram said. "I'm a fixer. Decoys are just raids that haven't committed yet." For the first time since she'd known him, he smiled, and it changed his whole face, made him look younger and more dangerous and somehow more honest. "You chose this freely, Delia. So did I. Let's be good at it."

* * *

It went wrong in the ways plans go wrong, which is to say: quickly, loudly, and survivably.

The rocks came off the rim like thunder—Kesh had found a natural chute and was feeding it boulders, and the sound in the gorge was apocalyptic, echoing, multiplying, sounding exactly like the army they weren't. The pursuit line broke exactly as Bram predicted, men scrambling for cover, radios crackling with reports of contact from multiple directions, and the leader—gray-haired, deliberate, hanging back with his phone—did the worst possible thing.

He climbed. Toward the sound. Alone, wanting to see with his own eyes, because men who buy valleys always want to see.

He came up the scree with his rifle slung and his phone in hand, and Bram took him from the side, low and fast, and it was over in six seconds—the man face-down in the scree with Bram's knee between his shoulders and the phone skittering away across the stone. Delia caught it, thumbed it awake, and found it open, unlocked, arrogant with certainty.

On the screen: a message thread. Coordinates. Timetables. And a directive, timestamped that morning, in clean corporate language that made her blood cold:

*Upon confirmation of salvage integrity, proceed to total site remediation. No assets remain on-site. No witnesses remain on-site.*

No witnesses.

She looked up. Below them, the pursuit line was regrouping, angry, confused, shooting at echoes. Above them, Kesh's avalanche was still rolling. Somewhere south, a helicopter was warming up somewhere she couldn't see, coming for a valley that a reasonable, civilized man had decided to erase.

"Bram," she said. "They're not here for the salvage."

He looked up from the pinned man, and read her face, and understood.

"They're here to bury it," he said. "All of it. Everyone in it."

Downstream, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind up the gorge: the rhythmic thrash of helicopter blades, still distant, still minutes out—but closing.

Delia keyed the decoy radio one last time, knowing Wendell might be close enough to hear, and sent the only warning she had time to send.

"Wendell. Whatever you're doing at that gate—do it faster."
