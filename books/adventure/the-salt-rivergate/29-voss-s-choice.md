## 29. Voss's Choice

Marguerite Voss had spent thirty years learning to read men the way pilots read weather, and she had never once been wrong about the shape of a threat. She read it now in the way Aldous Wren's advance team had spread themselves along the ridge—not as guards, but as an erasure crew. Four men with rifles and no interest in prisoners. A fifth at the treeline with a radio and a checklist posture. And Wren himself descending the slope toward the gate road with his hands clasped behind his back like a man touring a garden he intended to pave.

She had told herself, for eleven days, that she was the instrument and not the target. That her contract made her valuable enough to survive. That confirmation of salvage was worth more than silence.

The lie curdled somewhere over the last pass, when she'd watched the charter plane make its second orbit of the valley—slow, deliberate, photographing not the gate but the village downstream. The smokehouses. The fish racks. The school roof patched with tin from Wendell's supply drops.

Nobody photographs witnesses unless they're inventorying them.

Now she stood at the edge of the gate terrace with Elio's journal against her ribs and understood, with the cold clarity she usually reserved for balance sheets, that she had been hired to be the last witness who could plausibly be blamed. *Voss went native,* they'd say. *Voss turned on us.* Her death would close the ledger twice: once for the valley, once for her reputation. Wren didn't need to lift a finger himself. He only needed to wait until the terrain did the work, or until one of his men got nervous, or until Bram's leash pulled taut enough to snap.

She had been a tool with a return address, and the return address was a hole in the ground.

*You knew,* said a voice in her that sounded uncomfortably like Elio Marchetti, seven years younger, leaning on the rail of a survey barge with that grin that made competence look like joy. *You suspected before you signed.*

She had. She'd suspected and signed anyway, because the alternative was admitting that the most brilliant thing she'd ever touched—the expedition, the discovery, the whole blazing season of it—had ended in ash and a lie she'd helped bury. Because if the gate could be owned, then Elio hadn't died for nothing. He'd died early, that was all. Ahead of schedule. A rounding error on the path to something magnificent.

That arithmetic had kept her warm for seven years. It was failing her now, here, in the rain, watching Wren's men check their sightlines the way you check sightlines when the thing you're hunting is people.

*

Wendell Ishikawa crouched at the sluice mechanism forty meters below her, working beside Bram with the focused silence of two men who had decided, separately and without discussion, that they trusted each other exactly as far as this task required. Delia was somewhere upslope with the decoy party, feeding false positions into the stolen transmitter rhythm. Okafor lay fevered in the village, alive because villagers had carried medicine up through the rain for a man they'd never met—medicine Marguerite now realized Wendell had delivered, year after year, to people whose names he pretended not to keep.

She had bought his debt to own him. She had flown him into the throat of the range to use him. And every step of the traverse, he had spent himself keeping her crew alive as though survival were simply what you did with people, the way water did with valleys—found the low places and filled them.

Elio had been like that. She had told herself for years that Elio's softness was weakness, that Wendell's caution was cowardice, that the men who hesitated deserved what the mountain gave them. She had built a career on that creed. It had made her rich and it had made her alone and it had, this morning, put four rifles on a ridge above the only people who had ever let her walk among them without wanting anything back.

Wren reached the lower terrace. He saw her and smiled—the reasonable smile, the civilized one, the one that had closed a hundred deals and would close this one too, he believed.

"Marguerite." His voice carried easily across the wet stone; he wasn't shouting, just confident the acoustics were his. "You've done remarkable work under difficult conditions. I want you to know that."

"Do you."

"The board is grateful. The recovery phase will proceed smoothly now that we have your survey confirmations." He gestured at the gate behind her—the vast engineered throat of it, terraces and sluice gates and carved stone older than any nation, water moving through it in channels cut by hands ten centuries dead. "We'll begin controlled dismantling in spring. You'll want to be well clear by then, of course."

"Of course," she said. "And the valley?"

He tilted his head, patient with her, the way one is patient with a colleague having a sentimental moment. "What valley?"

And there it was. Not even a euphemism. Just the flat professional blankness of a man declining to acknowledge a fact that was about to stop existing.

"The eight hundred people downstream," she said. "The watershed. Every village that drinks from this river."

"Marguerite." He sighed, kindly. "Flood dynamics in unregulated systems are notoriously unpredictable. If something were to happen during decommissioning—an uncontrolled release, say—well. These things happen in remote country. Terrible tragedy. No one's fault." He spread his hands. "No one will even know there was anyone to blame."

She had imagined, in the long rain-soaked nights of the traverse, that this moment would feel like betrayal. It didn't. It felt like reading a contract she'd already signed, finally reaching the clause in small print. She had known. She had known and she had climbed into his helicopter economy anyway, because knowing and stopping are different verbs, and she had spent her whole life confusing them.

"You need my final report," she said. "The structural assessment. Without it your engineers can't touch the primary sluices safely."

"I have your interim reports."

"Interim." She shook her head. "You have my interim reports, which I wrote to be reassuring. The final assessment has the real numbers. Load tolerances. Failure modes. The sections of the gate that will kill your crews if they cut in the wrong order." She let him watch her understand it out loud. "Which means you need me alive until I hand it over. Which means—" she looked deliberately at the riflemen on the ridge, then back at him, letting him see that she'd counted them too— "that whatever happens next, it isn't supposed to include me surviving the paperwork."

For the first time, Wren's smile lost a degree of temperature. Not much. A man like him never gave you much. But she had negotiated against him for a decade and she could read a half-degree the way Wendell read cloud strata.

"You're being dramatic," he said.

"I'm being accurate. It's the same skill. You've just never paid me for this version."

Below them, Wendell had gone still at the sluice housing. Bram had shifted his weight, angling his body between the ridge and the mechanism—a small movement, instinctive, the movement of a man guarding something rather than stealing it. She catalogued it and moved on.

Here was the thing about power, she thought. Everyone believed power was the ability to take what you wanted. But she had sat in enough boardrooms to know better. Power was the ability to make other people believe their options were narrower than they were. Wren had narrowed hers beautifully: cooperate and die quietly later, resist and die loudly now. Classic structure. She'd have admired it in a prospectus.

But options were only narrow if you accepted the frame. And she had spent thirty years teaching junior analysts one lesson above all others: *when the deal looks closed, you've stopped looking at the table.*

Her evidence wasn't in the final report. Her evidence was everywhere—in the survey logs Okafor had risked his life carrying, in the photographs of the villages, in the correspondence she'd kept since the first expedition because some hoarding instinct in her had refused to destroy paper that proved what she'd seen. Seven years of receipts. She had brought them all, tucked into waterproof sleeves inside her pack, because she had told herself it was leverage.

Leverage against whom? she asked herself now. Leverage for what?

There was only one honest answer, and it had been waiting for her since the storm gate, since Voss's version of the story had come out of her own mouth and tasted, for the first time, like someone else's lie.

*

"Wendell!" she called down.

His head came up. Rain ran off the brim of his cap. Even at forty meters she could see the wariness settle over him—he had learned, these weeks, to brace whenever she raised her voice, and she hated that she'd taught him that.

"Your friend's journal," she called. "The letter the elder gave you. It named the organization. Did it name the man?"

A pause. Wind moved the wet grass along the terrace edge.

"It described him," Wendell said slowly. "'A man who talks about water like it's money.'"

Marguerite laughed—one short, ugly note—and turned back to Wren.

"Aldous. Do you know how Elio Marchetti described you? In a letter written the night before he died?" She took a step toward the terrace edge, putting herself deliberately between Wren's line of approach and the sluice works below. "He wrote that you'd offered him everything he ever wanted, and that the offer frightened him more than the mountain did, because he couldn't find the catch. He spent his last week looking for the catch. He found it. And then he burned."

Something flickered in Wren's face. Recognition, maybe. Or the small irritation of a man hearing his own history recited by someone he'd underestimated.

"Elio was unstable," Wren said. "Brilliant, but unstable. The altitude, the isolation—"

"He found the catch, Aldous. And so did I." She shrugged the pack off her shoulder, slow and deliberate, and held it up between them. "Seven years of documentation. Correspondence. The original survey contracts, including the indemnity clauses that assign liability for 'unintended hydrological consequences' to field contractors—which is me, which was Elio, which is every person you send into a place you intend to erase. Copies exist off-site. Dead-man releases. Three of them. If I don't check in by satellite phone every seventy-two hours, everything goes to regulators, journalists, and the descendants of everyone who ever signed one of your indemnity clauses."

It was half true. The dead-man releases existed—she'd set them up years ago as insurance against her own employers, a standard paranoia of her profession. But the off-site copies were thinner than she made them sound, and the satellite phone in her pack had been dead since the ford ambush, and Wren might know it, might already have intercepted the traffic, might—

Might. She watched him calculate. Watched him weigh the cost of calling her bluff against the cost of her being right. This was the table she'd stopped looking at, and now she looked at all of it: the riflemen shifting uncertainly on the ridge, because men with rifles hate ambiguity; Bram's stillness below; Wendell rising slowly to his feet with his eyes fixed on her, reading her the way she'd once taught him to read weather, back when they were young and she was generous.

"You're threatening me," Wren said, "with paper."

"I'm threatening you with the truth, which is worse. Paper burns. Truth gets retyped." She let the pack hang at her side, casual, a prop in a negotiation only she could see the full shape of. "Here's the new arrangement. Your men stand down from the ridge. Your charter leaves. You go home and tell your board the gate is structurally unsalvageable—I'll write the report myself, and it will be convincing, because unlike you I actually understand what this place is. And in exchange, my dead-man releases sleep forever, and you get to remain a respectable man instead of becoming a case study."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in seventy-two hours you become the most thoroughly documented criminal in three jurisdictions, and every contractor who ever worked for you starts doing arithmetic about indemnity clauses." She smiled, and was surprised to find it didn't hurt. "You taught me that move, Aldous. Chapter six of your own negotiation seminar. *Never threaten what you can't document; never document what you can't deliver.*"

The rain thickened. Somewhere above, thunder rolled along the range like a door being tested.

Wren looked at her for a long moment, and she watched the decent man drain out of his face entirely, leaving the efficient one, and she understood that she had perhaps ninety seconds before efficiency won. He would not accept the frame. Men like him never did. He would decide she was bluffing, or he would decide that seventy-two hours was time enough to solve her, and either way the rifles on the ridge would come down off safe.

Which meant the negotiation was over and the spending had begun.

She turned her head and shouted down the terrace, clear and flat, every word placed like cargo:

"Wendell! Bram! Whatever you're building down there—finish it! I'll buy you the time!"

Then she drew the flare pistol from her belt—Okafor's, borrowed at the ford, the one piece of armament the ambush had left them—and fired it straight up into the rain, red starburst blooming over the gate like a wound in the sky, the oldest signal in the mountains, the one every villager downstream would recognize:

*Trouble here. Come running.*

The riflemen on the ridge flinched toward the light. Wren took one sharp step forward, saying her name in a voice stripped of all its civilization, and Marguerite Voss stood her ground on the wet stone with an empty flare pistol in her hand and seven years of receipts at her hip, and felt—for the first time since a burning night on this same river—exactly where she stood.

From below, faint through the rain, came the sound of Bram laughing.

Then the ridge answered the flare.
