## 30. Sabotage

Bram had spent twenty-two years learning how things broke.

Locks, engines, contracts, marriages—he'd studied the failure points of all of them, professionally, dispassionately, the way a surgeon studies anatomy. It was what made him valuable. A man who knew exactly where a thing would give way could be paid to make it give way on schedule. He'd never once, in all those years, been paid to do the opposite.

Now he crouched in the throat of a two-thousand-year-old sluice gate with a wrench, a sledgehammer, and Wendell Ishikawa's handwriting on a scrap of paper, and he was being asked to make something *unbreakable*.

"You're sure about this one?" Bram called back through the stone channel. His voice came out flattened by the acoustics, swallowed by wet granite.

Wendell's head appeared around the corner of the passage, rain streaming off his jaw. "The counterweight linkage? Yes. That's the whole point."

"The whole point is that if Voss's employer sends engineers down here—and they will—they'll need to believe they can fix whatever we've done. Otherwise they don't touch it. They go around it." Bram tapped the massive iron arm overhead, green with centuries of patina, still seated true in its socket. "You break a thing past repair, a smart man walks away and finds another way in. You break a thing that looks *almost* repairable—"

"—he spends three days fixing it," Wendell finished. "And then he opens the upper sluice anyway, because his boss told him to, and the whole mechanism comes apart in his hands."

"And takes half the hillside with it."

"And takes half the hillside with him," Wendell said. "Yes."

Bram looked at the linkage again. It was beautiful work, honestly. Whoever had built this place had understood iron the way Delia understood engines—not as material but as argument. Every component made its case for why it belonged where it was. The counterweight arm was the pivot of the entire lower gate: lift it, and the flood doors swung; shear it, and nothing downstream of the headwaters ever controlled water again.

His plan was to leave it standing and hollow it out.

"Give me the drill," he said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the monsoon leaned on the valley like a drunk leaning on a doorframe. Rain came in sheets, then paused as if thinking better of it, then came again harder. The gate complex itself stayed strangely dry—a fact Okafor, feverish and half-lucid on the stretcher they'd left under an overhang near the entrance, had found fascinating before sleep took him again. The channels drained. The terraces above shed water into cut stone gutters that had been doing their job since before anyone alive had a language for the job they were doing.

Bram understood structures. He did not understand this one, not entirely, and the not-understanding sat in him like a swallowed hook.

"It's a machine," he said, during one of the pauses, when the rain let up enough to hear yourself think. "That's what I keep coming back to. Everyone keeps saying *the gate*, like it's a wall or a treasure. It's not. It's a machine with one moving part and no operator."

Wendell was coiling rope, hands moving without looking at it. "It has an operator."

"Who?"

"The river."

Bram turned that over. He'd heard mysticism from clients before—the wealthy were prone to it—but Ishikawa didn't have the tone of a man selling anything. He had the tone of a man stating freight weights.

"Explain it like I'm stupid," Bram said. "I'm paying attention either way."

Wendell set the coil down. "The upper sluice fills during high water. Weight of the water against these doors builds until the counterweight arms trip—that's the mechanism, right there, the arms we're working around. When they trip, the doors open just far enough to bleed off the surge into the spill channels. Then the weight drops, the doors close. No valves, no gears, no one turning anything. The river regulates itself through this thing. Has been for—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the terraces, the carved stones, the whole engineered spine of the valley "—a very long time."

"So the salvage everyone wants—"

"Is the mechanism. The metallurgy. The design. Voss's employer doesn't want gold, Bram. He wants *the idea*. Take it apart, patent it, sell flood control to governments at ten times what it costs to build. Or worse—sell the *absence* of it. Show a downstream country what happens when you remove a gate like this." Wendell's mouth went thin. "Elio figured out the second option first. That's why we buried it."

Bram nodded slowly. In his experience, men who talked about dead friends either wanted sympathy or wanted you to stop asking questions. Ishikawa wanted neither. He wanted to get back to work. Bram respected that more than he could say.

"Then here's my problem," Bram said, and held up the drill bit. "I can hollow the counterweight arm so it looks intact and shears under real load. I can pack the pivot housings with grit so any attempt to force the doors grinds the sockets to mush. I can do six other things I won't describe out loud. But every one of those failsafes assumes someone tries to *operate* the gate wrong. What if they don't operate it at all? What if they just bring cutting torches and take the arms away piece by piece?"

Wendell was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on stone.

"Then we've failed," he said. "So we make sure they can't get to the arms with torches until they've already committed to operating the gate. Come here—I'll show you what Elio saw the night we found this place."

* * *

What Elio had seen, according to Wendell, was a room.

Not a chamber—a room, in the domestic sense. Off the main channel, behind a slab door that weighed perhaps four hundred pounds but swung on a stone hinge so perfectly balanced that Wendell, a man of maybe eighty kilos, could move it with one hand. Inside: benches. Niches for lamps. A sloped channel in the floor that carried a trickle of water through the space, constant, cool—the original builders' answer to air conditioning, or prayer, or both.

And on the walls, in relief carving worn shallow by time: diagrams. The gate itself, exploded into components. The counterweight arms. The door profiles. Annotations in a script nobody living could read, arranged with the confident redundancy of an engineering manual.

"They documented themselves," Wendell said, running his light across the walls. "Every part. Right here, next to the parts. Seven years ago I stood in this room and thought: this is the most valuable thing in the valley, and it can't be moved, and whoever understands that will protect it, and whoever doesn't will destroy it trying."

"Elio wanted to sell it."

"Elio wanted to sell it," Wendell agreed. "For about four days. Then he read the annotations long enough—with Okafor's help, actually, though Okafor doesn't remember—to realize the annotations weren't instructions. They were warnings. There's a sequence to dismantling this gate. Do it in the right order, over months, with the water low, and you can take it apart clean. Do it in the wrong order—or fast, or in flood season—and the upper reservoir lets go all at once." He looked at Bram. "Everything downstream. Every village we've slept in. The people who fed us."

Bram felt the hook in his gut twist. He thought of the village that counted planes. He thought of Delia teaching children to gap spark plugs, if they all lived that long. He thought, unwelcome and unbidden, of every job he'd ever taken where the client's plan ended somewhere past the horizon and he'd made himself comfortable not looking.

"So the sabotage isn't the arms," he said slowly.

"The sabotage is the *room*," Wendell said. "We leave it. We leave every diagram, every warning, everything. Let them study it. Let them think they understand the sequence. And then we alter the one thing the diagrams can't show them—the condition of the metal inside the arms. Their own documentation will walk them into our trap. That's the beauty of it. We're not fighting them. We're letting their greed do the work."

Bram stared at him. "You learned this from a fixer?"

"I learned it from watching you," Wendell said, and went back to the rope.

* * *

They worked until dark, and then by lamplight, because the schedule didn't care about dark. The monsoon window was closing. Somewhere up on the ridge, the buyer's advance team was regrouping after the ford, and somewhere beyond them, the charter plane sat on a strip with men who wouldn't negotiate. Two days, Wendell guessed. Maybe three. Bram put it tighter. He'd been the kind of man who arrived in places like this; he knew how they traveled.

The work itself was the best of his life, and he knew it even as he did it, the way you know a perfect landing when you're still in the air.

He began with the counterweight arm nearest the main channel—the one any competent engineer would test first. Drilled into its core through a grease port that hadn't seen grease in centuries, following a path Wendell marked from the wall diagrams, until the drill sank into a cavity the original builders had left deliberately: a void, packed with resin, designed so the arm could be *sacrificed* in emergency. The ancients had built their own sabotage into the gate. All Bram had to do was restore it.

"That's why the diagrams warn about flood season," he breathed. "They could drop the arm themselves. Scuttle the whole system rather than let an enemy hold it."

"Now you see it," Wendell said quietly. "This place was built by people who expected to lose. Build accordingly."

Bram built accordingly.

He reamed the second arm's pivot housing and repacked it with a grit-and-grease slurry that would run smooth for exactly as many cycles as a survey team would need to convince themselves everything worked. He weakened the shear pin on the auxiliary spill door—not enough to fail now, not enough to fail in inspection, but enough to fail on the eleventh hard pull, which was how many pulls a man in a hurry would make. He did things to the winch anchor that he declined to name aloud even to Wendell, who watched him do them and asked no questions, which Bram decided was the single most restful professional relationship of his career.

Delia came down once, near midnight, with food and news. She moved through the lamplight like someone conserving herself for a longer fight.

"Ridge sent scouts down the trail," she reported. "Two of them, careful, good fieldcraft. I counted them from the terrace and got out before they saw me. They'll find the gate by morning."

"Then we're done here by morning," Wendell said.

Delia looked at the two of them—at the drill, the lamp, the scrap of paper covered in Wendell's hand, the ancient iron gleaming wetly where Bram had opened it to the sky. Something crossed her face that Bram couldn't read and didn't try to.

"You know what's funny," she said. "I've watched you two for weeks. Ishikawa, the honest man pretending he's small. Bram, the liar pretending he's simple. And put you in a room with a broken thing and you're identical. Same hands. Same patience." She set down the food. "Get some sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow you'll want steady hands."

She climbed back toward the surface. Bram listened to her footsteps fade.

"She's right," he said finally. "About the identical part. I hate it."

"I know," Wendell said. "Eat your food."

* * *

Dawn came gray and loud. The rain had settled into a rhythm now—hard, pause, harder—and the river below the gate had risen visibly overnight, brown water climbing the gauge stones in the outer channel. Bram stood at the entrance with Wendell and looked down-valley at the country they were arming.

"Walk me through it one more time," Wendell said. "From their side."

Bram closed his eyes. He was good at this—stepping into the mind of whoever came next. It had made him a good thief and a better tracker and a terrible person to trust, and today, for the first time, it made him something else.

"They arrive. Scouts confirm the structure matches the diagrams—because it does, we changed almost nothing visible. They report back. The buyer sends his engineers, because the prize is worth engineers. The engineers read the walls, they read the sequence, they test the mechanisms gently, and everything works. Smooth pivots, sound arms, functional doors. So they commit." He opened his eyes. "They wait for the water to rise—it's rising already, faster than they'll expect—and they open the upper sluice to relieve pressure, per the diagrams, per the sequence. First pull works. Second works. Third. They relax. Eleventh pull, the shear pin goes, the auxiliary door jams half-open, and the load transfers to the counterweight arms—which are hollow. One shears. The other binds in grit. And the upper reservoir, which by then is holding everything the monsoon can give it, starts eating through a door that will never close again."

"How bad?"

"Bad," Bram said. "But survivable—if, and only if, they evacuate downstream immediately and never come back. The flood scours the approach channels, buries the mechanism under fifty tons of gravel, and ends the conversation permanently. The gate dies as salvage. The valley loses some fields. Nobody loses their lives, provided the villages get warning." He glanced sideways. "Which is why your radio plan matters more than anything I drilled this week."

"Delia's already rigged it. Decoy traffic, ranger frequency, the works." Wendell was quiet a moment. "You realize what we've built."

"A trap."

"A promise," Wendell said. "Seven years late. Elio swore this place would stay closed. I ran from keeping that oath until it was the only thing left." He put out his hand—rough, rain-cold, steady. "Your work will keep it. Whatever else happens next."

Bram looked at the hand. In twenty-two years, clients had paid him, threatened him, praised him, and once tried to kill him. No one had ever shaken it.

He took the hand and held it.

"Whatever happens next," Bram repeated. "About that."

"What?"

He nodded up-trail, where the rain thinned between the trees. Two figures stood at the edge of the clearing, rifles slung, rain gear dark with water—one tall, one short, both very still, both very much not scouts. The short one raised a hand, not in greeting. In *stop*.

Behind them, faint through the weather, came the sound Bram had been dreading since the ford: rotors. A helicopter, descending toward the ridge, unhurried, certain, arriving like a man who believed the building was already his.

"Well," Bram said, easing his body between the strangers and the gate. "They're early."
