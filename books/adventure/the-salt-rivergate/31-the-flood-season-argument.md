## 31. The Flood Season Argument

The sound reached them before the meaning did—a deep, wrong note rolling up the gorge like a struck bell, the kind of sound water makes when it is told to go somewhere it has never been allowed to go.

Wendell was on the lower terrace of the gate complex, elbow-deep in Bram's sabotage work, when the valley answered. He felt it through his boots first: a tremor in the old stone, faint and patient, as if the mountain itself had cleared its throat. Then the river below them changed color, going from green-glass clarity to the brown of stirred silt, and every bird on the water lifted at once into a sky that had been threatening rain for two days without delivering.

Bram stood up from the sluice housing, wrench frozen mid-turn. "Tell me that wasn't what I think it was."

"That was the upper sluice," Wendell said. His voice came out flat and strange, the voice of a man reading his own death certificate. "They've breached it."

* * *

They ran. There was no plan yet, no argument, just the animal urgency of people who have heard a dam die. The trail up from the lower terraces switchbacked through terraced gardens gone wild—seven years of rain reclaiming what hands had built—and Wendell took it at a pace that tore his breath to ribbons. Voss was ahead of him. She had been ahead of him since dawn, since the radio call from Delia's decoy position on the east ridge: *men with cutting gear at the upper works, at least eight, they're not even hiding anymore.*

The buyer had stopped being reasonable sometime in the night.

Wendell crested the last rise and the upper sluice opened before him, and he understood in one glance that everything he and Bram had rigged—the collapse charges, the shear points, the beautiful elegant ruin they'd designed to kill the gate as salvage while letting it live as function—was now beside the point. Because the gate was already dying.

The breach was crude. They hadn't even used explosives; they'd cut the timber bracing of the auxiliary channel and pried the stone lintels loose with winches, letting the river find its own way through the wound. Water poured through the gap in a brown sheet thirty feet wide, chewing at the edges of the cut, widening it by inches every minute. Below, the middle terraces were already standing in water that should not have been there for another month. The flood season, held back for centuries behind engineered stone, was being released all at once into a valley full of villages.

"They want leverage," Voss said beside him. She was shouting over the water and still her voice sounded controlled, which Wendell knew by now was the most frightening thing about her. "Not destruction. Leverage. They'll let it build until we hand over the journal and stand aside, then they'll close the breach on their own schedule."

"You don't close a breach like that," Wendell said. "You can't. Not once it's running. That's not how any of this works."

"Then they don't know that."

"Then they're willing to drown the valley on a guess." He looked at her. "Is that what your employer does? Guesses?"

Voss didn't answer, and the not-answer was an answer.

* * *

The math came to him the way weather comes—uninvited, total, impossible to argue with. He made himself do it anyway, standing on the wet stone above the breach with the spray hitting him sideways, because this was the one calculation of his life he refused to fudge.

The upper sluice fed three channels. Two of them—the ones the gate's builders had designed to carry the spring melt down to the lowland villages in measured release—were intact. The third was the breached auxiliary, and through it the river was doing what rivers do: finding the lowest path, eroding, accelerating. By his rough count, the flow through the cut would double within six hours. Within twelve, it would undermine the central weir, and then it wouldn't be a leak anymore. It would be a collapse, and everything the gate held back would come down the gorge in one wall, and the villages downstream—Keshari's village, the ford where Okafor had bled, the terraces where children had learned the sound of Wendell's engine before they learned his name—would be under twenty feet of moving river.

There were two ways to stop it.

The first was to save the gate. Fight the breach directly—timber, stone, sandbags, bodies in the water, days of desperate work against a rising river, with eight armed men on the ridge above shooting at anyone who tried. Even if it worked, even if they somehow held the structure, the buyer's men would simply come back with more gear and more time, and the gate would remain exactly what it had always been to people like him: an asset. A thing worth killing for. As long as the Salt Rivergate existed as salvage, there would always be another Marguerite Voss, another polite man in a helicopter, another crew with winches. He could spend the rest of his life defending a machine instead of living in a valley.

The second way was to finish what he and Bram had started—but all the way, tonight, deliberately, on their terms instead of the river's. Trigger the controlled collapse. Drop the central weir in sequence, spill the reservoir in a managed surge down the channels the gate's original builders had cut for exactly this purpose, and let the flood season arrive the way it was always meant to: fast, loud, survivable. The villages downstream had lived with this river for longer than anyone could count. They had high ground. They had memory. What they did not have was a wall of water arriving without warning at three in the morning.

But if he dropped the weir, the gate died. Not as ruin-as-function, not as the careful compromise he and Bram had engineered—fully dead. No mechanism left to own, no sluice worth cutting out and selling, nothing but broken stone and free water. Seven years of hiding, one letter from a dead friend, one woman's confession, one fixer's redemption—all of it cashed in at once, spent on a single irreversible act.

And the buyer's men would know, immediately, that they had lost. Men who lose everything sometimes decide to take witnesses with them.

"Wendell." Voss's hand on his arm. "Talk to me. You've got the face you get before you land something ugly."

He told her. Both options, both prices. She listened the way she listened to nothing else—with her whole body, absolutely still, rain starting to fall around them in fat monsoon drops.

"The villagers," she said finally. "Can they be warned in time? If you go for the managed collapse?"

"If I start now. If someone runs the gorge trail to Keshari's village tonight. If the drums still mean what they meant when Elio—" He stopped. Started again. "If everything goes right, yes. Nothing about today has gone right."

"And if you try to hold the gate instead?"

"Then we fight armed men in floodwater for days, and if we lose—even once—we lose everything downstream. And if we win, we've only postponed this exact morning until the next time someone with money finds out what's up here." He heard his own voice steady down, the pilot's voice, the one that came out when the truth needed to be said plainly or people died. "Marguerite. There's no version of saving the gate that saves the valley. It's one or the other. It was always one or the other. Elio saw it seven years ago and it killed him, and I've been flying mail around the fact ever since."

She turned away from him and looked at the breach, at the brown water eating the world her employers had sent her to measure. When she spoke again her voice had lost its polish entirely.

"He gave me the journal," she said. "Did you know that? In the end. After the fire. He walked into my camp with his eyebrows burned off and his hands bandaged, and he handed it to me and said, *now nobody can buy it, because you'll never sell it.* And I thought—I actually thought—he was entrusting me. It took me years to understand he was disarming me. He put it somewhere I couldn't monetize and called it a gift." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "And then I sold myself to the people hunting it anyway. Because I told myself I'd manage it better than they would. Power over it, instead of—" She gestured at the valley, the drowned terraces, the free-running brown water. "Instead of this."

"Voss."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't absolve me. Just tell me what you need."

"I need Bram. I need everyone downstream warned. And I need you to go down to the middle channel and open the release gates manually—the big iron wheels, the ones the builders put there for flood season. All three. Full open. Can you do that?"

Something moved across her face—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The face of a woman being handed, at last, a job worthy of her.

"Yes," she said. "I can do that."

* * *

Bram took the news standing in the rain with the detonator wire coiled over his shoulder like a bandolier, and Wendell watched him weigh it—the mercenary arithmetic, the new ledger he'd been keeping since the village fed him, since Okafor took a bullet for a book he'd been paid to steal.

"So we're past sabotage," Bram said. "We're at demolition."

"We're at mercy," Wendell said. "Ours, not theirs. We drop the weir in sequence, the surge goes down the release channels, the villages get a hard flood they've survived a hundred times instead of a collapse they wouldn't survive once. And the gate becomes nothing. No salvage. No asset. The whole reason any of these people are here stops existing by tomorrow sunrise."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. Rain ran off his jaw.

"My handler's offer," he said, "was to preserve the gate intact. That was the deal. Confirm it exists, keep it undamaged, and the money keeps flowing." He looked up at the breach, at the buyer's men already visible on the far ridge, small and dark against the gray sky, watching their leverage build. "I'm going to enjoy this more than I should."

"There's a catch," Wendell said. "The sequencing. If the charges fire wrong—if the west abutment lets go before the center—the surge hits the gorge wall and reflects back into the lowland channel. We'd make it worse than the collapse would've been. You built the pattern. Can you fire it wet?"

Bram smiled, and it was the first honest smile Wendell had ever seen on him.

"I wired it for rain," he said. "I wired it for rain the night you told me what the gate was for. Question is whether you can live with it. Once these go, there's no version of your old life left to fly back to. No debt to hide under. No smallness. You'll be the man who broke the Salt Rivergate. Every government, every company, every historian on two continents will know your name, and half of them will hate you forever."

Wendell thought about the airstrip at Drowned Meadow, the bent gear, the long gray traverse. He thought about a village where children counted planes, and an elder who had kept a dead man's letter for seven years because the dead man had mattered to them. He thought about Elio's last line, the ink gone soft with handling: *Some things are only safe when they belong to everyone, and some people are only free when they owe nothing.*

"I've been nobody for seven years," Wendell said. "Turns out nobody was just a place I was hiding. Fire it when she gives the word."

* * *

Voss reached the middle channel wheels forty minutes later, soaked through, having crossed a footbridge that swayed over water running hard enough to carry trees. Wendell watched her from the control terrace through the rain—watched her set her shoulder against the first iron wheel, a woman who had spent her life acquiring things now spending the last of herself opening them—and when the wheel broke free and began to turn, the sound it made was older than any engine, a groan of bronze and stone, and the channel below her throat opened and swallowed the river's first offering.

Downstream, faint through the rain, a drum began to beat.

Then another. Then a dozen, relayed village to village down the watershed, the old signal, the flood-season signal, the one that meant *high ground now* in a language older than any of the governments that would soon be arguing about what happened here. Keshari's grandson had run the gorge trail. The valley was answering.

On the far ridge, the buyer's men had realized what was happening. Muzzle flashes stuttered in the rain, rounds cracking off the stone around Wendell and Bram, too distant to aim well, panicking, pointless. The leverage was dissolving out of their hands gallon by gallon, and they knew it, and men who watch their power drain away through an open sluice do not think clearly.

"Bram." Wendell flattened against the parapet, rain hammering his back. "When the channels are full-open, everything goes. On your mark."

Below, Voss threw her weight against the second wheel. The drumbeat rolled on, closer now, insistent, alive. The river rose through the opened channels in a white rush, and Wendell laid his palm flat on the wet ancient stone of the parapet—the same stone Elio's hands had touched, the same stone the nameless builders had dressed a thousand years ago so that their grandchildren's grandchildren would never go thirsty or drown—and he said the only words that fit:

"It was never yours. None of it was ever ours."

Bram crouched over the firing box, thumb on the key, waiting for the third wheel.

Voss threw the last lever home, turned, and raised her arm high against the rain—

—and on the far ridge, above the buyer's men, a charter plane that should not have been able to fly in this weather dropped out of the clouds with its engines screaming.
