## 33. Helicopter Down

Marguerite Voss had spent thirty years learning how powerful men moved when they were losing, and she had never once seen one run in a straight line. They cut corners. They abandoned baggage. They sacrificed people who had trusted them, and they did it with such smooth efficiency that the sacrifice looked, from a distance, like leadership.

So when the radio crackled at dawn and the buyer's voice came through—calm, reasonable, announcing his withdrawal to "reassess the situation from an operational standpoint"—she knew exactly what it meant. He was leaving. And he was leaving them.

She stood at the edge of the ruined sluice yard with the rain still dripping off her collar and watched Wendell's face as he listened. He'd been awake all night, she knew. They all had. The controlled collapse was twelve hours old; the gate lay open to the sky now, its great engineered channels breached in three places exactly where Bram's charges had been placed, water running free through the wreckage in silver ropes. It was beautiful in the way that only deliberate destruction could be beautiful. She had helped design that destruction. She intended to live long enough to be proud of it.

"He's pulling out," Wendell said, lowering the handset. His voice was flat. "Says he'll send boats for us. Says there's no hard feelings."

"No hard feelings," Voss repeated. She almost laughed. "He's going to burn the evidence on his way out. That includes us."

Okafor sat propped against a supply crate, his bandaged shoulder grey with fever but his eyes clear. Delia crouched beside him, wringing out a cloth. Bram stood apart, arms crossed, watching the treeline with the particular stillness of a man who had decided which side of a line he was standing on and was waiting for the world to confirm it.

"The helicopter can't lift a full load out of this valley in this air," Voss said. "Thin margin. He'll take two men, maybe three. The rest of the advance team stays behind to 'secure the site.'" She let the word sit there. Everyone understood what securing meant when there were witnesses to remove.

"So we stop the helicopter," Delia said.

"With what?" Okafor asked dryly. "Strong language?"

Voss reached into her pack and brought out the last charge. It was a small thing, half a kilo of plastic explosive wrapped in waxed paper, one of the four Bram had carried for the gate work. Three had gone into the sluice mechanisms. This one she had kept back without telling anyone, because Marguerite Voss had survived thirty years in salvage by never giving away her last argument.

"I've been thinking about this since the ford," she said. "Since before the ford, honestly. Since the day I signed the contract and realized I hadn't read the fine print because I didn't want to." She turned the charge over in her hands. "A helicopter is a machine that hates surprises. You don't have to hit it. You just have to make the ground near it untrustworthy."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Seven years ago, on the night everything burned, he had looked at her the same way—not with hatred, but with the terrible clarity of a man finally seeing someone whole. "You've done this before," he said. Not a question.

"Once," she said. "In the Congo. A man tried to leave a dig site with artifacts that belonged to the country he was standing in." She met his eyes. "He walked away. I made sure of it. I've regretted it every day since, and not for the reason you think." She paused. "I regret that I waited until he was holding something over other people's heads. I should have done it the first day he arrived."

* * *

The buyer's camp sat on the gravel bar below the ridge where his charter plane had landed its men two days ago. Through the morning fog they could see the helicopter being prepped—a heavy-lift machine, mud-splattered, its rotors drooping under the wet. Men moved around it with the brisk economy of people following a checklist that did not include them in its outcomes.

Voss went down alone.

That had been her condition. No escort, no covering fire, nothing that would turn an execution into a battle. If the advance team saw a fight forming, they would shoot first and ask questions never. But one woman walking down a gravel bar in the rain, empty-handed except for a canvas satchel—that was a negotiation. Powerful men always believed they could negotiate their way out of anything. It was their defining weakness.

The man who met her at the edge of the camp was not the buyer himself but his operations officer, a compact, courteous man named Reyes whom she had met exactly twice and disliked both times. He had the manner of a hotel manager delivering bad news about a booking.

"Ms. Voss. We weren't expecting you."

"I imagine not."

"Mr. Aldous wants you to know there's a place on the aircraft if you want it. He values your... institutional knowledge."

"Does he." She smiled the smile she used across boardroom tables, the one that showed teeth without warmth. "And the others?"

Reyes's pause was very slightly too long. "The situation on the ground has become complicated. Mr. Aldous feels it would be irresponsible to expose additional personnel to—"

"To witnesses," Voss said. "Yes. I've read the fine print now. All of it."

"Ms. Voss—"

"Tell him I'm coming up to say goodbye in person. He'll want that. He's a sentimental man—you know that better than anyone. Twenty-two years, isn't it? He doesn't leave people behind. He leaves them *arranged*."

Something flickered in Reyes's face—the first crack in the hotel-manager polish—and Voss knew she had guessed right. There was a history there. There was always a history. Powerful men collected loyal subordinates the way rivers collected silt, and eventually the silt remembered what it had buried.

She walked through the camp without hurrying. Tents sagged under the rain. Crates marked with the buyer's corporate shell companies sat stacked for loading—or for burning; she noted the drum of accelerant positioned near the document tent with professional appreciation. At the center, beneath a tarp strung between two trees, Aldous himself stood over a field table studying a map, and he looked up when her shadow fell across it with an expression of genuine, unguarded pleasure.

"Marguerite." He spread his hands. "I hoped you'd come. Sit. Have coffee. It's terrible, but it's hot."

"I'll stand."

"You always do." He gestured at the map. "Look at this. Do you know what we're looking at? Thirty years of work, undone in twelve hours by a bush pilot and a mechanic and—" he waved a hand vaguely toward the ridge, "—whatever Bram is. And you. I misjudged you, Marguerite. I thought you were ambitious. Ambition I can use. It turns out you're *principled*, which is far more expensive."

"I'm neither," Voss said. "I'm a woman who read a letter written by a dead man, and it rearranged my priorities. You should try it sometime. Reading letters. Listening to villagers. Looking at maps that predate your company's founding." She nodded at the map on the table. "That's a lie, by the way. Your own surveyors flagged the gate's hydraulic function eight months ago. You knew what it did downstream. You filed it under 'complications.'"

Aldous's pleasant expression didn't change, but something behind it did—the small recalibration of a man realizing a conversation has stopped being a conversation. "It's a machine," he said. "Machines can be rebuilt. Water can be redirected. What can't be rebuilt is a reputation, Marguerite. Yours is currently attached to an act of industrial sabotage in a sovereign watershed. Come away with me and in five years you'll be a footnote. Stay, and you'll be a case study."

"And if I stay?"

"Then I genuinely hope the rangers reach you before my men finish securing the site." He said it kindly. That was the worst part. He said everything kindly. "Now, if you'll excuse me—we have a weather window, and windows close."

"That they do," Voss agreed. "Yours closes in about ninety seconds."

She turned and walked back down the gravel bar, unhurried, counting. At forty paces she heard the rotor spooling up. At sixty, the helicopter lifted off the gravel in a wall of spray and noise, climbing steeply to clear the ridge, light and fast and empty of everything except Aldous, Reyes, the pilot, and two security men—

At eighty paces, the riverbank under its flight path detonated.

Voss had placed the charge at midnight, wading into the cold shallows with Bram holding the light, wedging it into the undercut beneath the gravel shelf directly below the departure corridor. Not to hit the aircraft—to make the earth lie to it. The blast threw a column of gravel and river water forty feet into the air, and the shockwave caught the helicopter just as it transitioned to forward flight, low and heavy and committed. She watched it yaw sideways with a violence no airframe enjoyed, watched the pilot fight it, watched it claw for altitude with the ugly, dragging sound of a machine that had taken damage it couldn't name.

It cleared the first ridge. It did not clear the second.

The impact was almost gentle from this distance—a crumpling, a bloom of torn metal, a silence that rushed back in behind the fading rotor noise like water filling a footprint. Smoke stood up from the treeline, thin and grey in the rain.

Voss stood in the falling rain on the gravel bar and did not move. Around her, the advance team had frozen—six men with rifles, staring at the smoke, then at her, then at each other, doing the arithmetic of a chain of command that had just ceased to exist overhead.

She walked back into the middle of them, soaked to the skin, and said, in the tone she used for closing deals: "Gentlemen. Your employer is alive—I built that charge to disable, not to kill, and I'd bet his pilot put it down hard enough to walk away from. Which means in about twenty minutes he's going to start walking, and every step he takes toward civilization is a step he takes as a fugitive instead of a chairman. Here's what happens next. The rangers are already en route—have been since yesterday, thanks to a young woman's radio work that your jamming schedule failed to account for. When they arrive, everyone standing on this gravel bar with a rifle is going to have a very simple choice: surrender to a government authority with paperwork, or explain to a court why you opened fire on citizens in a national watershed. I know which option has better dental."

Nobody moved. Then one of the men—a big, weathered Swede with the look of a career soldier who had stopped believing in his employers somewhere around year ten—slowly set his rifle down in the gravel.

"They told us it was a survey contract," he said. Nobody in particular. Maybe to the river.

"I know," Voss said. "They always do."

One by one, the rifles came down.

* * *

The rangers arrived at midday, six of them in a battered patrol boat, led by a captain named Okonjo who had clearly been briefed by radio and clearly did not entirely believe what he'd been briefed on until he saw the wrecked gate, the surrendered mercenaries sitting in a row on the gravel, and Marguerite Voss standing over them with a manifest of evidence in a waterproof case.

"Which one of you is Voss?" he asked.

"I am."

"You're either the most useful criminal I've met this year or the most dangerous law-abiding citizen, and I haven't decided which."

"Neither have I," she said. "Let's find out together. Start with the man walking out of the trees northeast of here with a dislocated shoulder and a briefcase full of documents he was going to burn. He'll claim diplomatic immunity. He doesn't have any—he renounced it quietly three years ago when it became inconvenient, and I have the filing date. Page nine."

Okonjo looked at her for a long moment. Then, remarkably, he almost smiled. "Page nine," he said, and keyed his radio.

They found Aldous at midafternoon, sitting on a fallen log at the edge of a meadow with his shoulder bound with his own shirt, his briefcase open beside him, feeding documents one at a time into a small windproof flame. He did not run when the rangers approached. Running was for people with somewhere left to be.

Voss stood at a distance and watched him being cuffed with the strange, hollow calm of a woman watching a storm finally make landfall after years of forecast. He turned his head and found her through the rain, and for a moment the pleasant mask came back—the boardroom smile, the reasonable man wronged by circumstance.

"You'll testify against me," he said. Not a question.

"Every page," she said. "Every map. Every file I copied the night I understood what you were. I've been building this case since before you knew I was building it. That's the difference between us, Aldous. You collect people. I collect paper."

"They'll offer you a plea deal inside a month. You'll take it. People like us always do."

"Then I'll testify again after the plea deal," she said. "I have time. I've learned how to wait."

They led him away through the wet grass. He went upright, unhurried, still somehow managing to look like a man between meetings, and Voss watched until the trees took him, and felt—nothing like triumph. Something quieter. Something like a door closing on a room she had lived in for thirty years, and the strange clean draft coming through the frame.

Delia found her there. "Okafor's fever broke this morning," she said. "First hour of real sleep in two days. The village medicine worked."

"Good," Voss said. "That's—good." Her voice did something unfamiliar at the end of it, and she covered it by checking her watch, an old habit, though there was nowhere she needed to be.

Downriver, the free-running water of the Salt Rivergate glittered through the breach, running the way it had run before anyone had thought to own it. Voss looked at it for a long time.

"Wendell's at the village," Delia said. "There's a letter. Elio's. The elder's been keeping it." She hesitated. "He's going to want to read it aloud. Tonight, I think. He asked if you'd be there."

Voss closed her eyes briefly. A dead man's handwriting, naming her employer, warning that the gate must stay closed—warning she had ignored for money, for ambition, for the comfortable fiction that fine print was someone else's problem.

"Tell him yes," she said. "Tell him I'll be there. And Delia—" she opened her eyes, and for once did not bother to arrange her face into anything boardroom-shaped, "—tell him I owe him an apology seven years overdue, and I intend to pay it in front of witnesses."

Delia studied her a moment, then nodded slowly. "He'll want to hear that part himself."

Above the valley, the clouds were breaking apart, and the first hard evening light came down the river like something being returned.
