## 34. Aftermath in the Valley

Okafor woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone humming.

The humming was wrong for a hospital, right for a village. It had the unhurried loop of a work song, the kind sung over grinding grain or mending nets. He opened his eyes to a ceiling of woven bamboo and a thread of daylight where the weave had frayed, and for a long, disoriented moment he could not remember what continent he was on, only that his left side hurt with the deep, organized ache of a wound that had been cleaned properly and stitched by someone who knew their business.

Then it all came back. The ford. The water going loud. Delia shouting his name in a voice he'd never heard her use before—flat, command-voice, no humor in it at all—and the crack that wasn't thunder, and the moment when his body made a decision his mind hadn't approved, turning sideways, putting his shoulder between the journal satchel and the man with the rifle.

He'd have done it again. That was the strange part. Lying there in the smoke-smell with his pulse ticking slow and even under a bandage, he tested the memory like a sore tooth and found no regret in it. Only a professional's satisfaction, faint but real: the maps had survived. Everything else was negotiable.

"You're awake." The humming stopped. A woman came into the light—old, broad-faced, hands stained yellow to the wrist from some root or dye. She set down a clay bowl and looked at him the way he imagined she looked at livestock she intended to keep alive: appraising, unimpressed, ultimately kind. "Fever broke two nights ago. You talked a great deal. Mostly about contour lines."

"Did I say anything embarrassing?"

"You said the river was lying about its elevation." She handed him the bowl. Some kind of broth, dense with rice and something bitter and medicinal. "I told you rivers don't lie. You called me a romantic."

Okafor drank. It was good. His hands shook slightly, which annoyed him more than the wound did. "Where is everyone?"

"Working," the woman said, as if this were an answer to a much larger question than he'd asked. "Your friends are fine. The loud one is fixing things that were not broken. The quiet one sits by the river and stares at the ruin. The pilot—" she paused, and something moved behind her face, some private recalculation, "—the pilot is being counted."

"Counted?"

She didn't explain. She took the empty bowl, told him to sleep, and resumed her humming on the way out, and Okafor lay listening to it fade and thought: *contour lines.* Even delirious, apparently, he'd been working. There was a version of himself that found this pathetic. He decided, generously, not to meet that version today.

* * *

He was up and walking by the third day, one hand on the eaves of houses, moving through the village like a surveyor taking inventory of his own survival.

It was a bigger settlement than he'd registered on the way in, half-carried through fever as they'd been. Terraced gardens climbing the valley walls in green steps. A longhouse whose ridgepole was a single carved timber older than any nation he could name. Children everywhere, doing the universal work of children—hauling, chasing, pretending not to stare at strangers—and staring anyway, at Delia especially, who had acquired a following of maybe eight of them who trailed her from engine to engine like ducklings.

That was the other thing he was taking stock of: the aircraft. Both of them, in their ways. Wendell's plane sat on the meadow shelf above the village where they'd coaxed it down after Drowned Meadow, gear bent, one wingtip scarred, looking less like a wreck than like a dog that had run itself lame and was waiting to be told it was still good. And up on the ridge, visible from anywhere in the valley, the buyer's charter sat where Voss's charge had broken its landing gear and its ambitions both, guarded now by rangers who'd arrived two days ago out of the monsoon mist with radios and handcuffs and the brisk, slightly disappointed air of men who had come expecting a war and found paperwork instead.

Okafor climbed to the meadow on the fourth day, taking the switchbacks slowly, and found Wendell sitting on the plane's wheel strut with a wrench in his hand and the cowling open, not obviously fixing anything.

"How's the patient?" Wendell said, without looking up.

"Alive. Embarrassed. The medic here says I'll fly again in six weeks if I'm sensible."

"She say anything about me?"

"That you're being counted." Okafor lowered himself onto a supply crate, testing the shoulder. "By the village. Whatever that means."

Wendell did look up then. He had the expression of a man who has been told something he already knew and is deciding whether to admit it. "They count planes here," he said. "Have for years, I think. Every aircraft that comes over the range gets logged—when, how many people, whether it landed or passed. Village records. Carved sticks, then paper, now a schoolteacher keeps a ledger." He turned the wrench over in his fingers. "My mail runs are in there. Seven years of them. Apparently I was never anonymous. I just couldn't read the file."

"And now?"

"Now the whole valley knows my name, my debts, my dead friend, and exactly which side I chose." He said it lightly, but Okafor had spent three weeks reading this man's silences the way he read weather, and he heard what was underneath: not fear. Relief, wearing fear's clothes because relief was unfamiliar. "So. Counted."

Okafor nodded slowly. In his profession, being counted was usually bad news. A survey crew counted things in order to value them, and valuation preceded acquisition, always. But this was the other kind of counting—the kind done by people who intended for you to still be there next season. He was surprised to find he wanted to be counted that way too. He'd spent twenty years making maps that helped strangers take places. It occurred to him, sitting on the crate with his shoulder throbbing in time with his heartbeat, that he had never once been included in what the map protected.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

"For what? You took a bullet."

"Not for the bullet. For the maps." He looked out past the meadow edge, down into the valley, where the river ran bright and unconstrained through the ruins of the gate. "I knew what Voss knew. Before we ever flew. I showed you those old charts in chapter—I mean, back at the staging town, and I let you believe we were searching. We weren't. We were confirming. I told myself it was just a job, that the coordinates were coordinates, that what got built on top of them wasn't my department." He shook his head. "A mapmaker who doesn't ask what the map is *for*. That's not innocence. That's cowardice with a legend scale."

Wendell considered this. Then he went back to the engine, tightening something that probably didn't need it. "You know what Elio used to say? That every instrument lies a little, and the pilot's job is knowing by how much." A pause. "People too, I guess. You lied a little. So did she. So did Bram, who lied professionally and then shot the lock off a sluice house for us, so." He shrugged. "Ledger balances funny sometimes. Doesn't mean it doesn't balance."

* * *

The accounting proper happened on the sixth day, in the longhouse, and Okafor attended it leaning on a carved staff the old woman pressed into his hands without asking whether he wanted it.

The rangers had set up a folding table beneath the great ridgepole, which felt like sacrilege and probably was, but the village elders had permitted it with the serene tolerance of people who understood that even absurd institutions occasionally served. On the table: Voss's records, three notebooks and a document case; Okafor's maps, the pre-official surveys and his own field annotations; Bram's testimony, delivered flatly and at length the day before; and the charter's own papers, seized from the cockpit, including a manifest that listed, among its cargo, four crates stenciled *agricultural equipment* that had in fact contained restraints and rifles.

Voss stood across the table from the ranking ranger—a compact woman named Ferro with grey at her temples and the patience of someone who had waited out longer storms than this—and answered questions in her usual register, which was to say: precisely, completely, and with the air of a woman doing arithmetic she resented having to show her work for.

But Okafor watched her closely, and he saw what the rangers didn't. She was giving them everything. Not strategically, not the curated partial disclosure he'd have expected from her—not the Marguerite Voss who bought debts and bent crews to her purposes. This was the other one, the one who'd stood at gunpoint between Wendell and the buyer's men and said, in a voice Okafor would remember until he died, *"You've misread your contract. I am the evidence."*

She'd turned her files inside out. Correspondence with the buyer's firm, seven years of it. Payment schedules. The original commission, which had never been recovery at all—it had been *verification*, confirm the salvage exists, confirm it's removable, and leave the rest to us. Her own marginal notes, increasingly skeptical, increasingly alarmed, preserved in her neat drafting hand: *they're not asking about structural integrity, they're asking about witnesses.* She'd kept all of it. Not to protect herself. To build the case she'd known, somewhere under the ambition, she might someday need against the man who paid her.

"Why?" Okafor asked her afterward, outside, rain dripping off the longhouse eaves between them. "Why keep it all? You could have burned it the first time you got nervous."

Voss lit nothing—she'd run out of cigarettes a week ago and refused to discuss it—but she went through the motion, fingers around an invisible paper, and seemed calmer for it. "Because I don't destroy records, Okafor. It's the one principle I have." A pause. Rain filled it. "And because part of me wanted to be caught. Eventually. By someone with the nerve."

"The nerve to do what?"

"To make me look at what I was building." She folded the ghost cigarette away. "You should have seen your face, you know. Back when you showed me those first old charts and realized I'd known the location all along. You looked at me like I was a corrupted file." She almost smiled. "Fair. But here's what your face didn't know: I'd already read the buyer's last letter by then. The one where he stopped saying *acquire* and started saying *resolve*. I signed on to recover a machine. Somewhere over the range I understood I'd been hired to bury a valley." She looked out at the ridgeline, where the charter sat gutted under guard. "People think ambition and conscience fight. Usually they just arrive late. Same meeting. Different order."

The charges, Ferro told them that evening, would be extensive. Conspiracy, smuggling of armed personnel, attempted cultural theft on a scale the legal categories barely contained—the gate was now formally recognized as engineered heritage, protected under treaties the buyer's lawyers had assumed applied to museums and not to watersheds. Voss's records gave them the network. Okafor's maps gave them the intent, dated years back, annotated in the buyer's own commissioned hand. Bram's testimony gave them the operational details, given freely once he understood that his handler's organization had marked him for cleanup along with everyone else.

"He'll be charged too," Ferro said to Okafor, nodding toward the fire where Bram sat apart, mending a net with the villagers' children watching solemnly, as though sabotage were a skill that could be absorbed by proximity. "Tracking, false identity, the transmitter. But his file reads differently now. There's a difference between a man who sells information and a man who stands in front of a sluice breach with a wrench while a valley floods." She gathered her folders. "Courage complicates sentencing. It always has. It's the one thing courts never quite know what to do with."

* * *

On the seventh day, Okafor walked down to the gate.

He went slowly, staff in hand, Delia shadowing him at a distance with elaborate casualness, and the trail took him along the canal cuts first—the ancient channels, stone-lined, still functioning, carrying snowmelt and monsoon alike in measured portions to terraces and villages downstream. He stopped at each one and did what he could not stop himself from doing: measured by eye, estimated gradient, checked the wear patterns on the stone. Two thousand years old, the elder's grandson had said, maybe older, and every sluice still seated true, still throttling the river's temper into something a valley could live with.

*Built,* he thought. Not found. Built, maintained, inherited. The greatest public works project in the hemisphere, and the official record had a blank space where it should have been, because blank spaces were where ownership went to incubate.

Then the gate itself.

It was ruined now, in the way Wendell and Bram had made it—collapsed in the precise places, intact in the others, so that it could never be dismantled and carried away in crates, and could never stop doing its work either. Water ran through the wreckage singing. Silt was already settling into the new configuration, the river accepting the change the way rivers accept everything: completely, and on its own terms.

Okafor stood at the edge for a long time, and then, because he was who he was, he took out his field notebook—the real one, the private one, not the one in the ranger's case—and began to draw.

Not a survey. Something closer to a portrait. The fall lines, the standing stones, the way the surviving sluices framed the water like cupped hands. He worked until his shoulder ached and the light went bronze, and when he finally capped his pen he understood that this drawing would never go in any report. It was the first map of his career that wasn't for sale, wasn't for conquest, wasn't for anyone. It was just a record of a thing that had nearly been taken, and hadn't been.

Delia came and stood beside him. For a while neither spoke. Downstream, faintly, the village was lighting its fires.

"They're staying," she said at last. "For the winter, anyway. All of us. Bram's already arguing with the blacksmith about bellows design—he thinks he's winning, which tells you everything about Bram. Voss is writing letters. Actual letters, dozens of them, to lawyers and ministries and I think one archbishop, God knows why." She smiled at the water. "And Wendell—"

"I know," Okafor said. "Counted."

"Worse." Delia laughed. "This morning the schoolteacher asked him to teach the aviation unit. Navigation, weather, the whole course. He said yes before she finished the sentence. Didn't even do the thing where he pretends to consider it." She shook her head, wonder and amusement braided together. "Seven years hiding, and it takes one winter for him to become faculty."

Okafor closed the notebook. His shoulder throbbed; his legs were tired; somewhere below, rice was cooking, and the smell of it rose up the valley like an answer to a question he'd been carrying since the ford.

He had come into these mountains as an instrument of someone else's acquisition, and he was leaving—or rather, not leaving—as something else entirely. The ledger, he thought, watching the water run free through the ruin. Balanced. Not settled—balanced, which was different, which meant the account stayed open, which meant there would be deposits and withdrawals still to come, seasons of them, years.

"Come on," Delia said. "Food. And you should hear what the elder's grandson found on the upper terrace today. He's been saving it for Wendell, but honestly, you're going to want your notebook for this one."

Okafor raised an eyebrow. "Found what?"

Delia was already walking, grinning over her shoulder. "Stairs," she said. "Going *up*."
