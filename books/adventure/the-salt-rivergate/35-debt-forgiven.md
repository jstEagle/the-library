## 35. Debt Forgiven

The rangers took Hollis Vane away in the same helicopter he'd arrived in, which Wendell thought was a kind of poetry the man would never appreciate. They zip-tied his wrists with the same efficient boredom they'd use on a poacher, and Vane went quietly, chin up, as if being arrested were simply another line item in an otherwise well-managed quarter. At the door of the aircraft he paused and looked back—not at Wendell, not at Voss, but at the rivergate itself, its broken sluices still dripping, the water running free through channels it had carved for people long dead.

"Seven hundred million dollars," Vane said, to no one in particular. "You people drowned seven hundred million dollars."

"You're welcome," said Delia.

The helicopter lifted off into a sky scrubbed clean by three days of rain, banked south over the ridge where the advance team had surrendered without a shot, and was gone. The sound of it faded into the ordinary sounds of the valley—water, birdsong, the elder's grandson laughing at something one of the rangers had said, the rangers themselves already unpacking ration tins because bureaucracy traveled light but ate heavily.

Wendell stood in the mud at the edge of the gate works and found that his hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the absence of it.

For seven years there had been a low hum underneath everything he did—the hum of a secret, of a debt, of a name he'd tried to shrink himself out of. He had flown small cargoes into small places and told himself that smallness was a choice. Now the secret was public record, the debt was about to be discussed over paperwork, and the name was being spoken aloud by strangers in ranger uniforms who kept glancing at him with the particular reverence reserved for men who had done something dramatic and survived it.

He didn't feel dramatic. He felt like a man standing in wet boots.

"Wendell." Marguerite Voss came down the stone stair from the upper sluice, her field coat ruined beyond any hope, her hair gray with ash and spray. She looked ten years older than she had at his airstrip, and twenty years more alive. "Walk with me."

"I've been walking for two weeks."

"Then you'll be good at it."

* * *

They walked the old maintenance path along the aqueduct wall, away from the others, toward the flat meadow where the crew had first camped. The morning was doing what mornings did here after rain: burning off the mist in gold sheets, filling the whole watershed with a light that seemed manufactured specifically for this place. Below them the river ran bright and unregulated, finding its own way through the ruins, and Wendell understood—for maybe the hundredth time since he'd triggered the collapse—that this was what Elio had seen. Not salvage. Not treasure. A machine built to serve water to everyone downstream, and therefore worth destroying rather than owning.

Voss walked in silence for a while. She was not a woman comfortable with silence; he could feel her assembling sentences the way she assembled arguments, testing load-bearing points. He let her work.

Finally she stopped at a bend in the path where a carved stone marker stood half-swallowed by moss—a survey point, Okafor had said, older than any map in any archive. She put her hand on it like a woman steadying herself against a railing.

"When I bought your debt," she said, "I told myself it was leverage. Clean, simple leverage. A man with nothing left to lose flies where I need him to fly."

"It was."

"It wasn't." She turned to face him. Up close, in this light, he could see how much the last month had cost her—the hollows under her eyes, the tremor she kept suppressing in her left hand. "I read everything, Wendell. Every file my employer's people had on the original expedition. Every transcript. And then I went looking for the things they didn't have, because I needed to know if you were a coward or a survivor before I staked my company on you." Her mouth twisted. "What I found was a man who watched his best friend die and spent seven years making sure no one else could profit from it. That's not cowardice. That's the most expensive fidelity I've ever seen."

"Elio's dead either way."

"Yes. But the gate isn't. And neither are you."

She reached into her coat and brought out a document wallet—leather, scuffed, absurdly formal for a woman who'd just spent two weeks eating rice out of a shared pot. She opened it and drew out the papers. Wendell knew them on sight. He'd signed them in his own hangar with a pen that was running dry, hating every stroke, telling himself it was temporary, telling himself he'd find a way out. His signature sat there in blue ink like a scar.

"The note against the aircraft," Voss said. "The interest accrued. The penalty clauses I wrote in myself, because I wanted you desperate enough to say yes." She held the pages out. "Take them."

He didn't move. "What's the catch?"

"No catch."

"There's always a catch. You taught me that."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly governed—and he realized with a small, unwelcome shock that he'd wounded her, and that wounding her mattered to him now in a way it hadn't a month ago. People changed when you starved beside them. People changed when they stood next to you at a ford while bullets crossed the water.

"The catch," she said evenly, "is that I'm going to stand here until you take them, and I'm wearing terrible boots."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he took the papers, because refusing twice would have been theater, and he was done with theater.

He expected her to walk away. Instead she waited, watching him, and after a moment he understood what she wanted, and he hated her a little for wanting it, and he did it anyway. He tore the top page in half. Then the next. Then the rest, methodically, the way Bram had rigged the sluice mechanisms—careful, complete, no piece left functional. The paper was good stock and resisted him, and he worked at it until his hands burned, and when it was done he stood holding a fistful of confetti that had owned his airplane, his livelihood, his sleep.

"Throw it," Voss said.

He threw it. The wind off the aqueduct took the pieces and scattered them over the meadow, white flecks against green, already becoming litter, already becoming nothing. Somewhere below, the river ran on, indifferent to finance.

Wendell let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding for seven years. It didn't feel like triumph. It felt like setting down a pack so heavy his shoulders didn't know their own weight yet.

"Well," he said.

"Well," said Voss.

* * *

She gave him exactly four seconds of peace, which for Marguerite Voss was practically a religious observance.

"Partnership," she said.

He turned. "No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear the terms. The answer is no."

Her jaw set. He recognized the expression from boardroom footage he'd once watched in a hotel bar, back when she was a rising name and he was a rising name and the world seemed large enough for both of them. "Wendell. You are the only pilot alive who has flown the throat of that range in monsoon conditions and lived. You know this watershed better than the rangers' own charts. My company is about to spend five years negotiating protection status for this valley, and I want you inside that process, not outside it."

"Then put me outside it and listen anyway."

"That's not how—"

"Marguerite." He said her name gently, and it stopped her better than shouting would have. "I spent seven years learning what happens when someone owns a piece of you. Your employer owned you. Look what he asked you to do. I'm not going to trade a debt for a contract and call it freedom. It's the same leash with a nicer collar."

"And if I offered no leash? Equal shares. You fly, I fund, we both answer to the valley."

"Equal shares means you can still outvote me. You have a company. I have a plane with bent gear and a radio Delia fixed with wire."

"So fix the terms. Name them. I'll sign anything reasonable."

He looked at her for a long moment. The thing about Marguerite—about the real Marguerite, the one under the armor—was that she meant it. She would sign anything. She would restructure her entire operation around his objections. And in a year, or five, some pressure would come, some crisis, some quarter that needed saving, and the partnership would bend him the way partnerships always bent the smaller party, politely, incrementally, until one day he'd look up and be flying cargo he hated for reasons he couldn't remember choosing.

Or—and this was the thought that surprised him—it wouldn't. Because she'd stood at gunpoint for him. Because she'd turned her evidence against her own buyer. Because redemption, once seized late and at cost, tended to hold.

It didn't matter. That was the thing he'd learned in the valley, from the villagers who counted planes, from the elder who'd kept Elio's letter safe for seven years without ever asking for anything: belonging wasn't a contract. You couldn't structure it. You couldn't hold equity in it.

"No," he said again, and made it soft. "But thank you. For offering. For meaning it."

Voss studied him the way Okafor studied terrain—looking for the route through, finding none, marking the obstacle instead. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed. It was a short, rusty sound, like an engine turning over after a cold night, and it transformed her face entirely.

"You're going to be insufferable about this," she said.

"Probably."

"Fine. No partnership. But I'm sending you contracts anyway—survey work, mail consolidation, medevac retainer for the valley clinics. Legitimate paper, fair rates, no clauses. You can burn those too if you like, but I'll keep sending them."

"Delia will make me sign them."

"Delia has sense. It's why I hired her." She tucked the empty leather wallet away and looked out over the meadow, and for a moment her voice lost its boardroom polish entirely. "Elio would have taken the partnership."

"Elio would have taken the partnership, the helicopter, and the helicopter pilot's lunch money."

"Yes," she said. "Yes, he would have." And there it was, the grief they shared, passing between them like a tool—no longer a weapon, just a thing they both carried, heavier for her because she'd carried it alone and guilty, lighter now because it was finally in the open air.

They walked back together. Neither spoke. It was, Wendell thought, the most honest conversation they'd ever had, and most of it had been tearing paper.

* * *

By afternoon the meadow had become a small, improbable village. Rangers ran antenna wire between trees. Villagers came up from the lower settlements carrying food no one had requested, on the theory—which proved correct—that outsiders fed by outsiders would eat badly. Okafor lay propped against a supply crate with his bandaged shoulder and a fever that had finally broken, arguing cartography with a ranger lieutenant half his age and winning. Delia had somehow acquired a kettle and was conducting tea operations with the authority of a general.

Bram sat apart, as Bram did, cleaning a rifle nobody had asked him to clean. When Wendell passed, the fixer looked up.

"Heard Voss tore up your papers."

"News travels fast."

"Delia travels fast." Bram ran an oiled cloth down the barrel, considering. "You going to sign her contracts?"

"Some of them."

"Huh." He said it the way other men said *good*, and went back to the rifle.

Wendell found Voss at the meadow's edge, standing where the ground fell away toward the river, watching the water. He came up beside her. For a while they just stood there, two people who had spent a month trying to destroy each other's plans and had instead destroyed a billionaire's.

"The rangers will want statements," she said eventually. "Testimony. There'll be inquiries, hearings, a year of lawyers. Your name is going to be in newspapers, Wendell. Real ones, southern ones. *Pilot destroys ancient treasure to save valley.* They'll make you a hero whether you like it or not."

"I'll decline interviews."

"They'll write them anyway."

"Then they'll get it wrong, and Elio will haunt me, and life will go on." He shrugged. "Invisibility was never really the point. I told myself it was. But hiding doesn't keep people safe. Showing up does. I'm learning that late."

"Late is a genre," Voss said. "Most of the best things I've done arrived late."

Below them, the free-running river caught the late sun and held it, a long unbroken ribbon of light running down out of the mountains toward towns and villages and strangers who would never know how close it had come to belonging to a ledger. Wendell watched it and felt the last knot in his chest loosen—not all at once, but the way ice lets go, gradually, in pieces, until one morning the water is simply moving.

"Marguerite," he said. "The forgiveness. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I profited from you for a month and nearly got us all killed."

"You also stood in front of a gun."

She didn't answer that. Some debts, he understood, ran both directions, and she wasn't ready to have hers named yet. He let it go. There would be time. There was, suddenly, an enormous amount of time—no storm chasing them, no shadow on their tail, no clock except the seasons.

From across the meadow, Delia's voice cut through the evening: "Ishikawa! If you're done having feelings with the boss, your airplane's gear is fixed and I want to test-fly it tomorrow, and you're coming, because if I bend it again I'm blaming you in the report!"

Voss raised an eyebrow. "*Your* airplane," she said. "Not mine. Interesting."

"It was always mine. You just held the title for a while."

"Get used to saying things like that," she said. "It gets easier."

He started across the meadow toward the voice, toward the kettle-smoke and the argument about maps and the plane waiting on the strip with its mended gear, and behind him he heard Voss say something quiet to the river that he chose not to hear, because some things belonged to the person saying them.

Tomorrow they'd fly out over the wreck corridor—tomorrow, he'd promised himself, he'd finish what the letter started. But tonight there was tea, and a fire, and a valley full of people who had never once asked him what he was worth.

That, it turned out, was the whole fortune.
