## 36. The Question of Going Home

The frost came in the night, and by morning the valley wore it like a held breath.

Wendell stood at the edge of the airstrip—the one flat stretch of ground between the village and the river terraces, packed earth and river gravel, barely long enough for a plane that knew what it was doing—and watched the light come up over the ridge where the Salt Rivergate lay in its deliberate ruin. Steam rose off the water below the old sluice works, ghost-thin columns climbing into air cold enough to bite. The gate was dead as salvage. It was alive as ruin. Every morning he woke to that distinction, and every morning it fit a little better against his chest, like a coat cut for him after all.

Seven years he'd kept himself small. Seven years of medicine crates and mail sacks and careful routines, of flying under other men's names on other men's manifests, of being nobody from nowhere so thoroughly that he'd almost believed it himself. And now—

"Now you're famous," Delia said, coming up beside him with two tin mugs. She handed him one. It was tea, sweetened with more sugar than anyone sensible would use, which meant she'd raided the elder's stores again. "Or infamous. Hard to say which is worse for business."

"Neither's good for invisibility."

"You gave that up." She blew on her tea. "You gave it up the day you pulled that lever."

He had. He'd traded his anonymity for the valley's water, and he'd do it again before breakfast if someone made him choose twice. But there were mornings—this one, for instance—when the arithmetic of it sat strangely. The buyer's people were in custody downriver. The rangers' reports had his name in them, spelled correctly, attached to statements. Somewhere in an office in the capital, a file was thickening with pages about Wendell Ishikawa, and no amount of small cargo would thin it out again.

"Voss wants to see you," Delia said. "She's been sitting in Okafor's tent since dawn, going through maps like she's paying rent on them. Says she has something for you. Paperwork." Delia smiled around the word, dry as gravel. "Her love language."

* * *

Marguerite Voss had lost weight since the traverse and gained something else in its place—a stillness that hadn't been there before, a way of holding her hands quiet on the map table instead of using them to conduct. The valley had done that to her, or the confession had, or the gunpoint moment on the ridge when she'd turned her evidence against the man who'd hired her and understood, all at once and forever, exactly how much a person could lose by owning things.

Okafor sat propped on a crate beside her, wound healing clean, leg still stiff, sketching corrections onto a survey sheet with the concentration of a man rebuilding his profession from the studs up. He nodded at Wendell without looking up.

"Wendell." Voss slid a folder across the table. Real paper, real weight, the kind of folder that had traveled in charter planes and government offices. "Read it."

He read it. A survey contract. Legitimate—stamped, countersigned, funded through channels that would survive an audit. Mapping the watershed properly, all of it, the tributaries and the terraces and the drowned meadows, so that the record finally told the truth about what was here. His name on the flight line. His aircraft insured. No debt attached, no leverage, no hidden destination waiting at the end of the route.

"Rangers want the basin charted," Voss said. "Government contract. Boring work. Two seasons, maybe three. You'd fly every corridor you've been avoiding for seven years, but this time with permission." She paused. "And pay."

He set the folder down. "Why?"

"Because you know this country better than any map I own, and I've seen your maps now, and I'm right." She met his eyes without flinching, which was new too; the old Marguerite had always flinched first and lied second. "And because I owe you a debt I can't tear up like I tore up the other papers. This is the only currency I have left that means anything."

"The partnership offer stands too?" He'd heard it already, twice, once drunk on celebration tea.

"It stands. But you'll say no to that, and we both know it. You don't want to be owned, Wendell, even by shares." Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "So take the contract instead. It's work, not ownership. You answer to the rangers and the weather, nothing else."

He looked at the folder a long time. Outside, a child was shouting something about a goat and the river, in that order of importance. The sound came through the canvas warm and ordinary, and it did something to him that the contract couldn't touch.

"I'll think about it," he said, which was true, and which was also the longest possible way of saying *not yet*, and Voss heard both meanings because she'd learned to hear them this season, in this valley, the hard way.

"Think fast," she said. "Snow closes the pass in ten days. If you sign, I fly the paperwork out myself."

* * *

Delia found him at the aircraft that afternoon.

The Cessna—his Cessna, bought back from the edge of repossession when Voss tore up the debt papers, its bent gear straightened by her own hands over three weeks of cursing and welding—sat on the frost-stiff grass looking better than it had any right to. Delia circled it slowly, the way she did, reading metal the way Okafor read stone.

"I fixed your number four cylinder," she said. "Again. It keeps trying to quit on you. I think it learned that from somebody."

"Very funny."

"I'm not joking about the south, Wendell." She stopped circling and faced him, arms crossed, grease to the elbow, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of inner tube. "I got a letter with the last mail run. My sister's shop in Puerto Verde needs a mechanic who can run a whole operation—engines, airframes, training apprentices. Good money. Real workshop. Roof that doesn't leak." She looked at the mountains, then back at him. "I want you to come."

"Delia—"

"Don't say no yet. Just hear it." Her voice stayed level, but her hands didn't; she squeezed her own elbows like she was holding herself in place. "You'd be good at it. Teaching, I mean. You taught me half of what I know about reading country, and you didn't even notice you were doing it. There are kids down there who've never seen a valley like this one, never seen flying mean anything except rich people moving fast." She shrugged, aggressive and casual at once. "I'm asking because I'd rather not go alone. That's the whole speech. Don't make it weird."

He looked at her—this woman who'd caught a saboteur in the dark and said nothing until saying something was safe, who'd led a decoy party up a ridge with armed men on it because someone had to and she'd decided it was her—and felt the full weight of what she was offering. Not a job. A door. An exit from everything that had happened here, a life with a roof and a workshop and no ghosts in the fuel lines.

"I won't make it weird," he said. "But I need to tell you something first."

"That sounds weird."

"It might be." He laughed, short and surprised at himself. "Ask me tomorrow. After I talk to the elder."

Delia studied him for a long moment, then nodded once, sharp, and went back to her engine. But as he walked away he heard her mutter, mostly to herself: *"Tomorrow. Men and their tomorrows."*

* * *

The elder received him in the evening, in the low room that smelled of woodsmoke and dried fish, with the grandson—Tomas, fourteen now, guide of the gate road, insufferable and indispensable—sitting cross-legged by the fire pretending not to listen, which was the family trade apparently.

"We have talked," the elder said, without preamble, because elders of the interior did not preamble. "The council. About you."

Wendell sat. Waited. Seven years of delivering medicine to these villages and he had never once been inside this room for anything but weather.

"The land above the airstrip," the elder said. "Two hectares, the bench above the spring. Good ground. Your ground, if you want it."

The words landed and kept landing, echo after echo. Wendell heard himself say, "I don't—" and stop, and start again. "I've never owned land anywhere."

"We know." The elder's eyes were old and amused and entirely without mercy. "That is why we offer. A man should own the place where he stops running." He leaned forward. "You think we did not count? Seven years of planes. Medicine in the bad fever year. Mail when the passes closed and everyone's letters stopped. The pilot who came in weather no one else would fly." He tapped the floorboards once, decisively. "We counted you before you counted yourself. This is only the arithmetic catching up."

Tomas, unable to bear it: "There's a flat spot for a hangar. I measured."

"Tomas."

"I measured it *twice*."

Wendell looked at the fire. He thought about Elio, who had loved this country loudly and died in it quietly, who had spent his final week in these villages and left behind a letter that had named the buyer and warned that the gate must stay closed—a letter Wendell had read aloud here, in this room, while the whole village listened, and afterward the elder had put a hand on his shoulder and said only, *Now you know why we counted him too.*

He thought about the contract in Voss's folder. The workshop in Puerto Verde. The bench above the spring.

Every door open. For the first time in his adult life, every single door standing wide, and no debt behind any of them.

"You honor me," he said, and found his voice steady. "Let me give you my answer in the morning. Properly. There's a flight I want to make first."

The elder nodded, satisfied, as if the delay itself were the correct answer. Perhaps it was. Tomas looked devastated, then immediately asked if the hangar could be painted.

* * *

He flew at dawn, alone, up the wreck corridor.

Not to scatter anything—that was done, the ashes-page gone to the wind over the crash site weeks ago, the rest of Elio's letter read aloud and folded into the elder's keeping. He flew it because the route was his now, reclaimed, and because he wanted to see the country with the question settled in his body even if his mouth hadn't answered yet.

The range stood white and enormous under the early sun. He threaded the canyon by memory, wings level, hands easy, and below him the river ran free—all the way down, silver and unbroken, past the ruins of the gate that held its shape and gave up its water, past the villages, past the drowned meadow where his gear had bent and his old life had ended. Nobody owned it. That was the whole miracle. Nobody owned it, and it ran anyway.

Somewhere over the high pass he understood it, the way you understand weather or engine noise or the weight shift of a loaded plane—not as a decision but as a fact already true, waiting only to be said aloud.

He wasn't choosing between doors. He hadn't been for weeks. The contract, the workshop, the land—they weren't alternatives. They were the same answer wearing different clothes, and the answer was this valley, this river, this life he'd been living all along without admitting it had a name. He could sign Voss's contract and sleep in his own bed above the spring. He could teach Tomas radio procedure and Delia's southern cousins could visit and marvel. Home wasn't a door you walked through. It was the place that kept counting you whether you counted yourself or not.

He banked toward the village, toward the smoke rising off the morning fires, toward the airstrip where a fourteen-year-old had measured a hangar site twice.

* * *

They were all waiting when he shut the engine down—Voss with her folder, Delia with grease still on her arms, Okafor leaning on his stick, the elder and Tomas and half the village behind them in the frost-bright morning, because of course they'd known the flight's meaning before he did. News traveled faster than planes here. It always had.

Wendell climbed out and stood in the prop wash silence.

"Yes to the land," he said. "Yes to the contract—but flown from here, on my terms, with Tomas as my guide and Delia's name on the maintenance log. Yes to teaching anyone who wants to learn." He looked at Delia. "Come south in the wet season if you must—I'll keep your tools oiled. But come home after."

Delia's face did something complicated and landed on fierce joy. "You're keeping my tools oiled. That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, and I hate that."

Voss handed him the pen without a word. He signed on the wing root, because there was no table out here and it seemed right, ink on cold aluminum, his name in the place where his name belonged.

The elder watched all of it with the expression of a man whose arithmetic had finally balanced. Then he said, quietly, for Wendell alone:

"There is one more thing. A letter came down with the ice yesterday—a traveler brought it over the pass before it closed. It's been waiting for you." He held out an envelope, travel-worn, addressed in a hand Wendell knew better than his own.

Elio's handwriting. Dated seven years ago. Postmarked from a town Wendell had never heard of—and a date, impossible, three days *after* the crash.

Wendell took it with hands that had forgotten how to shake and remembered all at once.

The snow began to fall, soft and unhurried, closing the range behind them like a book whose next chapter was already written.
