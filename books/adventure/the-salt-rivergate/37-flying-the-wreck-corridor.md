## 37. Flying the Wreck Corridor

The morning of the flight, Wendell woke before the birds and lay still in the dark of the elder's guest house, listening to the river.

It ran free now. That was the thing he kept having to relearn. For seven years the Salt River had been a sound he avoided—a name on maps he folded away, a corridor of airspace he routed around at the cost of fuel and hours, a wound in the shape of a valley. Now it was just water again, moving over stones below his window, doing what it had done for a thousand years before men built gates into its throat, and what it would do for a thousand years after.

He dressed without lighting the lamp. Delia had rebuilt the *Kestrel*'s number two cylinder herself, back in the first weeks of winter, and he'd flown her twice since—short hops to the lower villages, mail and vaccine, the old small cargo. But not north. Not up the watershed. Not yet.

Today he would.

He'd told no one except the elder's grandson, Tam, who was coming with him—not as passenger, exactly, though that was what the seat harness would say. As witness. Elio's letter had been read aloud to the village three nights ago, all but one page, and the elder had said what needed saying: *the dead man's words belong to the living air now, not to paper.* The rest of the letter—the pages naming the buyer's organization, warning that the gate must stay closed, asking whoever found them to look after the people of the valley—those had gone into the rangers' report, into Voss's evidence file, into the record where they could do work. But the last page, the one Elio had written in a hand gone loose and strange, the page that wasn't argument or warning at all—

That page was for the sky.

* * *

Tam met him at the strip as the light came up gray-gold through the mist, breath steaming, carrying a small clay pot sealed with wax and cloth.

"You didn't sleep," the boy said. He was sixteen and had decided sometime during the winter that this entitled him to speak to Wendell like a co-pilot.

"Neither did you."

"I slept fine." Tam handed up the pot, then climbed in and started checking the harness straps with the exaggerated competence of someone who'd been taught by Delia. "Is it true the wreck is visible from the air? Okafor says the tail section never burned."

"It's true." Wendell ran his own checks slower than he needed to. "Okafor's been studying the site from the survey photos. He asked me once why I never flew over."

"What did you tell him?"

Wendell thumbed the primer. "I told him I knew other routes."

The engine caught on the third blade, coughed, settled into its winter-tuned idle. Below them the village was waking—smoke from cook fires, the first children out on the terraces, Delia standing at the edge of the strip with her arms crossed against the cold, not waving, just watching, which was her way. Bram was beside her. Seven months ago Bram had been a man paid to track them; now he raised one hand, palm out, and Wendell raised his back, and neither of them made anything more of it than that.

He took the Kestrel off into the mist and turned north, up the river.

* * *

For the first twenty minutes it was just flying. Cold clear air, the kind that makes an aircraft honest, every control crisp as a struck match. The river unspooled beneath them, braided and bright, and the terraces gave way to forest, and the forest gave way to the steep dark country where the walls closed in and the water went from wide to wild.

Wendell had planned the route the way he planned everything now: deliberately, honestly, no tricks left. There was only one way up the gorge that made sense in a loaded plane, and it was the way he'd flown seven years ago with Elio in the right seat arguing about whether the clouds ahead were weather or just morning being dramatic.

They'd been dramatic. It hadn't mattered.

"There," Tam said, and pointed.

The crash site sat on a bench of rock above the river where the gorge kinked—a shelf maybe two hundred meters long, green now with seven summers of growth climbing over it. From the air you could see the scar the fuselage had plowed, a pale stripe in the vegetation running uphill, ending at the tail section Okafor had mentioned: upright, silver, weathered to the color of bone, absurdly intact, like a monument somebody had planted and forgotten to explain.

Wendell had imagined this moment for seven years. In most versions of it his hands shook. In some of them he couldn't make himself look. In one or two he'd imagined turning back at the mouth of the gorge, telling Tam the weather was wrong, and nobody would ever have known, because that was the man he'd been—smallness as armor, absence as a skill.

His hands were steady. That surprised him more than shaking would have.

He brought the Kestrel around in a slow orbit, wide enough to be respectful, close enough to see detail. The tail numbers were still legible. *SALT RIVERGATE SURVEY — EXPEDITION 3*. Below the lettering, someone had painted something new—recently, by the brightness of it. He banked tighter, squinting.

Words. Painted on the fuselage in careful block letters, in three languages, the middle one the valley tongue:

*ELIO MARIN FLEW HOME FROM HERE.*

He pulled back on the stick without deciding to. His vision blurred and he blinked it clear and flew level for a while, breathing, the autopilot of grief holding altitude while the man inside it caught up.

"Who painted that?" Tam asked quietly.

"The villagers." Wendell's voice came out rough. "Elder said—" He stopped. Started again. "The elder told me Elio spent his last week downriver. With them. They carried him up here afterward, when the mountain was done with him." He shook his head. "Seven years I thought he died alone in the rain. He died among friends. I just never came to meet them."

"He's been waiting," Tam said simply. "The whole time. Like a letter nobody delivered."

"Yeah." Wendell looked at the clay pot in Tam's hands. "Something like that."

* * *

He set down on the bench itself.

It was a terrible strip—uphill slope, loose scree at the top end, a drop into the gorge at the bottom—and he flew it the way he'd learned to fly strips like it in seven years of interior work, touching down heavy on the main gear and letting the slope bleed the speed off, rolling to a stop fifty meters short of the tail section with the engine idling and the propwash stirring the grass around the wreck like a hand smoothing a bed.

Nobody spoke. Even the engine seemed quieter than usual.

Wendell shut down and climbed out and stood in the cold thin air, and for a long moment he could not move toward it. The wind came up the gorge smelling of snowmelt and stone. Somewhere below, the river worked at its ancient argument with the rocks.

Then Tam touched his elbow—gently, the way you'd touch a horse—and Wendell walked.

Up close the tail section was bigger than the photographs suggested, taller than a house, its skin rippled with heat and age. The paint job was fresh enough that he could smell the solvent. Someone had come up here—climbed the whole gorge, packed paint and brushes—sometime in the last season. Maybe after the gate fell. Maybe after the letter was read aloud. He put his palm flat against the metal and it was cold and real and it did not burn him, though for seven years he'd been sure it would.

"I'm sorry," he said. His voice sounded very small against the mountains. "I'm late."

The wind moved through the grass. Nothing answered, and everything did.

He took the clay pot from Tam and broke the wax seal with his thumbnail. Inside, folded small, was the last page of Elio's letter—the page the elder had pressed back into his hands after the reading, saying, *this one isn't for us. This one is for wherever he stopped.*

Wendell had read it forty times since. He knew it by heart. He read it once more anyway, aloud, because some things should be spoken into the place they're meant for:

*"If you're reading this, Wendell, then you finally flew the route, which means you finally forgave yourself, which means I get to stop waiting, because honestly it's boring up here in your imagination and you've got me all wrong. I wasn't angry. I was never angry. I was flying home. You were my home, you idiot—all those years of mail runs and bad coffee and you pretending you weren't happy. Don't sell the river. Don't let anyone own the water. And don't hide anymore. Small cargo is good cargo. But you were never small."*

Wendell laughed. It came out cracked down the middle, half sob, and he let it be both.

"Okay," he said. "Okay, Elio."

He unfolded the page completely, held it high, and released it into the wind.

The paper lifted—hung for one impossible moment on the updraft off the gorge wall, white against the gray rock, turning—and then the wind took it the way the wind takes everything that belongs to it, out over the edge, down into the light and spray above the river, where it vanished between one blink and the next.

Gone. Given. Delivered, seven years late, to the only address it ever had.

Tam stood beside him with his head bowed. After a while he said, "Do we stay long?"

"No." Wendell wiped his face with the back of his glove, unhurried about it. "We go home."

* * *

He flew the rest of the corridor slowly.

Not out of dread—that was the miracle of it, the thing he kept testing like a sore tooth and finding healed. He flew slowly because for the first time he wanted to see it: the walls rising sheer and wet, the waterfalls threading down the rock in the morning sun, the narrow places where he and Elio had threaded a storm on instruments and nerve, the bend where—according to the official report, according to the version Wendell had carried like a stone in his chest—they'd hit downdraft and lost the plane and lost the man.

Voss's account had contradicted it. The letter had corrected it. The truth, it turned out, was simpler and worse and better: there had been no failure of nerve, no error of judgment, nothing to forgive anyone for. There had been weather, and a mountain, and a pilot who flew until flying was no longer possible, and who had spent his final week on earth being fed and sung to by strangers who remembered him with love.

The ghost had never been haunting the corridor. It had been waiting for Wendell to come collect it.

At the mouth of the gorge the valley opened and the river widened and went gold in the full sun, running free past terrace and village and the drowned ruin of the gate, gleaming like something that had finally been allowed to finish its sentence. Wendell keyed the radio.

"*Kestrel* to village. Coming home. Put the kettle on."

Delia's voice came back immediately, dry as always, warm underneath. "*Kestrel*, we heard you the whole way down the gorge. You were flying awfully slow, Ishikawa. Anything wrong with the aircraft?"

"No," Wendell said, and smiled at the windshield, at the river, at the whole enormous ordinary day. "Nothing wrong with the aircraft. First time in seven years."

There was a pause on the frequency, and then Delia, softer: "Copy that. Welcome home."

He banked toward the strip, toward the smoke and the kettle and the people who had counted his planes for years without his knowing, and behind him the Salt Rivergate stood in its ruins that held, and the water ran free.
