## 39. Spring Runoff

The river broke up on a Tuesday.

Wendell heard it before he saw it—a long groan rolling up the valley like something enormous turning over in its sleep, then the crack and boom of ice letting go all at once, three kilometers of it, the whole frozen spine of the Salt surrendering to spring in a single hour. He stood outside his hut with a tin cup of tea going cold in his hand and watched the rivergate ruins take their first free water in seven months, white water threading through the old sluice channels, finding the ways Elio had once described to him in the dark: *it doesn't dam anything, Wendell. It just decides where the water goes next. Somebody built that. Somebody who loved this valley.*

Somebody who loved this valley. He'd spent a winter learning what that meant from the inside—learning it by shoveling snow off Delia's workshop roof, by sitting with Okafor while the surveyor's wound healed slow and clean, by teaching Bram to play cards badly enough that the children could beat him. Learning it by staying.

Now the ice was gone and the sky was open and the mail had been piling up since October.

He loaded the plane at first light, when the frost still silvered the wings and his breath hung around him like a thought he hadn't finished. The cargo was small and good: vaccine boosters for four villages, a crate of schoolbooks ordered back in the autumn, bolts of cloth, a new radio battery for the elder's grandson Toma, who had appointed himself the valley's communications officer the day the rangers' transmitter arrived and had not slept properly since. And mail. Sacks of it. Letters written over the winter by lamplight, folded into envelopes sealed against the damp, every one of them addressed outward—to sons working the coast, to daughters married into towns downriver, to the world beyond the range that most of these people would never see except as ink.

Wendell carried their words out and carried other words back. It was, he understood now, the truest work he'd ever done. Not because it mattered in any ledger he'd once kept, but because it mattered in the only one that counted: the one the villages kept, in which his name appeared not as the man from the disaster but as the man who came.

He ran his preflight the way he always did, hands moving without hurry, and found himself humming. He noticed the humming the way you notice a bird landing near you—something that hadn't happened in years, happening now without permission.

Delia came across the frost with two cups and no ceremony.

"You'll want the south pass," she said. "Toma says the wind's already swinging. You'll have a tailwind home if you leave before ten."

"Toma says." Wendell took the cup. "He's eleven."

"He's eleven and he hasn't been wrong about weather since the monsoon. I'd trust him over the bureau."

"The bureau's forecast is a rumor with a letterhead."

"Then we agree." She looked at the plane, then at him, and there was something in her face she'd been carrying for a week or more, patient as ballast. "You're really going to do it. The whole run. Every village."

"Every village. That's the job."

"That's not what I asked."

He drank his tea. Below them, the river ran loud and bright through the gate ruins, and somewhere in that sound was the sound of everything he'd broken being put to a use he hadn't planned and couldn't own. "I know what you asked," he said finally. "And yes. I'm going to fly the corridor."

She didn't say *good* or *finally* or any of the things she had a right to say. She just nodded, the way mechanics nod, and went to check his fuel cap herself even though she'd watched him close it.

* * *

The south pass was kind to him.

He climbed out of the valley with the morning sun full on his left wing and the whole watershed laid out below like something newly made. In seven months the country had forgotten winter completely—or rather, winter had done its work and gone, leaving the land scrubbed and eager, meltwater standing in every hollow, the terraces above the old trail green-black with new growth. He flew the line of the river because he wanted to, not because he had to, and the river gave him back everything: the long glittering reaches, the white noise of the rapids, the gate itself sliding past beneath his wing—broken now, deliberately broken, its upper sluice collapsed into beautiful useless rubble that the water went around without complaint, the way water does, the way it always would.

*It lives as ruin,* he'd told the elders, the night after. *It can't be sold, but it can't be stopped either.*

*That,* the elder had said, *is the oldest engineering there is.*

He crossed the corridor at midmorning.

He'd told himself he wouldn't make a thing of it, and then he made a thing of it anyway, because some things deserve to be made a thing of. He throttled back. He let the plane settle into the slow, wide turn that seven years ago he could not bear to fly, and he looked down at the crash site—the scar in the treeline, grown over now with birch and alder, the fuselage fragments long since hauled out by the rangers, the ground itself just ground, holding nothing but what ground holds.

"Elio," he said, to the glass, to the wind, to whatever part of his friend had stayed in this air. "I'm flying your route. It's a good route. You always said it was a good route."

The plane rode the thermals off the burned slope, rising slightly, the way it always rose there—he remembered that, remembered Elio riding that same lift with his hand loose on the stick and his grin half-turned toward the passenger seat—and Wendell let the lift carry him, and did not fight it, and did not fall apart, and came out the far side of the corridor with wet eyes and steady hands, which seemed like the correct ratio.

At the first village he landed on the thawed strip to a crowd that included three dogs and a woman named Sera who pressed a package into his hands before he'd finished chocking the wheels.

"From your friend in the south," she said. "It came upriver with the traders last week. They said to hold it until the pilot came."

"My friend in the south" was how the villages referred to Marguerite Voss, an appellation Wendell had given up correcting sometime in December. He tucked the package behind his seat and flew on, and did not open it until he was parked at the second strip, alone, eating bread someone had handed him through a window.

Inside the wrapping: a folder, heavy paper, Voss's unmistakable handwriting on the cover—*Ishikawa. Read before you argue with me, which you will.*

He read.

There were three contracts inside. Not offers—contracts, drafted properly, the legal machinery already oiled and waiting. The first was a survey commission: mapping the high passes for the new ranger service, seasonal work, paid, legitimate, his name on the title page as principal pilot. The second was a supply contract—scheduled runs, the exact route he'd flown today, underwritten by the coastal consortium that Voss had spent the winter bending to her will with what Okafor called "the force of a weather system." The third was thinner, one page, and Wendell read it twice because he couldn't believe it the first time.

It was a deed option. Land. The terrace above the village, the one with the view of the gate ruins, held in trust for him at a price that was less than symbolic and more than charity, which was exactly the price Voss would have calculated to the decimal point so that accepting it wouldn't feel like either.

*You will notice,* her covering letter said, *that none of these require you to be anyone's partner, employee, or debt-holder. I learned my lesson about owning people. Consider these instead an apology with paperwork attached. Sign what you want. Burn the rest. —M.V.*

*P.S. The consortium wanted me to ask about the gate ruins again. I told them the ruins are a ruin. Some sentences are worth the money they cost you.*

Wendell sat in the warm cockpit with the door open and the spring coming in, and laughed—actually laughed, alone, the first time in longer than he could calculate—until a child's face appeared at the fence wondering what was funny, and he wiped his eyes and said, "Mail day," and the child nodded solemnly, as though that explained everything, which it did.

* * *

He flew the rest of the run over three days.

The world was opening. That was the word for it—not melting, not thawing, opening, the way a hand opens, the way a sentence opens onto the rest of the story. Passes that had been white walls in October were now corridors of running water and birdsong. Strips that had been buried were bare and firm. At every stop the same scene repeated in variations: the crowd, the crates, the letters passed hand to hand, the questions about the crew—*is the mechanic coming back, is the surveyor walking yet, is the quiet one staying*—and Wendell answering each one honestly, which was its own strange luxury.

Okafor was walking six kilometers a day now and complaining about it professionally, which everyone agreed was the surest sign of recovery. Delia had a waiting list for engine lessons that ran into next autumn. Bram had fixed the mill, the well pump, the schoolhouse stove, and a clock nobody had known was broken, and had done all of it without saying more than forty words a week, and the village had responded by simply absorbing him, the way a river absorbs a stone, no discussion required.

And Voss—Voss was in the capital, testifying. The hearings had started in March. Her records, Okafor's maps, the buyer's seized correspondence: enough evidence to build a case that would take years to fully prosecute and would reshape how the interior was governed for a generation. She wrote to Wendell every few weeks, short letters, unsentimental, always ending with some dry observation that was as close as she came to warmth. *The lawyers call me formidable. I've decided to accept it as a compliment.* *They've named the inquiry after the river. Fitting. Rivers get blamed for everything and remember nothing.* *Tell Delia the torque specs worked. Tell Bram nothing; he'll know.*

On the third evening he came back down the valley with the last light on the water and the sacks empty and the return mail stacked behind his seat—letters from the coast, from the capital, from sons and daughters and one fat envelope from the ranger service marked URGENT that he decided, deliberately, could wait until morning.

Toma met him at the strip with the radio log, because of course he did.

"Two messages," the boy said, breathless, important, eleven years old and carrying the valley's voice in his notebook. "One from the ranger post—they're sending a team up next month to mark the safe crossing at the ford, they wanted to know if you'd guide them. I said probably yes. Was that right?"

"That was right."

"And one—" Toma checked the notebook, though Wendell suspected he knew it by heart, "—one relayed from the capital station, for Miss Voss, care of you. They said it's about the inquiry. They said it's time-sensitive." The boy's eyes were enormous. "Wendell. Is it about the trial? Did they catch somebody? Is it bad?"

Wendell took the message slip. The relay was terse, station-to-station, the kind of language used when the sender wants no ambiguity:

*VOSS REQUESTS ISHIKAWA ATTEND FINAL SESSION IN PERSON. COMMITTEE REACHES DELIBERATION ON GATE SITE DISPOSITION WITHIN FORTNIGHT. YOUR TESTIMONY REQUESTED BY NAME. ADVISE EARLIEST FLIGHT SOUTH. —RANGER HQ*

Testimony. By name. His name, requested, in a hearing room in the capital, about the gate—the place he'd helped destroy in order to save, the decision he'd made in mud and rain with men shooting at him, the choice that had ended one life and begun another.

Seven years ago he had hidden his name so thoroughly that he'd nearly lost it. Now the capital wanted it spoken aloud, on the record, entered into the permanent account of what happened at the Salt Rivergate.

He looked past Toma to the valley—the lamps coming on one by one along the river path, the smell of woodsmoke and thawed earth, the ruins black against the last light with the free water shining through them, audible even here, a sound like the world clearing its throat.

"When do you leave?" Toma asked.

Wendell folded the slip into his breast pocket, over his heart, where he'd carried Elio's letter through the whole long winter and would carry it a while longer.

"First light," he said. "Get me a tailwind."
