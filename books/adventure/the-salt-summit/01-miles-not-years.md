## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Kessari basin woke the way it always did: gray water first, then birds, then the smell of woodsmoke rising out of the delta villages like the land itself was exhaling. Rosalind Oyelaran watched all of it from four hundred feet up, one hand loose on the yoke of a thirty-year-old bush plane that had more patches than paint, and thought about fuel.

She always thought about fuel. Fuel was honest. Fuel didn't lie to you the way people did, didn't promise things it couldn't deliver, didn't ask you to stay for supper and then look at you with that soft, pitying expression that said *you poor creature, flying alone all your life*. The gauge on the left tank read just under half. The gauge on the right read a hair above. She'd been nursing that asymmetry since Lowmark, running the right tank leaner to keep the wings level without touching the trim wheel, because the trim cable had begun to fray three months ago and she hadn't yet found a week — or the money — to replace it.

Time, for Rosalind, was not measured in years. Years were an abstraction, something other people marked with birthdays and anniversaries and the slow accumulation of photographs in frames. Rosalind measured time in fuel gauges and river bends. A bend of the Kessari was forty minutes at cruise. A full tank was five hours and change, six if she flew fat and slow. Her debt to Halloway Field — eleven hundred marks, give or take, owed mostly for the engine overhaul she'd put off until the old radial started throwing metal through its oil — was measured in runs like this one: nine medicine crates to the delta stations, paid at a rate that would clear the interest this month and maybe, if the weather held and nobody's engine quit, a slice of the principal.

"Maybe," she said aloud, to no one, because the cabin of a Cessna-class freighter with the second seat ripped out for cargo was the only conversation partner she'd ever kept longer than a season.

Below her, the river did what the river always did, which was everything.

The Kessari came down out of mountains no surveyor had properly mapped in sixty years, braided itself across two hundred miles of gravel and silt, and died in the sea in a fan of channels that changed their minds every flood season. Somewhere along that middle stretch, according to every chart she owned, the river had once run through a country called Veyl. There wasn't a chart anymore that showed Veyl. There wasn't much of anything anymore that showed Veyl — the nation had drowned or starved or simply stopped, depending on which drunk in which port town you asked, and the maps had been redrawn so many times that the old borders survived only as a smudge of older ink beneath the new.

Rosalind had flown over that smudge perhaps a thousand times. She'd never once thought about it for longer than it took to check her watch.

That was the trick of a life like hers. You kept the world at exactly the altitude where it couldn't reach you. Close enough to see the smoke from a village chimney and know someone was home; far enough that nobody could ask you to come down and be part of it.

* * *

The first drop was Sennet Bar, a medical station built on stilts over a channel that ate its pilings a little more every spring. She came in low over the water the way the station's nurse, an enormous patient woman named Doss, had taught her eight years ago — approach into the wind, flare over the gravel, roll out long — and killed the engine on the bar while Doss's kids came pelting out of the reeds to help unload.

"You're late," Doss said, which was how she said good morning.

"Wind out of the northeast." Rosalind handed down the first crate. Quinine, penicillin, the little foil packets of oral rehydration salts that saved more lives in the delta than anything else she carried, though they weighed almost nothing and paid almost nothing either. "Your channel's moved again. There's a sandbar sitting where your marker buoy used to be."

"There's always a sandbar." Doss pried the lid off with a flat iron bar and counted vials by eye, fast, the way she counted everything. "You eating?"

"I'm eating."

"Liar. You've got the look."

"What look?"

Doss looked at her over the crate, and her face did the thing Rosalind dreaded most in the world, which was soften. "The look of a woman who flies six hours a day on tea and stubbornness."

Rosalind grinned, because grinning was cheaper than arguing. "Stubbornness has excellent energy density."

Doss laughed despite herself, then pressed a wrapped parcel into her hands — fried fish and some kind of dense sweet bread, still warm. "For the cockpit. Don't argue, it makes your face go ugly."

It was, Rosalind reflected as she waved off Doss's offer of coffee and got the plane back into the air, exactly this kind of moment that a careful person had to manage. Kindness arrived in parcels. You took the parcel, you flew on, and you made sure never to owe anybody so much that leaving would hurt them. That was the rule. It wasn't written anywhere; rules like hers never were. But it governed everything: the way she paid cash when she could, the way she slept at the airfield bunkhouse instead of accepting the spare rooms people offered, the way she'd ended — cleanly, kindly, she'd told herself — the one thing in Lowmark that had come closest to being more than a friendship, two years back, with a harbor pilot who'd started asking questions like *where do you winter?* and *who do you fly home to?*

Nobody. Nowhere. That was the answer, and it was a fine answer, and she'd said it gently and taken off before the look on his face could turn into something she'd have to carry.

She was afraid of losing nothing. She repeated that to herself sometimes, on long legs, the way other pilots recited emergency checklists. *I am afraid of losing nothing.* If you said it enough times, it stayed true.

* * *

By midday she'd made four drops. The basin cooperated the way it rarely did — high thin cloud but no buildups, wind steady out of the northeast, the river lying silver and docile in its channels. Over the fifth leg, between the villages of Tarnholt and the mission at Greywater, she let herself climb to a thousand feet just to look, because even a woman who measured time in fuel gauges had eyes.

The Kessari spread below her like a spilled necklace. Channels split and rejoined around islands that wouldn't exist next season. A raft train crawled downstream, tiny as insects, hauling timber toward Lowmark. And far upstream, past the last of the delta's haze, the country rose in long green folds toward the highlands — toward the headwaters, toward the place the charts gave up and printed in blank cream paper with a single note in faded type: *UNSURVEYED ABOVE THE FALLS.*

Her grandmother had come from up there. Or near there. Rosalind had never been sure of the details, because her mother had never been sure, because the story had been worn smooth by telling, the way river stones are — a woman, a valley, a war or a famine or a drowning, a baby carried south in a canoe. Every family in the basin had some version of that story. It was practically the regional anthem. Rosalind had never seen the point of holding onto a piece of it. You couldn't buy fuel with ancestry.

She checked her watch, tipped into a gentle bank, and dropped back down to the working altitude where the world turned back into cargo and gauges.

Greywater's station keeper was a nervous young man named Pell who signed for his crates twice and asked her, while she lashed the tie-downs, whether she'd heard anything in Lowmark about salvage boats moving upriver.

"Salvage boats?" She cinched the knot. "There's always salvage boats. Half the delta economy is pulling dead men's property out of the mud."

"Not mud boats." Pell lowered his voice, glancing upriver as if the water might be listening. "Deep-draft. Gunmetal. They came through Tuesday, headed up past the toll bridge, asking after old Veyl sites. Bought charts off the harbormaster. Paid triple."

Rosalind straightened. "Who pays triple for charts? Everything above the falls is blank paper."

"That's what I said." Pell wiped his hands on his coat. "The man I talked to said they weren't looking for what's on the charts."

She laughed it off — what else did you do with a rumor in a basin that grew rumors like rice? — but the phrase stuck to her through the afternoon like burrs to wool. *They weren't looking for what's on the charts.* She turned it over during the long leg to the furthest station, a lonely outpost on a bluff where an old nun dispensed medicine and opinions in equal measure, and she was still turning it over at sunset when she put down at the last strip of the day, a scraped-flat meadow above a fishing camp, to wait out the light.

Because here was the arithmetic of her life: she could not make the return leg to Lowmark before dark, and she did not fly the Kessari gorge at night, not for any debt. So she anchored the plane, boiled tea on a little spirit stove in the lee of the fuselage, ate Doss's bread watching the river turn from copper to iron to black, and told herself the unease in her chest was just the weather turning.

It was the weather turning. That was the thing she'd learn later — she'd been right, and wrong, in the exact proportion that mattered least.

* * *

The storm came down out of the northeast an hour after full dark, and it came like a raid.

She heard it before she saw it — a sound upvalley like a freight train deciding to leave the tracks — and had just enough time to lash the plane to three trees with every strap she owned and get herself under the fishing camp's drying rack before the front hit. Rain came sideways. The river, which had gone to bed docile, stood up and began to shout. Lightning walked along the ridgelines in long white strokes, and in each flash Rosalind saw the Kessari running brown and swollen, carrying whole trees, carrying fences, carrying, briefly and impossibly, what looked like the roof of a boat shed spinning end over end through the rapids.

She sat under the rack with her back against a post and did the only sensible thing, which was nothing. You didn't fight a basin storm. You waited it out and totaled the damage in the morning, the same as you totaled debts.

Somewhere past midnight, the rain thinned to a hiss. The lightning moved off downvalley, flickering behind her now instead of ahead, and the river settled from a roar into a deep muttering grind — the sound of a million tons of gravel being rearranged without anyone's permission.

In the gray hour before dawn, cold to the bone and too tired to sleep, Rosalind walked down to the water's edge to look at what the night had done to her landing bar.

The bar had grown. The flood had dumped a fresh tongue of gravel across it, pale and wet, and strewn the whole reach with the storm's leavings: branches, reed mats, a drowned goat, half a canoe. And there, fifty yards downstream, catching the first light like a struck match —

A gleam. Bronze-colored. Deliberate.

Not driftwood. Driftwood didn't shine in straight lines.

Rosalind stood very still, tea-less and unarmed and alone at the edge of the biggest river in the world, and felt the particular pull she'd spent her whole adult life training out of herself — the pull of a question. Curiosity was expensive. Curiosity made you land where you shouldn't, stay longer than you'd planned, talk to strangers, keep things. Curiosity was how a solitary life sprang leaks.

She should walk back to the plane, run up the engine, and fly home to Lowmark and her debt and her rule.

She looked at the gleam one more time. In the strengthening light it resolved into corners. Edges. Something built, something bound, something that had been underwater for a long time and had chosen last night, of all nights, to come ashore ten minutes' walk from where she happened to be sleeping.

"Well," she said to the river, to the gravel, to nobody. "That's not mine to leave."

She was already walking toward it before she finished saying it, and some small dry voice at the back of her mind — the voice that kept her accounts, that measured everything in fuel and miles — noted, with something that was almost amusement, that the rule against attachments had just developed its first crack.

She didn't break stride. The river, muttering over its rearranged gravel, didn't tell her what it had brought her.
