## 2. What the River Keeps

The storm broke at dawn the way storms did over the Kessari basin — all at once, like a held breath finally released. Rosalind Oyelaran watched it go from the cockpit of the *Kestrel*, her old bush plane tied down at a fuel cache on a bluff above the river, and she watched the water change.

That was the thing about the Kessari that most pilots never learned, because most pilots crossed it at altitude and thought of it as scenery: the river had moods, and after a storm it had opinions. It ran brown and muscular through its channel, hauling whole trees downstream like kindling, rearranging gravel bars overnight the way a restless sleeper rearranges blankets. A bar that had been safe landing ground on Tuesday could be ten feet underwater by Thursday. The river kept its own ledger, and it never showed anyone the books.

Rosalind had learned to read it anyway. Eleven years flying medicine and mail and the occasional stubborn passenger up and down this basin had taught her the river's grammar — where it braided and where it bit, which bends silted shallow in dry years, which cutbanks were eating themselves alive. She measured her life in those bends now. Fourteen bends to Lowmark. Nine to the mission at Teller's Reach. Twenty-two to the fuel cache where she'd spent the night listening to rain hammer the fabric of her wings and thinking about nothing in particular, because thinking about things was how you got attached to them.

She ran her preflight with the methodical economy of long habit. Fuel — half tanks, enough for the run to Lowmark if the wind behaved, which it wouldn't. Control surfaces clear of debris. Fabric skin taut, no new tears from the storm. She patted the cowling the way another woman might pat a horse, said nothing to it because talking to your plane was the first step toward talking to yourself, and climbed in.

The engine caught on the third turn, coughed, settled into its familiar ragged hum. Good old girl. They understood each other. Neither of them needed anything they couldn't fix with wire and pliers.

She lifted off the bluff strip into air still rinsed clean by the storm, and turned downriver toward Lowmark with a crate of insulin and a bag of letters lashed behind her seat.

* * *

The Kessari from eight hundred feet was a brown ribbon stitched through green, and Rosalind flew it the way she did everything — alone, attentive, and slightly bored, which was the safest way to fly anything. Boredom meant nothing was wrong. Boredom meant the gauges sat where gauges should sit and the wind came from where wind should come from and the only surprise in the world was the one you chose.

She was four bends past the cache when the river surprised her anyway.

It was small, at first. A glint. Sunlight off wet metal, down on a gravel bar that had no business having metal on it — a long pale spit of stone and silt that the storm had clearly rebuilt overnight, because she didn't remember it quite so wide, quite so bare. The glint flashed again as she passed over, deliberate as a signal mirror.

Rosalind's first instinct was professional and cold: *not your problem.* Glints on gravel bars were wrecked canoes, lost stove parts, junk the river coughed up. She had landed for junk exactly twice in eleven years, and both times it had been junk.

Her second instinct arrived a half-second later and was not professional or cold at all. It was the instinct of a woman who had spent eleven years looking down at that river and never once been invited to look closer. The storm had turned over something. The river kept things — everyone in the basin knew that; the Kessari had drowned a nation once, or so the stories went, though nobody agreed on what nation or when — and every few years it gave a piece back. A coin. A boot. Once, an old man swore, a door.

The glint flashed a third time.

"Oh, hell," Rosalind said to the empty cockpit, and banked.

The bar was longer than it had looked from altitude, maybe two hundred meters of fresh-washed gravel and driftwood, sitting between the main channel and a side slough choked with willow. She overflew it twice, checking for soft spots — the darker streaks where silt still held water — and picked her line down the coarse light gravel near the upstream tip. Wind was quartering off the water at maybe eight knots. Doable. Not comfortable, but doable.

She set the *Kestrel* down with three bounces she chose not to count, rolled out onto the firm gravel, and shut down into a silence so complete she could hear the river working, a low continuous grind of stone against stone somewhere beneath everything, like the basin clearing its throat.

The glint was forty meters off her left wingtip.

She walked toward it with the shotgun slung on her back out of habit rather than expectation — nothing out here needed shooting but weather — and her boots crunched on gravel still wet from the receding flood. The storm had stripped this bar clean. Whatever the water had buried, it had decided, sometime in the night, to stop keeping.

It was a chest.

Not a crate, not a locker — a chest, iron-strapped and bronze-bound, the bronze gone green-black with age except where the flood had scoured it bright. It was perhaps a meter long, half again as deep, sitting half-buried at the bar's edge where the water could reach it, and it was stamped.

Rosalind crouched beside it and wiped mud from the lid with her sleeve.

The stamp was a seal, pressed deep into a bronze plate: a circle broken by a wavy line, and inside the circle, a tower. Or what she took for a tower — a slender vertical form rising out of the wave, crowned with something star-shaped. Around the rim, characters she didn't recognize, angular and dense, worn nearly smooth.

She knew the shape anyway. Everyone in the basin knew the shape, even if they couldn't have said where from. It was on the old coins that turned up in nets. It was carved, defaced, on a marker stone outside Lowmark that the town had stopped being able to read two generations ago. It was the seal of Veyl — the drowned nation, the sunken kingdom, the place that was either a fable or a cautionary tale depending on who was telling it and how much they'd had to drink.

Veyl had been real. That much the river itself attested, in coins and stones and whatever this was. Veyl had been upriver, in the high country where the Kessari was born, and Veyl had ended badly — famine, the story went, then collapse, then the river taking the rest. The survivors had scattered or died. The valley had emptied. Maps stopped naming it.

And now here was a piece of it, sitting on a gravel bar like a gift, or a trap.

Rosalind stood there for a long moment, hands in her pockets, wind pulling at her collar, and conducted the argument she always conducted with herself, the one that kept her alive and alone and fine.

*Landing costs you daylight. Cargo is perishable. You don't know whose this is. Things that wash out of rivers come with histories attached, and histories attach themselves to whoever picks them up.*

All true. All sensible.

But the chest had been under that water for — decades? A century? — and the storm had chosen last night, of all nights, to give it up, and it had given it up to *her*, four bends from nowhere, on a morning when she happened to be flying low enough to see it flash. She didn't believe in fate. She believed in fuel gauges and river bends. But she believed in coincidence less than she believed in fate, and this was neither. This was just a thing that was here, and her curiosity — which she kept locked up tighter than her debts, tighter than her heart, and which had gotten out exactly never — was standing at the lock rattling the door.

"Ten minutes," she told the river, as if it could hold her to it. "Then I fly."

The chest was heavier than it looked. The locks were long corroded, but the flood had done her work for her — the hasp was sprung, one hinge bent, and when Rosalind threw her weight against the lid it gave with a groan of bronze and a smell that hit her like a fist: river mud, yes, but under it paper. Old paper. Ink. The smell of a room that had been underwater for fifty years and still remembered being a room.

Inside, packed in tarred canvas that had mostly done its job, lay documents.

Hundreds of them. Bundles of them, wrapped and sealed with wax that had gone the color of bone. Ledgers with covers swollen fat from water. Folios bound in cord. And on top, in a leather case that had protected it best, a single large sheet — vellum, or something like it — covered edge to edge in that same angular script, with the broken-circle-and-tower seal stamped at its head in red wax that had somehow survived.

Rosalind knelt there in the wind and the river-grind and did not touch any of it for a full minute.

Because she could read the shape of a thing even when she couldn't read the thing itself, and the shape of this was official. This wasn't somebody's sea chest, somebody's family keepsakes washed out of a drowned farmhouse. This was filed. Sealed. Stamped. This was paperwork from a government, and governments did not pack their archives in tarred canvas and put them in bronze-bound chests unless they wanted them to survive something.

Or unless someone else had wanted them to survive, and had failed, and the river had finished the job of hiding them for a century until last night, when it changed its mind.

She lifted the top folio out with both hands, careful as lifting a bird. The pages inside were waterlogged into a single brick, but the outer page had dried enough in the wind to show its ink, and the ink had run into gray ghosts — columns of figures, lines of script, and here and there, in a different, heavier hand, annotations in a language she almost recognized. Basin trade-pidgin borrowed half its words from somewhere, and some of those somewheres were right here, staring up at her.

One word repeated. She'd seen it before, scratched on the defaced marker outside Lowmark, and an old net-mender had translated it for her once, laughing, drunk: *the counting-house.* The word for the counting-house appeared on this page more than a dozen times, beside columns of numbers, and beside some of the numbers — she leaned close, squinting, telling herself she was imagining it — beside some of the numbers someone had drawn a line. Struck them through. And next to the struck-through figures, in that heavy second hand, a shorter word, repeated, insistent, angry even through a century of drowning:

The old man's other translation surfaced unbidden. He'd used it for the marker stone too, the second line nobody could read. *Taken. Carried off. Sold.*

Sold. Beside grain quotas. In a government ledger. From a nation that had starved.

Rosalind sat back on her heels.

The story everyone told was simple: Veyl starved because the river failed, because the rains failed, because God or geology turned its face away, and the poor bastards drowned in their own valley's grief. A tragedy. An act of weather. Nobody's fault, least of all anyone still living.

But ledgers don't record acts of weather. Ledgers record decisions. And this ledger — this waterlogged, half-dissolved, impossible survivor of a ledger — had a column of grain quotas with a hand-written line struck through each figure, and a word beside each strike meaning *sold*, and it was stamped with the seal of the government that was supposed to have fed those people.

The wind gusted. The river ground its stones. Somewhere down the bar, the *Kestrel* ticked as her engine cooled, patient, indifferent, waiting to leave.

Rosalind looked at the chest, and the chest looked back at her with a century of patience, and she felt the first crack run through the wall she'd built — not a big crack, not yet, just a hairline, the kind you don't notice until the whole pane comes down. Because the rule was: *nothing attaches.* No debts you can't pay off in fuel. No people you can't wave goodbye to from altitude. No history you can't log as someone else's problem. She had built that rule plank by plank over eleven years and it had held against weather, against loneliness, against the nights when the silence in a one-room shack got loud enough to have opinions.

And now a dead nation had reached up out of its grave and handed her its books.

"They're not yours," she said aloud, to the chest, to the river, to whoever might be listening — and heard immediately how thin it sounded, how much it sounded like a woman arguing with herself and losing. "You're cargo. I found cargo. I'll turn it in at Lowmark, collect whatever salvage bounty's going, and be done with it."

The river said nothing. Rivers are honest that way.

She worked fast after that, decision made and hating herself for how relieved she was to have made it. The tarred canvas went back over the bundles. The folio went back on top. She dragged the chest to the plane — it cost her twenty minutes and every muscle in her back, wrestling it up onto the cargo deck behind the seats, lashing it down with the tie-downs she usually trusted with medicine crates — and by the time she climbed back into the cockpit her hands were shaking, and she told herself it was the cold.

It wasn't the cold.

She ran up the engine and taxied to the head of the bar, and paused there, throttle at idle, looking downriver toward Lowmark and everything sensible.

Behind her, upriver, the Kessari ran on toward its source, toward the high country where maps stopped naming things, carrying its secrets the way it always had — patiently, in bulk, giving up only what it chose.

In the mirror of her rearview glance, the empty gravel bar already looked like it had forgotten her.

Rosalind pushed the throttle forward and took off, and the chest rode behind her seat like a passenger who hadn't introduced himself yet, and eleven hundred meters below, the river went on keeping what it kept.

She told herself she'd hand it over in Lowmark. She told herself that the whole flight down, watching the bends count themselves off beneath her wings.

She was lying, and some part of her — the part that had rattled the lock — knew it.
