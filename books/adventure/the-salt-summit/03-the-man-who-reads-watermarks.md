## 3. The Man Who Reads Watermarks

The rain in Lowmark came down the way debt did — slowly at first, then all at once, and always with interest. Tobias Wrenn stood under the awning of a chandler's shop on Rope Street and watched it hammer the harbor into gray lace, and he thought, not for the first time, that a man could learn everything he needed to know about a document from how water treated it.

Ink ran. Vellum buckled but held its truth. Cheap paper dissolved like a lie confronted.

He had spent eleven years of his life reading watermarks for the Royal Archive of Veyl, and four years since reading anything at all, because there was no royal archive anymore, no Veyl, no archive — only a drowned nation and a disgraced man who drank too much sweet tea and pretended to catalog shipping manifests for the Harbor Authority at a wage that kept him fed and nothing else.

The photograph was folded into his inner pocket, against his heart, where he had carried it for three days now. He took it out again anyway. He couldn't help himself. It was becoming a habit, the way prayer becomes a habit in people who don't believe.

A pilot named Rosalind Oyelaran had fished a bronze-bound chest out of the Kessari River nine days ago. The *Lowmark Courier* had run it on page six, below an advertisement for rat poison: SALVAGE PIULL PULLS MYSTERY CHEST FROM FLOOD WATERS. The photograph accompanying it was grainy and badly cropped, taken from twenty feet by a reporter who hadn't wanted to get her boots wet.

It didn't matter. Tobias would have known that chest blindfolded, deaf, dead.

Bronze corner-bindings cast in the waveline pattern — three crests and a trough, repeated, the rhythm of the Kessari itself. The seal stamped into the lid's iron lockplate: a circle broken by a vertical line, the Sign of the Kept Record, which meant *this has been written down so that it cannot be unwritten*. And on the binding, if the photographer had been close enough — he wasn't, but Tobias knew it was there — the assay mark of the Third Scriptorium at Vel Sarat, the capital, the one that had produced the government's official record chests in the last decade before the end.

Official record chests were supposed to have gone down with the capital. All two hundred and eleven of them. Tobias had signed the manifest himself, the week before the dams failed, certifying their loading onto barges for evacuation to the western vaults.

The barges had never arrived. The official history said they'd been lost in the flooding.

Tobias had spent four years suspecting the official history was a eulogy written by the murderer, and here, on page six of the *Courier*, under the rat poison, was a chest that should be at the bottom of the reservoir trench, sitting on a gravel bar in the middle river, having traveled two hundred miles downstream from nowhere it was ever supposed to leave.

"Closing time," said the chandler behind him, not unkindly. "You've been standing there an hour, Mr. Wrenn."

"I'm admiring your awning," Tobias said. "Fine canvas work."

"It's rotten." The chandler squinted at him. "You look terrible, Wrenn."

"I'm an archivist," Tobias said. "We're supposed to look like this. It's in the charter."

* * *

His room above the Saltcellar Tavern was eight feet by ten and smelled of lamp oil and the previous tenant's pipe smoke, which had soaked into the plaster sometime during the reign of whoever had reigned before the last one. Tobias lit the lamp, hung his coat on the nail, and sat down at the table with the photograph and a jeweler's loupe he'd bought secondhand and told everyone was for inspecting stamps.

He looked at the chest the way other men looked at the faces of women they had loved.

There. On the left-hand front binding, even through the grain of the cheap newsprint plate: a discoloration, a patch where the bronze had been scoured bright against the patina around it. Someone had recently removed something riveted to that binding. A plaque. The record chests each bore an inventory plaque — chest number, scriptorium, contents class. Tobias had personally engraved more than forty of them in his last year of service, when the government had begun, quietly, to recall chests from the provincial vaults "for conservation."

Chests went west for conservation. Chests did not come back. When he'd asked, once, politely, as a junior archivist asking is permitted to ask, the deputy registrar had smiled at him with all the warmth of a locked door and said, *Wrenn, some records are conserved by being allowed to rest.*

Then the famine came. Then the government blamed the river — blamed the spring floods, blamed the silt, blamed God and geology and everything except the granary ledgers Tobias had later seen being carried out of the registrar's office in sacks, at night, by men who did not walk like clerks. Then the dams failed — or were failed — and Veyl went under sixty feet of green water with two hundred thousand souls, and the world held a decent interval of mourning and moved on to other catastrophes, and Tobias Wrenn drank sweet tea in a port town and cataloged shipping manifests and waited, without admitting he was waiting, for the river to give back an answer.

Now it had given back a chest with its inventory plaque pried off, sitting in flood gravel two hundred miles from its grave.

Someone had tried to erase this chest's identity before it sank. Which meant someone had known what was inside. Which meant someone had wanted it forgotten. Which meant —

Which meant the men on the stairs.

Tobias heard them before he saw them, because he had learned to listen the way he had once learned to read watermarks: patiently, professionally, as a matter of survival. Two sets of boots on the Saltcellar's back stair, moving without hurry. Men in a hurry were amateurs. These were professionals, and professionals in Lowmark meant only one organization these days: the Meridian Salvage Consortium, which had spent the last three years buying up salvage rights along the whole Kessari basin with the patient appetite of something that ate countries.

He had first noticed them two days after the *Courier* piece ran. A pair of broad men in oilskins too clean for fishermen, drinking tea at the Saltcellar's corner table, not talking. Yesterday there had been a third, younger, who'd asked the landlady — loudly, badly — whether the archivist fellow upstairs had any family. Today they had stopped pretending to drink tea.

Tobias blew out the lamp.

He worked in the dark the way he'd worked in the dark of the archive stacks, by touch and memory. Photograph into oilskin wallet. Wallet into coat. Loupe into pocket. The window of his room opened onto the tavern's lean-to roof, and the lean-to roof dropped to the alley behind the fishmonger's, and the alley ran downhill to Rope Street, and Rope Street ran to the harbor, and the harbor was full of boats, and any boat was better than a staircase with two professionals on it.

He was three steps onto the wet shingles when the door below him opened and light spilled out, and a voice said, conversationally, "Mr. Wrenn. Your lamp just went out. In my experience, honest men finish their evenings. Guilty ones put out lamps."

Tobias froze, one hand on the slates, rain running off his collar.

"I'm not going to chase you," the voice continued. It was a pleasant voice, low and unhurried, a voice built for boardrooms and interrogation rooms interchangeably. "Chasing is undignified, and I'm told you're a quick little man. I only want to talk about a chest. Page six. Under the rat poison advertisement — did you see it? I thought the placement was almost poetic."

"You have me at a disadvantage," Tobias said carefully. "You know my name."

"And you know mine, though we haven't met. Corvane. Meridian Consortium, Cultural Recovery Division." A pause, and Tobias could hear the smile in it. "Yes, we have a Cultural Recovery Division. We recover culture. It's very fashionable. There are grants."

"There are," Tobias agreed, from the roof, in the rain. "I've read the prospectus. It's beautifully typeset. Whoever did the ligatures deserves prison."

That got a real laugh, brief and genuine, which frightened Tobias more than a threat would have. "Mr. Wrenn. Four years ago you were the youngest senior conservator in the history of the Royal Archive. Three years ago you were dismissed for falsifying a provenance report. One year ago you wrote a letter to the *Courier* disputing the official casualty figures from the Veyl inundation, and it was never printed, and you were warned off by the Harbor Authority, who were, I'll note, paid by us to warn you off. You are a man who cannot stop reading documents that other people want closed. So when a chest surfaces with a scraped binding, I know exactly what you're doing up there with your loupe, and I'm here to make you an offer before you do something expensive."

"What offer?"

"Employment. Consultation. Your expertise, our resources. We'll pay you what the Archive never did. You'll have access, funding, a proper laboratory. All we ask in return is first refusal on the contents. Provenance research takes months, Mr. Wrenn. Paper dries badly. Ink blooms. By the time you've authenticated whatever's in that chest, half of it will be pulp. We can preserve it properly. That's the whole point of us."

"And then?"

"Then it goes to a museum," Corvane said smoothly. "Eventually. After assessment."

*After assessment.* Tobias had read enough consortium contracts — he'd cataloged three of them for the Harbor Authority, innocently, before he understood whose money moved underneath them — to know what *after assessment* meant. It meant the contents entered a private vault in a country with favorable laws and emerged, years later, as lot numbers in an auction catalog, or didn't emerge at all. It meant the last administrative memory of a murdered nation would spend eternity in a drawer labeled *pending*, owned by men who measured everything in tonnage.

"No," Tobias said.

"No," Corvane repeated, tasting it. "You know, most people negotiate. It's the polite thing. You say no, I improve the offer, you say no again, we meet somewhere with wine."

"I've read your contracts. There's no somewhere with wine. There's a clause fourteen."

Silence for a moment. Rain on the shingles. Somewhere across the harbor a foghorn moaned like something dreaming.

"Clause fourteen," Corvane said at last, "is a legal formality regarding title transfer, and I'm disappointed you've been gossiping with freight clerks. But let's not quarrel. Consider it a standing offer. Consider also — " and here the pleasantness thinned, just slightly, the way varnish thins over a knot in the wood — "that the chest is currently sitting in a salvage yard belonging to a woman with debts. Debts, Mr. Wrenn, are a kind of watermark. Hold paper up to the right light and you can always see who really owns it. Good night."

The door closed. Boots descended the stair, unhurried, and the back door of the Saltcellar opened and shut, and the alley below Tobias stayed empty, because they hadn't needed to cover it. They had never intended to catch him. They had intended to introduce themselves.

Tobias sat down on the wet shingles and laughed, quietly, helplessly, until the laugh turned into something else that he wiped off his face with his sleeve.

* * *

The salvage yard was called Oyelaran Air & River, though *yard* flattered it. It was a gravel spit upstream of the town where a corrugated hangar leaned against the wind like an old man against weather, a fuel bowser rusting into modern art, and one floatplane tied at a rickety dock, silver-bodied, scarred, its paint worn away at the wing roots where hands gripped it climbing in and out, thousands of times, alone.

Tobias stood at the edge of the property in the gray morning and watched the pilot work.

She was tall, dark-skinned, maybe thirty-five, with her hair cut practical-short and forearms that spoke of engines and cargo nets. She was pulling maintenance panels off the floatplane's cowling with the economy of someone who had no one to impress and no time to spare, and she talked to the engine while she worked — actual words, low and continuous, complaints and encouragement, the way lonely people talk to things that answer in torque.

Rosalind Oyelaran. He'd made inquiries, of course; inquiry was reflex. Licensed bush pilot, seven years in the basin. Medicine runs, mail, salvage under permit. No partner, no crew, no family listed. Rented the spit from the harbor trust, arrears two quarters deep. Reputation: reliable, solitary, and — this from a fuel clerk who'd talked too long — *"the kind of woman who'd fly into weather to keep a promise and charge you double for it, and good luck getting her to stay for dinner."*

A woman with debts. A woman with a rule against attachments, if the clerk's gossip was to be believed. A woman holding a scraped record chest that Meridian's agents would come for within days, because Corvane did not make standing offers he intended to let stand.

She was, Tobias thought, entirely wrong for what he needed. She was suspicious, isolated, financially desperate in a way that made her vulnerable to exactly the pressure Corvane applied, and she owed him nothing.

She was also, he realized, watching her wipe grease off her hands and stare upriver with an expression he recognized from his own mirror — the expression of a person who had found something in the water and hadn't slept properly since — already halfway to being the only person in the basin who might do the right thing instead of the profitable one.

He walked down the gravel spit with his coat buttoned and his heart going like a boy's, and when she saw him coming she set down her rag and looked at him the way border guards look at weather fronts.

"You're not a customer," she said. "Customers park at the office. You walked the long way around so you could watch me work first. Either you're casing the place or you rehearsed a speech."

"Both," Tobias admitted. "My name is Tobias Wrenn. I was senior conservator of the Royal Archive of Veyl. Before it was an archive of anything, or royal, or existing." He held up the photograph, unfolded now, soft at the creases from three days in his pocket. "And I believe you pulled the Third Scriptorium chest 177 out of the river nine days ago, and I believe I am the only man alive who can tell you what's inside it, and I believe that within the week, men will come to take it from you who will not offer to buy it twice."

The pilot looked at the photograph for a long moment. Then at him. Wind off the river flattened the grass along the spit and made the floatplane's tie-down lines hum.

"Inside it," she said. "You know what's in it."

"I know what was *supposed* to be in it," Tobias said. "And I know what that means, and I promise you, Ms. Oyelaran, it is worse than treasure and worth more than salvage, and I need to get to it before the people who erased its name do."

Behind her, in the open hangar, the chest sat on a workbench under a tarp, and the wind lifted the tarp's corner just enough to show a line of bronze binding, three crests and a trough, the rhythm of the river.

Rosalind Oyelaran followed his eyes to it, then looked back at him, and Tobias watched her weigh him — the way she'd weigh a passenger, a cargo manifest, a stretch of weather — and he understood, with the particular dread of a man who has spent his life among records, that he had just become a document she was deciding whether to open.

"Get in out of the wind," she said finally. "And start talking. You've got as long as it takes me to decide whether to believe you."

From the tree line above the spit, unnoticed by either of them, a man in an oilskin too clean for fishing lowered a small camera and began, unhurried, to walk back toward town.
