## 4. Passenger Unknown

The fuel gauge on the Starling's port tank had been lying to her for three years, and Rosalind Oyelaran trusted it the way she trusted weather reports: with a margin for error and a plan for when it failed. She was checking the lie against the dipstick at dawn when she heard the footsteps on the dock boards behind her — too heavy to be Nia, too hesitant to be the harbormaster, and arriving an hour earlier than any honest man would be awake.

Tobias Wrenn came down the ramp with his coat buttoned wrong and his satchel clutched like a life vest. He'd slept, if he'd slept at all, in the chair by her office window; she'd left him there with a blanket and a warning that she locked up at midnight, and this morning the blanket was folded into a shape so precise it looked like it had been pressed.

"You're still here," he said, as though he'd bet against it.

"Plane doesn't fly itself." She wiped the dipstick on a rag. "Fuel's paid. Weather window closes by ten — that front off the Kessari headwaters is going to slam the basin shut by afternoon. If we're going, we go now."

He nodded too many times. "I have money. Not much, but—"

"You have documents I want read properly before I decide what they're worth, and you have a story about men following you that I haven't heard the end of yet. That's payment enough for now." She said it flatly, because kindness made her itch, and because the truth underneath it was simpler: she hadn't decided to care about Tobias Wrenn, but she had decided the chest in her hold mattered more than her rule against cargo with a pulse. That was already one compromise too many, and she knew it.

She ran the preflight anyway, every check, twice through the list while Tobias stood at the edge of the apron holding his satchel and looking like a man waiting to be told he could come aboard. The Starling was a twenty-year-old bush plane with a patched cowling and a radio that worked when it felt like it, and Rosalind loved it the way she permitted herself to love anything: completely, and from a safe distance of ownership. Machines didn't leave. Machines didn't drown. Machines didn't look at you with your dead sister's stubbornness and ask to be taken somewhere.

"Get in," she said. "Straps tight. Anything loose goes in the net."

* * *

They lifted off Lowmark into a sky the color of old tin, the river unspooling below them like a rope someone had dropped from heaven and never bothered to retrieve. Rosalind flew the bends by memory — the long oxbow past the cannery ruins, the gravel shoals where the water went pale green over sand, the first of the gorges where the Kessari narrowed and turned the color of tea. Beside her, Tobias held his satchel in his lap and watched the country go by with an expression she couldn't read: not wonder exactly, and not fear. Recognition, maybe. Like a man seeing a house he'd only known from photographs.

"You've been up here before," she said over the engine noise.

"Never." He raised his voice to carry. "But I've spent eleven years reading about it. Every survey, every shipping manifest, every scrap of correspondence that survived the collapse. I know the bend of this river better than the men who drowned in it."

"That's a strange kind of knowing."

"It's the only kind I was left with." He looked down at the water. "They took my archive access when they took my post. Did I tell you that? Eleven years ago. One day I'm the youngest senior archivist the royal collection ever appointed, and the next I'm escorted out by two men who won't tell me what I did wrong, except that asking questions about Veyl is apparently a form of doing something wrong."

"And now you ask them anyway."

"Now I ask them anyway." A thin smile. "It's the only skill I have left."

She banked around a flock of river terns and let the silence stand. Below, the country changed — the fishing camps thinned out, then vanished, and the forest came down to the banks like it owned them, dark spruce and black water and no smoke anywhere. This was the part of the run where most passengers went quiet. The Kessari inland had a way of making people feel briefly, accurately small.

It was somewhere over the second gorge that she heard the thump.

Not engine trouble — she knew every sound the Starling made and its whole vocabulary of complaints. This came from behind her, from the cargo bay, where the chest was lashed under netting and where nothing else should have been. She turned in her seat, and Tobias saw her face change and turned too, and both of them looked back through the open cabin door at the tarpaulin covering the aft compartment.

The tarpaulin moved.

Rosalind put the plane level, trimmed her, and said, in a voice she kept very calm because calm was cheaper than panic at eight hundred feet: "Come out where I can see you. Slowly. Hands visible."

A pause. Then the tarp shifted and a girl crawled out into the cabin — sixteen, maybe seventeen, dark-haired, river-mud dried on her sleeves, wearing a canvas jacket two sizes too big with the cuffs rolled four times. She got her feet under her, braced against the fuselage wall, and looked Rosalind dead in the eye with the particular defiance of someone who has already calculated that being thrown out of a moving aircraft is unlikely and has decided to spend that advantage freely.

"I paid for the fuel," the girl said.

"You did *what?*"

"The harbormaster. Yesterday. I gave him coin to look the other way while I got in the hold." Her chin came up. "So technically I'm a fare, not a stowaway."

Rosalind stared at her. Behind her, Tobias made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The engine droned. The river slid past below, indifferent.

"Name," Rosalind said.

"Nia."

"Full name."

"Nia Sef-Adara." She pronounced it carefully, syllables placed like stones across a stream, and Rosalind caught Tobias go very still beside her. "My grandmother's name. It's Veyli. Mine too, sort of. She gave it to me before she died."

"When did she die?"

"Last winter." For the first time the girl's voice lost its edge. "Waiting. That's what everyone said — she died waiting. There was this thing she used to say, in her language, when the storms came off the headwaters. *The source will answer.* She'd say it like — like it was a promise somebody owed her." Nia shrugged, defiant again, armor back up. "I figured somebody should go collect."

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment. Sixteen years old. Mud on her sleeves. Coin spent on a harbormaster's silence, which meant the girl had walked away from everything she had to sit in a cold cargo hold all night breathing kerosene fumes, betting her whole life on a stranger's heading. And the worst of it — the part Rosalind resented even as it happened — was that some cold locked room inside her own chest, the one she kept swept and empty and locked, had heard *the source will answer* and turned over in its sleep.

"Strap in," she said finally. "There's a harness on the bulkhead. You ride back there, hands off the chest, and when we land we are going to have a conversation about the difference between a fare and a stowaway, and it is going to be longer than you want it to be."

Nia scrambled forward and buckled in, and if she smiled about winning, she had the sense to do it facing forward where Rosalind couldn't see.

* * *

They put down at midday on the shingle bar where the Kessari bent north, a strip of gravel wide enough for the Starling's wheels and nothing else, water loud on both sides. Rosalind had planned it as a fuel-and-decision stop: drain the reserve tank, eat, and decide whether Tobias's destination was a place or a suicide. What she hadn't planned was the conversation, and it found her anyway while she crouched at the fuel valve, because Nia appeared at her elbow holding three tin cups of tea like an offering, and refusing tea in the basin was close to a sin.

"My grandmother's name was Adara," Nia said, without preamble, sitting down on the gravel beside her. "Before she married. She came out of the collapse when she was nine. Walked downstream with her mother for six days, and her mother didn't make it. She never talked about it except in her sleep, and in her language, and she taught me the words without ever telling me what they were for." The girl wrapped both hands around her cup. "When she was dying she held my wrist really hard and said the source would answer. I thought she was confused. Old people get confused." A pause. "Then your archivist showed up in town asking about the seal on a chest, and I saw the drawing, and it was the same mark she had on her bracelet. So either it's a coincidence, or she wasn't confused, and I don't believe in coincidences that big."

From across the fire ring, Tobias said quietly, "What was the mark?"

Nia dug under her collar and pulled out a cord. On it hung a small bronze disc, green with age, stamped with the same coiled-river sigil that was embossed on the chest in Rosalind's hold. She held it out. Tobias took it with both hands, the way you handle a thing that might be the last of its kind, and for a moment he couldn't speak at all.

"This is a source-token," he said finally. "We had three in the collection. All catalogued, none explained. They were found downstream, always downstream, always decades after the collapse. We assumed they were votive objects. River offerings." He looked up at her. "Nobody ever asked who was offering, or to whom."

"Somebody upstream," Nia said. "That's what she meant. Somebody upstream who never answered." Her jaw set. "Until maybe now."

Rosalind drank her tea and said nothing, and hated how carefully she was listening.

* * *

The decision came at dusk, as decisions do, whether you invite them or not.

They'd repacked the plane, and Tobias had spread a handful of the driest archive pages on a groundsheet, weighting the corners with stones, and the three of them knelt around them in the last light while he translated haltingly — grain quotas, requisition orders, a signature repeated on page after page that he touched with one finger like a wound. And Rosalind understood, watching him, that whatever she decided tonight wasn't about cargo anymore. The chest had stopped being salvage the moment a sixteen-year-old put a dead woman's name between herself and it.

"We camp here tonight," she said, standing, brushing gravel off her knees. "Fly at first light. After that—" She stopped. Looked at Nia, who was watching her with her grandmother's stubbornness worn openly, like a borrowed coat. Looked at Tobias, who was watching her the way drowning men watch a rope.

"After that," she said, and heard her own voice do something it hadn't done in years, which was decide out loud in front of people, "we go upriver together. All three of us. But hear me, both of you — I fly the plane, I set the rules, and the first rule is nobody else hides in my hold. If you've got something to say, you say it to my face, even if it's bad news. Especially if it's bad news. Understood?"

"Understood," Tobias said.

"Yeah," Nia said. Then, quieter, almost grudging: "Thanks."

"Don't thank me yet." Rosalind banked the fire for the night and checked the tie-downs on the Starling one more time, though she'd already checked them, because checking things was how she kept the world at arm's length and the world, lately, kept stepping closer. "Thank me when we're still alive at the source."

Above the black line of the gorge, the first stars were out, and the river ran on toward the headwaters, carrying its drowned secrets north. Somewhere behind them, in Lowmark, men who wouldn't give their names were asking questions about a chest. Somewhere ahead, a valley full of answers sat behind whatever gate the water had built. And in between, one battered plane, one disgraced archivist, one stowaway-turned-fare, and a pilot who had spent her whole life measuring distance in miles instead of people, about to learn the exchange rate.

Rosalind lay back against the fuselage and listened to the Kessari run in the dark, and told herself the tightening in her chest was just the altitude.

It wasn't.
