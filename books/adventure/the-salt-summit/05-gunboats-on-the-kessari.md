## 5. Gunboats on the Kessari

Tobias Wrenn had spent eleven years learning to read the small signs of danger: a door left open that should have been shut, a clerk's smile held one beat too long, the particular silence of a room in which someone had recently been searching for something and failed to find it. In the royal archives he had catalogued these signals under a private heading he'd never dared write down — *evidence of intent* — because an archivist who admitted the archives were dangerous was an archivist already halfway to the gallows.

So when the fuel barge at Serren's Landing radioed ahead with cheerful efficiency about diesel prices and dock space, and when Rosalind took the call and frowned at nothing for four full seconds before answering in her flattest voice, Tobias felt the old familiar prickle at the back of his neck.

"Something wrong?" he asked.

"Nothing." She banked the plane gently toward the wide brown sweep of the river below. "Barge master's being very helpful. Nobody on this river is ever that helpful."

The landing appeared out of the morning haze like something remembered rather than seen: a floating platform of tarred logs lashed to pilings, a fuel shed with a corrugated roof gone orange with rust, three houseboats moored downstream, and beyond them the town itself — Lowmark's poorer cousin, all stilt-houses and drying nets and a single street that ended at the water as if the town had simply given up trying to go further inland. A church spire leaned eastward, patient as a man waiting out a long argument.

And moored at the far end of the platform, riding high and white and obscene against the working boats around it, was a gunboat.

Not a navy boat. Navy boats announced themselves. This one wore no markings at all, just clean lines and a canvas canopy aft and two crewmen in plain grey who stood at the rail doing an excellent impression of men with nothing to do.

"They were here before us," Tobias said quietly.

"Or they flew." Rosalind's hands moved through the landing checks without hurry, but he had learned by now to read her economy of motion the way he read watermarks — what she didn't do told him more than what she did. She hadn't touched the throttle trim. She wasn't looking for a place to turn around. "We need fuel. We take fuel. We leave. Simple transaction."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it isn't, and I've survived worse transactions." She glanced at him, and there was something in her face he was beginning to recognize — not fear, exactly, but a kind of cold bookkeeping. "Stay in your seat until I say otherwise. And Tobias — whatever happens, the chest stays under the tarp, and you don't confirm you know what's in it."

"I know how to be questioned," he said. "I was questioned professionally for nine years."

"That was people asking about grain shipments." She brought the plane down onto the river with barely a ripple. "This will be different."

* * *

The chest sat beneath the cargo tarp between them, and Tobias was aware of it the way a man is aware of a sleeping animal in the room — its weight, its warmth of implication, the sense that at any moment it might wake and change everything. He had spent the flight from Lowmark with his hand resting on the bronze binding through the canvas, not reading it, just confirming it. Waterlogged paper could still be saved. He had dried worse. He had dried the chancery records out of a flooded sub-basement once, page by page over charcoal braziers, while the palace above him burned in the revolution's first week. Paper was patient. Paper forgave almost anything if you got to it in time.

What paper did not forgive was fire, and what governments did not forgive was proof.

Nia was another matter. The girl had materialized in Lowmark like damp — discovered only after she'd already soaked into everything — and Rosalind had made three separate announcements that she would be put off at the next settlement, each of which had expired unenforced. Now Nia crouched behind the pilot seats with her grandmother's cloth bundle clutched to her chest, watching the gunboat with the fixed, unblinking attention of someone who had grown up around soldiers and knew exactly what their stillness meant.

"Don't stare," Tobias murmured without turning his head.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring the way my mother stared at tax assessors."

That earned him half a breath of something that was almost a laugh, which he counted as a victory. The girl frightened him, in truth — not her presence but her purpose. She spoke Veyli. Badly, childishly, the way heritage survives in kitchens and lullabies rather than courts, but she spoke it, and every word out of her mouth was a word the ledgers said had died with the drowned quarter-million. If she existed, others might. And if others did, then everything the syndicate wanted from the river was worth infinitely more than even Corvane's people understood.

He was still turning that thought over when the gangplank knocked against the pontoon.

The man who came up it did not look like a syndicate agent. He looked like a university lecturer on holiday — linen jacket, silver at the temples, a canvas hat carried in one hand with the easy courtesy of a man raised to remove it indoors. He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in a way that suggested the linen concealed muscle rather than softness, and he climbed aboard the moored plane as though he owned the river and was merely inspecting a tenant's property.

Rosalind stepped out of the cockpit to meet him, positioning herself — Tobias noted with professional admiration — precisely between the man and the cargo tarp.

"Miss Oyelaran," the man said. "Ros— may I? I'm given to understand pilots prefer brevity." He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Corvane. I represent the Meridian Recovery Consortium. We're conducting archaeological survey work upstream. I wonder if I might impose on a few minutes of your time."

"The sign on the barge says fuel sales," Rosalind said. "You'll want the office."

"I've already settled your fuel account." Corvane gestured vaguely shoreward, and Tobias saw, with a lurch, one of the grey-clad crewmen handing a folded wad of notes across to the barge master, who took it with the expression of a man accepting payment for something he preferred not to think about. "Consider it a professional courtesy. I find that conversations go better when no one is watching a meter."

"Then talk fast," Rosalind said, "because we're burning daylight."

"Daylight." Corvane turned the word over appreciatively. "Yes. You people who live by weather and fuel gauges — you have such a concrete relationship with time. I envy it." He let his gaze travel along the fuselage, unhurried, cataloguing. "May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He stepped past Rosalind — close enough that she shifted her weight, and Tobias saw her right hand drift toward the flare pistol clipped under the dash — and stopped at the cargo tarp, looking down at it the way a man looks at a wrapped gift whose shape he already knows.

"There was a storm last week," Corvane said conversationally. "Big one. Took a barge apart at the Halloway bend, drowned two deckhands, scattered cargo for forty miles of riverbank. Salvage crews have been walking the gravel bars ever since." He glanced back at Rosalind. "Mostly they find net floats and diesel drums and the sad ordinary debris of commerce. But one of our survey teams reported something unusual. Bronze strapping. Chancery-grade workmanship. A seal."

Nobody breathed. Downstream, water slapped against the pontoons in a rhythm like slow applause.

"Now," Corvane went on, pleasantly, "our team is thorough but unimaginative. They logged coordinates and moved on, because a sealed chest on a gravel bar is inventory, and inventory waits. But I am not unimaginative, Miss Oyelaran. I know what a Veyli chancery seal means. I know that everything official from that country sank with it, deliberately, in weighted crates, and I know that whoever pulled one up has in their possession either worthless scrap or the most valuable paper on this river." He smiled again, and this time the smile did not reach anywhere at all. "I'd very much like to know which."

"You're welcome to walk the gravel bars yourself," Rosalind said. Her voice was level. Tobias, who had spent his life listening for the tremor under composure, heard none — and marveled at it. "Sounds like your teams are already doing that."

"They are. But gravel bars move, Miss Oyelaran. Rivers rewrite themselves nightly. Coordinates from Tuesday are folklore by Friday." He spread his hands. "Which is why I prefer to deal with people rather than places. People hold still. People can be found again." His eyes moved, briefly, to Tobias — a flicker, precise as a watermark — and returned. "Your passenger, for instance. Dr. Tobias Wrenn. Formerly Keeper of the Royal Archive of Veyl. Currently a disgraced fugitive living under a borrowed name in a Lowmark boarding house, drinking too much and copying other people's shipping manifests for money."

Tobias felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped into still water. He had known, of course, that he was followed out of Lowmark. He had chosen not to name it, the way one chooses not to name a diagnosis. Hearing it spoken aloud in that mild, courteous voice was different. It made the thing real. It made it *filed*.

"You have me at a disadvantage," he managed.

"No, Doctor, I have you at an advantage." Corvane turned to face him fully, and for a moment the holiday-lecturer mask thinned and Tobias saw the machinery underneath — patient, vast, and entirely without malice, which was worse than malice. "Because unlike the committee that ruined you, I don't think you falsified the accession ledgers. I think you found something in them you shouldn't have, and you were made to carry the blame for finding it. Am I close?"

Tobias said nothing. Beside him, he felt Rosalind go very slightly stiller, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"So here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I respect your time." Corvane produced a slim leather folio from inside his jacket and laid it on the crate beside the tarp, open, facing them. Inside lay a banker's draft. Tobias's eyes went to the figure and his mind refused it, the way the eye refuses an impossible perspective. It was more money than the archive's entire annual acquisition budget had been, in the years when there had been an archive. "For the chest. Contents unseen, contents unexamined, contents delivered to my barge within the hour. You fly on. No names recorded, no questions asked, no interest taken in where you go or why. The Consortium forgets you exist. That," he added gently, "is the most valuable line item on the document, and it isn't printed."

"And if we decline?" Rosalind asked.

"Decline?" Corvane looked genuinely puzzled for a moment, as though she had used a word from a dead language. Then understanding arrived, and with it something almost like regret. "Ah. You're wondering about consequences. Miss Oyelaran, I want to be honest with you, because honesty is cheaper than violence and lasts longer. If you decline, nothing happens today. Nothing happens tomorrow. We are not pirates; we don't board planes and put guns to heads. That's crude, it attracts navies, and navies are bad for business." He picked up his hat, turned it once in his hands. "But the Consortium has contracts upstream. Survey rights, recovery rights, certain concessions that required — effort — to arrange. And those contracts depend on certain information remaining where it has been for twenty years: underwater, unofficial, unwitnessed. If that changes — if the story of Veyl's ending gets rewritten by amateurs with a chest full of paper — then the value of everything we've built drains away like fuel through a bad valve. And when a great deal of money is threatened, Doctor, Miss Oyelaran, the people who stand to lose it stop being reasonable. Not me. I stay reasonable to the end. But reason has employees, and employees have boats, and boats are faster than that plane of yours."

He said it all without raising his voice. That was the horror of it, Tobias thought later. There was no menace in the delivery at all. It was arithmetic. It was a man reading a ledger aloud, and in the ledger their lives appeared only as a rounding error — a cost to be priced, deferred, or absorbed.

Behind the seats, Nia made a sound. Small. Involuntary. Tobias heard it and closed his eyes for half a second, praying to a god he'd stopped believing in during the trial that the girl would keep silent.

Corvane heard it too. His head tilted, birdlike, toward the sound — and then, visibly, deliberately, he chose to ignore it. That choice frightened Tobias more than anything else the man had done. A man who noticed a hidden child and filed her away for later was a man who never wasted a move.

"Well." Corvane settled the hat on his head and stepped back toward the gangplank, pausing at the fuselage door with one hand on the frame. "The draft is valid for one hour. After that it's void, and so is the forgetting." He looked at Rosalind, and for just a moment the courtesy dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was neither cruel nor angry — only absolute, like deep water. "I should tell you something, since we're being honest. Twenty years ago I worked the recovery contracts on the Kessari salvage. I personally signed off on the manifests for the weighted crates. I know exactly what your government threw in this river and why, because I helped throw it." A pause. "There is no version of the truth in that chest that I haven't already profited from. Think about that while you decide whether the truth is worth more to you than your lives. Most people, in my experience, discover it isn't."

He went down the gangplank without hurrying. The grey crewmen fell in behind him. The gunboat's engine turned over — a soft, expensive sound — and the white boat slid away downstream, unhurried, insolent in its patience, and was gone into the haze.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. The fuel barge attendant came out, began pumping diesel with the studied deafness of a man who had heard everything and intended to repeat none of it.

"He knows my name," Tobias said at last. His own voice sounded far away to him. "My real name. My real position. That report was written years ago — the phrasing, the file references. Someone has been carrying my dossier for a decade, waiting for me to become useful."

"Or waiting for you to become a problem." Rosalind was already moving, checking the fuel gauge, checking the tie-downs, her hands busy so her mind could work. "They didn't follow you out of Lowmark, Tobias. They *let* you leave. You're a leash. They figured sooner or later you'd lead them to whatever you were looking for." She jerked her chin at the tarp. "And here we are."

"Then we shouldn't have stopped for fuel."

"We stop for fuel or we swim." She turned and looked at him straight on, and he saw that the cold bookkeeping was still there, but something else had joined it — something harder and hotter underneath. "One hour. That's his clock. So we break it. We fuel fast, we lift in twenty minutes, and we go somewhere his boats can't follow."

"Where?" It was Nia, emerging from behind the seats, chin up, eyes bright and furious. "Everything downstream is his. Everything on the charts is his. My gran said—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "My gran said the river keeps its own maps. The ones that aren't printed."

Tobias looked at the girl, then at the tarp-covered chest, then at the empty stretch of river where the gunboat had vanished, and felt the old archivist's instinct stir — the one that had gotten him ruined, the one that recognized a citation when it heard one.

"Nia," he said carefully. "What else did your grandmother say?"

Above them the haze was thickening, and somewhere upstream, faint as a watermark, thunder was already gathering.
