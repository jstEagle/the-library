## 6. The Guide with Two Names

The river woke before Emeric did, the way it always did — a low grinding of water over stone that came up through the floorboards of his shack like a second heartbeat. He lay in the dark a moment longer than he needed to, listening to it, the way other men listened for their wives' breathing or their children's crying. Forty-one years on the Kessari and its tributaries, and still the river had never once said his name.

It didn't know his name. That was rather the point of him.

He rose, banked the stove coals, and put water on for tea while the light came up gray through the frost-furred window. Outside, Lowmark was already stirring — gulls arguing over the fish market scraps, the first trawlers coughing out toward the delta bar, the smell of tar and diesel and cold mud that he'd stopped noticing decades ago and could not stop noticing now, when it mattered. Down at the moorings, his boat *Kestrel* sat low in the water where he'd left her, tarped against the drizzle, looking exactly like what she was supposed to be: an honest working craft belonging to an honest working guide with no history worth mentioning.

Emeric Sable. Thirty years under that name. Long enough that the name had grown skin around itself, callus over callus, until sometimes even he had trouble finding the man underneath. He'd been fourteen when his mother chose it, standing in the immigration queue at Lowmark with her hand crushing his shoulder and her eyes fixed anywhere but the clerk's face. *Sable,* she'd said, because it meant nothing. Because a name that meant nothing couldn't be traced back to anything. And *Emeric* because — well. Even she hadn't explained that one. Some things you kept even when you buried everything else.

His mother had been dead eleven years now, buried upriver in a village called Tarnholt where nobody knew what she'd carried across the border sewn into the lining of her coat, and Emeric had spent those eleven years being precisely as interesting as driftwood. He guided fishermen who wanted the pike holes. He ferried surveyors who paid badly and complained worse. He knew every bend of the Kessari from the delta to the Grayline Falls, every sandbar that moved with the spring melt, every rock that ate propellers — and he told none of them anything except what they needed to know to stay alive and come back and pay him.

It was a good life. It was a small life. He had built it small on purpose, the way you build a shelter in country where big things get struck by lightning.

So when the woman came down the dock at midmorning with a chest under her arm and two strangers trailing her like gulls after a gutting boat, Emeric's first instinct was to be busy elsewhere.

* * *

He saw them from the fuel shed, pretending to inventory rope. The pilot he knew by reputation: Rosalind Oyelaran, the bush flier who ran medicine up the basin in weather that grounded everyone else. Tall, brown-skinned, hair cropped close, moving with the particular economy of someone whose body was a tool she maintained rather than inhabited. She set the chest down on the dock planks with a care that told him everything before he'd seen so much as a hinge.

The old man beside her was another matter. Thin, rumpled, hands fluttering near the chest without quite touching it, the way a man hovers near a thing he loves and fears he doesn't deserve. Tobias Wrenn. Emeric knew the name the way everyone in Lowmark's older generation knew it — the royal archivist, the one they'd dragged out of the Veyl consulate the night the news came, the one who'd spent twenty years saying the famine wasn't famine. Disgraced. Struck from the rolls. Living above a bookbinder's shop and drinking tea instead of whiskey only because whiskey cost more.

And behind them both, half-hidden, a girl. Sixteen maybe, dark-eyed, watchful, wearing a coat too big for her and carrying herself like someone who had learned recently and painfully that doors close.

Emeric inventoried them the way he inventoried weather: fast, and with an eye for what would kill him.

"Guide?" the pilot said, when he finally let himself be found. "We need someone who knows the upper Kessari. Above Grayline. Where planes can't go."

"No one goes above Grayline," Emeric said. "That's why the planes can't go."

"We're going."

He looked at her. She looked back, unblinking, and there was something in her face he recognized from long ago — the look of a person who has decided and is merely waiting for the world to catch up. His mother had worn that look the day she packed one bag for two people.

"Salvage work?" he asked, nodding at the chest.

"Something like that."

"Syndicate pays better."

"I'm not syndicate."

"No," he agreed. "You're not." He'd seen the syndicate men in town all week — Corvane's people, crisp jackets and soft boots, asking questions at the harbormaster's office with money folded inside the questions. Whatever this woman had pulled out of the river, the sharks had already smelled the blood in the water. Which meant taking her contract meant swimming with the sharks, and Emeric Sable had survived thirty years by knowing exactly which waters held teeth.

"Two hundred a day," he said, naming a number designed to end the conversation. "Plus hazard pay past the falls. I don't do rescues. If you go in the water, I'll pull you out once. After that you're cargo."

The girl made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

The pilot didn't blink. "Done."

Emeric stared at her. "That was supposed to be insulting."

"It was. But we don't have time for you to enjoy it." She put out her hand. "Rosalind Oyelaran. This is Tobias Wrenn. The girl is Nia."

He shook her hand because refusing would have required an explanation, and explanations were the one currency Emeric Sable never carried. Her grip was dry and strong. Her eyes went briefly to the chest, then away, and he understood that she was watching to see if he looked at it too long.

He looked at it too long.

* * *

The seal was bronze, green with river patina, and it caught the flat morning light as Wrenn shifted the chest to load it aboard the *Kestrel*. A circle broken by three lines descending into a point — the Veyl mark, the sign of the mountain feeding the river, the emblem that had flown over a nation for six hundred years and then, in the space of one winter, over nothing at all.

Emeric had seen it twice in his life. Once in a history primer, printed small and safe, in a schoolroom in Lowmark where a teacher said *Veyl* the way you say a word for a disease. And once sewn into the lining of his mother's coat, worked in thread the color of dried blood, where she thought no one would ever find it and where he had found it anyway, at nine years old, hunting for coins.

She had caught him with it. He remembered the exact temperature of her hands as she took it from him — cold, always cold, his mother's hands, the cold of a country she never named — and the way she'd looked at him, not angry, just stripped open, a door blown off its hinges in a house he hadn't known was a house.

"You never speak of this," she'd said. "Not to anyone. Not ever. People who speak of Veyl disappear, my love. They disappeared there, and they disappear here. You want to live? Be a stone in the river. Stones don't drown."

He had been a stone for thirty years. He was very good at it. Stones got to watch the water go by.

"Emeric?" The pilot was looking at him. "You still with us?"

"Just pricing your cargo," he said. "Bronze like that, sealed bindings — it'll ride high and dry amidships, lashed to the thwart. Water gets in a boat, you want the heavy things centered." All true. All irrelevant. He took the chest from Wrenn and felt its weight settle into his arms, and something in his chest settled with it, some ballast shifting after decades of level trim.

Wrenn watched him handle it with naked anxiety. "Careful — the documents are wrapped in oilcloth, but—"

"They've been underwater thirty years, archivist. Another week won't kill them." He stowed it, lashed it, tested the lashings twice because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. When he straightened, the girl — Nia — was studying him with the frank, unnerving attention of the young.

"You knew what the seal was," she said. "Before he opened it. I saw your face."

"Fishing boats sink in this river," Emeric said. "Old wrecks come up all the time. You see enough wrecks, you learn the makers' marks." It was a good lie. It was one of his best lies, smooth as river stone, and it should have slid off her the way all his lies slid off the tourists and the surveyors and the tax collectors.

Nia looked at him a moment longer, and said nothing, and he understood that she was adding him to some ledger of her own.

* * *

They cast off at noon with the tide, and Emeric put the *Kestrel*'s nose upstream into the brown current and tried very hard to think about nothing but throttle angles and channel markers.

It didn't work.

Upstream was where his mother had come from. Not this stretch — she'd crossed the border far to the north, through passes he'd never seen, in a winter that killed the others in her party, the story she told once and never again. But the river was one animal from source to sea, and every mile of it he pushed against was a mile closer to the country she had fled and the name she had drowned so that he could float.

Sable. He turned the name over in his mind like a coin he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was currency. His mother's real name he knew — of course he knew it; she'd whispered it to him exactly once, the night before she died, in the fevered certainty that the room was full of listeners, gripping his wrist with those cold hands: *Maren Veylova. Say it once a year so it stays alive. Only once. Only in your head.* And he had. Every year, on the anniversary, alone in whatever shack or bunkhouse he occupied, he'd stood at a window facing north and said it once, silently, into the dark. Maren Veylova. Then he'd gone back to being Emeric Sable, stone in the river, and the year would grind on.

Thirty years of camouflage. He knew its price the way a soldier knows an old wound — not the sharp cost of the moment but the slow tax of every day since. Camouflage meant he could not mourn in public. Could not stand at the consulate wall on the anniversary of the collapse with the other survivors' children and light a lamp. Could not answer, when drunk fishermen joked about the drowned nation and its starving fools, *my mother was not a fool.* Camouflage meant that when the girl's grandmother died waiting for the source to answer — Nia had told them the story the night before, and Emeric had listened with his back carefully turned and his heart going like a trapped bird — Emeric had known exactly what kind of waiting that was, because his mother had done it too, standing at windows, listening to rivers, for forty years, for a homeland that had eaten itself.

And now here came this woman with a chest full of proof, and an archivist with nothing left to lose, and a girl who spoke eleven words of a language Emeric had been forbidden even to hum — and the river was pulling them all upstream together, toward the place where his mother's story had started and ended, and the stone in the river was starting, horribly, to feel the current move it.

He signed on for reasons he hid. That was the truth of it, and he held the truth the way he held everything: below the waterline, where the hull was thickest. He told himself it was the money. Two hundred a day was real money, and the airfield debts were real debts, and a man needed reasons that would survive scrutiny. He told himself it was the challenge — nobody ran the upper Kessari, and a guide who could would own the trade. Both reasons were true. Neither reason was the reason.

The reason was a coat lining and a whisper and a grave in Tarnholt with a false name carved on it, and a promise he'd made at nine years old and never once been able to keep properly: *I'll remember.* Remembering, it turned out, was not the same as honoring. Honoring might require him, at last, to stop being a stone.

* * *

The first day they made good distance, running the channel below the Grayline rapids with the engine at a steady thrum and the rain holding off like a favor. Rosalind turned out to be useful aboard — quick hands, no seasickness, an instinct for balance that he supposed came from flying, from living inside a machine that punished carelessness instantly. Wrenn sat aft most of the day with the chest open on his knees, drying pages by turns over a shielded lantern, and muttering to himself in a language that made the hairs on Emeric's arms stand up.

Veyli. The archivist spoke it fluently, prayer-soft, reading aloud to himself, and Emeric steered and did not turn around and felt each syllable land between his shoulder blades like sleet.

Nia sat near Wrenn, listening, occasionally repeating a word. Her accent was terrible. Her hunger for it was bottomless. Toward evening he heard her attempt a phrase over and over — something short, melodic, obviously memorized from a song — and Wrenn correcting her gently, and her trying again, and again, stubborn as water working at stone.

"What's the phrase?" Emeric heard himself ask, before the stone in him could stop his mouth.

Nia looked up, surprised and pleased. "My grandmother used to sing it. Wrenn says it means *the mountain remembers what the river forgets.*"

"The mountain remembers what the river forgets," Wrenn confirmed. "It's from a navigation song, actually — the old pilots' songs. Why?"

"No reason," Emeric said. "Sounds nice."

But his hands were not steady on the tiller for the next mile, because his mother had sung that. Snatches of it, washing dishes, mending nets, thinking herself unheard — a low tuneless humming that young Emeric had assumed was just a song, any song, the way children assume everything. The mountain remembers what the river forgets. She had carried the map of a drowned country in her mouth for forty years and sung it over dishwater in a fishing town that never once asked her where she'd learned it, because she had taught them not to ask, because asking was how people disappeared.

Stones don't drown, she'd told him. But stones also don't sing, and somewhere in the last thirty years Emeric had begun to suspect that the silence was drowning him by inches anyway, slow as rot, and that the only question left was whether he'd die a stone or risk becoming what he actually was.

Night came down. He anchored in a cutbank eddy below the first gorge, rigged the tarp, dished out stew from the thermos while the others talked in low voices about routes and radio frequencies and the syndicate gunboats that Wrenn swore were two days behind them. Gunboats. On his river. In the water where he'd scattered his mother's ashes — no. Where he'd promised to scatter them and hadn't yet, because Tarnholt was downstream and downstream was the past and he had spent thirty years pointed the other way.

He lay in the bow that night with the tarp dripping and the river talking to itself in the dark, and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the dock.

Above Grayline Falls, the maps went blank. Above the falls, if the stories were true — his mother's stories, the ones she'd told in fragments, the ones the girl's grandmother had sung — there was a valley. There were people. There might be a whole nation of stones who had chosen differently than his mother had, who had hidden so well they'd never had to stop being themselves.

If that valley existed, then Corvane's syndicate wanted it, and everything in Emeric that had spent a lifetime hiding understood, with sick clarity, exactly how a thing gets destroyed twice: once by its own government, and once by the people who come later with contracts and guns, calling it recovery.

He could take them up there. He could take their money, run the falls, drop them at the head of the gorge, and come back downstream to his shack and his small life and his once-a-year whisper, and no one would ever be the wiser. That was the stone's plan. That was the plan that had kept him alive.

Or.

In the stern, faintly, Nia was humming the phrase again in the dark, getting it wrong, getting it wrong, getting it less wrong. The mountain remembers what the river forgets.

Emeric closed his eyes and, for the first time in a year, said his mother's name — not on the anniversary, not alone at a window, but here, in his head, on the moving water, ahead of schedule, like a man breaking his own rules on purpose to see if the sky fell.

*Maren Veylova.*

The sky held. The river ran on. And two days upstream, at the toll bridge at Karst Landing, a man Emeric owed a very old debt was waiting with a signal lamp — though Emeric did not know it yet, and would not, until the choice he'd already made grew teeth.
