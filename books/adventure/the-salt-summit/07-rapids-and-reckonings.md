## 7. Rapids and Reckonings

The Kessari above Lowmark was a different animal than the one I'd been skimming at altitude for nine years. From the air it was a green ribbon stitched through brown country, lazy-looking, harmless. From the deck of Emeric Sable's flat-bottomed scow, it was a living thing with opinions, and most of its opinions were about killing us.

"Feet where I told you," Emeric said without turning around. He stood at the stern with one hand on the sweep oar, reading the water the way Tobias read watermarks — patiently, hungrily, like the river was a document that had been trying to lie to him. "Rosalind, your left foot. It's drifting."

I moved it. "You always this bossy?"

"Only when people who can't swim stand on my boat."

"I can swim."

"You can not-drown," he said. "Different skill. The river can tell the difference."

Nia laughed from the bow, where she'd appointed herself lookout, and Tobias sat amidships with the chest lashed between his knees and his arms wrapped around it like a man holding a sleeping animal he didn't entirely trust. Three days out from Lowmark and the scow was already starting to feel like a place rather than a vehicle. That should have worried me. Places had a way of collecting people, and I'd spent my whole adult life keeping my hangar swept clean of them.

I checked my watch, then the sky, out of habit. Fuel gauges, river bends — I'd always measured my life in those. But a scow didn't have a fuel gauge. It had current, and weather, and the four of us.

"Rapids after the next bend," Emeric said. "The Serrate. Half a mile of bad water. I've run it eleven times."

"And?" Tobias asked.

"Twice I didn't enjoy it."

* * *

The Serrate announced itself as a sound before it was a sight — a low, continuous grinding roar, like the river chewing stone. The banks narrowed. The water went from green to a bruised, muscular grey, and the whole surface began to tilt and shiver, as if the river had remembered it was falling.

"Listen to me," Emeric said, and his voice had changed — all the dry humor gone out of it, replaced by something flat and absolute. "When we hit the white water, nobody talks. Nobody moves unless I say. Tobias, the chest stays lashed to you and you stay lashed to the ring bolt. Nia—"

"I know. Low and center."

"Low and center. Rosalind, you're my second. When I point left, everyone shifts left. When I point right—"

"I've flown formation," I said. "I understand pointing."

Something that might have been a smile crossed his face and drowned. "It's not the rocks that kill you," he said. "It's the water that looks calm. The calm parts are where the holes are. Places the river sucks down and doesn't give back. So when it gets quiet — that's when you hold on hardest."

The bend came, and the river dropped out from under us.

I'd flown through storms that would peel the paint off a hangar. I'd landed on gravel bars in crosswinds that made the Kessari look like a puddle. None of it was like this. In the air, the violence was around you and you moved through it; here the violence was the road itself, and the road was trying to eat the vehicle. The scow slammed into the first standing wave and the world went white — spray like buckshot, cold enough to stop thought. The boat climbed, hung, and came down on its chine with a crack I felt in my teeth.

Emeric's sweep oar moved in small, vicious strokes. "Left," he said, and pointed, and we all shifted, and the scow slid off a rock I never saw — just heard it, a wet granite scrape along the hull that went on too long.

Then the quiet part.

The river smoothed out for maybe thirty yards, a stretch of grey-green glass between two chaos sections, and I understood what he'd meant about the calm parts, because the water here was moving faster, not slower, and it moved in circles — slow, deliberate, patient circles, like something stirring a pot.

"Hold," Emeric said quietly. "Hold. Hold—"

The scow's stern kicked sideways. The current had found the boat's hip and decided to keep it. We began to swing, broadside, toward a line of white ahead that had teeth in it, and Emeric hauled on the sweep with his whole body and the oar bent and the river didn't care.

"Sweep's not enough," he said, and there was no panic in his voice, which somehow made it worse. "Rosalind. Oar. Now."

There was a spare oar lashed along the gunwale beside me. I got the lashings off with fingers that didn't feel like mine and got the blade in the water, and the river took it like it takes everything — instantly, greedily. I braced my boots against the thwart and pulled, and the scow's swing slowed, hung, and then Nia was beside me, both hands on the same oar, small and fierce, and Tobias was hauling on his chest-lashings like he meant to pull the boat out by the chest alone, and the three of us pulled while Emeric steered, and the Serrate spat us out.

* * *

We came through the last of it into a long, slow pool below the rapids, water the color of bottle glass, and nobody spoke for a full minute. The roar fell away behind us. Mist hung in the pines on both banks. My arms were shaking, and I noticed I was still gripping the oar, so I made myself let go, finger by finger.

Nia was soaked to the skin and grinning like a lantern.

"Did you see—" she started.

"I saw," Emeric said. He'd gone back to facing forward, but his shoulders had come down from around his ears. "You pulled on the two count. Most first-timers pull on the three and we lose the boat."

"I counted your strokes," she said. "You pull on the two."

He was quiet for a second. "Yeah," he said. "I do."

Tobias made a sound that I initially took for a laugh and then realized was closer to a sob. He had the chest in his lap and his knuckles were white on the lashings, and when he looked up at me his glasses were fogged and spattered and his face underneath was grey.

"I held it," he said. "I want it noted, for the record, that I held it."

"Noted," I said.

"It's the archive of a murdered nation. I held the archive of a murdered nation while the river tried to — I'm going to be sick, actually, if nobody minds."

"Over the side," Emeric said, "and mind the current."

Tobias was sick over the side, and then he was sick again, and then Nia put a hand on his back, small and matter-of-fact, and he let her, and I watched a disgraced royal archivist — a man I'd known for four days and trusted for approximately none of them — shake and be steadied by a sixteen-year-old stowaway, and something in my chest did a thing I didn't have a name for and didn't want one.

We pulled into a gravel eddy below a bluff and made camp while the light went long and copper. Emeric built a fire with the economy of a man who'd done it a thousand times in a thousand places he'd never meant to stay. I got the tarp up. Nia stripped off her wet jacket and hung it by the fire and stood there steaming like a fresh loaf, and Tobias laid out the water-damaged pages from the chest on a spread of oilcloth, page by careful page, the way you'd lay out a patient.

"We nearly died," Tobias said, to no one in particular, "for paper."

"You nearly died for truth," Nia said.

"Paper is how truth travels," Tobias said. "It's how it survives. People burn, rivers drown them, years bury them — and the paper stays. That's the whole horror of it, and the whole hope." He smoothed a warped page flat with two fingers. "My grandmother used to say a lie can outrun a document, but it can't outlast one."

"Your grandmother spoke Veyli too?" Nia asked, and went very still the way she did whenever the language came up.

"A phrase here and there. She worked in the lower archives." Tobias's hands paused on the page. "She never talked about the end. Not once, in forty years. When I asked, she'd say, *the river keeps what the river keeps,* and change the subject."

Nia looked at the fire. "Mine said that too. The same words."

I felt that go through the whole camp like a current of its own. Emeric had stopped moving wood. He was looking at the flames with an expression I couldn't read, and I remembered — the way you remember a detail from a chart you studied years ago — that he'd said his mother came from somewhere downriver, and hadn't said where, and I'd been too busy not asking questions about my passenger to ask questions about my guide.

"The river keeps what the river keeps," Emeric said, finally, to the fire. "Huh."

* * *

We ate rice and smoked fish and the last of the Lowmark cheese, which Tobias guarded jealously. The stars came out hard and close over the canyon rim. The river talked to itself in the dark, quieter now, almost conversational.

Nia sat next to me at the fire, which was new. She'd spent the first two days keeping a careful arm's length from all of us, the way you do when you're not sure yet whether you've been adopted or merely detained. Tonight her shoulder was a hand's width from mine, and she was watching Tobias across the flames with her chin on her knees.

"He's sad all the way down," she said quietly. "Like a well."

"He lost his job, his name, and his country's whole history got called a lie. Sad seems proportionate."

"He's not sad about the job." She turned to look at me, firelight in her eyes. "You can see it. He's sad about the people. He does the reading and every page is somebody who died thinking nobody would ever know, and he knows them now, and nobody else does. That's a lot of people to be the only one who knows."

I didn't say anything for a while. I'm not built for this — the talk, the sitting-still, the letting people arrange themselves around you like furniture. My whole life I'd kept the hangar swept. One seat in the plane, one bunk in the quarters, debts to the airfield and nothing owed to anybody, because everything you let yourself need is something the world can take away, and I had already learned that lesson at an age when the learning breaks something.

But Nia was sixteen and had stowed away on a plane headed for a forbidden valley because her dead grandmother had told her to, and Tobias was the last man alive who could read the documents in his lap, and Emeric was steering us toward something he clearly dreaded for reasons he hadn't given, and I had hired on with all of it, and the plane — my plane, my one seat, my swept-clean life — was moored forty miles back with a cracked strut and a syndicate agent's business card tucked under its tie-down rope.

"Rosalind?" Nia said.

"You should sleep," I said. "Long day tomorrow. Emeric says the water after the Serrate gets worse before it gets better."

"You didn't answer me."

"I didn't hear a question."

"I said, do you ever get scared?"

I looked at the fire. "Every time I fly," I said. "That's the job. Scared and doing it anyway. The day you're not scared is the day you've stopped paying attention."

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. "That's not what I asked, though."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She smiled — a quick, sharp, knowing smile that made her look suddenly much older and much younger at the same time — and lay back on her bedroll, and within minutes she was asleep, breathing in the easy rhythm of someone who had decided she was safe, here, among strangers, on a gravel bar in the middle of nowhere.

I sat up long after the others turned in. Tobias slept with one hand on the chest. Emeric slept on his side facing the river, the way soldiers sleep. Nia slept like the dead, or like the living — like somebody with nothing yet to guard.

And I realized, sitting there in the cold with the river talking, that I'd been measuring my life in miles because miles don't ask anything of you. A mile doesn't need you to come back. But the scow behind me had four people in it, and tomorrow it had rapids, and I had caught myself — twice today, twice — checking on them before I checked the sky.

That was the crack. That was the water getting in.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and low. We struck camp and pushed off into a river that had changed overnight — higher, browner, faster, carrying debris from somewhere upstream. Emeric stood at the sweep and read it and I watched his jaw tighten.

"Flooding up-country," he said. "Rain in the high valley, maybe. Or somebody opened something."

"Is that bad?"

"It's bad for the map I had. The channels have moved." He glanced back at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen there before — not fear of the water. Fear of where the water came from. "There's a shortcut past the next gorge. Old survey channel. It'll save us a day."

"You said you've never run it."

"I said I've never run it," Emeric agreed, and put the sweep over, and turned the scow toward the gorge.

Tobias looked up from his pages. Nia looked at me. I looked at the guide with two names, who was taking us somewhere the charts didn't go, for reasons he hadn't given, into water that was rising.

"Hold on to something," I said. "All of you. Hold on to something."
