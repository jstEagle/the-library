## 8. The Burned Ledger

The fire was Emeric's idea. Everything practical seemed to be.

Tobias had spent twenty years in the Royal Archive of Veyl arguing about humidity control with men who had never once been cold, and it had not occurred to him — not once in all those years of scholarship — that the oldest preservation technology in the world was simply heat and patience. But here they were, on a gravel shelf above a bend of the Kessari that Emeric called the Long Eddy, with a fire built between two flat stones and a line of fishing wire strung between two alder saplings, and along that wire, clipped with split twigs, hung forty-one sheets of waterlogged paper turning slowly in the firelight like strange pale laundry.

Forty-one sheets out of perhaps three hundred. The rest had dissolved into pulp in the chest, or fused into bricks no blade could part without destroying them. Tobias had spent the afternoon separating what he could with a bone folder and river water warmed in Rosalind's dented kettle, sheet by sheet, breath held, hands trembling in a way he resented because trembling was for amateurs and grief, and he had promised himself he would be neither.

"You're going to burn them," Nia said.

She was crouched at the edge of the firelight, knees up, chin on her knees, watching him with the flat unblinking attention of a heron. She had barely spoken since the rapids. Since she'd gone into the water after his satchel without hesitation and come up gasping with it clutched to her chest, and then handed it back as if she hadn't just done something extraordinary, and then gone quiet in the way people go quiet when they've remembered something they were trying not to carry.

"I'm going to *dry* them," Tobias said. "There's a difference."

"They're already wet."

"Wet is a condition. Burned is a verdict." He adjusted a page on the wire — a fragment of a shipping manifest, its ink ghosted but legible if you knew how to look, which he did, which was the one thing in his life he had never doubted he could do. "Heat drives the water out before the fibers collapse. Cold lets ice crystals shred them from inside. If we'd found this chest in winter, there would be nothing left but a memory of paper."

"And if the fire spits?"

"Then I lose a page and I hate myself efficiently." He glanced at her. "You can sleep, you know. We'll take watches."

"I'm not tired."

She was. She had the specific gray look of someone running on fear and stubbornness, and Tobias recognized it because he had worn that face for eleven years, since Lowmark, since the trial, since the day the new government of the successor states had stood him in front of a commission and asked him to explain why the royal archive's famine records were incomplete, and he had said — honestly, stupidly, truthfully — *because I removed them*, and nothing he said afterward had mattered at all.

He turned back to the fire. Above the flames, the pages turned. Somewhere downriver, an owl asked its one question over and over.

* * *

Rosalind came back from checking the plane's mooring lines just as full dark settled. She moved through the camp the way she seemed to move through everything — economically, quietly, like someone who had long ago stopped expecting the world to make room for her and had learned instead to fit herself into whatever space remained. She crouched by the fire, held her hands out, and looked up at the hanging pages.

"How many are readable?" she asked.

"Perhaps sixty percent of what survived. Enough." He hesitated. "More than enough."

"Enough for what?"

"For proof." He heard how the word came out of him — hungry, almost violent — and made himself soften it. "For evidence. For the thing I've spent eleven years being told doesn't exist."

Rosalind studied him for a moment. Firelight did something complicated to her face; it took the wariness out of it and left something younger, or maybe just less defended. "Emeric says you were the royal archivist," she said. "Before."

"*Deputy* royal archivist. The title matters to me more than it should." He fed a stick to the fire. "I was thirty-four years old and I had keys to rooms most people didn't know existed. And then the flood came, and the nation drowned, and the successor states needed someone to blame besides themselves, and the archive — my archive — was inconvenient. So they convened a commission. And I told them the truth, which is the single most career-limiting thing a man can do."

"What truth?"

"That the records had been altered before the flood. That the grain ledgers I was trained to preserve had been falsified at the source — not copied wrong, not damaged, *falsified*, deliberately, by hands that knew exactly what they were doing. I proved it. Watermarks, ink chemistry, the sequence of quire signatures. I had the technical evidence and no political case, because the men who signed the falsifications were the same men who appointed the commission." He smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "They took my post, my pension, and my name off the rolls of the profession. They let me keep my hands, presumably so I could continue to work, since working people ask fewer questions."

Nia had shifted closer without seeming to move. "You said the ledger was sold," she said. "In the boat. You said somebody sold the grain."

Tobias looked at her. "You were listening."

"I always listen."

"Then you know more than the commission did." He rose, joints announcing themselves, and went to the wire. "Come here. Both of you. If we're going to do this, we do it now, while the pages are still soft enough to argue with."

* * *

The chest itself sat open near the fire, bronze-bound, its lid stamped with the seal of Veyl: the triple wave beneath the mountain crown, worn nearly smooth. It had been sealed with pitch and wax, someone's desperate attempt to make it watertight, and it had almost worked. Almost was the word that governed everything about Veyl now.

Tobias laid the driest pages on the flat stone beside the fire and weighted their corners with four small pebbles, and began, in the manner he had been trained in and had never stopped practicing even in exile, even in taverns, even in the years when he'd told himself he was done — he began to read.

"The first documents are routine," he said. "Harvest assessments, quota distributions, granary inventories. Standard administrative paper. This one—" he touched a fragment, "—is a requisition order for the eastern districts, dated fourteen months before the flood. Note the quantity."

Rosalind leaned in. "That can't be right. That's half the previous year's allocation."

"It is precisely half. Now watch what happens next." He drew out another page, this one darker, its edges charred where the fire had spat earlier despite his vigilance. "This is the counter-entry. Same date, same office, same clerk's hand. The eastern quota, reassigned. Not reduced — *reassigned*. To a private buyer."

"Who buys half a nation's grain?" Rosalind asked.

"A consortium," Tobias said. "Registered in Lowmark, which is why no one in Lowmark has ever wanted this document found. Three names on the charter. Two you've never heard of. The third—" he paused, because saying it aloud made it real in a way the reading had not, "—the third is Meric Halloway. Deputy Minister of Provisioning. The man who stood on the capital steps six weeks before the flood and told the crowds the river was failing them. Told them the Kessari had turned against Veyl. Told them famine was the river's doing, an act of the water, and that the government would negotiate with the flood as it had negotiated with every flood before."

Nia had gone very still. "My grandmother sang about that," she said. Her voice had changed — smaller, careful. "There's a verse. *The minister speaks and the river takes the blame.* I thought it was — I thought it was just a song. The way songs are."

"Songs are where nations hide what they can't say in documents," Tobias said. "Your grandmother preserved more history than my entire archive did. She just had better methods." He turned the darkened page toward the light. "Here. The transfer payments. Halloway's office received them through a shell house in the port district. And here—" his finger stopped, and he felt the old archivist's sickness rise in him, the terrible joy of confirmation arriving hand in hand with horror, "—here is the requisition for the relief shipments. Dated nine months before the flood. Approved. Funded. *Cancelled.*"

"Cancelled?" Rosalind said. "The relief was cancelled?"

"Redirected. Look at the annotation in the margin." He read it aloud, translating the cramped official shorthand as he went, his voice flattening into the neutral tone he used for cataloguing because the alternative was not neutral at all. "*Divert to southern terminals per Halloway instruction. Public position unchanged: shortage attributable to river conditions. Maintain narrative.*" He set the page down. His hand was not quite steady. "*Maintain narrative.* Four words. Nine hundred thousand people, and four words in a margin."

The fire cracked. Above them the remaining pages turned on their wire, patient, indifferent, bearing their cargo of dead ink.

"So they starved them," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone quiet and hard, like river stone. "They sold the grain, cancelled the relief, and blamed the river. And then the flood came and drowned the evidence, and everyone who might have testified."

"And the survivors scattered," Tobias said. "And the successor states wrote the official history, which is that Veyl fell to natural catastrophe compounded by poor governance — poor governance, note, attributed to incompetence rather than theft, because incompetence embarrasses no one important. And the descendants of the survivors grew up singing songs about ministers and rivers, and no one with standing ever listened." He looked at Nia. "Until now."

"Why now?" Nia asked. "Why does it matter? Everyone's dead. The ones who did it are dead too, probably. Old men in the ground."

"Because it isn't only about the dead." Tobias gathered the pages carefully, squaring their edges, an act of tidiness that was really an act of self-control. "Because Corvane's syndicate isn't dredging the Kessari for sentiment. They filed recovery permits under heritage law — I've seen the paperwork, it's how I tracked them to you, Rosalind — and heritage law grants salvage rights to anything provably connected to the Veyl state. Including, if it exists, the state's financial records. Including this ledger. Do you understand? They don't want to destroy the truth. They want to *own* it. A truth in private hands can be buried, edited, leaked selectively, sold. Halloway's heirs have money in Lowmark. The consortium's charter never expired. If this ledger surfaces through the syndicate, it will surface in fragments, deniable, priced. Or it will surface and be authenticated by experts they employ, and the authentication will be disputed forever, and the dispute will bury the fact as thoroughly as the river did."

"And if it comes from us?" Rosalind asked.

"Then it comes with provenance they can't buy. An archivist on the record — a disgraced one, which is perversely useful, because I have nothing left to protect. A pilot with witnesses. A girl who carries the language." He met Nia's eyes. "And possibly, at the end of the river, living descendants who can stand up and say *we are the ones they starved, and we are not dead.* You cannot forge that. You cannot buy it. It is the one authentication no syndicate can manufacture."

Nia pulled her jacket tighter around herself. "You want to use my grandmother's people as proof."

"I want to give them the chance to speak for themselves," Tobias said. "Which is different. If it isn't, I've learned nothing in eleven years, and I'd rather you tell me so now."

She looked at him for a long moment, heron-still, and then nodded once, a small precise motion, and turned away to check the drying pages on the far end of the wire, and he understood that he had passed some examination whose questions he hadn't known were being asked.

* * *

Later — the fire burned low, Rosalind on first watch somewhere out past the alders, Emeric asleep with his boots on, which Tobias had noticed and decided not to interpret — Tobias sat alone with the ledger pages spread across his knees and did the thing he had been avoiding all evening.

He read the final entry.

It was dated nineteen days before the flood. A summary memorandum, internal, marked for the Minister's eyes only — though how it had come to be sealed in a chest bound for the river country, Tobias could not yet say. There was a story in that too. There was always a story. Someone in the ministry had grown a conscience late, or wanted insurance, or both, and had packed the state's sins into bronze and pitch and sent them downstream ahead of the water, and the river had kept them for a generation until a bush pilot with debts and no attachments happened to land on a gravel bar after a storm.

The memorandum was short. It confirmed the transfers. It confirmed the cancellations. And in its closing paragraph, in a hand Tobias recognized as Halloway's own, it contained a sentence he would carry for the rest of his life:

*"When the waters come, the record will drown with the rest. Let the river hold what we cannot."*

They had known. They had known the flood was coming — not as prophecy, not as divine judgment, but as engineering, as schedule, as the predictable consequence of neglected levees and emptied coffers — and they had balanced the books accordingly. The famine thinned the population the granaries could no longer feed. The flood closed the accounts. The river took the blame, and the river also, in its slow patient way, took the evidence and hid it, and waited.

Tobias sat by the dying fire with the page in his hands and found, to his surprise, that he was not crying. He had expected to cry. Instead there was something else, something with a temperature to it, a cold bright weight behind his sternum that felt less like grief than like fuel.

Eleven years ago he had stood in front of a commission with fragments and inference, and they had dismantled him with a shrug, because fragments prove nothing and inference convinces no committee. But this — this was whole. Quire-signed, watermark-matched, internally consistent, corroborated by a child's song and a dead woman's memory. This was not a claim. This was a case.

He fed the last of the wood to the fire and watched the flames take it, and above him the remaining pages turned in the rising heat, and he thought: *dry fast, little ghosts. We have a river yet to run.*

From the darkness beyond the firelight, where Rosalind kept her watch, came a sound — not a word, not quite. A low whistle, two notes, sharp and deliberate. Then her voice, pitched low and carrying:

"Tobias. Wake Emeric. There's a lantern on the water."

Tobias was on his feet before the sentence ended, the ledger pages swept against his chest, the cold bright weight in him suddenly burning.
