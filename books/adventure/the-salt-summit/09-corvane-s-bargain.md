## 9. Corvane's Bargain

The gunboat *Meridian's Mercy* had been named by a committee, which was why it sounded like an insurance company and looked like a knife.

Corvane stood at the rail while the Kessari unspooled beneath him, brown-green and patient, carrying silt from a hundred drowned valleys toward a sea that didn't care what it swallowed. He liked rivers. Rivers were honest in the way people weren't. They went where the gradient told them to go, and they never pretended otherwise. A river would take your barge, your cargo, your corpse, all at the same flat rate, no haggling.

Behind him, on the aft deck, two of his men were playing cards and losing badly to each other, which was its own kind of discipline problem. Ahead, the basin opened into fog banks and dead channels where charts gave up and admitted they were guessing.

He checked his watch. Not because he needed to. Because checking his watch made other people check theirs, and a man who checked his watch was a man with somewhere to be, and a man with somewhere to be was a man whose time you were wasting if you disappointed him.

It worked on everyone eventually. It had even worked on him, once, back when he'd been the sort of junior clerk who believed institutions protected people. Twenty-two years in the Ministry of Recovery had cured him of that. The Ministry recovered things. That was the whole word. *Recovery.* Nobody ever asked recovery from what, or for whom. Corvane had asked, early, once, and been moved to a posting so far upriver that the mail came monthly and the mosquitoes filed formal complaints about the competition. He'd stopped asking. He'd started noticing that the men who did the asking never rose, and the men who did the pricing always did.

Pricing. That was the entire secret, and it fit on a coin. Everything had one. Grain had one. Silence had one. A drowned nation's archive had one, and the number currently written next to it was whatever it cost to keep four people from reaching a valley that three governments had agreed, on paper, did not exist.

"Sir." Vess, his second, materialized the way she always did — already there before he heard her. "Signal from Lowmark. The Wrenn sighting's confirmed. He flew out yesterday morning with the pilot woman. Oyelaran."

"And our friend at the airfield?"

"Bought and paid. Fuel ledgers, flight plan, the lot. They're running the river corridor. Two stops possible before the gorge — Callow's Landing and the fuel barge at Serren's Reach."

Corvane nodded slowly, watching the water. "And the chest?"

"Photographed at the airfield. Bronze-bound, official seal intact. The appraiser says—" She paused, and he could hear her deciding how much to soften it, which he appreciated. Vess softened nothing else; it was considerate of her to ration it. "It's real. First verified Veyli state archive recovered since the collapse. If the contents are what we think—"

"They're what we think." He turned from the rail. "They're exactly what we think, which is why a bush pilot is flying it upriver instead of selling it to a museum, and why a disgraced archivist is riding shotgun instead of hiding in some port town's drinking budget. People don't risk their lives for paper, Vess. They risk them for what the paper proves."

* * *

In the stern cabin, he laid out the map he allowed almost no one to see.

Not the public chart — the one with the source region left blank under sixty years of survey exemptions and bureaucratic fog. The real one. The one the syndicate had assembled over eleven years from missionary letters, banned folk songs transcribed by a linguist who'd died conveniently poor, a smuggler's deathbed confession, and — this was the piece Corvane was proudest of, the piece that had gotten him promoted out of the mosquito posting — a hydrological anomaly. Every tributary in the upper basin flowed from somewhere. Water is honest. Follow enough water uphill and the blank space on the map starts to feel less like ignorance and more like intent.

The valley was there. Roughly forty square kilometers of arable land at altitude, spring-fed, defensible, warm microclimate in a cold basin — everything the lower provinces spent fortunes pretending they could engineer. And sitting on top of it, according to every scrap of evidence, several hundred descendants of a nation that had officially died fifty-eight years ago.

Officially. That was the beautiful part. That was the load-bearing word.

Corvane poured himself the day's single measure of the good whisky and permitted himself the small pleasure of admiring the plan, the way an architect might admire a bridge he'd built over a gorge full of other people's bones.

Phase one: recovery archaeology. A licensed expedition, permits stamped by a ministry that owed the syndicate more than it owed its own pensioners. Respectable scholars — two of whom were already on retainer and delighted about it. Press releases about heritage, memory, giving the dead their dignity back. The public loved dignity for the dead. The dead were so much easier to dignify than the living.

Phase two: site control. Standard practice. Any excavation required secured perimeters, restricted access, logistics corridors. Gunboats became *security vessels*. Fences became *conservation barriers*. Within eighteen months the valley would be a worksite, and within three years a resource concession, and the several hundred living descendants of Veyl would be classified — this was the paperwork genius, the clause he'd drafted personally — as *unverified persons of uncertain provenance*, which was a legal category meaning they could be relocated, compensated, or processed without ever technically having existed.

No massacre. No famine. No soldiers shooting anyone. Just forms, fees, fences, and time. The same instruments that had killed Veyl the first time, applied more gently, because the second time you didn't need to kill anyone. You just needed them to stop being anywhere the ledger could find them.

His mentor, old Hask, had taught him that. *The first sin takes blood,* Hask used to say, sharpening a pencil down to nothing. *Every sin after that takes only signatures.* Hask had died rich and mourned by everyone who mattered, which was to say everyone who counted money.

Corvane drank. The whisky was excellent. He made sure it was always excellent; a man should enjoy the taste of his own decisions.

* * *

Which brought him to Tobias Wrenn, and the one flaw in the architecture.

The plan survived scrutiny only if the archive stayed illegitimate. An unsigned, unauthenticated trove of waterlogged papers was salvage — interesting, deniable, worth arguing about in journals for a decade. But authenticated? Verified against royal seals, cross-referenced with surviving census fragments, formally attributed by a credentialed archivist with a name the learned world still remembered despite everything?

Then it wasn't salvage. Then it was history. And history had a way of refusing to stay priced.

Tobias Wrenn was the last archivist alive who could authenticate Veyli state documents beyond argument. Corvane had read the man's file until he could recite it. Expelled from the Royal Archive eleven years ago — not for incompetence, which would have been useful, but for the opposite. For finding things. For publishing a reconstruction of the grain quota schedules that had embarrassed three ministries and gotten his patron ruined. Disgrace, in Wrenn's case, was just accuracy with bad timing.

That was why Wrenn had to come back alive. Not captured — *alive*, cooperative, and on the record. Because here was the final elegance, the detail Corvane had built the whole structure around:

If the syndicate simply destroyed the archive, the questions would never stop. Rumors would grow teeth. Somewhere, someday, another chest, another copy, another song. Drowned nations leaked. But if the syndicate *published* the archive — through Wrenn, authenticated by Wrenn, released under syndicate stewardship with a curated edition — then the syndicate controlled the truth itself. They would decide which pages were legible. Which entries were "too waterlogged to recover." Which names appeared in the index and which dissolved into footnote ambiguity. The famine would be confirmed, mourned, memorialized — and blamed, in the definitive scholarly record, on the river. On weather. On tragedy.

And the syndicate would be the heroic institution that recovered Veyl's memory. Funding museums. Naming exhibits. Holding the only keys.

You don't fight a story by burning it. You fight it by owning the printing press.

For that, he needed the archivist breathing, reasonably comfortable, and eventually reasonable. Wrenn's pride was a lever. His disgrace was a deeper one — a man who'd been cast out by the establishment would, Corvane judged, accept restoration from almost anyone who offered it sincerely enough. Everyone wanted to be right in the end. It was the most reliable hunger there was. Cheaper than gold, more predictable than fear.

The pilot was a different calculation. Oyelaran had no institution to leverage, no disgrace to soothe, no price visible from the air. Debts at the airfield — those were levers, but crude ones, and crude levers snapped. People like her, the file said. Self-contained. Competent. No family listed, no attachments flagged, which meant either genuinely alone or lying about it, and both types were dangerous in opposite directions.

He'd meet her soon enough. He found he was looking forward to it. You learned more from ten minutes across a table than from a year of files, and besides, he enjoyed the moment when capable people realized the situation was larger than their competence. Not cruelty. Calibration.

* * *

At dusk they put in at Serren's Reach, where the fuel barge sat chained to the bank like something that had given up swimming. Corvane went ashore with Vess and two men who knew how to stand in doorways.

The barge operator was a wet-browed man named Dulac who took the syndicate's retainer and hated taking it, which was the ideal arrangement — hatred kept a man careful, gratitude made him sloppy and familiar.

"They fueled there," Dulac said, wiping his hands that weren't dirty. "Yesterday evening. The pilot paid cash, which nobody does, and the old man bought tea and stood reading the notice board like it owed him money. There's a girl too. Young. Kept to the boat."

"A girl." Corvane let the word sit. "Describe her."

Dulac described her badly — dark hair, river clothes, young — and then, when Corvane simply waited, better: quick eyes, watched the gangplank the whole time like she was counting exits, and she'd asked him, casual as anything, how far the gorge road went and whether the toll bridge at Karst Crossing still took passengers.

Karst Crossing. Corvane felt the small cold pleasure of a pattern completing. The toll bridge was the last chokepoint before the falls country, and the syndicate owned the tollkeeper the way it owned the fuel barge, the airfield clerk, the harbor master at Lowmark, and a growing fraction of the map. Everything upstream funneled through Karst. Rivers, roads, plans. Even honesty funneled through Karst, if you waited long enough.

"They'll take the bridge," he said to Vess as they walked back down to the boat. "There's no other crossing for eighty miles, and the pilot will want the plane ferried or repaired wherever the damage is worst. Put word ahead. I want eyes on the bridge, but no move yet. Let them commit to the crossing. A trap works best after the prey has paid the toll — paid tolls make people feel legitimate, and legitimate people hesitate to run."

"And Wrenn?"

"When we take them — and we will take them, Vess, quietly, at a time of our choosing — Wrenn comes to me unharmed and unbruised. He's a guest, not a prisoner. Good food, good light, access to paper. A scholar surrenders to a well-stocked library faster than to any army." He paused at the rail, looking upriver, where the fog was thickening around the dark shoulders of the gorge country. "The others are negotiable. The pilot especially. Offer her debt clearance first. Cash second. If neither moves her, then she becomes a line item, and line items get handled."

Vess nodded and went to send the signal, and Corvane remained alone at the rail with his watch open in his palm, though he wasn't reading the time.

He was thinking about the girl.

A stowaway, probably. Grandmother from the old country, the type who sang songs to children about a valley that answered. He'd read the ethnographic reports; he knew the shape of it. Sentimental nonsense, mostly — but nonsense with a function, because songs were maps that couldn't be confiscated, and grandmothers were archives that couldn't be bought.

Someone should have collected those songs properly, years ago. It was the kind of oversight that turned into complications.

He closed the watch with a click that carried further than it should have over the water, and far upstream, past the toll bridge, past the falls, in a valley that did not exist, someone was almost certainly singing one now.

"Full steam at first light," he said. "We're done being patient."

* * *

That night, in the cabin, Corvane wrote two letters.

The first was to the ministry contact who handled survey exemptions, confirming the bribed surveyor's route through the falls country — a channel marked on no chart, sold to the syndicate for a sum the surveyor considered life-changing and Corvane considered rounding error. Gunboats couldn't run the falls. But gunboats could run *that*, if the pilot's party believed the falls impassable and stopped watching the water behind them. People always watched the direction they were traveling. It was a universal weakness, and Corvane had built a career on the fact that nobody ever checked behind them until it was educational.

The second letter was shorter, and he wrote it slowly, because it was the important one.

It was addressed to Tobias Wrenn, though he had no address for Wrenn and no intention of mailing it. It began: *Dear Colleague.* And it laid out, in courteous, reasonable, devastating prose, an offer of full professional restoration — directorship of the new Veyl Memorial Archive, complete access, complete funding, his name above the door — in exchange for authentication of the recovered materials, to be conducted under syndicate stewardship.

He knew Wrenn would refuse it, at first. They always refused at first; refusal was how idealists proved to themselves they still had any. But the letter would be waiting. Offers aged well. Pride soured fast.

He signed it, blotted it, and set it aside, and lay in his bunk listening to the river push against the hull — patient, honest, going where the gradient told it, asking no one's permission — and slept the sleep of a man whose accounts were all, as far as he knew, in order.

Upstream, in the dark, the Kessari kept carrying everything toward the sea: silt, secrets, and the small stubborn boats of people who had not yet learned what they were worth.
