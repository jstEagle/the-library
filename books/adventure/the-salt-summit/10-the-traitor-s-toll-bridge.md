## 10. The Traitor's Toll Bridge

The toll bridge announced itself an hour before they reached it, the way bad news does — a smell first, then a sound, then the thing itself standing in the river like a sentence you can't take back.

Emeric smelled woodsmoke and tar. Then he heard the chain: a great rusted links-and-logs affair strung across the Kessari where it narrowed between two bluffs, with a floating boom of lashed timber riding the current, and behind it the bridge itself — planked, stilt-legged, sagging in the middle like an old man's shoulders. A toll crossing. One of maybe six left on the whole upper river, run by whoever was strong enough that season to hold it.

He'd known it was coming. He'd had three days to know it was coming, three days of poling the flat-bottomed boat through braided shallows while Rosalind read the current from the bow and Nia asked questions and Tobias sat among his drying papers like a man tending a sick child. Three days to decide. He still hadn't decided, which told him he already had.

"You're quiet," Rosalind said. She said it without turning around. She had a way of talking to the river and letting him overhear.

"I'm reading water."

"Read out loud, then."

"Shallows on the left go to gravel in fifty meters. Right channel's deep but it bends toward the boom." He planted the pole and shoved, feeling the river's weight through his arms, through his back, through the soles of his boots. "There'll be men on the bridge. There are always men on the bridge."

"How many is always?"

"Enough."

She glanced back at him then, and her eyes did the thing they did — quick, weighing, gone again before he could decide what she'd concluded. "We pay and pass," she said. "Nobody's clever at a toll bridge. Clever gets people shot."

*Somebody has to be clever,* Emeric thought, and didn't say it, because saying it would have been the first step down a road he'd been walking toward all his life without ever admitting the road existed.

Nia sat amidships with Tobias's satchel in her lap, guarding it the way she'd guarded it since the rapids, since the moment she'd gone half into the white water for it and come up gasping with the strap in her fist and a look on her face Emeric recognized from mirrors he'd stopped owning years ago. The look of someone who had decided what mattered and would not be talked out of it. She was watching the bridge now too, chin up, sixteen years old and certain the world could be argued with.

He liked her. That was the problem. He'd spent four days not liking any of them, keeping them at the distance a pole keeps a boat off rocks, and somewhere in the spray of the rapids the distance had closed without his permission. Rosalind with her fuel-gauge silences. Tobias with his ruined hands and his grief that had teeth. Nia singing under her breath in a language Emeric hadn't heard spoken aloud since he was seven years old, since his mother's kitchen, since the last time anyone had said *good morning* in Veyli and meant it as an ordinary thing and not a secret.

His mother had fled Veyl's collapse pregnant, alone, down exactly this kind of river, through exactly this kind of country, carrying nothing but a name she would spend the rest of her life burying. She'd buried it so deep that when Emeric dug for it as a boy she'd given him another one instead. *Sable,* she'd said. *We are Sables now. Sable is a color. Nobody asks a color where it came from.*

And downstream, past this bridge, past the falls, two days' walk from the water, lay the village where she'd finally stopped running. Where she was buried under a stone that bore only the false name, because even dead, even in the ground, she hadn't been safe. Because the syndicate bought graveyards the way they bought everything else — cheap, and for what they could dig out of them.

Corvane's men had come through the village in the spring. Emeric knew because the letters came twice a year, unsigned, left in the hollow post at the ferry landing where his mother used to leave bread for travelers. *We know who your mother was. We know what your name was. The village stays healthy as long as the river stays quiet.* That was all. That was everything. Ten words of threat wearing the polite coat of ten words of reassurance, and every letter signed with nothing, which was worse than a name, because a name you could find.

So when Corvane's agent had found them at the fuel stop — all courtesy, all silver-tongued menace, offering to buy a drowned nation's chest off a bush pilot who'd refused with more spine than sense — Emeric had understood immediately how it would end. Men like Corvane did not accept refusal. They collected it. And they would collect it downstream, at a village with one street and forty souls and his mother's bones in its churchyard clay.

Unless someone gave them something better to collect.

* * *

The boom came up slow, hauled by rope and grudging muscle, and the current grabbed the boat and carried it into the toll pool beneath the bridge like a hand closing.

Emeric counted the men. Seven visible. Two on the near stilt platform with crossbows held casually wrong, the way amateurs hold weapons they've never fired at anything that shot back. Three on the bridge itself. Two more in a skiff tied in the lee of the far piling, and the skiff had a canvas over its middle that was the right shape for a swivel gun. His stomach did the arithmetic without being asked: seven men, one gun, and a toll keeper sitting in a chair dragged out onto the planks, fat and bored, with a ledger open on his knees.

"Toll!" the man bellowed, in the tone of someone who enjoyed the word more than the money. "Toll for passage! Boat and cargo, four marks. Boat, cargo, and passengers, eight!"

Rosalind stood, unhurried, and Emeric watched her put on the face she wore for airfield creditors — pleasant, immovable, faintly amused, the face of a woman who had calculated exactly how much trouble she could afford and found the number higher than you'd guessed. "Eight marks," she called up. "And we'll buy rope and lamp oil if you have it."

"We have it. At traveler's prices."

"At any prices," Tobias murmured, "when you're the only shop on the river."

They drifted in against the pilings. Ropes went up. Hands took the bow line, and Emeric let them take it, let the boat become theirs for the length of the transaction, because a boat being handled by strangers was a boat whose crew looked harmless. He climbed the ladder last, positioning himself below the others, below the sightlines, and no one thought anything of it because he was the hired guide, the river man, the help.

That was the other thing about camouflage. It worked both ways.

While Rosalind counted out marks and argued the oil price down by a third, Emeric moved along the platform to the toll keeper's ledger table, ostensibly to look at the posted chart of hazards downstream — every crossing kept one, inked and out of date, and guides were expected to study it. He studied it. He also noted, the way he noted everything, the fresh courier satchel hanging off the toll keeper's chair, wax-sealed, syndicate gray, waiting for the next boat heading downriver to carry it to the next station.

A relay. Of course it was a relay. Corvane ran the whole Kessari like a nervous system; every crossing was a ganglion, every toll keeper a nerve ending. Which meant the bridge wasn't just an obstacle.

It was a mouth. And mouths could be fed.

Emeric looked downstream. The river ran wide and brown-green there, sliding away between bluffs toward the falls, toward the portage, toward the source none of their charts showed and one song did. And further down, off the river entirely, a day's walk east through pine country: one street, forty souls, a churchyard, a stone with a lie carved on it.

He thought about his mother teaching him to gut fish, her hands over his hands, humming in Veyli because she thought he was too young to remember the words. He thought about the letters. *As long as the river stays quiet.*

He thought about Nia, asleep by the fire two nights ago, murmuring her grandmother's verses into her dreamsong, and how he'd lain awake listening and felt something crack open in his chest that he'd sealed shut at seven years old.

Then he turned to the toll keeper and smiled the smile his second name had taught him — easy, local, unremarkable — and said, "You'll want to send word downriver. You've got high-value traffic here and don't know it."

The toll keeper looked up from his ledger. He had the face of a man who'd heard every kind of talk a river could float to him and believed none of it on credit. "Have I."

"The pilot woman. Rosalind Oyelaran. She's carrying salvage out of the old Veyl grounds — bronze-bound chest, official seal, papers inside worth more than everything this crossing will take in ten years." Emeric kept his voice level, bored, the voice of a man selling gossip, which was the oldest trade on the river. "There's a syndicate agent named Corvane looking for her. Your gray satchel goes downriver anyway. Put a note in it. Costs you nothing, and the syndicate pays couriers' bonuses for confirmed sightings. Everyone I know would do it."

It was true. Everyone he knew would do it. That was the filthiest part — that betrayal on the Kessari wasn't even remarkable. It was commerce. It was weather.

The toll keeper's eyes had changed, though. Sharper now, weighing Emeric the way Rosalind weighed weather. "How is it you know what's in the lady's hold?"

"Because I'm the guide who helped load it, and because I can count, and because a chest like that means gunboats coming up this stretch inside a week, and gunboats don't pay tolls, they take them." He leaned on the table, confiding, one professional to another. "I'm telling you because when they come, I'd rather the men on this bridge knew I warned them than wondered why I didn't. You follow me."

The toll keeper followed him. Toll keepers always followed survival. He pulled the satchel over, took out a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper, and wrote while Emeric dictated: *Oyelaran party, upstream-bound, guide Emeric Sable, chest confirmed aboard, making for the falls portage.* He hesitated over the last part and added it anyway, because Corvane would learn it within days regardless, and a report that withheld the obvious was a report that got its author questioned. Better his name arrived attached to useful truth than to convenient silence.

He signed nothing. But the toll keeper looked at him once, long, and Emeric saw the man file his face away in whatever ledger toll keepers kept behind their eyes, and understood that he had just made himself a fixed point on somebody's map. Camouflage worked both ways. So did exposure.

"There," the toll keeper said, sealing the satchel. "Done charitable."

"Done business," Emeric said. "Same thing, up here."

He walked back along the platform with the hazard chart's layout memorized and his heart going like a drum in a parade, and he made himself look at each of them in turn — Rosalind counting change, Tobias rolling his maps, Nia crouched at the rail watching the water with her grandmother's language folded up inside her like a letter never sent — and he made himself feel nothing, because feeling was the one luxury the plan couldn't afford.

Except that it was already too late for that, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like swallowed glass.

* * *

They cast off on the falling afternoon, through the opened boom, and the bridge fell away behind them plank by plank, and Emeric poled them into the downstream current with the taste of what he'd done coating the back of his throat like river mud.

He'd done it cleanly. That was the horror of it — how cleanly. No trembling hands, no sweat. Thirty years of practice at being nobody had made him excellent at exactly this. His mother had taught him to survive by disappearing, and he had disappeared so thoroughly that betraying his own company took less effort than buying lamp oil.

*It buys time,* he told the part of himself that was already drafting the confession. *A sighting report isn't gunboats. Corvane still has to come upriver, still has to run the falls, still has to guess the route. Days. Maybe a week. A week is enough to reach the valley, deliver them, and be gone before—*

Before what? Before the village? The letters didn't promise the village safety forever. They promised it safety *as long as the river stayed quiet*, and the river had stopped being quiet the day Rosalind hauled a bronze-bound chest out of a gravel bar. His information didn't save the village. It bought a receipt. It bought the appearance of his cooperation, which was the only currency the syndicate honored, and receipts were good until they weren't, and when they weren't, forty people on one street paid the difference.

Unless the valley changed things. Unless the archive went out, the truth went out, the whole rotten ledger of Veyl's government went out over the radio waves to every ear in the basin — and then names would matter, and graves would matter, and a stone with a lie carved on it could be recut. He clung to that. It was thin ice and he knew it, but men had crossed worse rivers on thinner.

"Emeric."

Nia's voice. Close. He hadn't heard her come aft.

He turned, pole planted, smile ready — the Sable smile, the color smile, the one that asked nothing and offered less.

She was standing by the stern cargo net with Tobias's satchel hugged to her chest, and her face was doing something complicated, something young and old at once, and for one merciful second he thought she'd only come to ask about the rapids ahead.

"I went back for the oil tin," she said. "Left it on the platform. Rosalind sent me." Her eyes were steady on his. "The toll keeper was writing when I came around the pilings. I heard you dictating."

The river made its noise. The bluffs made theirs. Somewhere above them a bird screamed once and quit.

"My name," Nia said. "You gave them my name. And the chest, and the route, and—" Her voice caught, steadied, came back harder, which was the worst thing it could have done, because hard was how she sounded when she'd decided something and would not be argued with. "Why?"

Every answer he owned was a lie wearing a different coat, and he stood there in front of a sixteen-year-old holding a dead woman's songs, and found that not one of the lies would come out of his mouth. Not in Veyli. Not in any tongue.

"Nia—" he started.

"Don't." She stepped back, satchel tight, and the look on her face was the one he'd seen in the rapids, transposed, terrible: the look of someone deciding what matters. What mattered, apparently, was that she had sung her grandmother's verses in front of him. Had trusted him with the map that lived in her blood. "Was any of it real? The rapids? Any of it?"

From the bow, Rosalind's voice floated back, easy and unknowing: "What's the holdup back there? Nia, tell him we make the narrows before dark or we beach wet."

Nia didn't turn around. Neither did Emeric. Between them the river ran on, brown-green and patient, carrying its secrets the way it always had — down past the bridge, down past the falls, down to the sea, telling no one anything, keeping what it kept.

"He'll come for us," Nia whispered. "Whoever you told. They'll come, and it'll be my fault for singing, and—" She stopped. Looked at him the way you look at a bridge you have to cross knowing it might not hold. "Who *are* you?"

And Emeric Sable — born to another name, son of a woman buried under a lie, traitor for love, guide for hire, thirty years a color nobody questioned — opened his mouth to answer, and for the first time in his life had absolutely nothing to hide behind.
