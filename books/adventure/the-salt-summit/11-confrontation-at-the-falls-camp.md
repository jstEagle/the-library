## 11. Confrontation at the Falls Camp

The falls announced themselves an hour before we saw them — a low thunder rolling up the canyon like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Emeric took us out of the current and into a side eddy with the ease of a man parking a cart in his own barn, and we made camp on a gravel shelf beneath a leaning birch, close enough to the water that its spray hung in the air like cold smoke.

I built the fire small. Tobias laid the archive pages out on oilcloth to finish drying, weighted at the corners with river stones. Nia gathered wood without being asked, which she'd been doing since the rapids, as if usefulness were a currency she was afraid of running out of.

Emeric stood apart, facing upstream, his back to all of us. He'd been standing that way since noon.

I told myself it was nothing. A man watching the water. A guide doing his job. But I had spent thirty years reading weather from a cockpit, and I knew what it looked like when a front was building behind someone's eyes. He hadn't laughed once today. Not even when Nia's pole snagged and she nearly went over the gunwale chasing it, not even when Tobias recited a passage of Veyli he'd half-remembered and mangled so badly that Nia had corrected him twice before giving up in disgust. The man who had talked us through the rapids with jokes steady as handrails had gone quiet as ballast.

I was still deciding whether to ask him about it when Nia stopped asking herself.

She came around the fire with her arms full of wood and dropped it beside the flames hard enough to scatter sparks. "You signaled them."

Emeric didn't turn. "Wood's wet on the north bank. You want dry stuff, look under the overhang."

"Don't." Her voice cracked across the camp like a whip over a team. "Don't do that. Don't be the guide right now. At the toll bridge. This morning. You went up to the tollhouse alone, and you came back too calm, and you've been facing away from us ever since because you can't stand to look at us. I saw your hands. You did *something* with your hands."

Tobias sat up slowly among his drying pages. I kept my eyes on the fire and my hand near the axe.

"Nia," Emeric said, and there it was — the crack in the guide's voice, the other man underneath, the one whose name I still didn't know.

"The lamp," she said. "The signal lamp hanging in the tollhouse window. Three long, two short. My grandmother taught me river codes before she taught me prayers. That's syndicate traffic code. Everybody on the lower Kessari knows it. So don't tell me it was a lamp for lighting the tollkeeper's supper."

The spray drifted through the firelight. Somewhere above us, the falls kept up their patient grinding, as if the whole canyon were waiting to hear how this ended.

Emeric turned around.

I'll give him this: he didn't reach for the camouflage. He didn't smile, didn't shrug, didn't put the easy river-guide voice back on like a coat. He just stood there in the firelight looking older than he had that morning, and said, "You're right. I signaled them."

Tobias got to his feet. "You what?"

"I told them where we are. Not where we're going — they don't know about the song-map, they don't know about the gate. I gave them our position at the toll bridge and our heading. Upstream. Toward the falls." His jaw worked. "In exchange, they leave Kessari Mouth alone. The village downstream. Forty families. My mother is buried there."

"Your mother," Tobias repeated, "fled Veyl. You told us—"

"I told you what I've told everyone my whole life." And then, quieter, in a voice I hadn't heard from him yet: "My mother's name was Sable Veylan. She was sixteen when she came down the river with nothing but her sister's coat. She married a fisherman and buried her name so deep even I couldn't find it until after she died. I have spent forty years being Emeric Sable, guide, no history, no questions, because the last time anyone on this river admitted to Veyli blood, men came in the night and the village burned anyway. Famine, the histories say. The river turned against them, the histories say. I believed the histories, because believing them kept me safe." He looked at the archive spread on the oilcloth, at the ledger page with its burned edge. "Then you people come floating down my river with proof that everything I built my life on top of was a lie somebody sold. So yes. When I saw that lamp, I used it. I bought my mother's grave another season of peace with your location. I'm not proud of it. I'm not going to pretend I am, either."

"You sold us," Nia said. Her voice had gone very small and very hard, the way voices do when they're holding something back that would otherwise break loose. "You sat in that boat and steered us and joked with us and the whole time you were — we trusted you."

"Yes," Emeric said. "That's what it costs. Both directions."

Nia's arm went back. I thought she was going to throw something — a stone, a brand, her whole heart — and I moved before I decided to, catching her wrist mid-swing. She was shaking. Under my fingers her pulse hammered like a bird in a box.

"He watched my grandmother wait," she said to me, not to him. "Twenty years she waited by the river for the source to answer. Twenty years, and people called her mad, and now I know why nobody ever answered, why the valley never sent anyone down, why she died thinking she'd been forgotten — because people like him hid. People like him let the world believe Veyl was dead so they wouldn't have to be Veyli anymore." Her eyes were blazing and wet. "He could have told them no."

"He could have," I agreed. "And then Corvane's boats would have gone to Kessari Mouth instead of following us, and we'd be no closer to the source, and forty families would be hostages." I said it evenly, because someone had to do arithmetic while the rest of them bled. "That's the trade he made. It's ugly. It's also real."

"Rosalind." Tobias's voice was careful. "You're defending him?"

"I'm counting." I let go of Nia's wrist, gently. "Counting is what keeps people alive. Sit down. All of you. We're going to settle this in the next five minutes, because I've been listening to that canyon since sundown, and I don't like what it's stopped saying."

They looked at me. Even Emeric.

"The birds," I said. "Dippers work these rapids all night. You hear them under the falls' noise if you listen. They quit twenty minutes ago. Either the river changed or something moved into the gorge below us that the birds don't like." I fed a branch to the fire and watched the light jump. "Scouts travel quiet and they travel ahead of the boats. If Corvane got Emeric's message at the toll bridge this morning, fast crews could be at the foot of the portage by now. So whatever we decide about each other, we decide it *now*, and then we move."

Nia wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at her own tears, and sat down on a stone with her spine straight as a mast. "Fine. Decide."

I looked at Emeric. He met it. That mattered more than anything else he'd said — he didn't drop his eyes, didn't perform remorse, just stood there owning what he'd done the way a man owns a debt.

Here is what I knew, in the part of me that reads fuel gauges and river bends: I had known this man for six days. Six days ago I had a rule about attachments and a life measured in miles, and I would have told you I trusted no one and needed no one and that this was freedom. Then a chest came out of the river, and a stowaway came out of a locker, and an archivist came out of my past's shadow, and a guide came out of nowhere knowing every bend of water no chart named — and somewhere in the spray of that first rapid, without asking my permission, they had become the thing I was afraid of losing. Nothing, I'd insisted. I was afraid of losing nothing.

The lie in that had gotten people killed before. In me, it had only gotten me lonely.

"Emeric Sable," I said. "Or whoever you are. Here's my offer, and it's the only one coming. You stay. You take us past the falls tomorrow, up the portage, through every piece of water between here and the source that no map has ever survived. You use everything your mother gave you, every name she buried, every trick you learned hiding who you are — you spend all of it getting us there. And when we arrive, you tell the valley your mother's true name yourself, out loud, to their faces. That's the price. Not forgiveness. Forgiveness isn't mine to sell." I nodded at Nia. "Hers either. But she gets to decide whether she travels in the same boat with you, and I suspect she does, because she's braver than any of us."

"And if I run?" Emeric said quietly. "Tonight, while you sleep?"

"Then I'll know the man I gambled on was worth less than the gamble, and I'll grieve it later, when there's time." I shrugged. "But you won't run. Because running is what you've been doing for forty years, and you just told four strangers the truth for the first time in your life, and I watched your face while you did it. Men don't look like that when they're planning to keep hiding."

For a long moment the only sound was the falls.

Then Nia stood up. She walked over to Emeric, and I tensed, but she didn't swing. She held out her grandmother's river charm — a little beaten-silver fish on a leather cord, the one thing she'd carried from Lowmark — and closed his fingers around it.

"My grandmother said you forgive family in advance," she said. "Because you'll need to. I don't forgive you yet. But you're carrying this until I do. Lose it and we're done."

Emeric looked down at his fist like it might burn. When he spoke, his voice had roughened into something that wasn't the guide's voice at all. "Sable Veylan's son will get you to the source," he said. "On my mother's name."

"That's the idea," I said. "Now pack. We break camp in ten minutes, cold, no fire, and—"

A whistle sounded downstream. Thin, low, two notes — and then answered, fainter, from the far bank.

We all froze. Emeric's head came up, and I saw forty years of camouflage strip off him in a single heartbeat, leaving something sharp and terrified and awake.

"Two whistles means scouts have the water," he breathed. "Three means they've seen your fire."

The fire. Our small, careful, stupid fire, throwing its light up against the birch trunks like a beacon.

"Kill it," I said, already moving. "Tobias, the archive — waterproof roll, now. Nia, boat. Emeric—" He was already beside me, kicking gravel over the coals, and for one strange instant we moved like we'd been crew for years instead of enemies six hours ago.

Above the thunder of the falls, thin and clear across the black water, a third whistle rose.

* * *

We ran the boat out into the eddy with no lights and paddles muffled in cloth, and the canyon swallowed us. Behind us, on the gravel shelf, the last embers of our fire glowed like a dying eye, and I prayed it would buy us the minutes we needed. Ahead, the falls roared out of the dark, a wall of white noise and white water, and beyond it — if Emeric's mother's knowledge was worth what he'd promised — a portage trail that no syndicate chart had ever found.

Emeric knelt at the bow, the silver fish clenched in one fist, reading the dark water by feel.

"Hold together," he murmured — to the boat, to the river, maybe to the dead. "All of you. Hold together."

The first scout's lantern blinked on the bank behind us, and the current took us toward the falls.
