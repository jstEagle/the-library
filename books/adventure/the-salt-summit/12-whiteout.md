## 12. Whiteout

The storm came out of the north like something with a grudge.

I saw it first as a bruise on the horizon while we were still over open water, a smudge of violet-gray where there should have been sky, and I did what any bush pilot with half a brain would do: I turned for the nearest landing and started doing arithmetic. Distance to the gravel bar at Kessari Bend — eleven minutes. Ceiling dropping faster than I could climb under it. Wind out of the northwest at thirty knots and building.

"Rosalind?" Tobias's voice from behind me, thin over the engine noise. He'd learned to read my silences in the week we'd been on the river, but he hadn't learned to read my hands yet, and my hands were busy.

"Landing," I said. "Now. Strap everything down."

Nia was already moving before I finished the sentence. That was the thing about her nobody expected — she looked like a bird, all wrists and collarbones, but she moved like she'd been raised on boats in weather, which, it turned out, she had. She got the chest lashed down, got Emeric's pack wedged under the seat frame, got herself belted, all in the time it took me to line up the approach.

Emeric stood hip-deep in the shallows guiding me in by hand signals because the bar was littered with driftwood and I couldn't judge depth through the chop. His face was streaming wet and utterly calm. Men who grow up on rivers have a way of looking at storms that isn't brave exactly. It's more like recognition. *You again.*

We were down and tied off with twenty minutes to spare.

Then the world ended.

* * *

I've flown through whiteouts before. There's a version that happens in the air, when snow and cloud merge into one unbroken gray and you lose the horizon, lose the ground, lose your own wings from sight, and you fly on instruments and faith and the memory of what up used to look like. Pilots die of that every year. They call it the graveyard spiral — you feel like you're flying level and you're not, and by the time the trees tell you so, it's too late to matter.

This was worse. This was the whiteout with walls.

By full dark we couldn't see the tail of my own plane from the cockpit. We couldn't see each other past six feet. The wind came off the ice fields in long screaming gusts that made the fuselage rock on its tie-downs like a boat at anchor, and the snow didn't fall so much as travel sideways, a horizontal river of white, and somewhere in the middle of it was us, four people and a bronze-bound chest full of drowned nation, huddled in a machine never built for winter.

"We can't stay in here," Emeric said. He had to shout it against the wind even inside the cabin. "Temperature's going to keep falling. The fuel won't gel, the battery dies, and then we're sitting in an icebox with no door."

"The tent's gone," Tobias said. It had been. A gust had taken it off the cargo rack during the landing run, and we'd watched it cartwheel away down the bar like a pale ghost, gone in three seconds.

"So we dig," Emeric said.

And that's what we did. In the last usable light, with the storm chewing at our backs, we dug a trench in the gravel bank above the high-water line, lined it with the parachute I kept in the emergency kit — silk, light, worthless for anything else this far north — stacked driftwood over it, piled snow on top of the wood, and crawled in. A snow cave, more or less. My father had taught me to build one when I was nine years old, on a hunting trip I'd hated, and I'd resented the lesson for twenty years and thanked him for it every day since.

Four people in a space the size of a coffin laid sideways. Body heat, a single candle stub, and the wind howling overhead like something pacing the roof of the world.

"Well," Nia said, after a long silence. "This is cozy."

Nobody laughed, exactly. But something in all of us loosened, a half-inch, which is its own kind of laughter.

* * *

Here is what survival strips you down to, when it gets serious:

Calories. Water. Warmth. And the truth about yourself, which arrives whether you've invited it or not.

We'd left Lowmark with provisions for six days, generously packed, because Tobias had insisted on buying extra of everything and I'd let him, mostly to be kind. But the river route had eaten days we hadn't planned for — the toll bridge, the falls camp, the argument with Emeric that none of us talked about anymore and all of us thought about constantly — and now the arithmetic was ugly. I counted it by candlelight while the others slept, or pretended to: hardtack for two days if we ate full rations, four if we ate half. Tea, plenty. Dried fish, three days' worth. A tin of rendered fat. Nothing else.

Half rations, then. Four days. Five if the storm broke tomorrow and we could move.

It didn't break tomorrow. It didn't break the day after either.

* * *

On the second night, the cold found the weak point in all of us, and it wasn't our bodies.

Tobias talked. That was his weakness and his medicine both — when the silence got too large, he filled it, and in the cave with nothing to do but listen, we learned things about him that his books would never have told. That he'd had a wife once, before the disgrace, a woman named Ilse who'd corrected his proofs and laughed at his marginalia and died of a fever the same year the archive expelled him, so that he couldn't afterward separate the two griefs, couldn't tell which loss had hollowed him. That he kept her reading glasses in his coat pocket and had worn them, once, at a lecture, by accident, and had to leave the stage.

"I'm not telling you this for sympathy," he said, into the dark. His voice had gone rough. "I'm telling you because in two days we may be too hungry to talk, and there are things I want said while I still have the strength to say them prettily."

"You're not going to die, Tobias," Nia said. She said it with the flat certainty of sixteen, the age at which you still believe saying a thing makes it true.

"No," he agreed gently. "Probably not. But it costs nothing to say what matters while saying is easy."

Emeric hadn't spoken in hours. I heard him shift in the dark, heard the wet rasp of his breathing — he'd taken a chill wading in the shallows during the landing and was fighting it off with stubbornness and sips of pine tea I brewed from needles scraped off a stunted spruce near the bank. When he finally spoke, it was quiet enough that I had to lean toward him to catch it.

"My mother sang," he said. "Working songs. Veyli ones. I used to think they were just songs. Now I know they were probably maps, or prayers, or both, and I let myself forget every word of them because forgetting was safer." A pause. The wind screamed overhead. "That's what I want said while saying is easy. Don't wait forty years like I did. Whatever you're carrying — say it."

Nobody answered him right away. Then Nia, very softly, began to hum.

It was a melody I didn't know, five notes rising, five notes falling, repeated with small variations, and Emeric went absolutely still beside me. I felt the stillness the way you feel a held breath in a small room.

"That's—" he started, and stopped.

"Grandmother's," Nia said. "She sang it when the storms came. She said it was for finding your way home when you can't see."

She hummed it through twice more, and then, because none of us could bear it and all of us needed it, she taught it to us. Four voices in a hole in the ground, singing a dead woman's song to a drowned country, while the blizzard tried its best to erase the whole world and failed.

* * *

The third day, the plane told us how bad things were.

I dug out to check the tie-downs — you do that, storm or no storm; a pilot who stops checking her aircraft starts dying in small ways she doesn't notice — and found the wind had driven a raft of river ice against the hull sometime in the night. Not big floes. Grinding, shifting pans, pushed by the current under the snow, and they had worked the floats like a vise. One float was cracked along a seam. The strut attaching it to the fuselage was bent maybe four degrees, which doesn't sound like much until you know that struts are not forgiving of degrees.

I lay in the snow beside the wreck of my beautiful airplane for a long time, letting the wind hit me, doing the math I didn't want to do.

I could straighten the strut, maybe. With heat, with leverage, with luck. I could patch the float with canvas and dope if I had canvas and dope, which I had some of. But the real problem was underneath the problems: the river above the bend was freezing fast, closing from the banks inward, and even repaired, the Goose would need open water to lift off. Every hour the storm continued, the runway beneath us turned to stone.

We weren't stranded above the last navigable water. We were stranded above the last navigable *day*.

I crawled back into the cave and told them plainly, because I'd decided long ago that fear grows fastest in the dark and I wasn't going to farm it. Tobias took it like a scholar — quietly, already turning it over, looking for the footnote that changed the meaning. Nia took it like a stone dropped into her stomach; I watched her go somewhere private and bad for a moment, thinking of her grandmother waiting for an answer that never came, and then she came back.

Emeric took it like a man who'd been expecting it.

"There's a Veyli trail," he said. "Old trade route. It leaves the river above the falls and climbs the east wall of the canyon. My mother talked about it — refugees used it, during the collapse. It goes up onto the plateau and over, toward the source country." He looked at me across the candle. "If the plane's done, we walk."

"Walking takes food we don't have," I said.

"Staying takes nothing we have either."

He had me there, and he knew it, and I hated him a little and loved him a lot, which I was beginning to understand was simply what Emeric Sable did to people.

* * *

The fourth night the temperature fell so far that the candle flame shrank to a blue bead and our breath froze on the silk ceiling above us in a fine glittering frost, and huddling stopped being a strategy and became a fact. We lay pressed together spine to spine, alternating so no one's back faced the cold too long, rotating through the warm center position like a slow human wheel. Tobias's feet were the problem — circulation shot from decades at a desk — so Nia took them against her stomach without being asked, wrapped in the spare shirt, and complained about their temperature with such inventive profanity that Tobias, mortified and grateful, pretended to be asleep.

Somewhere in the smallest hours, half-dreaming, I felt Nia's hand find mine in the dark. Just hold it. No reason. Sixteen years old and starving and scared out of her mind, and she reached for me the way you reach for a rail in the night.

I should tell you what that cost me. I had a rule. Twenty years of a rule: nothing you can't leave in ten seconds flat, nobody whose absence will ground you. I'd watched attachments kill pilots — not dramatically, not in crashes, but slowly, in the hesitation before a storm turn, the extra minute spent worrying about someone instead of the gauge in front of you. Attachment was dead weight. I'd built a whole life on jettisoning it.

And here was a child's cold hand in mine, and I was holding on.

I measured my life in miles and fuel gauges. That was the whole architecture. And the architecture was gone — burned for warmth, eaten as rations, sung away in a snow cave by a girl who hummed like her grandmother — and what was left underneath, I discovered, was not nothing. What was left was these three impossible people, and the fierce, terrified, unwilling knowledge that I would dig through a mountain for any one of them, and that this knowledge had arrived without my permission and was not leaving.

"Tobias," I said, into the dark, because saying is easier while saying is easy. "When we get out of this — I want to see the ledger properly. All of it. I want to understand what we're carrying."

He was quiet a moment. "You will," he said. "It's yours as much as anyone's now. More than most."

"And Emeric. When we find whoever's left up there—" I stopped. Started again. "Your mother's name. You'll say it to them first. Before anyone else."

In the dark I heard him breathe, once, unsteadily. "Yes," he said. "I will."

Nia's grip tightened on my hand. Nobody said anything else. The wind walked its rounds over our roof, and the frost glittered, and we slept.

* * *

The fifth morning, the storm broke like a fever.

We came out of the cave into a world remade — sun on snow so bright it hurt, the air scoured clean and ringing cold, the Kessari below us locked bank to bank in gray-white ice. Silence so total we could hear our own heartbeats. The plane sat tilted on its cracked float like a wounded bird, and I put my hand on the cold cowling and let myself grieve it for exactly one minute, because that was all the time there was.

Then I turned to the others, and they were already looking at me, waiting, and I understood that somewhere in the last five nights I had become the one they waited on. Not because I'd claimed it. Because I'd stopped leaving.

"Rations for two days, walking pace," I said. "So we move today. Emeric — this trail of yours. How far to the plateau?"

"A day and a half, if the canyon wall hasn't fallen in. Two if it has."

"Then we take the chest, the ledger, the radio, and as much food as we can carry, and we leave the rest." I looked back once at the Goose — twelve years of my life, my home, my whole solitary architecture, sitting broken on the ice — and I made myself say the rest of it out loud, because saying is easy now or never. "She'll keep. Planes keep. People don't."

We were packing the sled — the cargo toboggan, stripped, lashed with parachute cord — when Nia suddenly went still, her head cocked, listening to something none of the rest of us could hear.

"What is it?" Tobias asked.

She frowned, digging in the inner pocket of her jacket, the one she guarded like a dragon. She brought out a scrap of oilcloth, folded and refolded a hundred times, soft as cloth from handling. Inside it was nothing — no paper, no ink. Just creases. Folds in oilcloth, worn nearly blind with age.

"She wouldn't write it down," Nia said slowly, turning it over. "She said paper burns and ink runs, but folds remember. She made me fold it with her a thousand times. Valley, river, falls, gate." Her fingers moved over the creases, tracing routes I couldn't see. "Rosalind. I think I know the way over the plateau. I think I've known it since I was eight years old, and I just figured out what I was learning."

The sun blazed on the snow. Somewhere south, downriver, I knew, gunboats were grinding through the last open channel, and a man with a courteous voice was making his own arithmetic.

"Then show us," I said.
