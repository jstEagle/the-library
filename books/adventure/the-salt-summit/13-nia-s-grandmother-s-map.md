## 13. Nia's Grandmother's Map

The blizzard broke on the third morning the way grief breaks — all at once, leaving everything exposed.

Nia woke to light so clean it hurt. The snow had stopped sometime in the night, and now the world above the last navigable water was a white page with nothing written on it yet: the wrecked plane wearing a drift like a shawl, the spruce bent and glittering, the fire pit steaming where Emeric had already coaxed the coals back to life. She lay in the shelter of the overturned canoe they'd dragged against the fuselage and listened to the others breathe — Tobias snoring with his mouth open like an old man twice his age, Rosalind silent as always, sleeping the way she did everything else, efficiently, without waste.

Nia had not slept well. She hadn't slept well since the toll bridge, since she'd seen Emeric raise that signal lamp toward the syndicate scouts and then lie about it with his whole honest face. But something else had kept her awake too, turning over and over under her ribs like a fish in shallow water. A secret of her own, older than his.

She'd been carrying it since Lowmark. Since before Lowmark. She'd been carrying it since she was seven years old, sitting on a stool in a kitchen that smelled of dried fish and camphor, learning verses she thought were just songs.

*Time to spend it,* she told herself, and got up, and stamped the feeling down into her boots where it could do some good.

* * *

They gathered around the fire because there was nowhere else to be. That was the thing about being stranded that nobody warned you about: it turned four strangers into an audience whether you wanted one or not. Tobias sat wrapped in both blankets, holding his hands out to the flames, his fingers still ink-stained even after two weeks of river water and frost. Rosalind crouched by the plane's cowling, doing something futile and faithful to the engine, the way she'd done every morning. Emeric was mending a tear in the canoe skin, working the pitch with his thumbs, not looking at anyone. He hadn't looked at anyone properly since the confrontation at the falls camp. Nia watched him work and felt the strange doubled thing she felt about him now — anger and pity braided together so tight she couldn't tell which strand was which.

"We're past the last charted water," Rosalind said, without looking up from the engine. "I've been through every map Tobias has, and every map I have. Above the falls, the Kessari stops existing on paper. The surveyors marked it *impassable* and went home."

"Surveyors are paid to go home," Emeric said quietly.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "Well." She wiped her hands on a rag and finally looked around the fire, and Nia saw her do the thing she did — the arithmetic, the weighing, the counting of fuel and food and days like a woman balancing a ledger she was afraid to total. "We have rations for nine days if we're careful. Ten if we're hungry. The syndicate knows roughly where we are, and Corvane doesn't strike me as a man who loses interest because of weather."

"So we need a route," Tobias said, "that no syndicate chart shows."

"That's what I said."

"And I'm saying," Emeric said, setting down the pitch pot, "that such a route exists. It just isn't on any chart. It never was."

Nia's heart was suddenly very loud. She stood up before she could talk herself out of it, and three faces turned to her, and she understood in one cold flash why her grandmother had made her memorize the verses over and over until they lived in her bones instead of her memory. Because there would be a morning like this one. Because the dead plan for the living.

"I know the way," she said.

* * *

They stared at her. Snow ticked off the spruce boughs. Somewhere downslope, the frozen falls made their long groaning sound, ice shifting against ice, like the river clearing its throat.

"You know the way past the falls," Rosalind repeated. Not mocking. Measuring. That was the thing about Rosalind Oyelaran — she didn't waste disbelief on people; she wasted assessment on them, which was worse and better at the same time.

"My grandmother taught me," Nia said. Her mouth had gone dry. She pushed through it. "She spoke Veyli. Old Veyli, the river dialect, not the port kind. She sang songs all the time when I was small — lullabies, work songs, whatever. And there was one song she made me learn word-perfect. She'd stop me in the middle and make me start over if I got a single line wrong. I used to think she was just being difficult." Nia laughed, and it came out cracked. "She wasn't being difficult. She was dying, and she knew it, and she was putting a map into me the way you'd put supplies into a boat."

Tobias had gone very still. It was the archivist stillness, Nia realized — the stillness of a man who has spent his life around documents and has just heard one speak.

"Sing it," he said softly. "Please."

"Not yet." Nia held up a hand, surprised by her own boldness, more surprised that it worked. "Because it's not just a song. If I sing it wrong, or you hear it wrong, we die in the canyon above the falls. So let me explain first, and then I'll sing it, and then—" she looked at Tobias— "you write it down, but you don't show anybody, ever, and you don't copy it, and if we get separated you keep it in your head too. Both of us. Like my grandmother and her sister did."

"How does a song hold a map?" Rosalind asked. She'd abandoned the engine entirely now. She came and sat on the canoe gunwale across the fire, elbows on knees, and Nia had the sudden vertiginous sense of being seen — really seen — by those gray pilot's eyes that usually measured only distance and weather.

"The same way a knot holds a rope," Nia said. "You just have to know where to pull." She crouched and drew in the snow with a stick: a long wavy line. "The river. Here's the falls — the big drop, where we portaged. Above it the canyon narrows. Every chart says the canyon is a dead end, walls too steep, water too fast. Right?"

"Right," Rosalind said.

"But the song says otherwise. The song has verses, and each verse is a day's travel, and each verse tells you what the river looks like when you're in the right place — and what to do there." She tapped the stick against her palm. "My grandmother called it *the answering*. The river asks questions in stone, and the song answers them. Verse one: *where the white stone leans like a grandmother over water, take the left ear of the current.* That's a bend where a pale rock juts out. You hug the left channel under its shadow. If you take the right, there's a sieve of rocks that eats boats. Verse two is a landing place. Verse three —" she hesitated — "verse three is the hard one. It's about walking."

"Walking?" Emeric said.

"Carrying the boat. There's a stretch where the river goes underground — the song calls it *the swallowed mile* — and you portage over the top of it, along a ledge, in the dark part of the canyon. My grandmother said her mother did it as a girl, during the collapse, with thirty other people and everything they owned on their backs." Nia's voice caught. She hadn't meant to say the next part yet, but it came out anyway, the way true things do. "Not all of them came out the other side. The verse counts them. It counts the ones who fell. That's how you know you're on the right ledge — there are markers. Cairns. One for each name in the song."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Upstream, faint and endless, the drowned river ran on beneath its lid of ice.

"That's why she made me learn it perfect," Nia finished, quieter. "It's not a map. It's a grave list and a map. They're the same document."

* * *

Tobias asked to hear it three times before he'd accept that she meant it, and then Nia stood up in the snow with the fire at her back and sang.

Her voice wasn't beautiful. She knew that. It was thin and a little flat in the high places, and the Veyli words came out of her mouth rounder than her grandmother's had, softened by sixteen years of Lowmark harbor speech. But the melody held. It was a strange melody, built for walking — steady, repetitive, with a rising phrase at the end of each verse like a hand pointing forward. She sang of the leaning white stone and the left ear of the current. She sang of the split boulder shaped like a closed eye, where you landed and slept with your boots on. She sang of the swallowed mile, and the count of the fallen — nine names, nine cairns, sung in order, oldest first — and she saw Tobias's lips moving silently, transcribing, his eyes wet and unashamed of it.

And she sang the last verse, the one her grandmother had saved for last on purpose, the one Nia had never understood until this winter:

*When the canyon opens like a hand unclenching,*
*and the water comes up young from the dark,*
*stand in the shallows and sing the first verse backward.*
*What answers you is home.*

"What does that mean?" Rosalind asked, when the silence had settled. "Backward? Singing a verse backward?"

"It means exactly what it says," Nia said. "I asked my grandmother the same thing when I was ten. She said, *the valley doesn't want to be found by people who read maps, little fish. It wants to be found by people who remember.* The gate — if there is a gate — it listens. You stand where the water rises and you give the song back to it, inside out, and that proves you came from someone it sent away." She shrugged, suddenly embarrassed, hugging her arms against the cold. "Or it's nonsense and we freeze to death in a pretty canyon. I honestly don't know. I've never met anyone who could check."

"I can check half of it," Tobias said. His voice was rough. He had the notebook open on his knee, pages bristling with his small precise script, and he looked up at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — not the scholar's hunger, not the exile's caution, but something older and simpler. Reverence. "The place-names are real. I catalogued Veyli river records for eleven years, and I've seen *the swallowed mile* referenced exactly once — in a grain-transport ledger fragment, a note about a route 'not fit for carts, fit for the desperate.' Everyone assumed it was metaphor." He laughed, a short disbelieving sound. "It was a shipping lane. For people with nothing left to lose."

"And the rest of it?" Emeric asked. He hadn't moved from the canoe. His hands were still around the pitch pot, knuckles white. When Nia looked at him, he looked away first — but not before she saw what was in his face, and it undid her a little. It wasn't skepticism. It was recognition. Somewhere behind his eyes, a boy was hearing his dead mother's language sung aloud for the first time in twenty years.

"The rest," Nia said, "we check by going."

* * *

Rosalind made them argue about it for an hour, because that was who she was. She walked the fire's perimeter and asked hard questions — how accurate could a child's memory be, what if the landmarks had shifted in sixty years, what if the song was a story grandmothers told and the canyon was exactly as impassable as the charts claimed. Nia answered what she could and admitted what she couldn't, and somewhere in the middle of it she realized that Rosalind wasn't trying to shoot the idea down. She was testing whether Nia would break under pressure, the way Emeric had broken, the way a person breaks when their secret is a lie instead of a load-bearing wall.

Nia didn't break. By the end she was almost enjoying it.

"Fine," Rosalind said at last. She came back to the fire and stood over it, and Nia watched her do the thing she'd watched her do every day since Lowmark: run the numbers, weigh the loads, calculate the odds like a woman pricing her own life. But this time, when she finished, something in her face was different. Looser. Almost — almost — hopeful. "Nine days of rations. A route the syndicate can't follow because they can't read it. A guide who knows the river below the falls, an archivist who knows the words above them, and a girl who carries the map in her head where no one can steal it." She looked around at the three of them, and her mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "That's the best hand I've been dealt since I fished that chest out of the river. Maybe ever."

"There's one condition," Nia said.

Rosalind raised an eyebrow.

"When we find them — the valley, the survivors, whoever's up there — the song gets sung back to them too. All of it. The grave names especially. My grandmother waited her whole life for the source to answer her, and it never did, and she died singing to a river that couldn't hear her." Nia's voice steadied itself on purpose. "So when we get there, somebody's going to hear those nine names spoken by a voice that learned them from her voice. That's not negotiable. That's the fare."

For a long moment Rosalind just looked at her. Then she nodded, once, the way she did everything — efficiently, without waste.

"Fare accepted," she said. "Pack up. We move at first light."

* * *

Nia didn't sleep that night either, but it was a different animal keeping her awake now. She lay in the shelter of the canoe and listened to the wind comb the spruce, and she sang the verses silently, over and over, checking them against herself like coins against teeth. Nine names. The leaning stone. The backward song. Sixty years old and still warm, still carrying weight, a dead woman's hands reaching up out of the ground to steer her granddaughter's boat.

*You thought I was teaching you songs,* her grandmother would have said, if dead women could laugh. *Little fish. I was teaching you the way home.*

Near dawn, Nia heard footsteps in the snow and opened her eyes. Emeric was standing at the edge of the firelight, looking upstream, toward the falls, toward the canyon beyond. And he was humming.

Low. Barely a breath of it. But she knew the melody instantly — the walking rhythm, the rising phrase — and she sat bolt upright, heart slamming, because he was humming the second verse, the landing verse, the one about the closed-eye boulder, and he was humming it *correctly*, in the old pronunciation, in a voice that had learned it somewhere other than a kitchen in Lowmark.

He felt her watching. He turned. For the first time since the toll bridge, Emeric Sable looked her full in the face, and his eyes were shining and terrified.

"My mother sang that," he said. "Every night. I thought it was just a lullaby."

Above the frozen falls, far up the black throat of the canyon, something that might have been wind — and might not have — moved through the ice, and the river beneath it seemed, for one impossible moment, to be listening.
