## 14. The Portage of Bones

The river told us to stop before the map did.

Emeric Sable had run water his whole life — smuggler's channels, log flumes, the brown arteries of the lowland delta where a wrong read meant a drowned boat and a quiet burial no one attended. He knew every way a river could say *no*. This one said it in ice: the Kessari's voice changing pitch as the current shallowed, white water braiding into something that looked less like rapids and more like the river tearing itself apart.

"Here," he said, and backed the oars, and the boat swung into an eddy beneath a wall of grey stone. "We walk from here."

Rosalind stood in the bow with the towline coiled over her shoulder like something she was considering strangling him with. "Walk. Through that." She nodded at the canyon ahead, where the walls pinched together and the river vanished into spray and thunder.

"Around it," he corrected. "There's a portage trail on the north bank. Old one. Older than the survey maps."

"How old?"

He didn't answer that. He shipped the oars and began unloading gear, because some questions were easier answered by doing than by saying, and because he'd been avoiding this particular answer since Lowmark.

Nia crouched at the gunwale, watching the water slide past with her grandmother's patience — the stillness of someone who'd learned young that rivers reward waiting. "You've been here before," she said. Not a question either. The girl asked very few questions and somehow got answers to all of them anyway.

"No," Emeric said.

"You said 'older than the survey maps' like a man quoting his mother."

Tobias Wrenn looked up from the chest he was lashing shut, his spectacles fogged, his hands — archivist's hands, ink-stained and soft — surprisingly steady on the buckle. In the two weeks since Lowmark, the disgraced royal archivist had grown a layer of river callus and lost none of his habit of listening the way other men talked. "Sable," he said mildly, "you lie the way most people breathe. Automatically, and badly under exertion."

"Then don't exert me."

Rosalind dropped the towline into the boat and stepped onto the gravel bar, boots crunching frost. She was watching him with those pilot's eyes that measured everything in margins — fuel, weather, people. He'd seen her look at Tobias that way when the archivist first came stumbling out of Lowmark's fog with a photograph and a story about drowned Veyl. She hadn't trusted him then either. She'd kept him anyway. That was the arithmetic Rosalind Oyelaran ran, Emeric had learned: not whether to trust, but what trusting would cost, and who would pay it.

Since the falls camp — since Nia had stood in the firelight with her fists shaking and made him say out loud what he'd signaled to Corvane's scouts at the toll bridge — the whole company had been running on that same cold arithmetic. They needed his river knowledge. So they kept him. Rosalind had said it plainly, without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty: *You carry us or you carry nothing, Sable. Choose which.* He'd chosen. He was still choosing, every bend of the river, every hour his betrayal sat in the boat between them like ballast.

But the portage — the portage he couldn't navigate by skill alone. And they were about to see why.

* * *

The trail began as a cut in the scree, barely shoulder-wide, climbing away from the river's roar into a silence that deepened with every switchback. Cold pooled in the canyon like water in a hull. The air smelled of stone and old snow and, faintly, of something else — something dry and mineral that Emeric's body recognized before his mind allowed it to.

Juniper smoke, he thought. No. Not smoke. Just the ghost-shape memory of it, the way a scar remembers the knife.

They climbed for an hour. Rosalind took point with the axe; Tobias carried the satchel of dried archive pages against his chest like a man carrying a heartbeat; Nia carried the song. She'd been humming it since morning, low, almost subvocal — the verses her grandmother had stitched into melody so a child could hold a route through mountains the way other children held nursery rhymes. *Where the river forgets its name, count the standing stones; where the stones lean left, the dead keep the path...*

Emeric heard the verse and his stomach dropped, because he knew what came next in it, and he knew what the next mile of trail held, and he had been praying — a word he didn't use, a thing he didn't do — that the girl wouldn't sing that far.

She sang that far.

"Where the path runs white," Nia's voice floated back down the line, thin and clear in the cold, "*the ones who ran are sleeping; step soft, little boat, you're crossing someone's home—*"

She stopped. Ahead of them, the trail crested a rib of rock and opened into a long shelf of canyon floor, sheltered from wind, sealed in permanent shadow, and Emeric watched the girl go still in a way that had nothing to do with caution.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. "Oh, *no*."

They came up beside her, and there they were.

The dead of Veyl lay along the portage trail in the dozens — perhaps hundreds, receding into the blue dark of the canyon — preserved by a cold that never lifted, mummified by dryness and altitude into leathery, wind-carved shapes that still wore the shapes of their flight. A woman seated against the canyon wall, arms folded over a bundle that might once have been a child. A man face-down on the trail, one hand stretched forward, fingers worn to bone against the stone, as if the last inch of the journey had simply been more than he could ask of himself. Families in clusters, lying the way exhaustion lays people down: tangled, undignified, human. Their clothing had gone the color of driftwood. Their faces had gone smooth and featureless, weathered to anonymity, so that after the first terrible moment they stopped being individuals and became something else — a landscape. A field of stones shaped like grief.

Nobody spoke. Even the river below had faded to a distant pulse.

It was Tobias who finally moved, and he moved the way Emeric imagined he moved through archives: slowly, hat in hand — he actually removed his hat, the ridiculous felt thing he'd refused to replace since Lowmark — stepping off the trail itself, walking on the scree beside it rather than across anyone. "This is them," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "The exodus columns. Forty-one years ago. They came up the Kessari when the grain failed, heading for the high valleys, and the passes—" He stopped. Adjusted his spectacles. "The histories I was trained on say the refugees scattered. Assimilated. That the nation dissolved *peacefully*, after the famine."

"They didn't scatter," Emeric said.

"No," Tobias said softly, looking down the long shelf of the dead. "I can see they didn't."

"The winter caught them here. This stretch of canyon — it holds cold like a cellar. My mother called it—" He stopped. Closed his mouth.

Rosalind turned. In the flat blue light her face was unreadable, but her eyes weren't; they'd gone sharp and careful, the way they went when she spotted weather building behind a clear horizon. "Called it what?"

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters," Nia said. She hadn't taken her eyes off the dead. Her breath came in small white ghosts. "My grandmother sang this trail. She never said what was on it. She just made the verses slow there. Slower. Like walking." The girl knelt, carefully, on bare stone, and did something Emeric hadn't seen anyone do in twenty years of passing as someone else: she pressed her palm flat to the ground, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "Someone should tell them it worked. Someone should tell them we're still going."

* * *

They crossed the field of bones in single file, off the trail, boots whispering on scree, and it took the better part of two hours because Tobias kept stopping.

He stopped to record. Of course he did — he was still, whatever Lowmark had done to his name, an archivist down to the marrow, and here lay an entire chapter of history the crown had papered over, written not in ink but in bodies. He worked with a charcoal stub and scraps of paper, sketching positions, counting, noting clothing fragments, the direction of travel, the evidence of packs abandoned and burned for warmth. He wept twice without appearing to notice, and both times Rosalind silently took the satchel from him, held it, and gave it back when his hands steadied.

Emeric walked last. He told himself it was to watch their backtrail — habit, discipline, the guide's job — but the truth was simpler and he knew it: he was walking last because ahead of him walked four people who had, in their various broken ways, already claimed him, and he could not bear for them to see his face while he crossed this ground.

Because he knew these dead.

Not these specific ones — he wasn't mad, he didn't recognize faces weathered to blankness — but he knew *this*. His mother had crossed a canyon like this one, further west, in the same winter, in the same column-formation of the desperate. She'd been nine years old. She'd walked between her mother and her aunt holding the hem of her aunt's coat, and somewhere in the freezing dark of that passage her mother — Emeric's grandmother — had lain down and not gotten up, and the column had kept moving because the column had no other choice, and a nine-year-old girl had come out the far end of the canyon with half a name and no one left who could pronounce the other half.

That was how Veyl died, Emeric thought, picking his way past a man whose hand still pointed downtrail. Not in the famine the government blamed on the river. Not even in the ledger Tobias had decoded by firelight, the one that showed the grain quotas sold abroad while the granaries stood empty. It died here, in places like this, one name at a time. Because the survivors learned the lesson the dead taught: *be no one. Be from nowhere. Speak the trade-tongue, take a trade-name, bury the old words so deep even your children can't dig them up.* Safety through erasure. The government had sold the nation's bread, and the survivors had finished the job by selling its memory, one syllable at a time, buying their children's lives with the only currency they had left.

His mother's name was not Emeric Sable's mother's name. That was the truth of it. The woman who raised him in the delta fishing camps, who taught him to read water and to keep his mouth shut, had been born *Savine au Veyl-Marenne* — third daughter of a lineage that went back further than any crown Lowmark recognized — and by the time Emeric was old enough to ask, she'd buried that name so thoroughly she flinched when he found it, once, scratched inside a locket she thought she'd lost. She'd told him never to speak it. She'd told him names were anchors and anchors sank you. She'd told him this with her hand shaking around a cup of tea, in a room with the shutter drawn, and he'd been eleven years old and obedient, and he had never spoken it. Not to her. Not after her. Not even alone, not even in the dark of his own skull, because obedience goes all the way down or it doesn't go at all.

At the toll bridge, trading their route to Corvane's men for the safety of his mother's grave village downstream, he'd told himself it was the same lesson. Protection through surrender. Keep what you love by hiding it.

Standing among the people his mother's people had become, Emeric understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the lungs, that his mother had been wrong, and that he had inherited the wrongness like an heirloom, and that the toll bridge had been him handing Corvane the shovel to bury it deeper.

* * *

They made camp that night in a shallow cave above the field, a hundred yards on, close enough that the dead seemed almost like company. Rosalind built the fire small and screened. Tobias laid archive pages near the heat, turning them with tongs made from green branches, handling the past with more tenderness than he'd ever handled himself. Nia slept curled against the chest, one hand on it, like a child holding a parent's sleeve.

Emeric took first watch. He sat at the cave mouth with his back to the firelight and the canyon dark in front of him, and below, faintly silvered by starlight, the long shelf of the sleeping dead.

Footsteps behind him. Rosalind. She lowered herself onto the stone beside him without asking, wrapped both hands around a tin cup, and handed him a second one. Broth, mostly. Salt and heat. Neither of them drank for a while.

"Nia asked me today," Rosalind said eventually, "what your real name is."

"What did you tell her?"

"That it wasn't mine to give." Rosalind stared out at the dark. "I've spent a lot of years keeping my own name light. Oyelaran — do you know what it means, where my father's from? *One who returns.* Every time I sign a manifest I write a promise I haven't kept." She shrugged, a small motion. "So I'm not going to push you. But I'll tell you what I've figured out flying solo all these years. There's a difference between traveling light and traveling empty. Took me too long to know which one I was doing."

Emeric looked at the cup in his hands. Below them, the dead kept their long silence, forty-one years deep.

"When my mother was dying," he said, and his voice came out strange, rusted, like a gate opening after a season locked, "she asked for water. Just water. And I brought it, and she held my wrist — hard, the way you grip when the current takes the boat — and she said a word. One word. I thought it was delirium. I thought it was the old tongue slipping loose because she was too weak to hold it back." He swallowed. "It wasn't delirium. It was her telling me. The only way she could. Dying people spend their last strength on true things."

Rosalind said nothing. She was good at that. She waited the way a pilot waits on weather — completely, without fidgeting, as if time were a resource she had decided to spend here and nowhere else.

Emeric set down the cup. He stood. He stepped to the edge of the cave mouth, where the starlight reached, where the canyon below lay full of the ones who had run and not arrived, and he did the thing he had not done in thirty years, the thing his mother had begged him never to do, the thing that had cost him — he understood now — every close friendship he'd ever sabotaged, every harbor he'd left too soon, every year he'd spent being nobody from nowhere, safe and hollow as a drifted log.

"Savine," he said.

His voice broke on it and he let it break.

"Savine au Veyl-Marenne. My mother's name. My mother's *true* name." The words went out into the dark over the field of bones, and the canyon took them the way it took everything — without echo, without judgment, keeping them. "Her name was Savine, and she was Marenne-line, and she crossed a canyon like this one when she was nine years old, and she survived by forgetting, and she hated herself for it every day of her life, and she loved me enough to make me forget too, and it was the cruelest thing anyone ever did for me." He was crying now, openly, in front of Rosalind Oyelaran and the dead, and he found he could not stop and did not especially want to. "Her name was Savine. Somebody should say it. Somebody should say it out loud where the ones who ran can hear it, so they know it wasn't for nothing. So they know somebody *made it*."

Behind him, quietly, Nia's voice joined the dark — she was awake, of course she was awake, she was always awake at the edges of things — and she began, very softly, the slow verse. *Where the path runs white, the ones who ran are sleeping.* And then Tobias's voice, rough and uncertain, adding the only thing an archivist knows how to add: "Recorded. Witnessed. Savine au Veyl-Marenne, daughter of Veyl. Entered into the account this night, by three witnesses and a canyon full of her kin." And Rosalind, who measured life in fuel gauges and river bends and had insisted for twenty-two chapters of her solitary life that she had nothing to lose, put her hand briefly on Emeric's shoulder — one pilot's grip, firm, brief, real — and said, "Somebody made it. You did. We did."

Below them the dead slept on, and for the first time in Emeric Sable's long camouflaged life, sleeping among them felt less like trespass and more like coming home to a house where the lights had been left on.

* * *

They broke camp at first grey light, moving fast, because the song-map said the gate country lay two days on and because Rosalind's rule was *miles before hunger, miles before weather.* They crossed the last of the field in silence, each of them, Emeric noticed, stepping off the trail now, unconsciously, the way you step around a sleeping elder's feet. Nia paused at the far end and turned back once, and pressed her palm to the air, and Emeric pretended not to see, and was glad of it.

By midmorning the canyon narrowed again and the river reappeared below them, angry and bright, and Emeric went forward to scout the descent to the water. He was halfway down the switchback when he saw it: on the far bank, small in the distance but unmistakable, a wake. Two wakes. Long, low, grey-hulled, running upstream against all sense and all season, driving hard for the head of the canyon.

Gunboats. Corvane had found the surveyor's route.

Emeric stood on the ledge with the wind pulling at his coat and his mother's name fresh and warm in his mouth like a coal, and he understood that the choice he'd made at the toll bridge had just come due with interest — because the boats below were following exactly the route he'd sold, and the only people who knew the way past them was a dead woman's song in a sixteen-year-old's throat.

He turned and started climbing, fast, shouting for Rosalind.
