## 15. Corvane Catches Up

The surveyor's name was Pell, and he had sold the river for less than Corvane would have paid for a decent bottle of brandy.

Corvane stood at the rail of the *Meridian's* lead gunboat and watched the falls come up out of the mist — the Kessari dropping in three white tiers through a canyon so narrow the water seemed to be screaming. On every official chart, this stretch was marked impassable. On every official chart, the river bent east two miles downstream and died against a wall of basalt that no boat could climb and no portage could cross.

On no chart at all — except the one rolled up inside Pell's coat, which now lived in Corvane's breast pocket — was there a surveyor's cut-line, an old logging road gone to moss, and a chain of shallow riffles that a determined crew with ropes and winches could use to lift four gunboats past the first tier of the falls.

Pell had found it while mapping flood easements for the provincial water board. He had kept it to himself for eleven years, the way a man keeps a knife under a floorboard. And then Corvane's people had found *him*, and it had turned out that eleven years of quiet resentment about his salary was worth exactly nine hundred marks and a promise not to mention certain debts to certain creditors.

"Tier two is the hard one," Pell said beside him. He was a small man with the complexion of someone who had spent decades apologizing to weather. "The winch anchors are still there from the logging days. Iron, driven into the rock. But the cable we brought will need doubling."

"It will need what it needs," Corvane said pleasantly. "You'll be on the first boat up."

Pell went pale in a way that suggested he hadn't fully considered that his route would carry its own seller. "I — I hadn't intended—"

"No one ever intends anything, Mr. Pell. That's why intentions are worthless and routes are priceless." Corvane turned from the rail. The spray had begun to reach them even here, a fine cold grit on the face, like the river spitting its opinion of visitors. "Get below. Stay out of my crew's way. If your cut-line is as good as you say, you'll be paid in full at the top. If it isn't—" He smiled, because it cost nothing and always unsettled people more than shouting. "Well. You know the river better than any of us. You'd survive overboard longer than most."

He left Pell standing there and went down into the wheelhouse, where Vess was studying the canyon walls through a long glass.

Vess was the only person Corvane had ever employed who never flinched, which Corvane valued above loyalty. Loyalty could be bought and therefore un-bought. Flinching was simply inefficient.

"Smoke," Vess said without lowering the glass. "Two miles downriver, on the south bank. A village. Fisher folk, by the nets."

"Kessari-mouth people?"

"Farther-flung. They trade smoked char down to Lowmark in autumn." A pause. "There's a woman on the bank watching us. She's been watching since we rounded the bend. She knows what gunboats mean."

Corvane took the glass himself. The village resolved out of the grey: a scatter of stilt houses, drying racks, a dozen boats pulled up on the shingle like sleeping animals. And yes — a woman, arms crossed, motionless on the bank, tracking them. Not running. Not waving. Just watching, with the particular stillness of someone who has seen trouble pass this way before and learned that stillness is the only prayer that works.

"They're downstream of us," Vess said. "Behind the line. Whatever happens at the falls, they can't interfere."

"Nothing interferes," Corvane said. "That's the entire discipline of it. Everything interferes unless you decide in advance what it's allowed to do." He handed back the glass. "But note the village, Vess. Note it carefully. We may want it later."

Vess's eyes came up, flat and patient. "For what?"

"For leverage. For provisions. For whatever the day requires." Corvane looked out at the falls again, listening to the roar build. Somewhere above that white violence, a bush pilot, a disgraced archivist, a girl, and a guide with a false name were hauling a chest of paper toward a valley that did not exist on any map — a valley containing perhaps two hundred living descendants of a drowned nation, sitting on timber, water rights, and mineral claims that the syndicate's lawyers had already drafted into existence.

Four days ago, Corvane had offered to buy the chest outright, all courtesy, aboard their moored plane. Refused. Politely, but refused — and worse, they had run. People who ran were people who knew what they carried. That confirmed everything Tobias Wrenn's file had promised: the man was a fanatic about provenance, and fanatics could not be bought, only broken or used.

Corvane preferred used.

"We make the first tier before dark," he said. "Double the cable. Wake me when Pell's route either works or kills him."

* * *

It worked, mostly.

The first tier took six hours and nearly took a deckhand's arm, and twice the *Meridian's* sister boat *Cormorant* swung sideways in the current and had to be hauled back by main force while the river tried to fold her in half. Corvane watched all of it from the bank with his hands clasped behind him, saying nothing, because crews worked faster when the man paying them wasn't screaming, and screamed faster when he was silent. It was a simple instrument, silence. He had built a career on instruments simpler than people believed.

At the second tier, Pell's iron anchors held. At the third, one didn't, and the *Cormorant* slid forty feet back down the chute before the safety line caught her, and a crate of winch gear went into the white water and was gone. No one died. Corvane noted the loss in his ledger — gear, time, morale — and moved on.

By the second dawn they were above the falls, on water no syndicate chart showed, in country that felt older than the maps. The canyon narrowed. The forest thickened into spruce so dark it looked bruised. And the river — the river changed character entirely. Below the falls it had been a working river, wide and brown and busy. Here it ran green and cold and fast, and it made Corvane think of the archive photographs in Wrenn's old files: the Kessari as it had been before Veyl drowned, before the government opened the diversion gates and let the valley drink itself.

He had read those files eleven times. He could recite the sequence: grain quotas sold abroad in the spring; the famine by winter; the exodus upriver in the spring after that; the diversion gates opened in summer, officially to save the hydroelectric works, unofficially to erase the evidence walking toward the source. Forty thousand people, give or take, and the nation of Veyl removed from the atlases within a decade, its survivors scattered or absorbed or quietly dead, its archives sealed and then lost — until a bush pilot pulled a bronze-bound chest out of a gravel bar and ruined everyone's tidy arrangements.

Corvane did not think of it as murder. Murder implied passion, and passion implied risk. He thought of it as accounting. The Veyl government had run the numbers and decided forty thousand lives cost less than prosecution. The syndicate had run different numbers and decided that a hidden valley cost less than the resources sitting under it. The arithmetic was identical. Only the bookkeeping style differed.

"Contact ahead," Vess called from the bow. "Campfire smoke. Fresh."

They came around a bluff and found it: a cold camp on a gravel shelf, fire ringed with stones, the ashes still warm when Vess dismounted to check. Four bedrolls. Boot prints — one set large, one set small, two sets in between. And pressed into the mud at the waterline, unmistakable, the twin keel-marks of a river boat drawn up and launched again.

"They portaged around something upstream," Vess said. "A day ahead. Maybe less."

"The guide knows this water," Corvane said. Emeric Sable — or whatever his true name was; the dossier on that was thin, which meant it was interesting. "He'll run them fast and quiet. He'll avoid the villages."

"Why avoid the villages?"

"Because he's smart, and smart fugitives know that strangers bring trouble to anyone who feeds them." Corvane smiled at the canyon walls. "Which is exactly why we're going to visit every village between here and the source. Every fish camp, every netting station, every family drying char on racks. We'll pay for supplies at triple rate. We'll ask questions politely. We'll leave medicine and salt as gifts."

Vess frowned — the closest thing to open confusion Vess ever produced. "We're funding goodwill for our enemies' route?"

"We're building a bell rope, Vess. Every person we kindness today will feel obliged tomorrow. When we come back down this river asking where four travelers passed, they'll tell us — not because we threaten them, but because we were *decent*, and decency is a debt people repay even to strangers." He turned back toward the boats. "And if decency fails anywhere along the line, then we escalate. But always decency first. Fear makes people lie. Obligation makes them honest."

It was, Corvane reflected, almost a shame that Wrenn would never appreciate the methodology. The archivist understood systems. He would have admired this one, right up until it closed around him.

* * *

The village was called Sennet Ford, though no sign said so; the name existed only in the mouths of the people who lived there, thirty-one souls by Vess's count, clustered on a bluff above a wide gravel shallows where the river braided and slowed.

Corvane went ashore alone.

This was theater, and he knew it, and he performed it anyway because theater worked. One man, unarmed, hands visible, walking up the bank path between the drying racks while four gunboats sat offshore with their engines idling low. Behind him, visibly, Vess and two crew waited at the boats — near enough to intervene, far enough to seem respectful.

The woman from the first sighting — she had apparently traveled ahead of them along the bank roads, or word had; these river communities were one long conversation — met him at the top of the path. She was sixty-odd, weathered to the color of the bluffs, with a fishing knife at her belt that she made no move to touch and no move to hide.

"You're not the first boat looking for people this season," she said.

"I imagine not." Corvane inclined his head. "Madam — I'm sorry, I don't know the courteous form of address here."

"There isn't one. You say what you want or you go."

"Then plainly: I'm looking for four travelers. A pilot, an older man, a young woman, a river guide. They have a chest with them. Bronze fittings. They passed this way, I believe, within the last two days."

"And if they did?"

"Then I'd like to know which fork of the channel they took at the shallows."

The woman studied him with the unhurried assessment of someone who had gutted ten thousand fish and could read men the same way — by what was inside, and how long it had been dead. "You're the third party asking after them," she said. "First was a trader, month back, wanting to know if anyone spoke Veyli. Second was a surveyor, wanting to buy land rights nobody owns. Now you." Her eyes didn't move from his face. "River says: when three wolves follow one trail, the trail leads somewhere the wolves shouldn't go."

"That's very poetic."

"It's very true." She shifted her weight. "I'll tell you what I told the others. Travelers pass. I don't track them. I don't sell them. This ford belongs to the fish and to us, and neither of us answers questions from boats."

Corvane nodded slowly, as if absorbing a wise defeat. Then he said, gently: "How is old Maretta's cough this season? And the Ferro boy — the one who fell through the ice last winter? Has his lung healed, or does he still rattle when he runs?"

The stillness that came over the woman was total. Even the wind seemed to step around her.

"Medicine," Corvane continued, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates. "Broad-spectrum antibiotic, lung salve, fever powder. Enough for your whole village through the winter. It's aboard my boat. It costs nothing. It requires nothing. I mention it only because I noticed, coming up the path, that your youngest has a barking cough, and winters on this river kill children every year, and I find that wasteful." He reached into his coat — slowly, letting her see every movement — and drew out not a weapon but a small tin, which he set on the fence post between them. "A taste. There's a great deal more where this came from."

"We don't take gifts from wolves."

"Of course not. Wolves don't offer them." He stepped back. "Keep the tin regardless. Think about the rest. I'll be moored below the ford until morning. If anyone remembers anything about four travelers — which channel, which night, whether the guide bought rope or lamp oil — send word down, and the whole winter's medicine comes up to Sennet Ford by noon." He bowed slightly. "If no one remembers anything, that's fine too. Children cough either way."

He walked back down the path, unhurried, feeling her eyes between his shoulder blades like a blade laid flat.

Vess fell into step beside him at the boats. "She won't talk."

"She'll talk."

"People like that don't break."

"They don't break, Vess. They *bend* — around their children." Corvane stepped into the launch. "I didn't threaten anyone. I offered medicine. By morning, half the village will hate me and half will look at that tin and think about the Ferro boy's lungs, and the half that thinks will win, because hope is louder than pride in places where children die in January. Someone will remember something. Someone always remembers something, once remembering becomes an act of love instead of betrayal."

Vess was quiet for a long moment as the launch pulled away. "Does it bother you," Vess said at last, "doing it this way?"

And there it was — the question Corvane had been asked, in various forms, across twenty years and three employers. He gave it the consideration it deserved, which was real, because he genuinely liked Vess and lying to people you liked was poor economy.

"No," he said. "It doesn't bother me. Because I've done the other way — the burning, the breaking, the bodies in the reeds — early on, when I was stupid and impatient. And do you know what I learned? The other way costs more. Bodies create investigators. Fires create legends. Legends create martyrs, and martyrs create movements, and movements cost ten times what patience costs." He looked back at Sennet Ford, at the smoke rising straight and thin from the chimneys, at the children he could hear faintly shrieking at play on the bluff. "I am not a cruel man, Vess. I'm a cheap one. Cruelty is expensive. This—" he gestured at the tin on the post, shrinking behind them, "—this is the cheapest thing on the river."

Ahead, the canyon walls leaned closer, and the green water quickened, and somewhere beyond the next bend a chest of drowned paper was moving toward a valley that owed Corvane nothing and would soon owe him everything.

* * *

Morning brought the answer, exactly as predicted.

Not from the old woman — from a boy of maybe twelve, who paddled a dugout out to the moored boats before sunrise, alone, wrapped in a blanket, with the medicine tin clutched in one fist like a talisman. He wouldn't come aboard. He stood off at ten feet and shouted across the water, voice cracking with fear and resolve in equal measure.

"The big man and the girl bought rope at the ford," he called. "Two nights back. The guide paid in silver, real silver, and the old woman said don't tell nobody, but Maretta's coughing blood now and Mama said—" His voice failed, steadied. "Mama said the boy's lungs matter more than strangers. They took the north channel. Toward the singing rocks. The guide told the old woman they'd be at the bone canyon in three days if the weather holds."

Then he paddled away fast, before Corvane could thank him, which was wise, because Corvane would have given him a second gift, and gifts were hooks, and the boy had enough hooks in him already.

Corvane stood at the rail and did the arithmetic.

Three days to the bone canyon — the portage route, if his reading of Wrenn's stolen files was right, the last stretch where the refugees of Veyl had walked into the winter and never walked out. After that, the song-country, the unmapped water, the gate. The fugitives had a guide who knew the river and possibly more. Corvane had gunboats, forty crew, a bribed surveyor, and a winter's worth of medicine deployed along two hundred miles of banks like lamps lit in windows.

They couldn't outrun him anymore. The falls had seen to that; there was no going back down. Ahead of them lay only the source, and the source was a bottle-neck, and bottles exist to be corked.

But there was a complication, and he examined it honestly, because self-deception was the one luxury he never permitted himself.

The villagers would remember. All of them, up and down the river — the tins on the posts, the boy in the dugout, the old woman's silence purchased with a child's lungs. When this was finished, when the valley was surveyed and claimed and the archive was either in syndicate hands or ash, there would be a string of riverside communities who knew exactly what their winter's medicine had bought. Some would keep quiet forever. Some would talk. And one of them, someday, would write it down — the way a disgraced archivist had once written down the grain quotas — and the ledger would tip again, the way ledgers always tipped, and someone like Corvane would be running numbers on the far side of it.

He found, examining the thought, that it didn't frighten him. It merely interested him, the way weather interests a farmer. Every system had its audit. You simply tried to be finished before the auditors arrived.

"Set course for the singing rocks," he told Vess. "Full steam. And signal the *Cormorant* and the *Petrel* — I want a landing party ready at each village ahead. Small parties. Polite ones. Tins and questions."

"And if the polite ones stop working?"

Corvane watched the mist burn off the canyon walls, revealing, high on the southern cliff, a streak of ancient red ochre — a handprint, huge, faded, painted by someone whose nation no longer appeared on maps.

"Then we take hostages," he said. "Starting with Sennet Ford. Put the old woman and the Ferro boy aboard the *Petrel*. Send word upriver: the chest for the child. Simple terms. Everyone understands a child." He turned for the wheelhouse. "Wrenn will surrender it. He's a sentimental man — sentiment is just another kind of arithmetic, and his always counts other people heavier than himself. That's his weakness."

"And yours?" Vess asked — not insolently; Vess never asked anything insolently, which was why the question landed like a blade laid flat.

Corvane paused at the wheelhouse door, and for a moment — a genuine moment, rare as the ochre handprint — he considered the truth: that he had no one, that his ledger counted only himself, that this was called strength by some and emptiness by others and he had stopped deciding which, years ago.

"My weakness," he said, "is assuming everyone keeps books."

He went inside and gave the order, and the gunboats surged up the green water toward the singing rocks, and behind them, on the bluff at Sennet Ford, a twelve-year-old boy watched them go and hid the medicine tin under the floorboards, next to a knife, the way people do.
