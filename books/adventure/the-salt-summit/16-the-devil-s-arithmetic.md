## 16. The Devil's Arithmetic

The message came up the river at dusk, tied to a gull's leg like something out of an old story, except the paper was syndicate stock and the handwriting was Corvane's — neat, unhurried, the penmanship of a man who had never once been interrupted.

*Three hostages from Ferrow's Landing. The miller's daughter among them. Bring the chest and the archivist to the lower falls by midday tomorrow, or I begin returning them to you one piece at a time.*

Rosalind read it twice by the light of the camp stove, then folded it along its creases until it was small enough to disappear inside her fist.

Behind her, Nia was teaching Tobias the second verse of the song-map, her voice low and careful, the way you handle something that might still be alive. Emeric stood at the edge of the firelight with his back to all of them, watching the black water slide past, and Rosalind understood without asking that he was doing arithmetic of his own — counting gunboats, counting hours, counting what his signal at the toll bridge had bought and what it had cost.

She let the note burn in the stove before anyone else could see it. That was cowardice, maybe. Or mercy. She'd stopped being sure which was which somewhere above the last navigable water.

*Rosalind.* She could hear her own voice in her head, flat and practical, the voice she used for fuel calculations and weather calls. *You have a plane that won't fly, four people who can't outrun a gunboat, and one asset Corvane wants more than he wants anything else on this river. Do the math.*

She had always been good at math.

* * *

She waited until the fire had burned down to coals and Nia had finally gone to sleep under the overturned canoe, one hand curled around the satchel of dried archive pages like they were a stuffed animal. Then she called Tobias and Emeric close and laid it out the way she laid out flight plans: clean, direct, no sentiment to snag on.

"Corvane wants two things," she said. "The chest, and the man who can read it. He doesn't particularly need me, and he doesn't know about Nia, and if we're lucky he doesn't fully trust Emeric yet. So." She drew a line in the ash with one finger. "Tomorrow I take the chest down to the lower falls. Alone. I give him what he wants, I keep him talking, and while he's busy cataloguing me, the three of you run the source route with the song-map. By the time he figures out the archive is copied and the valley is real, you're behind a gate no chart shows."

The silence lasted long enough for the river to say something.

"No," Tobias said.

"It's not a vote."

"Then it's not a discussion either." He leaned forward, and the firelight caught the gray in his beard and the fury in his eyes, and for a moment she saw not the rumpled, apologetic archivist she'd fished out of Lowmark but whatever he had been before the disgrace — a man who had spent thirty years guarding truth against exactly this kind of transaction. "You're proposing to hand yourself to a man who takes hostages. Who wrote *one piece at a time* and meant it. You've done the arithmetic, have you? What's your exchange rate? One pilot for three hostages? And then what happens when he decides he needs a pilot after all, to fly him to the gate?"

"He won't need me once he has the chest."

"The chest is paper, Rosalind. Paper he already knows is copied — because I watched you copy every page, and so did his scout at the toll crossing, or did you think Emeric's friends out there are blind?" His voice cracked on the last word and he didn't apologize for it. "He'll take you, take the chest, take your copies, and then he'll follow your friends' trail upstream anyway, and you'll have died buying nothing but a day."

"I'm not planning to die."

"Nobody plans to. That's what makes it arithmetic and not suicide — the numbers look survivable right up until they aren't."

Emeric hadn't moved from the edge of the light. Now he spoke without turning around. "She's wrong," he said quietly, "but not for the reason Wrenn thinks."

Rosalind looked at him.

"You go down there alone, Corvane doesn't even negotiate. Why would he? One woman, one chest, no leverage left on her side." He turned then, and his face was unreadable in the way it always was, the face of a man who had practiced unreadability since childhood. "But if we all come down together — if he sees the archivist, the girl, the guide, the whole bundle — then he has to split his force to guard us all, and splitting a force on a riverbank at night is how hired crews die. That's the only version where any of us walks away."

"That's insane," Tobias said.

"It's river logic," Emeric said. "You don't fight the current. You use it."

Rosalind stared at the ash line she'd drawn, the neat little plan already crumbling, and felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. All her life she had measured risk the same way she measured everything — in units of one. One plane. One pilot. One body between the gauges and the ground. It had kept her alive through twenty years of weather that killed better-funded men, because a fleet of one has nothing to lose and nothing to coordinate.

But sitting here, watching Tobias's jaw set and Emeric's eyes flick toward the canoe where Nia slept, she realized the equation had changed underneath her. She wasn't a fleet of one anymore. She was responsible for people who would refuse to be spent as currency — and who, God help her, would refuse to spend *her*.

That was the terrifying part. Not dying. Being *worth something* to someone.

"All right," she said slowly. "River logic. Nobody goes alone. So we don't give him a surrender — we give him a *bargain*, and we make it expensive enough that he has to listen." She pulled the map case over, unrolled it across her knees, and weighted the corners with stones. "Here's what we actually have. We have the only route past the falls that isn't on any chart. We have a radio in my plane that might fly again if the alternator holds. And we have the one thing Corvane can't buy, counterfeit, or shoot: the truth, read aloud, in a voice every gunboat crew on this river can hear."

Tobias went very still. "You want to broadcast."

"I want to make his own crews do our fighting for us. Hired men stay hired as long as they don't know what they're hauling guns toward. The moment they hear that Veyl was sold out by its own government — that their employer is following the same playbook into a valley full of living descendants — some of them walk. Maybe most of them. Corvane runs on plausible deniability, and a broadcast burns deniability like kerosene."

"And if it doesn't work?" Tobias asked. "If they're professionals enough to ignore it?"

"Then we've lost nothing but an evening and a little dignity." She looked at Emeric. "Can you get the plane airworthy by dawn? Just enough. Straight up, straight back, no maneuvers."

Emeric considered the dark water. "The alternator's cracked. She'll run for maybe forty minutes off the battery before the radio dies. After that she glides."

"Forty minutes is a broadcast and a half." She turned to Tobias. "I need the ledger. The worst pages. Names, dates, tonnage — the grain quota, the sale, the blame. Read it to me until I can recite it in my sleep, because I'll be reading it live with a gunboat on my tail and no second take."

Tobias looked at her for a long moment, and something in his expression eased — not forgiveness exactly, but recognition. Two people who had both spent years keeping things safe by keeping them hidden, arriving at the same conclusion from opposite directions.

"You understand," he said, "that once you say those names aloud, there's no taking them back. Not for you. Not for any of us. The syndicate will never stop hunting the people who broadcast this."

"I've been hunted since the toll bridge," Rosalind said. "I'd rather be hunted for something I said than something I found."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Tobias fed her the ledger page by page, translating the old Veyli script into plain speech, and Rosalind repeated each passage back until the words stopped sounding like history and started sounding like ammunition. *In the winter of the third failure, the Ministry of Provision sold nine thousand tons of reserved grain to the coastal combine at Harrow Point, recording the shipment as flood loss. Signatories: Deputy Minister Kel Sarran; Comptroller Ives Odell; countersigned, the seal of the Chancellor's office.* Nine thousand tons. She made herself feel the number — made herself picture the miller's daughter from Ferrow's Landing, held right now in a syndicate boat, and imagine her grandmother hungry in a drowned city while men with seals signed papers.

Grief curdled into purpose. Purpose hardened into a plan.

Nia woke near midnight and refused to go back to sleep, so they gave her a job: she sat cross-legged by the fire with the song-map verses spread across her knees, timing the route — how long the portage would take, when Emeric should have the others at the canyon mouth relative to Rosalind's broadcast window. She worked with a fierce, silent competence that made Rosalind's throat tight. Sixteen years old, orphaned by patience, carrying a dead woman's memory in her mouth like a lantern.

At some point Nia looked up and caught Rosalind watching her.

"You were going to leave us at the falls," Nia said. Not accusing. Just checking the math.

"Yes."

"What changed?"

Rosalind thought about lying, decided the night was too short for it. "I used to count everything in miles," she said. "Fuel range, river bends, days to the next safe landing. Miles don't ask anything of you. People do." She nodded at the ledger pages drying on the rocks. "Somewhere between the burned grain and the gull with the note, I switched currencies. Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

Nia almost smiled. Almost. Then she went back to her verses, and the fire popped, and the river kept saying the only thing it ever says.

Dawn came up gray and hard. Emeric had the plane's cowling open and closed twice, swore at the alternator in two languages, and pronounced her *flying, barely, don't bank*. They loaded the chest — the real one, because Corvane would weigh it, count its bronze corners, and accept no replica — and Rosalind stowed the copied archive in a waterproof roll strapped against her ribs, because a bargain is only leverage if both sides believe you can still walk away.

They gathered at the water's edge, and for a moment nobody spoke, and Rosalind understood that this was the moment the whole chapter of her life turned on. She could still give orders. She was still, nominally, the one with the plane.

Instead she said: "Canyon mouth by midday. If you hear the broadcast start, move faster. If you hear it stop—" She stopped herself. Recalculated. "Don't wait to find out why. Nia sings them through the gate. That's the mission. Not me."

Tobias gripped her shoulder, hard, once — an archivist's handshake, sealing a record. Emeric touched two fingers to his brow in something that wasn't quite a salute and wasn't quite a prayer. Nia hugged her quickly and fiercely, around the ribs, where the archive rode, and whispered, "Come find us. Grandmother said the source answers. Make yourself an answer."

Rosalind climbed into the plane, ran the checks with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect, and lifted off the gray water into the gray morning, the Kessari unspooling below her like a sentence she was finally ready to finish.

Ahead, at the lower falls, three gunboats sat anchored in the mist like punctuation.

She keyed the radio, checked the battery gauge — thirty-eight minutes — and began to speak.

"In the winter of the third failure," she said, to every crew, every village, every ear within a hundred miles of the basin, "the Ministry of Provision sold nine thousand tons of reserved grain—"

And far below, at the canyon mouth, four small figures slipped into the current and ran for the gate.
