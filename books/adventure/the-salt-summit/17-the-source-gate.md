## 17. The Source Gate

The song had a shape.

Nia had known that since she was seven years old, sitting on her grandmother's narrow bed in Lowmark while the harbor fog pressed against the window like something wanting in. The verses weren't just words — they were instructions wearing the clothes of a lullaby. *Where the river forgets its name, count three shadows and trust the fourth.* She'd thought it was nonsense for children, the way old women hum nonsense to keep the quiet from getting too loud.

Now she stood on a shelf of wet stone above the upper Kessari, and the river below them did not so much flow as *hesitate*, pooling green and glass-still before it committed itself to the falls ahead, and she understood that every syllable of the song had been waiting sixty years for someone to stand exactly here and sing it back.

"Say it again," Emeric said quietly. He'd been quiet all morning — quieter than even his usual careful quiet, which Nia was learning meant he was feeling things too large to pass as anyone else.

"*Where the water runs backward and the stone drinks light,*" Nia sang, low, half under her breath. Her grandmother's accent lived in her mouth better than it lived anywhere else; when she sang Veyli, she could feel the old woman's hand resting between her shoulder blades. "*Count three shadows and trust the fourth.*"

Rosalind shaded her eyes against the flat white glare off the canyon walls. "I count four shadows."

"That's the point." Tobias crouched at the shelf's edge, his satchel clutched to his chest like a child he refused to set down. He pointed with his chin. "Look. Three of those spires throw shadows east with the sun. The fourth—" He stopped, and Nia watched him do the thing he did, the archivist's thing, where his whole face went still and hungry at once. "The fourth spire is hollow. Or split. Its shadow isn't cast by anything solid."

They picked their way along the shelf toward it, boots scraping on stone still slick from yesterday's meltwater. The canyon narrowed here until Nia could have thrown a stone from one wall to the other, and the walls rose so sheer and so high that the sky became a ribbon of blue overhead, like a seam left open by accident. The air smelled different. Colder, but also greener — moss and pine and something mineral, like the inside of a cave that had decided to grow things.

The fourth shadow fell across a section of wall that looked, at first glance, like every other section: gray granite, streaked with rust-colored runoff, patched with lichen the color of old coins.

Except the lichen grew in lines.

Nia saw it before the others did — maybe because she'd spent her whole childhood looking at patterns her grandmother drew in spilled tea, or maybe because the song had already told her what to see. The lichen wasn't random. It followed grooves cut into the rock, deliberate grooves, and the grooves formed characters. Veyli characters. Dozens of them, worn nearly smooth, running floor to ceiling in columns.

"It's a door," she said. Her voice came out strange in the narrow canyon, flattened by stone. "It's been here the whole time, and everyone who ever came up this river walked right past it."

"Not everyone walked past," Emeric murmured. He had his hand flat against the wall, fingers tracing a groove, and his expression was one Nia had never seen on him — naked, unguarded, the two-names man stripped down to whichever one was real. "My mother used to draw these. In the dust. In flour on the table. I thought she was just... remembering out loud." His jaw worked. "She was writing home."

Tobias was already reading, lips moving, translating in that rapid mutter Nia found oddly comforting now. "'Gate of the Kept River. Speak, and be counted.'" He looked up. "It wants an answer. A passphrase."

"A song," Nia said. "It wants the song."

* * *

They tried Tobias first, because he insisted, and because none of them wanted to admit they were all thinking the same thing: that if this went wrong, it should go wrong on the expendable one. He sang the opening verse in his careful scholar's Veyli, pitch-perfect and bloodless, and the wall did nothing at all. The lichen didn't shift. The grooves didn't glow. The canyon held its breath and gave them back silence.

"Maybe it's broken," Rosalind said. "Sixty years—"

"It's not broken." Nia's heart was going hard enough that she could feel it in her throat. "You sang it like a document. My grandmother said the gate doesn't want the words. It wants—" She searched for it, the memory surfacing whole: her grandmother's hands folded in her lap, eyes closed, singing the third verse not as instruction but as *grief*, each line falling away from her like something she'd carried too long. "It wants you to mean it. It's not a lock. It's a question."

"And the answer?" Tobias asked gently.

Nia looked at the wall. At the worn characters that some mason had cut into living stone before her grandmother was born, trusting that someone, someday, would come needing them.

"The answer is why you came."

She stepped forward before she could talk herself out of it. The stone was cold through her sleeves where she braced her palm against it. And she sang.

She sang the third verse the way her grandmother had sung it — not the route-encoding lines, not the counting-shadows verse, but the one after, the one Nia had always assumed was just sentiment:

*O river that kept our names when no one else would,*
*we were not drowned, we were planted.*
*Open for the ones who carry us.*
*Close forever to the ones who sold us.*

Her voice cracked on *planted*. She let it crack. That was the meaning; that was the whole trick of it, she realized — the gate wasn't listening for accuracy, it was listening for the break, the place where the song stopped being information and became inheritance.

For three heartbeats, nothing.

Then the lichen shivered — actually shivered, like a sleeping animal disturbed — and somewhere deep inside the cliff face, counterweights moved. Stone ground against stone, a sound so vast and so low Nia felt it in her teeth, in the soles of her feet, in the water pooled at their boots. A seam appeared in the wall, hairline-fine, then widened. The section of granite swung inward — impossibly, silently smooth once it started — and warm air breathed out over them, air that smelled of woodsmoke and bread and growing things.

Beyond the gate: darkness, then lantern-light, then figures.

A lot of figures.

* * *

There were perhaps thirty of them standing in the passage and the chamber beyond, and every one of them held a bow drawn to full anchor.

Nia had time to register details with a terrible, floating clarity: the bows were recurved, horn and sinew, beautiful and absolutely functional. The arrows were aimed at Rosalind, at Tobias, at Emeric — at her. The people holding them wore wool dyed madder-red and river-slate blue, colors Nia knew from a single moth-eaten scarf in her grandmother's trunk. Their faces were brown and pale and everything between, and their eyes held the specific wariness of people who had survived something by deciding never to be surprised again.

An older woman stepped forward. Silver-braided hair, a burn scar down one forearm, a voice like gravel wrapped in velvet.

"You sing my grandmother's verse," she said in Veyli. Then, seeing their faces, she switched to trade-tongue, accented but fluent. "So. Sixty years, and the gate opens for a child. Tell me why we should not put arrows in your friends and keep you."

"We're not with the syndicate," Rosalind said. Her hands were visible, open, empty — Nia noticed she'd positioned herself slightly in front of Tobias without seeming to move at all. "We found the archive. The chest from the river. We know what your government did — the grain quota, the ledger, all of it. We came to bring it home."

"Home." The woman tasted the word. "Strangers use that word like a knife."

"He reads watermarks," Nia said, and immediately felt foolish, and then didn't, because the woman's gaze snapped to Tobias with sudden intensity.

"Wrenn," Tobias said. "Royal Archive, before — before the end. I was nineteen when it fell. I've spent thirty years trying to prove what happened to Veyl, and everyone called me a madman, and then this girl's grandmother's map led us up a river that isn't on any chart, and—" His voice failed him, which Nia had never once heard happen. "You're alive. There are *children*." He was looking past the bowmen now, into the lamplit chamber, where Nia could see hanging herbs, stacked firewood, a loom, the unmistakable clutter of lives being lived. "All this time, you were alive."

"Alive is a strong word," the woman said. "We are *kept*. There is a difference, and the difference cost us everything we were." She raised two fingers, and every bow lowered as one — though Nia noted the arrows stayed nocked. "I am Sefa Vane-Kessari. Keeper of the Gate. You will come inside. You will eat. And you will tell us everything, slowly, starting with how you found us — because if the syndicate's gunboats can find this valley, then everything my mother's generation bled for ends this winter."

"They can't find it," Emeric said. "Not yet." His voice was steady, but Nia caught the flinch under it, the guilt moving behind his eyes like a fish under ice. "But they're close. Closer than you think."

Sefa Vane-Kessari studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her mouth thin.

"Inside," she repeated. "All of you. And you—" her eyes found Nia's, and something in them shifted, softened by a single dangerous degree, "—you will sit at my table. You sing like your people. I would know whose granddaughter taught you."

Nia's throat closed. *Your people.* Two words, and they landed in her chest like a lit coal — warm and burning and terrifying, because she had spent sixteen years being nobody's, being the harbor kid with the dead grandmother and the foreign lullaby, and now a stranger with a burn scar and her grandmother's vowels was offering her a place at a table inside a mountain, and offering felt almost exactly like claiming.

"I—" she started.

"Nia." Rosalind's voice, quiet, from beside her. Not warning. Just her name, spoken the way Rosalind said few things: plainly, and meaning it.

Nia looked at the open gate. At the warmth breathing out of it. At the bows, still strung, still ready, belonging to people who had survived by closing.

And she heard herself say, in Veyli, in her grandmother's exact cadence: "I'll come in. But I'm not staying forever, and neither are they. We came to open the truth, not to hide inside it."

Silence. Somewhere deeper in the valley — the true valley, beyond the passage — water fell, unhurried, indifferent.

Sefa Vane-Kessari smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, but it was an honest one.

"Good," she said. "Because the elders have been arguing about exactly that for three days. And now, child, you can settle it." She turned, gesturing them through the gate, and added over her shoulder, words that dropped into Nia's stomach like a stone into still water:

"One of our scouts returned this morning. There are gunboats at the lower falls — and they have taken hostages at Millbrook Landing to make us trade."
