## 19. The Battle of the Gate

The transmitter smelled of cold iron and old varnish, like a church that had been used to store fish. Rosalind stood before it with her hands flat on its brass housing and felt the hum of it through her palms — a low, patient vibration, the sound of something that had waited sixty years in the dark for someone to speak into it.

Behind her, Tobias was reading.

He had been reading for two hours, his voice gone rough as rope, working his way down the burned ledger's columns while the elders' technicians — three women with silver-streaked braids and hands steady as surgeons' — tuned the array on the canyon wall above the gate. Each name he read out was another grain quota sold, another shipment diverted, another village written off in a clerk's neat hand while the government of Veyl told the world its people were starving because the river had failed them.

"—and here," Tobias said, tapping the page with a finger that trembled only slightly, "the entry dated the ninth of Thawmonth. *Transferred to reserve account, per directive of the Interior Council. Note: distribution suspension approved. The river will be blamed.*" He looked up at Rosalind. "They wrote it down. They actually wrote it down. Sixty years I've argued with men who claimed no record could survive the flood, and they wrote it down themselves."

"People always do," said one of the braided women, not looking up from her dials. "Guilt wants witnesses."

Rosalind turned back to the transmitter. Through the great slit window behind it she could see the source canyon falling away below — the white thread of the river, the terraced green of the valley floor, and far downstream, where the gorge narrowed at the Gate, the first pale smudge of dust rising off the water.

Corvane's gunboats. Three of them, maybe four. Emeric had counted masts through the elder's spyglass an hour ago and come away grim-faced, saying nothing for a long time, which from Emeric was worse than swearing.

She found him now at the edge of the terrace, talking low and fast with a circle of valley defenders — lean men and women in oiled wool, carrying bows and long-handled hooks that looked ceremonial until you noticed the wear on the grips. He had a stick and was drawing in the frost-scoured stone: the canyon's bends, the narrows, the shelf of rock where the falls thundered down past the hidden gate.

"They'll run the narrows in single file," Emeric was saying. "Corvane doesn't trust his crews enough to split them, and he can't afford to wait — every day up here costs him money and gives the valley time. So he'll push. That's his strength and it's going to kill him." He jabbed the stick at a bend upstream of the Gate. "Here. The S-Curve. Current runs against the north wall, fifteen feet deep, then shelves to four feet over gravel. A gunboat drawing six feet hits that shelf at speed and she broaches sideways. Blocks the channel. Every boat behind her piles up."

"And your people will make sure they hit it," Rosalind said.

The eldest of the defenders — a broad-shouldered man the others called Oren — shook his head slowly. "We can drop stone from the rim. We've done it before, against surveyors, against poachers. But these are warboats with mounted guns, and if we start dropping rock, they start shooting up the cliff face until they find our positions. People die. Maybe many people." His eyes went to Rosalind, level and unhostile. "You brought this war to our door, pilot. I'd hear what else you brought with it."

It wasn't fair, and it was entirely fair, and Rosalind had learned somewhere over twenty years of solo flying that the two often arrived in the same envelope. She looked at the drawing in the stone, at the S-Curve, at the narrows, and felt the old familiar arithmetic start up in her head — the one where everything was measured in fuel and distance and how much margin she could afford to burn.

Except the arithmetic had changed. Somewhere between Lowmark and the whiteout, between a stowaway's stubborn heartbeat and an archivist's ruined voice, the units had shifted. She wasn't counting miles anymore. She was counting people.

"The broadcast," she said. "That's what else I brought."

Oren frowned. "Your archivist has been reading names all morning. To whom? There is no one to hear."

"There's everyone to hear." She pointed downstream, toward the basin, toward Lowmark and the river towns and the syndicate's whole floating economy. "Every gunboat crew Corvane hired came out of those towns. Their brothers fish the Kessari. Their mothers remember Veyl. Corvane told them this was recovery archaeology — salvage rights, hazard pay, a legal expedition. He didn't tell them they were being sailed upriver to seize a living valley full of descendants of people their own grandparents watched drown." She tapped the transmitter. "When we put the ledger on open frequency — every band, full power — every crew on that river hears exactly what they're crewing for. And Corvane's strength stops being his strength too."

Emeric was already nodding, slow and hard. "Hired guns fight for pay. They don't die for it. Corvane knows that better than anyone — it's why he keeps hostages instead of taking risks himself." He looked at Oren. "Give her your power. Give her the morning. If the broadcast fails, we still have the S-Curve and the rim stones. But if it works—"

"If it works," Oren said, "we may not need to spill blood at all." He did not sound like a man who believed it. But he signaled, and the braided women began pulling breakers, and the transmitter's hum climbed a note into something like a voice clearing its throat.

* * *

Nia found Rosalind at the microphone.

The girl had been quiet since dawn — quieter than even the whiteout days, when quiet had been survival. Now she stood beside the transmitter with her grandmother's song folded inside her like a coal banked under ash, and when she spoke, her voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second.

"You're really going to say it. All of it. Out loud. To everyone."

"All of it," Rosalind said. "Out loud. To everyone."

"They might come for the valley anyway. Even after they know."

"Maybe." Rosalind checked the frequency dials, the power gauges, the ancient antenna feed running up the canyon wall on porcelain insulators older than both of them. "But there's a difference between coming for a secret and coming for a truth everybody already owns. Secrets can be stolen. Truth just sits there, and you have to live with it." She glanced sideways. "Besides — you're going to help."

Nia blinked. "Me?"

"Last verse of the song-map. The one about the gate opening for the ones who remember. Your grandmother sang it so it would survive. This—" Rosalind touched the microphone gently, the way she'd touch a control yoke in turbulence, "—this is how it survives louder."

Something moved through the girl's face — fear, and pride wrestling it to a draw. She took the second microphone, the one the braided women had rigged for translation, and gripped it with both hands like a rail in a storm.

Downstream, the dust plume had thickened. Somewhere below the horizon of the gorge, engines were idling, and men were checking guns, and a reasonable man named Corvane was telling them all it would be easy.

Rosalind keyed the transmitter.

"This is an open broadcast on all bands," she said, and heard her own voice come back off the canyon walls, multiplied, enormous. "This is Rosalind Oyelaran, speaking from the source of the Kessari. What follows is the official grain ledger of the nation of Veyl, recovered intact. It is not a story. It is not a legend. It is a record, in the government's own hand, of how Veyl starved."

She nodded to Tobias, and Tobias began to read, and the canyon carried him to the world.

* * *

At the S-Curve, the lead gunboat's captain heard it first.

His name was Doss Havel, and he had signed with the Meridian Salvage Consortium for the same reason most of his crew had: the pay was triple river-freight rates, the work sounded like treasure hunting, and a man with two daughters in Lowmark couldn't afford principles. He stood on his wheelhouse deck in the gray morning light, eleven miles below the Gate, and listened to a dead government confess its crimes through the speaker of his own radio set — a set Corvane's technicians had installed and sealed, supposedly for command traffic only.

Someone had unsealed every set on the flotilla. Later, Doss would learn it was two of Corvane's own riggers, boys from the delta towns who had heard the first ten minutes of the ledger read from a relay mast at the falls camp and decided, quietly, without discussion, to cut the locks.

On the foredeck, his gunner — a boy of nineteen who had never fired the mount at anything but driftwood — lowered his hands from the weapon and just stood there listening.

"*—distribution suspension approved. The river will be blamed,*" the radio said, in a tired academic voice that made each word sound like it weighed something. "*Note appended by the Interior Council, session forty-one: public messaging to attribute shortfall to hydrological failure. No inquiry to be commissioned.*"

Doss looked downstream, toward the Gate, toward the hidden valley that was supposed to hold salvage and instead held people — children on the terraces, smoke from morning fires, a whole town that had done nothing to anyone except survive.

"Skipper," his engineer said from the hatch, face pale. "There's more boats stopping. Marlowe's dropped anchor. The Vesper's turning around."

Ahead of them, at the mouth of the narrows, the second gunboat was indeed swinging, her wake curling wrong, her captain's voice crackling over the fleet channel: *"—not doing this. You hear me, Corvane? Not against civilians, not on a lie, not for you—"*

And then Corvane's voice came onto the channel, smooth as oil on water, and Doss felt the whole fragile morning tilt.

* * *

"Gentlemen." Corvane sat in the cabin of the flagship *Meridian Rose* with a cup of tea he had no intention of drinking, and spoke into the fleet radio the way he spoke into boardrooms: reasonably, patiently, as though the entire mutiny were a clerical error he was prepared to forgive. "I understand the broadcast is unsettling. It was designed to be. Propaganda always is."

"It's a ledger," someone said. "It's got dates and signatures."

"So does a forgery." He crossed one leg over the other. "Consider: you are eleven miles from the richest archaeological site in the hemisphere. Consider your contracts — and consider that breach clauses are enforced. Consider, finally, that whatever that woman claims, the consortium's position has not changed, and my patience, gentlemen, is the only thing standing between this expedition and a very different kind of management."

There was a silence on the channel. Corvane sipped his tea and let it work. Fear was slower than truth but heavier; given time, it always settled to the bottom of a man's chest and stayed there.

What he did not account for — what he had never once accounted for in a long career of pricing other people — was the sound of Nia's voice coming onto the same channel.

* * *

She almost couldn't do it. Her hands shook so badly the braided woman had to steady the microphone stand. But Rosalind stood beside her, and Tobias stood behind her, and somewhere in her chest her grandmother's voice rose up through sixty years of silence and said *now, little bird, now*, and Nia leaned into the microphone and began to sing.

The last verse of the song-map. Not the route this time — the promise. The verse her grandmother had saved for the end of every singing, the one in old Veyli that the valley's own defenders, hearing it relayed over the open channel, recognized with a shock that ran through the whole canyon like current through wire.

The gunboat crews heard a girl's voice singing a language half of them had heard their grandmothers use for lullabies. The valley heard a child outside the walls carry the memory home. And Corvane heard — clearly, unmistakably — the exact thing he had spent his career ensuring no one ever possessed: something money could not buy back once it was given away.

"Who is that?" he demanded on the channel. "Cut that transmission. Cut it now—"

But the riggers had already pulled the relay's fuses on his own flagship's external array, and the flagship could jam nothing but itself, and the song went rolling down the Kessari like weather.

In the wheelhouse of the lead boat, Doss Havel set down his coffee, walked out onto the foredeck, and threw the firing pin from his gun mount into the river. It was not a dramatic gesture. It was bookkeeping — the closing of an account he should never have opened.

"Turn us around," he told his engineer. "All ahead slow. And get me the fleet channel. There's some things I want to say to Mr. Corvane off the record."

* * *

By noon it was less a battle than a dissolution.

Two gunboats anchored and refused orders. One defected outright, running upstream under a white bedsheet and surrendering herself to the valley defenders at the Gate with her crew standing on deck, hands visible, faces ashamed and relieved in equal measure. Corvane's remaining loyalists — nine men on the flagship and a skiff — tried to force the narrows alone, hit the S-Curve shelf at exactly the speed Emeric had predicted, and spent thirty humiliating minutes broadside in the shallows while riflemen on the rim, who never fired a shot, watched them curse and shove and finally cut their own engines.

Corvane himself came ashore at the Gate under a flag of parley, immaculate as ever, and stood on the landing stones while Oren and a dozen bows regarded him from the heights.

"I'll want my boats back," he said. "Eventually. There are procedures."

"There are procedures," Oren agreed, and did not lower the bows.

Rosalind came down the switchback trail and stopped ten feet from him, close enough to see that for the first time since Lowmark, the reasonableness had cracked — hairline fractures around the eyes, the mouth held together by will alone.

"You understand," Corvane said quietly, "that you've accomplished nothing permanent. The consortium has lawyers. Ledgers can be disputed. In five years this will be a footnote and I will be—"

"On the radio," Rosalind said. "For the next hour, actually. Tobias isn't finished. There are four hundred pages." She stepped closer. "You priced everything, Corvane. Every person, every risk, every life on this river. You just never figured out what the truth cost, because it's the one thing nobody sells. It gets given away. For free. Forever. That's why you lost."

For a moment — one long moment — something moved behind his eyes that might have been understanding, or might merely have been recalculation arriving too late. Then the valley's guards took his arm, courteously, firmly, and led him up the trail to a room with a good view and a locked door, and Rosalind turned back to the transmitter, because the broadcast was not finished, and neither was the day.

Above her on the terrace, Nia was teaching the last verse to the braided women, her voice threading into theirs, the song multiplying. Emeric stood at the rim with the wind off the falls in his face, saying something to Oren that made the big man laugh. Tobias had fallen asleep mid-sentence over page three hundred and twelve, and no one had the heart to move him.

Rosalind keyed the microphone one more time, and paused.

Somewhere downstream, in a port town called Lowmark, in a hundred river houses, people were gathered around radios that had never carried anything but freight schedules and lottery numbers, and they were hearing their own history read aloud. Some of them were crying. She knew it the way she knew weather coming — not evidence, just certainty, the pilot's instinct for what the air holds.

She thought about the rule she'd kept for twenty years. *Nothing to lose. Nothing that can be taken.*

Too late for that now. She'd lost the rule somewhere on the Kessari, and gained — she counted them, the way she now counted everything — an archivist, a stowaway who wasn't a stowaway anymore, a guide with two names and one true one, and a valley full of strangers who had answered her grandmother's song with drawn bows and then, an hour later, with bread.

She keyed the mic.

"This is Rosalind Oyelaran, still speaking from the source. For those just joining us: the reading continues. And afterward—" she glanced up at Nia, whose song had risen over the sound of the falls like a bird finding its flock, "—afterward, there's a map. But the map only opens for the ones who remember. Stay tuned. Learn the song."

She set down the microphone. Below the terrace, Tobias stirred, blinked at the light, and sat bolt upright with a stricken expression.

"The cipher," he said hoarsely. "Rosalind — the elders' cipher for the coordinates. I've seen the key structure. It's not a cipher at all." His eyes were wide, alive, terrified, delighted. "It's a *tune*. The coordinates are encoded as notes. Which means the valley's location has been hiding inside the song-map all along — and there's a verse your grandmother never sang, Nia. A final verse. Someone in this valley knows it — and I think I know who kept it, and why they've never once spoken it aloud."

Rosalind looked at the sleeping archive, the singing child, the falls roaring their sixty-year secret into the gorge — and reached for her jacket, because the truth, it seemed, had one more door.
