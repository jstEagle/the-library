## 20. Broadcast from the Source

The transmitter lived in a stone room at the top of the valley's watchtower, and it was older than anything Rosalind had ever kept flying.

She'd spent two days inside its guts with Nia holding the lamp and Tobias reading out tolerances from a manual printed before either of them was born. The capacitors were wax-sealed relics; the wiring was cloth-insulated copper gone brittle as autumn grass. Half the tubes were dead. The elders produced replacements from cedar chests like a family producing photographs of the departed — each one wrapped in oilcloth, labeled in Veyli script, hoarded for sixty years against a night when someone would need to speak.

"You keep spare parts for a radio," Rosalind had said, "but you were going to seal the pass."

"We keep parts for the truth," Elder Sefa had answered. "We were only waiting to be sure which one deserved it."

Now it was the third evening after the battle, and the transmitter hummed under her hands with a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat — that low carrier-wave thrum, the machine alive and waiting to be given something to say.

Below the tower window, the valley lay in blue dusk. Cookfires. The long roofs of the sanctuary, smoke rising straight in the still air. Down at the gate, Emeric walked the wall with the syndicate's surrendered crews — forty men who had set down their weapons not because they were beaten but because they'd heard the first test broadcast and couldn't unhear it. Corvane himself sat in a stone cell beneath the granary, alive, disarmed, and furious about both.

Rosalind pressed her palm flat against the transmitter's warm casing and let herself feel it: fear. Not of the machine. Of the words.

Tobias stood beside her with the ledger open on a music stand, weighted at the corners with river stones. He'd transcribed the relevant pages onto fresh paper in his small, exact hand — the grain quotas, the sale dates, the names of the ministers who had signed them, the second ledger hidden behind the first showing where the money went. Sixty years of official history reduced to eleven pages of arithmetic.

"Once we begin," he said quietly, "there is no landing this plane."

"I know how broadcasts work, Wrenn."

"I know you do." He adjusted his spectacles. "I only wanted to hear someone say it aloud who wasn't me."

Nia sat cross-legged on the floor with the song-map in her head and her grandmother's notebook in her lap, and she was the calmest person in the room. She'd braided her hair the way the valley children wore theirs now — she'd learned it in four days, the way she learned everything, hungrily, as if making up for sixteen years of doing without.

"They're ready down there," Nia said. "Sefa lit the signal fire. Everyone in the valley can hear it through the horn on the cliff face." She paused. "And everyone downstream, if the weather holds."

Rosalind looked at the frequency dial. The basin's emergency band — the one every airfield, every riverboat, every trading post from Lowmark to the headwaters kept tuned at night, the way people once kept lamps in windows. She'd flown that band herself for ten years, calling in weather, calling in medevacs, calling in the coordinates of crashed friends. It was the one wire that connected everyone who lived out here, strung across a thousand miles of water and rock.

Tonight she would use it to tell all of them that their history had been stolen, sold, and drowned — and that some of the drowned had survived.

"Hand me the microphone," she said.

* * *

She began the way she began every flight. Routine. Procedure. A voice steady enough to land a plane by.

"This is Rosalind Oyelaran, callsign Kestrel, broadcasting on the basin emergency channel from an undisclosed location. What follows is a reading of primary documents from the nation of Veyl, destroyed sixty-one years ago. These documents are authentic. Their provenance will be published alongside them. I am going to read until I am finished. If you have never listened to this frequency before, listen now."

Beside her, Tobias turned to the first page.

She read the quotas first. Dry numbers, deliberately — let the listeners settle into the rhythm of figures, the way you let passengers settle into their seats before turbulence. Tons of grain allocated. Tons of grain actually shipped. The gap between them, growing year over year, three years running before the famine ever had a name.

Then the signatures.

She read each name slowly. Twice. Tobias had insisted on twice — "so no one can claim it was a misread, so the names burn themselves in." Minister of Provision Halden Voss. Deputy Quota Administrator Imre Callas. Seven more. Men whose portraits, she realized as she spoke, probably still hung somewhere in Lowmark's civic hall, men whose descendants ran shipping concerns and salvage firms and — her jaw tightened — syndicates.

"The government of Veyl," she read, from Tobias's summary page, "sold its people's famine reserve to foreign buyers over three consecutive harvests, concealed the sales by falsifying quota ledgers, and when the shortage became visible, blamed the river. They named the Kessari as the killer of their own nation. The river has carried that accusation for sixty-one years."

She stopped. Looked out the window at the dark water far below, the silver thread of it leaving the valley and going down into the world.

"I've flown that river for ten years," she said, off-script, and didn't care. "It never killed anyone who wasn't already drowning. That's what rivers do. They carry what falls in them. This one carried your grandparents' grief, and it carried these papers, and tonight it's done carrying both."

In the corner, Nia had her eyes closed. Her lips moved — following along, or praying, or singing. Rosalind couldn't tell and didn't ask.

* * *

They read for two hours.

The ledger. The correspondence between Voss and the foreign buyers, courteous and obscene, men discussing tons of grain the way other men discuss timber. The internal memo in which Callas proposed the cover story — *attribute the shortfall to river silting; the public already distrusts the water* — in handwriting so neat it could have been engraved. The final entry, dated nine days before the dams failed and the valley drowned: a payment confirmation. The last of the reserve, sold. The ministers' evacuation passage, booked and paid, though in the end only two of them had used it. The river had taken the rest, and Rosalind found she could not summon a single gram of pity.

Between documents, Tobias inserted provenance — where each page had been recovered, chain of custody, the chest, the gravel bar, the storm. He did it like a man setting load-bearing beams. Every claim braced. Every name nailed down.

And then, near midnight, Rosalind set down the last page and picked up the second document — the one the elders had spent two days arguing over, and finally, narrowly, voted to include.

"The following is a list of names," she said. "Survivors of Veyl. People who got out before the collapse, and their descendants. Three hundred and eleven names, compiled by the community that received them. We are reading these names because for sixty-one years the world believed there were no survivors. There were. There are. Some of them are listening right now, and some of you have known them for years as your neighbors, your pilots, your guides, and they never told you why they wouldn't speak of where they came from. After tonight, they shouldn't have to hide anymore. But their home stays theirs. Its location is not part of this broadcast. Don't look for it. If you go looking, you're not looking for the truth. You're looking for loot."

She nodded to Nia.

Nia stood, took the microphone in both hands the way you hold something living, and read the first fifty names herself — her grandmother's name third from the top, spoken in Veyli, the syllables round and sure. Then Sefa took over, then two other elders, then a gray-haired woman who had been twelve when she fled, reading names until her voice cracked and her daughter's arm came around her and the daughter continued without dropping a syllable.

Rosalind watched the needle on the signal-strength meter ride steady and thought: *somewhere out there, every radio in the basin is awake.* Boatmen idling on anchor lines. Airfield mechanics in lit hangars. The whole scattered human noise of the Kessari country, silent, listening, learning that the story they'd been told was a lie told by the killers.

Let Corvane hear it too. Let him sit in his stone cell and hear his industry's founding lie read out in a dead language by living mouths.

* * *

Near dawn, the elders came up the tower stairs to do the last thing.

They brought a lacquered box, and inside it a strip of heavy paper divided into cells — the cipher frame, hand-ruled, sixty years old and never used. Sefa laid it on the table beside the transmitter with the care of a woman laying out a corpse.

"The coordinates," she said. "You're certain this is what you want. Encoded so that only the song can open it."

"It's what we agreed," Rosalind said. "The valley doesn't get erased and it doesn't get exposed. It gets locked. Anyone who wants the location has to earn it — learn the verses, understand what they mean, come respectful. The map is the key and the memory is the lock. No chart, no survey, no gunboat gets in. But a granddaughter always can."

"And if the songs die out?"

Nia, half-asleep in her chair, opened one eye. "They won't. You just broadcast them to the whole basin. Every kid downstream is going to be singing that route verse by summer. You made the key famous, Sefa. That was the deal."

The old woman almost smiled. Almost. "Your grandmother would have called that reckless."

"She called everything reckless. She also taught me every word." Nia shrugged. "Reckless runs in the family."

They worked the cipher together — Tobias supplying the numeric base from the ledger's page-count, the elders layering the Veyli substitution, Rosalind checking the arithmetic twice because a wrong coordinate was worse than none. When it was done, the valley's location existed as thirty-one characters of nonsense that resolved only for someone who knew which line of the song-map mapped to which number, and why the falling-water verse meant *north*, and why the grandmother's verse about bread meant *the height of the second ridge.*

Tobias copied it out fair, sealed it in the box, and then — at Sefa's instruction — read it aloud once, into the transmitter, raw.

Rosalind stared at him. "Wrenn—"

"No one alive can decode it without the song," he said. "But it should exist in the air. In the record. So that someday, when someone does solve it, they'll know the valley chose to be findable — by the worthy, on its own terms. That's different from being lost." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, and his voice went rough. "I spent my career guarding archives that were guarded *into* lies. This is the first archive I've helped guard into honesty. Let it be heard. All of it. Even the parts that are still locked."

So it went out into the dark, thirty-one syllables of cipher riding the emergency band into a thousand radios, meaningless to everyone, permanent to everyone.

Then Rosalind keyed off, and the carrier wave died, and the room was suddenly very quiet — the particular ringing quiet that follows a transmission, that she'd known after every mayday relay and storm warning of her career, but never like this.

Nia broke it, because Nia always did. "So. Did it work?"

Rosalind glanced at the signal log, where the return traffic should start accumulating as stations acknowledged receipt. The needle twitched. Twitched again. Then the tape printer — a museum piece Tobias had coaxed back to life — began to chatter, spitting acknowledgment codes one after another: Lowmark harbor office. The Kessari mouth lightship. An airfield she recognized by code as Merrow Flats, four hundred miles south. Another. Another. A riverboat answering in plain text because its operator was too excited to use procedure: *WHO IS READING THE NAMES. MY FATHER WAS VEYLI. HE NEVER SAID. WHO IS READING THE NAMES.*

Rosalind picked up the microphone one more time.

"A friend of mine," she said. "Her name was Ayanna. She waited her whole life for the source to answer. Consider this the answer. Broadcast ends."

She set the mic down. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking — not from fear now, not anymore, just from the sheer tonnage of what had passed through them.

Down in the valley, the signal fire was burning low, and people were coming out of the long houses into the gray dawn light, and standing in their doorways, listening to the silence after, the way you listen after thunder — to find out whether the world is still there.

It was. For the first time in sixty-one years, it was.

* * *

She found Emeric at the gate an hour later, watching the river run out of the canyon.

He'd heard all of it — the whole valley had — and he stood differently this morning. She couldn't have named the change yesterday. This morning she could: he was standing like a man whose back didn't hurt. Like a man carrying nothing behind his shoulders for once.

"My mother's name was in the list," he said, without turning. "Third generation. Sefa read it herself." His voice caught, steadied. "Sixty years my family hid inside someone else's name, and last night an old woman said the true one out loud over an open channel to the entire basin, and the sky didn't fall."

"No," Rosalind said. "It held."

He looked at her then, and there was something in his face she'd only seen on him once before — at the portage, over the bones, when he'd spoken his mother's name into the cold. Gratitude, maybe. Or the thing underneath gratitude, the thing that doesn't have a clean word.

"There's a boat coming upriver," he said. "Two days out, maybe less. Syndicate hull, but it's not flying their colors. It's flying a survey pennant." He paused. "And it's not Corvane's remaining crew. Word is the board sent someone new. Someone to negotiate — officially. Recovery archaeology, restitution, the full performance." His mouth twisted around the words. "They want to make peace with the valley now that the truth is public. Which means they want to make peace with the ledger. And there's exactly one copy of the ledger that matters, and it's sitting on a music stand in that tower."

Rosalind followed his gaze downriver, to where the water slid out of the valley's keeping and into the world's.

"Then I guess," she said, "we'd better decide what the truth costs before somebody else sets the price."

Behind them, up in the tower, the transmitter hummed on — idle now, but warm, and ready.
