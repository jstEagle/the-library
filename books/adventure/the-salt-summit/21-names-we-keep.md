## 21. Names We Keep

### Tobias

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, carried up the river by the same mail skiff that had once delivered Tobias Wrenn his dismissal.

He recognized the envelope before he recognized the handwriting — the Archival Directorate's seal, pressed in grey wax, the same seal that had stamped the order stripping him of his post eleven years ago. He held it over the stove in Rosalind's hangar office for a long moment, considering whether to burn it. The fire would take it gladly. Everything burned here eventually: packing crates, old flight logs, the tar-paper shingles that curled off the roof every spring. Lowmark had taught him that documents were only paper until someone decided they were history. He had spent a decade learning what happened when people decided otherwise.

He opened it instead.

*To Tobias Wrenn, formerly Royal Archivist of Veyl,* it began, and he almost laughed at *formerly*, as if the title had been waiting for him all these years like a coat left in a closet. *By unanimous resolution of the restored Directorate, your dismissal is rescinded and annulled. Your role in the recovery and authentication of the Kessari Archive is hereby acknowledged as service above distinction. Your appointment as Keeper of the National Record is offered, effective immediately.*

Below the formal text, in different ink, someone had added a line by hand: *We should have listened to you about the watermarks. Forgive us the decade. — M.*

Tobias read the letter three times. Then he set it down on the workbench, next to the chest — the bronze-bound chest itself, which sat now under glass Rosalind had rigged from an old instrument panel, because some things deserved better than a shelf.

Eleven years ago he had stood in the Directorate's marble hall while men who had never touched a primary source explained to him that his insistence on verifying the grain ledgers was "unpatriotic speculation," that the famine was the river's doing, that the drowned nation had been a victim and not — as his cross-referencing suggested — its own executioner. They had taken his keys, his credentials, his name from the rolls. They had not, notably, taken his memory. He had carried the archive in his head through ten years of cheap rooms and cheaper wine, reciting catalog numbers to himself in the dark like prayers, certain that no one would ever ask him to say them aloud again.

Now the whole basin knew them. He had read the ledger into a transmitter microphone while Corvane's hired guns stood down along the canyon walls, and the words had gone out over every frequency from Lowmark to the timber camps, and men who had shot at him two hours earlier had laid those guns in the mud rather than be the ones who silenced them.

That was the part he still couldn't get right in his own telling of it. Not the vindication. The *listening*. All those years he had assumed the truth was a heavy thing, something you hauled uphill alone, and that if you ever reached the top you'd find no one there. Instead there had been a pilot who measured her life in fuel gauges, a girl with a song in her head, a guide with two names — and forty thousand strangers on the riverbanks, standing in the rain, hearing their grandparents' betrayal read out loud and refusing to look away.

A knock at the hangar door. Nia, of course; she knocked the way she did everything, twice and impatiently.

"You're supposed to be at the airfield," she said. "Rosalind says the Directorate sent a plane."

"I know. I have the letter."

"Not a letter. A *plane*. With wings and everything." She grinned, and it was strange and wonderful how quickly she had learned to grin, this girl who had arrived as a stowaway with her grandmother's grief sewn into her coat lining. "They want their Keeper back in Lowmark. Marble halls. Clerks. A salary with actual numbers in it."

"Yes," Tobias said. "All of that."

Nia's grin faltered. "You're going?"

He looked at the chest. At the letter. At the girl.

"Nia," he said, "I spent eleven years being nobody in particular. It turns out I'm quite good at being somebody specific. But a Keeper belongs where the record lives, and the record—" he tapped the glass, gently, the way one taps a sleeping animal, "—is about to have a second volume. The valley elders are sending their cipher-work downriver next month. Someone has to build the catalogue. Someone has to teach the clerks that a watermark can lie and a song can tell the truth." He folded the letter closed. "I'm going. And I am going to come back up this river so often that Rosalind will start charging me fuel."

Nia laughed, but her eyes stayed wet, and when she hugged him — quick, fierce, embarrassed by her own affection — he understood that this too was a restoration. Not of a profession. Of a family he had never applied for and could not now resign from.

"Tell them in Lowmark," she said into his shoulder, "that the archive has co-authors."

"I intend to put it in the dedication."

### Emeric

Emeric Sable had answered to that name for thirty-one years, and it took him less than a minute to stop.

It happened at the toll bridge — the same crossing where he had once signaled Corvane's scouts with a lantern, trading other people's secrets for the safety of a grave village downstream. The syndicate was broken now, its gunboats impounded or sold, its agents scattered like chaff, but the bridge remained, and the village remained, and on the first warm Sunday after the thaw the village came out to honor its dead.

They had asked him to speak. He had said yes before he understood why.

He stood on the planks where he had committed his small treason, in front of the row of cairns that marked the flood-year dead, and behind him the whole village: river families, valley folk who had made the journey downstream for the occasion, Rosalind with her arms crossed against the wind, Nia fidgeting, Tobias in a coat too fine for the country. His mother's grave lay at the end of the row, under a stone that read, in the alphabet of a language the carver hadn't spoken, a name that wasn't hers.

"My mother came down this river in the winter of the collapse," Emeric began, and his voice held — he'd half expected it not to. "She was nineteen. She walked out of the canyon with nothing, and when people asked who she was, she gave them a name that cost her nothing to say and everything to keep. She called herself Sable. She married a ferryman named Sable. She raised a son named Sable. And she died as Sable, and we buried her as Sable, and for thirty years I told myself that was survival, and survival doesn't owe anyone an apology."

He looked at the cairns. At the water sliding dark and patient beneath the bridge.

"But survival isn't the same as living, and my mother knew the difference even if she never said it. On her last night she sang me a verse in Veyli — just one, the counting-song children used at the harvest — and she said, *when it's safe, say my name.* Not remember it. Say it. Out loud. Where the river can hear."

He turned to the stone. He had rehearsed this a hundred times in the night, and every rehearsal had failed, and now, standing in front of everyone, the words came easily, the way true things do.

"Her name was Ilset Veylanne Kor. Daughter of the Kor line, keepers of the western terraces. She was not Sable. She was never Sable. Sable was the boat she hid in until the storm passed." He knelt and pressed his palm flat to the cold granite. "The storm passed, Mama. I'm saying it."

No one applauded, which was right. What happened instead was quieter and worse and better: an old woman near the front — valley-born, one of the elders' messengers — began, very softly, to sing the counting-song. The harvest verse. The one his mother had sung. And then others joined, and the melody rose over the bridge and out across the water, and Emeric knelt there with his hand on a stone that bore the wrong name and felt the right one settle over him like weather.

Afterward, Rosalind found him at the rail.

"Sable," she said, testing it. "Or—"

"Emeric Kor." He tried it in his mouth. It fit differently. Not better. Just *his*. "The guide license will need reprinting. So will the boat."

"The boat?"

"I'm going to carve it into the hull. Both names. Let the syndicate's ghosts choke on it." He glanced sideways at her. "You could do the same, you know."

"Do what?"

"Say yours. Oyelaran. You wear it like a work shirt — useful, anonymous. But there are people down in that crowd who'd stand in the rain to hear it."

Rosalind looked out at the river for a long time. "Maybe," she said, which from her was practically a speech.

### Nia

The valley gave Nia a name on the day the passes opened, and it was not a new one.

They gathered in the long hall beneath the terraces — the elders in their grey wool, the archivists' descendants with ink still on their fingers from copying the cipher-plates, and what seemed like half the valley's children, because word traveled fast among children when the word was *ceremony, skip chores.* Nia stood in the middle of the floor feeling like a specimen pinned under glass, until Elder Maera — the one with the laugh lines and the unerring aim with a thrown boot — stepped forward and asked her the question the gate had asked:

*Who remembers?*

"In my grandmother's tongue?" Nia checked, because she'd learned that much about ceremonies: always check.

"In any tongue you like," Maera said. "But we'd be honored by hers."

So Nia sang.

Not the route-verses this time, not the falls-and-portage map that had carried them through the canyon while Corvane's boats ground against the shallows. The other songs — the naming songs, the ones her grandmother had hummed over the washbasin and the cookfire in a language Nia had thought was just a private music between the two of them, a code with no message left to send. Her grandmother had died waiting for the source to answer, and it had taken Nia sixteen years and a chest in a river and a stolen ride in a floatplane to learn that the answer had been waiting too, upstream the whole time, keeping her grandmother's verses alive in a hundred other mouths.

When she finished, the hall was silent in the way halls are silent when several hundred people are swallowing the same feeling.

Maera spoke the name then — Nia's grandmother's name, and the family line it belonged to, the terrace her people had kept, the craft — dye-work, of all things; her grandmother had sung maps and dyed wool — and then Maera said, "And what shall we call you, child of that line?"

Nia opened her mouth to say *just Nia,* because that had been the truth her whole life: Nia of nowhere, Nia the stowaway, Nia the girl who belonged to a dead woman's memories and nothing else.

What came out instead was: "Nia Oyelaran-Kor."

Silence. Then, from the back of the hall, a strangled sound that was definitely Tobias choking on tea, and definitely Rosalind elbowing him.

"It's hyphenated," Nia said, chin up, ears burning, absolutely refusing to retract it. "Kor for blood. Oyelaran for the person who didn't throw me off her plane when she found me in the cargo bay eating her emergency rations. Emeric gets a hyphen too but he doesn't know it yet."

Maera's eyes crinkled. "And does the Oyelaran half consent to this arrangement?"

Everyone turned. Rosalind stood by the door with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore when the fuel gauge read lower than she liked — the one that meant she was calculating whether she could make the distance.

"Kid," she said finally, "you ate my emergency rations, dented my propeller blade, talked me into running a river I'd have sworn was unflyable, and reorganized my cargo bay according to a system known only to you." A pause. The pause stretched. Nia felt it in her ribs, the way you feel a rapids before you hear it. "Somebody has to inherit that airplane. Might as well be family."

The hall cheered. Nia launched herself across the floor and hit Rosalind like a boarding wave, and Rosalind — who did not hug, who had a rule about attachments the way other pilots had rules about weather minimums — held on.

### Rosalind

They offered her the house in the third week of spring.

It was Maera who did it, in the practical way of mountain people, handing it over like a tool rather than a gift: "The keeper's cottage below the transmitter station. Empty nine years. Roof's sound, stove draws well, and the antenna wire runs past the door, so you'll hear everything worth hearing. We're told you're the best bush pilot in the basin, and the valley needs one — mail, medicine, the cipher-plates going downriver. We pay in food, wool, and silence about your landing technique."

Rosalind looked at the cottage. Stone walls, blue door, a garden plot already turned and waiting. Behind it, the transmitter mast against the sky. Beyond that, the terraces climbing green toward the pass, and beyond *that*, the whole hidden world she had helped drag into the light without destroying — the trick she'd pulled off with a microphone and a cipher, broadcasting the truth while burying the coordinates inside a song only the dead could sing.

She had spent her whole adult life measuring distance from things. Fuel gauges and river bends, miles not years, a rule against attachments enforced with the discipline of a woman who knew exactly what losing things cost, because she'd paid the bill once and sworn never to run the tab again. And here was the valley offering her the one thing she'd never let herself price: a reason to land.

"I'll need a hangar," she heard herself say. "For the plane. And a proper strip — that meadow east of the terraces, graded, drained. If I'm flying your mail I'm not beaching on gravel like a hobbyist."

Maera smiled the smile of a woman who had already surveyed the meadow. "Done by midsummer."

"And the cottage stays mine. Not leased. Mine."

"Yours," Maera agreed. "Deed and all. We'll carve it in stone if you like — we're good at stone."

"No stone necessary." Rosalind stuck out her hand, and they shook on it, pilot-fashion, quick and hard.

That evening she flew the evening mail run down to the toll bridge and back — a twenty-minute hop she'd flown a dozen times since the thaw, and would fly a thousand more. Below her the Kessari ran high and bright with snowmelt, the river that had kept its secret for sixty years and given it up to a woman who'd only stopped to investigate a glint on a gravel bar. She banked over the canyon mouth, tipped a wing to the portage path, to the cairns, to the whole drowned-and-risen country, and leveled out for home.

Home. The word sat differently in her mouth now. Less like a place she was leaving. More like a heading.

At the cottage, light already burned in the window — Nia, supposedly doing cipher homework, actually raiding the pantry; Tobias's letters stacked on the table, the first volume of the new catalogue taking shape in his cramped, beautiful hand; Emeric's boat due up from the bridge tomorrow with the season's first dye-wool, both names fresh-carved on the hull. The kettle was on. Someone had left the door unlatched, because doors here were for walking through, not locking.

Rosalind set the plane down on the meadow strip soft as a moth landing, tied her off, and walked up through the blue door into the noise and warmth of people who knew her name — all of it — and used it freely, constantly, infuriatingly, like it was theirs to use.

Which, she was beginning to understand, it was.

From the transmitter shed, the evening broadcast crackled out over the valley and down the long river: the archive readings, the cipher lessons, the names of the dead and the names of the living, read aloud in Tobias's careful voice by rota, every night, so that no one would ever again confuse a drowned nation with a silenced one. Rosalind paused at the fence, listening, as the reader signed off with the closing line the elders had written and the whole basin had learned by heart:

*"The river keeps what we give it. Give it the truth."*

She smiled, shouldered her kit bag, and went inside.
