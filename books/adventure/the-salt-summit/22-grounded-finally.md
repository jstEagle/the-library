## 22. Grounded, Finally

The Kessari basin at dawn looked like something a careful god had spilled and then decided to leave where it fell.

Rosalind Oyelaran came in low over the eastern tributary with the sun behind her and the medicine crates lashed tight in the back of the old Otter, and for the first time in eleven years of flying this country she was not counting the river bends. She noticed that she wasn't counting. It startled her the way a missing tooth startles the tongue — the habit still there, reaching for its groove, finding nothing.

Eleven years she had measured her life in fuel gauges and gravel bars. In hours between paydowns on the airfield debt. In the exact number of minutes it took to cross from Lowmark to the mission at Serel's Landing, because a pilot who knew her numbers never had to know anything else about herself. Miles, not years. That had been the rule. Distance instead of duration, cargo instead of company, and no attachments — no attachments being the one rule she'd kept flawlessly right up until the morning she fished a bronze-bound chest out of the Kessari and every rule after that broke like ice under a boot heel.

She banked over the confluence where the eastern fork braided into the main channel, and below her the water ran high and silver with snowmelt, the same water that had nearly drowned them twice, that had carried Corvane's gunboats and Emeric's secrets and Nia's grandmother's song all the way up to the place where the river ended and the truth began.

Six months since the broadcast. Six months since she'd sat in the valley transmitter hut with the ledger open on her knees and read Veyl's betrayal out loud to an entire basin — the sold grain quotas, the falsified famine reports, the names of the ministers who had signed their own people's starvation into the record and then blamed the river. Six months since hired gun crews on the canyon rim had lowered their weapons one by one as the words went out over the frequencies, some of them weeping, two of them deserting outright, and Corvane standing alone on the shingle with his perfect coat and his reasonable voice and nothing left to be reasonable about.

She hadn't hated him, even then. That had surprised her most. She'd felt what she always felt over a wreck site: the arithmetic of it, the cold tally of choices made by people who thought they were buying survival. She knew that arithmetic. She'd been running it her whole life, just in smaller denominations.

The syndicate had dissolved within the season — not dramatically, the way villains were supposed to, but the way bad weather breaks: unevenly, with pockets of stubborn fog. The recovery-archaeology permits were rescinded. The surveyor who'd sold them the falls route was in custody in Lowmark, telling everything he knew to anyone who'd buy it. And Tobias Wrenn — disgraced, exiled, half-starved Tobias Wrenn — had been invited back to the capital to rebuild the royal archive from the ashes of its reputation, and had written back to say, in his fussy precise hand, that he would do it *only* if the archive's first mandate was the Veyl collection, and its second mandate was training archivists recruited from the source valley itself.

They'd said yes. Of course they'd said yes. The world had spent six months discovering that it wanted very badly to say yes to things it had spent fifty years refusing.

Rosalind tipped the Otter's wing and turned north, toward the canyon.

* * *

The supply run itself hadn't changed. That was the strange part. Same crates — penicillin, suture kits, chloroquine, a bolt of canvas someone at the mission had ordered without measuring whether it would fit through the cargo door (it wouldn't; it never did). Same airfield coffee, drunk standing up, while Hetta the mechanic yelled at her about the number three cylinder and Rosalind nodded in the places where nodding seemed expected. Same weight-and-balance arithmetic done in grease pencil on the cowling.

But the miles had a destination now, and that changed the texture of everything. Flying used to feel like escaping — each takeoff a small clean severance, wheels leaving earth the way she'd once left a dozen towns and one family before that, none of which she talked about. Now takeoff felt like a thread going taut. Not a leash. A line, like the ones Emeric rigged across the rapids. Something you could haul on, and be hauled by.

She crossed the ridge line at nine-forty and there it was: the source canyon, cutting its dark seam through the high plateau, invisible from any distance unless you already knew to look. Which was the point. The elders had seen to that.

The cipher had gone into the broadcast itself, woven into the final minute of the transmission — coordinates reduced to verse, the valley's location folded inside the song-map so that only someone carrying Nia's grandmother's melody could unfold it. Tobias had argued for weeks about the mathematics of it, delighted beyond speech, sketching substitution schemes on napkins until Emeric threatened to soak the napkins in the river. In the end it was simpler than any of Tobias's schemes: the song *was* the key, and songs don't survive in archives. They survive in grandmothers, and in granddaughters, and in the stubborn oral memory of people the world had tried very hard to erase.

So the map to Veyl's last living valley existed in exactly one form: sung. And the people who could sing it could be counted on two hands, and every one of those hands belonged to someone Rosalind trusted more than she had ever trusted anyone, which was still not saying much, but was saying considerably more than it used to.

She brought the Otter down along the canyon rim, slow, the way you approach a church or a cliff edge, and rocked her wings once.

It was a salute. She'd never served in any army, had no use for salutes, had spent her whole flying career treating ceremony as dead weight. But six months ago, on the day of the broadcast, Nia had stood on the transmitter hut's porch and watched the antenna wire hum, and when it was done she'd turned around with tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face and said, *My grandmother would have rocked her wings.* Because her grandmother had been a bush pilot too, in the years before the collapse — one of the last licensed flyers out of drowned Veyl, the woman who taught the song-map to anyone who'd hold still long enough to learn it, and who died in Lowmark waiting for the source to answer.

It had answered. Through a stowaway with a stolen radio and a memorized melody and absolutely no permission from anyone.

Rosalind rocked the wings, and below her the canyon walls caught the morning light and threw it back gold, and somewhere down in that hidden green fold of the world four hundred people were waking up to a valley that was no longer a secret but was still, impossibly, theirs.

Then she turned south for home.

* * *

Home. The word still fit strangely in her mouth, like a borrowed coat. She tested it anyway, sometimes, when the autopilot wasn't looking.

The strip at Serel's Landing had grown a second building over the winter — not for her, officially. Officially it was a freight depot, built with basin-cooperative money, for the new trade route that ran upriver now that the toll bridges had passed back into local hands. Unofficially, everyone including Hetta called it Roz's hangar, because it had a workbench she'd built, and a stove she'd fixed, and because three of the four bunks in the loft had been slept in, on various nights, by people whose names were on no manifest anywhere.

Tobias stayed when he passed through, en route between the capital and the valley, cataloguing as he traveled, unable to stop cataloguing. He'd grown a beard that made him look less like a drowned clerk and more like a disreputable prophet, and he'd stopped apologizing mid-sentence, mostly. Last month he'd stood in the loft doorway at midnight and told her, with the gravity of a man announcing a treaty, that he had nominated her for the archive's honorary commission, and she'd thrown a rag at him, and he'd caught it, and they'd both pretended the moment hadn't happened, which was how the two of them said almost everything that mattered.

Emeric came and went with the river. He'd taken his mother's name back in front of three hundred people at the spring gathering — *Sable* was the alias, the camouflage; her true name was **Emerica Vey-Solanne**, and he wore it now stitched inside his jacket collar where he could touch it, though he'd also had it painted, at Nia's insistence, on the bow of his boat, in letters big enough to read from the far bank. Some mornings he still flinched when strangers used it. But he was teaching river-running to valley kids now, and teaching them the old Veyli water-chants too, and every kid who learned one was another hand that could unfold the map. Camouflage becoming inheritance. He'd said that once, quietly, over the stove, and Rosalind had understood it applied to more people in that room than him.

And Nia.

Nia had refused, flatly and furiously, to be sent anywhere — not to the capital school Tobias arranged, not to the valley's own teachers, not anywhere she couldn't see the river. So they'd compromised, the way families do, which was a word Rosalind was still learning to use without checking over her shoulder. Nia wintered in the valley with the elders, learning the archive-side of the song: the verses that named the dead, the ones nobody else alive could carry. Summers she flew with Rosalind. Right-seat. Radio call sign she'd invented herself — *Little Wing*, which made Tobias snort tea through his nose the first time he heard it, and which Rosalind had pretended to hate for exactly one flight before surrendering completely.

Nia was fourteen hours behind her right now, coming upriver with Emeric on the last leg of the spring run, carrying seed stock and a crate of books Tobias had foisted on the valley school. Which meant that tonight, if the weather held and the river behaved, the loft above the hangar would have all four bunks full for the first time since the thaw.

Rosalind did the math without meaning to. Fuel burn to Serel's, minus headwind, plus the time it took Hetta to argue with a cylinder. Arrival: dusk, maybe a little after. She caught herself doing it and laughed out loud, alone at eight thousand feet, a short bark of a laugh that the wind took away.

A year ago she measured time in miles. Now she measured it in arrivals.

* * *

The weather did not entirely hold. It never did, on the Kessari; the basin considered clear skies a personal insult. A squall line built up over the middle reaches around noon, fat and blue-black, and Rosalind threaded east of it the way she'd threaded a hundred storms, hands easy, eyes moving, and somewhere over the second bend she realized her shoulders weren't up around her ears.

That was new too. Fear still came — it came on every flight, honest fear, the kind that keeps pilots alive — but the other kind, the low chronic dread of having something to lose, had stopped being a weight and become a kind of ballast. It steadied her. She flew better with it. She'd have denied this under oath.

Below, the river ran the color of hammered tin, and she picked out landmarks like old friends: the gravel bar where the chest had glinted, half-drowned now under spring water, unremarkable, finished with her. The burned-out toll bridge, rebuilt in timber by the cooperative, trucks crawling across it like patient beetles. The falls camp, where they'd nearly lost each other to anger and gunboats and the white water, and where someone — she suspected Emeric, she suspected it strongly — had nailed a small plank to the biggest pine, carved with four names and no explanation for whoever found it next.

She climbed over the squall's shoulder rather than fight it, and from up there the whole basin opened beneath her like a held breath finally let out: river and ridge and the smoke of a hundred cooking fires, all of it ordinary, all of it alive, all of it hers in the only way she'd ever wanted to own anything — by knowing it bend by bend, and being known back.

The radio crackled. Lowmark relay, then a familiar voice breaking through the static with terrible microphone technique.

*"Otter One-Niner, Otter One-Niner, this is Little Wing, do you copy, over."*

Rosalind grinned at the windshield. "Little Wing, I copy. You're supposed to be two days behind me."

*"We made time. River's high, current's friendly, Emeric says hello and also that your cylinder theory is wrong."*

"Tell Emeric my cylinder theory has landed us safely four hundred times."

*"He says that's statistics, not theory. Also —"* static swallowed a beat, and when Nia's voice came back it had gone quieter, the performative bravado set down like a heavy pack — *"also we picked up a passenger at the Landing junction. Kid, maybe twelve, says her brother works the source valley and she hasn't heard from him since freeze-up. Grandmother taught her some Veyli. Just a few words. Roz — she sang the greeting verse. Almost right."*

Rosalind was quiet for a moment, watching the canyon slide past off her left wing, gold light running along its rim.

"Almost right counts," she said finally. "Bring her home."

There was a pause on the frequency, and she could hear Nia smiling through the static, and underneath the smile something older and steadier, something that sounded remarkably like a girl who had once stowed away on a plane belonging to a woman famous for having no attachments, and who had dismantled that reputation in a single afternoon.

*"Copy,"* Nia said. *"Bringing her home. Little Wing out."*

Rosalind keyed off, trimmed the nose down, and pointed the Otter south toward Serel's Landing, toward the hangar with the stove and the workbench and the four bunks — one of which was about to need new blankets, and possibly a smaller pillow, and definitely a name scratched into the loft beam next to the others, because that was apparently how her family did things now: one name at a time, carved deep enough to outlast the weather.

The miles unwound beneath her, familiar as her own pulse. She counted none of them.

At the edge of the basin the sun broke clean through the last of the squall, and the whole Kessari flashed silver from bend to bend, upstream and down, all the way to the sea — and Rosalind Oyelaran, grounded at last in every way that mattered, flew on toward the people waiting, and found she was in no hurry at all.
