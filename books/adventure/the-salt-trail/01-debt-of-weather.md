## 1. Debt of Weather

The storm came up out of the southwest like a debt collector, and Hazel Pemberton drove toward it the way he did everything now—because running from it cost more.

He had learned to measure his life in miles. Not dollars; dollars were slippery things that bred in the dark of a ledger and doubled while you slept. But miles were honest. A mile driven was a mile paid. The Vane brothers kept their books in favors, which was worse than money, but even favors could be reckoned in miles if you squinted at them right, and Hazel had gotten very good at squinting.

The plains unrolled ahead of him, wheat-colored and wind-scoured, the sky above them stacking itself into towers of bruise. He had been chasing weather for eleven years—first for science, then for footage, then for nothing so dignified as either—and he knew this storm's mood before he'd seen its first lightning. It was a dry-lipped, fast-walking storm, all muscle and no rain, the kind that would eat a barn and spit out the nails. He loved it the way you love anything that doesn't know your name.

His rig was a twenty-year-old survey truck with a hail cage welded over the cab and a mast bolted to the bed, and it smelled of burnt coffee and ozone and the particular staleness of a man who lived in one seat. On the passenger floor sat a shoebox of unpaid bills he no longer opened, because opening them was like counting waves. Behind the seat, wrapped in oilcloth, was the anemometer his brother Aldous had given him when they still spoke—the last instrument Aldous ever calibrated by hand, back when calibration was something brothers did together in a garage that smelled of solder and their father's pipe smoke.

Hazel didn't look at the box. He looked at the road, and the road was good, and good roads were how he kept himself paid up against the dark.

* * *

The Vanes found him at a gas station in a town whose name had worn off the water tower, which felt about right. They arrived in a white pickup too clean for the county, and they got out smiling, because they always got out smiling. That was the thing people never understood about the Vane brothers until it was too late: they weren't wolves in fine clothes. They were fine clothes in wolf country, and they never once raised their voices.

"Hazel Pemberton." Corwin Vane said the name like a toast. He was the taller one, silver at the temples, with the easy hands of a man who'd never had to grip anything harder than a pen. "Chasing the big cell east of here. We saw your truck on the feed. You always did have an eye."

"I always did have a bill," Hazel said, filling his tank without hurrying. "That's what brings you."

"That's what *keeps* us," said the other one—Ambrose, shorter, softer, the one who did the actual arithmetic. Ambrose leaned against the pump housing and produced, from nowhere, a folded paper he did not offer. "You're carrying a balance with us, Hazel. Nothing shameful in it. Weather's a cruel employer. But balances want tending."

"Every mile I drive is a mile off it," Hazel said. "You know my arrangement."

"We know your arrangement." Corwin smiled wider, and there it was, just behind his teeth, the thing that made men sign things they shouldn't. "We also know your arrangement has a floor. Interest is interest, friend. Miles are lovely, but they don't compound in your favor."

Hazel screwed the cap back on the tank and finally turned to face them fully. Up close they smelled of cologne and cold coffee, of long drives in air-conditioned comfort, of decisions made on behalf of other people. "Then say what you came to say. I've got a wall cloud forty miles west and closing."

"A job," Ambrose said simply. "One run. Upriver."

"I don't do river work."

"You did once." Corwin's voice went gentle, which was worse than loud. "Before your brother went upriver and stopped writing. Before you decided water wasn't your element anymore. We remember everything, Hazel. It's why people trust us with their debts."

The wind shifted, and Hazel tasted the storm coming—dust first, then that green metallic edge underneath. Forty miles was becoming thirty. He thought about the shoebox on the floor of his truck. He thought about the number Ambrose carried around in his head, the number that grew while Hazel slept, patient as rust.

"What kind of run," he said.

"Survey crew of ours went quiet three weeks ago. Six souls, radio mast, satellite check-ins every twelve hours. The check-ins stopped." Ambrose unfolded his paper at last, and Hazel saw it was a river chart, annotated in a neat accountant's hand. "Last known position's past the bend where the valley narrows. Above that, the glacier. You know the country."

"Nobody knows that country. That's the point of it."

"Which is why we sent professionals," Corwin said. "And why we're sending you now. You've got the eye, the rig, and—" he tapped the chart, right on the bend, "—motivation. Bring us word of our people. One boat, one man, one report. And Hazel—" he paused, savoring it, "—the ledger burns. All of it. Every favor, every figure. You walk away owing the world nothing but its weather."

Hazel laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "Nothing's free. Especially not from you two."

"Of course not," Ambrose agreed pleasantly. "It's not free. It's paid. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of us."

* * *

The storm hit while they were still talking, because storms don't wait for business to conclude. The light went the color of old brass, and the first gust rocked the pumps and set the awning snapping like sails. Hazel's hands wanted the wheel the way a drowning man wants a rail. Somewhere west, the sky was putting on its whole show—wall cloud lowering, rotation tightening, the atmosphere wringing itself out over empty grassland—and every year of his life told him to go, go now, get ahead of it, be there when it dropped.

Instead he stood in the gas station's lee with two cheerful creditors and a river chart, and he did the only math that had ever come naturally to him.

One run. One report. Then zero.

Zero was a number he hadn't owned since he was twenty-six, since the year the family land went under and Aldous took the map of it upriver and Hazel took the debts downriver, and they had divided their inheritance like a wishbone—one half luckless, the other half gone. If the ledger burned, he could point the truck anywhere. Anywhere at all. He could chase storms for the love of them again, or stop chasing them entirely, or drive until the maps ran out. Zero was not freedom exactly. But it was the door to it, and the Vanes were holding it open with those pleasant, patient smiles.

"And if I find them dead?" Hazel asked.

"Then bring us word of that too," Corwin said. "We grieve with our people, Hazel. We're not monsters."

*They collect from monsters,* Hazel thought, and didn't say.

"And if I find them alive and they don't want to come down?" he asked instead. "River folk get strange. Isolation does it. What am I supposed to tell them?"

"Tell them their backers sent help," Ambrose said, folding the chart into Hazel's hand, pressing it flat against his chest the way you'd pin a medal or a target. "Tell them the river's opening soon and won't open again till calving season turns. Tell them whatever gets them talking." He smiled his brother's smile, smaller and sharper. "You're good at getting people talking. It runs in the family."

The wind screamed down the lot. Out west, the sky had gone the deep wrong green that meant the storm had stopped being weather and started being a decision, and Hazel felt the old pull in his chest, the hunger to be under it, measuring it, naming it—because a named thing couldn't hurt you, and an unnamed thing could take everything.

He looked at the chart. The bend. The narrowing. The blank space above it where the glacier sat doing whatever glaciers do with what falls into them.

"One run," Hazel said. "Word only. I'm not a recovery man."

"Word only," Corwin agreed, and shook his hand, and his grip was warm and dry and entirely without weight, which was how Hazel knew he'd just signed something.

* * *

He drove west after all—not toward the storm, though it filled his mirrors like a following crowd, but north, toward the river, toward the bend. The rig rode heavy with fuel and canned food and the rope he hadn't used in six years. The chart lay on the passenger seat. The shoebox stayed on the floor, lighter already in his imagination, though he knew better than most how heavy a thing could stay after you'd stopped carrying it.

At the turnoff where the pavement gave up and became gravel, he pulled over once and let the storm pass behind him, lightning walking the horizon on long white legs. He watched it go with something like grief. Eleven years of measuring his life in miles, and now every mile pointed upstream, into the silence, toward a brother who had stopped writing and a debt that was about to be paid in a currency he hadn't agreed to.

"Just another run," he told the windshield, and put the truck in gear.

The river, when he reached it near dusk, was high and brown and moving fast, carrying meltwater and broken ice and, somewhere far up along its course, six sets of answers he'd been hired to fetch home. He backed the trailer down the ramp at the old landing, and the water took the hull with a sound like a held breath released.

Above the bend, the light was already failing. Above the glacier, it had failed years ago.
