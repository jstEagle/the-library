## 2. The Silent Bend

The river went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone stops breathing in it.

Hazel had been on the water two days, running the current north with the outboard at half-throttle because fuel was weight and weight was money, and for two days the river had talked to him—gurgling over gravel bars, slapping the hull, muttering its one long sentence. Then, an hour past the second bend, it stopped. Not froze. Stopped. The water slid past smooth as poured glass, and the silence that replaced the sound was so complete he could hear his own pulse ticking in his ears, and beneath that, fainter, something else: a smell.

Salt. Out here, a hundred miles from any sea, the air carried salt like a rumor.

He killed the engine and let the boat drift. Ahead, the valley narrowed between shoulders of grey rock, and the glacier showed itself for the first time—a white wall at the head of the valley, so distant it looked painted on, so vast it made the distance feel like a lie. The survey expedition had come up this same water six weeks ago. Nine people, two boats, a radio mast, a contract from the Vanes to map the melt channels above the source. Six weeks ago they'd been alive and chattering down the frequency every evening at seven, cheerful as men on holiday.

Then nothing. Fourteen days of static where voices should have been.

Hazel shipped the oars and rowed, because some silences deserved to be entered quietly.

* * *

The first camp told him nothing except that people had left it in a hurry and meant to come back. Tents still staked, guy-lines taut, a kettle sitting on a cold stove with water gone green inside it. A pair of boots by the tent flap, laced, waiting for feet. He walked the perimeter twice and found no blood, no scuffle, no sign of anything but departure—the particular disorder of a place abandoned mid-sentence. A journal lay open on a crate, its last entry a supply list, half finished: *paraffin, spare carburetor needle, more of Marisol's coffee if she'll trade.*

He took the paraffin. He wasn't proud of it, but debt makes a man practical about other people's leavings.

The second camp, twelve miles up, was different. That one had been struck properly—canvas folded, gear cached under tarps weighted with stones, everything squared away by hands that expected to return and found the tidiness worth keeping. But the tarps were furred with frost-rot, and when Hazel lifted one he found the cache picked over, and among the crates a scatter of paper: survey sheets, contour maps of the upper valley, all of them defaced. Every elevation figure scratched out. Every grid reference blacked through with a pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

Somebody upriver didn't want these numbers read.

* * *

He found the mast on the third day, and it stopped him cold.

It stood on a shelf of rock above a sharp bend where the river doubled back on itself—the silent bend, he'd already started calling it in his head—a lattice of galvanized steel forty feet tall, or it had been. Now it lay folded against the cliffside like a broken wing, snapped at the third joint, the whole structure dragged down and bent inward, toward the rock, as though something enormous had leaned on it. The guy-wires were cut. Not frayed, not parted by wind-load. Cut, with a tool, cleanly, all four of them, which meant someone had climbed the mast first or rigged the fall deliberately, because a mast doesn't fold politely against a cliff on its own.

The radio hut beside it was worse. Door off its hinges. Inside, the set itself smashed—not looted, smashed, the vacuum tubes ground to powder under a boot heel, the chassis bent double. Whoever did it hadn't wanted the equipment. They'd wanted the voice. And they'd wanted anyone coming after to know the voice was dead.

Hazel stood in the wrecked hut a long time, listening to the river say nothing.

Fourteen days of static. He'd assumed storm damage, dead batteries, the ordinary failures of remote work. Standing there among the powdered glass, he understood he'd been sent upriver not to find lost men but to find out who had silenced them—and the answer was waiting somewhere ahead of him, patient as the ice.

He got back in the boat. There was nowhere else to go but forward, and forward was where the money was, and the money was where his brother had last been heard from, and he refused, with the full stubborn weight of thirty-one years, to think too hard about that last part.

* * *

She came out of the rocks at dusk, when the light went copper and long, and she nearly put a knife in him before he got his hands up.

"Vane," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the flag stitched on his pack, the little blue pennant the brothers sewed into everything they lent against.

"Hazel Pemberton," he said. "Contract retrieval. I'm looking for the survey."

"The survey's dead." She said it flat, the way you report weather. She was small and brown and wind-scoured, maybe thirty, with the cracked lips and hollow cheeks of someone who'd been eating very little for a very long time, and she held the knife the way a person holds it who has recently learned exactly what it's for. "You can turn around."

"I can't."

"No," she said, studying him. "I can see you can't." Something moved behind her eyes—calculation, or pity, hard to tell which. "Marisol Vega. Translator. What's left of the expedition."

"What happened to the rest?"

She didn't answer. She looked upriver instead, toward the glacier, and her jaw worked once, twice, like she was chewing a sentence and couldn't swallow it. "Come on," she said finally. "Before dark. If you're going to be a problem, I'd rather it happen where I can see it coming."

* * *

She led him up a goat track above the bend, switchbacking through scree the color of old bone, until the river was a silver thread far below and the air began, unmistakably, to taste of salt. Not the faint rumor from before. Salt thick enough to sting the nose, salt laying a white bloom on the stones, and then the cave mouth opened in the cliff face like a wound—high, dry, walled in halite that caught the last light and threw it back amber and rose, the whole chamber glowing like the inside of a lamp.

And standing in that glow, thin as a fence post, beard gone grey at the chin, was Aldous.

Hazel had rehearsed this moment for eleven years without knowing it. In the rehearsal there was shouting, or there was forgiveness, or there was nothing at all—just two men nodding across a distance too wide to cross. What actually happened was smaller and stranger than any of it. Aldous looked at him, and his face did three things in one second—recognition, relief, terror—and settled on none of them.

"Hazel," he said. His voice was rust. "They sent you."

"They sent me to find the survey."

"You found what's left of it." Aldous's eyes went past him to Marisol, and something passed between them, quick and unreadable, a look like a door closing. "How many boats did they send?"

"One. Just me."

"Just you." Aldous laughed, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion ground fine as the salt dust under their feet. "That's almost funny. Eleven years, and when they finally need me handled, they send my brother."

"Handled." Hazel set his pack down slowly, never taking his eyes off him. "Aldous. Where are the others? Eight people came up this river. The camps are empty. The mast is cut. The radio is powder. Somebody did that with their hands, and there's nobody up here but you and her."

Marisol turned away, suddenly very interested in the cave wall.

Aldous came closer. Up close he smelled of stone and starvation, and his eyes had the fixed brightness Hazel had seen once before, years ago, the night their father's house burned and Aldous stood in the yard watching it with his hands open at his sides, calm as a man watching rain. "I need you to listen," he said, low, urgent, gripping Hazel's sleeve. "In the back of this cave there is a thing the Vane brothers' employers will spend any amount of money to bury, and I mean that literally—they will pay to have it buried, and the men who carry it buried with it. Everything that happened up here happened because of that thing. Everything. And I would do all of it again."

"All of what?" Hazel said. "Aldous. All of *what*?"

His brother smiled, and it was the smile from the burning yard, serene and ruined. "You always measured everything in miles," he said. "Miles you could pay off. This isn't miles, Hazel. This is the one debt nobody survives paying."

Behind them, far down the valley, so faint it might have been imagination, came the sound Hazel had been dreading since the silent bend: an engine. A second boat, running upstream against the current, coming fast.
