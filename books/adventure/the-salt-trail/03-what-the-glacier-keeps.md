## 3. What the Glacier Keeps

The cave had a sound to it, Aldous thought, like the inside of a held breath. The salt walls drank every echo before it could form, so that even his own voice came back to him flattened, owned, as if the mountain were keeping copies of everything said here and would one day present them for judgment. He had lived with that sound for eleven days. He had stopped apologizing to it.

He watched Hazel come up the last of the rope ladder from the waterline, salt crusting his brother's sleeves white at the elbows, and felt the old arithmetic of their childhood reassert itself: *he is smaller than me, he is colder than me, I must have brought him here for a reason.* Except Hazel wasn't small anymore. He stood in the blue light of the lantern with riverwater dripping off him and looked around the chamber the way he looked at weather—reading it, pricing it—and Aldous understood that whatever reason he'd constructed was already under audit.

"You said the others were dead," Hazel said. "You didn't say how."

"I said what was true."

"Marisol says you were there when they died. She won't say more than that. She looks at you like you're a hole in the ice."

Aldous turned to the wall behind him, because it was easier than turning to his brother's face. He lifted the oilcloth away from the shelf he'd spent three days cutting into the salt.

The archive came out of the dark in pieces. Ledger books first—nine of them, swollen with damp, their spines furred with crystal. Then the tin document case, its lock rusted open years ago. Then the photographs, glass-plate negatives wrapped in waxed paper, and last the small brass canister that held what Aldous privately called the confession, though the man who wrote it had called it an accounting.

"The Harrow fortune," Aldous said. "Your Vanes' money, half the banks on the river, the trust that owns the valley's water rights—it all comes out of this cave. Or out of what this cave proves."

Hazel came closer. He always moved toward evidence; it was the one greed Aldous had never been able to hate in him. "Proves what?"

"That the founding silver wasn't mined. It was taken." He opened the oldest ledger to the page he knew by heart now, the way you know a scar. "Spring of the second year. A camp of Kettle River people—forty-one names, counted twice by two different clerks who each tried to make the number smaller. They were paid in salt. Packed into the workings below the glacier tongue when it was still an open shaft, and the entrance collapsed the same week. The company called it subsidence. The company paid itself condolences." He touched the ledger line. "The founder signed this page himself. His grandson endowed the university library where I found the reference that led me here. Nobody burned these because nobody believed anyone would ever climb high enough to be bothered."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. In the lantern light his face did something complicated—Aldous watched grief arrive and then watch itself arrive, watched his brother catch the feeling and hold it at arm's length the way he held everything, measuring.

"They sent us to bury this," Hazel said finally. "The Vanes. That's the favor. That's what my debt buys."

"Yes."

"And you?"

"I was hired to find it too," Aldous said. "Six months ago. Different letterhead, same money. I was supposed to confirm it existed and report coordinates and let professionals handle the rest." He closed the ledger. "I confirmed it existed."

* * *

He told it in order, because disorder would sound like excuse. The expedition had come upriver in June, nine of them, good people mostly—the surveyor Wrenn who sang hymns off-key, the twins who ran the drill, old Corbett who'd mapped three valleys and lied about all of them. For six weeks they worked above the waterline while the glacier ticked and settled around them like a house cooling at night. And when they broke through into this chamber and Aldous laid the ledgers out on the salt shelf and read them aloud by lamplight, there had been a silence he mistook, at first, for the same kind of awe he felt.

It wasn't awe. It was appraisal.

"It was Wrenn who said it first," Aldous told Hazel. "She said, *do you know what this is worth?* Not what it means. What it's worth. The descendants would pay anything to keep it buried—a private purchase, clean, deniable. And the rest—" he made a gesture that took in the whole cave, the whole valley, "—the rest agreed inside a day. Corbett drew up terms. They voted. Nine to one."

"And you counted yourself the one."

"I counted myself the only one who remembered the forty-one names." His voice came out level, which surprised him. He had expected it to crack. "They weren't going to sell the truth, Hazel. They were going to sell the *silence*. Same commodity the Vanes deal in. Same commodity everyone downriver deals in. And I—" 

He stopped. This was the part he never got through cleanly, not even alone, not even to the salt.

"There was a storm coming. A real one—I've chased enough of them with you to know the signs, and this valley funnels them like a throat. I told them the shaft mouth below was unstable, that we should move the boats. Half of them went down to secure the gear. The ice let go while they were under it." He met his brother's eyes. "I knew it would. I'd watched that face calve twice already that week. I told them to go down anyway."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, the glacier shifted its weight, a long groan like a ship changing its mind.

"Four died under the ice," Aldous said. "Wrenn and Corbett and the twins. The other four scattered—one went downriver with the records he could carry, to sell them himself, and I caught him at the bend. Marisol ran. Two others I never found, and I tell myself the river took them, and some nights I believe it." He set his hand flat on the ledger, on the forty-one names beneath his palm. "I am not sorry. That's the thing you need to understand before you decide what you think of me. I would do it again. Some truths are load-bearing, Hazel. You don't get to sell the keystone and keep the arch."

Hazel had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a storm cell build on a horizon he can't outrun. When he spoke, his voice was careful, stripped of everything.

"You used to say debt was the only honest weather. That you pay what the sky asks or you get buried."

"I did."

"This is what the sky asks?" Hazel gestured at the cave—at the ledgers, at the darkness beyond the shelf where four men's gear still hung on pegs, patient as laundry. "You're collecting favors from dead men now? You've become a Vane, Aldous. You've become worse. They just want their money. You want your *certainty*, and you'll drown anyone who prices it differently."

Aldous felt the words go in and could not honestly say they missed anything vital. "Someone has to keep it," he said, weaker than he meant to.

"No," Hazel said. "Someone has to *carry* it. There's a difference, and it's the width of a river."

That was when Marisol, at the ladder head, made a sound—not a word, just a short exhalation, the sound of a woman seeing something she'd hoped not to see. Aldous crossed to the cave mouth in three strides and looked down the valley.

Downriver, past the wrecked mast and the abandoned camps, a boat was coming up against the current. Second boat. Vane gray, Vane lines, running lights doused, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had done this stretch of water before and expected to do it again. Even at this distance Aldous could see there were too many men in her for a survey crew.

"They're early," he said. "Calving doesn't open the route for days."

"Then they didn't come for the route," Hazel said quietly. "They came because someone signaled."

And Aldous understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the glacier, exactly who had signaled, and when, and why—because Marisol had been gone from the camp for six hours two nights ago, and had come back with wet boots and dry eyes, and he had chosen not to ask.

Below them, the boat put in at the bend. Men began to climb.
