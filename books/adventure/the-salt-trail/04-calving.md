## 4. Calving

The glacier announced itself before it moved. All night the sound had come down the valley like a door being tested in its frame—a groan, a pause, a groan again—until Hazel stopped pretending to sleep and sat up in the cold dark of the tent, listening to a wall of ice two hundred feet high decide what it was going to do.

Marisol had told him about calving season on the row upriver, back when she still thought they were both going home. *The tongue pulls back from the shore,* she'd said, drawing the river's shape in spilled tea on the tabletop. *For a few hours the channel opens where it's been choked all year. Then it closes again until next spring.* She'd said it the way sailors talk about weather—not as danger but as schedule. The glacier kept appointments. You either made them or you didn't.

Hazel had spent his whole adult life making appointments with weather. He knew how to read a sky the way Aldous read documents: patiently, hungrily, looking for the flaw that would tell him what was coming apart underneath. And every instinct he owned said this sky was wrong. Too clear. Too still. The kind of stillness that meant something enormous was holding its breath.

He broke camp before first light and climbed to the cave one last time.

Aldous was awake too. He was sitting at the mouth of the salt cave with the archive spread around him like a man playing solitaire with his own life—the tin boxes open, the wax-sealed packets fanned out, the photographs of the massacre laid face-up in the grey pre-dawn light as if he were introducing them to each other. He didn't look up when Hazel's boots crunched on the salt crust. He'd known who it was by the walk. Brothers always did.

"It's opening," Hazel said.

"I know." Aldous touched one of the packets without picking it up. "I've been listening to it all night. It sounds almost like speech, doesn't it? Like it's trying to say something."

"It's trying to fall down. That's what it's saying." Hazel crouched and began gathering the packets into the oilskin satchel, working fast, not looking at his brother. "We take the eastern ridge. There's a shelf above the channel where I can get a signal out. Marisol showed me the bearing before—" He stopped. He made himself say it. "Before they took her."

Aldous flinched. It was small, but Hazel saw it, and he hated himself for cataloguing it the way he catalogued everything now, the way the Vanes had taught him to catalogue debts.

"They'll have someone watching the ridge," Aldous said.

"Probably."

"And you're going anyway."

"You already know I am, so let's skip the part where you argue." Hazel cinched the satchel shut and slung it across his chest. It weighed maybe forty pounds. It weighed more than any debt he'd ever carried, and it was the only weight he'd ever wanted to carry. "The broadcast goes out from the ridge. Every page, every photograph, every name. Then I row down through the gap while it's open, and by the time anyone reaches me I'm just a man in a boat with an empty bag."

"And me?"

Hazel finally looked at him. His brother was fifty-one years old and looked older, salt-bleached and hollowed, with frostbite scarring two of his fingers black. Six weeks alone in a cave guarding paper. Six weeks since he'd sealed the expedition's fate with a rockfall and a lie about the route, since he'd watched men he'd eaten beside for months die of cold and pride and his own arithmetic. Hazel understood it now—he'd understood it since the night in the cave when Aldous had explained, calm as a ledger, why the truth needed protecting even from the people paid to carry it. Aldous believed the archive was worth more than the people around it. That was the whole of him. That had always been the whole of him, Hazel realized; the expeditions, the disappearances, the decade of silence between them—all of it the same belief wearing different coats.

"The boat holds two," Hazel said. "You know that."

"I know that."

"So come."

Aldous shook his head slowly, and there it was—not fear, not regret, but certainty, that terrible clean thing his brother had built his whole ruin out of. "If I leave, they hunt us both, and they find the originals eventually, because we're carrying them. If I stay, if the cave goes into the water with everything else—" he gestured at the walls, the white ribs of ancient ice threaded through the salt, "—then there's nothing left to chase. Copies only. Copies can't be bought back."

"The copies are already going out. To everyone. That's the point, Aldous. You can't buy back a broadcast."

"No," Aldous said softly. "But they'll try. They'll spend fortunes trying, and some of it will work, and in thirty years it'll be a rumor again unless someone stays who knows where every packet came from, which seam of ice, which stratum. Provenance is the spine of a thing like this. Without provenance it's just a story about dead Indians and guilty founders, and stories get rewritten." He smiled, and it was the smile Hazel remembered from childhood, the one Aldous wore when he'd decided something no one could talk him out of. "I stay. The mountain keeps me honest."

"That's not honesty, it's martyrdom, and nobody's asking for it."

"Somebody has to pay for Marisol."

The words landed like a dropped anchor. Hazel stood very still. Below them, far down the valley, the glacier gave a long tearing groan, and a crack ran up the face of the ice wall like lightning frozen mid-strike.

"She wouldn't want this," Hazel said.

"No," Aldous agreed. "She wouldn't. That's what makes it payment instead of theater." He began repacking the empty boxes, unhurried, folding the wax cloth with the neatness of a man closing accounts. "Go, Hazel. The gap won't hold past noon."

* * *

The ridge was a mile of broken scree and old snow, and Hazel covered it in fifty minutes with forty pounds of other people's dead pulling at his shoulders. Twice the glacier spoke behind him—once a boom like quarry blasting, once a sound he had no word for, a vast wet sigh, the sound of something letting go—and twice he made himself keep walking instead of turning around.

The shelf Marisol had shown him was narrow as a church pew, jutting over the valley with the whole drowned world below it: the channel, the ice wall, the tiny white scar of the cave mouth three hundred feet up the valley wall. The radio was a survey unit, salvaged from the wrecked mast, jury-rigged to a battery Aldous had been hoarding. Hazel's hands shook so badly on the first attempt that he keyed pure static across half the territory, and he had to stop, breathe, press his palms flat against the cold stone until they remembered they belonged to a man who had threaded storms for a living.

Then he began to read.

He read everything. The surveyor's ledger with its columns of wages paid in company scrip. The missionary's letter describing the winter camp at the valley mouth, and the second letter apologizing for the delay in writing, and the third letter that wasn't a letter at all but a list—one hundred and fourteen names, ages, families. The founder's private correspondence, cheerful and brisk, ordering the road built through the camp, ordering the matter handled quietly, ordering the ledgers burned. The ledgers hadn't burned. The glacier had taken them instead, folded them into its blue heart, and held them for ninety years the way it held everything: patiently, impartially, waiting for the warm years to give them back.

Behind him, the ice wall began to die.

He heard it go in stages. First the deep report, felt in his teeth rather than heard. Then a roar like a train taking a bridge, and the shelf shuddered under him, and he kept reading, kept keying, voice cracking on the names, reading all one hundred and fourteen because someone had to and because their names were the only thing left of them that wasn't owned. When he reached the last name he looked up.

The cave was gone. Not collapsed—*gone*, the whole shelf sheared away and riding the flood, and where it had been there was only raw blue ice and falling water and a cloud of salt dust hanging in the air like breath in winter. Somewhere under that white water was his brother, and his brother's certainty, and the original sin of the valley, all of it delivered together to the one keeper that never sold anything back.

Hazel keyed the set one last time. "That's all of it. Come and look if you don't believe me. Everyone's welcome." Then he cut the power, shouldered the empty satchel, and went down to the boat.

* * *

The gap in the channel was exactly as wide as Marisol had promised and no wider, a corridor of churning meltwater between walls of grinding floes, and Hazel rowed it the way he'd driven through hail corridors all those years—hands loose, eyes moving, trusting the current more than himself. The Vane brothers' second boat was moored at the lower bend, two men aboard, and they watched him pass without raising a hand. One of them touched his cap. Word had already gone out ahead of him, faster than any boat; that was what broadcasts did. There would be no debt to collect now. There was nothing left to buy, and everyone knew it, and the knowledge sat in the air over the water like weather.

By evening the river widened and gentled and the mountains let go of the sky, and Hazel rowed into the long gold light feeling lighter than he had since he was a boy—lighter than the day he'd signed the Vanes' papers, lighter than the day Aldous had walked out of his life. Emptier too. Grief, he discovered, weighed nothing at all. It was the carrying of it that cost, and he intended to carry it the rest of the way.

At the first settlement lights he shipped the oars and drifted, and in his pocket his fingers found the one thing he'd kept out of the satchel: a single photograph, the youngest face on the list, a girl of nine with her hand raised against the camera's flash. He'd show it to Marisol, when he found her. He owed her that much, and this was one debt he meant to pay in person.
