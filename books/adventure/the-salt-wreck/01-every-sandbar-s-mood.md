## 1. Every Sandbar's Mood

The river woke before Mirabel did, the way it always did — a low grumble of water over gravel, the creak of the mooring line against the Salt Wreck's bow cleat, and somewhere upstream the long mournful note of a foghorn that had no business being out in this shallow stretch. She lay in her bunk beneath the wheelhouse and listened to it all with her eyes closed, cataloguing each sound the way other people said their prayers.

Line's holding. Current's up half a foot — rain in the high country. That horn means the *Vespera* is running early, which means old Captain Idris is drunk again and pushing his luck past the second bend.

Only when she'd accounted for everything did she swing her legs off the bunk and stand, bare feet on cold deck planking, and look at the compass.

It sat on its little shelf by the wheel, brass gone green at the seams, glass scratched from forty years of river grit. Her father's compass. It pointed north the way it always had — stubbornly, slightly wrong, two degrees east of true, a defect he'd never fixed because, he used to say, *a compass that lies to you a little teaches you to think for yourself.* He'd drowned eleven years ago with his pockets full of someone else's debt, and the river had given back only three things: one boot, one oar, and the compass, snagged in a willow root thirty miles downstream as if it had been trying to come home the whole time.

Mirabel picked it up, thumbed the needle steady, and hung it around her neck where it lay against her sternum like a small cold heart.

"Morning," she told the boat.

The Salt Wreck answered with a groan of timber. Forty years old if she was a day, sixty feet of blackened teak and patched ironwood, named — so the story went — for the wreck she'd been built from, some salt-eaten hulk hauled up off the delta and cut into planks that still smelled faintly of the sea no matter how far inland they traveled. Mirabel had grown up on her decks. She'd learned to walk on them, then to swear on them, then to pilot them through water so shallow the fish had to lie flat. The boat was all she had, and every year the river took a little more of her, and every year Mirabel paid to take it back.

She lit the stove, put water on for coffee that would be more chicory than coffee, and climbed to the wheelhouse while it heated.

Dawn came up the color of pewter. Mist stood on the water in columns, and through it the far bank showed as a smudge of green — cane and fig and the pale skeletons of dead trees the floods had drowned but never bothered to kill. A heron stood on the sandbar below her anchorage with the patience of something that had never owed anyone money. Mirabel envied it sincerely.

She was untying the mooring line when she saw the skiff.

It came around the tail of the sandbar quiet as spilled oil — a narrow black boat, two men in it, one rowing, one sitting in the stern with his hands folded like a man arriving at church. Mirabel knew the posture. She knew the boat, too: river-authority gray, government-issue, the kind of craft that existed only to carry bad news at official speeds.

"Well," she said to the heron, "you get to fly away. Lucky bird."

The heron did exactly that.

She could have cast off and run. The current was with her; she'd be around the first bend before they closed half the distance. But running cost fuel, and fuel cost money, and money was the whole reason the skiff was coming at all. So instead she stayed on deck, arms crossed, and waited with the weary competence of a woman who had done this arithmetic too many times.

The skiff bumped against her hull. The man in the stern came up the ladder without asking — they never asked — and produced a folded paper sealed with the wax stamp of the Vess River Court.

"Captain Mirabel Iversen?" His voice was soft, apologetic, the voice of a man whose job required him to be neither.

"That depends on what the paper says."

"It says you're summoned." He held it out. "Hearing's in nine days. Regarding outstanding debt to the Mercantile Trust of Vess. Principal plus four years' interest."

"I know what it says. I wrote the debt."

He blinked. Most debtors wept or swore. Mirabel just looked tired, which unsettled them more.

"Then you'll attend?"

"I'll attend." She took the paper, broke the seal without ceremony, scanned it, and folded it into her breast pocket, where it sat over her heart next to nothing at all. "Tell Master Quillon at the Trust that payment is coming. Tell him the *Salt Wreck* has a contract running."

"He'll want details."

"He can want." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "That's free."

The man retreated down the ladder with the relief of a soldier leaving a trench. The skiff pulled away. Mirabel watched it go until the mist swallowed it, then stood a moment longer with her hand resting flat on the rail, feeling the boat breathe under her palm.

Four hundred and twelve crowns. That was the number. Four hundred and twelve, compounding, a debt her father had signed and she had inherited along with the compass and the boat and the name — because in the river country, debts floated downstream to whoever was left standing. Nine days to find that kind of money, or the court would sell the *Salt Wreck* out from under her keel, and she'd watch some fat merchant's son put a brass bell on her wheelhouse and rename her something obscene like *The Prosperity.*

"No," Mirabel told the mist. "Not today."

She went below, drank her chicory standing up, and got under way.

* * *

The run downriver to Vess took six hours, and Mirabel spent all of them working.

This was the part outsiders never understood — that piloting wasn't steering, it was reading. The river changed its mind hourly, daily, seasonally; every sandbar had a mood, her father used to say, and your job was to know which one it woke up in. Today the bar below Miller's Reach was sulking, its downstream spit reaching further than the charts claimed, so she gave it a wide berth and threaded instead through the flooded willows where the water ran deep but the branches clawed at her rigging like an old woman begging. Past the Reach the channel split three ways around a midstream island, and she took the middle passage on instinct alone, watching the water's surface the way a fortune-teller watches palms — the smooth glassy tongue where the current ran fast and deep, the wrinkled frown of water over the submerged snag, the slow boil downstream of it marking the eddy that would try to spin her stern around if she let it.

Her hands moved on wheel and throttle without consulting her. That was the truth of it: after twenty years, the boat and she shared one nervous system. When the hull shuddered once, briefly, kissing mud off the edge of the bar, she felt it in her teeth before she heard it, and corrected before the sound finished.

A barge passed going the other way, loaded with timber, and the boy at its rail stared at her — a woman alone at the wheel of a riverboat, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth, a brass compass swinging at her throat. Children stared. Old women spat. Men made offers. Mirabel ignored all three categories with equal grace.

By early afternoon the banks widened and softened, the hills fell away, and Vess announced itself the way it always did: by smell. Tar and fish sauce and coal smoke and the sweet rot of the fruit market, all of it braided together and carried a mile downwind. Then the masts, a forest of them, and the gulls screaming their eternal grievances, and the town itself climbing its hillside in crooked tiers of yellow plaster and rust-red tile, crowned by the customs house with its flag hanging limp as a dead thing.

The harbor master waved her to a berth on the south quay, between a shrimp trawler and a grain barge, and Mirabel brought the *Salt Wreck* in with a touch so gentle the fenders barely sighed.

"Captain Iversen." The harbor master, a leathery woman named Odile with ink-stained fingers, handed up the mooring receipt and a slip of paper with it. "Message came for you yesterday. Fella's been asking after boats going upriver. Big money, he says."

"How big?"

"Big enough that he's been buying drinks for every captain in port, and nobody's taken the job yet."

Mirabel read the slip. An address on Lantern Row, above the chandlery. A name: **Corvane**. She didn't know it, which meant either new money or hidden money, and in Vess those were usually the same thing.

"Upriver's a hard sell this time of year," she said. "Monsoon's stacking up over the high country. Another week, maybe two, and the gorges flood."

Odile shrugged. "He knows. Says that's why he's paying what he's paying." She squinted at Mirabel with one eye, the way she did when she was about to say something she'd deny later. "Be careful, girl. Boats that go up there for him — I don't see the same captains come back down. Or I see 'em different."

"Different how?"

"Quieter." Odile turned away, already bored of her own warning. "Berth's paid through tomorrow. Don't sink at my dock."

* * *

Lantern Row smelled of lamp oil and ambition. The chandlery below was shuttered; the stairs beside it climbed to a door of dark varnished wood with a brass knocker polished bright. Mirabel knocked, and the door opened before the echo died, which told her he'd been waiting.

Mr. Corvane was not what she expected. She'd braced for muscle or menace, and instead got elegance: a tall man in a linen suit the color of cream, silver at his temples, a signet ring on one hand and a smile on his face that fit so well it might have been fitted by a tailor. His office behind him was book-lined and cool, and on the desk sat a map of the upper river — the gorge country, the falls, the plateau beyond — weighted open at the exact stretch most captains refused to run.

"Captain Iversen." His voice was warm oil. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Wine? Water? I have coffee, though I'm told it's an insult to the bean."

"Water's fine." She sat, declined the water when it came, and got straight to it, because men like Corvane respected that and counted on the opposite. "You need supplies run upriver. To whom?"

"The Hollis expedition. Archaeological. Professor Aldous Hollis — you may have read about the dig in the papers. Temple complex, pre-delta civilization, very exciting, very dusty." He spread his hands. "They're running short of everything. Food, lamp oil, medicine, tools. The monsoon will close the gorges soon; whatever goes up must go now, and it must go in one load. Your boat is the right size. Your reputation is the right reputation."

"My reputation for what?"

"For getting through." He smiled again. "And for discretion, Captain. That matters to me. Academic expeditions are delicate things. Rivalries, thefts, jealousies. I prefer my deliveries quiet."

"Quiet costs extra."

"Everything costs extra with you, I'm told. Good. I admire a woman who knows her price." He slid a paper across the desk. "Six hundred crowns. Half now, half on delivery."

Mirabel kept her face still, which took work. Six hundred. Six hundred cleared the debt with a hundred and eighty-eight to spare — fuel, food, a season's breathing room. She made herself read the contract twice, slowly, looking for the hook. There was always a hook.

There it was, small as a fishhook in minnow bait: *delivery to coordinates specified by the employer, route and timing at employer's discretion.*

"You're specifying my route," she said.

"I'm specifying your destination. The route is yours — you're the pilot. But timing matters. And I'll want word when you've delivered. A signal fire on the bluff above the falls, say. My people watch for it."

"Why does a supply contract need a signal fire?"

"Why does a supply captain need to ask?" He said it pleasantly, and let the silence do its work, and when she didn't flinch he laughed softly, genuinely delighted. "You're exactly what they said. Very well — call it insurance. If the fire burns, I pay the balance. If it doesn't, I assume the expedition is lost, and I pay you anyway, because that's not your fault. Either way, Captain, you win. That's rare in this world. Take the rare thing."

She should have walked. Later — much later, standing in ash — she would replay this hour and count every wrong note: the overpayment, the route clause, the way his eyes went to the window when he said *the falls*, the map weighted open at the gorges like a man studying a wound. But the summons sat in her breast pocket over her heart, four hundred and twelve crowns heavy, and the monsoon clock was ticking, and Mr. Corvane's money smelled exactly as good as it needed to.

"One condition," she said. "I inspect everything I carry. No livestock, no passengers, nothing that breathes, nothing that burns hotter than lamp oil. I'm a freighter, not a smuggler."

"Of course." He agreed so smoothly that suspicion, which had begun to rise in her chest, found nothing to grip and sat back down. "Loading begins at dawn, Pier Seven. The crates are marked H."

* * *

Pier Seven at dawn was all shadow and fish-rot and the slap of water against pilings. The crates were waiting under a tarp, eight of them, stenciled with a neat white H — and they were wrong.

Mirabel knew cargo the way she knew water. She knew what a crate of rice weighed, what a crate of lamp oil sloshed like, what medical stores rattled. These crates were dense. Dead-dense. When her dockhand — a one-eyed boy called Finch who worked for food and gossip — tried to lift the first one, it barely tilted.

"Captain," he whispered, "what's in these? Lead?"

"Supplies," said the man supervising the loading, a broad silent type with rope-scarred hands who hadn't offered a name. "Heavy tools. Survey equipment."

"Survey equipment for a dig that's already dug," Mirabel said, but she said it to herself, quietly, watching the crane swing the crate aboard and feeling the *Salt Wreck* settle deeper in the water than eight crates of anything legal should make her settle. She walked the deck after, tapping here and there, listening to her boat's voice change under the weight. The old timbers complained in a register she hadn't heard since the flood year.

Finch coiled lines and shot her a look full of questions. She shook her head. Not here. Not ever, probably.

By midmorning the *Salt Wreck* sat low and sullen at the pier, loaded, fueled, provisioned, committed. Mirabel stood at the wheel with the compass warm against her skin and the contract folded in her pocket, and looked upriver — past the masts, past the breakwater, to where the river narrowed in the haze and the gorges began, blue-black walls of stone swallowing the water whole. Above them, far off, the sky over the high country had begun to stack itself with clouds, white towers building in the heat, patient as creditors.

Six hundred crowns. A hundred and eighty-eight clear. Nine days to spare.

So why, she asked the compass, and the compass — lying two degrees east, as always — gave her no answer at all.

She cast off. The current took the bow and swung it upriver, toward the silence and the smoke she couldn't yet see, and the *Salt Wreck* went to meet them both.
