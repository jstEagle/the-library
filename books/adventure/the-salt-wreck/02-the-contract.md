## 2. The Contract

Vess smelled of tar and rotting fruit and money changing hands, and Mirabel Iversen had never trusted any town that smelled of all three at once.

She brought the *Salt Wreck* in on the morning tide, threading the mooring stakes with the ease of long habit — port to starboard, a kiss of reverse, the hull settling against the pilings with a groan like an old man sitting down. The river here was wide and brown and busy, crowded with barges and fishing skiffs and one naval cutter flying the river authority's pennant, its crew lounging on deck in the heat. She noted it, logged it somewhere in the back of her mind where she kept such things, and forgot it.

The harbor master took her fee without looking up from his ledger. The chandler's boy took her list without meeting her eyes. Vess was that kind of place. Everyone was paid; no one was present.

She had six hundred crowns owed and a hundred and eighty-eight clear after this run, if the run went clean, and she had learned — her father had learned before her, though he'd learned it too late — that clean was a word the river used only as bait.

The contract found her before noon.

She was eating a bowl of fish stew at the Saltcellar, the dockside tavern whose owner watered the beer and salted the stew and somehow stayed in business regardless, when the man sat down across from her without asking. That alone would have earned most men a look. He earned one anyway.

He was perhaps fifty, dressed the way rich men dressed when they wanted to seem less rich than they were: good linen, but sun-faded; a plain coat, but cut by someone who understood shoulders. His hair was silver at the temples and oiled flat, and his hands were soft and clean and folded neatly on the table like something he kept for company. He smiled the way a door opens — politely, and because he intended to.

"Captain Iversen." Not a question. He knew her name, which meant he'd asked, which meant he'd spent money on the asking. "My name is Corvane. Aldous Corvane. I'm told you're the best pilot working the upper gorge."

"You were misinformed." She spooned more stew. "I'm the cheapest."

"Modesty. Charming." He signaled the barkeep with two fingers, and a fresh cup arrived at her elbow — coffee, real coffee, dark and expensive, not the chicory swill the Saltcellar served. He'd brought his own supply. That told her more than his name did. "I have cargo for the Hollis expedition. Upstream of the falls."

Mirabel set down her spoon.

Upstream of the falls was three days of gorge running, water that dropped forty feet through channels barely wider than her boat, sandbars that moved with every flood and shifted their moods like cats. Most pilots wouldn't take the contract at any price. The ones who would charged accordingly.

"The Hollis expedition," she said. "The university dig."

"The same. Professor Hollis is an old acquaintance." Something crossed Corvane's face when he said *acquaintance* — brief, unreadable, gone. "They're running low on everything. Food, lamp oil, medicine. The monsoon will close the river within the month, and after that they're cut off until spring. I need this delivered, Captain. Soon, and safely."

"And you came to me because—"

"Because you're the only captain fool enough or desperate enough to make the run, and I've been told which of those two you are." He smiled again, warmer now, and it was a good smile, she'd give him that. It reached his eyes. It just didn't come from them. "Name your price."

"Four hundred crowns. Half now."

"Six hundred. All of it now."

Mirabel looked at him. Six hundred crowns was more than double the going rate. Six hundred crowns was her debt to the harbormaster's office in Lowmark paid off with two hundred left over, was a new stern bearing and a season's worth of hull pitch, was the difference between owning the *Salt Wreck* outright and merely keeping her alive. Six hundred crowns was the kind of money that made a person stop asking questions, and Aldous Corvane knew that better than anyone at the table.

That was exactly what made her ask them.

"Why so much?"

"Because the run is dangerous, and I pay for danger." He spread his soft hands. "Because time matters, and I pay for speed. Because—" and here he leaned forward slightly, and the warmth drained out of the smile so smoothly it was almost impressive, "—I pay for discretion, Captain. My business dealings are my own. What I ship, why I ship it, and to whom — these are questions that don't concern you, and I'd take it as a professional courtesy if you didn't concern yourself with them."

"There it is," Mirabel said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The courtesy. There it is. You got there faster than most." She drank the coffee. It was very good, and she hated him a little for it. "I haul what's on the manifest and I don't open crates that aren't mine. That's not discretion, Mr. Corvane, that's just how freight works. But I'll tell you what I do care about, since we're being courteous." She held up fingers. "One: I don't carry passengers who aren't listed. Two: if the river authority boards me, your manifest better survive inspection. Three: half up front means half up front, and the other half when I tie off at the expedition anchorage — not before, not 'on return,' not 'when my associate confirms.' Cash."

"All reasonable." He produced a leather folio from inside his coat and counted out bills onto the table between them — crisp, banded, real. Three hundred crowns in a stack thick enough to lean on. Then, on top of it, another hundred. "A bonus. For accepting quickly."

Mirabel stared at the money. Four hundred crowns, sitting on a tavern table next to a bowl of fish stew, offered by a stranger who knew her name and her debts and her tolerance for risk. Every instinct she owned stood up and started shouting.

She took the money anyway. She was her father's daughter, and her father had died poor, and poor was the one inheritance she'd sworn to refuse.

"When do you want me loaded?"

"This afternoon. Pier nine." He rose, buttoned his coat, and paused — and here was the thing she'd remember later, in the dark, on the water, when remembering was all she could do. Here was the crack in the lacquer. "One more question, Captain, purely professional. Above the falls — the gorge narrows at Kessel Bend, doesn't it? Before the Devil's Teeth?"

"It narrows everywhere above the falls."

"But Kessel Bend specifically. Is it passable for a vessel of, say, thirty feet? Steel-hulled. Shallow draft."

"A launch, you mean." She watched his face. "Yes. If the pilot knows the channel. Why?"

"No reason. Curiosity." The smile again, the door opening onto nothing. "I've always been curious about rivers. They remember everything and forgive nothing. A remarkable quality in a landscape." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Safe passage, Captain Iversen. I look forward to hearing of your success."

He left. The coffee was still warm. The money was still real. And Mirabel sat there with both and felt, beneath the greed warming her chest like a swallowed coal, a small cold stone of unease settle into her stomach and make itself comfortable.

Steel-hulled, shallow draft, thirty feet. Nobody asks about launches and Kessel Bend unless they intend to send one.

* * *

Pier nine was at the far end of the harbor, past the fish market and the customs house and the row of shuttered warehouses that everyone in Vess pretended weren't used for anything interesting. Two men were waiting with a handcart, and neither of them spoke, and both of them watched her the whole time she worked, which was answer enough about the nature of the cargo.

The manifest said grain, lamp oil, tinned food, medical stores, rope. Eleven crates and four barrels. The barrels were barrels — she rolled one, heard liquid shift, knocked and heard the hollow oil-sound. Fine.

The crates were wrong.

Crates of grain are heavy, but they're heavy evenly, densely, like a sleeping animal. These were heavy the way a stone is heavy — concentrated, dense past reason, each one taking two men and a curse to move. She palmed a corner of the nearest while the handlers argued over the cart and felt the wood: new pine, unbranded, no shipping marks, no customs stamps, no university seal. Nothing. Cargo that traveled upriver to a legitimate archaeological dig ought to have the dig's mark on it somewhere. This had nothing. Cargo with nothing on it was cargo that didn't want to be traced.

And underneath the pine smell, faint but unmistakable to a woman who'd hauled everything the river ever carried, was another scent. Oiled metal. Packing straw. Something old and dense wrapped in something protective.

She'd carried contraband before — every river pilot had, once or twice, a cask of untaxed brandy, a bolt of silk, small sins for small fees. This wasn't small. Eleven crates at this weight was a fortune in whatever it was, and fortunes in crates get people killed, usually the people carrying them.

The handlers finished loading and stood waiting, arms crossed, watching her sign the manifest. She signed it. Her name went down on paper next to eleven unmarked crates, and the pen scratched loud in the afternoon quiet, and she thought of her father saying, years ago, laughing, *the river forgives nothing, Mira, but paperwork remembers even less.*

"Mr. Corvane said to tell you," one of the men finally spoke, voice flat as slate, "he wants you underway by dawn tomorrow. Before the fog lifts."

"Fog's cover," Mirabel said. "For whom?"

The man almost smiled. Almost. "For whoever's watching the pier," he said, and walked away.

* * *

She didn't sleep well. She told herself it was the coffee, then the heat, then the noise from the fish market starting before light, and none of it was true. She lay in her bunk in the *Salt Wreck*'s little cabin with the hatch cracked to the river smell and her father's compass on the shelf above her head, its brass case catching the lamplight, its needle lying steady except for that faithful two-degree lie to the east, and she turned the day over and over like a stone in a current.

Six hundred crowns. Paid in full, up front, plus a bonus for speed. No trader pays that for grain and lamp oil. No honest man asks about steel-hulled launches and Kessel Bend. No legitimate cargo ships in unmarked crates under fog cover.

And no pilot with debts like hers says no to six hundred crowns, either. That was the arithmetic that mattered, and she hated how cleanly it summed.

Toward midnight she heard footsteps on the pier — slow, unhurried, pausing near her mooring. She slid from the bunk barefoot, took the boathook from its brackets, and stood in the dark of the cabin doorway listening. The footsteps stopped. Someone stood on the pier above her, breathing the same river air, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

Then a match flared. By its light she saw, briefly, a man's silhouette at the pier rail — broad, still, looking not at her boat but upriver, toward the falls, toward the black line of the gorge where the water ran fast and quiet and kept its secrets. The match went out. The footsteps receded. Whoever it was hadn't looked at the *Salt Wreck* at all.

Upriver. Everyone in this story kept looking upriver.

Mirabel stood in her doorway until the harbor was silent again, then went back to bed and lay staring at the overhead beams until the sky behind the hatch went grey, then pale, then gold. At first light she lit the boiler, cast off the lines, and took the *Salt Wreck* out of Vess with the fog still thick on the water, exactly as instructed, exactly on schedule, eleven impossible crates lashed in her hold and four hundred crowns sewn into her coat lining.

The fog closed behind her like a curtain. Ahead, faint through the murk, the falls muttered their low continuous warning, and beyond the falls the gorge waited, and beyond the gorge —

Beyond the gorge, three days up, a professor she'd never met was waiting for supplies that might not be supplies at all.

She checked the compass. Two degrees east, as always. She corrected for it, the way she always did, and drove on into the white silence.
