## 3. Upstream of Silence

The gorge began the way all bad ideas begin: gradually, then all at once.

For the first hour out of Vess the river had been wide and lazy, brown-gold in the morning light, fringed with reeds that hissed when the wind moved through them. Mirabel had run that stretch so many times she could have steered it blind — every sandbar's mood memorized, every snag logged in the back of her skull alongside her father's voice saying *watch the water, not the chart, the water tells you first*. But past the old customs post the banks began to lean inward, and the river remembered it was young.

The walls rose gray and dripping on either side, close enough some mornings to touch if she'd been fool enough to lean out over the rail. Ferns hung from cracks in the limestone like green tongues. The air changed too — cooler, wetter, tasting of stone and rot and something mineral, almost metallic, as though the gorge itself were bleeding slowly into the current. Echoes came back wrong here. Her engine note doubled and trebled off the cliffs until it sounded like three boats running where there was only one, and once, in a narrow bend where the water funneled between two leaning slabs of rock, the echo came back *before* the sound, or seemed to, and she'd gripped the wheel tighter without knowing why.

She ran the wheel with two fingers and let the boat talk to her. That was the trick nobody could teach you. The Salt Wreck was forty years old and honest about her opinions; she shuddered a half-second before she hit shallow water, grumbled through her timbers when the current shoved her stern sideways, and this morning she was complaining steadily about the load. Those crates sat stacked amidships under tarps, lashed down twice because Mirabel didn't trust them. They rode too low for their size. When the boat rolled in the current's eddies, they shifted with a heavy, deliberate slowness, like sleeping animals turning over.

*Stone*, she'd thought, back in Vess, watching the dockhands sweat. *Or metal.* She hadn't asked. Corvane had paid double the going rate and asked her to keep questions off the manifest, and greed, she told herself, was just hunger wearing a better coat. Her father had starved politely his whole life and drowned owing money anyway. She would not be polite.

Still, her eyes kept drifting to the tarps.

The radio crackled at midday.

It had been muttering static since dawn — the gorge ate signals the way it ate echoes — but at noon, as she threaded the Narrows where the channel kinked twice around a drowned forest of dead snags, the static suddenly organized itself into a voice.

Mirabel lunged for the set, one hand leaving the wheel, and the Salt Wreck drifted a boat-length toward the snags before she hauled her back. She stood with her hip against the console, ear bent to the speaker, heart going hard against her ribs.

"—copy, *Salt Wreck*, do you—" A burst of static tore the words apart. Then, faintly, a man's voice again, thinner now, stretched taut as wire: "—repeat, do not come up. I say again—"

"Expedition vessel, this is *Salt Wreck*," she said into the handset. "I'm reading you broken. Say again your last."

Static. Long enough that she thought the connection had died. Then the voice came back, closer somehow, as if the man had carried the radio to a window or a hilltop, and what he said next came through almost clean:

"—not who we thought—"

And then the gorge took it. The signal shredded into white noise, and beneath the noise, very briefly, she heard something else — a second voice, farther away, shouting words she couldn't make out, and a sound like a door slamming, or a shot, flattened by distance into a single dull knock.

Then nothing. Just the river hiss of dead air.

Mirabel stood holding the handset for a long time, long enough that her arm ached, calling every few minutes. "*Salt Wreck* to expedition camp. Come in." Nothing. The radio gave back only that dry ocean sound, static like sand pouring through a hole in the world.

*Not who we thought.*

She set the handset down and looked upriver, at the place where the gorge bent north and the cliffs closed until you couldn't see water at all, only rock and the pale slit of sky above it. Somewhere beyond that bend, two days' run upstream, thirty people were waiting on her supplies. Or had been waiting. Or —

She made herself finish the thought, because unfinished thoughts were worse. *Or were waiting for something else entirely.*

Hollis. Professor Aldous Hollis, according to the contract — an archaeologist with a university seal on his letterhead and a grant number and the fussy, precise handwriting of a man who counted his pencils. The dig site lay above the falls, in country so remote the maps went vague, marked with the cartographer's shrug of dotted lines and a note in faded ink: *ruins, extent unknown*. Hollis had written asking after river conditions above the falls, seasonal water levels, the mood of the gorges in monsoon. Sensible questions. Scholarly questions.

Corvane had asked different ones. How fast could she run loaded. Did she know the back channels below Kessel Bend. Had she ever carried cargo she preferred not to declare. He'd smiled when he asked, smooth as oiled teak, and she'd answered carefully, and taken the money, and told herself the unease in her gut was just the river changing weather.

Now the radio said otherwise. Now the radio said *do not come up* in a voice scraped raw, and said *not who we thought*, and somewhere behind those words a door had slammed or a gun had gone off, and no amount of calling would raise anyone again.

She could turn around.

The thought arrived quietly, reasonably, wearing sensible clothes. Turn the boat at the next wide bend, run back down to Vess, hand Corvane his crates unopened, tell him the gorge was impassable, the monsoon early, anything. Eat the loss of half the payment — he'd never pay the balance, not now — and lose the boat anyway, eventually, to the debt collectors circling her like gulls over a dying fish. Because that was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: the Salt Wreck was mortgaged down to her father's compass, and the only reason she was in this gorge at all was that Corvane's money was the first money in two years big enough to matter.

Turn around, and the debt collectors got the boat by winter. Keep going, and whatever waited above the falls got its chance at her instead.

Mirabel laughed once, short and without humor, and the echo threw it back at her from three directions, so that the gorge itself seemed to chuckle at her reasoning.

"Watch the water," she said aloud, to nobody, to him, and put both hands on the wheel.

* * *

She made the upper pools by late afternoon.

Here the gorge opened its throat a little — the cliffs fell back to leave a bowl of sluggish, deep-green water, ringed with mangroves that grew right down into the current, their tangled roots making black lacework on the surface. Insects rose off the water in columns you could see from fifty yards, glittering spirals in the last of the light. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful, in the way a photograph of a stranger's family is beautiful: technically lovely, and utterly indifferent to you.

But it was quiet. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that raised the hair on her forearms. Gorges like this were never quiet. There should have been birds working the shallows, monkeys arguing in the canopy, the far-off knock of woodpeckers. Instead there was only the engine's idle and the water lapping at the hull and, once, a single bird cry that echoed and echoed and echoed until it sounded less like a bird than like the gorge mocking the idea of birds.

Something had gone through this country and emptied it.

She found the mooring pool the expedition's advance letter had described — a cut in the east bank where the current slackened, deep enough to swing at anchor, sheltered from the worst of any coming weather by a shoulder of rock. She brought the Salt Wreck in slow, backing down against the current, and dropped the hook with a rattle that rang off the cliffs like a gunshot. The echoes took nearly a minute to die.

While the light lasted she worked. Checked the lashings on the crates — and hated herself a little for how carefully she checked them, pressing her palm flat against the tarp, feeling the cold solidity underneath, trying to guess at contents by weight and shape the way a blind woman reads a face. Hauled the bilge pump onto deck and tested it. Coiled lines. Oiled the wheel bearing until it turned silent as a thought. Work was good. Work kept the mind in the hands, and her hands knew what they were doing even when the rest of her did not.

The sky was the problem.

All afternoon the clouds had been building in the northwest, stacking themselves in enormous silent towers above the gorge rim, white and gold and then, as the sun dropped, a bruised violet-gray underneath. They didn't drift the way clouds were supposed to drift. They *built*, rising like smoke from a fire too far away to hear, layer upon layer, and the air had gone still and heavy in that particular way that made every breath feel borrowed. Old river people called it the weighing — the sky deciding what it would spend. Monsoon season wasn't due for another week. The sky had clearly not read the calendar.

Her father used to say the monsoon didn't arrive, it *remembered* — came back every year like a creditor, and the whole river paid.

Mirabel cooked rice and dried fish on the little deck stove and ate sitting on the hatch coaming with her back against the wheelhouse, watching the light go. The mosquitoes came at dusk in singing clouds and she smoked them off with a coil of punk that smelled like burnt sugar and church incense. The first stars came out overhead, hard and white in the narrow slot of sky, but the northwest stayed dark — not cloud-dark, but *full*-dark, a horizon piled with waiting weather, and every few minutes heat lightning flickered inside it, silent, like something blinking behind a closed door.

She thought about the voice on the radio. She kept hearing it in different registers — frightened, angry, urgent — trying to fit it to a face she'd never seen. *Do not come up.* A man who'd known what was coming. Who'd had maybe seconds to say it, and spent them on a stranger in a boat.

*Not who we thought.*

Not who we thought. Which meant they had thought someone was someone else. Which meant the expedition had trusted somebody — hired somebody, welcomed somebody aboard — who was not what they claimed. And the professor's careful letters, the university seal, the grant number — how hard was it to forge a letterhead, really? How hard was it to hire a riverboat captain who was desperate enough not to look too closely at her cargo?

The answer arrived in her chest like cold water: not hard at all. Not hard at all, if you found a woman whose boat was mortgaged and whose pride was bigger than her caution, and offered her double rate.

"They hired me," she said to the dark water. "Whoever they are. They hired *me*."

The river said nothing. The river was very good at saying nothing.

* * *

She slept badly, in the bunk under the wheelhouse with the hatch cracked for air, and woke twice — once to the anchor line creaking its slow rhythm, once to nothing at all, just that animal certainty of being watched, lying rigid in the dark while the insects sang and the unseen cliffs leaned over her. The third time she woke it was nearly dawn, and the world had changed color.

The storm front had come down in the night. Not the rain yet — that was still hours off, marching toward her across the high country in a gray wall she could feel more than see — but the light had gone strange, greenish and underwater, and the air pressed down like a hand. The surface of the pool lay flat and oily, not a ripple on it, as if the river were holding its breath. Even the insects had stopped.

Mirabel stood at the rail with her father's compass open in her palm, though she wasn't navigating anywhere. She just wanted the weight of it, the brass worn bright by forty years of thumbs, his and hers. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with its small stubborn faith. Everything else in the world might be a lie — contracts, professors, cargoes, the smooth-voiced men who paid double — but the needle pointed true. Her father had died broke and drowned and the compass had come home to her downstream, fished out of the reeds by a boy who sold it back to her for what she had in her pocket, and still it pointed true.

"Right," she said. Her voice fell flat in the dead air, swallowed whole. "Upstream, then."

She weighed anchor as the first fat drops came down, great slow scattered bombs that struck the pool and left rings like the marks of enormous invisible fingers. Ahead, upriver, the gorge narrowed again into shadow, and above the falls, two days away, whatever remained of Professor Hollis's expedition waited in the silence — or didn't wait, or waited for her specifically, and she would know which soon enough.

Behind her, to the south, faint as a rumor, an engine sound carried across the water. Too steady for the river traffic this far up. Too patient.

Mirabel turned the Salt Wreck's bow into the falling rain and did not look back.
