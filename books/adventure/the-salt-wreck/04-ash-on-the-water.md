## 4. Ash on the Water

The river told her before her eyes did.

Mirabel felt it first as a wrongness in the current — a faint sweetness riding the water where there should have been nothing but green rot and silt. Then came the smell, thin at first, like a match struck in another room, and then thickening until it filled the wheelhouse and sat on the back of her tongue: char, wet ash, something underneath that she chose not to name.

She cut the engine to half and stood a long moment with both hands on the wheel, watching the gorge walls slide past. The monsoon clouds had come down off the ridgeline overnight and now hung low enough to touch, gray as unwashed wool, pressing the river flat. No birds. That was the other thing. For two days the banks had been loud with hornbills and the idiot chatter of kingfishers, and now the air held nothing but the slap of water against the hull and the far-off mutter of the falls behind them.

"Camp's around the next bend," she said aloud, though there was no one to hear it. She'd been talking to herself since Vess. It was a habit of long solo runs, one she'd never managed to break, and she found she didn't want to break it now. A voice — any voice — was a kind of ballast.

The Salt Wreck rounded the bend at a crawl, and Mirabel saw what three days of rain had left of the Hollis expedition.

The camp had been built on a shelf of ground above the east bank, a scatter of canvas tents and timber frames strung along sixty yards of shoreline, and it had burned the way things burn when someone means them to burn — thoroughly, from the center out, so that what remained was not wreckage but architecture: black ribs of joists, a chimney standing alone in its own rubble, tents reduced to lace. The rain had beaten the ash into the mud until the whole site gleamed like a thing oiled. Steam still lifted off it in slow rags where the deeper coals breathed under the wet.

Three days cold, she judged. Maybe four. The rain had done its work well.

She brought the boat alongside the bank without quite deciding to, hands moving on their own old knowledge, lines out, bow fender kissing the mud. The silence bothered her more than the smell did. An expedition this size — Corvane's manifest had listed eleven names, plus porters — would have a cookfire going even dead, would have someone shouting at someone else about crates or coordinates. There should have been noise. There was nothing but the river working at the bank and the hiss of steam.

She took the crowbar from under the wheelhouse seat and stepped onto the shelf.

The mud took her boots to the ankle. She moved through the camp the way she'd once moved through a flooded hold, careful where she put her weight, reading the ground. And the ground read wrong. Ash didn't lie to a woman who'd grown up on a river where disputes were settled with fire as often as fists. This wasn't a cooking fire escaped its pit, wasn't a lamp knocked over in a tent. The burn started at the mess hall — the largest structure, the one at the center — and spread outward evenly, and there were dark stains in the mud that the rain hadn't fully claimed, stains that were not ash and not mud, and boot prints, many boot prints, all of them headed the same direction: down to the water, toward a mooring scar on the bank where a launch had tied off.

They'd come by boat. They'd burned the place, and they'd left by boat, and whoever they were, they hadn't been in any hurry about the burning.

Mirabel stood in the ruin of the mess hall, crowbar loose in her hand, and let herself feel it — that cold drop down the spine, the one that said *you've been paid by the wrong people*. Eleven names on a manifest. She counted what she could find of the camp and stopped counting when she understood that counting wasn't going to give her a number she wanted.

The supply crates were stacked near the landing, half of them split open by heat or by hands. She pried one lid with the crowbar and looked down into the straw packing and the dull shapes nested inside, and closed it again without taking anything out. Whatever those crates held, it wasn't tinned peaches and lamp oil. She'd known that since Vess, in the place where she kept the things she didn't say. Now the knowing had weight.

*Not who we thought.* The radio transmission, three nights ago, torn apart by static. She'd assumed then it meant bandits, or a rival claim-jumper, some ordinary river violence. Standing in the ash, she revised her assumptions downward into darker country.

A sound. Small — a shift of weight, a foot finding a drier stone — somewhere back in the mangroves that crowded the upstream edge of the camp.

Mirabel didn't turn. She went on examining the crates, letting her shoulders loosen, letting her breathing stay slow, while every nerve she owned went to work mapping the space behind her. Mangrove roots made a wall of knees and knuckles twenty feet deep, shadowed under the low sky, full of places a body could wait. Whoever was in there had been in there a while. Three days, maybe. Long enough to watch her boat come upriver, to watch her walk the camp, to decide whether she was rescue or executioner.

"I'm not with whoever did this," she said, conversational, to the crates. "I'm the supply run. Out of Vess. Contract dated the ninth." She pried open a second crate, mostly for show. "Which I'm starting to think makes me part of the problem, but nobody's asked my opinion."

Silence. But a listening silence now, not an empty one. There was a difference; her father had taught her that difference before he taught her to read charts. Empty silence is just absence. Listening silence has a shape.

"You've got three choices," she went on. "Come out, run, or shoot me. If you're going to shoot me, do it soon, because I've got a boat on a falling tide and a schedule I already regret."

The mangroves moved.

He came out badly — that was her first impression, and it stayed. A tall man, gaunt under a week of fear, pushing through the root-wall with a rifle held in both hands in a grip that belonged on a garden tool. He wore a field coat gone gray with mud, spectacles fogged, hair plastered flat, and he held the rifle pointed at a patch of empty air a full yard to her left, barrel wobbling with the tremor in his arms. He'd been crying recently or sweating heavily or both; his face shone.

"Don't," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Don't move. I'll — I know how to use this."

"No, you don't," Mirabel said.

His jaw worked. The rifle dipped, steadied, dipped again. "I've — it's loaded. I checked."

"The safety's on," she said, because it was; she could see the little lever from where she stood. "And you're holding it like a shovel. And you've been crouched in mangrove mud for three days, so your arms are shot. If you try to fire that thing you'll miss me and probably deafen yourself, and then we'll both be embarrassed."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Something closer to relief, terrible and involuntary, the look of a man who has been holding a heavy door shut alone for days and hears another pair of hands. He hated the relief; she could see him hating it. He forced the rifle up again, straightened his elbows, and aimed it at her chest with the grim determination of a man aiming a broom at a snake.

"Who sent you?"

"Corvane. Mr. Aurel Corvane, out of Vess. Supply contract, sealed, overpaid." She watched his face as she said the name, and what crossed it confirmed everything the ash had already told her. "You know him."

"I know the name." His voice had gone flat. Careful. "Everyone upriver knows the name."

"And that's why you're asking who sent me instead of saying thank God." She set the crowbar down on a crate, slowly, showing him her empty hands. "Fair enough. So here's everything I know, and you can weigh it. Three days ago your radio crackled through my set with maybe six clean words out of forty. One of them was *not*, and two of them together were *who we thought*. Since then, nothing. I ran the gorges anyway, because I'm paid to, and because I'm stupid about money. I got here this morning and found this." She turned a slow half-circle, indicating the ruin entire. "Eleven names on the manifest I was given. I count a camp built for twelve, maybe fourteen. I count no living souls except whatever's been breathing in those mangroves hard enough to flatten the ferns. And I count boot prints going down to a mooring scar, which means whoever burned this place left by water, which means they may still be on it."

The rifle sagged. He didn't seem to notice. "Twelve," he said hoarsely. "There were twelve of us. Plus Professor Hollis made thirteen."

"And now?"

"Now there's me." He said it the way a man reports a debt. "Elio Sarr. Cartographer." A pause, and then, with bitter precision, as if the title were a wound he was choosing to press: "*Formerly* cartographer. Of the Hollis expedition."

"Mirabel Iversen." She nodded at the rifle. "You going to point that at me all afternoon? My tea's getting cold."

"There is no tea."

"No. There isn't." She looked at him properly then — the shaking arms, the mud to the thigh, the eyes red-rimmed and sunken and absolutely awake, awake the way only the freshly bereaved are awake, running on some fuel that burns hot and fast and leaves nothing behind. He was starving. He was terrified. And he had survived three days within earshot of this ruin, close enough to smell it, hiding from men who would come back for him if they suspected.

"Put it down, Elio Sarr," she said. "Or keep it. But if you keep it, keep it down. Because here's my situation: I have a boat, I have a contract I no longer believe in, and I have a growing suspicion that the man who signed it is the reason your camp smells like this. Which means you and I might be useful to each other, or it means you're bait and I'm the hook, and either way I'd rather talk about it over something warmer than ash."

He lowered the rifle an inch. Then another inch. Then, all at once, as if the decision had been made somewhere below thought, he let the butt sink into the mud and leaned on it like a staff, and the trembling that had been held off by purpose arrived all at once and shook him visibly, head to boots.

"They came at night," he said. Mirabel waited. She was good at waiting; it was most of what river work was. "Four days ago. Five. I don't — the days have gone soft. A launch with no lights, and then lanterns, and then—" His throat worked. "The professor had found something in the crates. Something he wasn't supposed to find. He confronted them. There was an argument, and then there were guns, and I ran. I took the case and I ran, and I have been sitting in that swamp telling myself I went back for people, and knowing I didn't. Knowing I heard—" He stopped. Shut his mouth on whatever came next. Opened it again. "Thirteen people," he said. "And I'm the arithmetic that's left."

Mirabel looked at the ruined mess hall, at the chimney standing in its own rubble, and said nothing, because there was nothing true enough to say.

"What case?" she asked finally.

His hand went to his side — quick, instinctive, the way a man touches a wound — and she saw the outline of it then, slung under his coat against his hip: a hard rectangle, waterproof, the kind of case surveyors carried instruments in and worse men carried other things in.

"It doesn't matter," he said. Too fast. Much too fast.

"Everything matters right now. Everything with edges matters." She took a step toward him and he flinched the rifle up between them, and she stopped, palms out. "Listen to me, cartographer. I hauled your expedition's crates two hundred miles upriver through the Widow's Gorge on a hull that groans like my grandmother. Those crates are wrong — too heavy, too sealed, packed in straw like eggs. Your professor is dead, your camp is ash, and the men who did it left by water on a river I have to go back down. You are standing on my only route home holding a gun you can't fire and a case you won't explain, and you want me to pretend none of that connects?" She shook her head. "I've buried a father on this river. I don't need to understand your grief. But I will not carry secrets blind through water that's already rising. Monsoon's coming — you can see it stacking on the ridge. When it breaks, this whole valley becomes a chute, and everything on this river gets sorted into two piles: what floats, and what doesn't. I intend to be in the first pile. Decide which pile you're helping."

For a long moment he only looked at her, and she let him look, because she knew what he was doing — weighing her the way she'd weighed the crates, testing the load. Rain began to fall, fine and cold, beading on the ash around them, dimpling the puddles that lay in the bootprints of dead men.

"The case stays with me," he said at last. "That's not negotiable. Not for triple pay, not for a gun to my head. Whatever happens — however much you end up wishing you'd left me in the mangroves — that case goes where I go, and it opens when I say, and not before."

"That's a lot of conditions from a man whose safety lever is still on."

"Yes," he said. "It is." And beneath the fear, for the first time, she saw the other thing in him — the stubborn ore under the mud. Men who folded broke in the first hour. This one had held a swamp for three days with a dead man's business strapped to his hip.

Mirabel exhaled through her nose. She picked up her crowbar, turned, and started walking toward the boat, boots sucking in the mud.

"Well?" she said over her shoulder. "Are you coming aboard, or does the swamp get the last word?"

He caught up to her at the gangplank, dragging the rifle, clutching the case, a drowned scholar with a dead man's cargo and no destination — and as he stepped onto her deck, dripping, Mirabel looked past him at the ridge where the monsoon clouds had begun, very quietly, to boil.

* * *

She fed him first. Hardtack, salt pork, the last of the coffee stretched twice — not kindness, she told herself, just fuel management, a crew member was a warm body and warm bodies needed calories. He ate like a man who'd forgotten food existed, mechanically, staring at nothing, and when the cup was empty his hands finally stopped shaking.

Then she put him to work casting off, and he was useless at that too, and she revised her estimate of him upward slightly anyway, because he asked questions instead of pretending. What line went where. Why the fender went amidships. He wrote things down — actually wrote them, in a sodden notebook, in a cartographer's cramped hand — and something about that steadied her more than competence would have.

By the time the Salt Wreck turned her bow downriver, the rain had thickened to a steady gray curtain and the first real wind was worrying the surface into whitecaps. Mirabel stood at the wheel with the compass light on and her father's needle swinging steady north-by-downstream, and beside her the survivor stood holding the rail with both hands, watching the burned camp shrink into the rain.

"They'll come back for the case," he said quietly. "When they realize I have it. They'll come, and they won't send a parley."

Mirabel said nothing. Ahead, far down the gray channel, so small it might have been a trick of the rain, something sat on the water that hadn't been there on her way up.

It was moving. And it was coming toward them.
