## 5. What the Case Holds

The rifle was still in my hands when she came below.

I heard her boots on the deck plates first — a heavy, unhurried tread that belonged to a woman who had already decided what she thought of me and saw no reason to revise it — and then the companionway hatch banged open and Mirabel Iversen filled the doorway with the grey light of the gorge behind her.

"You going to point that at me all night," she said, "or are you going to tell me why three men died upriver?"

"It's not loaded," I said. It was the only honest thing I had to offer, and it sounded pathetic even to me.

"I know it's not loaded. I watched you carry it out of the mangroves like it was a snake that might apologize." She came down the ladder without waiting for permission, which I would learn later was simply how she did everything, and lowered herself onto the bench across from me, forearms on her knees. The lamp between us guttered in the draft from the hatch. Her face was half amber, half shadow, and her eyes went straight past mine to the case strapped across my chest.

The waterproof case. The professor's case. I had slept with it buckled against my ribs for three nights, through the mangroves and the leech-water and the long crawl downstream, and I suppose I had begun to think of it as an organ of my body. She looked at it the way a butcher looks at a carcass.

"That stays with me," I said.

"Everything on this boat stays with you until I say otherwise," she said. "That's not a threat. That's just how boats work. Now talk."

* * *

Here is what I should have said, if I were a brave man: *I knew. From the beginning, I knew, and I took the money anyway.*

Instead I started with the crates, because the crates were easier.

"The supplies you carried up," I said. "You noticed they were wrong."

Her jaw tightened. "Heavy. Sealed. Marked with a shipping company I've never heard of, and I've heard of every shipping company on this river since I was six years old."

"Hollis Antiquarian Transport," I said. "It doesn't exist. It existed once, forty years ago, as a front for something else, and someone at Corvane's desk dug the name out of an archive because dead companies don't ask questions." I unlaced my fingers, laced them again. My hands had been shaking since the camp; I had gotten very good at hiding it, the way a man gets good at hiding a limp. "Professor Hollis — the real one, Aldous Hollis, the man whose name is on the expedition charter — spent eleven seasons mapping the temple complexes above the falls. Genuine work. Careful work. Work nobody funded because there's no gold in survey grids and potsherds. Two years ago the money suddenly arrived. A foundation grant. Generous beyond anything his field had ever seen."

"And he didn't wonder why?"

"He wondered," I said. "He was old, Captain, not stupid. But he was also tired, and his funding had dried up twice, and the university wanted him retired, and here was a benefactor who asked only one thing: that the dig continue, and that certain sealed consignments be stored at the camp and moved upriver on the expedition's own barges when instructed." I looked at the lamp instead of at her. "You carried some of them. The ones heavier than they should have been."

"Bronze," she said flatly. "Or stone. Something dense."

"Chryselephantine figures. Bactrian silver. Funerary bronzes pulled out of sites in the northern valleys by men with shovels and no permits. Looted antiquities, graded and packed in Vess, shipped upriver under a scholar's name, and transferred at the falls to overland couriers who take them to buyers who will never see a customs stamp in their lives." I heard my own voice go dry and clinical, the voice I used for map legends, and hated it. "Black-market antiquities, Captain. Your boat has been a mule for them twice this season, whether you knew it or not."

The silence after that had weight. Somewhere above us the river worked at its endless labor against the hull, that deep patient grinding, and rain began to tick against the deck plating, soft at first, testing.

Mirabel didn't move. "And Hollis?"

"For two years he told himself the story every compromised man tells himself. That he was only storing boxes. That he wasn't complicit, merely convenient. He even managed to skim a little genuine scholarship out from under it — published two papers the foundation couldn't stop him writing." My throat closed. I made myself go on. "But Aldous Hollis kept records. Every consignment number, every date, every transfer point, every signature. He photographed documents. He copied manifests. And eight days ago, one of the couriers got drunk at the falls crossing and mentioned a buyer's name in front of him — a name that connected this river to a collection house in another country entirely — and something in the old man broke. Or woke up. I honestly don't know which."

"What did he do?"

"He did what cartographers do," I said. "He documented. He'd been building the file for months, maybe longer than I know — I only ever saw the last of it. Photographs, ledgers, correspondence, a full accounting of every piece that had passed through the camp and every hand it passed through on the way. Names, Captain. Real names, including names upstream of Corvane, people who think themselves untouchable." I finally unbuckled the strap. My hands were steadier doing it than they had been all day, which tells you something about what fear does and doesn't live in. "Three nights ago he called the senior staff into his tent and showed us. He said, *this goes downriver tomorrow, and then we are done with these people.*"

The lamp flame leaned. Rain was coming harder now, drumming on the deck overhead, and beneath it the river ground on.

"And Corvane's men?"

"Came the next night," I said. "Not smugglers skulking — soldiers of a kind. Six of them, maybe eight, with a launch and fuel drums and a very short list of tasks. They took the consignment crates that mattered. They burned the rest. The tents, the stores, the records Hollis kept outside the case, the boats." I stopped. Started again. "They shot the professor on the bank. I want to say he died quickly but I wasn't there — none of us who lived were there, that's rather the point of us. Amara saw the fire from the cook shelter. Juno — the professor's daughter — Juno was in the survey trench and she ran for the reeds and I—" 

I had not said any of this aloud before. I discovered that saying it aloud was a different act entirely from knowing it, the way a chart is a different thing from the water. In the chart, the deaths were marks. Out loud, they were people, and my voice cracked on the girl's name like a hull on a sandbar.

Mirabel waited. She was good at waiting. I would learn that too.

"I ran," I said. "I'm the one who drew the escape routes on the site maps — flood routes, evacuation routes, it's standard practice, I drew them myself two seasons ago and never thought I'd need them. So I knew where the mangrove channel was, and I took the case, because Hollis put it in my hands an hour before and said *you're the one who knows the water, Elio, get it downriver,* and I went into the reeds while my colleagues burned." I looked at my hands. "Two others made it out. You'll probably find them downstream. The cook, Amara — wounded, I don't know how badly. And the professor's daughter. Fifteen years old. I lost them in the dark. I've been moving downstream along the bank hoping — " I stopped. "Hoping."

The rain hammered. Above us, thunder rolled down the gorge like a barrel of stones.

* * *

Mirabel reached across the table and turned the lamp up. A small thing. It felt enormous, that small mercy of light.

"So the case," she said.

I set it on the table between us. It was dented along one edge where I'd fallen, scarred with mud, and lighter than it looked, which had always struck me as wrong — such a small box to hold so much ruin. "Aldous Hollis's ledger. His evidence. Every transaction, every name, every photograph he could take or copy over two years, sealed in oilcloth inside a waterproof shell. He built it to survive exactly this — he used to joke that he was packing his own coffin, except coffins rot and this won't." I laid my palm flat on the lid. "It is, as far as I know, the only complete record in existence of what Mr. Corvane has been doing on this river. Which means it is worth more than your boat, my life, and everyone else's combined, to the people who want it gone."

"And to everyone else?"

"To everyone else?" I almost laughed. "To everyone else it's a chance. That's what Hollis believed, at the end. That paper could beat money. That if it reached the river authority — the magistrate at Kessel Bend, the survey office, anyone with a seal and a conscience — that the law would do what laws are supposed to do." I looked at her, and I saw her face do something complicated, some private arithmetic she didn't share. "You think it won't."

"I think," Mirabel said slowly, "that I've watched the river courts look the other way my whole life. But that's not the question tonight." She tapped the case once with one blunt finger. "The question is why you. Why did a professor with a whole staff hand his life's evidence to the expedition's *cartographer*? With respect, you draw maps."

"Because I'm the one who knew what the crates were," I said, "and never said so."

There it was. The thing I'd been carrying around the confession like a stone in my boot, working it toward the surface all evening, and now it was out in the lamplight where I couldn't take it back.

She didn't gloat. She just waited, which was worse.

"I was hired eighteen months ago," I said. "Good pay, better than the university ever offered, and I asked exactly one question about the source of it and accepted the answer I was given. I mapped the transfer points for them, Captain. Not the smuggling — I told myself I wasn't mapping the smuggling, only the river, only the channels, only the depths at the falls crossing where their barges needed safe water. But a map of the channels *is* the smuggling. Without my charts their loads don't cross. I drew the roads and told myself I'd never walked them." My voice came out low and even, which surprised me. "When Hollis showed us the ledger, he looked at me first. Not with anger. With something worse. He said, *you could have told me, Elio. Any time in two years, you could have told me.* And then he handed me the case anyway, because I was the one who knew the water."

The rain had settled into its monsoon rhythm now, steady and vast, the sound of a season changing its mind about the world. Through the porthole I could see the gorge walls gone black, the river running high and brown, and somewhere far off lightning stitched the clouds above the falls we'd climbed three days ago, in another life.

Mirabel sat back. For a long moment she said nothing, and I braced for the verdict — the contempt, the order to swim for shore, whatever a woman like her did with a man like me.

What she said was: "My father drowned running a debt he couldn't pay."

I blinked. "I — I'm sorry?"

"He borrowed from men like Corvane. Smaller scale, same animal. Boat repairs, a bad season, interest like a tide that only comes in." She was looking at the porthole, not at me. "So I'm not going to pretend I don't understand how a person ends up rowing for people they shouldn't. Understanding isn't forgiveness. But it's not nothing, either." She turned back, and her eyes were hard and clear and terribly tired. "Here's what matters to me, Sarr. Those men burned a camp and killed an old man and left a fifteen-year-old girl orphaned in the mangroves, and they'll burn whatever else is in their way, including a riverboat captain who asks too few questions. I am now involved whether I like it or not, because I carried the crates and they know my boat and my face. So the case comes downriver on my vessel. Not because I trust you. Because the alternative is letting them win, and I have spent my whole life losing to men like them one debt at a time, and I find I'm done."

She stood. At the foot of the ladder she paused, one hand on the rail.

"One more thing," she said. "You said names. In the ledger. Names upstream of Corvane."

"Officials," I said. "Concession holders. Men who sign the permits that make the smuggling legal-shaped."

Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, or dread, or both wearing the same coat.

"Then we'd best hope," she said quietly, "that whoever those names belong to hasn't already bought the people we're sailing toward. Get some sleep. We run at first light, and if those clouds mean what I think they mean, we'll be racing weather the whole way down."

She went up the ladder. The hatch closed. I sat alone with the lamp and the case and the sound of the rising river, and I put my hand flat on the oilcloth inside my shirt, over the heartbeat of a dead man's proof, and understood that I had traded one cargo for another — that I was still, even now, carrying freight for other people's fortunes.

Outside, the monsoon came down like a verdict.

And far downstream, though I wouldn't know it until morning, a launch with no lights was already on the water.
