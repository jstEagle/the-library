## 6. The Debt Collector

The river went quiet an hour before Rook came.

Mirabel felt it first in the wheel — a lightness, as if the current itself had drawn breath. The Salt Wreck's bow swung a half-point off true and she corrected without thinking, the way she'd correct her own heartbeat if she could reach it. Then the birds stopped. Not scattered, not startled — simply stopped, all at once, along both banks of the gorge, as though someone had passed a hand over the world and hushed it.

She had been running the middle channel since dawn, threading the flooded gorge with the monsoon clouds stacking behind her like a debt come to collect. Elio slept below, or pretended to, curled around that waterproof case the way a man curls around a wound. She'd offered twice to take it from him. Twice he'd looked at her with those bruised, careful eyes and said nothing, and the nothing had been answer enough.

The case was trouble. She'd known it since the mangroves, since he'd walked out of the ash-smell of that burned camp with a rifle he held like a garden tool and a look on his face like a man who had seen the bottom of something deep. Trouble came in two kinds on this river: the kind you outran, and the kind you carried. She was carrying this one, and she was carrying it upriver of her own debts, which meant she couldn't afford to put it down.

Her father's compass sat in its brass cradle by the wheel, needle steady, pointing them home the long way around. *Home* being a boat with a strained hull and a hold full of crates she should never have agreed to haul.

"Still with us?" she called down through the deck hatch.

A pause. Then Elio's voice, rough with sleep: "The water sounds different."

"It always does before bad news." She said it lightly. She wasn't feeling light.

* * *

Rook's launch appeared at the bend below Kessel Bend, where the gorge narrowed and the cliffs threw their shadows across the water early, so that noon arrived looking like dusk. Mirabel saw the wake first — a white seam stitched straight up the river, too fast, too straight, no fisherman alive who ran a boat like that. Then the shape of it: low, black-hulled, engines tuned to a snarl, and a man standing at the bow with the relaxed posture of someone who had never once been in a hurry because everyone else hurried toward him.

Dane Rook. She didn't know the name yet, but she knew the type. Corvane's money had a smell, and this man wore it like cologne.

He came alongside without slowing until the last possible moment, then killed his engines so smoothly the launch drifted the final twenty yards and kissed its fenders against the Salt Wreck's hull with a courtesy that made her skin crawl. Politeness, from men like this, was just violence wearing its good coat.

"Captain Iversen." His voice carried across the still water easily, pitched for her alone. He was lean, gray at the temples, with a face like a folded map — all creases and no expression you could read twice the same way. "Mr. Corvane sends his regards."

"Never met the man," Mirabel said. "I meet his money, though. It spends fine."

"It does." He smiled without warmth, the way ice smiles. "And it would like to spend more. Triple your contract. Cash, on delivery, when you hand over your passenger and his luggage."

Below her feet, she heard the deck creak — Elio, awake now, listening. Good. Let him hear what his company cost.

"Triple," Mirabel repeated. She leaned on the rail, made herself comfortable, made him wait. "That's a lot of money for one skinny cartographer and a box."

"The box is the valuable part. The man is negotiable." Rook's eyes moved past her, cataloguing the boat — the patched gunwale, the tired engine housing, the water-stain creeping along the hull line where she'd been taking a little more than seepage since the gorge run. He saw everything. He was letting her see him see everything. "You're riding low, Captain. Monsoon's coming. Your bilge pump is working harder than your engine, if my ears are right. A woman in your position shouldn't be choosy about income."

"My position is my business."

"Your position is Mr. Corvane's business now. That's how contracts work. You signed one." He tilted his head. "Or did nobody explain to you what you were carrying? No — don't answer. It doesn't matter. What matters is that you're a transport, Captain, and transports get paid. Men who confuse themselves with something more than transports get buried under their own keels. There are three boats on this river that used to belong to such men. I can point out the sandbars they're under."

The gorge walls caught his voice and gave it back doubled, so it seemed the cliffs themselves were making the offer.

Mirabel thought about the number. She'd be lying — she was lying, right there behind her teeth — if she said triple pay didn't light something up in her chest. Three contracts' worth of coin would clear the yard debt in Vess, recaulk the hull proper, replace the pump, buy fuel enough to run fat and easy through the whole monsoon season instead of thin and desperate. It would buy the thing she wanted most in the world, which was not owing anyone.

And that was exactly why she couldn't take it. Because the money wasn't a payment. It was a hook, and hooks only look like gifts from the outside.

"You know what my father told me about offers on the water?" she said.

"I imagine he told you many things."

"He drowned." She said it flat, watching Rook's face for the flicker. "Running cargo for a man who talked smooth. So here's my counteroffer. You turn that pretty launch around, you go back to your Mr. Corvane, and you tell him the Salt Wreck delivers what it's paid to deliver. All of it. To whom it's owed."

Something moved behind Rook's eyes — not anger. Interest. That was worse.

"Spite," he said softly. "Not principle. I can hear the difference. Principle takes payment; spite doesn't. You're not refusing because the man below decks deserves protecting. You're refusing because you hate being bought." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and it was terrible. "Good. Spite keeps. We can work with spite later, when the money matters more."

"There won't be a later."

"There's always a later, Captain. The river guarantees it." He straightened, and the transactional warmth drained out of him like water through a cracked hull, leaving something colder underneath. "Last courtesy, then. The case. Hand it over and walk away rich, or keep it and learn what it costs to be the only thing standing between Mr. Corvane and his property. I'd think hard. Your boat leaks. Your season is dying. And I have all the time the river allows."

"And I have a schedule," Mirabel said. "Get off my deck."

"I'm not on your deck."

"Then get off my river."

For a moment — just a moment — something almost like respect crossed his folded face. Then he stepped back onto his launch, unhurried, and his crew cast off the fender lines, and the black hull slid away from hers with the same terrible smoothness it had arrived.

"One more thing, Captain." He raised a hand, casual as a man waving to a neighbor. "Since you've chosen the expensive option."

The shot came off the launch's stern rail and cracked across the gorge like the sky splitting. The ball took the water ten feet off the Salt Wreck's bow and threw up a column of spray that rained across the foredeck, and the echo went rolling up the cliff walls and came back doubled, tripled, the whole gorge ringing with it like a bell struck by God.

Elio came up the hatch ladder three rungs at a time, white-faced, the case clutched to his chest. "What—"

"Hold something," Mirabel snapped, already at the throttle.

The second shot hit the wheelhouse roof with a sound like a hammer on a coffin lid, and splinters stung the back of her neck, and she stopped thinking entirely.

She brought the Salt Wreck around hard, engine screaming, and drove her at the launch — not away from it, *at* it, because Rook expected flight and flight was a pattern and patterns got people killed on this river. The black hull's crew scrambled, surprised, oaths carrying across the water, and for three glorious seconds Dane Rook's folded face showed genuine uncertainty as forty tons of aging riverboat bore down on him with her horn bellowing like something prehistoric.

Then she swung off, at the last possible instant, using the wash of her own turn to slap a wave against the launch's flank and send it rocking, and she opened the throttle all the way and ran for the narrows above Kessel Bend, where the Salt Wreck drew less water than anything Rook's launch could follow.

Behind them, the third shot fell short and died in the current.

* * *

They ran the narrows in silence except for the engine and the water and Mirabel's heart, which had climbed into her throat and refused to come down. Only when the cliffs closed behind them and the channel widened into brown, slow water did she ease back on the throttle and let her hands remember how to shake.

Elio stood beside her in the wheelhouse, dripping spray, holding the case like a shield. He was staring back the way they'd come.

"He let us go," Mirabel said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You understand that. He could have put a ball through our hull below the waterline any time he liked. He shot the bow. He shot the roof. He shot *water*."

"Maybe he's a bad shot."

"Nobody pays a man like that to be a bad shot." She wiped spray off the compass glass with her sleeve. The needle hadn't wavered once. Good old brass. Good old dead father. "He wanted us running. Scared boats make mistakes, and scared captains make deals. He'll come again when we're tired, or leaking worse, or cornered somewhere with no narrows to hide in."

Elio sat down slowly on the engineer's stool, the case across his knees. "Triple pay," he said quietly. "You could have cleared every debt you have."

"I know what I could have cleared."

"Why, then? Truly. If not virtue—" He caught her look and had the grace to flinch. "Forgive me. But a man burned my camp and killed my professor, and I watched a stranger refuse a fortune rather than hand me over. I'd like to understand the arithmetic."

Mirabel was quiet a while. The river slid by, brown and patient, carrying its silt and its secrets down toward the sea. Somewhere ahead the first rain-smell was coming off the water, green and mineral, the monsoon announcing itself the way creditors announce themselves — politely, from a distance, with plenty of time to prepare.

"Not arithmetic," she said finally. "Spite. Like he said. I spent six years paying off a debt that started with a smooth-talking man offering my father money on the water. I know how those offers end. They end with the boat going down and the family paying for the privilege." She turned the compass a quarter-turn, needlessly, just to touch it. "Corvane can't buy me because I'm not for sale. And he can't threaten me because—" her jaw tightened— "because I am *so tired* of being threatened. That's all it is. Don't dress it up as courage. Courage costs extra."

Elio almost smiled. It was the first thing on his face that hadn't been grief since the mangroves. "Spite," he said. "As a moral philosophy."

"It's kept me afloat longer than virtue ever did."

But even as she said it, her eyes went to the hull line, where the water-stain had crept another inch since morning, and to the sky behind them, where the clouds had finished stacking and begun, very quietly, to move.

The bilge pump coughed — a wet, laboring sound, like a man climbing stairs with stones in his pockets — and started again.

Mirabel listened to it, and to the rain beginning to speckle the river far upstream, and understood that Rook had been right about one thing.

There was always a later.
