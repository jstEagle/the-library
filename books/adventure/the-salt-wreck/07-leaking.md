## 7. Leaking

The sound woke me before the water did.

A knock from below the deck — soft, patient, like a knuckle rapping on a door that had no business being there. I lay in the narrow bunk Mirabel had grudgingly surrendered to me, listening to the river slide past the hull, and told myself it was driftwood. It was always driftwood on a river. The water was full of things that knocked.

Then I put my hand flat against the planking beside my head and felt the cold come through.

Not the ordinary cold of river-water held at arm's length by good oakum and honest caulking. This was a thin, insistent chill, spreading across my palm like breath on glass. I pressed harder. Somewhere beneath my fingers, in the dark between frames, the Salt Wreck was drinking.

I dressed in the grey half-light without lighting the lamp. The cabin smelled of diesel and old coffee and the ghost of someone else's tobacco — Mirabel's father, I supposed; the boat kept his habits the way widows keep their husbands' coats. I climbed out into a morning the color of pewter. The monsoon clouds had come down off the hills overnight and now stood along the whole southern horizon like a wall built by something with too much time on its hands. Ahead of us the gorge walls fell away into the first wide reach of the lower river, brown and swollen and deceptively calm.

Mirabel was at the wheel, one hand resting on it the way other people rest a hand on a sleeping animal. She didn't look around when I came up.

"You're up early," she said.

"There's water in the bilge."

"I know."

"How long have you known?"

"Since about three this morning." She adjusted the wheel a degree, maybe two. The compass — her father's compass, brass gone green at the seams, the needle trembling in its card like something alive — sat in its box at her elbow. "She's been weeping since the gorge run. Strained her on the third chute when I took that line too close to the north wall. My fault. I was in a hurry."

"A hurry to get away from the man who shot at us," I said. "Forgive me for not flagellating myself over your haste."

Her mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile, but it lived on the same street. "Bilge's got maybe four inches in her now. That's not a problem. Four inches an hour would be a problem." She nodded toward a hatch aft of the wheelhouse. "Pump's under there. Hand pump. You're going to learn how it works."

"I'm a cartographer," I said. "I draw coastlines. I don't fight them."

"Today you do."

* * *

The pump was older than I was, which is a thing you could say about most of the Salt Wreck. A cast-iron barrel painted the green of old bottle-glass, a wooden handle worn smooth and pale where forty years of hands had gripped it, a leather cup at the bottom of the bore that Mirabel showed me how to wet so it would seat against the cylinder and actually move water instead of just performing the idea of pumping.

"Long strokes," she said. "Let the handle come all the way down. If you rush it you'll cavitate and pull air, and then you'll be pumping nothing with great enthusiasm, which describes most men I've met."

I set my hands on the wood and began.

It is brutal work, bailing a boat, and I say that as a man who once spent eleven days surveying a swamp with a fever. The handle fought me on every downstroke. Water geysered out of the discharge spout in ragged pulses, arcing over the rail and back into the river it had come from, and after ten minutes my shoulders were burning and my shirt was soaked with spray and sweat in equal measure. After twenty minutes I stopped counting minutes and started counting strokes, and after thirty I stopped counting anything at all and simply became a machine for moving water, breathing in time with the handle, the river chattering past the hull a foot below my boots.

Mirabel ran the boat and watched me sideways.

At some point she came aft, crouched at the bilge inspection port, and shone a flashlight down into the dark. "Slowing," she reported. "You're beating her. Barely."

"Barely counts?"

"On this river, barely is a currency. Spend it carefully." She straightened, wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime in the previous decade, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read. "There's a seam forward of the engine bed that's opened up. Maybe a hand's width. I can't get at it properly while we're underway — need to beach her somewhere quiet, careen her, recaulk. There's a slough about six miles down, past the drowned orchard. We'll stop there tonight."

"And Rook?"

"Rook's launch draws too much to follow us into a slough." She said it lightly, but her eyes went south, to the horizon where the clouds were stacking themselves into architecture. "Weather'll cover us anyway. First real rain should hit by tomorrow night. Maybe the day after."

"So we hide."

"We *maintain*," she corrected. "Hiding is what cowards call maintenance."

I pumped. The morning aged. The gorge gave way to open country — terraced hillsides running down to the water, a fishing platform on stilts where an old man raised one hand as we passed, not waving exactly, more like acknowledging that we existed and permitting it. The river here was broad and slow and brown as tea, carrying whole trees on its back, and it was hard to believe that two days ago it had been trying to kill us in the narrows, and harder still to believe that in another day or two it would try again in earnest, with the monsoon behind it.

My arms were dead weight by noon. Mirabel relieved me without comment and pumped for an hour herself, standing at the handle with her hips squared and her strokes long and even, making it look like the easiest thing in the world, which I resented deeply and silently.

We ate rice and tinned fish in the wheelhouse. She ate the way she did everything — efficiently, without ceremony, watching the water even between bites. I ate the way I did everything, which lately was badly.

"The case," she said, halfway through. Not looking at me. "The professor's ledger. You've been carrying it like it's a sleeping child. I want to know what's in it. All of it. Before I decide how much trouble I'm in."

"You already know how much trouble you're in."

"Humor me."

So I told her. Or some of it. I told her about the crates — how they'd come upriver on Corvane's barges under tarps, manifest listed as expedition equipment, and how heavy they'd been, heavier than any instruments, and how the diggers had been paid extra not to ask. How Professor Hollis had pried one open on the third night, alone, with a crowbar, because he'd noticed the crates were being stored in *his* tent compound and never moved, never unpacked, never inventoried. Inside: wrapped antiquities. Temple bronzes. Stele fragments. Things pulled out of flooded shrines upriver by men with no permits and no scruples, crated for a buyer downriver who paid in ways that never touched a bank.

"He confronted Corvane's man — Voss, the site security chief — quietly, at first. Thought it was a misunderstanding. Thought he could negotiate." I turned the tin plate in my hands. "Corvane doesn't negotiate. He acquires. When the professor wouldn't be acquired, they burned the camp and shot anyone who ran toward the light instead of away from it."

"And you ran away from it."

"I ran *toward* the mangroves, which is the same thing with better cover." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it does when I'm stepping around something. "I took the ledger out of his tent before the fire reached it. He'd told me where it was. Two days before. He'd said, *if anything happens to me, Elio, the case goes to the river authority at Kessel Bend.*"

"And instead it went into the mangroves with you."

"For three days, yes."

Mirabel set down her fork. "Why?"

The question hung there. I could have lied. I'd been composing lies since the mangroves — good ones, cartographer's lies, precise and plausible. But she had spent the morning pumping beside me, and she had taken a bullet across her bow rather than sell me, and there is a kind of arithmetic that happens between people who have been shot at together, and the lie wouldn't balance.

"Because I owed money," I said. "To Corvane's people, as it happens. Same as half the river. I signed on with the expedition because Hollis paid well and because the salary came with a clause — Corvane's office handled the payroll, and part of my debt was forgiven for every month I served. I knew what the crates were. Not exactly, not the details, but I knew they weren't shovels. I knew enough to know what I was looking at, and I took the money anyway, because the alternative was the kind of debt that follows you onto boats and into towns and eventually through a door at night." I looked at the compass in its box, needle steady, pointing at a north none of us would ever reach. "Your father's boat. Your father's debts. I've read the pattern, Captain. It runs all the way down this river like a fault line."

She was quiet for a long moment. The engine ticked. Somewhere ahead, thunder cleared its throat.

"My father ran cargo," she said finally. "Legitimate, mostly. The last run wasn't. He owed Corvane's predecessor — this goes back further than Corvane, the rot does, Corvane just inherited it like the family silver. One bad monsoon, one overloaded hold, and the river took him at the Teeth. They recovered the compass downstream. They never recovered him." Her thumb moved over the brass box, an unconscious gesture, a woman touching a scar. "I was fourteen. I've been paying his debt ever since — or Corvane's people have been collecting it, which isn't quite the same thing. Every contract I take, a cut goes upstream. That's why I took this one. Triple rate. I thought I could clear the last of it."

"And instead you're ferrying the evidence that could burn Corvane's whole operation, with a hole in your hull and a gunboat somewhere behind us."

"When you put it that way it sounds almost noble."

"It isn't," I said. "Neither is mine. I didn't keep the ledger out of principle. I kept it because giving it up meant admitting what I'd done for the money, and I wasn't ready. Three days in the mangroves eating raw fish, and what I couldn't face wasn't Voss's men. It was the look on Hollis's face when he opened that crate. He looked at me, Mirabel. He looked at everyone who'd helped load them, and he didn't say a word, and that was worse than shouting."

She studied me across the wheelhouse, rain beginning to stipple the windows behind her, fine and cold.

"Well," she said at last. "Now we're both guilty, and both broke, and both afloat. Barely." She stood, gathering the plates. "That's more common ground than most marriages start on. Pump."

* * *

We reached the slough as the light failed.

It opened off the main channel behind a screen of dead snags — a drowned orchard, Mirabel called it, fruit trees planted by someone optimistic two generations ago and swallowed when the river shifted. Their black branches stood out of the brown water like the rigging of sunken ships. Beyond them the slough narrowed into stillness, walled with reeds and willow, deep enough for the Salt Wreck's modest draft and invisible from the channel unless you knew precisely where to look. Mirabel knew. Of course she knew. She knew this river the way I knew my charts — not as information but as memory, the kind that lives in the hands.

She backed us in under the willows, killed the engine, and the silence that followed was enormous. Just water dripping from the branches, frogs tuning up in the reeds, and far to the south a low continuous mutter that might have been wind and might have been the monsoon clearing its throat again.

We worked until full dark, by lantern light. She ran the boat gently aground on the mud shelf at the slough's edge, winched her over on her beam ends with a block and tackle rigged to a willow trunk thick as a church pillar, and exposed the port side of the hull like a surgeon exposing a wound.

The seam was worse than she'd let on. A hand's width was generous; it was closer to two, the caulking rolled out of it in a long grey worm, the planks themselves sprung at the edges where the gorge had hammered us. Looking at it, I understood the knocking I'd heard in the night. The river hadn't been asking to come in. It had been coming in, patiently, all along, and we had been bailing out its patience.

"Reeve me that oakum," Mirabel said, and I learned a second trade.

I learned that oakum is tarred hemp fiber, and that you tease it into loose ropes and drive it into the seam with a caulking iron and a mallet, and that the iron has to strike at the right angle or the fiber just laughs at you. I learned that after the oakum comes hot pitch, poured from a kettle that smells like a road crew in August, and that you seal it with a strip of canvas painted over, and that all of this is done lying on your side in river mud with mosquitoes conducting their evening service around your ears. I learned that Mirabel Marchetti worked in complete silence except for a low stream of profanity delivered with such rhythmic precision it approached music.

And somewhere in the second hour, driving oakum into a seam by lantern light while the first fat drops of the coming storm ticked down through the willow leaves, I understood that something had changed between us. Not friendship — it was too early, and neither of us was built for it yet. Something more useful. The particular trust of two people who have each seen the other's worst room and decided not to mention it again. She knew I'd sold my eyes to Corvane for debt relief. I knew she'd taken this contract to buy her way out of the same trap, and that if the price had been right she might have handed me over at Vess without losing sleep. We were not good people, either of us. We were afloat people. On this river, in this season, that was the higher calling.

"There," she said at last, sitting back on her heels, mud to the elbows, examining the sealed seam the way a jeweler examines a setting. "Good for another season, if the season lets us live that long." She glanced up at the sky, where the cloud-wall had eaten the last of the stars. "Rain by midnight. Real rain. We'll want the pump going on watches through tomorrow regardless — new caulking always weeps a little till it swells."

"I'll take first watch."

"You'll take first watch," she agreed, and there was something in it that wasn't quite surprise, or wasn't only surprise. "Wake me at two."

She went aboard to sleep. I stayed out with the lantern and the mallet and the kettle of pitch, finishing the small seams, and the rain arrived on schedule, first in scattered heavy drops that punched little craters in the slough's skin, then in earnest, a grey curtain marching across the water toward us with the sound of a crowd applauding something terrible.

I sat under the willows with the lantern hooded and watched it come, and I thought about Hollis's face, and about the case lashed under my bunk with its hundred pages of proof, and about the river authority at Kessel Bend that we were supposedly running toward — and about the fact that neither Mirabel nor I had said aloud, yet, the question that had been sitting between us since the mangroves:

Who, exactly, downriver, could we give this ledger to that Corvane hadn't already bought?

The rain hit the slough like a dropped ceiling. And out in the dark, downstream, faint under the drumming — so faint I told myself I imagined it — I heard an engine, running slow, running quiet, hunting the banks.
