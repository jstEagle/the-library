## 9. Monsoon

The sky had been threatening for two days, stacking itself in bruised towers along the western ridge like a fleet at anchor, and on the third morning it finally came for them.

Mirabel heard it before she saw it — a sound like the river itself turning over in its sleep, a low rolling thunder that didn't come from above but from upriver, from around the bend where the gorge narrowed. She was at the wheel with a mug of tea going cold in her hand when the water changed. The current, which had been running lazy and brown all week, suddenly stood up in ridges. Debris came downstream in a ragged flotilla — branches, a drowned goat, half of someone's roof, a woven basket still floating upright as if it had somewhere to be.

"Elio," she said, and her voice came out flatter than she intended, because flatness was how you kept panic from spreading through a boat like bilgewater. "Get below. Tie down anything that isn't tied already."

He appeared at the cabin door with his hair stuck up from sleep and his shirt buttoned wrong, and he looked at the sky the way a man looks at a debt collector's letter — with that particular helpless recognition. "Is that—"

"That's the monsoon." She set down the tea. "Not the edge of it. Not the warning. That's the whole thing arriving at once."

The first rain hit like gravel thrown by an angry hand. Then the second wave hit like something with intent. Within minutes the world beyond the pilothouse glass ceased to exist — no gorge walls, no ridge line, no horizon, just a gray churning nothing with the river rising through the middle of it. The Salt Wreck's deck ran white with water. The rain made a sound against the roof like applause, like a crowd, like ten thousand small fists demanding entry.

Mirabel had run this river eleven seasons. She knew its moods the way other people knew their mothers' faces — every sandbar's temperament, every eddy's grudge, every stretch of water that smiled at you right up until it didn't. And she knew what a river did when the monsoon broke over its headwaters: it became something else entirely. Something with opinions.

"We can't run this," she said aloud, to herself as much as to Elio. "Not in flood. Not with the hull already talking to us."

The hull *was* talking. It had been since the gorge — a faint weeping at the seams below the waterline, a slow ingress they'd been holding at bay with pitch and prayer and Elio's increasingly competent bailing. In flood water, with the river pressing up against the planks like a thumb testing fruit, that leak would go from conversation to argument to screaming match in under an hour.

She needed shelter. Real shelter, stone shelter, something the river couldn't reach into and rearrange.

There was one place. Her father had shown it to her when she was nine years old, pointing it out from the wheelhouse the way he pointed out everything — casually, once, expecting her to remember forever. A limestone cave on the north bank, three miles up, where the cliff had been hollowed out by some patient ancient water. He'd called it the Priest's Hole, because a missionary had supposedly hidden there from headhunters a century back, though Mirabel suspected her father had invented the story entirely, the way he invented half his river lore. What mattered was that the mouth sat thirty feet above the low-water mark, wide enough to take a boat of the Salt Wreck's beam, and deep enough that the back of it had never seen daylight.

If it was still there. If the flood hadn't already claimed it. If her memory hadn't dressed it up in the flattering light of childhood.

"Three miles," she told Elio. "Then we get out of the rain."

* * *

Getting there took the better part of two hours and everything the Salt Wreck had.

The river in flood was not the river. It was the river's angry ghost, wearing its shape. Where there had been channels there were now brown plains; where there had been sandbars there were whirlpools spinning like drains in the sky's spilled bathwater. Mirabel drove by memory alone, reading the water underneath the chaos — feeling through the wheel for the old bones of the channel, the way you'd find a stair rail in a dark house. Twice the boat shuddered as something struck the hull below — a log, maybe, or the top of a drowned tree — and both times she held her breath and waited for the weeping seam to become a wound, and both times the old girl shrugged it off.

Elio stood beside her in the pilothouse, one hand clamped on the rail, the other holding the waterproof case against his chest like a child holds a toy in a storm. He hadn't let go of it in four days. She'd stopped commenting on it. You didn't take a security blanket away from a man whose whole world had burned.

"There," he shouted at last, pointing left into the gray. "Is that—"

It was. The black slot of the cave mouth, high on the cliff face, with water sheeting off the rock above it like a curtain. Below it, the flood had swallowed the usual landing shelf entirely, but the mouth itself was dry, and the overhang threw a wedge of calm water at its base where the current died.

Getting in was the kind of maneuver that aged a person. Mirabel brought the Salt Wreck in sideways, letting the flood do half the work, throttling against the current with the wheel hard over, and the moment the bow crossed into dead water she killed the engine and let momentum carry them in. The boat drifted through the cave mouth like a leaf into a drainpipe, and then, abruptly, there was silence — real silence, the first in hours — as the rock closed around them and the monsoon became something happening elsewhere, to other people.

They warped her off to port side with lines run to natural stone bollards, smooth pillars of flowstone that had been growing in the dark for longer than there had been boats on this river. The cave opened inland of them like the inside of a throat: a vaulted chamber perhaps eighty feet across, its ceiling hung with stalactites that dripped in slow metronome time, its floor a shelf of pale sand that sloped down to the black mirror of flooded deeper passages. Somewhere back in the dark, water moved with a sound like slow breathing.

The Salt Wreck sat at anchor in the mouth, half in, half out, her lantern light making a small warm island in all that cold stone.

Elio stood on the foredeck and turned in a slow circle, and Mirabel watched his face do something complicated. "I've mapped coastlines," he said quietly. "Harbors. River deltas on three continents. I have never once drawn a place like this."

"Most people haven't seen it." She tossed him a coil of line. "My father found it. Or claimed to."

"You said he—" Elio hesitated, the way he always hesitated now when the conversation edged toward her history, as if he'd learned that her past had sharp edges. "You don't talk about him much."

"There's not much to talk about that ends well."

But the rain was hammering outside like it meant to go on all week, and the work was done — lines set, hatches battened, the leak checked and holding, Amara's fever down another degree thanks to Juno's careful hands and the last of Nesta Kessel's willow bark. There was nothing to do but wait. And waiting, Mirabel had learned, was when the past came knocking.

* * *

They ate in the cabin — rice and dried fish, the galley stove hissing against the damp — and then Juno took the first watch below with Amara, and Elio climbed up to sit in the pilothouse where the rain made a gray curtain beyond the glass, and Mirabel brought up the bottle.

Not a good bottle. A river bottle, bought off Nesta Kessel, distilled from cane sugar and regret, the kind of liquor that tasted the way its maker's life had probably gone. But it was warm going down, and warmth mattered tonight.

She poured two tin cups and carried them up, and Elio looked at hers, and then at her, and raised an eyebrow in a way that managed to be both polite and skeptical.

"Don't make that face. It's medicinal."

"It smells like it needs a doctor itself."

"Drink it anyway." She settled onto the bench beside him, shoulder almost touching his shoulder, close enough for warmth and not so close as to mean anything yet. Outside, the rain had found a rhythm, long sustained sheets punctuated by gusts that hit the windows like thrown water. The river beyond was invisible. The world had contracted to this room, this lamplight, these two cups.

For a while neither of them spoke. That was the thing about shared shelter, she thought — it did what weeks of working side by side couldn't. Stranded together in a small lit space while the world outside tried to drown itself, you stopped performing. You just sat.

Elio drank. He winced. He drank again. "So," he said eventually, in the tone of a man opening a door he wasn't sure was locked. "Your father."

"What about him?"

"You keep his compass on the binnacle. You touch it before every run — I've watched you do it, I didn't think you noticed me noticing. And you named your boat after—" He paused. "After a wreck."

"The Salt Wreck." She turned her cup in her hands. "Everyone assumes it's about a ship. It isn't."

The lamp flickered in a draft from the hatch. Beyond the glass, lightning stitched the far canyon wall, and the thunder arrived seconds later, rolling down the gorge like furniture being moved upstairs in a house the size of the world.

"He was a runner," she said. "Like me. Before me. Ran cargo up and down this same water, small boat, smaller margins. Good man. Bad with money — or rather, good with money right up until he wasn't. He had that weakness, common enough on this river, of believing the next contract would fix everything. Every debt could be outrun by the next payday. He believed it the way some men believe in saints."

Elio said nothing. He was good at saying nothing, Elio. He listened the way cartographers must listen — taking in the shape of things without insisting they be any particular shape.

"Year I was twelve, he took a contract hauling timber down from the high camps. Paid too little, timed too tight, and he took it anyway because the alternative was the moneylender in Vess, and the moneylender doesn't wait. Monsoon year, like this one. Early break, worse than anyone predicted." She nodded at the window. "Like that."

"He went through the lower gorge in flood," she said. "They found the boat two days later, splintered, forty miles down. They found him five days after that." She stopped. The words got heavier near the end and she'd never quite learned to carry them smoothly. "Downstream. At a place called the Widow's Reach. A fishing family pulled him out of their nets."

"I'm sorry," Elio said, simply, and the simplicity of it landed better than any elaborate sympathy would have.

"They recovered his compass from the wreck site. Floating in its case. Still pointing north." She touched the doorframe, where the compass lived when she slept — always within reach, always. "That's why I named the boat what I named it. Not because a ship broke. Because a man did, and the river kept the pieces, and I grew up among those pieces. The Salt Wreck is what happens when the river takes everything from a family and leaves them the name of the losing."

"And you stayed on the water anyway."

"Where else?" She laughed, short and dry. "People ask that. People who've never loved something that could kill them. You don't leave. You learn it better. You learn it so well it can't surprise you into killing you. That's the whole art — not safety, familiarity. My father died of unfamiliar water. Flood he hadn't seen before, gorge he'd run fifty times in normal season. I tell myself if I know every mood of every mile, I'll die of old age instead."

"Do you believe that?"

She considered. The rain drummed. Back in the dark of the cave, the hidden water breathed.

"No," she admitted. "But it keeps the fear organized."

Elio turned his cup slowly. "I took the expedition's money knowing what the crates were," he said, without preamble, without looking at her. "I've said it before. I'll say it again because it sits differently every time I say it. I needed the pay. My charts had been discredited — fairly, mostly, I cut corners on a survey and a ferry captain paid for my error with a broken hull, and after that no reputable expedition would hire me. Corvane's people would. They asked no questions about my past because they preferred employees with pasts. Leverage comes built-in."

"You've told me this."

"I've told you the facts of it. Not—" He exhaled. "Not the part where I lay awake at night doing arithmetic. What my silence cost versus what my honesty would have cost. Professor Hollis trusted me. Trusted me enough to put me on the survey team, to defend me when others objected to my record. And I watched the crates come ashore and I said nothing, because saying something meant the road ended there — no more pay, no more references, no way home except the moneylenders' road. I told myself I'd report it later. From safety. Everyone tells themselves that. Later. When it costs less."

The lamp hissed. Lightning again, closer now, the thunder arriving almost on top of it.

"Later arrived," Elio said, "and it was fire."

Mirabel reached over and refilled his cup without asking. "Here's what I know about debts," she said. "The river collects them all eventually. Every one. It doesn't care whether you borrowed in good faith or desperation, whether you meant to repay or never thought about it at all. Interest accrues the same. My father's debt collected itself out of his hull in flood water. Yours is collecting out of you right now — you're paying it in guilt, in sleep, in this endless carrying of a box that isn't yours to carry anymore. The professor's dead either way. Carrying the ledger day and night won't raise him. But you carry it because putting it down feels like the fire winning."

"Yes," Elio said quietly. "Exactly that."

"So here's what I propose. We put it down — not forever, just for tonight. It stays in the strongbox below, dry, safe, guarded by a locked door and a teenage girl with a rifle she's learning to use better than you ever learned. And you and I drink this terrible liquor and talk about literally anything else until the rain stops or we fall asleep, whichever comes first. Because tomorrow we go back to being people with a mission. Tonight we're just two people in a hole in a cliff, waiting out weather."

Elio looked at her for a long moment. Then he set the case down — actually set it down, on the deck between his feet, unstrapped from his chest for the first time since the mangroves — and picked up his cup.

"To unfamiliar water," he said. "May we survive it."

"To familiar water," she countered. "May we know it well enough to survive it anyway."

They drank. The liquor was awful. It was perfect.

* * *

The rain eased sometime past midnight, settling from fury into a steady drumming, and the cave filled with sounds — drips keeping time, the river muttering to itself outside, the creak of the Salt Wreck's lines as she swayed gently at her moorings. Mirabel lay in her bunk listening to all of it, the compass on the shelf above her head, its brass case catching a sliver of lantern light.

Sleep came slow. In the borderlands before it, she heard Elio moving below — checking the bilge one last time, she guessed, or standing watch over the strongbox despite everything she'd said. Some debts you couldn't put down even overnight. She understood that. She'd named a whole boat after it.

She was nearly under when a sound cut through the rain-noise. Not water. Not wind.

An engine. Far off, upstream, muffled by the storm but unmistakable — the flat, insistent throb of a launch motor working hard against flood current. No honest boat ran this stretch at night in a monsoon. No fisherman, no trader, nobody with legitimate business on the water.

Somebody was coming upriver. Somebody who knew exactly where the only shelter for miles happened to be.

Mirabel lay still in the dark, heart ticking up, and listened harder.
