## 10. Rook's Bargain

I woke to the sound of the engine dying.

That was worse than hearing it run. A running engine could be anything — a lost trader, a patrol boat chasing its own tail through the flood. But an engine that stopped meant a man who had arrived where he intended to be, and there was only one place to be in this drowned stretch of river: here, at the cave mouth, where the Salt Wreck sat moored in the shallows like a wounded animal too tired to hide.

I lay on my bedroll against the limestone wall and did not move. The rain came down outside in sheets so thick they had a sound of their own, a white roar that swallowed everything smaller than itself, and beneath it I could hear Mirabel breathing — awake, I realized. She'd been awake for some time. Her breathing had the controlled quality of a woman listening with her whole body.

The cave was not large. The monsoon had raised the river until it lapped at the lip of the entrance, and we had backed the Salt Wreck into the flooded chamber an hour before dark, her mast just clearing the stone, lanterns doused, everything stowed. It had felt like safety then. Caves feel like safety until you remember they have exactly one door.

A boot scraped on wet rock.

Not inside. Outside — on the shelf of stone at the entrance, where the water ran shallow over gravel. One step. Then another. Deliberate. Unhurried. A man who wanted us to hear him coming.

Mirabel's hand found my shoulder in the dark and pressed once. *Stay.* I felt rather than saw her rise, felt the whisper of oilcloth as she took up the rifle — Elio Sarr's rifle, though I had never once fired it, though I held it now the way I held all tools of violence: like something borrowed from a language I didn't speak.

"Captain Iversen." The voice carried through the rain-noise, deep and even, pitched to be heard without being shouted. "You have a fine hiding place. My compliments."

Dane Rook. I had seen him once, from across the Vess harbor — a big man moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had never needed to hurry, because everyone else always got out of his way first. Corvane's enforcer. The professor's people had spoken his name the way sailors speak of a shoal: quietly, and only to mark where it lay.

"I'm armed," Mirabel called back. Her voice was flat and steady, and I marveled at it, because mine would have shaken.

"You are," Rook agreed. "And I'm standing in the rain, which means whatever you do next, you'll do it to a man who's already wet. That tends to make people reasonable." A pause. Water dripped somewhere in the dark between us, counting seconds. "I'm not here for a fight, Captain. I'm here to make you rich."

* * *

She made him wait while she lit the lantern.

It was, I understood later, a deliberate choice — a way of saying *this is my cave, my light, my terms* — but in the moment it simply stretched the silence until it screamed. When the flame finally caught and threw our shadows huge against the dripping stone, I saw him framed in the entrance: a broad silhouette streaming rain, hands open and empty at his sides, a pistol holstered high on his chest where he could reach it but where the gesture said he wouldn't.

His eyes went past Mirabel to me, and stayed.

"Elio Sarr," he said, and smiled without warmth. "The cartographer. You know, when the camp burned, Corvane's list had you down as dead. It would have been simpler for everyone if you had stayed dead."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, and surprised myself with how level my voice came out, "that I've never been simple."

Something flickered behind his eyes — amusement, maybe, or appraisal. He looked at me the way a buyer looks at livestock whose price he intends to argue down.

"Here's how the night goes," Rook said. He did not step further inside; he stood with the rain running off him, patient as stone. "Option one. Captain Iversen hands me the case, and I hand her a purse with three times what Corvane paid her, and she goes back downriver a wealthy woman before the flood peaks. Option two—" he shrugged, a small, terrible movement— "option two is a longer conversation, and I don't think any of us wants that in a cave this size."

"And me?" I asked. "In option one?"

"In option one," Rook said, "you walk out of here alive. That's the part I want you to understand, Mr. Sarr, because I don't think you do understand it. You think you're carrying evidence. You think there's a chain of custody, a filing system, some clerk in the river authority who's going to open your professor's ledger and gasp and start writing memos." He shook his head slowly, almost pitying. "Let me save you the trip."

Mirabel shifted beside me. "He's lying."

"He isn't," Rook said, and the honesty in it was somehow worse than a lie. "Ask him. Go on, cartographer. Ask me who chairs the licensing board at the river authority."

I knew. God help me, I knew before he said it, because I had spent six years drawing charts for men who paid in concessions, and I had watched names move through paperwork like fish through reeds, always surfacing downstream of wherever the money entered.

"Vessmar," I said. "Deputy Commissioner Aldous Vessmar."

Rook's smile widened by a degree. "There it is. The man who will receive your report, review your evidence, convene the inquiry, appoint the investigators, and bury the whole thing so deep it'll take a hundred years to surface — if anyone's still looking. Corvane doesn't bribe around the law, Mr. Sarr. He bought the law years ago. It was the cheapest thing on the river." He spread his hands. "Your ledger is paper. Paper goes in files. Files go to Vessmar. And Vessmar's shredder is very well maintained."

The rain filled the silence. I stood in the lantern light and felt something go out of me — not hope exactly; I had been rationing hope since the night the camp burned — but the *structure* of hope, the scaffolding I had built the whole escape on. Get the ledger downriver. Give it to the authority. Let the law do what laws do. Every mile of flooded gorge, every hour bailing water out of the Salt Wreck's straining hull, every time I had told myself the professor hadn't died for nothing — all of it had rested on that one belief, and now Rook stood in the doorway and pulled it out from under me with the ease of a man lifting a latch.

"Who else knows?" I heard myself ask. "About Vessmar."

"Corvane. Me. Now you." He tilted his head. "And that's precisely why option one exists, Mr. Sarr. Because a man who knows the law is bought is a dangerous man, and dangerous men get options or they get graves. Corvane prefers options. Graves make noise. Options make money."

"And the professor?" Mirabel's voice cut in, low and hard. "Did Hollis get an option?"

Something moved behind Rook's face — the first true thing I'd seen there. Not remorse. Regret is for men with souls; this was more like a craftsman noting a flaw in his own work.

"The professor got an option," Rook said. "He refused it. That was his mistake, and it wasn't mine to correct." His eyes came back to me. "Don't make his mistake, Mr. Sarr. He was an old man with a finished life. You're a young man with maps nobody else can draw. There's work in this world for you either way. The question is whether it's work above ground."

* * *

They talked terms after that, Mirabel and Rook, in the clipped language of people who both knew the negotiation was theater — she named a figure absurdly high, he countered with the same figure he'd started at, and neither of them believed a word, because the real transaction happening in that cave was not about money at all. It was about whether I would hand over the case.

I stood apart with the case in my hands. Leather, brass corners, waterproofed seams — the professor's own kit, the one he'd kept under his bunk and checked nightly like a man checking on a sleeping child. Inside it: the ledger. Names, dates, manifests, payments. Six years of Corvane's empire written in a dead man's careful hand. Juno's father's last work.

I thought about Vessmar's office. I had been in it once, delivering survey charts, and I remembered the smell of it — pipe smoke and varnish and old paper — and the deputy commissioner himself, soft-voiced, ringed fingers, asking me questions about the upper gorge that had nothing to do with cartography and everything to do with what could be moved through it unseen. I had thought nothing of it then. I had thought nothing of a great many things then.

"Mr. Sarr." Rook's voice again, gentler now, which was the most frightening register it had. "You're doing arithmetic. I can see it. So let me finish the sum for you. Even if — *if* — you found some honest clerk, some corner of the bureaucracy Corvane missed, what then? An inquiry takes a year. Corvane hears about it in a week. By the time the inquiry reports, there'll be no Salt Wreck, no crew, no witnesses, and no you. The law isn't slow, Mr. Sarr. The law is *his*. Handing that ledger to the river authority isn't justice. It's mailing your professor's bones to the man who broke them."

"And giving it to you?" I said. "What does Corvane call that?"

"Survival," Rook said simply. "Which is more than the law was ever going to give you."

I looked down at the case. The lantern light lay along its leather like water.

And here is the thing I have never told Mirabel, the thing I will carry longer than the debt I already carry: in that moment, I considered it. Not long. Not seriously. But the thought passed through me, clean and cold as the floodwater outside — *give it to him, live, disappear upriver, draw new charts under a new name* — and I let it pass through instead of drowning it, because I wanted to know it was there. I wanted to see the size of my own cowardice before I chose against it. The professor used to say that a mapmaker's first duty was honest survey: you chart the swamp before you decide the road. So I surveyed myself, and found the swamp exactly where I expected it, and chose the road anyway.

"No," I said.

Rook exhaled through his nose. Not surprise — recalculation. "No?"

"You offered me my life for a dead man's work," I said. "But you've got the exchange rate wrong. My life isn't worth the ledger. It's worth *less*. That's the whole point of what the professor wrote down — that Corvane's entire river runs on men trading things that aren't theirs for lives that aren't worth much. Ferry contracts. Concessions. Cooks' wages. Cartographers' silence." I lifted my chin. "I won't add one more entry to his book."

For a long moment Rook said nothing. Rain drummed on the stone behind him. Somewhere out on the black water, thunder rolled downstream like furniture dragged across a floor.

Then he nodded, slowly, as if I had confirmed something he'd suspected all along.

"All right," he said. "I gave you the option. Corvane can't say I didn't." And his hand drifted — casually, terribly — toward the holster on his chest.

Mirabel's rifle came up off her shoulder in the same breath, and the lantern light caught the barrel, and for one suspended second the whole cave balanced on a point finer than any needle I'd ever plotted.

But Rook only touched the holster strap. Adjusted it. Smiled.

"Rain's letting up by morning," he said, stepping backward into the dark. "Flood crests day after. After that, there's nowhere on this river my launch can't go and your wreck can't outrun — except one place." His voice dropped, and the last words came out of the darkness almost kindly. "You'll figure out which place I mean. Everyone does, eventually. It's the reason they call them the Devil's Teeth."

His boots crunched away over the gravel. The engine woke far out on the water, worked briefly against the current, and faded.

Mirabel lowered the rifle. Her hands, I noticed, were shaking now that it was over — shaking hard — and she shoved them into her coat before I could see, though I had already seen.

"Well," she said, to the entrance, to the rain, to the river. "Now we know."

"Yes," I said, and hugged the case to my chest like something alive. "We know the law is dead. We know there's no one to deliver this to." I looked at her, and she looked back, and I watched the same terrible arithmetic dawn in her eyes that had already finished its work in mine.

"There's still one thing on this river Corvane doesn't own," Mirabel said slowly. "The water itself."

Outside, the monsoon roared on, and downstream — where the gorge narrowed and the charts turned blank and every pilot's hand trembled — the Devil's Teeth waited, white and hungry, for whoever was desperate enough to come.
