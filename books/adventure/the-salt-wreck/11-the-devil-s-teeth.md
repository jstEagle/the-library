## 11. The Devil's Teeth

The rain had stopped an hour before dusk, and the river lay flat as poured pewter between the gorge walls, so still that Mirabel could see the inverted cliffs hanging beneath her keel like a second world. She cut the engine and let the Salt Wreck drift, listening.

There it was again. Under the water-sound, under the drip of the mangroves and the tick of the cooling engine — a low, continuous mutter from around the bend ahead. Not rapids exactly. Something older than rapids. The sound a river makes when it is chewing on stone and has been chewing for ten thousand years and will still be chewing when every boat on it is rust.

The Devil's Teeth.

Her father had named them that, or someone before him had, and the name had stuck the way names stick to places that kill people. Every pilot on the upper river knew them the way you know a grave in your own family's plot: by reputation, by avoidance, by the stories told about the ones who'd tried. No one ran the Teeth. Not in living memory. Not in the memory carved into the cliff-side marker at Kessel Bend, where Nesta kept a list of names going back sixty years, most of them followed by a date and nothing else, because there was never anything left to bury.

Mirabel stood at the wheel with her hands resting on the spokes and listened to the water chew.

Behind her, Elio came up from below decks with the chart case under his arm and his hair still wet from bailing. He'd taken to wearing the professor's old canvas coat, which hung off him like a tent, and he moved through the boat now with something approaching purpose — checking lines, testing seams, pressing his palm against the hull planking the way a doctor palpates a patient. Three days ago he hadn't known a bilge pump from a bilge rat. Now he knew where every leak in her hull lived by name.

"You heard it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Been hearing it since the bend." She nodded upstream. "That's the outer bar singing. Water going over the first teeth."

Elio set the case down on the chart table and didn't open it. He looked at her instead, and she recognized the look — the one people gave her when they wanted her to say the impossible thing was possible because they needed it to be. Juno had given her that look over Amara's fever. Amara had given it to her through the delirium. Now the cartographer gave it to her over open water.

"Rook's launch is two miles behind us," Elio said. "Maybe less. We saw its wake from the cave mouth."

"I know what we saw."

"The toll bridge checkpoint is closed — Corvane's men hold both banks. The eastern channel silted out after the spring floods; I mapped it myself last season, there isn't eighteen inches of draft through the middle of it." He spread his hands. "Every road down this river goes through Corvane except one."

"And the one that doesn't goes through rock." Mirabel turned back to the wheel. Ahead, the gorge narrowed, the walls leaning in until the sky above was a strip of bruised violet no wider than a road. Somewhere past that narrowing, the river dropped forty feet in half a mile through a field of granite fangs, and no boat that entered had ever come out whole. "You want me to run the Teeth."

"I want us to live," Elio said quietly. "The Teeth are just the only place that wants the same thing."

* * *

They anchored in the lee of a fallen slab of limestone the size of a chapel, close enough to the north wall that the overhang kept off the worst of the damp. Mirabel killed the running lights. If Rook's launch came upriver in the dark, let him find nothing but black water and black stone.

Below, in the lamplit cabin, Amara slept with the fever sweat shining on her face, and Juno sat beside her with a wet cloth, doing the small tireless work of keeping a dying woman cool. The girl looked up when Mirabel came down for the strongbox under the bunk, and her eyes went straight to what Mirabel carried: not money, not papers, but a battered oilskin packet gone soft as cloth with age.

"That's his," Juno said. "The notes. Papa talked about them. He said if anyone ever understood the Teeth, it was the man who wrote those."

"He never met my father, then," Mirabel said. "Or he did, and knew better than to understand him."

She climbed back up to the wheelhouse with the packet under her arm, and there she sat through the whole of the night with a lamp turned low and her father's handwriting marching across forty years of paper.

He had been obsessed with the Teeth. She'd known that as a child the way you know a parent's strangeness — as weather, as fact. There were seasons when he'd take the skiff up to the overlook at dawn and just sit, watching the water, filling page after page with figures she couldn't read. Her mother used to say he was courting the devil. Her mother said a lot of things before the fever took her, and then it was just the two of them, and the boat, and the obsession.

Now, alone in the wheelhouse with the mutter of the rapids coming through the hull like something breathing, Mirabel read her father's obsession line by line, and understood it for the first time.

It wasn't madness. It was method. Page after page, year after year: water heights at different seasons, logged against dates. Sketches of the wave trains at each stage of the drop. Notes on where the eddies folded back, where the counter-currents ran, where the rocks that showed at low water vanished entirely at high. He'd interviewed wreck survivors — there were transcripts, three of them, men who'd gone partway through the Teeth in flood and lived to wash up downstream. Their accounts matched his sketches. He'd cross-checked everything twice, three times, in his cramped left-slanting hand, and in the margins of the last pages he'd written the thing he'd apparently never told anyone:

*The Teeth cannot be run. But they can be threaded. The difference is that threading requires knowing which tooth bites at which hour, and being willing to bet your life that the river keeps its own appointments.*

At the bottom of the final page, dated eleven days before the accident that drowned him:

*Ran the lower third in the skiff at medium water. Held the line. The rest is only nerve.*

Mirabel sat very still for a long time.

He'd done it. Part of it. In a skiff, alone, without telling her — and eleven days later he was dead in the river anyway, drowned running a debt, and she had spent twenty years believing the Teeth had taken him, because it was easier to believe the river took him than the debt did. Because the river you could respect. The debt you could only hate.

She pressed the page flat with both hands and found that they were shaking.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. Elio was already gone from his bunk when Mirabel finally climbed down, stiff-necked, the oilskin packet under her arm. She found him on the foredeck with Juno, and the two of them were bent over his charts, and the girl was pointing at the cliff wall upstream with an expression Mirabel had last seen on her father's face.

"There's a goat track," Juno said. "Papa used it. It goes up to the overlook — you can see the whole drop from there."

"You're not climbing anywhere," Mirabel said.

"I'm not asking permission." Juno lifted her chin, and for a second she was so much her father's daughter that Mirabel's chest hurt. "Amara needs us to get through. I can map water from up there better than anyone can from a deck. Papa taught me. Please."

Elio glanced at Mirabel. "She's right about the vantage. And I can't read the water from below — the entry angle's hidden by the bend. We need eyes on the whole run."

Mirabel looked at the girl — sixteen years old, sleeves rolled, mud already on her knees from scrambling the shingle — and thought of herself at sixteen, already running the gorge alone because there was no one to tell her not to.

"One hour," she said. "Rope on. You stay behind the marker cairn and away from the lip. If the fog comes up, you come straight down."

Juno grinned like sunrise and went for the rope coil.

* * *

By mid-morning Mirabel stood at the base of the cliff with her father's packet open on a flat stone, while above her, tiny against the gray sky, Elio and Juno picked their way up the goat track. The river here ran fast and green-black toward the bend, and beyond the bend the muttering had become a growl. She could feel it through her boot soles.

She laid out the pages in order. Entry channel. First teeth — five granite spires, the two on the river-right submerged at current height. The mid-channel slot between the second and third, wide enough for a boat of the Salt Wreck's beam — barely — *if* the helmsman held the line through the standing wave that formed across it at anything above medium water. Then the chute, the long slide where the river gathered itself and dropped, walls too close to turn in, no second chances, ending in the boil below the fifth tooth where the water stood up in hills and the undertow pulled everything toward the outer bar.

Her father had drawn it all. Forty years of watching, distilled into ink. And in the margins, in the same cramped hand, the corrections that mattered — the things that changed with the monsoon.

Mirabel measured the water marks on the cliff stones. High. Higher than any of his entries. The monsoon had swollen the river by four feet, maybe five, and every figure in the packet was calibrated to a drier season.

The standing wave across the mid-slot would be bigger. Faster. The chute would run like a millrace. And the boil below —

She was still staring at the margin notes when the rope ladder swayed and Elio came down it two-handed, wild-eyed, with a roll of fresh-drawn charts jammed in his coat.

"It's changed," he said, before his feet properly touched ground. "Your father's drawings — the second tooth has shifted. There's a new rock, or an old one's fallen; either way the slot's moved six feet to port. And the boil—" He stopped, caught his breath. "The boil's worse than he drew it. But Mirabel — the entry channel's *wider*. The flood has scoured out the left bank. What he called impossible at low water, the river has opened up for us."

"Or dug our graves deeper," Mirabel said.

"Both can be true." He spread the fresh charts on the stone beside her father's pages, and the two sets of lines lay side by side — the dead man's careful ink and the living man's pencil — and where they overlapped, the truth of the river emerged between them like a joint authorship. "Look. His line through the first teeth still holds. Your instinct says hold river-left off the entry; his figures agree. The slot's moved, but it's *there*, and it's clean. If we hit it at speed, square on—"

"—the wave will try to broach us."

"So we don't fight it. We ride it. Your father wrote that, right here—" Elio tapped the margin. *'Do not fight the standing wave. Give it your bow and steer with the current, not against it.'*

Mirabel looked at the words a long moment. All those years she'd kept the packet in the strongbox like a relic, unread, untouchable — grief fossilized into oilcloth. And all along he'd left her instructions. He'd left her a map out of the one place he'd never been able to take her.

Above them, Juno's voice floated down thin from the overlook, calling measurements, and Elio scrambled up the ladder to mark them, and Mirabel stayed at the stone with the pages moving in the wind, and somewhere in the next hour the terror in her chest — the cold animal terror that had been sitting there since Rook's shot crossed their bow — began, slowly, to change shape. It didn't leave. Terror didn't leave. But it compacted itself, the way coal compacts into diamond, into something hard and bright and usable.

Resolve.

Not because she believed they'd live. She'd piloted too many funerals to believe that. But because the alternative was the toll bridge, and Corvane's soft voice, and the ledger burning in a stove while Amara's fever cooked her alive, and every debt on this river getting paid except the ones that mattered.

Her father had threaded the lower third and died of a debt. She would thread the whole of it and pay one off.

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and Mirabel worked the boat like a woman dressing a bride for a funeral. Everything loose lashed down. The longboat secured amidships, upside down, ready to float free if the hull broke. Juno and the feverish Amara wrapped in cork vests below, wedged into the strongest corner of the cabin with mattresses strapped around them like a nest. Elio rigged the bail buckets on lines so they couldn't wash away, and triple-checked the patchwork on the forward seam, and then stood in the wheelhouse doorway watching Mirabel study the water with an expression she couldn't name.

"You've never run anything like this," he said.

"No."

"And your father's notes are twenty years old, and the river's in flood, and we get one pass."

"Yes."

Elio was quiet a moment. "Then why do you look calm?"

Mirabel almost laughed. She turned the compass in its gimbal — her father's compass, needle steady on north as it had been through everything, through fire and flood and the whole long drowning weight of her life — and found that her hands had gone perfectly still.

"Because I finally read his notes," she said. "And because Rook's launch just came around the bend."

Downstream, perhaps a mile and closing, a gray shape slid out of the evening haze with its engines idling low, patient as a shark. Rook had found them. Of course he had; the river was narrow and the world was small and men like Dane Rook always arrived exactly when hope was most expensive.

Elio went white. "We're not ready. The light's failing — we'd be running the Teeth at dusk—"

"We'd be running them at dawn with a gunboat at our stern, or at dusk with one behind us." Mirabel rang the engine bell, once, clear as a church across the water. Below, she heard Juno's voice rise, steadying Amara. "Either way we run them. The only question is whether we do it scared or furious."

She put the wheel over and brought the Salt Wreck's bow around toward the bend, toward the growl of the granite, toward the last unmapped mile of her father's life.

"Furious," she told the river. "Hold onto something, Elio. We're going home the hard way."
