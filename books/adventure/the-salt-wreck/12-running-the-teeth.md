## 12. Running the Teeth

Dawn came to the Devil's Teeth the color of a bruise — gray-green light seeping through the monsoon overcast, the river below the cliff running in long silver ropes of foam. Mirabel stood at the rail of the Salt Wreck and watched the water that meant to kill her, and found, to her own surprise, that she was not afraid. Not yet. Fear would come later, she knew, out in the middle of it, when there was nothing left to do but feel it. Here at the edge, with the plan still whole and the boat still dry, there was only a strange and terrible calm.

Elio came down from the cliff top at first light, mud to his knees, his satchel of charts clutched against his chest like a child. His hair was plastered flat with rain. He looked older than he had two days ago, and younger too — some of the guilt burned off him, replaced by something harder.

"It's there," he said, without preamble. "The channel. Your father found it." He spread the old oilcloth on the deck boards, weighting the corners with river stones, and Mirabel crouched over it beside him. Two maps lay overlapped: Elio's own survey of the gorge, precise as surgery, and her father's hand-drawn notes on a scrap of sailcloth gone soft as cloth-paper with handling. His writing slanted hard to starboard, the letters of a man who'd learned to write on a moving deck.

"Here." Elio's finger traced the white chaos below. "Everyone reads the Teeth as a wall. Eleven rows of rock, and the current piles against the outer line like it's trying to climb out of the gorge. That's where Rook's launch will go if he follows — the obvious line, the deep water. It looks deep. It isn't. There's a drowned ledge off the third tooth, maybe four feet under, and the launch draws five."

"And us?"

Elio's finger moved left, into what every chart in the province marked with a single word: *impassable*. "The inner throat. Your father's line. It's narrow — I'd say nine meters, maybe less where the second and third teeth nearly close. The water through it runs faster, but it runs *clean*. No submerged rock, no ledges. He wrote—" Elio squinted at the sailcloth, deciphering. "*The river forgives speed. It does not forgive hesitation.*"

Mirabel read the words herself, though she knew them by heart. Her father had written that a year before the water took him, and not in the Teeth — in a debt collector's flood, in a river of paper. She had spent twenty years thinking the line was about money. Standing here, she understood it had always been about this.

"He never ran it," she said.

"No," Elio agreed quietly. "He mapped it from the cliff, same as me. It's one thing to draw a line through hell. It's another to steer a boat down it."

"Then we'll be the first." She rolled the maps together and stood. "Get below. You're on the bail bucket."

* * *

They went in at midmorning, when the rain thinned to a fine gray mist and the light over the gorge turned the color of pewter. Mirabel had wanted to wait for better weather. There was no better weather coming — the monsoon had settled in for the season, and every hour at anchor was an hour for Rook's launch to come around the bend and end the argument with a rifle. Better weather was a luxury for people with time. She had a holed boat, a hunted passenger, and a debt collector upstream gaining.

"Juno, below with Elio," she said. "Amara — you know the engine. If she falters, you keep her turning. Don't think, just keep her turning."

Amara's wound had gone angry overnight, the bandage spotted brown, but she'd climbed up onto the engine box on her own and now sat with her good hand flat on the housing like a woman resting her palm on the flank of a nervous horse. "She'll turn," Amara said. Her voice was thin but level. "Boats want to live. Same as us."

Mirabel took the wheel.

The last calm water lasted perhaps four minutes. She spent them looking at the river ahead — at the white noise rising out of the gorge like smoke, at the mist standing above the Teeth in a permanent trembling column, at the walls of limestone closing in on either side, streaked black with a hundred years of flood-stain. The sound reached her before the water did: not a roar, exactly. A grinding. The sound of a mountain being argued with.

Then the gorge took them.

The first drop hit like a fist under the keel. The Salt Wreck leapt — there was no other word for it — thirty tons of riverboat airborne for a half-second that lasted a year, and Mirabel's stomach floated up into her throat and stayed there. She came down hard, both hands white on the wheel, and the bow buried itself in the trough beyond and threw up a wall of green water that broke across the deck and found every gap in the planking at once.

Below her feet, she heard Elio start to scream — not in fear, she realized, but in report. "Water's coming fast — I've got it — *I've got it* —" The bucket began its rhythm. *Slap, slosh, heave.* She fixed on that sound and let it be her metronome.

The second tooth rose off the port bow like the shoulder of a drowned animal, black rock furred with foam. Her father's line said *hard to starboard, then trust the throat*. She put the wheel over and the boat answered — slow, sluggish, heavy with the water in her bilge — and for three endless seconds they slid sideways toward the rock and Mirabel understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy, exactly how her father's compass had ended up downstream.

Then the current caught the stern and swung them, the way a partner swings a dancer, and the throat opened ahead of them: a slot of green-black water no wider than a country road, walled in foam on both sides, running faster than anything she'd ever steered. The Salt Wreck went into it like a cork into a bottle.

Speed. The river forgave speed.

She gave it everything. The engine howled, Amara's hand flat on its housing, the propeller biting into water that was already running eight knots against them, and somehow the combination worked — boat-speed and river-speed canceling into something controllable, something like flight. The walls of the throat blurred past close enough to read the moss on them. Spray came over the bow in sheets. The hull began to scream — a long, rising, metallic note as the planking flexed against the frames, the sound of a boat being asked to be more than a boat — and Mirabel screamed back at it, wordless, daring it.

*Slap, slosh, heave.* Below, Elio's rhythm never broke.

* * *

The middle of the Teeth was a place with no time in it.

She would remember it afterward in fragments, the way you remember a fever. The fourth tooth, where the throat kinked left and her father's notes said *do not look at the rock, look at the gap* — and she'd looked at the rock, just once, just a flick of the eyes, and the boat had begun to follow her gaze, and she'd wrenched her eyes back to the gap and hauled the wheel and felt the port side pass within an arm's reach of stone that would have opened them like a letter. The fifth, where a fallen tree spun in an eddy, its roots reaching like something drowning. The sixth, where the throat widened for a merciful hundred meters and she dared one breath, one full breath, and in that breath heard Juno's voice through the deck boards, steady and small: "Is she all right?" and Elio answering, "She's all right. *Bail.*"

The seventh tooth nearly had them. A back-eddy set up behind the rock, a whirlpool the size of a chapel, and the Salt Wreck's stern dropped into it and the whole boat began to rotate — slow, ponderous, irresistible, the river deciding to turn them broadside and fold them in half. Mirabel felt it in the wheel before her eyes confirmed it, that sickening loss of authority, and she did the only thing the moment allowed: she gave the engine the last of everything, full ahead, and drove the bow *down into* the whirlpool's eye, where the water, for one impossible moment, ran smooth.

The boat shot through. The whirlpool spat them out the far side like a seed from between fingers.

"HALFWAY," she roared, not knowing if anyone could hear her over the water, not caring. Her arms burned. Her hands had molded themselves to the wheel and she would carry the shape of it in her palms for a week.

And then — because the Teeth were not done with her — the ninth.

Her father's notes had a single word for the ninth tooth, and the word was *commit*. The throat narrowed one final time, to less than eight meters, and the drop beyond it was the worst of the run — a chute of water falling six feet into a churning basin, with the outer teeth standing off to starboard like a row of broken gravestones. There was no line through it. There was only a direction, and the willingness to point the boat down it and keep her hands off the wheel while the river did what the river was always going to do.

She pointed the bow down the chute. She took her hands off the wheel.

The Salt Wreck fell.

For a moment they were not a boat but a stone, dropping through green light, and Mirabel heard — distantly, as if from another life — the crack. A deep, structural sound, a rib giving way somewhere below the waterline, and she felt the deck shudder under her boots and knew: holed. Holed for certain now, below the load line, in the last of the run.

It didn't matter. Nothing mattered except the far side.

The bow came up out of the basin shedding water like a living thing, and ahead of them — *ahead* — the river ran wide and brown and fast but sane, the Teeth falling away behind in their column of mist, and the current carried them out into the long pool below the rapids where the water gathered itself and remembered how to be a river again.

Mirabel steered for the reed bank, and her hands, when she finally let go of the wheel, would not uncurl. Amara had to pry her fingers loose one at a time, laughing and crying at once, her bandage soaked through, her eyes bright.

* * *

They grounded her in the shallows at the mouth of a side slough, and Mirabel went below into a bilge that sloshed at her knees. Elio stood in it to his thighs, gray-faced, still holding the bucket.

"How bad," she said.

"Bad." He said it simply, without drama, and she was grateful for that. "The crack from the drop — port side, aft of the hold. She's taking faster than I can bail now. We've got the rest of the day, maybe. She'll float till tomorrow if we beach her and brace her."

"Then we beach her and brace her." Mirabel looked around the little cabin — the chart table where her father's compass sat in its gimbal, still pointing its patient, faithful north; the bunk where she'd slept every night of her adult life; the hairline scars in the wood from twenty years of her own living. The Salt Wreck had taken the Teeth and lived, holed but afloat, and there was a pride in that which sat strangely beside the grief. "She's earned her rest."

They were dragging the last line ashore when Elio went still, his head cocked toward the gorge behind them. "Listen."

Mirabel listened. Under the river's voice, thin and getting thinner, an engine — the flat, laboring note of a launch pushing hard against current.

They climbed the bank to the outcrop and looked back upriver, and there he was. Rook's launch, running the outer channel, the deep-looking water, the line any sane pilot would choose. Even at this distance Mirabel could see the white water stacking against the outer teeth, could see the launch driving into it anyway because Rook had never met a river he didn't think he could buy. The boat climbed the standing wave off the third tooth — climbed, and hung, and Mirabel found she was holding her breath, and found too that what she felt was not hope for the men aboard.

The drowned ledge did the rest. The launch struck it at speed with a crack they could hear across the water, and the river took it the way the river took everything — spinning it, folding it, feeding it into the foam between the teeth. Figures spilled into the current, tiny and black, some of them moving, some of them not. The launch went under stern-first and did not come up.

Elio exhaled slowly. "Rook?"

"Maybe," Mirabel said. "Maybe not. The river doesn't give receipts." She watched the water a moment longer. "But a wreck like that will be reported, and reports travel downriver faster than boats. Corvane will know his launch didn't make it. And he'll know whose fault it is."

She turned back to her boat, sitting wounded in the shallows, her father's compass gleaming in the cabin's dimness, still pointing true.

"Come on," she said. "We've got a hull to patch, and four people to keep alive. And something's been bothering me since the cave — Rook said the river authority has Corvane's man inside it. Which means the ledger's worth nothing if it lands on an official's desk." She looked downriver, toward Kessel Bend, toward the widow who'd lost her son to these same men. "We're not delivering this to the law, Elio. We're delivering it to the river."
