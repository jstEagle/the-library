## 13. Strangers in the Reeds

Elio Sarr had spent eleven years drawing rivers, and he had never once drawn them honestly.

That was the confession he kept returning to as the Salt Wreck slid down the flooded channel below the Devil's Teeth, her hull groaning like an old man climbing stairs, the patched section weeping a thin silver thread of water into the bilge that Mirabel had taught him to listen for. *Hear it change pitch,* she'd said, two days ago, crouched beside him in the engine room with her ear almost touching the planking. *High and thin means she's holding. Low and lazy means she's filling.* He heard it now — high and thin — and found, absurdly, that he trusted the sound more than he had ever trusted his own maps. A map was a lie agreed upon. The hull told the truth or it sank you.

The river below the Teeth was another country entirely. Above the falls the gorge had been all shadow and vertical stone, walls so close he could have touched both banks with an oar. Here the flood had drowned the valley whole: trees standing to their shoulders in brown water, rooftops of abandoned farmsteads breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, fences running in long submerged lines that snagged drifting reed-mats and held them, turning slowly, like things caught in thought. The monsoon had passed through in the night and left behind a sky scrubbed pale and enormous, and a smell of wet iron and crushed vegetation that Elio suspected would live in his clothes until he died.

He stood at the bow rail with the professor's ledger strapped against his chest under his coat, because he had not been able to stop doing that since the cave. It was a foolish habit. There was nowhere on this boat safer than anywhere else if Rook came again. But the weight of it against his sternum was the only proof he carried that Hollis had existed at all — that a man with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a coughing engine had stood in a tent three weeks ago and said, *Elio, something's wrong with these crates, weigh one* — and Elio had weighed one, and known, and said nothing, because the money was good and his name was already ruined and what difference did one more silence make.

Three people were dead because of his silence. Maybe four. Maybe more, upriver, in places the smoke hadn't reached yet.

"You're doing the face," Mirabel said from behind him.

He turned. She stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the spokes, not steering so much as conferring with them, her eyes moving over the drowned treeline in short practiced sweeps. She had a way of looking at water that he envied — not reading it, exactly, but greeting it, the way you greet a neighbor whose moods you've catalogued over years. Every sandbar's mood, she called it. He'd asked her once what she meant and she'd said, *Every bar has a temper. Some sulk. Some bite. You learn which is which before you lean on them.*

"I don't have a face," Elio said.

"Everyone has a face. Yours is the one where you look like you're doing arithmetic on your own guilt." She nodded toward the starboard bank. "There's a channel marker gone. Flood took it. Which means—" she squinted, lips moving through some private arithmetic of current and depth— "we're further north than my father's notes put us. Good news, actually."

"How is that good?"

"Because there's a cut through the reeds about half a mile down that only floods in high water. Leads to a backwater where I can beach her shallow and check the patch without sinking." She glanced at him, and something in her expression softened by a degree, which for Mirabel Iversen was practically an embrace. "And because north of here is Kessel Bend territory, and Nesta says Corvane's men don't like fishing these reaches. Too many eyes."

Elio looked at the water sliding past the hull, opaque and patient, carrying its cargo of leaves and broken branches southward toward the sea. *Too many eyes.* He thought about how many eyes had watched the camp burn. How many had seen the professor marched down to the water's edge. How many had said nothing, for the same reasons he had said nothing, and how the river kept all of it, the way it kept everything, dissolving evidence into silt.

Then he saw the smoke.

It wasn't much — a gray smudge above the reed line, half a mile downstream and off to the east, thin enough to be a cookfire, wrong enough to be something else. Cookfires in this country meant fishermen, and fishermen didn't hide their fires; smoke was how they signaled prices to each other across the flooded flats. This smoke rose from a hollow, low, deliberate, the way smoke rises when someone has built a fire in a pit and banked it to be small.

Mirabel saw it a heartbeat after he did. He knew because her hand stopped moving on the wheel spoke.

"That's not a fisherman," she said quietly.

"No," Elio said. His mouth had gone dry. "But it might be someone who doesn't want to be found."

She was silent for a moment, weighing it — he could see her weighing it, the same way she weighed every risk, every channel, every stranger. Then she rang the engine telegraph down to half ahead and brought the boat's nose around toward the eastern bank with a gentleness that seemed almost tender.

"We'll look," she said. "From the reeds first. If it's trouble, we back out and run."

"And if it's not trouble?"

"Then somebody out there is having a worse week than us," Mirabel said, "which I'd have to see to believe."

* * *

They went in on the skiff, leaving the Salt Wreck anchored in the channel with her engine idling low — Mirabel's condition, non-negotiable, delivered in the flat voice she used for facts rather than requests. Elio rowed. She sat in the stern with a boathook across her knees and the current's map unfolded in her head, calling the strokes softly — *left, left, ease* — threading them between drowned fence posts and the crowns of submerged bushes that dragged at the keel like grasping hands.

The reeds closed behind them and the world became green noise. Eight feet tall, ten, growing straight out of four feet of floodwater, rasping against the skiff's gunwales with a dry whisper that made Elio think of paper burning. Of tents burning. He gripped the oars tighter and rowed.

The hollow opened without warning — a bowl of higher ground maybe forty yards across, ringed by reeds, with a single stunted fig tree leaning over a fire pit that had been dug into the earth and lined with stones. The fire itself was nearly out, a red eye dimming under ash. Beside it, two bedrolls. A dented pot. And no one.

Elio felt the hair rise on his arms. An empty camp was worse than an occupied one. An empty camp meant—

"Don't move," said a voice above and behind them, young and cracked and trying very hard to be hard. "Either of you. I have a rifle."

Elio raised his hands slowly, oars trailing. Mirabel didn't raise hers. She turned her head, unhurried, and looked up into the reeds at the edge of the hollow.

"So do I," she said. "Mine's bigger. But let's not compare."

A figure stepped out from the reed screen — a girl, sixteen at most, gaunt in the way that hunger makes people geometric, cheekbones and wrists and elbows all suddenly prominent. She held a rifle in both hands and she held it badly, muzzle wavering, finger inside the guard on the trigger, and Elio's heart clenched because he knew that rifle. It was the professor's. He'd seen it racked in Hollis's tent a hundred mornings, oiled and loved.

The girl's eyes were red-rimmed and ferocious. They went from Elio to Mirabel and back, hunting for the thing she clearly expected: uniforms, or the black rain jackets Corvane's men wore, or simply the lie that always came next.

"We're not them," Elio said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Juno. Juno Hollis. It's Elio. Elio Sarr. I was the cartographer. I drew the survey grids — you used to bring me coffee in the mapping tent, you spilled it twice, you—"

The rifle barrel dipped an inch.

"You're dead," the girl said. Her voice cracked clean through on the second word. "They said everyone was dead. Amara said—"

"Amara," Mirabel repeated. "Where is she?"

For answer, the girl lowered the rifle all the way and turned and shouted into the reeds on the far side of the hollow, and the shout cracked too, halfway through, becoming just a girl's voice calling a name. A pause. Then the reeds moved, and a woman came out of them supporting herself on a broken oar like a staff.

She was broad-shouldered and dark-faced and she walked the way people walk when every step is a negotiation with pain. Her left thigh was wrapped in strips of tent canvas gone brown-black with old blood, and the wrap had been changed recently — Elio could see the cleaner bandage beneath — but the skin above it was hot-shiny and the flesh around the wound was swollen tight. She reached the fire pit, looked at the two strangers in the skiff for a long, level moment, and then sat down heavily on a stone, because standing had evidently cost everything she had.

"Elio Sarr," she said. Her voice was low and hoarse, scraped raw. "The map man. You ran."

It wasn't an accusation. That was the worst part. It was stated the way you state the weather.

"Yes," Elio said. Nothing in him wanted to defend it. "I ran the night before they came. I took my pay and I went downriver and I heard the radio traffic from a fuel dock forty miles away." He swallowed. "I'm sorry isn't—"

"It isn't," Amara agreed. She coughed, and the cough had a wet rattle in it that made Mirabel's eyes sharpen. "But you came back upriver after the Teeth, which is either stupid or sorry enough to count for something. We'll find out which."

Mirabel had already waded the skiff's painter to shore. She crouched by Amara with the brisk economy of someone who has dealt with wounds before — Elio wondered briefly about that, filed it away — and peeled back the edge of the canvas wrap. The smell that came off it made even her go still for a moment. Sweetish. Wrong.

"When?" she asked.

"Nine days ago. Crossing the flats, the night of the fire. Fence post, underwater, went through clean." Amara shrugged with one shoulder. "I sewed it myself. Fishing line. It held for a while."

"It's not holding now." Mirabel looked up, and Elio saw her doing the arithmetic he couldn't read — distance, time, fever, tide. Whatever sum she reached, she kept it behind her teeth. "You can't stay here. Neither of you. This—" she gestured at the hollow, the cold fire, the pitiful camp— "this was never going to hold. You know that."

"We know that," Juno said. She had come to stand beside Amara, one hand on the woman's shoulder, and up close Elio could see how much of her was being held together by that single gesture — how the ferocity was scaffolding around something that would otherwise fold. "We were going to move tomorrow. Downstream. Toward Kessel Bend. Nesta Kessel knew my father. He wrote letters to her."

"He did," Elio said quietly. "I carried some of them."

Juno looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw in his face made her chin tremble once before she locked it down. "Then you know he was a good man," she said. "Whatever anyone tells you. Whatever they burned. He was good."

"I know," Elio said. And he did know, and the knowing was a knife, because knowing it had never once stopped him taking the money.

* * *

Getting them to the boat was a slow, careful business. Mirabel rigged a sling from the skiff's spare canvas and they moved Amara down to the waterline in relays, the wounded woman refusing — flatly, furiously — to be carried, insisting on making each step herself with the oar-staff and Mirabel's shoulder. Juno carried everything else: a canvas bag of salvaged things, the professor's rifle slung across her back now with the muzzle finally pointed somewhere safe, and a tin box that clinked. She wouldn't say what was in the tin box. Elio didn't ask. Everyone on this river was carrying something they wouldn't talk about; it seemed to be the local currency.

The Salt Wreck received them the way old boats receive the drowning — without judgment. Mirabel laid Amara in the aft cabin on the bunk beneath the single porthole, propped her leg on a rolled blanket, and set Juno to boiling water on the spirit stove while she cleaned and repacked the wound with a bottle of raw spirits that made Amara bite down on a leather strap and say words in three languages, none of them prayers.

Elio stood in the companionway and watched, useless again, the familiar uselessness sitting in him like ballast. Bailing he could do. Patching he could do, badly. But this — the fevered flesh, the measured pour, the calm voice Mirabel kept talking in, low and steady, telling Amara exactly what she was doing before she did it — this was a competence he didn't own and couldn't fake.

"The fever's in the lymph," Mirabel said at last, sitting back on her heels. She said it to the cabin generally, but her eyes found Elio's. "Red streaks haven't started yet. If they start, we have maybe two days. Three if she's stubborn, and she strikes me as stubborn."

"The toll bridge," Elio said. The words came out before he could weigh them. "Corvane has a checkpoint at the toll bridge. Doctor past that point is Vess, two days' run. Before it there's nothing but Nesta Kessel and whatever herbs she keeps."

"Then we'll want Nesta's herbs and a fast run," Mirabel said, "and we'll want them soon." She stood, wiped her hands on a rag, and looked at the two of them — the disgraced cartographer and the professor's daughter, standing in her cabin like refugees, which is what they were. Something crossed her face that Elio couldn't name. Not softness exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman counting heads and finding more than she'd budgeted for.

"This boat was built for one," she said. "Me and the river. That was the arrangement." She glanced at the porthole, where evening light was going copper on the flooded forest. "Now she's a lifeboat for four and holed below the waterline, running downriver through a smuggler's kingdom with a fevered woman in my bunk and a ledger in your coat that's worth more than every hull on this water combined."

When nobody answered, she almost smiled.

"Well," Mirabel Iversen said. "No sense wasting a good crisis. Juno — galley's aft, there's rice and salt pork, feed your friend. Elio — chart table. If we're running for Kessel Bend tonight, I want your eyes on the water and mine on the wheel, and between us we're going to find a channel the gunboat can't follow."

Juno hesitated at the companionway. She turned back, and for a moment the scaffolding slipped and Elio saw the girl underneath, sixteen years old and orphaned in a burned camp nine days ago, holding herself upright by will alone.

"Mr. Sarr," she said. "My father's ledgers. The real ones. Did they—"

"They're safe," Elio said. He put his hand flat against his chest, over the oilcloth-wrapped weight of it. "All of them. Every page."

Something went out of her shoulders then — not grief, grief stayed, but a fraction of the fear that had been living inside it. She nodded once and went aft, and a minute later Elio heard the small domestic sounds of a stove lighting and rice being measured, and the strange, impossible ordinariness of it made his throat close.

Four of them now. A holed boat, a hunted ledger, a fever rising in the bunk below. And somewhere behind them, patient as the flood, Dane Rook's launch.

Elio climbed to the chart table and unrolled his maps, and began, for the first time in eleven years, to draw a river honestly.
