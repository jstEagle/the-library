## 14. Fever

The fever came at dawn on the third day past the reeds, and it came like the monsoon had — all at once, without negotiation.

Mirabel knew it first by sound. Amara's breathing had changed in the night, from the slow even pull of exhausted sleep to something shallow and quick, a bird's rhythm, wrong for a woman built like an anchor. Mirabel lay in her bunk listening to it for a long time before she got up, because there was a part of her that hoped if she didn't move, the sound would resolve itself into ordinary snoring, ordinary waking, ordinary morning.

It didn't. When she climbed down into the hold where they'd made Amara a nest of cargo blankets between the water barrels, the cook's skin had gone the color of old brass, slick with sweat that smelled sweet and sour at once, like fruit rotting in a closed room. Her eyes were half open and fixed on nothing, on some middle distance only she could see.

"No," Mirabel said, to no one, to the boat, to the river. "No, no."

She pressed the back of her hand to Amara's forehead and snatched it away as if scalded. The heat coming off the woman was not a fever's warmth. It was a furnace door left open. Beneath the bandage on Amara's side — Elio had changed it twice a day, gentle as a man defusing something — the flesh around the wound had gone red and angry, streaked now with thin dark lines climbing toward her ribs like roots growing in fast motion.

Elio appeared at the top of the hold ladder, hair wild, Juno peering around his legs. He'd been sleeping in the wheelhouse chair again, refusing the bunk, refusing to take anything from Mirabel he hadn't earned. He looked down at Amara and Mirabel watched his face do what hers must have done: go still, then pale, then set.

"That's sepsis," he said. Not a question. His father had been a field surgeon before the maps took him; Elio had grown up reading wound charts the way other children read adventure serials. "The red lines mean it's moving toward her heart. She has — I don't know. A day. Maybe two."

"Then we get her to Doctor Ilse at Tarn Hollow." Mirabel was already moving, already calculating, because calculation was the thing she did instead of panicking. "Two days' run down the east channel, wide water, easy going. We can make it inside—"

"Tarn Hollow's east channel," Elio said quietly, "is four days this time of year. The flood's turned the marsh into a lake. You told me yourself, when we passed the marker buoys."

He was right. She'd told him herself, standing at the rail two nights ago, pointing out how the channel markers had vanished under brown water, how the east route she'd run since she was seventeen was now a drowned country with no banks and no bottom you could trust. Four days. Maybe five, if the rain kept up, and the rain kept up the way grief kept up — relentlessly, and with an appetite.

Juno made a small sound. Mirabel glanced up and saw the girl hugging herself against the ladder, watching Amara with an expression Mirabel recognized too well: the look of someone who had already buried one parent and could not afford the arithmetic of burying anyone else.

"There's another way," Mirabel said, and hated the words even as they left her mouth, because she knew exactly which way she meant, and she'd been trying very hard not to think about it since Kessel Bend.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse while the rain drummed the roof like impatient fingers. Amara slept below, or burned below; sleep seemed the wrong word. On the chart table, Mirabel spread the river map, the good one, the one Elio had corrected in his own careful hand until it was less a map than a portrait of the river's moods.

"The toll bridge," she said, and put her finger on it. The bridge sat across the river's throat forty miles downstream, the last deep-water crossing before the delta opened out. Corvane held it. Had held it for years, everyone said, though officially it belonged to the river authority, and officially the tolls went to the authority, and officially nobody asked why the toll collectors wore matching jackets and carried guns instead of ledgers. "Doctor Ilse is at Tarn Hollow, four days east. But there's a surgery barge moored at Pell's Landing, just past the bridge. Half a day. Amara could be on a table by tomorrow night."

"And Corvane's men will be looking for us," Elio said. "At the one place on the whole river they're guaranteed to be standing."

"They don't know our faces. They know the *Salt Wreck*." Mirabel traced the bridge's span with her nail. "Rook saw the boat. Rook knows the boat. If Rook survived the Teeth — and men like him always survive, it's their one talent — then every collector at that bridge has our description by now."

"So we're sailing our own wanted poster into their front gate."

"Yes."

Silence, except the rain. Juno spoke first, which surprised them both.

"She saved us." The girl's voice cracked but held. "Amara carried me through the mangroves for a day and a night when the camp burned. She carried me, and she's twice my size but she was bleeding the whole time, and she never once said stop. So whatever the risk is—" her chin came up, and Mirabel saw the professor in her suddenly, the same stubborn architecture of the jaw, "—we pay it. That's how it works. That's how it should work."

Mirabel looked at Elio. Elio looked at the map, at the bridge, at his own hands, and Mirabel understood he was doing the same arithmetic she was, the arithmetic that had no clean answer, only a dirty one and a dirtier one.

"If we run the east channel," he said slowly, "she dies. Probably. Fever like that doesn't wait for convenient geography."

"If we run the bridge, we might all die. Or worse — the ledger goes to the bottom of the river, and everything the professor died for goes with it." He touched the waterproof case, strapped as always to his chest like a second heartbeat. "I'm not saying don't do it. I'm saying I want to hear you say why."

"Why?" Mirabel laughed, and it came out harsher than she intended. "You want the noble version? Fine. Because a debt is a debt, and Amara pulled your girl through the mangroves, and you don't leave a crew member burning in your hold. But here's the true version, Elio, the one I'll say once and never repeat." She leaned on the chart table, and the rain filled the pause. "My father died of a slow choice. He owed money, and he took the long safe route every single time — long safe routes cost more, see, and costing more meant borrowing more, and borrowing more meant owing more, until one morning the river had him and the debt didn't care. I swore on his grave I would never let caution kill anybody on my boat. Caution killed my father. Speed might kill us. But speed is at least honest about what it is."

Elio held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod over charts when the decision is made and all that remains is the terror.

"Pell's Landing," he said. "By tomorrow night."

* * *

They prepared the way people prepare for storms — grimly, thoroughly, and with too much time to think.

Elio stripped the Salt Wreck of anything that marked her. It hurt Mirabel to watch, though she kept her face neutral, because a boat's name and paint are a captain's face, and he was asking her to wear a mask. They painted over the name with bilge-black and mud — Juno did the actual painting, small and steady-handed on the bow, while Mirabel steered and pretended not to hear the brush strokes. They hung mismatched tarpaulins along the rails to break her silhouette. Elio suggested changing the wheelhouse windows' shape with painted boards, and Mirabel allowed it, and hated it.

Below, they built Amara a hiding place that was also a sickbed. The forward hold had a false floor over the ballast space, a leftover from the boat's smuggling days before Mirabel's time — her father had bought her cheap from a man who'd used her for exactly the trade Corvane ran now, a symmetry Mirabel tried not to dwell on. They lined the cavity with dry blankets, rigged a lantern on a dim wick, and made a shelf for water and the last of Nesta Kessel's willow-bark and feverfew.

"We move her when?" Elio asked.

"At the bridge. Not before. If the collectors board us, they'll search a merchant boat top to bottom, but they won't crawl into ballast spaces unless something makes them curious." Mirabel wiped Amara's brow with a wet cloth, and the cook stirred and muttered — names, mostly, fragments of a family in some upriver village, a debt figure repeated like a prayer. Mirabel's chest tightened around those numbers. She knew that prayer. She'd grown up hearing it in her father's voice.

Amara's eyes opened, briefly, glassy and unfocused, and found Mirabel's face.

"Bridge," the cook rasped. She'd heard more than they thought. "You're thinking the bridge."

"Thinking it. Haven't said yes."

"Liar." A ghost of Amara's usual grin, terrible on a fevered face. "You said yes the moment the girl looked at you." Her hand found Mirabel's wrist, grip shockingly strong for a dying woman. "Listen. If they find me — you keep going. You hear? You don't stop the boat. You don't trade the ledger for me. I loaded crates for that man. Whatever happens to me, part of it's earned."

"It isn't," Mirabel said. "And I don't leave people."

"You left your father's route," Amara whispered, and her eyes slid shut again, and Mirabel stood there holding a wet cloth and feeling the words go into her like a splinter, because it was true, she had, she'd rerouted her whole life away from the channels her father drowned in, and she had never once thought of it as leaving anyone.

* * *

Night before the bridge, Mirabel couldn't sleep. She stood her own watch at the wheel, running dark, no lamp, only the river's silver noise under a torn sky where the monsoon was spending itself in ragged sheets. Elio came up around midnight with two cups of chicory and handed her one without asking, which she decided was either friendship or presumption, and possibly the same thing.

"Tell me about the checkpoint," he said. "Exactly how it works. I've been drawing it in my head and I need real details or my plan is fiction."

So she told him. Two barges lashed mid-channel as a platform, chains across the water at night, raised for paying traffic. A signal lamp. A collector on each barge, two more on the bridge deck above with a rifle apiece, and — the detail that mattered — a boarding skiff that came alongside every commercial vessel, two men in it, one to inspect, one to watch the crew. The inspector wrote in a ledger of his own. Everyone paid, everyone smiled, everyone went through, and the river authority's official reports showed a tidy, lawful toll operation, because the man who signed those reports dined at Corvane's table.

"One inspector boards," Elio murmured. "One watches. And the inspector writes everything down." He was quiet a moment, staring at the black water. "Then we don't hide from the inspection. We perform it. Give them a boat so boring, so ordinary, so *paid-up* that writing it down is the most interesting thing they do all night."

"And Amara?"

"Stays below. And the ledger—" he touched the case, "—stays on my body, under my shirt, where a bored inspector doesn't look. Boredom is our weapon, Mirabel. We have to be the least memorable boat on this river."

"And Juno?"

Elio hesitated, and Mirabel saw him arrive at the idea he'd clearly been carrying since evening: the professor's daughter, unknown to Corvane's people, a face that matched no description. A merchant's child. Papers could be drawn — Elio's hand could forge a bill of lading in twenty minutes; disgraced cartographers, it turned out, learned lettering that fooled banks. A girl in a good coat, sitting in plain sight, yawning. Nothing disarms a suspicious man like a bored teenager.

"She won't like being bait," Mirabel said.

"She won't be bait. She'll be scenery. There's a difference, and I'll spend the whole approach explaining it to her, and she'll still hate it, and she'll still do it, because she's her father's daughter." He paused. "Is that what you tell yourself? About the risks other people take for your plans?"

The question landed harder than he probably intended, or maybe exactly as hard as he intended. Mirabel drank her chicory and watched the rain walk across the water in gray curtains, and thought about her father's compass in her pocket, needle steady, pointing at nothing useful, pointing north out of pure habit.

"I tell myself," she said finally, "that the price gets paid by somebody, sooner or later. My father paid it slow. Amara's paying it in blood. Maybe tonight we pay it in fear, and fear's the cheapest coin there is." She drained the cup. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we spend."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and with it came the bridge.

Mirabel saw it first as a smudge, then as a line, then as a fact: the great iron span shouldering across the flooded river, its trusses dripping, the twin toll barges crouched beneath it like animals under a fence. Smoke from a cookstove. The glint of a signal lamp. And on the bridge deck, small with distance, two figures walking their post, rifles slung, unhurried. Men who expected the day to be exactly like every other day.

Her mouth was dry. Below her feet, in the dark of the ballast space, Amara burned. At her elbow, Juno sat in a borrowed coat three sizes too big, a forged bill of lading folded in her lap, wearing an expression of magnificent teenage boredom that Mirabel suspected was only ten percent performance. Elio stood at the rail with the ledger flat against his stomach and his hands loose at his sides, and only Mirabel, who had learned to read him the way she read water, could see the current underneath.

"Chains are down," she said. "Traffic light's green. They're taking boats through." She swallowed. "Last chance for the east channel."

Nobody answered, which was answer enough. The Salt Wreck — nameless, mud-painted, disguised from her own reflection — slid in toward the toll barges with the current helping, and Mirabel put her father's compass on the binnacle where she could see it, and rang the bell once, polite as any merchant, and waited for the skiff to come alongside.

It came fast. Two men in matching jackets. One stayed low in the boat, hand resting on a gunwale-mounted rifle, eyes moving across her deck with a professional's boredom. The other hooked a ladder and climbed, heavy-bodied, sweating, ledger under his arm, and stepped aboard as if he owned the planks — which, Mirabel reflected, in every way that mattered on this river, he did.

"Morning," the inspector said. "Cargo and destination."

"Timber bindings and salt fish," Mirabel said, "for Pell's Landing," and smiled the smile of a woman with nothing in the world to hide, while behind her, below the deck, beneath the waterline, in the dark, a fevered woman breathed shallow and fast, and a leather case full of a dead man's truth rode against a living man's ribs, and the inspector opened his book, and licked his thumb, and reached for his pen.
