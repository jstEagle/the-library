## 15. The Toll Bridge

The bridge announced itself an hour before we saw it — not by sight, but by sound. A low, rhythmic groaning carried upriver on the rain-washed air, the sound of the great chains flexing as the toll barges rose and fell against their moorings. Then the smell: coal smoke and tar, the particular stench of a place where men had built something permanent in the middle of all this water.

Elio Sarr stood at the chart table in the Salt Wreck's cramped cabin and discovered that his hands were shaking.

He had drawn maps of this river. That was the bitter joke of it, the joke he could not stop telling himself as he traced the last bend with one finger. He had drawn this very reach, this exact mile of brown water, in the third year of his apprenticeship, when the professor had still trusted him and the river authority had still paid him and he had still believed that a map was a kind of truth. *Toll bridge at Kilometre 214. Chain-span construction. Inspection station, east bank.* Neat little symbols. Neat little lies of confidence, because no symbol he had ever drawn had contained Corvane.

"He's there," Mirabel said. She stood at the wheel, one hand resting on it the way other people rested a hand on a shoulder — familiar, proprietary, the gesture of twenty years. "I can see the masthead light. Two of them, actually. They've doubled the watch."

"Maybe it's routine."

"Nothing about that bridge has been routine since Corvane bought the concession. Look at the searchlight." She nodded toward the east bank, where a white beam swept the water in slow, patient arcs, the way a man sweeps a room he already knows he'll search. "Routine is a lantern and a bored clerk. That is a man being paid to find something."

Below them, muffled by the deck planking, came a thin sound — a cough, quickly smothered. Amara. Her fever had broken in the night, the first mercy in four days, but she was still too weak to climb a ladder, let alone run, and so she lay in the bilge space beneath the engine housing with a tarpaulin over her and a coil of rope for a pillow, breathing the smell of diesel and old wood, waiting to be discovered or not discovered. There was no third option. Elio had checked.

Juno sat on the bench by the chart table, very still, the way she had been still for four days — still the way a held breath is still. She was wearing the dress Mirabel had found folded in a locker, a merchant's child's good dress, yellow gone pale with mildew at the hem, and her hair had been braided by Amara's shaking hands before the fever climbed too high. She looked, Elio thought, like a girl playing at being someone else. He only prayed she looked that way to him and no one else.

"Tell me again," Mirabel said.

"I'm a survey clerk," Elio said. "Ferro & Doss, out of Vess. I'm running a gauge survey for the flood season — that's true enough, I did it for three years, I have the old instruments in my kit and I know the forms by heart. You're my contracted boat. The girl is the daughter of a merchant house, traveling down to her uncle's. Her name is Sefi Doss. My employer's niece. It's all in the papers."

"And the papers?"

He lifted the oilskin wallet from the chart drawer and set it on the table between them. Inside: a survey commission, three years old, with dates altered in ink that matched the original hand because Elio had mixed the ink himself from the same iron-gall recipe. A letter of introduction, written last night by lamplight in the careful clerk's script he had spent a decade perfecting. A bill of lading for the Salt Wreck listing cargo as *survey instruments, provisions, personal effects.* And a merchant's seal — borrowed, with Nesta Kessel's blessing and a prayer, from a trading family two bends upriver who owed the old woman more than they could ever say aloud.

"And the ledger," Mirabel said.

The cabin went very quiet. Rain ticked against the deck above. Somewhere below, the bilge pump that Elio had repaired twice and cursed at nightly gave its familiar wet, laboring cough.

"The ledger," Elio said slowly, "is in the one place he will not look, because it is the one place that would ruin everything if he did look, and men like Corvane trust their own cleverness more than they trust their luck."

"You've hidden it somewhere clever."

"I've hidden it somewhere *stupid.* That's the point." He tapped the deck with his toe. "The bail bucket. The one that lives under the engine housing, the one I've been hauling water with for a week. It's got a false bottom — I put it there the second night, before I knew we'd need it, because hiding things is the first thing a disgraced cartographer learns and the only useful thing my old trade ever taught me. It's full of actual bilgewater. It smells like bilgewater. It's been bailing this boat for six days. No man alive searches the bucket that's been in his own way all week."

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. In the lamplight her face was all angles and river-shadow, and Elio could not read it, which frightened him more than the searchlight did.

"You've been bailing with the evidence under the professor's ledger," she said at last, "in a bucket."

"Yes."

"If it leaks —"

"It won't leak. The case is waterproof. The professor built it to survive a flood." Elio heard his own voice go strange and tight. "It was built to survive everything except being read by the wrong man. I intend to make sure it's read by the right ones."

Above them, the groaning of the chains grew louder.

* * *

They came around the last bend at half past four in the afternoon, with the rain holding off in that grudging way the monsoon had — not finished, never finished, just pausing to reload — and Elio saw the toll bridge for the first time in six years and understood that maps were a kind of lie after all, because no symbol he had ever drawn had conveyed the sheer weight of the thing. Two stone piers rose out of the river like the legs of some drowned giant, and between them swung the chain-span deck, and on the deck stood men with rifles slung across their backs like farm implements, and beneath the deck the water ran fast and brown and deep, funneled between the piers into a single channel no wider than the Salt Wreck herself.

There was no other way through. That was the whole point of a toll bridge. That had always been the whole point of a toll bridge.

"Starboard side," Mirabel murmured. "The inspection float. Keep your papers in your hand, not your pocket. A man who reaches for his pocket looks like he's deciding what to show. A man who's already holding them looks bored."

"Are you bored?"

"I am bored out of my mind, clerk." But her knuckles were white on the wheel, and Elio loved her a little for the lie.

They slid in toward the float — a broad, tarred platform moored against the current, roofed in corrugated iron, where a clerk's desk stood under a rain lamp and two soldiers stood with the particular stillness of men who have been standing for hours. And behind the desk, in a chair that had been carried out from some office and set down on the wet boards as though the wet boards were beneath it, sat a man in a pale linen suit.

Elio's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming about.

He knew that suit. He had seen it across a campfire at the expedition's third week, when the "supply contractor" had come upriver with his compliments and his questions — *how far above the falls, how deep the gorges, how many men does the professor keep* — and the professor had answered with the cheerful, empty courtesy of a man who noticed everything and said nothing, and Elio had stood at the edge of the firelight with his charts under his arm and thought: *there is a man who is not what he says he is.* He had thought it and said nothing. He had taken the expedition's money and said nothing. That was the arithmetic of his guilt, and it had no bottom.

Corvane looked up from a ledger of his own — the irony of it was so sharp Elio nearly laughed — and smiled.

"Captain Iversen." His voice carried across the water, warm and unhurried. "I confess I didn't expect you. I'd heard the Salt Wreck went into the gorge country and hadn't come out. Rivers eat boats up there. Rivers eat people too."

"Rivers eat the careless," Mirabel called back, bringing the boat alongside with a gentleness that seemed impossible given the speed of the current. "I'm not careless. Survey run for Ferro & Doss — gauge readings for the flood season. Papers are in order."

"I'm sure they are." Corvane rose. He was smaller than Elio remembered, or perhaps the firelight had flattered him; but his hands were exactly as Elio remembered, long and clean and still, the hands of a man who had never in his life hauled a line or fired a shot he could pay someone else to fire. He stepped down onto the Salt Wreck's deck without asking, which was itself an answer, and stood there taking inventory of the boat with the mild, terrible attention of an appraiser.

"Survey clerk." His eyes found Elio. "You have the look of a clerk. Ink on the fingers, squint from too many small numbers." He smiled again. "I have great respect for clerks. They know where everything is. Where everything *is*, and where everything *was*, and — most valuably — where everything is *going*." He let that sit in the rain-damp air. "Your papers, clerk."

Elio handed them over. His hands, he noted with distant surprise, had stopped shaking. There was a strange calm that descended when the thing you dreaded finally arrived, the calm of a man in fast water who has stopped fighting the current and started reading it. Corvane read the commission, the letter, the bill of lading. He held the merchant's seal up to the rain lamp and turned it, and turned it again.

"Ferro & Doss," he said. "Solid house. Old money. Careful people." His eyes came up. "Careful people don't usually hire a boat called the *Salt Wreck*."

"Careful people," Elio said, "hire whoever's available in a flood season. Half the river's fleet is laid up. Ask anyone at the Vess docks."

"Oh, I will. Eventually. That's the trouble with rivers, clerk — everything flows downstream, and everything that floats past me eventually floats past someone I know." He returned the papers with a small bow. "Well. Papers in order, cargo as listed. I'll just have a look at the cargo."

And he began to inspect the boat.

Elio had planned for this. He had planned for it across two sleepless nights, walking the deck in his mind, deciding what Corvane would see and in what order. The survey instruments were real and laid out real: the brass theodolite on its tripod, the sounding leads coiled on the foredeck, the gauge boards, the notebooks — actual notebooks, filled in Elio's own hand with actual soundings he had actually taken during the gorge run, because a forgery could be caught but real work could only be admired. The provisions were provisions. The personal effects were personal effects. Everything was true except the truth, and the truth lay under six inches of bilgewater in a bail bucket beneath the engine housing, four feet from where Corvane was now standing.

Corvane lifted the theodolite's cover and looked at the instrument the way a man looks at a rival's wife. "Fine brass. German. Your employer trusts you with German brass, clerk."

"He trusts me with his niece, too." Elio nodded toward the bench, where Juno sat with her hands folded in her lap and her chin lifted at precisely the angle of a girl who has been told all her life that the world is her furniture. "Sefi. My employer's brother's girl. Traveling down to Vess. She's been ill — river fever — so forgive her if she doesn't perform."

"Ill," Corvane said softly, and looked at Juno, and Elio felt the whole fragile architecture of the morning balance on that one soft word.

Juno did not flinch. Later, Elio would think about this often — how a fourteen-year-old girl whose father had been murdered three weeks ago sat on a bench in a dead woman's dress and looked at the man who had ordered it done with nothing in her face but the mild, bored patience of a merchant's child waiting out an adult's conversation. She had her mother's face, Elio realized. The professor had kept a photograph in his cabin, and the mother had had exactly that level gray gaze, that stillness. Corvane looked at her and saw a merchant's niece, because he was a man who saw what he expected to see, and no one expected the professor's daughter to be alive.

"River fever's bad this season," Corvane said. "I lost two men to it in the spring."

"Three of our crew," Juno said. Her voice was flat and perfect. "We buried them above the falls."

It was true, in its way. Elio did not let himself think about how true.

Corvane smiled at her, and then, without any change in his expression at all, he turned and walked aft — straight toward the engine housing, straight toward the bilge space, straight toward the bucket.

* * *

The next ninety seconds, Elio would remember all his life in fragments, the way a man remembers a wreck.

He remembered Mirabel moving first — not toward Corvane, that would have been fatal, but toward the engine, bending over it with a wrench as though she had been waiting for an excuse, and saying, in the tone of a captain showing off her ship, "Mind the housing, Mr. Corvane, she's a temperamental old girl, and she's leaking like they all leak. I keep a boy below bailing when we're under way, but she's off watch." A lie, smooth as river mud. There was no boy. There was a wounded cook under a tarpaulin four feet from Corvane's shoes, breathing so slowly and so shallowly that Elio, watching, found himself breathing in the same rhythm, as though the two of them shared one set of lungs.

He remembered Corvane crouching. The pale suit did not crouch well; it pulled at the shoulders, and there was a moment — a long, wet, endless moment — when the man's face was level with the engine housing and his eyes moved across the shadow beneath it, across the coiled rope and the tarpaulin and the bail bucket sitting exactly where a bail bucket sits, dented, stained, half-full of gray water that smelled of the boat's whole suffering week.

He remembered the bucket lifting.

Corvane held it in those long clean hands, and the water inside it swayed, and Elio stood three feet away with his heart going like a paddle wheel and his face arranged into the mild blankness of a man who has watched a hundred men pick up a hundred buckets, and Corvane looked into the bucket — looked into the gray slopping water, at the sediment and the diesel rainbow on its surface — and Elio saw the exact moment the ledger's corner, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted down, invisible beneath the murk, was one inch from the light.

Corvane's eyes narrowed. Not at the bucket. At the water line of the hull.

"She is leaking," he said. "Badly."

"She's an old boat," Mirabel said, from the engine, without looking up. "Aren't we all."

And then — the fragment Elio would return to at night, the fragment he could never fully explain — Corvane set the bucket down. He set it down gently, in its place, and stood, and brushed nothing at all from his knees, and said: "You'll want to care for that. A boat that leaks in flood season is a coffin with opinions." And he laughed, a short pleasant sound, and turned forward, and said, "Well. Everything as listed. You may pass."

The word *pass* went through Elio like a current through a cable.

"Though —" Corvane paused at the gangway, one hand on the rail, and the rain, which had held off all afternoon, chose that moment to begin again, fat drops ticking on the corrugated roof of the float, on the deck, on the tarpaulin over Amara. "Captain. A piece of unpaid advice, from one professional to another. There's a man I do business with. Dane Rook. You may have met." His smile was pleasant and bottomless. "He survived the Teeth. He's angry about it, and he's thorough, and he says — his words — that the *Salt Wreck* is carrying something of mine. Now, I told him that was impossible, because if it were true, you'd never be fool enough to come through my bridge." He looked at each of them in turn — Mirabel at her engine, Elio with his blank clerk's face, Juno on her bench with her mother's still gray eyes. "So you understand that when you pass under my bridge just now, I am staking my reputation on your honesty. Do take care of it. Reputation. It's the only cargo that never floats back upstream."

He stepped onto the float. The soldiers parted for him. And Elio stood on the deck of the Salt Wreck and did not breathe until the chain-span deck above them began, groan by groan, to rise.

* * *

They went through at walking speed, the current grabbing them the moment they cleared the piers, the searchlight's beam sliding off their hull like water off oilcloth. No one spoke. Below, faintly, they could hear Amara coughing again, and the bilge pump laboring, and the river roaring its one long syllable.

It was Juno who moved first. She stood up from the bench, walked to the rail with the yellow dress hanging off her like a shed skin, and was quietly, thoroughly sick into the brown water. Elio went to her and held her shoulders and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie, and she finished and straightened and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and looked at him with her father's eyes in her mother's face and said, in a whisper:

"He set the bucket down."

"I know."

"He *looked* at it. He looked right at it and he set it down."

"I know," Elio said again. And then, because his mind was a clerk's mind and would not stop working the numbers: "He knew. Juno. He knew the whole time. The papers, the girl, the bucket — he saw all of it. He let us through anyway."

Mirabel's voice came low from the wheel, where she stood with the rain running off her chin. "Why would a man do that?"

And Elio looked back upstream, at the bridge already shrinking into the rain, at the small pale figure on the inspection float who had not gone inside, who was still watching them go, and the answer arrived cold and complete, the way soundings arrive when the lead finally touches bottom.

"Because he doesn't need to stop us," he said. "He needs to know where we're going. And now he does — downriver, to the Kessels' country, to the river families. We just led his reputation straight to the only people in the world who can use that ledger." He swallowed. "He didn't let us pass, Captain. He let us *lead*."

Ahead, the river bent east into the gathering dark, and somewhere behind them, soft as rain, a launch engine started up.
