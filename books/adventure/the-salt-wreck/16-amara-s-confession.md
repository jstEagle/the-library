## 16. Amara's Confession

The fever broke on the third night, and I woke to the sound of rain on a tin roof and the smell of onions frying in old grease.

For a long moment I didn't know where I was. The world was a swaying dark, and my leg was a column of fire from ankle to hip, and someone had folded a blanket over me that smelled of engine oil and river mud and, beneath all of it, faintly, of lavender — the kind of lavender that comes in little paper sachets, the kind a mother tucks into a sea chest. I lay still and listened to the rain and tried to remember how I'd come to be lying under a blanket that smelled like my mother's hands.

Then it came back. The camp burning. Professor Hollis shouting at us to run, run, don't stop for anything, not even him — *especially* not him. The mangroves closing around me like green fingers. Juno's small cold hand in mine as we waded through the reeds. And then the wound, which I hadn't felt when it happened, only later, when the blood had soaked through two bandages and Mirabel Iversen had looked at me with those flat grey eyes and said, without a scrap of pity, *you'll live if we're fast.*

So here I was. Alive because we were fast. Lying in the stern cabin of the Salt Wreck while the monsoon hammered the river flat and someone fried onions.

I knew it was Mirabel before I saw her. You learn a person's footsteps fast on a boat this size — there are only so many places they can go. Elio moved like an apology, soft and hesitant, always half-sorry for taking up space. Juno moved quick and light, a girl who'd learned to be invisible in her own father's camp. But Mirabel walked like the deck owed her money. Heel first, deliberate, the whole hull registering her opinion.

She came down the companionway with a bowl in one hand and a bottle in the other and set both on the crate beside my bunk.

"Onions," she said. "It's mostly onions. Elio thinks food is medicine."

"Elio thinks everything is a chart he hasn't drawn yet," I said. My voice came out cracked as driftwood. "Where is he?"

"Cliff top with Juno, mapping the water. He maps things when he's frightened. It's how he prays." She sat down on the crate, which groaned, and studied me the way she studied water — looking for the sandbar under the surface. "Fever's down. Your color's back. Can you sit?"

"I can sit."

"Then sit up and eat, because what I have to say goes better with something in your stomach."

That sentence has a shape, I thought. That sentence has a shape I know. I'd heard it before, in other mouths, in other rooms — my mother saying it before she told me Papa's ferry license hadn't been renewed; the loan man in Vess saying it before he told me what the interest really did. Sentences that begin with *eat something* always end somewhere you'd rather not arrive.

But I sat up, and I ate, because my body wanted living more than my pride wanted dignity. The onions were good. Everything is good after three days of fever.

Mirabel waited until I'd finished. Then she took the bowl away, set it aside, and leaned forward with her elbows on her knees, and the lantern light caught the scar on her forearm — the one I'd noticed the first day, a pale rope of tissue running from wrist to elbow, the kind you get from rope under load, the kind you get when a line parts or when you catch it wrong trying to save something going under.

"You helped them load the crates," she said.

Not a question. I made myself meet her eyes anyway.

"Yes."

"At Vess. Before the expedition ever went upriver. You were the cook, but you were on the dock the night the crates came aboard. Elio remembers your hands on the winch."

"Elio remembers everything," I said. "It's his worst quality."

"It's the reason any of us are alive." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Mirabel Iversen — anger didn't lift her voice, it lowered it, pressed it flat and quiet like a thumb pressing down on a scale. "You knew what was in them."

I looked at my hands. They were still swollen from the fever, the knuckles shiny, and there was a burn scar across the back of the left one from a galley pan, years old, and I thought: these hands. These hands loaded crates. These hands stirred soup for a professor who trusted me. These hands held a teenage girl's hand in the mangroves so she wouldn't be swept away. Hands don't get to be only one thing. Nobody tells you that when you're young. Everybody pretends their hands are one thing.

"I knew some of it," I said. "Not all. Not at first."

"Tell me."

*Tell me.* Two words, no mercy in them, but no cruelty either. Just the flat demand of a woman who had pulled a stranger out of a fever and fed her onions and now wanted the truth the way a pilot wants depth readings — because her life depended on knowing what was under her.

So I told her.

* * *

"My family ferries passengers on the lower delta," I said. "Small boat, eleven seats. My father ran it twenty-two years. And then the river authority changed the licensing rules — new inspections, new fees, new *fees*, always new fees — and every year the fees got bigger and the inspectors got hungrier, and you couldn't pay them in coin, do you understand? Coin was fine. Coin was never the problem. You paid them in *yes*. Yes, I'll carry your cousin's cargo and not ask what's in it. Yes, I'll look the other way at the toll bridge. Yes, yes, yes, until the word wore smooth as a river stone and meant nothing at all."

Mirabel said nothing. But something in her face shifted, a hairline crack of recognition, and I understood that she knew about *yes*. Everyone on this river knew about *yes*.

"When Papa got sick, the boat sat empty for a season, and the debts didn't. Debts are the only thing on this river that never take a season off." I laughed, and it hurt my chest, the fever still sitting in my lungs like wet sand. "So when the Hollis expedition posted for a cook — good wages, room aboard, travel upriver — I took it. Honest work. Real work. Cooking for scholars. My mother cried, she was so proud."

"And then the crates came."

"And then the crates came." I turned the burn scar toward the light, as if it could speak for me. "They arrived at night. No manifests. Men I'd never seen, wearing river authority jackets that fit them badly, like costumes. And Corvane's man — the smooth one, the one who smelled of cedar oil — he counted out wages for *loading*, separate from my cooking wage, cash, more in one hour than I made in a week. And he smiled at me and said, 'Heavy cargo needs strong friends, and strong friends eat free all season.'"

"What was in them?"

"I didn't open them. I want you to know that. Whatever else you think of me — I didn't open them. But I lifted them, and I'm a cook, Mirabel, I lift flour sacks all day, I know weight like you know current. Those crates were heavy wrong. Stone-heavy. Old-heavy. And one of them had a corner knocked loose in transit, and through the gap I saw—" I stopped. My mouth had gone dry. "Gold leaf. A fragment of painted wood. A face, part of a face, gilded, older than anything I'd ever seen. And I understood, and I loaded it anyway, because the money was already in my pocket, and because I told myself the story every loader tells: *not my cargo, not my crime, just my hands.*"

The rain filled the silence. Rain on a riverboat is a whole language — it drums the cabin top, hisses the side planks, murmurs under the floorboards where the bilge water talks back. We sat inside that language and let it say the things neither of us could.

"The professor found out," Mirabel said at last. It wasn't a question either. Elio must have told her the shape of it — the ledger, the confrontation, the fire.

"He found out near the end. He'd been suspicious for weeks — Corvane's men coming and going, supplies that weren't supplies. He was careful, the professor. Careful like a man walking on ice, testing every step. And then one evening he asked me to stay behind while the others went to the village, and he opened one of the crates right there in the mess tent, and he looked at me — not angry. That was the worst of it. He looked *sad*. He said, 'Amara, whose debt is this?'"

My voice broke on the next part. I let it.

"He knew. He knew before I said anything. He said the same thing happened at his last dig, and the dig before that — the antiquities trade doesn't hire guards, it hires people with debts. People who can't say no. He said, 'You're not the criminal here, you're the collateral.' And then he started writing in that ledger, and I helped him, God forgive me, I helped him write it down — every crate, every date, every payment, every name I could remember — because by then I wanted it stopped more than I wanted my wages. And then—"

"And then the fire."

"And then the fire." I closed my eyes and it was all there, waiting, the way it would be there forever: the orange light through canvas, the shouts, the professor pushing Juno toward the treeline with both hands and turning back for his notes, turning *back*, and me screaming at him not to, and the smoke taking him the way the river takes things — completely, without malice, without even noticing.

When I could speak again, Mirabel was still watching me. She hadn't moved. She had the stillness of deep water.

"So now you know," I said. "Your cargo, your crime, your cook. I helped put the stones on the boat that killed the best man I ever met. If you want to put me off at the next landing, I'll go. I owe you my life and I've made it heavier than it needed to be."

* * *

She was quiet for so long I thought she was composing the sentence that would end me. On this river, I've learned, the quiet ones are the dangerous ones; the loud men are just weather, but the quiet women are currents, and currents drown you.

Instead she reached down and pulled up the hem of her own trouser leg, and showed me the inside of her ankle, where there was a mark I hadn't seen — a small inked number, faded almost to nothing, the kind the lending houses in Vess tattoo on borrowers who've missed too many payments. Collateral, written on skin.

"Sixty-one," she said. "That was my father's number. When he drowned, the house transferred it to me. I was nineteen." She let the trouser leg fall. "He ran cargo too. Not antiquities. Worse, maybe — whatever paid. He told himself the same story you did. *Just my hands.* And then one night the cargo was heavier than the boat, and the storm was worse than the forecast, and he went down between Kessel Bend and the Teeth with a hold full of other men's sins, and the river kept the boat and gave back his compass, and the lenders came to his funeral to see who'd inherit the debt."

I couldn't say anything. There was a knot in my throat the size of a fist.

"So don't stand there and offer to get off at the next landing," Mirabel said, and now her voice did change — not louder, but rougher, like water over gravel. "Don't you dare make this clean. You loaded crates because the river backed you into a corner and a man with cedar-oil hands counted cash into yours, and every soul on this waterway has taken that same deal in one shape or another, and the difference between you and the ones who talk loudest about honesty is that you're the only one telling the truth about it." She stood, and the cabin seemed smaller with her standing, the way it does around certain people. "The professor called you collateral. Fine. But collateral that picks up a pen and writes down names — collateral that carries a wounded girl through mangrove water on a bad leg — that's not collateral anymore. That's a witness. And witnesses are worth more than cargo."

"Worth enough to keep aboard?" I asked, and hated how small my voice sounded.

"Worth enough to feed." She picked up the onion bowl and paused at the foot of the ladder, her back to me, and for a moment she wasn't a captain, just a tired woman in a lamplit box with rain coming down on all sides. "My father died of a debt. I've spent my whole life rowing away from that grave. And now I've got a boat full of people who fell in the same hole, and I find I can't throw any of you back." She climbed two rungs, then stopped. "Rest the leg. Elio will want to show you his charts when he comes down — pretend to be impressed, it keeps him calm."

And she was gone up into the rain-smell and the lamplight of the deck, leaving me alone with the drumming roof and the blanket that smelled of lavender and engine oil.

* * *

I slept, and dreamed of the camp burning, and woke once to hear Juno's voice above, thin and clear through the planks, reading numbers aloud — depths, probably, Elio's depths — and it sounded like a child counting stars, and I lay there and let it wash over me.

Near dawn the fever came back a little, a low tide of heat, and with it a dream that wasn't quite a dream: the professor standing in the mess tent holding the gilded face, saying *whose debt is this?* — and then the dream changed, the way dreams do, and it was Mirabel asking it, and then it was my mother, and then it was me, holding the question out to someone I couldn't see, and the someone took it, and it was heavy, stone-heavy, old-heavy, and I woke for good with tears drying on my ears and the rain gone soft.

Up on deck, voices. Elio's, quick and excited. Juno's, answering. And Mirabel's underneath them, low and flat, and one word carried down the companionway clear as a bell through fog:

"—checkpoint."

I pushed the blanket back and swung my legs off the bunk, and my leg screamed, and I made myself listen anyway.

Because I knew what checkpoint she meant. There was only one between here and the delta. The toll bridge. Corvane's toll bridge. And I knew something else too — something I'd been carrying since Vess like a stone in my shoe, something I'd told no one, not even the professor:

I knew the man who worked the bridge booth.

He used to ferry with my father.
