## 17. What Corvane Wants

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the second night — not the monsoon's hammering fury anymore but a steady, patient drumming on the Salt Wreck's patched roof, like a clerk tapping a pen while he waited for you to sign something you'd rather not. Elio Sarr sat at the little table beneath the lamp with the professor's case open in front of him, and he had not moved in an hour.

Mirabel had given him the table because his hands were the steadiest. That was her reasoning, anyway, delivered in that flat river voice of hers that never quite admitted it was kindness: *You read maps all day, cartographer. You can read paper.* Juno was asleep in the bow, curled against Amara's good side, and Mirabel was up top keeping the anchor watch, because she did not sleep well at anchor and had stopped pretending otherwise around them.

So it was Elio alone who opened the ledger.

He had been avoiding it, if he was honest with himself. He had carried the case out of the burning camp with the professor's blood still wet on his sleeve, and in all the days since — the gorge, Rook's launch, the cave, the Teeth — he had told himself there would be time. Time enough later, when they were safe, when Amara's fever broke, when the river let them breathe. But safety was a sandbar; it shifted under you. And tonight, with the rain talking its patient talk and the lamp burning low, there were no more excuses left to hide behind.

The ledger itself was unremarkable. A surveyor's field book, canvas-bound, water-stained at one corner where the professor had caught it in the flood two seasons back. Hollis had kept everything in these — Elio had watched him do it a hundred times, hunched over the camp table with his spectacles pushed up into his gray hair, transcribing measurements in that small, ruthless hand. *A map is a promise,* he used to say. *Every line you draw, you're promising someone the ground is true.*

Elio turned to the first page and understood, slowly, the way a man understands he has stepped off into deep water only after his foot finds no bottom, that Hollis had spent the last year mapping something no one had asked him to map.

* * *

It began as excavation notes. Trench logs, strata drawings, catalog numbers for potsherds and bronze fittings — the ordinary grammar of a dig. Elio knew this language fluently; he had spoken it for six years before the expedition took him on, before the scandal, before his name became a thing other cartographers said quietly and then stopped saying at all. He read the first twenty pages almost fondly. It was like hearing a dead man's voice again, and the professor's voice came through clear even here: fussy about provenance, merciless about error, occasionally given to marginal notes that were half scholarship and half complaint. *Site 4 disturbed. Modern intrusion. Ask Corvane's men why they were digging at night.*

There it was. The first crack in the wall.

Elio turned the pages faster now. The night-digging entries multiplied. Crates logged as *equipment* whose weights Hollis had recorded with a surveyor's precision and whose contents he had clearly, eventually, opened. A sketch of a packing crate stamped with a mark Elio recognized from the loading dock at Vess — a coiled serpent biting its own tail, pressed into the wood in black tar. Beside it, in Hollis's hand: *Not ours. Not archaeology. Third shipment this month. Where are these going?*

And then the ledger changed character entirely, the way a river changes character past a bend — gradually and then all at once.

Hollis had started following the crates. Not physically — Elio could see the shape of how he'd done it, through questions, through documents, through the slow patient accumulation of a scholar who could not stop asking *why*. There were copied pages here, torn from shipping manifests. Names of vessels Elio had never heard of, running routes that made no commercial sense: lighters going downriver empty and coming back heavy, calling at landings so small they didn't appear on any chart Elio had ever drawn. And threaded through it all, in increasingly agitated handwriting, the name that Elio had already learned to hate.

Corvane.

But not Corvane the patron. Not Corvane the smooth-voiced gentleman who overpaid a riverboat captain and asked charming questions about the water above the falls. This was another Corvane — or rather, it was the same man seen finally in full, the way a reef is the same rock whether the tide hides it or shows it.

Elio sat back. His mouth had gone dry. He looked up through the little aft window at the rain, at the black shape of the gorge walls beyond it, and felt the peculiar vertigo of a man whose map has just proved wrong at the scale of everything.

He went back to the beginning and read it properly this time, every page, taking notes in the margin of his own chart book because his hands needed something to do.

* * *

What Corvane wanted — what the ledger laid bare across forty pages of the professor's meticulous, damning arithmetic — was not antiquities. The antiquities were incidental. Profitable, certainly; Hollis had estimated the value of three shipments and arrived at figures that made Elio's eyes sting. But the statues and the bronzes and the looted grave-goods were, in Corvane's economy, almost a side business. Seed money. Cash flow.

What Corvane was buying was the river.

Page by page, entry by entry, Hollis had assembled the shape of it. Concession deeds — copies, excerpts, references — for riparian land on both banks, bought through shell companies with names so bland they were practically camouflage: the Kestrel Trading Concern, the Meridian Water Company, the Vess Landing Improvement Society. Toll rights on two of the three navigable channels above the falls. Mooring leases at every deep-water berth for sixty miles. Fuel depots. Warehouse fronts. And woven through the property holdings, in Hollis's careful cross-references, the human architecture that held it all up: names of river authority officials beside sums of money, dates of payments matched to dates of permits granted, a customs inspector's signature appearing on manifests for ships that had never carried what their papers said.

Elio recognized some of the names. Everyone on the river would. That was the horror of it — the quiet, spreading horror that rose in him like floodwater as he turned the pages. Aldous Ren, the harbor master at Vess, who had once waved Elio's own survey boat through without inspection and bought him a drink after. The toll-keeper at Graywater. Two members of the river court itself, the institution that was supposed to be the law's last word between the falls and the delta. Hollis had documented bribes going back four years. Four years of a river being sold piece by piece, landing by landing, and nobody below decks had felt the keel change.

And at the center of it, the thing that made Elio close the ledger for a moment and press the heels of his hands against his eyes:

The professor's death hadn't been an accident of greed. It had been maintenance.

Hollis had figured it out — the ledger showed exactly when, a margin note dated eleven months ago, the handwriting suddenly cramped and hurried: *They know I know. Careful now.* He had stayed anyway. He had kept recording. He had turned his dig into a siege position and his field book into a weapon, and he had died holding the evidence that Corvane's whole structure depended on never reaching daylight. Because that was the point Elio kept circling back to, the fact that made his chest tight and his thoughts run cold: the professor's evidence wasn't just proof of crimes. It was the only complete copy. Corvane's empire ran on paper — deeds, concessions, permits, bribes — and Hollis had collected the paper that could unravel all of it. Burn the ledger and the empire stood. Deliver it, and every name in it was answerable.

No wonder Rook had chased them through the Teeth. No wonder Corvane had a man inside the river authority, inside the courts, inside everything. They weren't fleeing a smuggler's anger. They were carrying the only thing standing between a man who owned the river and a man who owned nothing but a leaking boat.

Elio thought of Mirabel's face when she talked about her debt. He thought of Dane Rook's offer in the cave mouth — *the ledger for your life* — and understood now that it hadn't been a generous bargain. It had been the price of a nail being pulled out of a collapsing building.

* * *

"You've been reading all night."

Mirabel came down the companionway with rain still beaded in her hair, moving with the boat's roll the way other people moved with their own pulse. She poured coffee from the dented pot without asking, set a cup in front of him, and leaned against the bulkhead with her arms crossed, waiting. She had a way of waiting that felt like depth sounding — dropping a line and reading what came back.

"It's worse than we thought," Elio said.

"Everything on this river is worse than we thought." But she pulled out the opposite bench and sat, and he saw that she meant to hear all of it.

He told her. He laid the ledger open between them and walked her through it the way he'd walk her through a chart — start at the falls, work downstream. The shipments first, then the shell companies, then the concessions, then the names. He watched her face as he went. She was hard to read, Mirabel Iversen; her expressions moved underneath her skin like fish under brown water. But at certain points he saw the surface break. At the toll rights. At the fuel depots. And most of all at the name Aldous Ren, harbor master of Vess, where Mirabel had taken on Corvane's crates and taken Corvane's coin and sailed away feeling clever about the overpayment.

"He bought the harbor," she said flatly. "I tied up at his dock. I shook his hand."

"He probably owns the handshake too."

She didn't laugh. She turned a page with one finger, careful, as if the paper might bite. "These concessions. This stretch here—" she tapped a deed reference, "—that's Kessel Bend. That's Nesta's stilt-house, or the water under it. He owns the water under her house."

"He owns the water under most houses," Elio said. "That's the design. You don't have to rule people if you own everything they need. Berths, fuel, channels, bridges. Every captain on this river works for him whether they know it or not. They just think they're working for themselves." He heard his own voice go bitter and didn't try to soften it. "That's how it got me, the first time. Nobody points a gun at you. They just make the honest route cost more than you can pay."

Mirabel was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the roof. In the bow, Amara shifted in her sleep and murmured something, and Juno's small hand tightened on her sleeve without waking.

"The river court," Mirabel said at last. "Two of them. You're sure?"

"Hollis was sure. Dates, amounts, signatures." Elio tapped the cross-referenced pages. "This is why nothing ever happens to men like Corvane. Why Nesta's son's ferry burned and nobody answered for it. It's not that the law is weak out here. It's that the law is *purchased*. We keep thinking if we get the ledger to the authorities, justice happens. But the authorities—" he gestured at the open pages, at four years of names, "—are the thing we're delivering it *against*."

"So where does it go?" Mirabel asked. "If not to the river authority, if not to the court — where? You don't carry a thing like this four hundred miles to burn it in a cookstove."

"I don't know yet," Elio admitted. "But I know this much. A document like this isn't finished until it's *read*. Until enough people see it that it can't be unseen. One copy, one fire, one drowned courier — gone. But if it lands somewhere it multiplies..." He trailed off, looking at the ledger, at the professor's small ruthless hand marching down the pages. Hollis had known that too. Elio was certain of it now. Somewhere in these pages, or in the way the old man had organized them, there would be an intended destination. Hollis had always planned three moves ahead. He had taught Elio that a mapmaker's real job wasn't drawing the terrain — it was anticipating where the reader would need to go next.

"We're not running anymore," Mirabel said.

Elio looked up.

She was staring at the ledger with an expression he hadn't seen on her before — not the weary competence, not the flinty spite she wore like armor. Something harder and more awake underneath both. "I've been thinking this whole time we're cargo haulers who picked up bad freight. Get downstream, deliver the box, collect whatever's left of our lives. But that's not what this is." She put her palm flat on the open page, over the coiled serpent mark, over the list of concessions that included the water beneath Nesta Kessel's house. "This is a war already happening. We just found out which side we're on."

"Corvane has a gunboat," Elio said. "And a harbor master, and a customs service, and a court, and more money than either of us will see in ten lifetimes. We have a holed riverboat, a bailing bucket, a wounded cook, and a teenager."

"And we have the only copy of the truth," Mirabel said, "and a captain who knows every sandbar between here and the delta better than the men who bought the charts." She closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a coffin, and slid it back into the waterproof case. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow you're going to earn your keep. I want you to map us a route to the delta that doesn't touch a single thing Corvane owns — and then I want you to tell me where it touches everything he owns, because I'm done running past his property."

She climbed back up into the rain to take her watch, and Elio sat alone with the lamp and the case and the sound of the river moving dark beneath the hull.

Before he slept, he opened his chart book to a fresh page and drew the river entire, from the falls to the sea, in one unbroken line. Then, consulting the ledger, he began to mark it — a small serpent at every landing Corvane held, every channel he taxed, every official he owned. By the time the lamp burned out, the map of the river they both loved had become something else entirely: a body with the corruption showing through its skin, marked in black ink from head to tail.

Only one long stretch stayed clean. Downstream of the Devil's Teeth, past the flooded country, a winding thread of back-channels and family landings that appeared on no company chart — the river the river families kept for themselves.

At the end of that thread, Elio wrote a single word in the margin, and looked at it for a long time before he blew out the lamp.

*Kessel.*
