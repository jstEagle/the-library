## 18. The Trap at Low Water

The rain had thinned to a gray whisper by the time they reached the mouth of the Sallow Cut, and Mirabel brought the Salt Wreck in against the current with her heart hammering harder than the engines.

She'd known something was wrong before she saw it. The river told her first — the way the fishing platforms stood empty on their poles, the way no smoke rose from the reed camps where the eel-catchers lived all season without fail. Then the smell: coal smoke and diesel, riding downstream on a wind that should have carried nothing but mud and rot and rain.

She cut the engines to idle and let the boat drift, and there it was, half a mile ahead where the Sallow Cut narrowed between bluffs of red clay: the blockade.

Two barges lashed together across the channel, gunwales bristling with men, a chain heavy as a church bell's tongue sagging between them into the water. Behind them, moored in the shadow of the eastern bluff like a sleeping animal, sat the gunboat — Corvane's *Vigilant*, all iron and smokestack, its bow gun covered with a tarp that fooled nobody.

Elio came up beside her at the wheel, Juno a step behind him with Amara leaning on her shoulder. The cook was upright now, which was something, but she moved like a woman carrying a sack of wet sand where her side used to be, and every few steps the fever flickered back behind her eyes.

"Well," Elio said quietly. "That's that, then."

"That's not that," Mirabel said.

She looked at the blockade the way she'd been taught to look at rivers since she was small enough to stand on a bait crate to reach the wheel. Not as a wall. As water. Water always went somewhere. The question was whether it went where you wanted or where it drowned you.

"The Cut's the last deep channel this far down," she said. "Everything east of here is braided shallows until you hit Kessel Bend. Everything west is the Teeth." She nodded upstream, toward the white noise of the rapids still faintly audible even over the rain, like a beast breathing in its sleep. "Corvane knows it too. He's not blockading the Cut because he thinks we might come through. He's blockading it because he knows we have nowhere else."

"Then what do we do?" Juno asked. Her voice was steady. She'd stopped crying somewhere around the second day after they'd found her in the reeds, and Mirabel hadn't decided yet whether that was strength or something sadder wearing strength's clothes.

Mirabel didn't answer right away. She turned the wheel a fraction and eased the Salt Wreck backward, downriver, around the shoulder of a willow island, and dropped anchor in a backwater where the water lay brown and still as tea left standing. Only then did she look at the four of them — Elio with his ink-stained fingers and his ruined charts, Juno with her father's jaw set stubborn in her face, Amara gray-lipped and burning, and herself, reflected dimly in the cabin glass, looking older than thirty-two ought to look.

"We stop running," she said.

* * *

She laid it out on the chart table while the rain ticked against the deck above, and she watched their faces as she did it, because she needed to know which of them would flinch.

"Rook survived the Teeth," she said. "I know because I've spent three days listening to the river, and the river says he pulled two men off the outer bar and limped back up to Corvane with a holed launch and a grudge. He won't make the mistake of underestimating me again. That's good for him. Bad for us."

"And so?" Elio said.

"So Corvane has one gunboat, one blockade, and one problem: he can't find us. This stretch of river is forty miles of channels, oxbows, flooded timber, and reed beds, and I could hide this boat in any hundred of them until the monsoon drowns us all. He can't search it all. He doesn't need to." She put her finger on the chart, on the wide pale smear of the Vexshall Flats, upstream of the blockade but below the Teeth. "He just needs to wait. He knows we need the Cut eventually. He knows Amara needs a doctor eventually. He can sit behind that chain until the river itself hands us to him."

Amara laughed — a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it. "Then we're already dead. We're just arguing about the date."

"No," Mirabel said. "Because waiting is only smart if you're the one being hunted. And I'm done being hunted."

She looked down at the flats on the chart, at the pencil marks her own hand had made over years — soundings, snags, the shifting skeleton of sandbars she knew the way other people knew the streets of their hometown. Her father's handwriting was on this chart too, faded brown, from before she was born. *Bar moves east after high water. Trust the reeds, not your eyes.*

"He wants us to come to him through deep water, where his gun draws and his draft is king," she said. "So we don't. We go to him through water where his boat can't follow and mine can. We use the Salt Wreck as bait."

Silence. Rain on the roof. Somewhere far off, the Teeth grinding their stones.

"You want to sail *toward* them," Juno said slowly.

"I want to sail toward them *loudly*. Smoke, lights, engine noise — everything a man who's been humiliated once wants to see coming. Rook will chase. He'll chase fast, because pride makes men stupid and Corvane makes them desperate. And I'll run him straight across the Vexshall Flats at low water, when the bars are bare and the channels are lies." She tapped the chart again, twice, hard. "There's a channel through those flats that's six feet deep at low water. There's a sandbar a quarter mile south of it that looks exactly like six feet of water and isn't. It's eighteen inches. His draft is five. My draft is four and a half, and I know the line down the middle of that channel better than I know my own hands."

Elio was already pulling his pencils toward him. She could see the cartographer waking up behind his eyes, the part of him that loved water for its shape rather than fearing it for its teeth. "You'd need the bar positions exact. They'll have shifted with the flood crest."

"They will. So we go look. Tonight, at low water, in the skiff. You map, I steer."

"And then?" Amara said. "Say it works. Say you ground him. What then? You think Rook and a dozen armed men are going to stand on a sandbar and apologize?"

Mirabel met her eyes. The fever had burned the softness out of the cook's face and left something harder underneath, something that had loaded contraband crates to pay family debts and regretted it ever since, and Mirabel found she trusted that hardness more than most people's kindness.

"No," she said. "They're going to answer for the professor. Or swim."

* * *

They argued until dark. Of course they did. Elio argued because mapping a trap was one thing and boarding an armed gunboat was another, and he said, in his precise, infuriating way, that the plan required everything to work perfectly and plans never worked perfectly. Juno argued because she was fifteen and furious and wanted to burn Corvane's whole empire down tonight with her own hands, which was its own kind of problem. Amara argued least, because Amara understood debts, and understood that some debts you pay with money and some you pay with blood, and that the second kind at least was honest.

But in the end they were four people with nowhere else to go, and the plan was the only door in the room, and doors don't care whether you knock politely.

By full dark the rain had stopped altogether and the clouds had torn open along the western horizon, letting down a single blade of moonlight that turned the whole river to hammered tin. Low water. Mirabel took the skiff with Elio and poled out into the flats, leaving Juno aboard the Salt Wreck with instructions to keep the lamp lit and Amara warm, and if anyone but them came up the channel, to blow the whistle and run her aground and take to the mangroves.

The flats at low water were a different country. Sandbars stood bare and glistening, long whales' backs of wet sand steaming faintly in the cold. Channels that had been ten feet deep at flood were now shin-deep rills threading between them, and the reeds stood tall and black against the moonlight, hissing with wind and hidden birds. Out past the eastern edge, miles away, the blockade's lanterns burned in a ragged string, and beyond them the gunboat showed a single light at its masthead, patient as a spider.

"There," Elio whispered, crouched in the bow with his board across his knees, sketching by feel more than sight. "The main channel bends left after the third bar. Your chart says straight."

"My chart's a year old. The flood moved it." Mirabel poled them along the edge of the bar, reading the water the way her father had taught her — the smooth glassy tongue where current ran deep, the wrinkled boil where it dragged over sand, the dead spot behind every snag where debris collected and lied. "Mark it. Left bend, then hard right around the snag line. There's a hole behind the fourth bar, see the foam? Deep enough to turn a boat in, if you're quick."

They worked for three hours, poling and drifting, Elio sketching, Mirabel sounding with the pole and memorizing, until the map in her head and the map on his board were the same thing. Twice they heard the blockade's patrol launch coughing distantly in the Cut, and twice they went still among the reeds and let the darkness fold over them until it passed.

On the pole back, Elio spoke without turning around. "You know he'll shoot at you. On the run in. Before he grounds."

"Yes."

"And the hull—"

"Is already scarred worse than my knuckles. She'll hold." Mirabel shipped the pole and let the current carry them the last hundred yards. "You know what my father used to say about the flats?"

"No."

"He said the river doesn't cheat. It just tells the truth slower than men want to hear it. Every bar, every hole, every lie in the water — it's all written down if you're patient enough to read it. Corvane reads ledgers. Rook reads faces." She looked up at the moon. "Nobody out here reads water except us."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old pewter, and with it came the argument Mirabel had been saving for last — the one with herself.

She stood at the wheel with the engine ticking over below her, watching the mist lift off the flats, and she made herself look honestly at what she was about to do. Not the navigation; that part was clean, almost holy, the one thing in her life she'd never doubted. The rest of it. Boarding a gunboat. Putting Elio and Amara over the side with hooks and knives against men with rifles. Turning her father's boat — her inheritance, her whole self, the only thing she'd ever loved that hadn't left or died — into a weapon pointed at other human beings.

Her father had never fought anyone in his life. He'd paid, and paid, and paid, until the river took him, and the compass had come home without him, and Mirabel had grown up learning that patience was a virtue because the alternative was admitting patience got you killed.

Well. She was done being patient. Dane Rook had put a bullet across her bow. Corvane had burned a camp full of scholars and murdered an old man whose only crime was writing things down. The law was bought, the courts were bought, the river authority was bought, and the only justice left on this water was the kind you built with your own hands, the same way Nesta Kessel's people had been building it for twenty years, plank by plank, name by name, grudge by grudge.

"Helm's yours," she said to Elio, stepping aside. "Take us up the west channel, slow. When you clear the willow point, give her everything she has and head for the flats. Loud. Ugly. Like a boat that's scared and stupid."

"And you?"

"I'll be on the roof with the rifle, being the bait they can see." She checked the action — she'd taken it off the burned camp's armory rack, and unlike Elio, she actually knew how to use it. "Juno, you're below with Amara. Hatches battened. If we ground, you bail. If we're boarded—" she met the girl's eyes, "—you stay below. Whatever you hear. That's not a request."

Juno's jaw set exactly the way her father's had in the photograph Elio kept folded in his ledger case. "And if you don't come back?"

"Then you take the skiff and the knowledge in Elio's head and you get to Nesta Kessel, and you finish what we started." Mirabel turned back to the wheel. "But I've run worse water than this with less reason to live. I intend to come back."

Elio eased the throttle forward. The Salt Wreck gathered herself, shouldered into the current, and turned her bow upriver, toward the lanterns of the blockade, toward the iron shadow of the gunboat, toward the trap Mirabel had spent three nights building in the shallow places where the river told the truth slow.

Mist peeled away. The sun came up red behind the Teeth. And somewhere ahead, a lookout on a barge shouted, and a bell began to ring.

"They've seen us," Elio said.

"Good," said Mirabel, and opened the throttle all the way.
