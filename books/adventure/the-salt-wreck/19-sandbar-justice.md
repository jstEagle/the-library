## 19. Sandbar Justice

The rain came down the way it had come down for three days now, without temper and without mercy, and Mirabel Iversen stood at the wheel of the *Salt Wreck* and loved it.

That was the strange truth of it. She should have hated the monsoon — it had soaked her charts, drowned her engine room twice, turned every rope on the boat into something that weighed like a dead man — but the rain was hers now. It was the only ally she had that Corvane's gunboat didn't. The *Vigilant*, all steel and searchlight and swagger, was built for open water and open threats. It was not built for a river gone blind, a river running high and brown and full of lies, where every sandbar had shifted and every channel had a mood and only one woman alive knew them all by heart.

"You're smiling," Elio said. He stood at her shoulder, rain sheeting off the brim of his hat, a hand-inked chart of the lower reach clutched under his oilskin. He'd spent two days on the cliff tops and two nights at the drafting table, and the map he'd made was better than anything the river authority had ever published — because he'd made it for killing, not for shipping.

"Am I?"

"You are. It's frightening."

"Good." Mirabel eased the wheel a spoke to port, feeling the current lean against the hull like a drunk leaning on a fence. "Let them be frightened too."

Ahead, through the gray weave of rain, the drowned willows of the Sedge Reach bent and streamed. Beyond them, half a mile on, the last deep channel of the river narrowed between two bluffs — the only water deep enough for the *Vigilant* to make speed, the only water Corvane could blockade, and the only water, if Mirabel had done her arithmetic right, that would carry his gunboat straight onto her father's oldest secret.

She checked the compass. Her father's compass, brass worn to gold at the edges, needle steady under glass gone cloudy as an old man's eye. It pointed north. It always pointed north. It had pointed north through debt and drowning and every bad decision of her life, and she had decided that if she was going to do one good thing on this river before the season took her boat, it would be under that needle's honest eye.

Below decks, Amara was wrapping the stock of the expedition rifle in oilcloth so her bandaged hands wouldn't slip. Juno was aft with the ledger in a waterproof satchel, ready to run it into the reeds if everything went wrong. And Mirabel was about to do the thing she'd sworn on her father's grave she'd never do.

She was going to give the *Salt Wreck* to the river as bait.

* * *

They had argued about it for a night and half a day, the four of them hunched in the flooded cave while the monsoon hammered the limestone like a fist on a door.

"You're asking that boat to lie to the water," Elio had said. "You've told me a hundred times the *Salt Wreck* draws too much for the Sedge Reach. If the bar has shifted —"

"Then she grounds in two feet of water instead of one, and we wade off, and I've lost nothing but my pride." Mirabel had said it flat, and it had been a lie, and everyone in the cave had known it. "Rook won't resist. He's chased us for a hundred miles and I'm going to hand him the kill. A lone riverboat, running the narrows in a monsoon, wallowing like a wounded duck. He'll come in at full steam to take us before we can reach the bluffs."

"And when he comes in at full steam," Juno had said slowly, the professor's daughter, the sharp one, "he'll be doing it blind. In water he's never sounded."

"In water nobody's sounded," Mirabel said. "That's the point. There's a bar off the willow point — my father called it the Widow's Apron. It doesn't show at any water level. It's not a shoal, it's a shelf of old river clay with silt packed on it like mortar, and the current pours over it so clean it reads as deep water on any sounder ever made. My father found it the hard way. Cost him his rudder and a season's wages. He marked it in his notes — not on any chart, in his notes, because he said the river gives you nothing for free and neither should he."

She'd pulled the oilskin packet then, her father's cramped pencil notes, soft with handling, and laid it on the crate between them. Elio had read it in the lamplight, lips moving, the cartographer's hunger in his face — the hunger of a man who had spent his life mapping other people's secrets and had just been handed one worth keeping.

"He never published this," Elio said.

"He never told anyone but me. I'm telling you because if I'm standing at the wheel when we do this, I'll want your voice on the water with me. Distances. Angles. My eyes will be full of rain."

Amara had said nothing through most of it. She'd sat with her wound propped on a crate, fever-flushed, and when the plan was settled she'd said only: "Rook shot at us at the toll bridge. He'll shoot first at the boarding. I want to be on the first rope."

"You can barely hold a cup," Mirabel said.

"I can hold a rifle across my lap and pull a trigger with one finger. And I've got the most reason on this boat to see it done." Her eyes had gone to the ledger satchel, and Mirabel had understood. Amara had loaded Corvane's crates with her own hands. She had helped build the thing they were about to bury. There was a debt on her too, and debts on this river got paid or they got passed down, and Amara had decided, with the clarity of the fevered, that hers would end here.

So it was settled the way things on the river were always settled — not by law, not by vote, but by who was willing to stand in the water.

* * *

Now the willows came up on the left, streaming, and Mirabel brought the wheel over and let the *Salt Wreck* swing wide and slow, wallowing exactly as much as she'd promised Elio she would. She'd cut the engine to half. She'd left the deck lights burning. Every coward's instinct in her body screamed to kill the lights, and she overrode it the way you override a limb gone to sleep, with deliberate, grinding effort.

Bait had to be seen.

"There," Elio said. He didn't point. Cartographers never pointed — they spoke in bearings. "Bearing three-one-zero off the bluff notch. Smoke, low, under the rain."

Mirabel found it. A smudge of coal smoke flattened by the downpour, and beneath it, growing by degrees, the gray shape of the *Vigilant*, riding the channel like a knife riding a sheath. Searchlight off — no need for it, not against one crippled riverboat. She could see figures at the rail. She could see, at the bow gun, the shape of the man who'd fired across her stern a lifetime ago.

Dane Rook had survived the Devil's Teeth. She'd known he would. Men like Rook didn't drown; they floated to the surface of other people's disasters and climbed out dripping someone else's blood.

"He sees us," Juno called from aft.

"Let him look," Mirabel said, and put the wheel over, and ran her father's boat straight at the Widow's Apron.

* * *

The next four minutes were the longest acting performance of her life.

She let the *Salt Wreck* stagger. She let her fall off a wave wrong, ship water over the gunwale, wallow broadside to the current for three full seconds — three seconds in which Mirabel's hands shook on the wheel not from performance but from the animal horror of feeling her boat helpless — before she brought her head around and limped on toward the willows. Behind her, gaining, the *Vigilant* came on at full steam, exactly as promised, exactly as hoped, her bow wave a white scar in the brown water, Rook standing at her rail with the rain streaming off him and the whole river's patience in his face.

"He's not sounding," Elio said, voice tight. "Mirabel, he's not sounding. He's reading your wake. He thinks your wake is his water."

"He's been chasing us for a hundred miles," Mirabel said. "He's stopped thinking. That's the whole plan."

The willow point came up. The bluff notch came into line — a notch in the far bluff that existed on no chart, that her father had penciled into his notes with a single word: *gate*. When the notch, the willow, and the bow of the *Vigilant* made one straight line, Mirabel would have thirty seconds.

"Gate's closing," Elio said. "Now."

She spun the wheel hard to starboard.

The *Salt Wreck* heeled, wallowed, and — Mirabel's heart in her throat — grounded, gently, horribly, with a long soft shudder, on the near edge of the bar, in water barely up to her knees. Exactly as planned. Exactly as promised. A wounded duck, beached and burning her deck lights, twenty yards from deep water.

And the *Vigilant*, at full steam, in the rain, following a wake into water that read deep and wasn't, came on like a train coming off a bridge that isn't there.

Mirabel watched it happen and would remember it all her life: the moment the gunboat's bow lifted — that first wrong lift, the sea suddenly deciding, the whole two hundred tons of steel and arrogance rising as if to look over something — and then the sound. Not a crash. A grinding, a long tearing groan, steel meeting old river clay with the whole river's current behind it, and the *Vigilant* stopped. Not slowed. *Stopped*, the way a nail stops when it hits bone, her momentum converted in one screaming second into a list to port and a bow buried to the hawseholes in the Widow's Apron.

Men went down at the rail. The bow gun swung wild. Somewhere aft a voice was already shouting about backing full, and Mirabel, standing in the rain on the deck of her grounded, beloved, expendable boat, began to laugh — a low, ugly, triumphant sound that she didn't recognize as her own.

"Full steam astern," Elio said, watching. "She's not going anywhere. That bar holds like a hand."

"Then it's time," Mirabel said, "to go collect."

* * *

They went in the rain, in the skiff, three of them — Mirabel at the oars, Amara in the bow with the rifle across her lap, Elio amidships with a boathook and, Mirabel suspected, absolutely no plan for using it. Juno stayed with the *Salt Wreck* and the ledger, the satchel strapped to her chest, a pistol in her hands that she'd learned to use in three days of monsoon afternoons. The rain covered them the way a curtain covers a stage. The *Vigilant*'s crew, busy with the drowning of their own ship, never saw the skiff slide out of the willows and cross the twenty yards of churning brown water.

They came up the gunboat's starboard quarter, under her high stern, and Mirabel held the skiff against the hull while Amara went up the ladder first — one-armed, one-fingered, fevered, faster than any of them. Then Elio. Then Mirabel, oars shipped, and the three of them came over the rail into the rain and the chaos and stood on the deck of the *Vigilant* with the river's whole verdict in their wet fists.

The crew never fought. That was the thing Mirabel hadn't dared hope for and got anyway. Half a dozen hired men on a grounded, flooding ship in a monsoon, and the arithmetic was plain to every one of them: the river had already beaten them, and the only question left was whose side of the ledger they wanted to be standing on when it closed. Two of them put their hands up before Amara's rifle even came around. The rest simply stopped working and looked at the gunwales, measuring the water, and Mirabel let them look. The river would sort them. It sorted everyone.

Rook was at the bow, at the buried gun, shouting orders nobody was following, and when he turned and saw them — saw the three of them walking up his dead ship's spine through the rain — Mirabel watched something go out of his face. Not fear. Calculation, and then, when the calculation came up empty, the thing underneath it.

"You're dead," he said. "All of you. Corvane doesn't — you think this ends here? You think one sandbar —"

"I think," Mirabel said, "that you're standing on a sandbar."

She said it quietly, and the rain carried it, and she watched it land. Rook looked down, as if the deck might tell him something, and Amara's rifle came up and rested its sight picture on his chest, and the shouting from the engine room died, and there was only the rain and the river grinding the steel into the clay and the four of them — no, the three of them and the man — alone on the bow of the *Vigilant*.

"You shot across my boat," Mirabel said. "You chased a cartographer and a dead man's ledger a hundred miles. You burned nothing yourself — I know that, Rook, the camp wasn't your work — but you carried the water for the men who did. You'd have put a bullet in a girl at the toll bridge. I saw you deciding to."

"She's a witness," Rook said. "She's cargo that talks. That's all any of this is. That's all *you* are. You think you're punishing me? Corvane will build another boat. He'll hire another me by Thursday. You've got a ledger and a leaky hull and —"

"And the river," Mirabel said.

Rook stopped.

"The river," she said again. "You've been on it a long time, Dane. You've been paid to be on it. But you've never once been *of* it, and that's the difference, and that's why you're standing where you're standing. This river has a law older than any court Corvane's bought. My father told me about it. Nesta Kessel told me about it. It's not written down anywhere. It's just this: the river gives everyone the same choice in the end, and it doesn't care who you work for."

She nodded past him, at the water churning off the bar, brown and muscular and patient.

"Swim," she said, "or answer for the professor."

* * *

He looked at the water. She watched him do it — watched him weigh it, the way she'd watched a hundred men weigh it on this river, the current against the consequence. The water was high, fast, cold, full of drowned willows. It was not a kind option. But the other option was Amara, and the ledger, and the river families down at Kessel Bend who had waited years for a face to put to the fire that took Nesta's son, and Rook was a survivor before he was anything, survivors before anything, always.

"You'll never get him to talk," Rook said, and it wasn't a threat anymore. It was almost — almost — a warning. Almost a man's last honest act. "Corvane's got the river authority in his pocket and half the provincial bench besides. You take me down there, your ledger dies in a filing cabinet. You know it. I know it."

"We know it," Mirabel said. "That's why we're not taking you to the authority."

"Then where?"

"Kessel Bend," Amara said. Her voice was rough with fever and it carried anyway, flat and absolute. "To Nesta. To the families. To the ones who've been keeping their own list since before you were hired." She worked the rifle's action with one finger. "They've waited a long time, Dane. I'd think about the swim."

And Rook — Mirabel saw it happen — Rook looked at the rifle, and at the rain, and at the two hired crewmen edging toward the stern, and at the brown water pouring over the Widow's Apron, and he made the choice the river gives everyone.

He went over the side.

He went in clean, arms wrapped, a professional's entry, and the current took him the way the current takes everything, fast and impartial, and Elio was at the rail with a line before Mirabel could speak — because Elio was like that, Elio would have thrown a line to Corvane himself if the arithmetic of mercy allowed it — and the line went out and the rain came down and for a hundred yards they tracked the bobbing, fighting shape of Dane Rook swimming for the willow shore with everything he had.

They pulled him out at the willows, half-drowned, coughing the river out of his lungs, and Amara stood over him with the rifle and said, "You swim good. You'll want to keep swimming. Downstream. There's a town past the bend where nobody knows your face, and if you're smart you'll be on a cart before dawn. And if you're not —" she didn't finish it, and the not-finishing was worse than any threat.

Rook lay in the reeds, gasping, and looked up at Mirabel through the rain.

"The professor," he said. It wasn't defiance. It was something else — a debt of his own, maybe, paid in the only coin he had left. "Corvane didn't order it. Not at first. The professor found the second ledger — the real one, the concessions, the land — and he sent a copy downriver. Corvane found out it wasn't the only copy. That's when the camp burned. That's what you're carrying. It's not evidence, woman. It's the last copy of the only thing he's afraid of." He pushed himself up on his elbows, water streaming off him. "He knows it's on the river. He's known since the Teeth. He's not sending Rook after you anymore."

Mirabel felt the rain go cold on her neck.

"He's coming himself," Rook said. "And he doesn't need a gunboat. He owns the toll bridge, the locks, and every deep channel left between here and the sea. You beat the river, Iver sen. See if you can beat the man who bought it."

Then he rolled, and crawled into the willows, and the rain closed over him, and he was gone — downstream, or to Corvane, or into the river's long memory, and Mirabel found she didn't care which.

* * *

They stood on the grounded *Vigilant* while her crew waded away into the rain, and the gunboat settled by degrees onto the bar with a long, groaning sigh, like something finally allowed to lie down. Amara sat against the buried bow gun with her wound propped on a bollard, gray-faced and grinning. Elio was already sketching — the man could not stop, would not stop, drawing the wreck, the bar, the water, the whole shape of the morning, as if a thing didn't truly happen until it existed on paper.

Mirabel stood at the rail and looked down at the brown water pouring over the Widow's Apron, and thought about her father, and about debts, and about how the river had just collected one, in full, in front of witnesses.

It wasn't over. Rook's last words sat in her chest like swallowed stone. Corvane was coming, and he owned everything the river still had left to give.

But that was the next reach. That was the next water.

"Get us off this bar," she said, turning for the rail, for the skiff, for home. "We've got a boat to save — and I have a feeling we're going to need every inch of her before this river's done."

Behind them, through the rain, far upstream, a sound came down the drowned gorge that none of them had heard before and all of them understood at once: the deep, patient, mechanical throb of a much larger engine, coming down the last open channel.

Corvane was on the river.
