## 20. Scuttling

I had never heard a river go quiet before.

In the weeks since the camp burned I had learned the Salt Wreck's every voice — the cough of her engine, the groan of her patched hull, the way Mirabel talked to her in a low running murmur like a woman soothing a horse through a storm. The river itself had its own vocabulary: rain on water, rain on canvas, the long hiss of the gorge releasing us into the drowned flats below the Teeth. But when Corvane's gunboat came around the last bend above the sandbar justice we had just delivered, the river went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a dangerous man walks into it. Even the rain seemed to fall more carefully.

From my place at the stern rail, soaked to the skin, hands still shaking from the boarding — from Rook's face when Amara put the rifle against his jaw and asked him, very politely, whether he could swim — I watched the gunboat come down the channel like a piece of the storm given a hull.

She was bigger than anything that should have been this far upriver in monsoon flood. Grey-green paint, a wheelhouse armored with riveted plate, a bow built to shove debris out of her way rather than go around it. She rode low and heavy, and she did not slow for the flooded timber that the river had spent three days throwing across every channel. She simply went through it, and the sound of it carried across the water — cracking, grinding, indifferent — like something eating.

"Corvane," Juno said beside me. She said it without fear, which frightened me more than the boat did. Since the sandbar she had not spoken above a whisper; grief had taken her voice down to almost nothing. Now it came back all at once, flat and clear, her father's daughter to the bone. "That's his boat. I've seen it at the dig. He used to bring supplies."

Supplies. The word sat in my mouth like ash. I had loaded those crates myself, back in Vess, back when I was a man who took an expedition's money knowing what it carried. Heavier than they should have been. I had told myself it didn't matter what a contract held as long as the money cleared. Every one of us on this deck had a version of that sentence. Amara's had been about her family's debts. Mirabel's had been about her own. Mine had been the oldest and the smallest and somehow the most shameful: *it isn't my business.* The professor had died because four people — five, counting Rook's crew, ten, counting every official on Corvane's payroll — had each decided the crates were someone else's business.

"Mirabel," I called forward. "Company."

She was already looking. Of course she was. She stood at the helm with the rain streaming off her hat brim, one hand on the wheel, and she watched the gunboat approach with the expression I had come to know over these weeks — the one that wasn't calm at all but was load-bearing, the way a beam is load-bearing. Behind it, I knew now, there was always calculation. Water depth. Current. Distance. Escape.

There was no escape. That was the arithmetic none of us needed doing aloud. The Salt Wreck was holed in two places, patched with canvas and prayer, riding light because we'd jettisoned half her ballast to survive the Teeth. The gunboat could run us down in open water the way a dog runs down a crippled bird.

"Get below," Mirabel said. "All of you. Take the ledger case into the engine compartment and sit on it if you have to."

"Mirabel—" Amara started, her wounded arm bound tight against her chest, her face grey with fever and exhaustion and the particular sickness that comes after you've held a rifle on a man and meant it.

"That wasn't a request." Then, softer, without turning her head: "Elio. Stay."

* * *

The gunboat came on until she was two hundred yards off our starboard quarter, and there she stopped, engines idling, wallowing in the swell like a sleeping animal deciding whether to wake. Rain sheeted between us. Through the murk I could make out figures at her rail — armed men, six or eight of them — and one figure apart from the rest, standing at the bow under an umbrella he didn't need, because the umbrella was not for the rain. It was for theater. Mr. Corvane had always understood theater. In Vess he had negotiated our contract leaning back in his chair with his fingers steepled, asking his too-many questions about the river above the falls, and even then I had thought: *this man has rehearsed being charming.* Now he had rehearsed something else, and he performed it across two hundred yards of rain with a speaking trumpet.

"Captain Iversen!" His voice carried strangely well, flattened by the rain into something almost gentle. "You've cost me a great deal of money today. A boat. A launch. An enforcer whose pride will take years to recover." A ripple of laughter from his men, thin and obedient. "But I'm a reasonable man. You have something of mine aboard. Give it up, keep your vessel, keep your lives, and I'll even forgive the debt you owe me. All of it. Clean slate. You could buy a new boat. A better one."

I looked at Mirabel. Her hand hadn't moved on the wheel. But I saw her jaw work once, twice, the way it did when she was chewing on something bitter.

"You know what's funny, Corvane?" she called back, and her voice cracked on the first word and steadied after, and I understood she'd been holding that crack in reserve, letting him hear it, letting him think it was fear. "Everyone keeps offering me clean slates. Rook offered me triple pay. You're offering me forgiveness. Nobody ever asks what I want."

"And what do you want, Captain?"

"I want the professor's ledger to reach people who'll read it out loud," she said. "And I want you to explain to the river why you killed a man who only wanted to draw its maps."

The pause that followed was long enough for the rain to fill it completely. When Corvane spoke again, the charm had been set down like a tool no longer useful.

"So be it."

He turned away from the bow. The gunboat's engines came up from idle to a full-throated roar, and the whole hull swung toward us with terrible unhurried grace, the way weather swings, the way debt swings.

"Hard to port!" I shouted, though there was nowhere to port to — the flats were shallow maze-water, reed islands and drowned fence lines and channels that changed with every hour of rain. "Mirabel, she can't catch us in the shallows—"

"She doesn't need to catch us." Mirabel threw the wheel over and the Salt Wreck leaned into her turn, groaning, the patched hull complaining in a voice I had learned to dread. "She needs to hit us once. Look at her draft, Elio. She's riding heavy. She's built to ram."

We ran. God help me, we ran — the old boat throwing herself through the reed water with everything her tired engine had left, spray coming over the rails in sheets, the four of us below decks clinging to whatever was bolted down while Mirabel threaded channels she was reading not from any chart but from the color of the water and the lean of the reeds and, I swear, some older inheritance than either. I came up to the stern once, twice, and both times the gunboat was there, falling behind in the narrow water, gaining in the open, patient as arithmetic. Corvane had stopped performing. He simply drove us.

And then I understood — watching him swing wide to cut off the last deep channel south, watching him herd us exactly where he wanted, toward the open flats where his draft wouldn't matter and ours would be a death sentence — that this had never been a chase. It was a closing of a hand.

"Mirabel!" I shouted into the engine roar. "He's driving us to deep water!"

"I know."

"There's nothing out there but him!"

"I *know*, Elio." And then she did the thing I would think about for the rest of my life, the thing that made me understand finally what kind of woman I had signed on with, back in Vess, back when I thought I was just a cartographer taking a supply job. She turned the wheel hard to starboard, away from the deep channel, away from escape, and ran the Salt Wreck straight at the shallows — the drowned field north of the old Kessel ferry line, the water Nesta Kessel had warned us about, waist-deep over a bed of silt and old pilings, water no gunboat could follow and no sane captain would enter.

Because no sane captain would enter it planning to leave, either.

"What are you doing?" Juno was at the companionway, Amara behind her, both of them reading the shallows ahead and Mirabel's course and arriving, as I was arriving, at the same terrible sum. "Mirabel, we can't get out that way either—"

"No," Mirabel said. "We can't."

* * *

There is a moment — I have it now, fixed, permanent, the way a map fixes a coastline — when I saw her look at her boat.

Not at the gunboat. Not at the shallows. At the *Salt Wreck*. Her hand came off the wheel and lay flat on the wood beside it, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder, and her mouth moved around words that the rain took from her, and whatever they were, they were not navigation. They were goodbye. Her father had built this boat's keel before she was born. Her father had drowned owing money, and this boat was the only thing the river had ever given back. She had told me that in the cave, in the dark, in the voice people use for things they've never said aloud. *Everything I am floats.*

"Everybody off," she said. "Now. Skiff. Take the case."

"We're not leaving you—" Amara began.

"I'm right behind you. Go *now*."

They went. Juno first, clutching the ledger case to her chest like a child, then Amara, hauling herself over the rail with her bad arm and dropping into the skiff where Juno caught her. I hesitated one second — one — and Mirabel grabbed my collar and put her face close to mine, close enough that I could see the rain and something else mixing on her cheeks.

"Elio. If you drown, who charts the way home? *Go.*"

I went. Over the rail, into the skiff, into chaos — rain, reeds, Juno screaming at me to row, the Salt Wreck surging on behind us with Mirabel alone at her wheel, holding her course into the shallows at full throttle, drawing the gunboat after her like a matador drawing a bull past the cape.

Corvane took it. Of course he took it. Rage does that; desperation does that; the sight of a lone woman in a crippled boat refusing to stop does that. The gunboat came on at full speed, straight into water that swallowed her bow to the anchor hawse, and for one glorious, impossible moment I thought the river itself might do our work for us — that she'd ground and heel and drown her engines in the mud, and it would end there, in the reeds, without another shot fired.

But Corvane was not trying to follow anymore. He was trying to kill.

At the last moment, with the Salt Wreck wallowing in water too shallow to float her much longer, the gunboat swung her heavy bow and *rammed* — not a glancing blow, not a warning, but a full-speed strike into the old boat's starboard quarter, the sound of it carrying across the flats like a door slamming in a house, oak and iron meeting with a crack I felt in my teeth.

The world went white with spray. When it cleared, the Salt Wreck was listing hard, her stern already low, black water pouring through the opened planking where the gunboat's stem had folded her frame like paper. And Corvane's boat was backing off, engines screaming in reverse, dragging herself free of the mud, turning — turning toward the skiff. Toward us. Toward the ledger.

Toward Mirabel, who was still aboard.

"ROW," I screamed, and Juno and Amara rowed, and I rowed, tearing the skin off my palms, while behind us the Salt Wreck settled by degrees into the brown water, and a figure moved along her tilting deck — small, deliberate, unpanicked — gathering things. The compass. I saw her take the compass from the binnacle and put it inside her coat, against her heart, and then she stepped onto the rail as the deck went under her boots, and dove.

She surfaced twenty feet from us, gasping, and we hauled her in over the transom, and she knelt in the bilge water among us, dripping, watching her father's boat go down.

It did not go quickly. The Salt Wreck died the way she had lived — stubbornly, by inches, her bow rising as her stern filled, her mast leaning, her name-board catching the grey light one last time before the river closed over it. No drama. No explosion. Just water doing what water does, patiently, to everything that floats.

On the gunboat, a hundred yards off, men were cheering. I heard Corvane's voice, high and triumphant, giving orders to pull free of the mud, to run us down, to finish it —

And then the gunboat shuddered, listed, and stopped.

* * *

Here is what Mirabel knew and Corvane did not, because here is what Mirabel had always known and Corvane had never bothered to learn: the river is not a road. The shallows she had chosen were not random. Under that brown floodwater lay the bones of the old Kessel ferry landing — pilings, chain, a sunken barge from the wreck that had killed Nesta Kessel's son, the wreck the river courts had never punished anyone for. Corvane's gunboat, backing hard off the mud in panic, had driven her hull directly onto the barge's ribbed spine, and she was fast upon it, holed, settling, her engines flooding, her armed men suddenly men standing in water that was rising around their boots.

For a long moment nobody in our skiff said anything. We drifted among the reeds, four of us and a ledger, watching the great grey boat sag in the shallows with her people scrambling, and the rain came down, and the river made its quiet sounds around us, and Mirabel knelt in the bilge with her fist closed around her father's compass so tightly her knuckles went white.

"He rammed her," she said at last. Her voice was very small. "He didn't have to ram her. He could have let her sink on her own. She was going down anyway." A breath that shook going in and came out level. "He couldn't stand for her to die of anything but him."

"Mirabel," Juno said gently, "we have to move. They have a skiff. They'll—"

"They have no skiff," Amara said. There was blood in her voice as well as fever, and something harder underneath. "Look. Look at them."

We looked. The gunboat's men were abandoning her — wading, swimming, floundering toward the reed banks in ones and twos, their rifles held overhead, their fine armor-plate wheelhouse tilting like a tombstone going into the ground. Somewhere in the scrum of them, a figure in a ruined suit was shouting orders that no one obeyed, and then was not shouting, and then was climbing, alone, onto the gunboat's highest point — the roof of the wheelhouse — as the water took the rest.

Mr. Corvane stood on his sinking empire in the middle of the flooded flats, and the river rose around him, and nobody came when he called.

Mirabel watched him for a long time. I watched her watch him. I had seen her furious and I had seen her afraid and I had seen her, on the sandbar, hand Rook the choice the river gives everyone. This was none of those. This was something quieter and worse — a woman looking at the man who had killed a good man and sunk her whole inheritance, and finding that the river had already done the arithmetic, and that there was nothing left in her hands to add to it.

"The ledger gets to Kessel Bend," she said finally. Her voice came out scraped raw and steady. "Nesta's network. The families. Every name in that book, read out loud on every dock from here to the delta." She opened her fist and looked at the compass, its needle swinging, finding north through the rain, through everything. "My father ran from a debt his whole life and it drowned him. I'm done running from mine. This—" she touched the boat-shaped absence in the air where the Salt Wreck had been, the gesture heartbreaking in its precision, as if she could feel the hull still there beneath the water, "—this was the price. Fine. Paid. Let it buy something."

Then she looked up at the wheelhouse roof, at the small drowning figure of Mr. Corvane, and the rain ran off her chin, and she said, in the tone of a woman setting terms with the river itself:

"But first — somebody fetch that man out of the water. Alive. He owes the professor's daughter an answer, and I intend to hear him give it."

Juno stood up in the skiff, the ledger case strapped to her back, sixteen years old and lit from inside by something that had been waiting all this time for permission.

"Row," she said.
