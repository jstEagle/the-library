## 22. A New Hull

The river gave her back in pieces, and the river families carried each piece home.

Mirabel stood on the mudflats below Kessel Bend on the first morning of the dry season and watched them come. Boats of every description — pirogues riding low with tools, flat-bottomed punts stacked with salvaged timber, a patched excursion skiff that had no business floating and floated anyway — converging on the shallows where the bones of the Salt Wreck lay half-buried in silt, her keel pointing downstream like a finger accusing the water.

Three weeks since the Devil's Teeth. Three weeks since Corvane's boat had come at them out of the rain with murder in its engine note, and Mirabel had done the only arithmetic left to her: her hull against theirs, her shallows against their draft, and everyone she loved off her decks before the impact came. She had stood on Nesta Kessel's stilt-house landing and watched the Salt Wreck heel over in the shallows with a hole torn in her flank, and she had not wept, because the water in her eyes would have been salt and the river had enough of that.

Now the families were here, and the water was low and clear, and the wreck lay exposed like something washed up from a dream.

"There she is," Nesta said, coming to stand beside her. The old woman's hands were wrapped in tar-stained cloth, and her voice had the gravel of someone who had spent eighty years shouting over current. "Ugliest boat on the river. Always was."

"She was beautiful."

"She was a floating argument with a hull. I remember when your father launched her. Half of Vess came to watch, and half of those came to laugh." Nesta squinted at the wreck, at the ribs showing through the torn planking like the bones of a great fish. "He laughed loudest of anyone when she took on her first water."

Mirabel smiled despite herself. That sounded like him. The compass sat heavy in her breast pocket, as it always did, and she touched it through the cloth without thinking, the way other people touched a charm or a scar.

Down on the flats, the work had already begun. Amara stood waist-deep in the shallows with a chain over her shoulder, her wound long healed down to a puckered star of scar tissue she showed to children like a medal, hauling on the line with the same stubborn rhythm she'd once used bailing. Juno was up on the mudbank with a slate, calling depths, her voice cracking less than it used to — the girl had grown a season's worth in a monsoon, the way river children did, grief and danger boiling her down to something harder and clearer. And Elio — Elio was everywhere and nowhere, moving along the waterline with his sketchbook, measuring, muttering, drawing the wreck the way he'd once drawn coastlines: with a cartographer's hunger, as if the boat were a country he intended to map so thoroughly she could never be lost again.

"He hasn't slept," Nesta observed.

"He sleeps. He just draws in his sleep. I've seen him."

* * *

They raised the hull on the third day, and it took forty people to do it.

The river families had their own engineering, older than any of the river authority's stamped documents — a grammar of levers and rollers and sung cadences, each family owning a different verse. The Ondos brought their capstan, a thing like a wooden drum with a century of rope-grooves worn into it. The Ferro cousins rigged the cradles from green bamboo and old ferry cable. Nesta directed from her landing with a boat hook she used less as a tool than as a conductor's baton, and when the keel finally broke free of the mud with a sound like a held breath released, the whole bend of the river shouted, and somewhere upstream a heron went up in a white panic, and Mirabel stood in the shallows with river water in her boots and her father's compass against her heart and let herself feel all of it at once, which was more than she had let herself feel in years.

Elio found her afterward, sitting on the capstan drum with her feet in the water, watching the wreck sit on its cradles above the tide line for the first time in three weeks.

"You're smiling," he said, suspicious. "I've made notes. This is historically significant."

"Don't chart it."

"Too late. I've already fixed its position." He sat down beside her, and for a while they said nothing, which was the language they'd become fluent in over a monsoon and a gunboat and ten thousand miles of shared fear. Then: "The planking's worse than I thought. Six strakes gone on the port side. The frames are sound — your father overbuilt her, thank God — but we'll need timber, and good timber, and the sawyers up at Two Forks will want paying."

"I know what they'll want paying."

"Mirabel." He said her name the way he said *north* — carefully, because everything depended on it. "The debt. Corvane's contracts. The money you took from him in Vess. The river families are talking about it. They're saying the debt died with the gunboat, that no court will enforce a smuggler's paper now that the ledger's landed—"

"The ledger landed with Nesta's people, not the courts. Nesta's people don't cancel debts. They *remember* them." Mirabel turned the compass over in her hands, its brass worn to brightness at the hinges where her father's thumb had lived. "I took Corvane's money. I'll pay it back. Every coin, even if he never sees a coin of it. I'll pay it to the river, if that's what it comes to. But it gets paid."

Elio studied her for a long moment. Then he opened his sketchbook to a page she hadn't seen — and there it was, drawn in his precise, patient hand: the Salt Wreck as she would be, not as she was. New planking. A raised wheelhouse. A shallow-draft hull with lines he'd clearly stolen from the pirogues, the punts, the families' boats — a riverboat designed by a man who had spent a monsoon learning how the river actually moved.

"I've been working on the plans," he said, "since the day she went down. I couldn't sleep. This was the only thing that helped." He hesitated, and the hesitation was so unlike him that Mirabel looked up. "I drew her the way *I'd* build her. I know she was your father's. I know every line of her is yours. If you want her rebuilt exactly as she was—"

Mirabel looked at the drawing a long time. Her father's boat, line for line, was right there in her memory; she could have rebuilt it blindfolded, could have felt her way along the old frames in the dark and known every joint. And it would be a beautiful thing, a faithful thing, a tomb.

"She was built to run supplies," Mirabel said slowly. "Cargo and a crew of two, and a skipper who trusted no one enough to feed them at her table." She tapped the drawing, where Elio had sketched a deckhouse with windows all around, and benches, and room. "She never had windows in the wheelhouse. My father said windows were just holes waiting to be broken."

"He was wrong about that," Elio said. "A window's just a wall that decided to let the light in."

Mirabel laughed — a short, surprised sound, rusty from disuse. "You've been talking to Juno."

"Juno says you should paint her green. Amara says red. Amara also threatened Juno with the ladle, so the vote may be compromised."

* * *

The work took the rest of the dry season, and Mirabel learned what it was to be rebuilt alongside her boat.

The sawyers at Two Forks, when they heard whose boat it was and whose ledger had broken Corvane, sent timber down at half price and pretended they hadn't. The Ondo women taught Juno to caulk seams, and the girl's hands blistered and healed and blistered and finally hardened, and she stopped flinching at the sound of the mallets — which, Mirabel noticed, she had never stopped doing before, not once in all the weeks since the camp burned. The mallets sounded like something else, Juno admitted once, late, in the dark of the half-built wheelhouse. They sounded like that night. But now, she said, they sounded like the night the families came, too, and the second sound was winning.

Amara cooked for forty on a stove built from two oil drums, and the flats below Kessel Bend began to smell, every evening, of river fish and peppers and the particular bread she made that Mirabel would have crossed the Devil's Teeth again to eat. Amara never asked forgiveness again. She had made her confession at the fever's edge and Mirabel had raged and then relented, and between those two poles they had found a working peace, the way a boat finds its trim — not stillness, but balance, adjusted hour by hour. Some evenings Mirabel caught herself laughing at something Amara said and had to stop, startled, like a woman hearing her own voice from another room.

And Elio drew. He drew the frames and the strakes and the sternpost; he drew the wheelhouse with its windows; he drew, on the last page of a fresh sketchbook Nesta bought him in Vess, a small chart of the river from the Devil's Teeth down to the sea, with the Salt Wreck's new position marked at Kessel Bend, and beside the mark, in his small careful hand: *home*.

Mirabel found that page by accident and said nothing about it, and he knew she'd found it, and neither of them said anything, and that was its own kind of vow.

* * *

On the last day of the dry season, they launched her.

Not the Salt Wreck — the families were firm on this, Nesta firmest of all. A boat that had drowned and risen again needed a new name, or no name at all until the river gave her one. So they built her unnamed, and painted her — by compromise and near-mutiny — a deep river green below the waterline and a red so dark it was nearly the color of old blood above it, and Juno painted a small white compass rose on the bow with her own hand, because she said every boat should know where it was going even if its skipper didn't.

They slid her down the rollers at dawn, with the mist standing on the water in columns and the whole bend of the river turned out to watch, and she took the water like something remembering its own name. No list. No groan of strained frames. Elio had overbuilt her, the way Mirabel's father had overbuilt the first one, and when Mirabel took the wheel — a new wheel, but her hands knew its circle the way they knew the compass — the boat answered her so exactly that for a moment she couldn't speak.

She ran her upriver a mile and back, alone, because some things a captain does alone the first time. The current pushed and the hull answered and the windows — the windows Elio had insisted on — threw early light across the wheelhouse in long gold bars, and Mirabel stood in the middle of them and let the light fall on her, and did not think of holes, or breaking.

When she came back down to the Bend, they were all waiting on the landing: Nesta with her boat hook held like a standard, Amara with bread, Juno with the slate she still carried everywhere out of habit, Elio with his sketchbook and an expression he was failing to keep casual.

"So," Mirabel said, bringing her alongside soft as a breath. "She needs a name."

"The river will give her one," Nesta said. "Give it time."

"Or," Juno said, and then stopped, and went red, and Amara nudged her, and she blurted: "Or we could just pick one. I've been thinking. Since the camp. I've had a lot of time to think about names." She looked at the bow, at her own white compass rose. "The *Ledger*."

Silence on the landing. Amara's hand found Juno's shoulder.

"Too heavy," Elio said quietly. "A boat named after a book of debts — she'd sit low in the water her whole life. I know the physics of it. I've drawn it." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and certain, the way they'd been at the cliff top above the Teeth. "Name her for what she carried, not what she proved. She carried us. All of us, people who had nothing to do with each other, from one side of a monsoon to the other." He closed the sketchbook. "The *Found*."

"Found what?" Amara said.

"Just found. Like a boat the river gives back. Like a crew." He shrugged, suddenly shy. "Like a family that wasn't looking for one."

Mirabel stood at the wheel and looked at them — the old woman, the cook, the girl, the cartographer — and felt the compass against her breast like a second heartbeat, pointing, as it always had, not north but true.

"The *Found*," she said, and the landing shouted it back, and up and down the bend of the river the families took it up, and somewhere in Vess, in a courtroom that no longer answered to Corvane, clerks were already entering the testimony of forty river households into a record that could never be bought — but that was downstream, and later, and the law's business. Here, in the gold morning, there was only a boat, and a name, and the water taking both.

She cast off the lines herself. Amara came aboard first, uninvited, unapologetic, and took the stove without asking. Juno took the bow. Elio took the chart table he'd built into the wheelhouse, and opened a blank page.

"Where to, Captain?" he said.

Mirabel put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the compass, and felt it swing, and settle, and point.

"Upriver," she said. "There's sandbars I've never met. I'd like to learn their moods."

And the *Found* turned her bow to the current, and the river — which gives, and takes, and gives again — carried them home the long way, by water, in sunlight, with the whole day ahead.
