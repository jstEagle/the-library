## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river changed its mind three times before breakfast, and Lena Amberdale had already argued with it twice.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with her bare feet braced against the deck plates gone slick with morning condensation, reading the water the way other people read letters—slowly, carefully, and with the understanding that anything might be a lie. Ahead, the Sepik spread itself wide and brown as tea left too long in the pot, deceptively calm in the early light. But she could see the mood of it. A long seam of ruffled water downstream where the current argued with itself, and beyond that, the pale suggestion of a sandbar that hadn't been there last month, growing fat and confident on silt the floods had brought down from the highlands.

"Move, then," she told it quietly, and swung the wheel two spokes to starboard.

The *Kestrel* answered with the grudging dignity of a boat her age—forty years old if she was a day, sixty feet of ironbark and rust, patched in a dozen places with a dozen different metals. She'd been built to carry copra and mission supplies. Now she carried anything that paid: drums of kerosene, sacks of rice, a crocodile farmer's crates of yearlings that had spent three days complaining in the hold, chickens that hadn't stopped complaining at all. This morning her deck was stacked with timber cut at Ambunti and bound for a sawmill downriver, and the smell of fresh-cut wood mixed with diesel and river mud into something Lena had long ago stopped noticing and would have missed anywhere else.

The sandbar slid past on the port side, close enough that she could see the water peeling off it in long, clean curves, like a knife being drawn along a whetstone. A heron stood on its spine, unimpressed by her.

"You and me both," she said.

She ran the *Kestrel* alone. Everyone on the river knew it, and everyone on the river thought they knew why—a dead husband, most of them decided, or a debt, or simply the particular madness that came to women who'd been on the water too long. They were welcome to their theories. The truth was simpler and more dangerous: the fewer people aboard, the fewer people who could be asked about her, and the fewer people who could answer.

Six years she'd been Lena Amberdale now. Long enough that the name fit like a shirt worn soft. She almost never caught herself reaching for the old one anymore. *Almost.*

The river bent north around a bluff of black rock hung with vines, and the channel narrowed, and she forgot everything but the work. This was the part of the day she loved, if she was honest enough to use the word—the hour when the water demanded everything and gave her back a silence in her head. There was a sandbar here that migrated like a nomad, appearing and vanishing with the seasons, and the channel past it threaded between two drowned logs she'd memorized by their broken shapes. She took the *Kestrel* through at half-throttle, hands moving without instruction, eyes flicking from the ripple line to the foam pattern to the single leaf spinning in an eddy that told her the current had shifted overnight.

The river was a language. She'd learned it the hard way, by nearly dying in it, and now it was the only conversation she trusted.

* * *

Timbunki landing announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke, dried fish, the sweet rot of fruit heaped at the market stalls. Then the village itself resolved out of the green: stilt houses leaning over the water, canoes crisscrossing like stitches, and the long tin-roofed shed that served as the harbor office, warehouse, and throne room of Boss Angra.

Lena brought the *Kestrel* in against the current, eased her alongside the rotting jetty with a touch of reverse engine that made the whole hull shudder, and had the lines made off before the villagers who'd gathered to watch had finished gathering. She paid two boys in tobacco to swing the timber ashore, and by the time the last plank was stacked, the heat had come up thick enough to chew.

Angra was waiting for her in the shade of the shed, as she'd known he would be. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, in a shirt that had been white in some other decade, and he sat in the shed's only proper chair with the air of a king who had learned that a throne was just a chair nobody else was allowed to sit in.

"Captain Amberdale." He pronounced it carefully, savoring it, the way he savored everything he intended to use against someone. "You are early. The river must be in a good mood."

"The river's never in a good mood. I'm just early."

"And the debt?"

Straight to it, then. Lena had expected nothing else. She'd been carrying Angra's debt for eleven months—four hundred kina borrowed in the desperate wet season when the *Kestrel*'s engine had seized and every other lender on the river had somehow heard, before she'd even asked, that Captain Amberdale was not to be extended credit. She'd never learned how the word had traveled so fast, or who had started it. She had her suspicions, and the suspicions had a face she saw in her nightmares.

She counted the notes out of the oilskin pouch at her belt and laid them on the crate beside his chair. Two hundred and sixty. The last of the timber run, plus what the kerosene had brought at Ambunti, plus the coins she'd been meaning to replace in the emergency jar under her bunk.

Angra counted it again, slowly, wetting his thumb, though she'd watched him count money faster than most men could read. "Two hundred and sixty," he said. "You still owe one hundred and forty."

"The debt was four hundred."

"The debt was four hundred." He smiled. His teeth were red with betel nut, like a warning. "The *interest* is the rest. You did not think I lend money for free, Captain. I am not a mission."

Lena kept her face still. On the river, your face was the only thing that couldn't be boarded or searched. "We agreed on one hundred in total."

"We agreed when the river was low and you had no choice. The river is high now." He spread his hands, a fat man's shrug, all apology and no concession. "You know how it is. Everything grows in the wet season. The grass. The mosquitoes. The debt."

There was a time—not so long ago, though it felt like another woman's life—when Lena would have told him exactly where he could put his interest, in words that would have made the dock boys whistle. That woman had learned, at terrible cost, what happened when you made the wrong man lose face in front of a crowd. So instead she said nothing, and let the silence do the talking, and watched Angra enjoy it.

"I will tell you what," he said at last, benevolent as a crocodile in the sun. "You run cargo for me. Three runs, Ambunti to the coast, my price, my schedule. We call it even after."

Your price, your schedule, and your name on my papers, Lena thought. Your hands on my boat. That was how men like Angra collected—not the debt, but the debtor. She'd seen it happen to three captains already. Two of them still ran his freight, hollow-eyed, taking whatever he told them to carry and never asking what was inside the sealed drums. The third had asked, once, and now fished a stretch of river two hundred miles downstream, if he fished at all.

"I'll think about it," she said.

"Think quickly. The monsoon is coming early this year. Everyone says so." He picked up her money, folded it into his shirt with a tenderness he'd never shown a person, and waved her off like a fly. "Go. Drink something. You look like the river already got you."

She walked out into the heat with her spine straight and her stomach cold, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for a week. One hundred and forty kina. The emergency jar held thirty. The next cargo run, if the weather held and the engine held and the sandbars behaved, might clear sixty. The monsoon, if it came early, would close the upper river for weeks and leave her anchored, bleeding interest, until Angra's offer looked like the only floating thing in a drowned world.

He knows, she thought, standing at the jetty's end, watching the brown water slide past. He knows I can't say no much longer. And someone told him when to start squeezing.

She pushed the thought down where she kept all the others, in the flooded hold of her memory, and went to buy diesel and rice.

* * *

The trade store at Timbunki was a hot tin box run by a Chinese family who'd been there longer than the government, and it was where the river's news changed hands along with its goods. Lena kept her head down and her list short—diesel filters, rope, quinine, the good black tea she allowed herself as her single extravagance—but the store had its own current, and it pulled her in despite herself.

"—saw them at Pagwi, two boats, white men but not mission men—"

"—asking about a prospector, a German or a half-caste, name of Voss—"

"—clean boots, that's what I heard. Who walks the Sepik in clean boots?"

Lena paid for her tea and did not hurry, and did not look up, and listened with her whole body the way she listened to water.

They'd come up from the coast a week ago, the talk said. Two boats with outboards that cost more than a village, men in pressed shirts who showed a paper with an official stamp and asked their questions softly, over and over, at every landing from Angoram up. Looking for a prospector. Elias Voss. Old, sick, half-mad, they said—went into the middle country two years ago after gold and came out the other side, if he'd come out at all. The men in clean boots wanted very much to find him. They were offering to pay for information. They were also, the talk agreed more quietly, offering the other thing to anyone who'd already given information and might be tempted to give more.

The storekeeper's wife, old Mrs. Chen, slid Lena's change across the counter and said, very softly, in the tone of a woman discussing the weather: "They asked about boats, too. Which captains run alone."

Lena met her eyes for exactly one second. "Did they like the answer?"

"Nobody runs alone," Mrs. Chen said, "unless they have a reason. They wrote down the names of all the captains with reasons."

Lena walked back to the *Kestrel* through the market noise without hearing any of it. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the first thing the river had taught her, that panic was a luxury that sank boats. But under the steadiness, something old had opened one eye.

*Voss.* She didn't know the name. That was the trouble—she didn't *not* know it either. It sat in her mind like a leaf spinning in an eddy, marking a current that had shifted. Men in clean boots did not spend syndicate money chasing a dead prospector's ghost. Which meant Elias Voss was not dead, or not yet, or had left something behind that was worth more than he was.

She cast off at mid-afternoon, ahead of the heat's worst hours, and took the *Kestrel* downriver alone. The channel past Timbunki ran wide and slow through country so green it seemed the water was only tolerated there, a guest in the forest's house. Egrets lifted off the banks in white sheets. Somewhere behind the wall of trees a cockatoo screamed and screamed and was answered.

She stood her watch at the wheel and let the boat think for both of them, and by the time the sun came down huge and orange behind the western bluffs, she had talked herself into calm. So there were strangers on the river asking questions. There were always strangers on the river asking questions. Her papers said Lena Amberdale, master of the *Kestrel*, born in a coastal town she'd picked off a map for its dullness, and the papers were good ones—she'd paid a man in Rabaul too much for them, and he'd been worth every coin. Six years of invisibility didn't crack because two boats with expensive outboards came asking after a prospector's name.

The night came up out of the water like a tide. She anchored in a side channel she knew, a black pool under fig trees where the current was kind, and ate rice and tinned fish on the aft deck while the frogs took over the world. The stars came out in their brutal southern abundance, so many that the darkness seemed the exception rather than the rule. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered—the monsoon clearing its throat, weeks early, just as Angra had said.

She was almost asleep when she heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. A wrongness in the sound. The *Kestrel* swung gently on her anchor with the current, and the reeds along the bank whispered their constant whisper, and threaded through that whisper came something that didn't belong—a rhythm broken, a rustle with weight behind it. An animal, she told herself. A pig from the gardens, a crocodile hauling out. She lay in her bunk and listened the way she read water, and the wrongness came again, closer, and this time it had a shape: the dragging, labored movement of something that wanted to reach the water and was spending its last strength getting there.

Lena got up. She told herself it was to protect the boat—a wounded animal blundering aboard could wreck a night's sleep, and a man blundering aboard could wreck a great deal more—but her hand found the torch and her feet found the deck before the argument was finished.

She played the torch along the bank, low, under the reed tops the way she'd learned you should, and the light caught what the sound had promised: a man on his belly at the water's edge, half in the reeds, half in the river, one arm stretched out toward the channel as if the water were a woman he'd been dancing with and couldn't bear to let go. His shirt was mud to the shoulders. His boots—she registered this with a cold, slow turn of her stomach—were ruined, but they'd been good ones once. City boots. Clean-country boots.

The man's head came up. The torchlight found his face: gray as river clay, hollowed, a beard gone to seed, and eyes that burned with the particular terrible brightness she'd seen twice before in her life and hoped never to see again. The brightness of a man spending his last coin of life all at once.

"Kestrel," he said. His voice was a ruin. "You're the Kestrel. They told me—downstream—they told me you run alone."

Lena stood at the rail with the torch in her hand and six years of careful, invisible, hard-won silence all around her, and heard it begin to crack like ice under a boot.

"Go away," she said, without much conviction, as the man in the reeds began, with the last of his strength, to crawl toward her boat.
