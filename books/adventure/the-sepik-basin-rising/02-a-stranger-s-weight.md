## 2. A Stranger's Weight

He crawled the way drowning men crawl—slow, deliberate, as if the mud itself had agreed to carry him and might change its mind.

Lena watched from the rail of the *Kestrel*, torch held high, and did the arithmetic she always did. Distance to the boat: ten meters of black water and a tangle of reeds. Time to cast off and be gone: under a minute. Cost of helping: uncountable.

The torchlight found his face. It was a bad face to look at—hollowed at the cheeks, skin gone the gray-yellow of old paper, lips cracked and dark. Fever had him in its fist already; she could see it in the way his eyes wouldn't settle on her, sliding past her shoulder to things that weren't there. But his hands were working, pulling himself through the muck hand over hand with a stubbornness that meant he'd come a long way on that strength alone, and it was nearly gone.

"Please," he said. The word came out wet.

Lena Amberdale had rules. They were not written down anywhere, because writing things down was how trouble started, but they were carved into her the way the river carved the banks: slowly, and with great patience, and never to be undone. Rule one: no passengers without papers. Rule two: no papers, no exceptions. Rule three, which contained all the others: nobody learns your name, your route, or your business, because the moment someone knows those three things about you, you are no longer invisible, and invisibility is the only armor a woman with her history can wear.

The man in the reeds broke rule one by existing.

"You're the Kestrel," he said again, and coughed, a deep wet sound like something tearing loose. "They said downstream. At Angoram. A woman runs alone. Fast boat. Doesn't ask questions."

"That's me," Lena said. "Doesn't ask questions. Doesn't answer them either."

She should have doused the torch. She should have been gone already, the diesel coughing awake, the current taking her into the dark where the channel forked three ways and only she knew which branch ran deep this season. Every second she stood there was a second the river could turn against her—a floating log, a sudden squall, another boat's lantern coming up the bend. And more than the river: every second was a second this man could remember her face, her boat, her heading, and sell all three to whoever asked.

But she didn't move. That was the thing she would think about later, lying awake while the rain worked the canvas—the thing she couldn't explain even to herself. He had crawled toward her light instead of away from it. In six years on this river she had learned that everything living fled fire when it was weak enough to know better. This man came toward it because he had nothing left to lose, and there was something in that which reached past her rules and gripped something older and less careful underneath.

"I'm not a passenger boat," she called down.

"I'm not—" Another coughing fit bent him double in the reeds. When he straightened, there was blood on his palm, a fine bright thread of it. "I'm not asking for charity. I'm asking for passage. Upstream." His eyes found hers finally, and for a moment the fever receded like water off a sandbar, and what looked back at her was clear and desperate and terribly sane. "Past Ambunti. As far as you'll take me."

Ambunti. Two days' hard running through channels that were strangling themselves with silt before the rains. She hadn't gone past Ambunti in four years, and there were reasons, and the reasons all wore uniforms or suits.

"No," she said.

She turned to go below. That was the plan—douse the torch, start the engine, let the dark do what her conscience couldn't. Her hand was actually on the hatch when he spoke again, and what he said stopped her the way a snag stops a hull: suddenly, and with a grinding that you feel in your teeth.

"I'll pay."

"Everyone pays. I don't take sick men upstream to die in my cargo hold."

"Two hundred kina."

The number hung in the steamy air between them. It was a lot. It was enough to make her pause, and he saw her pause, and she hated herself for the pause.

"Not enough," she said. "Not for what you're asking. Past Ambunti is syndicate water. Their patrols run the gorge. If they board me with a fevered stranger aboard who they're clearly looking for—" she gestured at him, at the ruin of him, "—and they are looking for you, don't tell me they aren't, I can smell it—then I lose my boat, my cargo, and probably my freedom. Two hundred kina doesn't cover that. Nothing covers that."

"They're not looking for me." He laughed, and it turned into another cough. "Nobody's looking for me. That's rather the point of me."

"Then who beat you half to death and threw you in the river?"

"The river did most of that. The rest was a crocodile, or a log. I genuinely don't remember." He tried to smile. It didn't hold. "I've been in the water since yesterday. I walked out of the bush at Karawari with nothing and I've been trying to flag boats ever since. You're the first one that stopped."

*You're the first one that stopped.* Because every other captain on this river had done exactly the arithmetic she'd done and arrived at the same sum. She was not compassionate. She was simply the slowest to finish the calculation.

"Three hundred," he said.

"Go home, old man."

"I don't have a home." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way a man reports the weather, and somehow that was worse than weeping would have been. "That's rather the point of me too."

Lena stood at the rail and felt the two halves of herself pull apart like a mooring line under strain. One half was the woman she had built, plank by careful plank, over six years: the woman who ran alone, who paid her debts and kept her mouth shut and moved like smoke through the basin, who had survived a courtroom and a prison and the long gray afterward by never, ever letting anyone close enough to hurt her. That woman knew exactly what to do with a feverish stranger in the reeds. That woman was already halfway downriver.

The other half was quieter and much older, and it remembered being nineteen years old and alone in a city that had decided what she was before she opened her mouth, and how one person—a dockhand, a stranger, a woman whose name she'd never learned—had bought her a meal and told her which wharf master didn't ask questions. One meal. One piece of information. It had kept her alive long enough to become someone else.

She looked at the man in the reeds. He had stopped crawling. He lay with his cheek in the mud, breathing in shallow sips, and his right hand was still clenched around something pressed to his chest—an oilskin packet, wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord. Even delirious, even drowning, he had not let go of it. Whatever it was, it mattered more to him than staying above water.

"What's in the package?" she heard herself ask, and immediately wished she hadn't, because asking was the first crack, and cracks in hulls don't heal, they only get patched, and patches fail at the worst possible moment.

"My life's work," he said. Then, seeing her expression: "Maps. Survey maps. Nothing worth stealing, I promise you. Paper and ink and thirty years of walking."

"Surveyors work for the government, and the government works for the syndicate. Which makes you either very brave or very stupid."

"Both, usually." His eyes were drifting again, going glassy. "Captain. Three hundred and fifty. And I'll work the deck if I'm able. I'm stronger than I look."

"You look dead," she said. "Dead men can't work decks."

"Four hundred."

The number went through her like a spear. Four hundred kina. Her debt to Old Man Sione at the Angoram fuel dock sat at three hundred and eighty, and it grew ten kina a week in interest, and Sione was patient the way crocodiles are patient, and the week after next he would stop being patient, because monsoon was coming and everyone's debts came due before the rains. Four hundred cleared it. Four hundred put diesel in her tanks and rice in her hold and maybe, if the season was kind, a little aside against the day the *Kestrel*'s hull finally gave up its long argument with the river.

Four hundred bought her another year of invisibility.

And it would cost her that invisibility to earn it, because a man who'd seen her face and knew her boat and knew her heading was a thread hanging loose, and loose threads got pulled by people like Mr.—no. Don't think about that name. Not tonight.

"Last offer," the man said. His voice was failing now, fraying like rope. "Five hundred. Everything I have. Passage past Ambunti, and you never see me again after."

Silence, except the river talking to itself in the reeds, and somewhere far off across the black water a night bird calling its one question over and over.

Lena closed her eyes. She thought: *this is how it starts. This is exactly how it starts. One stranger, one exception, one small bending of the rules, and six years of careful work comes apart like wet paper.* She thought about the magistrate's voice in the courtroom, smooth as oiled wood, reading out the verdict that had been written before she ever walked in. She thought about how the ones who helped you were always the ones who got hurt for it.

She thought about the dockhand with the meal, whose face she could no longer recall, and hoped wherever she was, someone had once stopped for her too.

"Five hundred," Lena said. "Half now."

The man fumbled at his shirt with fingers that barely worked. She came down the ladder, tied the torch to the rail, and waded out into the shallows to meet him, the mud sucking at her boots, the warm black water climbing her thighs. Up close he smelled of river rot and sickness and, beneath it, faintly, of pipe tobacco and machine oil—a working man's smell, an honest one, though she'd learned long ago that honest smells could lie.

His money belt was soaked through. He peeled notes out of it with agonizing slowness, and she took them and counted them twice by torchlight—two hundred and fifty—and tucked them into her jacket without comment. The rest he could owe her at Ambunti, if he lived to get there, which she privately doubted.

"Can you walk?" she asked.

"I can try."

He couldn't, really. She got her shoulder under his arm and hauled, and together they made the five meters to the ladder in something that wasn't quite walking and wasn't quite swimming, and then it was all she could do to push him up the rungs ahead of her. He collapsed on her deck like landed fish, chest heaving, and she stood over him dripping river water, wondering what precisely she had just done, and knowing the answer perfectly well.

She had just stopped being invisible.

* * *

Below, in the cabin that smelled of diesel and mildew and the dried fish hanging from the roof beams, she got him out of his ruined clothes and into a blanket and laid him on the bench beside the chart table. He was thinner than he'd seemed in the torchlight—all wire and knuckle, a body that had been burning itself for fuel for weeks. There were cuts on his shins and forearms, leech bites, a bruise along his ribs the color of storm clouds. She cleaned what she could reach with boiled water and the last of her antiseptic, and he bore it without a sound except once, when she probed a gash above his knee, when he made a small noise like a man remembering something painful from long ago.

"Fever's bad," she said. "I've got quinine tablets. Two now, two in the morning. If it's not malaria it'll still knock it down some."

"Thank you." His eyes were closed. "Elias Voss."

"I didn't ask."

"I know you didn't. That's why I'm telling you." He opened his eyes, and they were clearer than they had any right to be, fixed on her with a sober attention. "A man who won't ask names deserves to be given one anyway. Elias Voss. Prospector. Surveyor, sometimes. Thirty years in this basin."

"Lena." She heard herself say it before she could stop, and cursed inwardly. Just the one name. Just the boat name, the safe one, the alias worn smooth as a river stone. "Lena Amberdale."

"Amberdale." He tried the word out, tasting it. "Not a Sepik name."

"It's mine. That's all it needs to be."

He almost smiled. "Fair enough, Captain Amberdale."

She shook two quinine tablets into his palm and held the cup while he drank. Outside, the rain began—not the monsoon yet, just the ordinary evening rain, soft and steady on the canvas roof, filling the silence the way rivers fill valleys. The lamp swung gently with the current pushing at the anchored hull, and shadows moved across his face.

"Past Ambunti," he murmured. His words were slowing, sinking. "There's a place I need to go. Above the gorge. Karawari Bend."

"That's highlands edge. That's not a trip, that's an expedition. Monsoon's three weeks out, maybe less. Nobody runs the upper gorge this late."

"Then I picked a poor time to drown." His breath caught. His hand crept to his chest again, to the oilskin packet, which she had set on the chart table within his sight. He watched it the way a starving man watches food. "It has to be delivered. Before the rains. It has to be—"

"Delivered? To whom?"

But he was going under, the fever pulling him down like an anchor, and whatever he said next dissolved into muttering. She caught fragments—*the seam, the coordinates, my girl, my Mara, she won't know, she has to know*—and then just sounds, and then nothing but rough breathing.

Lena sat in the lamplight and looked at the oilskin packet on her chart table, in her cabin, on her boat, and felt the weight of it the way she'd feel a change in the current: subtle, and total, and impossible to ignore.

Paper and ink and thirty years of walking, he'd said. Maybe. But men didn't drown themselves crossing the basin clutching worthless paper. Men didn't crawl toward a stranger's torchlight with their last strength for worthless paper. Whatever slept inside that oilskin, somebody wanted it—she was sure of that the way she was sure of snags and sandbars—and the somebody would follow it here eventually, following him, the way a wound follows blood.

She should throw it in the river. She should throw it and him both, and be downstream by dawn, and let the basin keep its secrets.

Instead she banked the lamp low, checked his breathing, and climbed up to the wheelhouse to sit her watch, listening to the rain thicken, and telling herself that in the morning she'd drop him at the mission station and wash her hands of the whole affair.

In the morning, Elias Voss's fever burned higher, and the oilskin packet lay where he could see it, and his cracked lips moved all night with a single word that Lena, standing her sleepless watch, finally leaned close enough to hear.

It was a name. *Mara.*

And when dawn came gray over the reeds, he gripped her wrist with surprising strength and said, in a voice scraped down to the boards: "Swear it, Captain. Swear you'll take it to my daughter—or tell me now, so I can find another boat, another river, another fool."

Lena looked down at the dying man's hand on her arm, and at the packet, and at the wide brown river already brightening toward morning, and understood that whatever she said next would decide everything.
