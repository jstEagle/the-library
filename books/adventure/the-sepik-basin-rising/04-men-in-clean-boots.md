## 4. Men in Clean Boots

They came at mid-morning, when the river lay flat as poured tin and the dock smelled of tar and drying fish. Lena saw them before their boat even touched the pilings—three men standing rigid in a hired launch, and she knew them for what they were by the way they stood. River men leaned. River men shifted their weight, braced against a current that never stopped talking. These men stood like furniture.

And their boots were clean.

That was the thing about the Sepik Basin: it got into everything. Into the grain of the wood, into the seams of your clothes, into the creases of your hands until you stopped trying to scrub it out. Nobody came upriver with white boots and stayed white for an hour. So either these men had just stepped off the government steamer from the coast, or they had spent the whole journey standing on planks laid over the deck like the mud itself might contaminate them.

Lena set down the coil of rope she was mending and watched the launch come in. She had learned long ago that trouble announced itself in small ways—a silence among the gulls, a dockhand suddenly finding urgent business elsewhere, the harbor boss's door staying shut when it was usually open to anyone with coin. This morning it was the boots, and the way the taller of the three men scanned the moored boats with the unhurried thoroughness of a man counting money he already owned.

The Kestrel sat third from the end, low in the water, her patched hull looking exactly as disreputable as Lena wanted it to. Elias Voss lay below decks under a wool blanket, burning through his fever, breathing in shallow sips. The oilskin packet rode inside her shirt, against her sternum, where she'd told herself she was only keeping it until morning.

She had been telling herself that for two days now.

* * *

The tall man stepped onto the dock without waiting for the launch to be tied off, which told Lena he'd never once worried about falling in. He wore a linen suit the color of bone, pressed despite the climate, and a pith helmet that some colonial tailor back on the coast had probably assured him was appropriate. Behind him came two others: a heavyset man with a ledger under one arm and the eyes of someone who counted things for a living, and a younger one who moved wrong—too smooth, too quiet, the kind of stillness that cost money to train.

"Good morning." The tall man's voice carried across the water without effort, warm as bathwater. "I'm looking for the owner of this establishment."

Old Tamu emerged from his shed wiping his hands on a rag, performing the shuffling deference he performed for every official who came upriver, though Lena had seen him shortchange three of them in a single afternoon. "Owner? I only manage the berths, sir. Whatever cargo business you want—"

"Cargo business. Yes." The man smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, the kind that reached the eyes because it had been built to. "My name is Calloway. Mr. Ambrose Calloway. I represent the Meridian Consolidated Mining Company, and we're seeking a passenger—one Elias Voss, a prospector. He was reported on this stretch of river several days ago. Perhaps you've seen him?"

Lena drifted closer along the dock, carrying her rope coil like she had somewhere to be, close enough to hear and unremarkable enough not to be heard hearing. A woman with tar on her hands and a boat to mend was furniture too.

Tamu spread his palms. "Prospectors come through all the time, sir. Half of them dying, half of them drunk, most of both."

"This one would have been feverish. Possibly injured. He may have paid for passage upstream." Calloway produced a folded document from his breast pocket and held it out—not quite offering it, just displaying it, the way a man displays a pistol he doesn't intend to use yet. "We have authority here. Magistrate's warrant."

Lena's hands went still on the rope.

She shouldn't look. Every instinct honed across six years of being nobody said don't look, keep moving, be the woman with the rope. But warrants had signatures, and signatures had names, and there was a name she had spent six years listening for the way sailors listen for weather.

She looked.

The paper was standard issue, government stock, the kind stamped in Ambunti and carried upriver by men who liked the weight of it. The seal was right. The language was the usual boilerplate about persons of interest and cooperation expected of all subjects of the Crown. And at the bottom, in ink gone slightly brown with age or humidity, a signature she would have known blindfolded, upside down, underwater:

**R. Hessler, District Magistrate.**

The rope bit into her palm. She kept walking. One foot, then the other, past the shed, past the water barrels, her face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman thinking about fish.

Hessler.

Six years, and the name could still stop her heart between beats. She had thought she'd buried it deep enough—the courtroom in Ambunti with its ceiling fans turning slow as wounded birds, the testimony read aloud in a voice like dry paper, *the accused did knowingly remove company property valued at*, the magistrate's gavel coming down with the soft finality of a coffin lid. She had been twenty-four years old and she had never stolen so much as a biscuit in her life, and it hadn't mattered, because Hessler had needed a thief and she had been convenient. A clerk who asked questions about ledgers. A clerk whose family had no influence. A clerk who could be made to disappear into a conviction and then, when the sentence ran out, into the river country, where she became Lena Amberdale and let the girl she'd been drown quietly somewhere behind her.

*You're imagining things,* she told herself. *Half the warrants in the basin carry his seal. He signs everything.*

But her pulse didn't believe her, and her pulse was usually right.

* * *

"—and if he passed through," Calloway was saying as she came within easy earshot again, "we'd naturally compensate any party who assisted him. Generously." He let the word sit in the air like bait in clear water. "The company rewards helpfulness, Mr.—"

"Tamu."

"Mr. Tamu. And it remembers the opposite, of course." He said it lightly, almost kindly, which made it worse. "Now. I'm told there's a riverboat captain here. A woman. Amberdale. Runs a boat called the Kestrel."

The world narrowed to a point.

Lena turned around slowly, deliberately, letting the rope coil hang from her hand, and gave Calloway the look she gave drunk passengers and dishonest shippers: polite, tired, faintly bored. "You're asking after me, then."

Calloway's gaze moved over her the way the heavyset man's fingers would move over a ledger—cataloguing, assessing, filing. She watched him take in the tar-stained shirt, the sun-cracked hands, the salt-stiff braid, and arrive at a conclusion she could see forming behind his eyes: *harmless.* Good. Let him file her under harmless. People filed her under harmless right up until the moment she wasn't.

"Captain Amberdale." He inclined his head. "Ambrose Calloway, Meridian Consolidated. We understand you took a passenger aboard recently. A sick man, pulled from the reeds near the lower channel."

"I pull a lot of men from the reeds." She set the rope down on a barrel, unhurried. "River gives them back sometimes. Usually I wish it wouldn't."

"And this one? Tall fellow, gray beard, German accent, answers to Voss?"

"I had a passenger two days back. Paid his fare at the mouth of the Bakari and went on his way." She shrugged. "Feverish types don't tend to linger where I put ashore. Too many mosquitoes, not enough whisky."

Something flickered in the younger man's stillness—not a movement, but a readiness, like a trap spring tightening half a turn. Calloway noticed nothing, or pretended not to. "Went on his way," he repeated pleasantly. "Upstream, I assume."

"Everything goes upstream eventually. Or downstream. Depends on the current and how much sense the man has."

Calloway laughed—a real-sounding laugh, warm, delighted, the laugh of a man who found the world endlessly amusing and himself its chief entertainment. "Captain, you're a pleasure. Truly. Most people out here either grovel or lie badly. You do neither." He stepped closer, and the warmth in his voice thinned to something with edges underneath. "So let me ask plainly, since plainness seems to be your dialect. Did Elias Voss board your vessel?"

"No."

"Is he aboard it now?"

"No."

"Would you object if my associates had a look? Standard procedure. The warrant covers inspection of vessels operating in district waters."

There it was. Lena felt the old fear rise—the cold one, the one from the courtroom, the one that lived in the base of her spine and whispered *run, run now, cut the lines and run*—and she did something she hadn't done in six years.

She let it burn instead.

It started low, in the same place the fear lived, but it came up different. Hotter. Older than fear, maybe; older than the trial, older than the ledgers, back to some first version of herself who had once believed that the truth mattered and that saying it out loud was enough. That girl had died in a courtroom. But apparently something of her had been down there all along, waiting in the dark like a banked fire, and all it took was a warrant with Hessler's signature and a clean-booted man asking permission to search her home.

Because the Kestrel was her home. That was the thing Calloway couldn't know. Six years of sleeping in a bunk the width of a coffin, of knowing every plank and every leak and every place the river spoke through the hull at night—and this man in his bone-colored suit wanted to walk through it with his boots on.

"You can look," she said. "After you show me the warrant. Properly. In my hand, not waved around like a menu." She held out her palm. "Rules of the dock. Tamu's rules, and he's touchy about them."

Tamu, wisely, developed a sudden fascination with his rag.

Calloway handed her the paper with a small flourish, as if conceding a move in a game he was enjoying. Lena took it and read it through properly, every line, making him wait. Her hands were steady. She was almost proud of that. The signature sat at the bottom like a spider.

R. Hessler. The same R. Hessler. Not a son, not a namesake—she checked the commission date, the district appointment, and there it was, six years stale and still in power, still signing papers that turned people into cargo.

She handed it back. "Vessel's aft. Mind the bilge boards, they're loose, and if you fall in the hold I'm charging you salvage rates."

The heavyset man went aboard with his ledger clutched to his chest like a hymnal. The young one followed, silent as spilled oil. Calloway remained on the dock beside her, hands clasped behind his back, watching the water with the contentment of a man on holiday.

"You lied well," he said conversationally.

"I answered your questions."

"Mm. About the passenger who 'paid his fare and went on his way.'" He turned his head, and for just a moment the amusement drained out of his face entirely, and what was underneath was patient and cold and very, very competent. "Captain, I've been doing this work for eleven years. I've heard every lie the basin speaks, and yours had the right shape but the wrong temperature. People who tell the truth get bored. People who lie get precise. You were precise."

Below decks, faintly, Elias Voss coughed.

It was a small sound. A wet, rattling sound, muffled by planking and blanket. On a quiet dock in the mid-morning hush, it might as well have been a gunshot.

Nobody moved. The heavyset man paused halfway down the companionway ladder. The young one's head tilted, birdlike. Calloway smiled again, and this time the smile didn't bother reaching his eyes at all.

"Your boat creaks," Lena said.

"They all do."

"Not like that."

For a long moment the only sounds were the water slapping the pilings and the far-off complaint of a whistling duck. Then Calloway sighed, genuinely regretful, a schoolmaster disappointed in a promising student. "Here's what happens now, Captain. My associates finish their inspection. If they find nothing—which is possible; feverish men are quiet men—they'll note your vessel's registration and move on, and you'll go on being Lena Amberdale, ferrywoman, honest as river gravel." He straightened his cuffs. "And if they find something, the warrant covers that too. Detention of persons traveling under false pretenses. Impoundment of vessels aiding fugitives. All very legal. All signed by a magistrate whose patience, I should warn you, is shorter than mine."

He said the last part lightly. But Lena caught it—the way he'd said *magistrate* with a small, proprietary ease, the way a man says *my colleague* or *my partner*. Not an agent obeying a warrant. An agent who helped write it.

"How long has Meridian been writing the law out here?" she asked.

Calloway's eyebrows rose, pleased. "There it is again. Precision." He glanced toward the Kestrel, where the sound of careful footsteps crossed her deck overhead. "The company and the district administration enjoy a cooperative relationship. You'd be amazed how much smoother the river runs when everyone pulls in the same direction. Gold does that. Unifies people." He looked back at her, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Which brings me to my actual question, Captain, the one beneath the question. A woman alone, running cargo on a leaking hull, dodging sandbars and officials alike—that's a hard life built on staying invisible. Invisible women don't argue with men holding warrants. They agree quickly and forget faster. So I find myself wondering what makes you different."

Lena looked at him. Six years of practice kept her face still, but inside, the fire was climbing now, steady and bright, burning off the cold that had lived in her since a courtroom ceiling fan turned above her head.

"Maybe I just don't like your boots," she said.

Above them, the heavyset man emerged from the hold, shaking his head in a small arc: nothing. The young one followed, and Lena allowed herself one breath—the breath she'd been holding since the cough—before she remembered that breathing was a skill for later.

"Pity," Calloway said. "Truly. Well." He tipped his pith helmet a precise inch. "We'll continue upstream ourselves, of course. If Voss went on his way, he went somewhere, and the basin only has so many somewheres. Karawari Bend. The highland stations. We'll find him, or we'll find whoever's keeping him, and either way the company will remember who was helpful." His gaze rested on her a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Do take care of that hull, Captain. I hear she's taking water. Boats that leak have a way of going down with everything aboard."

He walked back to the launch without hurrying. The heavyset man untied the line himself rather than ask a dockhand. The engine turned over, and the three clean boots carried themselves away upriver, leaving a wake that rocked the moored boats and a smell of petrol and money hanging over the water.

* * *

Lena waited until the launch rounded the far bend. Then she went down into the hold, knelt beside the bunk, and pulled back the blanket.

Elias Voss looked up at her with eyes sunk in a face the color of wet ash. The fever had him by the throat, but something behind the delirium had tracked every word on that dock; she could see it in the way his cracked lips shaped themselves around an apology.

"They'll... come back," he rasped.

"Yes."

"For the map."

"I know."

His hand crawled out from under the blanket and closed around her wrist—weak as a child's grip, desperate as a drowning man's. "Then you can't stay. Captain. Listen to me. They'll watch the river now. Every landing, every post. You have to go tonight. Before—" A cough tore through him, bending him double, and when it passed he sagged back, spent, still holding on. "Before they figure out the boat they searched isn't the boat they should have searched."

Lena sat back on her heels. Through the open hatch above, she could see the sky building its afternoon clouds, fat and dark over the western hills. The monsoon was early this year; the old-timers had been muttering about it for a week. A leaking hull, a dying passenger, a warrant with Hessler's name on it, and a season of rain about to fall on all of it.

Every sensible part of her said *give him to the mission station, give the map to the river, save yourself.*

But the fire was still burning, and it had shown her something on that dock. For six years she had run from Hessler's name, and running had kept her alive and kept her small and kept her nothing. Today, hearing it, she had felt something besides fear for the first time since the courtroom.

She wanted him to lose.

"Tonight, then," she said to Elias Voss, and began pulling out the patching canvas.
