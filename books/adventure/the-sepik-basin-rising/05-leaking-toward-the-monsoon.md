## 5. Leaking Toward the Monsoon

The rain came at dusk, a thin gray whisper against the Kestrel's canvas, and Lena Amberdale stood at the wheel in the dark and counted her reasons for leaving like a miser counting coins.

One: Elias Voss was dead, and dead men could not be interrogated, but their promises could. She had sworn an oath over his body with the river muttering its amen below, and she had learned long ago that broken oaths had a way of surfacing—like drowned things, bloated and undeniable—at the worst possible moments.

Two: Mr. Calloway's men had walked her dock that morning in their clean boots, asking questions with soft voices, and men like that did not ask twice.

Three—and this was the one she kept returning to, turning it over like a stone in her palm—the warrant they'd carried bore Magistrate Hessler's signature. Eight years she had run from that name, and now it had found her anyway, riding upriver on official paper, patient as silt.

She could stay. She could hand over the map, or burn it, or bury it in a tin beneath the floorboards and go back to hauling copra and crocodile skins until the monsoon swallowed the season whole. Any of those choices would keep her small, and smallness had kept her alive this long.

But Elias Voss had died holding her wrist with a grip like river clay, and he had said *my daughter*, and he had said *promise me*, and Lena—who had spent eight years being no one, who had practiced invisibility until it fit her like a second skin—had heard herself say yes.

*You fool,* she told herself, watching the rain bead on the brass rail. *You sentimental, drowning fool.*

Below deck, the oilskin packet sat wrapped in her spare shirt at the bottom of the rice chest, beneath a false layer of weevils she'd cultivated deliberately. Nobody rifled through weevils. The map rode under six inches of crawling protein, which was more security than most governments provided their treaties.

She checked it a third time anyway. Then she checked the bilge.

That was when the evening turned from bad to arithmetic.

***

The Kestrel was forty years old if she was a day, built of some hardwood whose name the yard owner had never bothered to learn, and she leaked the way old women talk—a constant low commentary, manageable if you listened, catastrophic if you ignored. Lena knew her ship's voice the way she knew her own pulse. Two pumps of the bilge every hour in calm water. Four in the current. Six if the wind came around the point at Ambunti.

Tonight, standing in the engine room with the lamp held low, she watched water slide across the plates toward the drain channel and understood that the voice had changed.

It wasn't talking anymore. It was confessing.

The patch she'd laid over the cracked strake three days ago—tarred canvas and a scrap of sheet copper, pressed home while hanging off the hull by one arm in brown water full of things she preferred not to enumerate—was lifting at the upstream edge. Not peeling. *Lifting*, the way a scab lifts, with a dark seam underneath that breathed a thin ribbon of river into the hold every time the boat rolled.

Lena crouched and put her palm flat against the hull planking. Cold. The cold of deep water, of pressure, of the river pressing its whole gray weight against forty feet of aging timber and finding, at last, a place where the argument was going its way.

"Two days," she said aloud, because saying it made it a number instead of a fear. "Maybe three."

Three days to Karawari Bend if the weather held, which it would not, because the clouds stacking up over the western ranges all afternoon had been building themselves into something with a name and a temper. Three days on a hull that wanted two. And behind her, downstream, Calloway's launch with its clean-booted passengers, and behind that, somewhere in the administrative geometry of the territory, Hessler himself, signing papers that turned lies into law.

She climbed back into the rain and cast off the lines herself, not waiting for the harbor boy who usually took them, because harbor boys remembered faces and faces were currency she couldn't afford to spend tonight.

The Kestrel slid out into the black water at half past eleven, running without lights, her engine muffled to a murmur, and the town sank away behind her in a smear of lamplight and drum-sound, and Lena Amberdale stood alone at the wheel of everything she owned and felt the particular loneliness of a woman who has just made a choice she cannot unmake.

The river took her the way it took everything: without comment.

***

She ran the first stretch by memory and moon-glimmer, threading the channel markers she'd set herself over four seasons of runs—half-submerged stumps marked with bleached rags, a leaning palm that meant *shoal water east*, the drowned tree at Kilometer Nine that everyone else gave a wide berth but which hid, on its far side, six meters of honest depth. The Kestrel handled heavy, though. That was the thing she noticed first, before the numbers, before the fear. Heavy in the turns. Slow to answer her helm, as if the river had gotten into her blood and thickened it.

At two in the morning she lit the lamp in the engine room again and did not like what the light showed her.

Water over the sole plates. An inch of it, maybe more, sliding with a lazy confidence that said *I live here now*. The bilge pump coughed, spat a brown gout through the discharge, and settled into its rhythm, and Lena stood in the rising wash in her bare feet and did the mathematics she'd been avoiding since dusk.

Inflow exceeded outflow. Not by much—not yet—but the patch wasn't holding, and there was a second seam weeping above the first that she hadn't even known about, and the pump was older than the boat and had opinions about overtime.

She could turn back. The thought arrived uninvited, wearing reasonable clothes. Turn back, beach her at the mudflats below the town, wait out the monsoon, re-plank the hull properly with money she didn't have, in a port where Calloway's men were walking the docks with a photograph of a dead man and questions about his last passage.

Or she could go on, into rising water, on a failing hull, carrying a dead man's promise toward a daughter who might not even want it.

Lena braced both hands on the engine housing and let the boat roll beneath her, and for a long moment she simply stood in the dark and the damp and admitted the truth she'd been circling for eight years: there was no going back. There never had been. Going back was a country she'd lost her passport to the day Hessler read out her name in a courtroom that smelled of varnish and lies, and every mile since had been forward motion dressed up as drifting.

"You'll not sink on my watch," she told the Kestrel, and the words came out steadier than she felt, which was the whole trick of command, and possibly the whole trick of living. "Not with him aboard." She meant the map. She meant the promise. She meant, though she didn't say it, the version of herself that had said *yes* to a dying stranger, because that woman was aboard too, and Lena was beginning to suspect she'd be needing her.

She stripped to her undershirt, found the spare tar pot and the last of the canvas scraps, and went down the ladder into the bilge to fight for her ship's life by lamplight, hip-deep in cold water, while the rain outside thickened from whisper to conversation.

***

Dawn found her still alive, still afloat, and filthy past redemption—tar to the elbows, hair plastered flat, hands nicked and shaking. The patch had held. Barely. The new seam she'd stuffed with oakum teased from an old mooring line and sealed with candle wax and stubbornness, and the pump was winning its war again, narrowly, the way all her wars were won these days.

But the sky to the west had gone the color of a bruise going bad, and the air had that pressed, breathless quality she'd learned to dread in her first season on the river, when a squall had nearly put her ashore in the mangroves minus the boat. The monsoon wasn't coming. It was *here*, early, ahead of every forecast and every almanac, and the river already wore its opinion of that fact in a skin of fast-moving debris—whole trees sailing downstream, their roots waving like drowned men's hair.

And then she saw the smoke.

Thin, deliberate, answering-smoke, rising off the point ahead where the channel bent north. A signal fire. And beneath it, drawn up on the shingle where any passing boat would see them, three canoes flying nothing at all—which on this river meant either fishermen, or men who wanted very much to look like fishermen.

Lena cut speed and studied them through the rain-haze. Too many paddles stacked neatly. No nets drying. One man standing at the waterline with the particular stillness of someone whose job was to be seen.

Scouts. Calloway's, or Hessler's, or some third party's—it hardly mattered. What mattered was that they were posted at the bend, which meant the syndicate's reach ran further upriver than she'd guessed, which meant her night of quiet departure had bought her hours, not days.

She looked at the smoke. She looked at the western sky. She looked, finally, down through the deck boards at the water sliding over her sole plates, patient as debt.

There was a trading post twelve kilometers on—Wagu Landing, a scatter of rusted roofs and a Chinese storehouse where a man named Fong sold diesel, rope, and discretion at fair prices. If she could reach Wagu, she could patch properly, take on supplies, and find out what those canoes were doing on *her* river. If she couldn't reach Wagu—

Well. The river had always been honest about alternatives.

Lena opened the throttle as far as she dared, and the Kestrel groaned and leaned into the current, and the rain came down harder, and the smoke signal fell astern with its watchers surely noting her heading, and none of it—none of it—changed the arithmetic. She was committed. Committed to the channel, to the promise, to the mad upstream run toward a highlands settlement she'd never seen, carrying a dead man's coordinates and her own buried name.

Somewhere behind her, thunder rolled along the ranges like a door being closed.

By mid-afternoon the inflow was winning again. The pump ran continuously now, and the Kestrel sat a hand's width lower in the water than she should have, and Lena steered with her jaw set and her eyes flicking between the channel and the gauge and the sky, running the boat the way you run from anything—with total attention and no looking back.

She saw Wagu Landing at four o'clock, a gray smudge through the rain, and felt something in her chest unknot half a turn—until the Kestrel shuddered, shipped a wave broadside across the bow, and the pump, faithful old veteran, chose that exact moment to die with a clank she felt through the soles of her feet.

The water didn't rush in. It *arrived*, with the calm entitlement of something that had been invited all along.

Lena hauled the wheel hard over, ran the engine to its ragged limit, and drove the dying Kestrel at the landing shingle with the river chasing her transom like a debt collector. The hull struck gravel with a crack she'd hear in her dreams, slewed, ground, and stopped—settling onto the bank with a sigh, listing gently to starboard, taking water in the shallows but no longer going anywhere but down.

She stood on the tilting deck in the hammering rain, soaked through, bleeding from a split lip she didn't remember earning, and laughed—one short, ugly bark of sound that the storm ate instantly.

Then she saw the figure standing at the top of the shingle under a leaking veranda roof. A man, thin as a piling, holding a bottle by the neck and watching her grounded boat with eyes that had once, clearly, been very good at reading things.

"Well," the man called over the rain, in the tone of someone commenting on weather. "That's the worst piece of seamanship I've seen all year."

Lena wiped blood from her mouth and glared at him through the deluge.

"She's not sunk," she said.

"No," he agreed, taking a pull from the bottle, unimpressed. "But give her an hour."
