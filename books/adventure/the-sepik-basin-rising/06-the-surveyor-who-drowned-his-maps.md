## 6. The Surveyor Who Drowned His Maps

Tomas Reyes had a system for drinking at Wewak Landing's only trading post, and the system was this: start with whatever was cheapest, drink it slow enough to seem like a man with nothing to prove, and stop before the tremors in his hands got bad enough that people started asking questions. He'd been running the system for eleven months, ever since the day he'd walked out of the Lands Survey Office in Port Moresby with his resignation letter unsigned, his field kit under one arm, and a reputation for being difficult that followed him up the coast like a smell.

The trading post sat on stilts above the mud like everything else at Wewak Landing, its floorboards bleached silver by sun and rot. In the wet season the river came up under it and you could feel the water breathing through the boards. In the dry season—such as it was—you could smell it instead: silt, diesel, fish gone off in the sun, and the sweet-rot sweetness of the pandanus groves downstream. Tomas had a table near the window because the light was good there, and the light mattered, because when he wasn't drinking he was drawing. Little maps on butcher paper, on the backs of cigarette packets, on the flyleaves of the Bible somebody had left behind that he'd long since stopped pretending to read. Coastlines. River bends. The shape of country he'd never survey again, drawn from memory with a precision that made his hands ache.

Tonight the paper was a torn-open flour sack, flattened against the table with an empty bottle as a weight, and he was drawing the Bakari gorge from memory—the way the river pinched between limestone walls and went from brown to green to something almost black, the way the current piled up against the eastern bank where the rock shelved underwater. He'd run that stretch twice, years ago, with a government crew and a government mandate, and he remembered every eddy line the way other men remembered their mothers' faces.

"You're dripping," said the trade store owner's wife, passing with a lamp.

"It's the humidity."

"It's the rain." She set the lamp down, looked at the flour sack, looked at him with the particular pity reserved for educated men who drank. "You should eat something, Mr. Reyes."

"I'll eat when I'm hungry."

"You said that Tuesday."

He gave her half a smile to make her go away, and she went, and he bent back over the map. That was the trouble with Wewak Landing. Nothing happened here. A barge came through every nine days. A patrol officer came through every six weeks and pretended not to know who Tomas Reyes was or why a man with a surveyor's registration number tattooed into his service record was pouring beer down his throat in a place with no surveyed anything within forty miles. Nothing happened here, which was exactly what he'd paid for with his career, and some nights the silence of it pressed on him like floodwater.

Some nights—not tonight—he thought about the letter. Not his resignation letter; the other one. The one from the syndicate's land agent, the smooth one, Calloway, offering three times his salary to redraw the boundary lines around the Karawari tributaries so that a few thousand hectares of village land would fall neatly inside a mining lease. *Just lines on paper, Reyes. Nobody lives on paper.* He'd refused. He'd refused loudly, in an office with thin walls, and two months later his field reports had been declared falsified, his registration suspended pending review pending appeal pending nothing, and the review board had contained two men whose signatures he recognized from the syndicate's prospectus. He hadn't even been framed well. He'd simply been made inconvenient, and inconvenience, in the territories, was a terminal condition.

So: Wewak Landing. Cheap beer, good light, and a flour sack full of the Bakari gorge.

* * *

The woman came in just after dark, and Tomas noticed her the way he noticed everything now—peripherally, without lifting his head, the habit of a man who'd spent six years in country where noticing things slowly could get you killed.

She was riverboat stock, that much was obvious. Canvas jacket gone soft with age, salt-crusted boots, hair tied back with no vanity about it, and the walk of someone whose knees understood decks. She ordered coffee, not beer, which marked her as either sick, broke, or disciplined—and then she paid for the whole pot plus a room, which marked her as none of those things. She took the table by the door, back to the wall, sightline to the stairs. Tomas knew that seating chart. He'd invented it himself, once, in another life.

He went back to his flour sack and let ten minutes pass. Then she crossed the room—not to him, to the store counter—and asked the owner's wife a question too low for him to catch, and the wife pointed, and the pointing finger swung around and settled on him like a searchlight.

Tomas sighed and capped his pen.

"Captain Amberdale," the wife announced, unnecessarily, and retreated behind her counter with the satisfaction of a woman who has successfully introduced two problems to each other.

The woman came over and stood across the table without asking to sit. Up close she was younger than her hands and older than her face, if that made any sense, and her eyes did a thing he recognized: they catalogued him. Boots, hands, the ink stains on his fingers, the empty bottles arranged in descending order like a bar graph of his evening. He watched her decide several things about him in about four seconds.

"Tomas Reyes," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Depends who's asking."

"Lena Amberdale. I run cargo on the Kestrel." She didn't sit. "You were district surveyor for the middle Sepik. Before."

"*Before*," Tomas repeated, tasting the word. "That's a generous way to put it. Most people say *before the scandal*, or *before he disgraced himself*, or my favorite, *before they caught him doing whatever it was they say he did*." He turned the flour sack around so she could see it. "Since we're being introduced. This is the Bakari gorge. Drawn from memory. Would you like to see the Purari delta too? I do a very good Purari delta."

Something flickered across her face—not amusement, but the neighbor of it. "I need a navigator."

"No, you don't. You need a navigator the way I need another drink. What you actually need—" he tapped the table, "—is someone who can tell you why you shouldn't take your boat where you're planning to take it. And since nobody comes to Wewak Landing asking for me unless they've already decided to go somewhere stupid, let's skip to that part."

She pulled out the chair opposite and sat, finally, and leaned forward, and lowered her voice to something meant only for him. "I have a map. Oilskin. It shows a gold seam up the Karawari headwaters. Unclaimed. And I need to be at Karawari Bend before the monsoon closes the gorges."

Tomas kept his face still, which took effort, because everything in him had just stood up and started shouting.

A gold seam. Upstream. In country that the syndicate had spent two years trying to lease through channels fair and foul, in country whose boundary lines a smooth-voiced man had once asked him to move with a pencil. And here was a riverboat captain with a stranger's eyes asking him—him—to navigate straight into the middle of it.

"Show me," he said.

She glanced around the room. Two laborers asleep at a corner table, the wife stacking tins, rain hammering the roof. She reached inside her jacket anyway, unhurried, and laid a folded oilskin packet on the table between them, and opened it just far enough.

Tomas looked down at the map and forgot how to breathe.

It wasn't the seam itself that hit him—it was the terrain around it. The contour work, the way the watercourses were rendered, the little hooked symbols for rapids and the careful notation of a side channel that appeared on no government chart he'd ever worked from. He knew this hand. He'd spent a week, four years ago, cross-referencing an old German-era survey against his own field notes, and he'd wondered then who had produced the original, because whoever it was had understood the Karawari country better than the government men who'd inherited it. Better than him, even. He'd said as much to his assistant, and his assistant had laughed and said, *whoever he was, he's dead or drunk somewhere.*

Elias Voss. The name surfaced from some forgotten ledger page, from a marginal note in a colonial file: *Voss, E., prospector, uncooperative re: lease negotiations.*

"Where did you get this?" His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"A dying man gave it to me. Why?"

"Because I've seen this terrain before." He touched the edge of the oilskin, lightly, the way you'd touch something sleeping. "Not this map. The country. Four years ago I ran a traverse up the eastern tributary and my instruments went mad—magnetic interference, ironstone in the ridges—and I logged it and reported it and six weeks later a land agent offered me a great deal of money to lose the report." He looked up at her. "That agent works for the same people who are going to come looking for this map. You understand that, don't you? Whatever deal you made with a dying prospector, you didn't inherit a fortune. You inherited a target."

"I know," Lena Amberdale said, and the steadiness of it was the most frightening thing about her. "That's why I'm hiring you and not asking directions."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low.

Or rather—she talked in short, hard sentences and he talked too much, the way he always did when the maps came out, and somewhere past midnight the conversation stopped being an interview and became something else. She told him about the Kestrel: thirty-one feet, clinker-built, a hull that had been patched twice since Ambunti and would need patching again. She told him her draft was too deep for the upper Karawari in dry season and suicidal in wet. She told him she had fuel for maybe five days and food for four and a debt at Wewak Landing she'd cleared that morning with money a dead man had left her, and she said all of it flatly, without self-pity, itemizing her own desperation like cargo manifest.

And Tomas, who had spent eleven months perfecting the art of saying nothing to anyone, heard himself telling her the rest.

"The syndicate wanted the Karawari tributaries inside their lease boundaries. There are villages up there—fishing villages, sago camps—that have used that water since before anyone drew a line on paper. To put them inside a mining lease you have to redraw the boundaries, and to redraw the boundaries you need a licensed surveyor to certify the lines, and in 1911 there was exactly one licensed surveyor within reach who knew that country." He turned his glass in a slow circle on the wood. "Calloway. That was his name. He came to my office with a contract and a smile and told me nobody lives on paper. And I told him that on paper was precisely where these people lived now, thanks to us, and that if I moved the line I'd be moving their houses and their gardens and their graves onto land a dredge was going to eat. He said I was being sentimental. I said worse. And eight weeks later my registration was suspended for falsifying field data I had never falsified, and the appeal process ate itself, and here I am."

"Here you are," she said quietly.

"Drawing the Bakari gorge on a flour sack."

"That's a good Bakari gorge."

He laughed—a real laugh, rusty, surprising them both. "It should be. I drowned myself learning it. Ran that gorge twice with a government crew, took soundings every fifty meters, and wrote it all up in a report that is currently sitting in a filing cabinet in Port Moresby being worth nothing to anybody." He paused. "Why me? You could buy a pilot cheaper. Half the boatmen on this river know the middle Sepik."

"Half the boatmen on this river know the middle Sepik," she agreed. "None of them know the Karawari headwaters. None of them can read a contour line or tell me where the magnetic iron will send a compass lying. And none of them—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide to say it, watched her choose honesty like a route through rapids, "—none of them have a reason to hate the same people I have reason to hate. I need a navigator who won't sell me at the first checkpoint. A man with nothing left to lose is expensive, Mr. Reyes, but he's honest."

"That's a terrible economic theory."

"It's held up so far."

He looked at her across the ruined flour sack and the dead lamp and the bottles, and he thought: *she's hiding too.* It was there in the way she'd taken the seat facing the door, in the name she wore like a borrowed coat, in the flinch—small, fast, professional—when the store owner's wife had called her *Captain Amberdale* a shade too loudly. Eleven months he'd assumed he was the only man in the territories living under a sentence he hadn't earned. Sitting across from him was a woman wearing hers more gracefully than he'd ever worn his, and he found that the knowledge didn't frighten him. It steadied him. Misery shared was still misery, but exile shared was a crew.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When this is done—whatever happens with the gold, whatever happens with the syndicate—I want my report back. The traverse data. The magnetic anomaly survey. It exists, it's accurate, and it proves what's in those hills. If we're going up there anyway, I want it out of that filing cabinet and into daylight."

Lena studied him for a long moment. "You don't want a share of the seam."

"I want the truth un-falsified. It's the only thing I ever surveyed properly." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own sincerity. "Besides, gold spends. Paper doesn't burn twice."

"Done." She stood, and shook his hand, and her grip was deck-hard and brief. "We leave tomorrow, an hour before dawn, ahead of the traffic. Can you be ready?"

"I've been ready for eleven months. I just didn't know it."

She gathered the oilskin packet with the care of a woman handling something alive, nodded once, and went up the stairs to her room. Tomas sat alone in the lamplight with the flour sack and the bottles and the sound of the rain coming harder off the river, and he smoothed the Bakari gorge flat and looked at it for a long time.

Then he began, in the margins, in small precise letters, to write down everything he could remember about the checkpoints between Wewak Landing and the Karawari confluence—who manned them, what they asked, which ones could be bluffed and which ones shot. Because if the syndicate had a land agent offering bribes four years ago over a report nobody was supposed to care about, then they had never stopped caring, and a dying prospector's map was not a treasure.

It was a fuse.

Outside, far downriver, a boat engine started in the dark—too late for cargo, too quiet for a patrol launch, idling low like something waiting. Tomas listened to it for a while, then blew out the lamp and sat in the black, memorizing the sound.
