## 7. First Rapids

Lena heard the Bakari before she saw it—a long, low thunder rolling up the river valley like the voice of something buried and impatient. By midmorning the sound had grown teeth. The water beneath the Kestrel's hull quickened, darkened, began to pull.

"You feel that?" she said, one hand on the wheel, the other resting on the throttle. "She's leaning. River wants us downstream faster than I want to go."

Tomas Reyes stood at the bow rail with a pair of battered binoculars he'd produced from his pack at dawn, along with a folded survey chart soft as cloth from years of handling. He'd barely spoken since they'd cast off from the trading post, and Lena had decided she didn't mind. Silence on a boat was a kind of courtesy. But now he turned, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the sodden apology of the man she'd found drinking rum at dawn, but attention. Sharp, hungry attention.

"The Bakari drops eleven meters over two kilometers," he said. "Three channels. The northern one's the deep water, but it dog-legs hard against a granite shelf. The southern is wide and looks easy, and that's what kills people—looks easy. It shallows out over a gravel fan. You'd grind the Kestrel's keel off like a carrot."

"You've run it?"

"Twice. Surveying. Once in a flood year." He hesitated, and she watched him decide to say the rest. "The second time I ran it, I was following a syndicate barge that didn't make it. Four men drowned. I wrote the report. The syndicate's version of the report said the channel was safe."

Lena looked at him a long moment. "And which version did you sign?"

"Neither." His jaw worked. "I refused to sign anything. That was the beginning of the end of my career, though I didn't know it yet. I thought refusing was enough. It isn't, Captain. Refusing is just the first lie you tell yourself."

The honesty of it caught her off guard. She turned back to the wheel. "Northern channel, then. The dog-leg. I'll need your eyes on the water, not on your conscience. Call the line for me—every rock, every boil, every place the current changes its mind. I can't read it from back here and steer at the same time."

"You're trusting me already?"

"I'm trusting your fear of drowning," she said. "It's the most reliable thing about any man on a river."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face, there and gone, like a fish rising. He climbed to the bow and braced himself against the rail, binoculars up.

Ahead, the river narrowed between walls of rainforest that leaned over the water as if to watch. The thunder grew. The Kestrel, patched and leaking and stubborn, picked up speed as the gradient took hold of her, and Lena felt the old animal joy rise in her chest despite everything—the same joy that had first put her on a river at nineteen, before the courts and the ledgers and the name she'd buried. The water didn't care who she'd been. The water only cared what she could do.

"Green water center-left," Tomas called, voice pitched to carry over the roar. "Hold that line. Dark seam ahead—shelf rock under the surface, maybe a meter down. Push right. Right, now!"

Lena pushed the wheel over. The Kestrel heeled, cargo shifting below with a groan of timber, and they slid past the seam close enough that she could see the brown shadow of it sliding beneath the hull like a sleeping animal.

"Good," Tomas said. "Now the dog-leg. It comes fast. The channel turns hard to starboard past that split rock—see the one that looks like a broken tooth? Everything left of it is a hole. The current will try to throw us into it. You have to fight it before it starts fighting you, or you'll never win."

"Anticipate," she said. "Not react."

"Anticipate," he agreed. "Most pilots learn it too late."

The broken tooth of rock rushed toward them, white water fanning off its upstream face. Lena set her line early, angled the bow into the turn while the current was still deciding what to do with her, and gave the Kestrel full throttle. The engine howled. The river howled louder. For three heart-stopping seconds the boat sat in the seam between two currents, trembling like a live thing, and then the starboard flow caught her stern and swung her through the dog-leg with a lurch that threw Tomas against the rail.

And then—open water. The roar fell away behind them. The river widened, calmed, went green and deep and quiet, as if nothing had happened.

Lena realized she was grinning. She wiped the expression off before Tomas turned around, but not fast enough.

"You've done this before," he said, coming aft.

"Once or twice."

"Where did you learn? That wasn't learned on cargo runs. That was learned somewhere the water fights back for a living."

"White salmon river, north coast, before the government put a road in." She said it easily, the lie worn smooth as river stone. There had been a river, and she had learned on it—but under another name, in another life, with a father at the wheel who'd taught her to anticipate. She let the lie lie. "Keep your eyes forward. Rapids don't come one at a time."

* * *

Tomas had not lied to her, and it surprised him how much that mattered—how much it mattered that she hadn't flinched when he'd told her about the report he'd refused to sign. Most people flinched. Most people's eyes did a small, quick arithmetic: *how much is this man worth to me, and what will his ruin cost me?* Lena Amberdale's eyes had done no arithmetic at all. They had simply weighed him, the way she seemed to weigh everything—cargo, current, weather, men—and moved on.

He stood at the bow through the long afternoon as the Bakari's middle section unspooled beneath them, calling the water, and she steered it, and between them the Kestrel moved through country no chart in his old agency's drawers described accurately. That was the thing about the Sepik that the surveys never captured, he thought. The maps were always a year out of date, and the river was always a day out of date with itself. It remade its own channels nightly, moved its sandbars like furniture, erased and redrew. A pilot here didn't navigate a river. She negotiated with one, constantly, a diplomacy conducted at the speed of the current.

By late afternoon the character of the banks changed. The rainforest pulled back from the water, and the trees gave way to a broad, flat gravel beach on the northern shore where the government, in one of its periodic fits of administration, had built a checkpoint: a corrugated-roof shelter, a flagpole with a flag gone grey, and a timber barrier arm stretched across a landing where the river traffic was expected to stop and present papers.

Two men stood under the shelter. A third sat on a crate, and even at two hundred meters Tomas could see the way he sat—elbows on knees, rifle across his thighs, the posture of a man who was not bored but waiting.

"Checkpoint," he said quietly. "That's new. There was nothing there in the old surveys, and nothing when I came through last year."

"Government?"

"Government checkpoints don't sit on gravel beaches with their rifles across their knees." He lowered the binoculars. "Look at the boats."

Moored at the landing was a long, low skiff with an outboard motor, the kind the syndicate used on its mine hauls—painted the flat, unremarkable grey that was supposed to say *utility* and instead, on this river, said *money*. And stenciled on the bow, small and neat, a registration number that Tomas recognized with a cold drop in his stomach. It was from the block of numbers the syndicate had registered through the land office, the same block that had appeared on the falsified survey documents he'd refused to sign.

"Scouts," he said. "Hessler's people are ahead of us. Which means they know Voss came upstream, and they're guessing we'd run the Bakari, because it's the only navigable line north."

Lena came forward, wiping her hands on a rag, and studied the landing through the binoculars for a long minute. He watched her face do what it had done at the trading post: nothing. A stillness came over her like a lid closing, and behind the stillness something worked, fast and cold.

"Three men," she said. "The one on the crate is the shooter. The two standing are talkers, and the tall one is in charge—he keeps touching his collar when he looks at the water, which means he doesn't like it, which means he's a land man doing a river job. He'll be nervous. Nervous men either overreach or fold. We want him to fold."

Tomas stared at her. "You read all that from two hundred meters?"

"I read people the way you read water." She handed the binoculars back. "It's the same skill. You watch where the pressure is, and you know where the surface is going to break."

"What's the plan? We could turn back—"

"Turn back into the rapids with a patched hull and a night coming on? No." She was already moving aft, pulling a canvas tarp off a stack of crates. "And we can't run past them—the barrier's positioned where the current sweeps you right onto their landing. They thought about it. So we stop, and we show them exactly what they expect to see, and nothing else."

"Which is what?"

"A leaking cargo boat with a tired captain and a hired hand, carrying dried fish and kerosene, in a hurry to beat the monsoon." She pulled the tarp over the crates where Voss's oilskin packet lay hidden beneath the floorboards of the wheelhouse—she'd moved it there two nights ago, he realized, without a word, without telling him—and then she paused and looked at him with an expression he couldn't name. "Tomas. When they ask you questions, don't answer them well. Answer them like a man who's slightly stupid and very tired. Can you do that?"

"I spent three years at the land office," he said. "I can do stupid and tired."

"Good. And if it goes wrong—" she touched the hatch cover over the floorboards, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, "—it won't."

* * *

The barrier arm came down across their approach with a squeal of rusted pulley, and the tall man walked to the edge of the landing with the particular unhurried gait of someone performing authority he wasn't sure he owned. Up close he was worse than he'd been at two hundred meters: sweat-stained collar, city boots crusted with river mud he clearly resented, a clipboard held against his chest like a shield.

"Papers," he said.

Lena climbed down from the wheelhouse with her logbook already open. "Morning's papers, afternoon's papers, or tomorrow's papers? Because I've got cargo spoiling and a monsoon coming, and I'd hate for you to have to write up why a licensed trader lost her load waiting on a beach."

The man blinked. Tomas, standing at the rail with his cap pulled low and his face arranged into vacant fatigue, felt the first small shift in the man's weight—the tiny redistribution of a man who had expected fear and received paperwork instead.

"Name of the vessel."

"Kestrel. Registered out of Angoram, and it's in the log, which I can read to you if your eyes are tired."

"Your name."

"Lena Amberdale." She said it without a flicker, and Tomas marveled at it—watched her hand over a false name the way she handed over the logbook, smoothly, with the small impatience of a busy woman. He understood then that the name was not a mask to her. It was a hull. And she had been living inside it so long she could steer it through anything.

The tall man flipped through the log with two fingers, not really reading. "Cargo?"

"Dried fish, kerosene, rope, and a consignment of roofing iron for the mission at Ambunti. You're welcome to count it, but you'll be counting it in the rain." As if on cue, the first fat drops of the afternoon buildup began to spot the gravel. "And who do I have the pleasure of refusing to count my fish for?"

"Provincial inspection," the man said. "We're looking for a passenger. Man named Voss. Prospector. Older, feverish, half-drowned when he was seen last. He may have boarded a vessel on this reach."

"Voss." Lena let the name sit in her mouth like something she was tasting for the first time. She frowned—a real frown, Tomas noted, or indistinguishable from one—and then shook her head. "I pulled a drowned man out of the reeds below the trading post three days back. Local fellow. Not Voss. He'd been in the water a long time, poor devil. If your prospector went into the river around there, you're likely looking for a body, not a passenger."

It was beautiful, Tomas thought. Not one word of it was a denial. Every word was a truth wearing another truth's clothes.

The tall man looked past her at Tomas. "You the hand?"

"Navigator," Tomas said, and then, catching himself, added with a shrug: "Well. Sort of. She found me at the trading post. I used to do survey work."

"Survey work?" Something sharpened behind the man's eyes, and Tomas felt the current shift—the pressure moving, the surface about to break. "For the government?"

"Used to." He let his shoulders sag, let three years of disgrace come up into his face, and found, to his surprise, that he didn't have to act much of it. "Before they decided my charts weren't flattering enough. Now I read water for kerosene money. You want to inspect the hold or not? Because that rain is getting serious, and I'd like to be through the middle reach before dark."

The man hesitated. Tomas watched him do the arithmetic Lena had predicted: a disgraced surveyor, a leaky trader, roofing iron for a mission, a storm coming on. Nothing here was worth a night on a gravel beach in the rain. And underneath the arithmetic, something else—the land man's dislike of the river, the rifleman on the crate already hunching against the wet, the whole small garrison of a checkpoint that had been told to watch for a feverish old man and had instead been handed a tired woman and a washed-up drunk.

"Move along, then," the tall man said, and lifted the barrier arm with the squeal of a man conceding.

Lena took the Kestrel out from the landing at a working tramp, unhurried, exactly as fast as an innocent boat would go. She held the line until the checkpoint was a grey smudge in the rain, and then she brought the boat around the first bend, and only then did Tomas see her shoulders come down from wherever they had been.

* * *

They anchored that night in a backwater behind an island, engine cold, lamp hooded, the rain drumming on the deck like a crowd applauding something they couldn't see. Lena cooked rice and dried fish on the small stove, and they ate in the wheelhouse out of the wet, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"You lied well," Tomas said finally.

"So did you." She poked at the fire. "The survey work part wasn't even a lie. That was the clever bit. You gave them a true thing to swallow the false thing with."

"My father used to say that. Bait the hook with what's real." He looked into his tin plate. "He was a fisherman. Before the syndicate's dredge killed the run on his river."

The rain filled the silence. Lena glanced at him, and he saw her deciding something, weighing him again, and this time the weighing went deeper.

"The man on the landing," she said. "He had a list. A paper list of names, and Voss's was on it, and he checked it against me twice. Which means the syndicate isn't guessing anymore. They're working a search pattern, reach by reach." She looked up. "They'll have the next checkpoint manned by tomorrow. Maybe two. And the one after that."

"So we go overland."

"With a leaking boat full of cargo? No. We go through. Faster than they can move their checkpoints, and we stop looking like what we were." She was quiet a moment, turning something over. "Tomas. When they asked about the survey work—when you said the charts weren't flattering enough. What did they want you to make flattering?"

He set down his plate. He had not told anyone the whole of it, not even the drinking companions at the trading post, who hadn't wanted the whole of anything. But the rain was loud, and the lamp was low, and she had lied for him today with a smoothness that deserved a truth in return.

"Land claims," he said. "Villages that had never signed anything, signing. River frontages that belonged to clans who'd fished them for six generations, transferred to the syndicate on paper. I was told to draw boundaries that didn't exist and certify them. And I did refuse, and I did lose my license, and I thought that was the end of it." He looked at her. "But today, when I saw that registration number on their skiff, I recognized it. It was from the same block of documents. The same land office file. Which means the man who signed those falsified claims is connected to the man who sent those scouts after Voss. It's all one thing, Captain. It's all the same hand."

Lena had gone very still. In the hooded lamplight her face was half shadow, and in the shadow something moved—something old and hot that she kept on a very short leash.

"Whose signature was on the claims?" she asked, and her voice had changed. It had gone quiet the way a river goes quiet just before it drops.

"The magistrate's office countersigned every one," Tomas said. "Magistrate Hessler. Why? You know the name?"

Lena looked out into the rain, at the black water, at the country ahead of them where the river climbed toward the highlands and the man who had made her was waiting with a list of names.

"You could say," she said, "that I've been reading him for years."
