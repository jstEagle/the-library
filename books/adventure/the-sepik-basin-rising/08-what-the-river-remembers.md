## 8. What the River Remembers

The village announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and sago and the green rot of the flooded forest—and Tomas Reyes felt something in his chest loosen for the first time since the Bakari rapids.

"Smoke means people," he said, pointing with the pole. "People mean dry ground, dry ground means we sleep horizontal tonight."

Lena stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with her arm wrapped in a fresh bandage, her eyes doing that thing he'd learned to read: sweeping the bank twice before she let herself believe it was safe. "People also mean questions."

"People mean information," Tomas said. "And we're running blind. I've been navigating off a dead man's map through country I half-remember from a survey I did nine years ago. Rivers move, Lena. Channels shift every wet season. What Voss drew from memory might as well be poetry."

She didn't answer right away. The Kestrel slid past a drowned pandanus grove, its roots combing the brown water, and beyond it the village emerged—stilt houses strung along a natural levee, canoes pulled up on the mud like sleeping animals, children already shouting at the sight of them.

"They'll have seen the boat," Lena said finally. "Word travels faster than we do. If Calloway's people come asking—"

"Then we tell the truth carefully." He looked at her. "You've been lying so long you've forgotten there are other options."

Something flickered across her face—not anger, exactly. Something more tired than anger.

"Iri," she called down to the hold, where the boy had made himself a nest among the surviving crates. "You know this village?"

Iri's head appeared through the hatch, hair still damp from his morning swim. "Ambakan? My mother's sister married a man from here. They grow tobacco." A pause, and then, with the careful neutrality of a child who has learned not to want things: "They're good people."

Lena's jaw worked. Tomas watched her weigh it—the same calculation she ran on everything now, everything since the knife wound and the sabotage and the long fevered days when she'd steered them by shouting instructions from a delirium. Trust cost something. She paid it anyway, coin by coin, and hated herself for the spending.

"Then we stop," she said. "One night. We buy food, we ask about the channel ahead, and we keep the map out of sight."

* * *

The elders received them in the largest stilt house, a cavernous space built around a central hearth where the smoke rose through a gap in the roof and stained the rafters black. There were five of them, old men and one ancient woman whose eyes had gone the pale blue of river ice, and they sat on carved benches while the whole village crowded the doorway to look at the strangers.

Tomas had done this before—in his surveying years, a hundred villages up and down the basin, sitting through the formal courtesies because the courtesies *were* the work. You couldn't map land you hadn't been given permission to walk. He'd understood that even then, even before he understood what the maps were being used *for*.

He introduced himself in the trade pidgin, then Lena, then Iri, who was claimed immediately by a round-faced woman with laughing eyes—family, or near enough. The Kestrel's crew sat on the visitors' bench and were given betel nut and smoked fish, and the talk moved the way village talk always moved: slow, circling, generous, asking nothing directly but reading everything.

It was the old woman who turned the current.

"You came up from Bakari," she said. Her pidgin was slow and precise, each word placed like a stone in a fish weir. "In the flood season. With a leaking boat."

"We did," Tomas said.

"And you carry something heavy. Not cargo." Her clouded eyes found him with unsettling accuracy. "Heavy here." She touched her own chest. "I am Yalama. I remember things. That is my work now. The young ones plant and fish and fight. I remember."

Tomas glanced at Lena. She gave him the smallest nod—*go on*—and he realized, with a strange lurch, that she was delegating. That somewhere between the rapids and the checkpoint bluff and the nights of shared watch, she had decided he could be trusted to speak for them.

"We're carrying a map," he said. "Drawn by a prospector named Elias Voss. It shows a place in the highlands—a gold seam. His daughter lives at Karawari Bend. We're taking it to her."

The name landed like a stone in still water. The ripples went around the room. One of the younger elders, a broad man with a scarred shoulder, muttered something low, and the others went quiet in a way that had weight to it.

"You know the name," Tomas said.

"Everyone in this basin knows that name," Yalama said. "Voss. The whiteman who walked our rivers for twenty years with his notebook and his instruments, and wrote down what he saw." She leaned forward. "Did he write down Ambakan?"

"No," Tomas said slowly. "I don't think—his notes covered the highlands, mostly. The seam. Why?"

"Because thirty years ago, Elias Voss sat where you are sitting." Her voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was worse. "On that bench. And he asked us about the place you call the seam. And we told him what we will tell you."

* * *

They walked her down to the water at dusk, just Tomas and Lena—Iri had been absorbed into a game of canoe-tag with the village children, and for once no one objected to letting him go. Yalama moved with a stick but without hurry, and the three of them stood on the levee as the light went copper on the flooded forest.

"The place on your map," she said. "The men of my grandmother's time called it *Wanem Solwara*—the Water That Weeps. Not for sadness." She shook her head. "For the springs. Cold water comes out of the limestone there, year-round, and the fish gather, and the birds. It was a feeding ground. A meeting place. When two peoples quarreled over gardens or pigs or women, they went to the Weeping Water to settle it, because no one carried weapons there. The ground itself forbade it."

"A truce ground," Tomas said quietly. He'd heard of such places. Every river system had one or two, sanctified by generations of use into something older than any law the colonial administration had ever imposed.

"Older than your maps," Yalama said, as if she'd heard the thought. "Then the miners came. Not many. Men with test drills and poison, working quiet, upstream of everything. They told the government they had claims. They told the government the ground was empty." Her mouth twisted. "The government believed them, because the government's maps said empty. Maps drawn by men who never asked."

Tomas felt the words go into him like a hook going into a fish—clean, deep, barbed. He thought of his own survey years. Thought of the forms he'd filled out. *Occupied status: unclaimed.* He'd written those words. He'd written them dozens of times, because the men who signed his paychecks wanted them written, and because it was easier to believe a blank page than to walk an extra day and ask.

"What happened?" Lena asked. Her voice was flat, controlled, but Tomas had learned to hear the strain under it, the way you hear strain in a hull.

Yalama turned her face toward the darkening water.

"There was a village," she said. "Koragu. Downstream of the Weeping Water. My husband's people. The miners' digging poisoned the springs—the fish died, belly-up, for a week. The people of Koragu went to the district office to complain. Loudly. With witnesses." She paused. "The magistrate sent soldiers to make the complaint go away. But complaints are like seeds. So they burned the village instead, and the witnesses with it. Forty-one people. Children among them. The official record says a landslide."

The levee was very quiet. Somewhere behind them, the children's game had ended; Iri's laughter carried faintly across the water, and it sounded impossibly far away.

"The magistrate," Tomas said. His mouth was dry. "What was his name?"

"You know his name," Yalama said. "That is why you asked it that way."

Hessler. Of course it was Hessler. The same name on the warrant at the dock, the same signature, the same man reaching out of the past with the same hands. Tomas had spent three years telling himself the syndicate was a machine, that Hessler was just the part of it visible above the waterline, that corruption was weather—you walked in it, you got wet, you dried off. Standing on the levee listening to an old woman count her dead, he could no longer pretend it was weather. It was architecture. Someone had built it. Someone was maintaining it.

"Elias Voss knew," Yalama continued. "He came back after the burning. He walked the site. He took his instruments and his notebook and he measured what was left, and he wrote it all down, and then—" her voice caught, the first crack in the stone of it, "—then he did nothing. For years. He told us he would take it to the government. He told us justice moves slowly. We watched him leave with his papers, and we waited, and nothing came. Nothing ever came." She looked at Tomas with those ice-pale eyes. "So when you say you carry his map, you must understand what you are carrying. It is not a treasure. It is a debt. Thirty years unpaid."

* * *

Lena didn't speak until they were back aboard the Kestrel, moored against the levee with a lantern lit in the cabin—the first light they'd allowed themselves since the fuel depot. She sat at the little table with the oilskin packet between her hands, not opening it, just holding it, the way you'd hold something hot.

"He mapped it," she said. "All this time I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "A dying man gives me a map to a gold seam and asks me to carry it to his daughter. I thought it was a fortune. I thought it was a target. I never once thought it was a *testimony*."

"It's both," Tomas said. "That's what makes it dangerous. Gold explains greed. Nobody burns a village over coordinates for a massacre—unless the coordinates prove the massacre happened where the syndicate needs to dig."

She looked up at him sharply. "Say that again."

"The seam is at Wanem Solwara. The sacred ground. The truce ground." He laid it out piece by piece, watching it assemble itself in the air between them. "Hessler's men can't legally claim it—it's customary land, held by every community in the basin, disputed by none. To file a claim, they need it recorded as vacant government land. Which means they need the old evidence destroyed or discredited. Voss's map puts the seam inside the massacre site. The massacre site is the proof that the land was never empty—that people died defending it. The map isn't just directions, Lena. It's the one document that makes their claim impossible."

The lantern flame bent in a draft from the hatch. Outside, the village sounds settled into evening—pigs grumbling, a drum starting somewhere, soft and conversational.

"So they don't just want the map," Lena said. "They *need* it gone. And they need Voss's daughter gone too, if she knows what it is." Her hands tightened on the oilskin. "Mara. At Karawari Bend. Running a waystation for escaped laborers." She exhaled slowly. "She's not hiding from them. She's fighting them. And she doesn't even have her father's proof yet."

"She has half of it," Tomas said. "The stories. The survivors. What she doesn't have is the thing a court—or a confluence gathering, or whatever passes for justice out here—will accept as *evidence*. Coordinates. Measurements. A whiteman's handwriting." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to soften it. "That's the ugly truth of it. Yalama can testify until she dies and the magistrate's office will call it superstition. Voss's map, in Voss's hand, with Voss's instruments—that they have to reckon with. That's why Elias sat on that bench thirty years ago and asked his questions. He wasn't hunting gold. He was building a case. And he ran out of time to deliver it."

Lena was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, steadier, the weariness burned off it like fog off morning water.

"When I took his promise, I thought I was carrying a dead man's treasure to his daughter." She pushed the oilskin packet across the table toward him. "Open it."

He did. The map lay in the lantern light, Voss's fine surveyor's hand marching across the highlands, contour lines and spring symbols and, in the corner, in smaller script, a column of figures Tomas hadn't examined closely before. Water readings. Dates spanning eleven years. Acidity levels climbing like a fever chart.

"He tested the springs," Tomas breathed. "Every trip. Eleven years of readings. This isn't a claim map—it's a *poisoning* record. The watershed data alone would sink every permit the syndicate holds." He looked up. "Lena. This doesn't just expose a massacre. It exposes the whole operation. Hessler's verdicts, the falsified surveys, the poisoned river—all of it, tied together in one document."

"That's why Calloway is willing to burn the basin for it."

"That's why we can't just deliver it and disappear." Tomas heard himself say it and felt the strange vertigo of a man watching his own reflection change. Three years ago he'd refused to falsify a single land claim and it had cost him his career, his license, his name in the agency rolls—and he'd told himself that was enough, that one refusal was a life's worth of principle. Now the principle was staring at him off a dead man's graph paper, demanding more. "We deliver it to Mara. And then we use it. All of it. Because if we don't—if we hand it over and slip downstream and let her fight alone—then we're Elias Voss on that bench, promising justice and rowing away."

Lena studied him across the lantern. "You've changed your mind about the job."

"I've changed my mind about what the job is." He rolled the map closed with careful hands. "I spent six years drawing maps for men who used them as weapons. This is the first one I've carried that could be used as a shield."

Outside, a drum changed rhythm—faster, insistent—and voices rose along the levee. Tomas was moving before he'd finished thinking, Lena right behind him, out onto the deck where Iri stood at the rail pointing upstream with his whole arm rigid.

"Canoes," the boy said. "Coming fast. Six of them." He turned, and his face in the lantern spill was the face of a child who had seen this before and knew how it ended. "Not villagers. Villagers paddle sitting. These ones stand."

Tomas squinted into the dark. Six long shapes knifing upriver against the current, riding engines no village owned, and at the bow of the lead canoe a lantern swung deliberately—once, twice, three times. A signal. Answered, a moment later, from somewhere back along the bank behind them.

They weren't lost. They were converging.

"Lena," Tomas said quietly. "How much do you trust Yalama's memory?"
