## 9. Calloway's Offer

The mission station at Ambunti sat on a bluff above the river like a patient old bird, its corrugated roof rusted the color of dried blood, its chapel windows fogged with salt and humidity. Lena tied the *Kestrel* to the pilings just before dusk, when the light went amber and long and the mosquitoes rose off the water in singing columns. She told herself it was a safe harbor. Mission stations were supposed to be neutral ground—God's territory, not Hessler's, not the syndicate's.

She should have known better. In her experience, God's territory was exactly where men like Calloway did their best work.

Tomas stayed aboard with Elias's body, wrapped now in canvas and weighted with river stones they hadn't yet had the heart to drop overboard. There was a Catholic plot behind the chapel, and Father Anselm—a stooped Italian who'd spent forty years sweating through his cassock in the basin—had agreed to bury him in the morning, with proper words, if not proper rites. It was the least Lena could do for a man whose name she barely knew and whose daughter she'd promised nothing she knew how to keep.

She went up the bluff path alone to arrange the burial details, carrying a lantern she didn't need yet and a lie she'd been rehearsing since the dock: that Voss had been a stranger, a fare, a fever case she'd pulled from the water out of common decency and nothing more. That there was no map. That there was no promise.

The station compound was quiet in the way of places that emptied at sundown. A cook fire smoked in an open-sided kitchen. Laundry hung between two palms like signal flags. And beneath the great mango tree at the center of the yard, where the roots made natural benches and generations of converts had sweated through sermons, sat a man in a white linen suit, perfectly pressed, as though he'd stepped off a steamer from some cooler, cleaner world.

He was eating a mango. Neatly. With a small knife.

"Captain Amberdale," he said, without looking up. "Or may I call you Lena? I feel we're about to become well acquainted."

Lena stopped. Her hand found the lantern handle and settled there—not because she needed a weapon, but because her hands needed something to do besides shake.

"I don't know you."

"No," he agreed. He cut another slice of mango, precise as a surgeon. "But I know you. That's rather the point of this evening." He gestured with the knife at the root beside him. "Please. Sit. The ground's damp and the conversation is long."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." He smiled—not unkindly, which was worse. "Mr. Calloway," he said, as though introducing himself at a garden party. "I represent certain commercial interests upstream. Mining concerns. You may have heard my name mentioned at the docks, in connection with a missing prospector."

"I heard men asking after a sick old man," Lena said. "I told them what I tell everyone. I don't carry passengers I don't know."

"Mm." He set down the fruit, wiped the knife on a white handkerchief, folded the handkerchief with the same terrible neatness. "And yet your boat left Wewak two days after he vanished from the reeds, heading upstream, against the current, burning fuel you can scarcely afford, toward country no cargo run justifies this time of year. Captain, I've read shipping manifests for eleven years. Lies have a shape. Yours has the shape of a woman running somewhere specific with something specific."

The lantern trembled slightly in her grip. She steadied it.

"Then you've wasted a trip."

"I don't waste trips." He stood, brushing mango fiber from his trousers, and looked up at her with an expression of genuine, almost paternal sympathy. It was the expression of a man about to do something cruel and wanting credit for doing it gently. "May I show you something?"

He drew an envelope from inside his jacket. Cream-colored paper, expensive, watermarked. He held it out. She didn't take it.

"It's not a warrant," he said. "Warrants are for magistrates. This is an offer of employment. Terms are inside. But I'll summarize, since you seem the suspicious type." He smiled again. "Full pardon. Complete expungement of the record. A new name—your choice, within reason—and passage anywhere the syndicate has ports, which is most of them. A stipend that would make your debt to Boss Kade look like pocket lint. All of it, in exchange for one item of negligible value to anyone but us."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the item becomes evidence in an ongoing investigation, and you become an accessory. There's a magistrate upstream who takes a very dim view of obstruction." He tilted his head. "You might even say a personal one."

The word *pardon* landed in Lena's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it echo all the way to the bottom—the courtroom in Port Moresby, the smell of varnish and sweat, Hessler's voice reading the verdict in that flat administrative tone, the testimony of a witness she'd never seen before and never saw again, the way her own name had turned into a sentence she'd serve forever. Seven years. Seven years of *Amberdale*, of sandbars and debts and looking over her shoulder at every uniformed man on every dock.

They were offering it back. Her name. Her life. Clean paper.

That was the trap, of course. She knew it was the trap. But knowing a trap is baited doesn't stop your mouth from watering.

"You can't pardon what wasn't a crime," she said quietly. "There was no theft. There was no trial worth the name. Your magistrate convicted me on testimony bought and paid for, and everyone in that courtroom knew it, including him."

Calloway's eyebrows rose, and for a moment the garden-party polish slipped, revealing something sharper underneath—interest, appetite, the look of a fisherman feeling a real pull on the line.

"There it is," he said softly. "You see, this is why I wanted to speak with you personally rather than send the usual sort. Most people would have taken the envelope and thanked me. But you—" he tapped the cream paper against his palm, "—you want to talk about justice. About Hessler. About that miserable little courtroom." He shook his head, almost fondly. "Do you know what I admire about you, Captain? Nothing. But I recognize utility when I see it."

"What do you want, Calloway? Say it plainly."

"The map, plainly. The oilskin packet Voss carried. Coordinates, survey notes, whatever else the old man scribbled before the river finished him." He paused. "And whatever he told you. Every word. Delirious men are surprisingly thorough."

"He told me nothing. He was delirious."

"He told you enough to make you burn fuel upstream." Calloway sighed, and the sympathy came back, thicker than before. "Let me save us both the theater. I know who you are, Lena Amberdale. Not the boat-woman. Not the sandbar ghost. The original article."

The evening seemed to tilt. Somewhere behind the kitchen, the cook fire popped. A gecko chirred twice and went silent, as though it too was listening.

"My employers maintain excellent archives," Calloway continued, conversational now, strolling a slow half-circle around the mango tree's roots. "Shipping registries, court records, land office filings. Three years ago, a clerk named—" he consulted nothing; he simply said it, savoring it, "—*Elena Vasquez*, working in the Magistrate's Office at Port Moresby, flagged irregularities in a series of verdicts. Bribed juries. Purchased witnesses. Verdicts sold to the highest bidder, with Hessler's signature on the authorization chits. She compiled a private ledger. She intended to take it to the Attorney General." He glanced up at her. "Instead, she was arrested for embezzlement, tried by the very magistrate she'd investigated, convicted on the testimony of a witness whose travel expenses were reimbursed by a mining concern, and sentenced to seven years. She escaped custody during transfer—there was a ferry accident, very tragic, two bodies never recovered—and resurfaced eighteen months later upriver, calling herself Amberdale, with new papers purchased from a forger in Madang who has since, I'm pleased to report, been paid in full for his silence."

Every word was a stone laid precisely on her chest. Lena stood very still, the way you stand still when a snake crosses the path—because movement is what it's waiting for.

"So you understand my position," Calloway said. "I don't need to threaten you. Threats are crude. I simply need to remind you of the arithmetic. One word from me—one telegram to the right desk—and Elena Vasquez is a fugitive again, hunted on two counts instead of one, with no river deep enough and no name clean enough. Or—" he held up the envelope, "—you take this, and Elena Vasquez dies officially, permanently, and a woman with a beautiful new name starts a beautiful new life. Everyone gets what they want. Even the dead clerk. Especially her."

The lantern had gone out at some point; she didn't remember pinching it. Dusk had thickened into true dark, and the mango tree threw black lace across the yard, and Calloway's white suit glowed faintly, like something that had drifted up from the bottom of the river.

She thought about the envelope. God help her, she thought about it—really thought, let herself feel the whole weight of the offer, the pardon, the clean slate, the door out of a life spent counting fuel drums and dodging sandbars and flinching at boots on docks. Seven years was a prison even outside the walls. He was offering her the walls torn down.

And he was offering them with Hessler's fingerprints all over them. That was the thing. The pardon would come through channels Hessler controlled, signed by authority Hessler owned, honored only so long as Hessler was served. It wasn't freedom. It was a longer chain, gilded.

"You killed the witness," Lena said. Her voice came out low and even, surprising her. "The one who testified against me. Afterward. When he was no longer useful."

Calloway's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a flicker, a recalculation, the briefest pause of a man discovering the prey had teeth.

"I couldn't say."

"No. You couldn't." She took a breath. The fury was there, the old furnace of it, the one she'd banked and buried and fed on cold nights—but standing here under the mango tree, hearing her own dead name spoken aloud in that pleasant voice, she found the fury had changed temperature. It wasn't hot anymore. It was cold, and cold burns longer. "Here's my answer, Mr. Calloway. I don't want your pardon. I don't want a new name. I want the old one back—the real one, unsullied, with the conviction reversed and the record burned and Hessler answering for every verdict he ever sold. And that isn't yours to give. It never was. So take your envelope, and your arithmetic, and get off this mission station before I wake Father Anselm and tell him what kind of business is being conducted under his tree."

For a long moment Calloway simply looked at her. Then he laughed—a short, genuine sound, almost delighted.

"Oh, that's much worse," he said. "Much, much worse. A woman who wants to run, I can chase. A woman who wants *justice*—" he tucked the envelope away, "—that's a different animal entirely. Those have to be put down." He picked up his hat from the root beside him and settled it on his head. "You should know, Captain, that Voss's map leads to ground my employers have already claimed on paper—ground with a massacre on it that certain parties would very much prefer stayed buried. You aren't carrying a treasure. You're carrying a grave marker. And graves, unlike gold, attract diggers of the worst kind."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"Do." He turned, then paused, half-facing her, the lantern-light from the kitchen catching the edge of his profile. "One more thing, gratis, because I respect the performance tonight. My gunboat *Meridian* is anchored below the confluence bend. Forty hours, full steam. Whatever route you're planning—I'd plan faster."

He walked away across the yard, unhurried, his footsteps crisp on the packed earth, and let himself out through the chapel gate like a guest leaving a dinner party he'd found mildly diverting.

Lena stood under the mango tree until his footsteps faded, until the cook fire burned down, until the first frogs started up along the bluff. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had finally stopped shaking. What remained underneath was quieter and far more dangerous: clarity.

Her cover was gone. The name she'd built out of river mud and silence was worthless as of tonight—Calloway would sell it the moment it suited him, and probably sooner. There was no more hiding, no more *Amberdale*, no more invisible woman on an invisible boat. The choice he'd offered was no choice at all: kneel or run.

But he'd made a mistake, the same mistake powerful men always made. He'd assumed she was still running.

She climbed the bluff path back to the *Kestrel*, where Tomas sat smoking in the dark beside the canvas-wrapped shape of Elias Voss, and the river breathed below them, huge and patient, already carrying her answer downstream.

"Tomas," she said. "How fast can you learn the channel marks above the gorge?"

He looked up, read her face in one long second, and stubbed out the cigarette. "Fast enough. Why?"

"Because we leave tonight," Lena said, "and we're done hiding."

* * *

They buried Elias Voss at dawn, in the little plot behind the chapel, while Father Anselm mumbled Latin over red clay and the mist came off the river in slow silver sheets. Tomas dug; Lena held the map's oilskin wrapping, empty now, having kept only the map itself, which slept sewn into the lining of her coat where no envelope, no archive, no magistrate's finger would ever find it.

Iri appeared at the edge of the cemetery halfway through the service—a thin boy with river-mud feet and eyes too old for his face, drawn by the smell of fresh-turned earth and the rumor of strangers with a fast boat. He watched them fill the grave without a word.

When Father Anselm finished, Lena stood a moment longer, looking down at the mound.

"You kept your promise to the wrong person," she murmured. "But I'll keep mine to the right one."

Then she turned, and walked down to the *Kestrel*, and cast off into the rising mist—upstream, toward Karawari Bend, with a dead man's map against her ribs, a living man's charts in her hands, and a gunboat named *Meridian* somewhere behind her, coming on at full steam.
