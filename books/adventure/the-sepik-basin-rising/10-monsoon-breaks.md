## 10. Monsoon Breaks

The sky had been lying to them for two days.

Lena had learned to read the basin's weather the way other people read faces—by the small betrayals, the tells that preceded a change of heart. A greenish cast to the horizon at dusk. The way the birds went quiet in the reeds, not singing but waiting. The river itself, running brown and swollen days before any rain fell, as if it knew something the clouds hadn't yet confessed.

"It's early," Tomas said, standing beside her at the wheel. He'd been saying it since dawn, each time with more conviction, like repetition could hold back the season. "Monsoon's not due for another week. Ten days, by the almanac."

"The almanac was written by men who surveyed from offices." Lena eased the Kestrel around a half-submerged log, its roots clawing at the current like fingers. "The river doesn't keep their calendar."

They were three hours out of the last trading post, pushing up the Bakari's western fork toward the gorge country, when the first wind came. It didn't arrive so much as fall—a single enormous exhalation from the highlands that flattened the sago palms along both banks and turned the river's surface to hammered tin. Lena killed the engine and listened. Underneath the wind she heard it: a sound like distant surf, though the nearest sea was four hundred kilometers away.

"What is that?" Iri asked, emerging from under the cargo tarp where he'd been mending a torn seam with the patient stitches of someone who'd learned young that broken things don't fix themselves.

"Rain," Lena said. "Upstream. All of it at once."

Tomas shaded his eyes and looked north, toward the wall of black cloud building over the ranges. His face did something complicated. "Flash flood," he said quietly. "The gorges funnel everything. We need high ground. Now."

"There's no high ground on this stretch. It's all—" She stopped. She'd run this fork twice before, years ago, in drier seasons. Three kilometers ahead the banks pinched together where an old limestone ridge shouldered into the channel, and above it—

"Above the pinch," she said. "There's a cave mouth. Old flood line. Fishermen used it."

"Used it for what?"

"For surviving exactly this."

The first drops hit the deck like thrown gravel, fat and warm and shockingly hard, and then the sky simply opened. Not rain—the word was too gentle for what came down. It was the river's whole watershed arriving overhead at once, a roaring grey curtain that erased the far bank, erased the sky, erased everything but the wheel in her hands and the sound of water hitting water.

"Get below!" she shouted at Iri, and saw him scramble, and then gave the Kestrel everything her tired engine had left.

* * *

Tomas had never been in weather that felt personal before.

He'd worked through storms on government surveys—had once spent a week in a canvas tent on a ridge while a cyclone tried to peel the highlands open—but this was different. This rain had intent. It came down in columns rather than drops, and where the columns struck the river they raised a kind of smoke, and the whole world smelled of iron and crushed vegetation and something older underneath, the mineral breath of stone being washed for the ten-thousandth time.

He hauled on the forward line while Lena fought the wheel, both of them soaked past the point of wetness into some new state of being, and watched the river rise even as they watched it. It wasn't subtle. The current that had been carrying them at six knots now dragged them backward when the engine faltered, and the snags that had been visible hazards an hour ago were gone entirely, drowned markers in a drowning channel.

"There!" Lena pointed. The limestone ridge rose sheer off the eastern bank, black rock streaked white with bird lime, and forty feet up its face—impossibly high, impossibly close—a dark horizontal slash. The cave.

"We'll never get lines up there in this."

"We're not getting lines up." She was already turning the boat, reading the eddy behind the ridge the way she read everything, in one fast comprehensive swallow. "There's a shelf below the cave. Flood-cut. If I can put us against it, you climb. You throw down a line. We winch the cargo up to you and abandon the hull if we have to."

"Abandon the—" His voice cracked with something between outrage and grief. "She's barely holding together as it is. If we leave her against that rock in a flood—"

"I know what it does to her." Lena's jaw was set in a way he was beginning to recognize: the look of a woman pricing a loss she couldn't afford and deciding to pay anyway. "But boats are replaceable. People aren't. Move."

The Kestrel came in on the eddy's shoulder like a drunk leaning on a friend. Tomas got the bowline around a stunted fig tree growing from a crack in the shelf—one knot, two, his hands shaking with cold and adrenaline—and then Lena cut the engine and the river took over, slamming the hull against the shelf with a boom that he felt in his teeth.

"Climb!"

He climbed. The limestone was slick as soap, the rain hammering his shoulders, and twice his feet shot out from under him and he hung from his fingertips over thirty feet of churning brown water, thinking with strange clarity: *I refused to falsify one map and now I'm going to die on an unmapped cliff.* Then his hand found the ledge, and he rolled onto it, and lay gasping on stone while the storm shouted around him.

Below, Lena was throwing cargo crates up to Iri, who had appeared at the cave mouth without anyone telling him to be there, catching and hauling with the economy of a boy who'd done heavy work all his short life. Rice. Fuel drums. The tool chest. The medicine box, which Lena threw third, before the rice, and which told Tomas something about her priorities that he filed away carefully.

The river rose. He could measure it against the Kestrel's waterline—two feet in twenty minutes, three—and the current in the main channel had stopped looking like water at all. It looked like land moving. Whole trees went past, upright, sailing, their crowns still green. Something long and pale turned in an eddy and was gone.

"Lena!" he shouted down. "That's enough! Come up!"

"One more drum!" Her voice came thin through the rain.

"That's enough! Now!"

She hesitated—he saw it, the captain's instinct to save the ship warring with the survivor's instinct to save herself—and then the river made the decision for her. A surge came down the channel, a standing wave of debris and brown water taller than the boat, and Tomas screamed a warning he couldn't hear himself say, and the wave took the Kestrel and drove her sideways onto the shelf with a shriek of tearing timber.

For one endless moment the boat lay against the rock like a wounded animal, and then the water lifted her, rolled her, and she was going—over, foundering, her stern rising as the bow flooded, cargo netting snapping loose, and Lena was still aboard.

Tomas went down the cliff without deciding to. Later he would have no memory of the descent, only of hitting the shelf knee-first, of grabbing the gunwale as the hull rolled beneath him, of finding Lena in the flooding cabin with water to her waist, shoving the oilskin-wrapped map case into his arms with both hands.

"Take it up," she said. Calmly. In the middle of a drowning ship. "Then come back for me."

"Like hell." He shoved the case into its sling across his own chest, knotted it, and grabbed her wrist. "Together or not at all. That's how partnerships work. I read it somewhere."

The second surge hit as they reached the shelf, and it picked the Kestrel up and folded her against the limestone like paper, and Tomas heard—Iri heard, they all heard—the hull open along her seams with a sound like a giant sighing. They came over the lip of the cave mouth as the boat that had carried Lena Amberdale through seven years of hiding slid backward off the shelf, rolled once, and vanished into the brown.

The rain kept falling. It did not care.

* * *

The cave was dry, mostly, and enormous.

It ran back into the ridge farther than the light of their one lamp could follow, its ceiling ribbed with flowstone, its floor scattered with old flood debris—driftwood, a rotted canoe, fish bones in drifts that marked high-water lines from floods older than any of them. Someone had built a hearth near the entrance once; the stones were smoke-blackened. Generations of fishermen, Lena said, riding out exactly this.

Iri got a fire going with wood from the driftwood pile and a match kept dry inside his shirt, tucked against skin, because—as he explained with a shrug—"the river takes things. You keep one thing where it can't reach." Tomas watched the boy feed the flame and thought about what it meant to grow up learning that lesson at eight instead of forty.

Lena sat apart, wrapped in the one dry blanket, staring at the entrance where the rain fell in a solid silver sheet. She hadn't spoken since the Kestrel went down. Tomas didn't try to talk to her. Some losses needed silence around them before they could be carried.

It was Iri who finally broke it, in the oblique way children break things.

"She was leaking anyway," the boy said to the fire. "Before. I patched her twice at the post. The planks near the keel were soft. You could push a thumb through." A pause. "She would have sunk soon regardless. Maybe the river just... chose the moment."

"She didn't sink," Lena said, to no one, to the fire. "I sank her. Seven years I kept her patched and floating because floating was easier than stopping. And the first time I let go of the wheel, she goes down in ninety seconds." A rough almost-laugh. "Maybe she was trying to tell me something."

Tomas crouched by the fire, warming his hands, watching her profile against the lamplight. "What's she telling you?"

"That I've been living on a boat named after a bird that never lands." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Go to sleep, Tomas. One of us should."

He didn't sleep. Instead, sometime deep in the night, with Lena breathing slow under her blanket and Iri curled like a comma near the fire, Tomas took the lamp and walked back into the cave.

He told himself it was restlessness. But his feet knew better than his excuses did. Twenty years a surveyor: put the man in any enclosed space and he would read it, whether he wanted to or not. So he walked, and he read—chamber after chamber, the lamp throwing his shadow huge against flowstone curtains, until the passage narrowed and turned and he noticed something that stopped him mid-stride.

Marks on the wall. Fresh ones.

Not the fishermen's old charcoal smudges, softened by decades of damp. These were sharp. Paint. He raised the lamp and his stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Survey markers. The standard syndicate scheme he'd been ordered to falsify, once, and refused: a red triangle with a code stenciled beneath it, and beside it, in careful engineer's hand, elevation figures and chainage distances. *KB-14. +212m. Chain 4400.* He knew the notation system. He had helped design parts of it, back when he believed maps served the people who lived inside them.

The paint was fresh. Days old, at most. In a cave that fishermen used only in flood season—which meant whoever painted these had come recently, deliberately, scouting the high-water route through the gorge country.

Hessler's men weren't chasing them anymore.

They were mapping ahead of them.

Tomas stood in the dripping dark with the lamp trembling slightly in his hand and counted the markers. Fourteen visible in this chamber alone, and the passage continued beyond the lamplight, which meant the chain continued, which meant somewhere upstream—perhaps at Karawari Bend itself, perhaps at the seam—there was a survey team working right now, tonight, in this same storm, laying out the route Hessler's dredges and guards would follow once the rains passed.

They weren't fleeing. They were being herded. Every safe anchorage, every shortcut, every cave—known. Charted. Owned.

He walked back to the fire slowly. Lena was awake. He knew it before he saw her eyes open, because she'd taught him that much about herself already: she slept like a sentry, surfacing at every change in the night's sound.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," she murmured.

"So are you." He crouched beside her and lowered his voice, glancing at Iri's small sleeping shape. "You need to see something. Back chamber, past the turn. Bring the lamp."

She saw it in silence. He watched her trace the red triangles with one finger, not touching the paint, and watched her face do the thing it did when fear turned into something with edges.

"Chain 4400," she said at last. "Where does that put them?"

"If the chainage runs from the confluence, it puts their next station within a day's walk of Karawari Bend." He paused. "Lena. They have the whole route. They have elevations. They may already have people at the Bend."

"Then we stop being a boat crew." She straightened, and in the lamplight her eyes were the color of the flooded river—brown, opaque, moving fast over hidden rocks. "A boat needs channels. We're done with channels. From here we go overland, the way Iri's people do, the way no survey chain follows, and we move faster than men who think the map is already finished."

"And the Kestrel?"

Lena looked back down the passage, toward the cave mouth and the silver roar beyond it, where seven years of hiding lay somewhere under the floodwater.

"The river can keep her," she said. "We have a promise to deliver."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, Iri sat up in the dark by the dead fire, listening—and quietly began pulling on his boots.
