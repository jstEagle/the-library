## 11. The Stowaway

The smell found her first.

Lena had been down in the hold since dawn, hauling crates of dried fish and kerosene tins that had shifted when they'd run the shallows above Ambunti, and beneath the brine and lamp-oil and river-damp there was something else—something human. Sweat. Woodsmoke. The particular sourness of a body that had been afraid for a long time.

She stood still among the crates and listened.

The Kestrel's engine was a low, constant heartbeat through the hull, and under it, water chuckled against the planking, and under that—breathing. Quick and shallow, from the gap between the rice crates and the starboard bulkhead. A breathing that went still the moment she stopped moving, the way a bird goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

Lena had been running things that hid from her for six years. She knew the difference between cargo that shifted and cargo that breathed.

"I can hear you," she said, not loudly. "Come out before I come get you, because I won't be gentle."

Nothing. The engine thumped. Somewhere above, Tomas's boots crossed the deck, and the wheel creaked as he corrected their heading.

She took the kerosene lantern off its hook and held it low, letting the light fall into the gap between the crates. Two eyes caught it—wide, dark, furious with the particular fury of the young and cornered.

"I said come out."

A crate scraped. A leg appeared—bare, mud-scarred, one foot wrapped in a rag that had been white once. Then the rest of him folded out of the gap like something unfolding from a seed pod: a boy, maybe fourteen, maybe sixteen, it was hard to tell with river children who aged fast and ate little. He was all elbows and collarbones, wearing a shirt that had belonged to a much larger man, and he came up out of the hold's gloom holding a fish-gutting knife in a grip that meant it.

Lena didn't reach for the machete at her belt. That was a lesson you learned early on this river: never give a frightened thing a reason to use what it's holding.

"Put that down before you cut yourself," she said. "It's sharper than you think."

"I'll cut you."

"You'll try." She crouched, slow, setting the lantern on the deck between them so the light came up soft instead of hard. "How long?"

The boy's eyes flicked to the hatch, to the ladder, back to her. Calculating. She'd seen that arithmetic in men twice his age and it had never once saved them.

"Since the fuel depot," he said. His voice cracked on the depot and he hated it—she watched him hate it, watched him force the next words out level. "Two nights."

Two nights. Through the fuel depot, through the boarding, through the knife that had opened her arm and the sabotage that had emptied their tanks. He'd been folded into that gap no wider than a coffin while strangers with machetes walked the deck above him and Lena had bled into her shirt not four feet from where he hid.

"You heard all of it," she said.

"I heard enough." His chin came up. "I heard them say they'd take the boat. I heard you say the map was a lie. You lied good."

"Thank you."

"I wasn't—"

"How did you get aboard?"

Something shifted in his face then. The bravado thinned and she saw what was under it, the way you see the riverbed when the floodwater drops: something raw and recent.

"I work the depot," he said. "Worked. I carry fuel drums. They came at night, your enemies, the ones in the clean boots, and the depot man, Salik, he told them things. Told them where boats were going. And I thought—if they pay Salik, they'll pay anyone, and if they pay anyone, then anyone will sell me." His knuckles whitened on the knife. "So I got in your hold. Your boat was going upstream, away. That's all I wanted. Away."

"From whom?"

The silence stretched. Outside, a heron lifted off the bank in a slap of wings. When the boy finally spoke, the words came out flat and practiced, the way children recite things they've had to say too many times.

"The recruiters. They come to the villages with a truck and a ledger. They say the mines pay good wages, they say it's three years and then you come home. My brother went. He was seventeen." He swallowed. "Nobody comes home, lady. The letters stop. My mother went looking and then she stopped coming to market and then she stopped coming anywhere. So when the recruiters came back for me, I ran. I've been running since. Salik fed me for drum-work and I thought the depot was far enough." His jaw tightened. "It wasn't far enough."

Lena rose. Her arm ached where the healer's stitches pulled, and the ache steadied her the way pain always had—reminding her the body was still hers, still making its own small arguments against the world.

"You stole passage on a boat full of people being hunted," she said. "You understand that. You hid in a hold that men with knives searched, and you're lucky they were looking for paper and not for boys, because if they'd found you, they'd have cut your throat and weighted you and never broken stride."

"I know."

"You know nothing. You know the shape of it." She climbed the ladder, then stopped with her head and shoulders in the daylight and looked back down at him, still crouched among the crates with his useless knife. "Stay there. Touch nothing. I'll decide what to do with you."

* * *

She decided over the wheel, with Tomas beside her and the river running wide and brown-gold under a sky the color of hammered tin.

"A stowaway," Tomas said. He rubbed the stubble on his jaw, a habit she'd catalogued as his tell for thinking. "Through all that. Through the boarding."

"Through the boarding."

"And you want to do what with him?"

"Put him off at Wagu. It's a mission station, there's a father there who takes in strays. We'll be past it by midday tomorrow."

Tomas was quiet for a while. The Kestrel's patched hull groaned beneath them, that low complaint she'd stopped hearing and now heard always, the sound of a boat living on borrowed time. Ahead, the banks narrowed into the drowned forest country, trees standing hip-deep in floodwater, their reflections so perfect it looked like the world had been folded in half.

"You know what they do to boys at the mission stations," Tomas said finally. "They keep them a year and then the recruiters come to the mission stations. It's cleaner for everyone. The fathers get a fee for the boy's keep and the syndicate gets its labor and nobody has to chase anyone through the bush."

"I know how it works, Tomas."

"Then you're not putting him off at Wagu because you think he'll be safe. You're putting him off because he's a problem and problems go over the side." He said it without heat, which was the worst way to say it. "That's not a criticism. I've thrown a lot over the side to keep a hull light. I'm only saying call it what it is."

Lena's hands tightened on the wheel. The river slid by, indifferent, gorgeous, full of things eating each other politely beneath the surface.

"He lied his way onto my boat," she said. "He hid through a boarding that got me cut. And now he's a mouth to feed on short rations, in monsoon, on a river full of men paid to find us."

"Yes."

"And if we're caught, they'll kill him for what he's heard."

"Yes."

"Say something useful, Tomas."

"I am." He leaned on the rail, watching the drowned trees go by. "You pulled Voss out of the reeds when every smart bone in your body said leave him. You're carrying his map to a daughter who may or may not want it. You took on a disgraced surveyor because he could read water. There's a pattern, Lena, and the pattern is that you keep picking up people the river spat out, and it keeps working out, and I think it frightens you more than Calloway does."

"That's not a pattern. That's bad luck repeating."

"Sure." He smiled at the water. "Put him off at Wagu, then. And every market day for the rest of your life, some part of you will wonder which truck he was standing beside when the ledger came out."

* * *

She went down into the hold at dusk, when the light through the hatch had gone the color of old honey, and found him exactly where she'd left him, as if moving might forfeit whatever fragile claim he'd staked. He'd eaten nothing. She'd left nothing to eat, which was its own kind of test, and he'd passed it, or failed it, depending on how you read a boy who wouldn't touch a stranger's stores.

She sat down on a crate across from him, her wounded arm cradled, and put a tin of biscuits and a strip of dried fish on the deck between them.

"Your name."

"Iri."

"Just Iri?"

"Just Iri." He took the fish but not the biscuits, and tore a piece off and chewed it like a boy who'd learned to eat fast because eating slow meant sharing. "Village called Yentchen, up the Yuat. You won't have heard of it."

"I've heard of it."

He stopped chewing.

"Small place," she said. "Two canoes and a smokehouse. Burned eight years ago. Officially it was a land dispute. Unofficially—" She watched his face. "You'd have been six. Maybe seven."

"My mother carried me out through the sago swamp," he said, after a while. His voice had gone very quiet. "I don't remember the fire. I remember the water. Everyone remembers the water." He looked up at her, and the last of the cornered-animal glare was gone, and what was underneath was worse, because it was steady. "The men who burned it wore the same boots as the men at the depot. I know the boots. So when I heard them on the deck that night, I stayed in my hole, because I already know how that story ends for people in my way."

Lena found she had nothing to say to that. Eight years she'd been carrying the knowledge of Yentchen in the same pocket as everything else Hessler had burned through her life, and here was a boy who was the ash of it, sitting in her hold, alive and chewing her dried fish.

"Here's what happens," she said at last. "Tomorrow we pass Wagu. I could put you off there. I'm not going to. Not because I'm kind—kindness is expensive and I'm poor. Because Tomas is right, and I hate that he's right, and the mission stations are just recruiting stations with hymns." She stood, her knees cracking. "You'll work. Fuel, lines, the pump when the hull complains, which is always. You'll do what I say when I say it, and when I say hide, you'll hide so well I can't find you. And if we're boarded again—"

"I know the hold," he said. "I know every gap in it. I lived in it two nights."

"Then you're the only one aboard who does." She turned for the ladder, and his voice stopped her.

"Captain."

She looked back. He was standing now, and the lantern light made him seem briefly older, briefly the man he'd be if the river ever gave him the years.

"You're going up past the Bakari, yes? Into the gorge country?"

"Past it. Why?"

"Because everyone goes around, through the long channel." His eyes had lit with something that on an adult face she'd have called ambition. On his, it was pure survival arithmetic. "Nobody takes the shortcut. Nobody outsider. But my father ran timber through it before the fire—before I was born he did, and he showed me the marks, the way you show a son, in case. The Gorge of Echoes. Three days saved, maybe four, and the gunboats can't follow. It's too narrow and the water's too clever for them."

"The water's too clever for them," Lena repeated. "And for us."

"For them, definitely." He almost smiled. It transformed his whole face and then vanished. "For us—my father ran it forty times. I've run it once, with him. I remember the marks. Most of them."

"*Most* of them."

"The ones that matter." He said it with the magnificent confidence of the fourteen-year-old, and Lena, who had navigated her whole life on the magnificent confidence of people who turned out to be right just often enough, felt something in her chest that was not quite hope and not quite surrender.

She climbed the ladder. At the top, she looked down at him one more time, this small burned remnant of a village she'd once read about in a magistrate's file and filed away as weather.

"Most of them had better be all of them, Iri. Because if you put us on the rocks, I'm putting you off the boat at the next sandbar, and the river can have you."

"Fair," he said.

* * *

They ran the last hours of daylight with Iri on deck, and it was Tomas who noticed it first—how the boy moved. He worked the fuel line without being told twice. He read the water the way Tomas read charts, pointing out the submerged snag before the Kestrel's bow even began to answer it, calling depths by the color of the current the way other children called birds by their songs.

"Where did he learn that?" Tomas murmured, as Iri crouched at the bow, one hand on the rail, his whole body angled into the river like a compass needle.

"His father ran timber," Lena said. "Through the gorge."

"Through the *Gorge of Echoes*?" Tomas turned, and for the first time since she'd known him, the disgraced surveyor looked genuinely, openly startled. "Lena. That gorge isn't on any chart I ever drew, and I drew the charts. The government mapped that whole reach and the gorge is a blank—marked *impassable, seasonal*. If there's a runnable channel through it, and the boy knows the marks—"

"Then we're four days ahead of Calloway instead of behind him."

"Or on the rocks at the bottom of a hole no one will ever find." He ran his hand through his hair. "And we'd be taking him back toward it. You understand that. The gorge country is where the recruiters work the river villages hardest. We'd be sailing the boy home to the wolves."

Lena watched Iri at the bow, silhouetted against the last copper light, and thought about a boy folded into a coffin-gap while knives walked over his head, choosing that over the certainty of the truck and the ledger. She thought about Elias Voss dying at dawn with her hand around his, buying her promise with his last breath. She thought about a file with the word Yentchen in it, and a magistrate's signature, and eight years of being no one.

"Then we'd better get good at outrunning wolves," she said.

The river bent north into the hills, and the first far thunder of the monsoon rolled down the valley like a door closing behind them.
