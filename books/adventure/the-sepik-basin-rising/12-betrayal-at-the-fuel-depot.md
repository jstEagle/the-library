## 12. Betrayal at the Fuel Depot

The fuel depot at Ambunti Junction was the kind of place Tomas Reyes had spent ten years learning to distrust.

It sat on a mud shelf where two channels braided together, a sprawl of rusted drums under corrugated tin, a plank office with a radio mast leaning like a drunk against its guy-wires. A diesel smell hung over everything, thick enough to taste. The man who ran it — Weller, according to the sign, though the paint had eaten half the letters — came down to the dock wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime in the previous decade.

"Kestrel," Weller said, reading the name off her transom with professional interest. "Haven't seen that hull up here since the old Amberdale run."

Tomas felt Lena go still beside him. It was a small thing, a hitch in her breathing, but he'd spent three weeks learning to read her the way he read water — the surface telling you almost nothing, everything happening underneath.

"New owner," she said flatly. "We need diesel. Forty drums if you have them. We're running light."

"I have drums." Weller's eyes moved across the boat with an appraiser's patience: the patched hull, the crates lashed under canvas, Iri crouched in the shadow of the wheelhouse pretending not to listen, which no child alive could actually manage. "Payment?"

"Cash."

"Then we'll talk inside." He folded his rag with strange precision and led them up the plank walk, and Tomas followed because there was nowhere else to be, and because something about the man's hands bothered him. Hands too clean for the work. Fingernails trimmed square. A depot man's nails went black in the first month and never came back.

Inside, the office smelled of kerosene and old paper. A calendar from a Port Moresby shipping firm hung crooked on the wall, three years out of date. And pinned above the desk, half-hidden behind a fly-swatter, was a survey commission certificate — the crest of the Lands Directorate, the same crest that had been stamped on every document Tomas had ever signed before they burned his signature off the rolls.

"You were Directorate," Tomas said.

Weller glanced at the certificate as if noticing it for the first time. "Another life." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the corners, the smile of a man who wanted to be liked. "Tomás Reyes."

The sound of his own name in this stranger's mouth landed like a dropped anchor. Beside him, Lena's head turned slowly.

"They told us you drowned your maps and walked into the bush," Weller went on, easy, conversational, pouring tea from a dented thermos as though they'd all arranged to meet. "Reyes, the surveyor who wouldn't play. There are men in Port Moresby who tell that story like a parable. You made some of the honest ones feel less alone, I'll say that."

"Who told you I was here?"

"No one told me you were here." Weller set a chipped enamel cup in front of him. "I recognized you. I sat across from you at the Karawari hearings, oh — six years ago? You testified against the Ambunti lease consolidation. You had figures written on your cuff so your hands wouldn't shake. I remember thinking, there's a man about to be fed into a machine." He sipped his own tea. "And then you were. And here we both are, selling fuel and drinking bad tea in the middle of nowhere. The Directorate has a long memory and short arms."

Lena hadn't touched her cup. "You know who we are. Who else does?"

Weller spread his hands — those clean, square-nailed hands — in a gesture of wounded openness. "Captain. Surveyor. I'm a fuel merchant. My politics died with my pension. What I care about is that you pay cash, which you've said you will, and that nobody shoots at my drums, which is between you and whoever might want to." He leaned back. "Forty drums, delivered to your dock by morning. You'll want to leave before the river drops again. Monsoon's late, but it's coming — you can smell it."

He was right. You could smell it: a green metallic edge under the diesel, the whole basin holding its breath.

They paid. They shook the hand offered to them. And all the way back down the plank walk, Tomas turned the encounter over like a stone looking for what lived under it, and found only the smooth warm face of a man who had known his name and used it gently, and the memory of his own handwriting on a cuff six years ago, and the fact that in all the basin there were perhaps four people who could have connected Tomás Reyes to the Kestrel, and now one of them ran a fuel depot at the exact junction any boat heading for the highlands would have to use.

"He knew too much," Lena said quietly, when they were aboard.

"He knew old news. The hearings were public record."

"The hearings didn't list what boat you'd be standing on." She looked at him, and her eyes in the failing light were the color of the channel water — brown hiding green hiding brown. "Tomas. Who else knew you'd taken work on the Kestrel?"

* * *

He lay awake that night in the wheelhouse, which had become his bunk by unspoken agreement after the second week, when sleeping in the same cabin as Lena had started to feel like a door neither of them had agreed to open or close. Mosquito coil smoke curled blue around the compass binnacle. Through the open window he could see the depot lights, a scatter of yellow across the black water, and hear the frogs going mad in the reeds the way they always did before rain.

He took out the map.

Not Voss's map — that slept in its oilskin inside Lena's shirt, against her skin, where she'd kept it since the third night, a habit she probably thought he hadn't noticed. This was Tomas's own work: the chart he'd been drafting since the Bakari rapids, pencil on tracing paper, the river rendered in his old Directorate hand. He added tonight's fix by lamplight. Ambunti Junction. Fuel secured. Next leg, the Karawari cut, then the climb.

His pencil paused.

There was a discipline surveyors learned early: never trust a position you haven't cross-checked. One bearing tells you a line; two tell you a point; three tell you whether either of the first two was lying. He had built a career on triangulation, and somewhere along the way — he couldn't name the day, but it had happened, sometime between the rapids and the cave and the boy stealing food from his plate and grinning about it — he had stopped triangulating people. He had decided to trust Lena because she ran from the same machinery that had chewed him. He had decided to trust Iri because the boy was fourteen and hungry. He had decided, tonight, to trust Weller because Weller had once been one of them.

One bearing. One line. No cross-check.

He thought about the certificate on the wall, displayed just crookedly enough to look forgotten. He thought about the tea poured before any business was discussed, hospitality as a technique. He thought about how Weller had said *the Directorate has a long memory and short arms*, and how a man planning to betray you often tells you so, plainly, because some part of him wants to be caught — wants the guilt converted into punishment, wants someone else to make the choice he can't make himself.

Or maybe he was paranoid. Maybe six years of exile had rotted something in him, the way rot gets into a hull below the waterline where no one thinks to look.

He got up quietly, crossed the deck, and checked the mooring lines. Then the fuel drums, lashed amidships under tarpaulin. Then the crates. Iri slept in the gap between cargo and rail, one arm flung over a rice sack, dead to the world the way only children and the truly exhausted can be.

Everything was fine. Everything was exactly as they'd left it.

That was the thing about sabotage, he thought, walking back. Done well, you never saw it until the moment you needed the thing you'd lost.

He did not sleep again.

* * *

They came at the hour the frogs went quiet — somewhere past two, in that dead band of night when even the river seems to hold its tongue.

Tomas heard it first as absence. The insects along the bank stopped, section by section, like lamps being shuttered, and his body understood before his mind did: something was moving through the reeds, and the reeds knew it.

He reached for the boathook and put his hand over Iri's mouth before the boy could wake loud. "Quiet," he breathed. "Don't scream. Wake Lena. Crawl."

The boy's eyes were enormous and perfectly awake — a child raised in syndicate country, Tomas realized, had probably lain listening for footsteps in the dark more nights than he'd slept through them. Iri nodded once and moved low and fast along the deck.

Tomas counted shapes coming up the bank. Four. Five. Moving well, spread out, no lights — these weren't dock thugs, these were men who'd done this before. The lead figure carried something long-barreled and low-slung. Behind him, another had a blade already out, catching the depot's distant glow along its edge.

"Lena," Tomas said, low, at the cabin door. "Five men. Boarding port side and stern."

She was already awake. He didn't know how long she'd been awake — perhaps since the frogs stopped, perhaps since Ambunti Junction, perhaps since 1904, for all he knew of the nights she carried. She rolled out of the bunk with the machete she kept under it, and in the lamplight her face had gone very calm, which frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Stern side," she said. "They'll expect us to defend the cargo. Let them commit to the cargo. Iri — under the wheelhouse floor. Don't come out."

"And you?" Tomas said.

"I'm going to teach somebody a lesson about boarding without asking." She moved past him, barefoot, silent, and the last he saw of her was the machete turning once in her grip like a thing settling into a familiar hand.

What followed took less than four minutes and stayed with him for years.

The first man over the rail met a thrown bilge bucket to the face and went backward into the shallows with a splash that covered the sound of everything else. The second and third came together over the cargo, cutting the tarpaulin, and Lena let them get one boot each on deck before she came out of the dark between the crates — low, left-handed, the machete flat rather than edged, the flat cracking against a knee with a sound like green wood breaking. The man's scream tore the night open. His partner swung the knife wild and caught her — Tomas saw it happen as if underwater, saw the blade skip off her forearm as she twisted, opening a line from elbow to wrist that immediately went black and shining — and then she had his wrist in her other hand and bent it until the knife dropped, and drove him into the rail hard enough that the whole boat shuddered.

The fourth man fired. The shot went high and wide, splintering the wheelhouse trim a hand's width from Tomas's head, and then Tomas did the only useful thing he did that night: he threw the boathook, end over end, badly, the way you throw anything in terror — and by pure accident the hook's butt caught the shooter across the temple and dropped him into the scuppers.

The fifth man was already gone. Tomas heard him crashing away through the reeds toward the depot, and understood, with a cold clarity that arrived ahead of any feeling, that the fifth man had never intended to fight. The fifth man had come aboard last, gone straight to the fuel drums while everyone watched the machete, and done whatever he had come to do.

By the time they got a lantern lit, the reeds were empty, the shallows held one groaning man and one fled one, and two others had dragged themselves off into the dark leaving blood trails like signatures. Lena stood at the rail with her forearm wrapped in a cargo rag already soaked through, breathing hard, watching the depot.

The depot lights were on. All of them. And Weller stood on his plank walk in the glow, arms folded, watching the Kestrel with the mild interest of a man observing weather.

"He sold us," Tomas said. The words tasted like the tea. "Tonight. He sold us tonight."

"He sold us the moment he heard your name." Lena's voice was steady, but she was pressing the rag hard, and beneath the steadiness Tomas could hear the pain working its way in, patient as water. "Get the lamp. Look at my arm properly. And then look at the drums."

* * *

The drums told the story better than any confession.

Three of the forty had been opened and refilled — not emptied, which they'd have noticed by weight, but adulterated. Tomas dipped a rag in and held it to the lantern: the diesel came away thinned, cloudy, cut with something that smelled faintly of solvent. Water, maybe. Something worse. Enough to run sweet for an hour and then seize an engine in the worst possible reach of river, miles from any shore that wasn't syndicate country.

"They didn't come to kill us," he said. "They came to break us *out there*. In the gorge, or the flood forest, wherever the river does their work for free." He looked at the lantern flame and felt the paranoia settle into him like ballast — permanent, structural, part of the boat now. "Calloway doesn't want bodies in the basin. Bodies bring investigators. He wants us to vanish the way boats vanish. Bad fuel, monsoon season, tragic loss."

Lena sat on the crate while he cleaned the wound. She didn't flinch when the antiseptic went in, which somehow made it worse to do. The cut was long but shallow — a slicing blow, not a stabbing one, the knife turned at the last instant either by her twist or by the man's nerves. Her forearm would carry a scar like a river on a map.

"You should have woken me sooner," she said. "You handled it."

"I threw a boathook and hit a man by accident."

"A hit's a hit." Almost a smile. Then it faded, and she looked out at the black water, at the depot lights burning their honest yellow, and Tomas watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he'd been doing since the office: every person who knows your name is a coordinate, and enough coordinates fix your position, and a fixed position can be fired upon.

"Weller knew mine," Tomas said. "From six years ago. From a hearing room in Port Moresby." He tied off the bandage, tighter than strictly necessary, and hated himself for the small cruelty of it. "Lena — I brought this aboard. My name. My history. Calloway's people didn't find you because of Voss's map. They found the route because I am a known quantity, and I walked into a depot and let a stranger pour me tea."

"Tomas."

"One bearing," he said. "That's all I ever gave anyone. One line, no cross-check, and now there's a knife scar on you because of it."

She caught his wrist — the uninjured hand, quick as a trapline. Her grip was stronger than her arm suggested.

"Listen to me," she said. "Six years ago a man in a hearing room watched you testify with figures on your cuff, and he remembered it, and he remembered *you*, and tonight he chose a side. That's not your failure. That's the world showing you its shape." She released him. "I've spent five years believing that if I told no one anything, no one could hurt me. You know what it bought me? A leaking boat and a debt. Trust isn't the thing that got us cut, Tomas. Trust is the thing that got us *warned* — Iri heard the reeds stop, you heard the frogs, we were awake because we've been watching out for each other for three weeks. Calloway's men came expecting a crew of strangers who'd panic. They found a crew."

In the silence after that, Iri climbed out from under the wheelhouse floor, dusted rice-husk from his hair, and looked at the bloody scuppers, the ruined drums, the bandaged arm, with the unsentimental audit of the young.

"We can't use the fuel," he said.

"No," Tomas said.

"And they know we know, so they'll expect us to stay here and buy more tomorrow. Or try to." The boy crouched, traced a line in the spilled diesel film on the deck planking — the channel downstream, the gorge beyond. "So we go tonight. Now. Before they set the next net. There's a backwater past the depot bend — old logging cut, shallow, but the Kestrel draws light since you threw half your cargo overboard in the flood." He glanced up, and for a moment the cockiness fell away and he was just a kid who had hidden under a floorboard while men with knives crossed the deck above him. "I don't want to be on this boat when the next ones come. And there *will* be next ones."

Lena stood. She tested her arm, flexing the fingers, wincing once and putting the wince away like a tool she'd need later. She looked at the sabotaged drums, the dark channel, the depot lights — and then, Tomas saw, at the oilskin shape under her shirt, and something in her face closed around a decision.

"Cast off the stern line," she said. "Tomas, take the wheel. Iri, forward — call the logs. We run dark, no lamp, no pump noise until we're past the bend." She stepped to the helm beside him, close enough that he could see the bandage gleaming in the starlight, and her voice dropped to something meant only for him.

"And Tomas — when we're clear, I want you to draft a letter. To every honest man you ever knew in the Directorate. Weller remembered a hearing from six years ago. That means memory runs both ways. Somebody out there owes you the truth, and somebody out there can be trusted with it." She put her hand briefly on the wheel, over his. "Because we can't do this alone anymore. Tonight proved that trusting costs. But not trusting—" she nodded at the ruined fuel, the fleeing shadows, the whole treacherous glittering basin, "—costs more."

Tomas eased the throttle forward. The Kestrel slid off the mud shelf into the current, dark and silent, her patched hull whispering against the water, and behind them the depot lights burned on over an empty dock — and over Weller, Tomas was certain, already lifting a radio handset, already speaking their names into the wire, sending word downriver to a man with clean boots and a gunboat.

Let him send it, Tomas thought, as the bend swallowed them. Let Calloway chase a ghost up a flooded river with a crew that had already survived his best trick.

But in the pit of his stomach, where the paranoia had taken up permanent residence, a colder voice noted the arithmetic: they had no usable fuel, a wounded captain, a hundred miles of monsoon country ahead — and somewhere downstream, moving faster than any loaded boat could, the syndicate's answer was already coming up to meet them.
