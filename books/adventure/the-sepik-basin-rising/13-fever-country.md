## 13. Fever Country

The rain came down like the sky had given up holding itself together, and Iri learned that a riverboat could drown without ever leaving the water.

He stood in the stern of the *Kestrel* with a pole as long as he was tall, driving it down into water that should have been channel but was now just forest—trees standing to their waists, their roots drinking, their leaves hanging heavy and dripping. The flooded forest had no banks. It had no current you could trust. It was a drowned country where every direction looked like every other direction, and somewhere in it, Lena Amberdale lay below deck with her arm swollen twice its size and her mind gone walking somewhere none of them could follow.

"Left," Tomas called from the bow. "No—starboard. Starboard, Iri. There's a deadfall."

Iri saw it a heartbeat later: a great black trunk lying just under the surface, its branches fanned out like fingers reaching for the hull. He jammed the pole down, felt mud instead of gravel, and threw his whole weight against it. The *Kestrel* groaned, slid sideways, and scraped past with a sound like a fingernail drawn along wood.

"You're getting better at that," Tomas said.

"You're getting worse at shouting directions early enough."

Tomas almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. Nothing reached his eyes these days except worry, and most of that was aimed downward, through the deck planks, at the woman who had told them both exactly what to do before the fever took her voice away from her.

*Follow the old logging cut until the water goes brown,* she'd said, three days ago, propped against the wheelhouse door with her bandaged arm cradled like something broken off her own body. *Then west through the flooded pandanus—you'll know it because the trees grow in circles, like old men standing around a fire. Stay out of the main channel. Calloway will be watching the main channel. And whatever happens, don't stop moving. Fever doesn't kill fast, but stagnation does.*

She'd said it all in one breath, the way she said everything, as if words were cargo that had to be loaded quickly or not at all. Then she'd lain down on her bunk and hadn't properly risen since.

Iri poled. The rain hammered. Somewhere ahead, the brown water waited.

* * *

They made camp that night lashed to two ironwood trunks, the boat tied off so tight she couldn't roll even if the flood wanted her to. Tomas rigged a tarp over the aft deck and they ate cold rice and smoked fish that Iri had traded for at the last village, back when there had still been villages, back when the world still had edges.

"I'll sit with her first," Iri said.

Tomas looked up from the billy can he was pretending to clean. "You've sat with her every night for three days."

"So have you."

"That's different. I'm the adult."

"You're the drunk," Iri said, and then immediately wished he could pull the words back out of the air, because Tomas's face did something complicated—not anger, exactly, but an old wound flexing. "Sorry. That was—I didn't mean—"

"No." Tomas set the can down. "You meant it. And you're mostly right, which is the worst part." He rubbed his face with both hands, and in the lantern light Iri saw how gray he'd gone, how the stubble on his jaw had turned from black to salt-and-pepper in the space of a single monsoon. "Go on, then. Wake me if she gets worse. You know what worse looks like?"

"The talking," Iri said. "When she starts talking to people who aren't there."

"And if she stops talking altogether—"

"I wake you. I know."

Tomas hesitated at the companionway, one hand on the frame. "Iri. What she says down there—the things she says when she's burning up. None of us heard anything. Understood? Whatever comes out of that cabin stays in your head, and then it dies there."

"Why?" Iri asked, though he already knew. "Because it would shame her?"

"Because it isn't ours." Tomas went down into the dark.

* * *

The cabin smelled of rain-damp and the sharp herbal reek of the poultice Tomas kept changing—a paste of crushed leaves some woman at Ambunti had pressed into his hands along with instructions he'd repeated wrong twice. Lena lay on her bunk with the blanket kicked half off, her good arm flung above her head, her bad arm bound tight against her chest. Sweat had soaked her hair flat. In the small swinging light of the oil lamp, her face looked younger than Iri had ever seen it, and older too, both at once, the way a riverbed looks when the water drops and shows you everything it's been hiding.

"Lena," he said softly. "It's Iri. I'm going to change the cloth on your arm."

Her eyes opened. For a moment they were clear, and Iri's heart lifted stupidly—but no. They were clear the way glass is clear; there was nothing behind them looking out. She was seeing some other room, some other year.

"They asked for the ledger," she said. Her voice was cracked and small. "I told them I never saw any ledger. I told them I was only a clerk. I only filed things. I only—" A shudder went through her whole body. "Don't sign it. Don't sign the confession, whatever they do. If you sign it, it becomes true. If nobody signs it, it's only paper. It's only paper, Mama, it's only—"

Iri's hands had stopped moving on the bandage. He knelt there, frozen, watching a woman who never spoke of herself speak of nothing else, pouring out years through a crack the fever had split open in her.

"He took my name," she whispered. "That's the thing nobody understands. Not my freedom. My name. He wrote it down wrong on purpose, and now when I say it, it sounds like a lie even to me."

She turned her face toward him—or toward whoever stood in her fever-country—and her eyes focused for one terrible instant on Iri's face.

"Iri," she said. Actually him. Actually here. "Are we still moving?"

"Yes," he lied gently. "We're still moving."

"Good boy. Keep moving. Stagnation kills faster than fever." Her eyes drifted shut. "Don't let Tomas drink the medicine money. He'll tell you it's for charts. It's never for charts."

A ghost of a smile crossed her lips, and then she was under again, breathing fast and shallow, muttering names Iri didn't know—*Elias, Elias, hold on, hold on*—and names he did.

He finished the bandage with shaking hands. Then he climbed up into the rain and found Tomas under the tarp, and sat down across from him, and said nothing for a long time, because he was trying to decide whether a promise made to a man about a dying woman counted more than a promise made by a dying woman to a boy.

"She talked," Iri said finally.

Tomas closed his eyes. "Iri—"

"A clerk," Iri said. "She was a clerk. She found a ledger—Hessler's ledger, bribed verdicts, all written down neat as a shipping manifest. And they framed her for theft to shut her up, and she wouldn't sign the confession, so they signed it for her, or made it look like she had, I don't know, I couldn't follow all of it. But Tomas—" His voice caught. "She said he took her name. Not her freedom. Her name. She said when she says her own name now it sounds like a lie even to her."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the tarp like ten thousand small impatient fingers.

Tomas stared out at the drowned trees. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "I falsified nothing. That's the whole story of my ruin—one line I refused to draw on one map, and twenty years of work erased. I thought I understood what it costs, having your name dragged through the mud of someone else's making." He shook his head slowly. "But I got to keep my name. Everyone just decided it was worth less. Hers was taken outright and replaced with a fiction, and she's been living inside the fiction for years, piloting this boat, being nobody, because being nobody was safer than being the woman who saw the ledger." He looked at Iri. "And she pulled you out of the water anyway. Took on a stowaway with syndicate recruiters hunting him. Carried a dead man's promise upstream through a war zone. All while carrying that."

"She'd hate that we know," Iri said.

"She'd hate it more if we pretended we didn't." Tomas fed a splinter of dry wood to the spirit stove, and the small flame lit his face from below. "Here's what we do with it, then. We don't pity her—we'd hear about that for the rest of our lives. We get her to Karawari Bend. We get her arm healed. And when she's strong enough to be furious at us, we let her be furious, and then we help her take her name back. Because that ledger she found—it's still real. Paper doesn't burn just because the person who read it ran away. Somewhere in Hessler's office there is a book with her testimony in it, whether she signed it or not. And evidence, Iri, is like a seed in floodwater. It waits. It travels. It comes up where nobody planted it."

Iri thought about that. He was fourteen, and he had lost his village to men in clean boots, and he had learned that the world divided simply into the powerful and the swept-away. But Tomas talked about evidence like it was a living thing, and looking down through the deck at the woman underneath—small, burning, stubborn as a root in concrete—he thought maybe he believed it.

"Watch," he said. "When she wakes up, she won't thank us. She'll ask how much fuel we burned sitting anchored."

Tomas laughed—a short, surprised bark. "Yes. She will."

* * *

Two more days. Two more nights of poling through the circles of pandanus trees, exactly as she'd described them, old men standing around a fire, and Iri began to understand that Lena Amberdale carried maps in her head the way other people carried scars—every bend of this basin printed somewhere behind her eyes, laid down year by patient year while she was busy being nobody.

On the third morning the fever broke.

Iri knew it before Tomas did. He was below with the breakfast rice when Lena's breathing changed—slowed, deepened, settled into a rhythm instead of a scramble. Her eyelids fluttered. Her good hand came up and pressed flat against the bunk, testing, the way you test a deck in rough water.

"Tomas," Iri called up the companionway, and his voice came out strange and bright. "Tomas, she's surfacing."

They crowded into the little cabin, ridiculous, two grown men and a boy hovering over a sweating woman like herons over a fishhole. Lena's eyes opened all the way. They moved from Tomas to Iri and back, and Iri watched her do the arithmetic—how many days, how much water gained, how much fuel lost—behind her eyes before she'd spent a single word.

"How long," she rasped.

"Five days," Tomas said. "Fever peaked yesterday. Your arm's a horror, but the poultice is drawing it. You'll keep the arm. Whether you keep the temper remains to be seen."

"Fuel?"

"We poled most of it. Flooded forest, no current to fight, no fuel needed. We've maybe two days' worth left in the tank."

"Supplies?"

"Rice for six days if we're careful. Fish if Iri keeps being lucky."

Lena absorbed this. Then her gaze snagged on Iri and stayed there, and something flickered across her face—something quick and unreadable, like a fish turning under the surface. She knew. Iri watched her know. Five days of fever-talk, and she was lying there doing the same sum he had done: *what did I say, and who heard it.*

"Iri," she said.

"Yes, Captain."

"Did I say anything embarrassing?"

He held her gaze. Everything in him wanted to look away, and everything in him understood that looking away would be the one unforgivable thing. "You said to keep moving," he said. "Stagnation kills faster than fever. I remembered."

Something in her shoulders let go, a tension releasing by a single degree. It wasn't belief—he could see it wasn't belief—but it was permission, offered across a gap neither of them would name. *Not yet. Maybe not ever. But thank you.* She turned to Tomas.

"How far to Karawari Bend?"

"Three days, if the gorge Iri keeps mentioning actually exists."

"It exists," Iri said.

Lena pushed herself up on her good elbow, and even that small motion cost her—Iri saw the wince she refused to show—and looked at him with eyes that were still fever-bright at the edges but otherwise entirely, formidably her own.

"Then draw it for me," she said. "Every bend, every rock, every place the water eats the bank. Draw it now, before you eat, and if you leave anything out to make yourself look clever, I'll know."

Iri grinned and went for the chart paper, and behind him he heard Tomas say quietly, "Welcome back, Captain," and heard Lena answer, in a voice sanded thin but steady:

"Don't welcome me yet. Get me to that confluence. I have a name to collect."

Outside, the rain had softened to a steady whisper, and through the cabin's little porthole Iri could see the flooded forest opening ahead into a long brown corridor of water—and at the far end of it, faint as a pencil line, the dark notch of the gorge, waiting.
