## 14. The Medicine Woman of Ambunti

The fever broke on the third morning, or so Iri told me later. I remembered none of it—only fragments, the way a river remembers a flood in silt lines and broken branches rather than in the water itself.

I remembered heat. Not the wet heat of the basin, which sits on you like a second skin, but a dry, interior heat, as though someone had lit a fire behind my eyes and was feeding it page by page from the ledger of my life. I remembered Tomas's voice, low and steady, reading me river distances like scripture: *Ambunti by dark, Ambunti by dark.* And I remembered a woman's hands—strong, cool, smelling of crushed bark and woodsmoke—turning my ruined arm over as if it were something worth saving.

When I finally surfaced for good, it was to the smell of smoke and river mud and something bitter boiling in an iron pot, and to the sound of rain on thatch. Real rain, patient rain, the monsoon settling into its long argument with the earth. I lay on a woven mat in a stilt-house above brown water, my left arm bandaged from wrist to shoulder in strips of pale bark-cloth, and every heartbeat sent a dull, honest ache up through the wound instead of the bright, crawling fire that had been living there before.

"You are back," said a voice above me.

I turned my head. The woman sitting beside me was old in the way that river people are old—not gray so much as weathered, her face a map of channels and sandbars, her eyes the flat, unreadable brown of deep water. She wore a faded blouse and a skirt of woven fiber, and around her neck hung a small pouch of woven grass that she touched, absently, the way another woman might touch a crucifix.

"You cut deep," she said, "and you let it rot for two days. Another day and we would be talking to your spirit instead of your mouth." She lifted a clay cup from beside the fire pit. "Drink. It will taste like punishment. That is how you know it is working."

I drank. It tasted like punishment. I drank it all anyway.

"I'm—" I started, and stopped, because the name that rose to my lips was *Lena Amberdale*, and after Calloway's smile at the mission station, after he'd spoken my true name aloud like a man flipping over a stone to see what crawled beneath, I no longer knew which name was the lie.

The woman watched me struggle with it and almost smiled. "You do not have to give me any name," she said. "Names are like knives. Useful, but you should not hand them to strangers." She set down the cup. "I am called Serau. In this house, that is enough."

* * *

Ambunti was less a town than a rumor of one—a scatter of stilt-houses along a high bank where the Sepik widened into drowned grassland, canoes tied everywhere like moored thoughts. The trading post had burned years ago; its black skeleton still stood at the waterline, and children fished from its charred beams. From the veranda of Serau's house I could see the whole gray sweep of the flooded plain, the rain stitching itself into the river, and the Kestrel riding at anchor with Tomas aboard, keeping watch, refusing to come inside until I was well enough to argue with him about it.

He came twice a day anyway. He brought news the way a man brings water to a fire he cannot put out: in small, useless amounts. Syndicate boats had been seen heading downstream, not up—Calloway regrouping, perhaps, or hunting elsewhere. The fuel stores were beyond salvage; Tomas had traded our remaining tobacco for enough diesel to reach Karawari Bend, barely. Iri had made friends with half the village in three days and had eaten his way through most of their hospitality.

"And your arm?" Tomas asked on the fifth evening, standing at the foot of my mat with his hat in his hands, looking like a man at a funeral who isn't sure whose it is.

"Attached," I said. "Complaining."

"That's more than I can say for the rest of us."

He lingered. I knew that kind of lingering; I'd done it myself outside doors I was afraid to knock on. Finally he said, "Serau asked me what you did. Before. I told her I didn't know. She said—" he hesitated, turning his hat brim between his fingers— "she said that when you could sit up without the fever shaking you, she wanted the story. All of it. She said the medicine only finishes when the story is told."

"That sounds like something invented to make sick women talk."

"Probably," Tomas agreed. "But she also has a rifle over her door and everyone in this village does exactly what she says, so I'd lean toward believing her."

* * *

She came for me on the seventh night, after the rain had softened to a whisper and the river below the house had gone the color of hammered pewter. She built up the fire, sat down across from me with her knees drawn up, and waited. She was very good at waiting. I understood then that this was not a woman who asked questions; this was a woman who created silences large enough that other people fell into them.

"The boy says you were stabbed by men in a green boat," she said at last.

"They boarded us at a fuel depot. Four of them. They were looking for a map."

"And they found a knife instead."

"One of them did."

She nodded slowly, feeding a stick into the fire. "My brother was killed by men in a green boat," she said. "Eleven years ago. They came at night and they burned the mission storehouse where he kept his papers, and when he ran out through the back they were waiting in the reeds. They took the papers and they took him, and the river gave back neither."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't cheap.

"He was a clerk," Serau went on. "Not a government clerk—a church clerk, but it is the same disease. He wrote things down. Petitions. Complaints. Records of who signed what, who was paid, who was ordered off land that belonged to their grandmothers. He believed paper was stronger than men with guns." Her eyes stayed on the fire. "He learned otherwise. But before he learned it, he was careful. He copied everything. And he sent copies away, piece by piece, hidden in letters, in bales of copra, once inside a hollowed-out drum of communion wine. To people he trusted. One of those people was me."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the water a night bird called, three notes, like a question repeated.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because the boy says the men in the green boat want a map, and because your surveyor drinks like a man who was ruined by the same government that ruined my brother, and because you flinched—I saw it, the first night, when I touched your wrist and said the word *magistrate*. You flinched like a dog that has been kicked by a particular boot." She looked at me then, directly, and her eyes were not flat at all; they were very deep. "So. You tell me your story, captain. All of it. And then I will decide whether my brother's papers belong in your hands."

* * *

I hadn't told the whole story to anyone. Not to Tomas, not to Iri, not to the harbor bosses and dock clerks and customs men who had accepted *Lena Amberdale* for six years without ever asking where she'd come from. Telling it felt like opening a hull seam underwater—you know the water is coming in, you just don't know how fast.

But the fever had already cracked me open. What was one more seam?

"My name is Lena Voss—no relation to Elias, before you ask," I said, and heard Tomas, who had appeared silently at the doorway with Iri behind him, go very still. "Before Amberdale, before the river, I was a records clerk in the district office at Timunke. Land titles, court filings, shipping manifests. Boring work. Honest work. I was good at it because I liked order, because I liked knowing that the paper matched the world."

"Hessler," Serau said. Not a question.

"Magistrate Hessler." Saying the name aloud, in a house where it meant a murdered brother, changed its weight entirely. "I processed his court filings for three years. Verdicts, fines, land seizures. And then one quarter-end I was reconciling the fee ledgers against the case registers, and the numbers didn't match. Payments recorded for verdicts that had never been entered. Fines waived in exchange for fees paid to a private account. A whole shadow ledger running underneath the official one—mining companies buying verdicts the way you'd buy cargo. Hessler's signature, or his initials, on forty-one separate transactions going back four years."

"And you reported it."

"I was twenty-three and I believed in paper." I laughed, and it hurt my ribs. "I made a copy of the ledger first—I wasn't entirely stupid—and I took it to the district commissioner. Who thanked me, praised my diligence, and handed the ledger straight back to Hessler, because the commissioner's own brother-in-law held the mining supply contracts. Within a week I was charged with embezzlement. Funds missing from the records office. My own access logs, altered, used as evidence. Two witnesses testified—they were clerks under Hessler, they testified to entries they must have known were false, and their testimony carried the trial. Convicted. Stripped of posting. Sentenced to labor transport upriver, which is where I jumped ship at Angoram and became nobody."

"The witnesses," Serau said quietly. "Their names."

"Rukas Beni. And a woman called Odile Mambu. I've thought about them for six years. Whether they were threatened or bought or both. Whether they ever lost a night's sleep over me." I looked into the fire. "Whether they're even alive."

Serau rose without a word, crossed the room, and took down from a rafter a small wooden chest bound in rattan. From the chest she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth—oilcloth again, always oilcloth in this country, oilcloth and secrets—and unwrapped it on the mat between us.

Papers. Dozens of them, yellowed, soft at the folds, some written in a fine clerk's hand I recognized instantly because it was the same training mine had been: copies of court filings, fee ledgers, correspondence. And clipped to the front, a single sheet in larger writing, a list.

"Eleven years ago my brother smuggled these out," Serau said. "He knew Hessler would come for him eventually, so he scattered copies the way a man scatters seed. This list is his own making—the names of every witness, clerk, and officer who helped Hessler bury a verdict, with dates and case numbers. Rukas Beni is on it. So is Odile Mambu." Her finger stopped on a line near the bottom. "And here is what happened to them. Beni—dismissed from service, eight months later, cause unstated. Last seen taking contract work at the syndicate camp at Yuat Junction. Mambu—transferred to a station upriver, resigned within the year. My brother's last letter says she went to live with her sister's family near Pagwi, and that she wrote to him once, full of shame, asking whether the woman he'd spoken of—the young clerk who was convicted—had survived."

The house was very quiet. Even the rain seemed to be listening.

"She's alive," I said. My voice came out strange, thin. "Odile Mambu might be alive."

"She may be. People move. Rivers change course. But a thread exists." Serau pressed the bundle into my hands, and her grip was strong despite her age, strong as the hands that had pulled the poison out of my arm. "For eleven years I have kept these papers the way you keep a wound clean—wrapped, hidden, waiting. I am too old to carry them anywhere, and too known in this river to pass a syndicate checkpoint safely. But you—" she looked at my bandaged arm, then at my face— "you are already carrying a dead man's promise upstream. What is one more weight?"

"It's not a favor I can repay."

"No," she agreed. "It is not a favor. It is a debt being transferred, the way debts travel on this river, from the dead to the living. My brother could not finish his work. Perhaps you can. Or perhaps you will die in the gorge with the papers soaked through, and I will have been a fool. Old women are allowed to gamble."

Tomas came forward slowly and crouched beside the papers, and I watched his face as he read—watched the surveyor's precision war with the man's grief. "Case numbers, signatures, dates," he murmured. "This isn't testimony, Lena. This is documentation. Cross-referenced against the map, against what Mara knows—" He stopped himself, glancing at Serau, and lowered his voice. "This changes what we're carrying."

"It changes everything," I said.

Iri, who had been hovering in the doorway radiating the patience of the young, which is to say none at all, finally blurted: "Does this mean we're not just delivering gold anymore?"

I looked at the papers in my lap—at my own conviction, in effect, lying there in another man's handwriting, the witnesses named, the machinery of the frame exposed—and felt something shift in my chest that had nothing to do with fever. For six years I had thought of myself as a woman hiding from a lie. But a lie needs only silence to survive, and I was holding eleven years of noise.

"We were never delivering gold," I said. "We just didn't know it yet."

* * *

We left Ambunti two days later, on a gray morning when the rain had thinned to mist and the flooded plain stretched silver to the horizon. Serau stood on her veranda as we cast off, small and upright against the thatch, one hand resting on the rail.

I had repacked her brother's papers myself, in fresh oilcloth, wrapped in waxed canvas, and stowed them inside the false bottom of the chart drawer where Elias's map had ridden since the night he died. Two cargoes now. Two promises. The Kestrel rode lower in the water than her papers claimed, and so, I thought, did I.

Tomas took the wheel. Iri scrambled along the gunwale coiling lines with the exaggerated competence of a boy performing for an audience. The current took us, and Ambunti slid astern, and the channel narrowed ahead toward the highlands, toward Karawari Bend, toward whatever Mara Voss would make of the father's map and the mother-country of secrets we were bringing her.

"Course?" Tomas asked.

I checked the chart, then looked up at the water itself—the color of it, the drift of the debris, the way the current leaned. Somewhere upriver, Odile Mambu might be washing dishes or mending nets, not knowing that her shame had a witness, that her silence had an expiration date now. Somewhere upriver, Calloway's green boats were hunting. Somewhere upriver, the gorge waited.

"Steer for the far channel past the burned post," I said. "And Tomas—when we make Karawari Bend, the first person Mara Voss meets is going to be Odile Mambu's ghost. Make sure she's ready for that."

He glanced at me, questions forming and dying behind his eyes, and rang up full ahead. The Kestrel leaned into the current, patched and leaking and stubborn as her captain, and carried us north into the narrowing river, into the country where the truth we carried grew heavier with every mile—and where, though none of us could know it yet, the first outriders of the gathering storm were already waiting at the bend.
