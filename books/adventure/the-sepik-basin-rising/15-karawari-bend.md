## 15. Karawari Bend

The river changed its mind about being a river two days out of Ambunti.

Lena felt it in the wheel before she saw it in the water—the Kestrel's rudder going light, the current slackening as the channel braided into a dozen shallow threads fanning across a gravel plain. Ahead, the land lifted. Not mountains yet, not the true highlands, but foothills rising blue and folded against a sky that had finally stopped raining, as if the whole country were catching its breath after three weeks of monsoon.

"Karawari Bend," Tomas said, coming up beside her with the chart rolled under his arm like a man carrying a grudge. He pointed with his chin at the confluence where the Karawari spilled down from the hills and married the Sepik's brown water in a long, churning seam of green and gold. "My old agency had this on file as a Class Four settlement. Trading post, mission school, one government rest house." He paused. "That was six years ago."

"It's grown," Iri said from the bow, where he'd spent most of the morning standing like a figurehead, one hand shading his eyes.

It had grown. The settlement climbed the riverbank in terraces—stilt houses with iron roofs gone orange with rust, gardens cut into the slope in neat green squares, laundry strung between posts like signal flags. And below it all, along a stretch of shingle beach that served as a landing, maybe thirty boats lay pulled up on rollers: dugouts, trading punts, a couple of flat-bottomed freight barges with their gunwales scarred by chains.

And people. Lena counted them without meaning to—an old habit, counting exits, counting hands, counting who stood where. Dock workers hauling crates. Women smoking fish over racks. Children racing each other along the waterline. And moving through all of it, in ones and twos, men with the particular hollowed-out look she'd learned to recognize somewhere around chapter four of her own life: men who walked like they were still carrying loads too heavy to set down. Mine laborers. Runaways. Men whose hands were stained yellow to the wrist with something that wasn't river mud.

"Cyanide," Tomas said quietly, following her gaze. "The syndicate's processing camps use it downstream of the claims. You can always tell the ones who worked the sluice lines."

The Kestrel slid into the landing with her usual gracelessness, hull groaning, and Lena threw the line to a boy on the shingle who caught it and hauled with the easy strength of someone who'd been mooring boats since before he could swim. The moment her boots hit the beach, she felt the settlement notice her—not hostile, not welcoming, just noticing, the way a body notices a change in weather.

A woman detached herself from the fish racks and came down the beach at a walk that was not quite hurried and not quite casual, and Lena understood immediately that everything else in Karawari Bend was decoration and this woman was the load-bearing wall.

She was perhaps forty, broad-shouldered, with a face that might once have been open and generous and had since been closed like a ledger after an audit. Her hair was cropped short. She wore trousers, boots, and a canvas jacket with the sleeves rolled over forearms corded with rope-work muscle, and she carried a machete at her hip the way other women carried handbags. When she reached them she stopped three paces off—the exact distance, Lena noted, at which a machete becomes relevant—and looked them over: Lena first, longest; then Tomas; then Iri, with something in her eyes that softened for exactly half a second before the ledger closed again.

"You're flying no colors," the woman said. "No trade flag, no mission pennant, no district stamp." Her voice was low and even, a voice that had learned not to waste volume. "So either you're smugglers, which is fine, or you're government, which is worse, or you're looking for me specifically, which is worst of all."

"We're looking for Mara Voss," Lena said.

Something moved behind the woman's eyes—a shutter swinging, a weight shifting onto a different foot. "Who sent you?"

"A dying man," Lena said. "On the Sepik, five weeks ago. Half-drowned, feverish, shot through the shoulder. He paid me to bring something upstream to his daughter in Karawari Bend."

The silence stretched. Down the beach, a boat crew began drumming cargo barrels ashore, and the sound rolled across the shingle like distant artillery. The woman's hand had drifted, very slightly, toward the machete.

"He wouldn't have paid," she said at last. "He didn't believe in paying for what should be given freely, and he didn't believe anything should be given freely. So either you're lying, or—" She stopped. Lena watched her do the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the word *dying*. "Or he's dead."

"I'm sorry," Lena said. "He died at dawn. Three days after I pulled him from the reeds. He talked some, when the fever let him. He was proud of you. He said you were the only thing he ever finished properly."

Mara Voss turned away. It was not dramatic; that was the terrible part of it. She simply looked out at the river for a long moment, jaw working, and when she turned back her face had been reorganized into something functional. Grief filed away for later, in a drawer that Lena suspected did not fully close.

"Come up to the station," Mara said. "All three of you. And whatever you're carrying for me, keep your hand away from it until we're behind my door. There are men in this settlement who count strangers' packages with their eyes."

* * *

The waystation sat halfway up the second terrace, a long building of bush timber and corrugated iron that had clearly begun life as a warehouse and been argued into becoming a home. Lena read it the way she read any vessel: by its modifications. Extra bunks partitioned off behind canvas. A cook shack tacked onto the side. A back door that opened onto a footpath heading uphill into the trees, worn deep by feet—worn deep enough to be an escape route rather than a garden path. A place built by someone who expected trouble and planned to be leaving through the rear when it arrived.

Inside, it smelled of woodsmoke, tar, and rice. Six men sat at a long table eating in silence, and every one of them had the yellow-stained hands. They looked up when Mara entered, took the measure of the strangers, and went back to their food—but Lena noticed that none of them truly relaxed, and that two of them shifted so their backs were to the wall.

Mara led them through to a small room at the far end, shut the door, and lit a lamp even though daylight came gray through the shutters. Then she held out her hand, palm up.

"The map."

Lena didn't move. Five weeks of instinct said *not yet*, and five weeks of instinct had kept her alive. "How do I know you're Mara Voss?"

"How do I know you're not Calloway's?" Mara countered. "Because if you are, this is the most elaborate production I've seen, and I've seen them dress a spy as a leper to get inside a mission hospital."

"Calloway," Lena repeated, carefully flat.

"Don't." Mara's mouth twisted. "Don't do the innocent face. Mr. Calloway of the Meridian Consolidated Mining Company has been trying to find my father for two years. He sends men through here every few months, polite as tax collectors, asking after a prospector named Voss, showing a photograph that's ten years out of date. Last time, they offered me money for information. The time before, they offered me money and a warning." She stepped closer. The lamplight caught old scars on her forearm—burn scars, Lena thought, or rope burns, and decided it didn't matter which. "So you'll understand why a stranger walking off a boat saying my father's name makes me want to check your teeth like a horse at market."

Tomas cleared his throat. "I'm Tomas Reyes. Formerly of the District Survey Office. Your father's map—it's real. I've seen it. The terrain work is extraordinary. Whoever drew it knew that country better than the government does."

"My father surveyed for the syndicate for eleven years," Mara said. "Before he understood what he was helping them do. Before he understood what *I* was doing for them, either." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it heavier. "You want to know why I run a waystation full of men with poisoned hands? Because I recruited half of them. I was a labor agent for Meridian Consolidated when I was nineteen years old. I rode up and down these rivers signing contracts with boys who couldn't read them, promising wages and rations, and delivering them to camps where the wage was company scrip, the ration was rotten rice, and the exit was a river full of mercury." She laughed, short and bitter. "My father found out what I'd become before I did. That was the last real conversation we had. Eleven years ago."

"Mara—" Lena started.

"No." Mara raised a hand. "You don't get to soften it. You brought me a package and a dead man's regrets, and I'm telling you what you've actually delivered them to. So." She held out her hand again. "The map. Or tell me why I shouldn't take it."

Lena looked at her. She thought about Elias Voss in the fever-dark of her cabin, gripping her wrist with a strength that fever should have burned away, saying *she'll know what to do with it, she's the only one who'll know.* She thought about how little that promise had asked of her, and how much it had cost already, and how the cost was still climbing.

And she thought—coldly, honestly—that Mara Voss was exactly right to distrust her. Because Lena Amberdale was not a name, it was a shell, and shells exist to hide things, and the thing this particular shell hid was a woman who had once trusted a magistrate's signature because it looked official.

"Elias said you'd know what to do with it," Lena said. "Not what you'd want to hear. What to *do*. He said the seam isn't what people think it is. He said—" she searched the delirium-fragments, laying them out like salvaged cargo— "he said he mapped it twice. Once for the company, and once for the truth. He said the coordinates matter more than the gold."

For the first time, something cracked in Mara's composure. It was small—a breath taken through the nose, a blink held a moment too long—but Lena, who had spent years reading water and men, read it plainly.

"Twice," Mara whispered. "He used to say that. *Survey it twice, girl. Once for the paymaster, once for God.*" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she lowered it, the decision had been made, though her voice hadn't caught up. "Show me."

Lena untied the oilskin packet from inside her shirt, where it had lived against her skin for five weeks, warm as a second heartbeat. She unwrapped it on the table: the map itself, drawn in Elias Voss's precise surveyor's hand, annotated in margins that grew denser toward the upper edge, and beneath it, folded, the pages of field notes.

Mara bent over the map and did not touch it. She read it the way Tomas read water—whole, at a glance, topography as sentence. Her finger hovered above the coordinates without landing, tracing the route upstream through the gorge country to a valley marked with a symbol Lena didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a wavy line.

"That's not a claim marker," Tomas said quietly. "That's a hydrological notation. Water source. He marked the seam's location relative to the watershed."

"Yes," Mara said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "He did." She straightened up and looked at Lena, and for the first time there was something in her face besides the ledger—something raw and young and frightened. "Do you know what's at those coordinates? Do you know what my father actually found?"

"Gold," Lena said. "An unclaimed seam. He told me it was worth more than anything in the basin."

Mara shook her head slowly. "My father didn't come home for eleven years because he was ashamed of what he'd surveyed for them. And now he sends me a map worth a fortune, and tells a stranger I'll know what to do with it." She laid her palm flat on the oilskin, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure wouldn't bite. "Captain, I don't trust this delivery. I don't trust you—I don't know you, and everyone I've ever known has sold something. But I trust *him*, and he trusted you, and that's a problem for both of us."

Outside, somewhere down the terrace, a dog began barking—not the idle barking of dogs everywhere, but the sharp, rhythmic alarm of a dog that has seen something specific. One of the men at the table rose without hurry and went to the window.

Mara's head came up. In one motion the grief and the rawness vanished, packed away with a speed that told Lena everything about how often she needed to pack them away.

"Two men just came off the launch at the landing," the man at the window reported. "Clean shirts. Boots. One's carrying a district folder."

Mara looked at Lena, and the look was an entire conversation: *you brought this here.*

"They followed us," Tomas breathed. "They couldn't have—we ran dark through the Bakari—"

"They didn't follow you," Mara said grimly, already moving to the back door, throwing it open onto the escape path worn deep in the hillside. "They were already coming. My father's dead, the map's surfaced, and Hessler's hounds have picked up the scent." She jerked her chin at the door. "Out the back, all of you. There's a trail over the ridge. We'll talk about what my father really found when we're breathing air that isn't full of clean-booted liars."

Lena wrapped the map back in its oilskin and tied it against her body, and followed the daughter of the mapmaker up into the highlands, while below them, unhurried, the men in clean boots started up the stairs to the waystation door.
