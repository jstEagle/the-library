## 16. Daughter of the Mapmaker

Mara Voss had learned to read people the way her father had taught her to read contour lines: by what they concealed. A steep face hid its gradient behind a smooth silhouette. A friendly man hid his price behind a smile. So when the riverboat woman stood in the doorway of the waystation with rain streaming off her oilskin and a wrapped bundle held out like an offering, Mara did not see a messenger. She saw a courier carrying something dangerous, and couriers, in her experience, always wanted paying.

"Your father sent me," the woman said. "Elias Voss."

The name went through Mara like a spear through shallow water—fast, clean, and then the wound opened all at once. She kept her hands busy, unhooking the lantern from its bracket, because hands that stopped moving started shaking.

"Elias Voss died four years ago," she said. "Officially. There was a notice in the Ambunti gazette. Drowned, it said. Very tidy."

"He wasn't drowned. He was dying. Fever, and something worse eating at him from the inside." The woman set the bundle on the scarred plank table without being asked, which Mara noted—people who waited for permission were usually performing respect. This one simply acted, the way someone acts when they've carried a thing too long and their arms are tired. "He made me promise to bring you this."

Mara didn't touch it. "And what did he pay you?"

The woman's jaw tightened. "Nothing. He paid me nothing. He paid my debt, if you want the truth of it, and then he died on my deck at dawn, and I've been running upstream from men in clean boots ever since." She wiped rain from her face with the back of her wrist. "My name is Lena Amberdale. That's Tomas Reyes at the door, and the boy is Iri. We've lost a boat's worth of cargo, most of our fuel, and I've got a knife hole in my arm that a healer in Ambunti sewed shut twice. I'm not here to sell you anything, Miss Voss. I'm here because a dying man asked."

*Miss Voss.* Nobody had called her that since she'd stopped being anyone's daughter and started being the woman at the waystation who could get a laborer over the ridge for three kina and a lie about his destination. It sat strangely on her, like a coat cut for a smaller body.

She lit the lantern properly, hung it back up, and finally looked at the bundle. Oilskin, tied with waxed cord, the knots done in her father's style—two loops and a hitch, left-handed, a habit from thirty years of keeping maps dry in dugout canoes. Her chest did something painful.

"You said he died at dawn," she said.

"On the eleventh. I buried him on a sandbar above Bakari. Marked it with his pan."

Mara closed her eyes. Four years of believing the worst had prepared her for grief as an old, settled thing, like silt. This was different. This was grief arriving late and finding everything rearranged. She breathed once, twice, and when she trusted her voice she said, "Sit down, Captain Amberdale. All of you. There's tea, and there's dried fish, and neither of them is good, but you look like people who've stopped being particular."

* * *

They ate in the waystation's back room, where the walls were papered with old survey sheets Mara had salvaged from syndicate burn piles—her own small archive of things the company wanted forgotten. Iri fell asleep sitting up within ten minutes, his head against a crate of trade tobacco, and Lena lifted him onto a mat without waking him, moving with a gentleness that didn't match her voice at all. Mara filed that away too.

Then Lena untied the knots—two loops and a hitch—and unrolled the oilskin.

Her father's hand. She knew it before she understood a single line: the fine, patient drafting, the little marginal notes in pencil gone soft with damp, the way he drew water as a series of living lines rather than a flat blue shape. The map showed the upper tributaries past Karawari Bend, into country marked on no government chart she'd ever seen. And there, boxed in red ink that had faded to rust, a set of coordinates and a seam line labeled in his cramped capitals: *AURIFEROUS SHALE — EXTENT UNKNOWN — SAMPLES ATTACHED.*

"There were samples," Lena said. "In a tin. They're yours too."

Mara barely heard her. She was tracing the coordinates with one finger, and the finger had begun, very slightly, to tremble—not with greed, she realized, but with recognition. Because she knew those numbers. She had seen them before, written in a different hand, in a document she'd spent two years trying to forget.

"Tomas," she said. "You're the surveyor. Come here."

Tomas Reyes leaned over the map, and she watched him do what surveyors do: orient, verify, cross-reference. His eyes went to the coordinates, then to the wall of salvaged sheets behind her, then back. "These are government datum points," he said slowly. "Pre-war triangulation. Whoever drafted this used the old base stations." He looked up at her. "This is professional work. Better than professional."

"It's my father's work," Mara said. "And these coordinates—" She crossed to the wall, pulled down a sheet she had taken from a burning pile outside Timbunke three years ago, and spread it beside the map. A syndicate land-claim survey, stamped and countersigned. She put her finger on the claim block's corner marker. Then on her father's red box.

They were the same ground.

"That's not possible," Tomas said. "The syndicate claims are all registered. If Voss mapped the same block—"

"The syndicate didn't register a discovery," Mara said. "They registered a *suppression*." She pulled down another sheet, and another, laying them across the table until the wood disappeared under overlapping paper: claim blocks, water-quality reports, a medical census from a mission clinic, and last, carefully flattened, a page she had copied out by hand because the original had been too fragile to handle. A witness list. Seven names. Six of them struck through in pencil.

Lena had gone very still. "What am I looking at?"

"You're looking at why my father died," Mara said. "Or why he had to stop being alive." She sat down, because this story was long and she had learned to tell it standing only to people who already believed it. "Everyone thinks Elias Voss was a prospector. He was, at the start—a good one, the kind who could smell color in gravel. But my father got curious the way other men get thirsty. Twenty years ago he was doing contract sampling for the colonial administration, before the syndicate bought the administration. And he found gold, yes. Up past the third gorge, exactly where that box sits. But he found something else in the same samples."

She turned the water-quality report toward them. Arsenic figures, mercury figures, numbers so far beyond safe that they read like errors until you noticed they were consistent across six years of testing.

"Cyanide tailings," Tomas said quietly. "From test leaching. Someone ran industrial extraction up there and never contained it."

"The syndicate did. Trial works, unregistered, around the time Hessler first took office. My father traced the drainage—the creek that runs off that seam feeds the whole middle watershed. Every village downstream of the gorge has been drinking it for twenty years. The fish die-offs, the skin sickness at Wagu, the stillbirths the mission recorded and couldn't explain." Her voice stayed level; she had practiced that. "It wasn't a discovery. It was a crime scene with gold in it."

"And the massacre," Lena said. Not a question.

Mara looked at her. "How do you know about a massacre?"

"A village elder told us. Men from Hessler burned a village years ago to hide something. Tomas thought it was connected to the seam."

"It was the village at the mouth of the sample creek. Yambun." The name still tasted of smoke when she said it, though she'd never been there; she'd only read the ash of it in documents. "The elders refused to move off the watershed. They'd watched their children sicken and they knew where the sickness came from, and my father had shown them the tests—he was honest, that was his disease, he couldn't stop showing people the truth even when the truth was going to get him killed. So the elders started talking. To missionaries, to a visiting magistrate from another district, to anyone who might carry it further than the river carried water."

She touched the witness list. "Seven people agreed to testify. My father collected their statements himself, thinking a proper legal process would fix it—naive, brave man. Before any testimony reached a court, Yambun burned. Forty-one people. The official record says intertribal raiding. The seven witnesses scattered or died—one drowned, one was arrested for theft he didn't commit and transported, one simply vanished between two villages on a straight stretch of river." She glanced up, and found Lena Amberdale watching her with an expression like a door opening onto a dark room. "The seventh was a young clerk in the district registry office. A woman. She'd found something in the ledgers—Hessler's ledgers—and she was asking questions about the Yambun payments."

The silence in the room had weight, like deep water.

"They framed her for theft," Lena said. Her voice was very quiet. "Falsified an audit. Convicted her on testimony from a clerk who owed Hessler money. Transported her downriver." She looked at her own bandaged arm as if surprised by it. "That was me. That was my name, before I bought a new one and a leaky boat."

Mara had suspected—had seen it building since the woman walked in, the way certain details align for a surveyor—but hearing it spoken aloud still landed hard. "Then you're not a courier," she said. "You're the eighth witness."

"I'm nobody. I'm a boat captain."

"You're a woman whose name is in my father's papers." Mara pulled the last item from the oilskin's fold: a thin sheaf beneath the map, pages in Elias Voss's hand, and held it out. "Read the last page."

Lena read. Her lips moved slightly. Then she set the page down flat, pressed both palms against the table, and stood there breathing, and Mara gave her the courtesy of pretending to study the map while the boat captain put herself back together.

When Lena spoke again, her voice was rough but steady. "He wrote that the clerk's conviction was ordered the same week the Yambun fire report was signed. Same ink, nearly the same date. He compared the ledgers."

"My father spent four years after the fire collecting proof instead of gold. Samples, statements, copies of ledgers smuggled out by a mission brother. Then Hessler's men came for him, and he let them believe they'd succeeded. He let the gazette print that he drowned." Mara allowed herself the smallest bitter smile. "He was better at disappearing than I am. I inherited the hiding and he kept the fighting. Until the fever caught him anyway, and he decided the map should go to me, and he gambled everything on a stranger with a boat."

"He won the bet," Lena said.

"Did he?" Mara stood, and began, deliberately, to roll the map closed. "Because here's what happens next, Captain. You take this to the seam. You stake it, or you sell it, or you trade it to whoever scares you least. The syndicate gets a claim, or some new syndicate does, and the watershed keeps drinking poison, and forty-one people stay a rounding error in an intertribal raid. My father's life's work becomes somebody's fortune." She tied the cord—two loops and a hitch—and set the roll on the table between them like a blade laid flat. "I'm not letting that happen."

"So don't," Tomas said. "Come with us."

"I intend to. On my terms." Mara met Lena's eyes and held them, the way you hold a bearing. "I'll guide you to the seam. I know the ridgelines better than any map, including his. But we go for one purpose: to finish the documentation. Photograph the tailings, the dead creek, the trial works. Copy the map, cache the originals somewhere Calloway can't burn them. The evidence goes to the confluence gathering—all of it, the massacre, the poisoning, Hessler's ledgers, and your conviction, Captain, because your name is part of the record whether you like it or not. And when it's done, nobody profits. Not the syndicate. Not us. The seam stays unclaimed until the valley decides what it wants done with it. Those are my terms. Swear them or walk out that door."

She expected argument. She expected the boat captain's caution, the survivor's arithmetic that had kept Lena Amberdale invisible for years. Instead Lena looked at the map roll for a long moment, then at the wall of salvaged surveys, then at the sleeping boy on the mat.

"You sound like him," Lena said at last. "Your father. He made me swear a promise too. Seems to be how Vosses do business." She extended her hand across the table. "Justice, not gold. I swore an oath on a sandbar to a dying man. One more won't kill me."

Mara shook it. The hand was calloused, fever-warm, and did not flinch.

* * *

They left before first light, because Mara had learned that dawn belonged to her and not to the syndicate's spies. The waystation's regulars would cover for her absence with the usual stories—that she'd gone to Timbunke for supplies, that she was sick, that she'd taken a lover downriver, whatever version each gossip preferred. She packed light: instruments, film for the camera Tomas had produced from his kit with the sheepish air of a man revealing a vice, dried food, and her father's field notebook, the one thing she'd never let out of her sight in four years.

At the door she paused and looked back at the room where she had lived half a life—half a name, half a purpose, waiting without admitting she was waiting. Her father had drawn maps of country no chart acknowledged. She understood now that he hadn't been hiding the seam. He'd been leaving a trail, and it had taken a stranger with a leaky boat to follow it home.

Iri appeared at her elbow, pack already on, eyes bright with the particular terror and joy of a boy who has chosen his road. "The gorge path," he said. "Through the Echoes. It saves four days, maybe five, with the monsoon flooding the low track."

"You've walked it?" Tomas asked.

"I was born walking it." The boy grinned. "But nobody walks it in monsoon. That's why nobody will be following us."

Mara looked at the eastern sky, where the clouds had organized themselves into something vast and patient, and felt—for the first time in four years—not like a woman preserving the past, but like a surveyor closing a traverse: every bearing finally agreeing, the error of closure small enough to trust.

"Then we walk it in monsoon," she said.

Behind them, far down the valley, thunder answered—or perhaps it was only the river, remembering.
