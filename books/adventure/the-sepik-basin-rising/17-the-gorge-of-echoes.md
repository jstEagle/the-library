## 17. The Gorge of Echoes

*Iri*

The gorge did not have a name on any map Tomas owned, which was the whole point of it.

Iri stood at the bow of the Kestrel in the gray half-light before dawn and pointed with his chin, the way his grandfather had taught him—never your finger, boy, a finger points at what you want; a chin shows the way you know. "There. Where the water goes quiet between the two leaning trees."

Lena squinted through the drizzle. "That's not quiet. That's a lie the river tells before it drops."

"It's the door," Iri said. "My grandfather poled dugouts through it when he ran salt to the highlands. The soldiers never found it because they look for big water. This is small water that becomes big water fast."

Tomas had the survey charts spread under an oilcloth on the cargo deck, weighted with tin cups, and he kept glancing from them to the narrow slot in the cliff face as if the paper might confess something the rock would not. "There's no contour here," he said. "No sounding. Nothing."

"That's why it's still ours," Iri said.

Mara crouched beside him at the rail. She'd been doing that more lately—crouching down to his level, asking questions like his answers were worth the asking. "How deep is the channel at the entrance?"

"Deep enough for the Kestrel if we take everything off the deck and Lena doesn't fight the current." He looked up at her. "You have to let the gorge carry you. Everyone who dies in there is fighting."

Lena was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the wheelhouse roof. Behind them, downstream, the monsoon had swallowed the main channel whole—the Bakari running brown and insane, carrying whole trees like matchsticks—and Calloway's gunboat was somewhere inside all that gray violence, hunting them by fuel smell and rumor. Iri had heard the engine twice in the night, a low animal grumble through the rain, and both times Lena had killed their own lamp and let the Kestrel drift like a log until it passed.

"Small water that becomes big water fast," Lena repeated. She said it the way she read currents, tasting each word. Then she nodded once. "Strip the deck. Everything lashed low. Tomas, I want your weight aft—I need you reading the drop when we're committed. Mara, you're on bailer. Iri—" she put her hand on his shoulder, and it was heavy and warm and it made something in his chest go strange and bright, "—you're my eyes. You stand where I can see you and you tell me true, even when telling me true means shouting at me."

"I can shout," Iri said.

"I know you can. You've been shouting at me since Ambunti."

They worked in the rain without much talk, the four of them moving around each other in the old rhythm of people who have been cold and wet and afraid together long enough to stop pretending otherwise. The crates of evidence went into the hold, wrapped in oilskin, double-lashed. The last of the fuel drums. Mara's satchel of copied documents, which she wore across her body like a bandolier and refused to set down even to bail.

Before they cast off, Iri did what his grandfather would have done. He took a pinch of ash from the cookstove and scattered it on the water at the bow, and he said the words quietly, in the language of his village, so that only the river would hear them: *We are strangers. We come through your house. We will not take more than passage.*

"What did you say?" Mara asked.

"That we'll be polite," Iri said.

* * *

*Lena*

The entrance took the Kestrel the way a hand closes around a fish.

One moment they were in open flooded forest, poling through drowned saplings, and the next the cliffs rose on either side—black limestone streaked white with bird lime, close enough that Lena could have touched both walls with the boat hooks—and the current seized them and there was no more steering, only choosing among the choices the water allowed.

"Left!" Iri shouted, planted at the bow with one hand on the forestay, hair plastered to his skull. "Hard left past the fallen tree—then right, RIGHT, the eddy behind the rock—"

Lena hauled the wheel. The Kestrel groaned, swung, and shot through a gap that could not possibly have been wide enough, and then they were in the second chamber and the roar of the water changed pitch, dropping away below them into a sound like the gorge swallowing.

"Tomas?" she called.

"Drop of maybe three meters over fifty," Tomas called back from the stern, flat and professional, which was how she knew he was terrified. "It's runnable. Keep her square. Whatever you do, don't let her broach sideways."

"Square," Lena said. "Square. Iri!"

"Through the middle! The middle looks worst but the rocks under the white are rounded—my grandfather said the river smooths what it uses!"

She trusted him. That was the astonishing thing, standing at the wheel with the rain like nails against her face and the whole gorge echoing with the drumroll of the flood—that she trusted a fourteen-year-old orphan's dead grandfather more than she trusted her own thirty years on this river. But the old man had been right about everything else. She drove the Kestrel straight at the ugliest water she'd ever seen and felt the hull lift, hang, and slam down the drop, and the world became white noise and green water over the rails and Mara screaming something that might have been a prayer or a curse, and then—

Then they were through. Drifting in a long dark pool between walls so high the sky was a ragged silver ribbon overhead. The echo came back off the stone in layers, their own engine noise chasing itself, and Iri turned around at the bow with rain and river spray streaming off him and grinned so wide it looked like it might split his face.

"My grandfather's channel," he said. "Nobody's used it since he died. Until today."

Lena laughed—a short, cracked sound she didn't recognize as coming out of her own throat. "Tell your grandfather thank you."

"He knows," Iri said simply. "He's listening. That's why they call it the Gorge of Echoes. Everything you say in here, somebody hears it."

And that was when the echo brought back a sound none of them had made.

An engine. Deep, diesel-throated, amplified by the stone until it seemed to come from everywhere—from the rock above them, from the water beneath, from the future and the past all at once.

Lena's laugh died in her throat. She cut the Kestrel's engine to a whisper and held up one fist, and the four of them stood motionless in the drifting boat while the sound grew, and grew, and resolved.

Around the bend ahead, sliding out of the rain-haze like a knife drawn slowly from a sheath, came the gunboat.

* * *

*Tomas*

Calloway stood at the gunboat's rail under an umbrella held by one of his men, and even at forty meters, even through rain, Tomas could see the man was smiling.

"There she is," Calloway called, and his voice rolled down the gorge in beautiful rolling echoes, *there she is, there she is, there.* "Captain Amberdale! Or should I say—" and here he paused, savoring it, letting the stone carry the name to every corner of the canyon, "—Miss Wren Corliss? Late of the District Records Office? Convicted thief?"

Tomas saw Lena's knuckles whiten on the wheel. Saw her jaw set. But it was Mara who stepped to the rail, satchel strapped across her chest, and shouted back, and her voice hit the gorge walls and multiplied into a chorus of Maras: "You know nothing about her and less about us! What do you want?"

"The same thing I've wanted since Ambunti." Calloway spread his hands, reasonable as a banker. "The map. The documents. Hand them over and I give you my word—every one of you walks. Even the boy. Even the captain with her false name and her forged papers. There's a pardon in my breast pocket with a blank space for a name. Any name she likes." He tilted his head. "A fresh start, Miss Corliss. Isn't that all you've ever wanted?"

Tomas moved without deciding to move. He was already at the hold hatch, already dragging the tarpaulin back, when Lena caught his arm.

"What are you doing?"

"Looking at our options." There weren't many. Ahead: the gunboat, blocking the channel, its bow gun—a real gun, a mounted thing, not a hunting rifle—covered by two men. Behind: the drop they'd run, suicide in this water. Above: cliffs going up sixty, eighty meters, sheer, streaming with runoff. And beneath them—

Beneath them, the pool. Deep, black, slow. Fed by the flood upstream through channels Tomas could now hear clearly, multiple channels, the gorge drinking hard from the drowned country above. Which meant outflow somewhere too. Water this deep, this enclosed, always had an exit. It had to. Otherwise it would be a lake.

"Lena," he said quietly. "How much freeboard does she have left? Honestly."

Her eyes went to his. She understood instantly—he watched it land in her face, watched her understand what he was proposing—and for one long moment the rain fell between them and she said nothing.

"She's been taking six inches an hour since Ambunti," Lena said finally. "The patch is holding by paint and prayer. Why?"

"Because a scuttled boat sinks in ninety seconds," Tomas said. "And a sunken hull in a channel this narrow is a dam. And a dam in a flash-flood gorge is—" he gestured upward, at the silver ribbon of sky, at the drowning country pouring its heart into the stone above them, "—a wall of water with nowhere to go but forward. Through anything in its way."

"You're talking about sinking the Kestrel."

"I'm talking about sinking the Kestrel *on purpose*, in the exact spot I choose, and riding the surge out of here while Calloway's gunboat is being folded against the cliff like a tin cup."

Silence. The engine idled. The echoes whispered their whispers.

"She's my home," Lena said. It wasn't an argument. It was a fact laid on the table, the way you lay down a card you know loses the hand but has to be played anyway.

"I know," Tomas said. "That's why it has to be you who opens the seacocks. I won't take that from you. But Lena—she was never your hiding place. She was your cage with good views. Let her do one last useful thing."

Lena looked at the boat. At the wheelhouse where she'd slept a thousand nights, at the galley where the coffee tin lived, at the deck where Elias Voss had died at dawn with his daughter's name on his lips. Tomas watched her say goodbye to all of it in the space of four seconds, the way river people do—fast, because the river doesn't wait for grief.

"Mara," Lena said, turning. "Get Iri and yourself onto the cliff shelf on the starboard side. Take the documents. Climb. Don't argue."

Mara didn't argue. She grabbed Iri's collar, hauled him toward the starboard rail where a ledge of eroded limestone jutted out barely wide enough for goats, and pushed the boy up first. Iri twisted at the top and shouted down, "Lena! The outflow—it's on the LEFT, past the third stalactite, where the wall is wet! My grandfather said the water leaves where the wall weeps!"

"Where the wall weeps," Tomas repeated, already scanning. There. A dark seam in the cliff, slick and running, a crack in the stone wide enough for a loaded boat if the pilot was mad and lucky. "Lena. There's our door."

"And Calloway's in front of it."

"Then we'd better make some noise on the way."

* * *

*Mara*

Mara pressed herself against the wet limestone with Iri beside her, both of them forty feet above the water, and watched her father's map buy its final passage.

Below, Lena spun the Kestrel broadside in the pool—an act of pure defiance, presenting her flank to the gunboat like an insult—and opened the throttle. The engine roared. The echo took the roar and made it ten engines, twenty, a fleet of ghosts charging up the gorge, and every head on the gunboat turned toward the noise, toward the madwoman circling in the flood pool, and no one was watching the stern where Tomas Reyes knelt at the bilge access with a wrench in his hand.

Mara couldn't see the seacocks open. She saw the result. The Kestrel shuddered, listed, and began, gently, terribly, to settle.

"Now!" Tomas bellowed, and Lena threw the wheel over and gave it everything, driving the dying boat straight for the weeping wall—for the crack, the door, the outflow—with the gunboat's crew finally understanding, finally scrambling, Calloway's umbrella spinning away into the water as he screamed orders that the gorge ate alive.

The Kestrel reached the seam of black water at the base of the cliff just as the river reached her decks.

Mara had grown up on flood stories. Her mother had told them; the elders at Karawari Bend told them better. None of them had prepared her for the actual sound. It wasn't loud so much as total—a single vast exhalation, as if the gorge itself had been holding its breath since the rains began. The flood pool, dammed now by a hundred tons of settling steel, found the pressure too much to contain and detonated through the outflow channel, and the Kestrel rode the front of it like a leaf on a broom.

The gunboat tried to turn. In a channel that narrow, with that much water arriving, turning was a word for children's games. The surge took the vessel amidships and folded it against the eastern cliff with a crunch of metal that echoed for a full ten seconds, men spilling into the flood like spilled coins, Calloway himself swept off his own deck and carried downstream into the roaring white.

Mara lost sight of everything. The world was spray and thunder. She felt Iri's hand lock around her wrist—his grip astonishingly strong—and realized she'd been leaning too far over the ledge, and let him pull her back against the stone.

When the roar dropped to a bellow, and the bellow to a growl, she looked down.

The Kestrel was gone. Not sunk—gone. Swallowed whole into the outflow channel, hull, wheelhouse, mast, everything, taken by the river into whatever dark cathedral lay beneath the gorge. On the churning surface floated one wooden hatch cover, turning slowly, and a tin cup, and a coil of rope.

And then, impossibly, from the mouth of the outflow channel—bursting out of the rock like a breath held too long—came the launch.

Tomas stood at the tiller, soaked black, hauling on the little emergency skiff's line with both hands. Lena crouched low in the bow, and as the launch swept beneath the ledge she looked up and found Mara's eyes, and Mara saw that her face was streaming and that she was not hiding it.

"She went through the wall," Lena shouted up, and her voice broke on the last word, and the gorge took the sound and gave it back and back and back, *through the wall, through the wall, through,* until it seemed the whole canyon was saying it with them.

They clambered down to the water's edge, the four of them, and stood in the rain on the black stones watching the hatch cover circle in the eddy. Nobody spoke for a long time. The gunboat lay crumpled against the far cliff, dead, its surviving crew clinging to the rocks downstream, no threat to anyone for days.

Iri picked up the tin cup when it washed to his feet. He looked at it, then at Lena, and held it out to her without a word.

Lena took it. Turned it over in her hands. It was dented, ancient, the enamel worn through to the metal in the shape of a thumbprint—her thumbprint, ten thousand mornings of river coffee.

"She wasn't my hiding place," Lena said quietly. It was unclear whether she was speaking to them or to the water or to the echo. "Tomas told me that. I didn't believe him until just now." She closed her fist around the cup. "She was my coffin with the lid propped open. And I just let the river nail it shut."

Mara touched her shoulder. "What do we do now?"

Lena lifted her head. Rain ran off her jaw. When she answered, her voice had changed—lower, steadier, stripped of everything soft.

"Now we walk. Up the goat track Iri's going to show us, over the spur, down into the Karawari headwaters. Three days, maybe four, if the landslides haven't closed the pass." She slung the cup's rope over her shoulder like a bandolier to match Mara's satchel. "Calloway's swimming somewhere downstream with a broken boat and a ruined reputation, and Hessler thinks his gunboat has us trapped in a gorge. By the time anyone tells him different, we'll be over the top and gone."

"And after that?" Iri asked.

Lena looked up at the cliffs, at the ragged ribbon of storm-light, at the long climb ahead—and Mara saw something in her face that hadn't been there in all the weeks since Ambunti. Not hope exactly. Certainty. The look of a woman who has finally stopped running and started advancing.

"After that," Lena said, "we go to the confluence. And I tell several thousand people my real name."

She turned and started up the goat track, and the gorge echoed her footsteps all the way, as if the stone itself were counting them—one, two, three, four—marching toward the gathering.
