## 18. What Lena Buried

The mountain trail climbed out of the gorge country in switchbacks so steep that the crew walked most of the morning with their heads down, watching their boots find purchase on wet stone and root. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that beaded on everything—on Iri's hair, on the oilskin satchel Mara carried slung across her chest like a child, on the canvas of the packs Tomas had salvaged from the Kestrel before she went down into the channel of the Gorge of Echoes.

Lena walked at the rear. It was habit more than choice; a captain walks her stern, even when there is no boat left to walk. Her arm ached under its fresh bandaging where the healer's poultice sat against the knife wound, a deep green smell rising off it every time she swung the limb. Behind them, far below and already out of sight, the Kestrel lay scuttled across the gorge channel like a fallen tree, her hull full of river, her name—*Lena Amberdale's* name, the false one, the hiding one—dissolving in the current.

They made camp at midday in the lee of an overhang where a previous traveler had stacked stones into a rough hearth. Iri went upslope to check the snares he'd set at dawn, moving through the mist with the sureness of someone who had grown up reading country instead of charts. Tomas built a small fire, feeding it dry fern stalks until it caught, and Mara sat with the map unrolled across her knees, though by now she knew every line of it better than Lena did.

Lena stood at the edge of the overhang looking back the way they'd come. Below, the cloud sea filled the valleys to the brim. Somewhere under all that white was the river, and the channel, and the wreck.

"You keep looking for her," Tomas said quietly, coming to stand beside her. He handed her a tin cup of something hot and bitter—bark tea, the kind the healer at Ambunti had pressed on them when they left. "The boat."

"I'm not."

"You are. Every hour since the gorge." He didn't smile, but there was no judgment in it either. "I drowned my maps, remember. I know what it is to stand over the place where you put something down and wonder if you did the right thing."

Lena drank the tea. It scalded going down and she let it. "It wasn't the right thing," she said. "Scuttling her. It felt like cutting off my own hand."

"It felt like that because you'd made the boat into the person you were pretending to be." Tomas looked out at the cloud sea. "And now the pretending is sunk too, and what's left is just you. Standing on a mountain. With a story nobody's heard."

Mara looked up from the map. She said nothing, but she folded it closed, deliberately, and gave him her whole attention, and Lena understood that this had been arranged—that these two had decided somewhere on the trail behind her, in some low-voiced conference while she slept, that tonight the story would come out or they would stop asking forever.

"Iri will want to hear it," Mara said. "When he comes back."

"He already knows half of it," Lena said. "He told you, didn't he. Whatever I said in the fever."

"He told us you talked about ledgers," Mara said. "About a magistrate's office. About being afraid of a man's voice." She paused. "He told us you said *I only copied it down, I swear*, over and over, like you were still standing in front of somebody demanding proof."

The fire popped. Down in the white below, thunder muttered, distant and directionless.

Lena set down the cup. She found that her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. Perhaps there was nothing left in her to shake loose. Perhaps the fever had burned it all out of her already—the last of the concealment, the last of the careful silence she'd maintained for six years, the architecture of small lies and changed details that made up a woman called Lena Amberdale.

"All right," she said. "Sit down. Both of you. Because if I'm going to tell it, I'm telling it once, start to finish, and then we're never speaking of it again until the confluence—and at the confluence, everyone speaks of nothing else."

* * *

"My name isn't Lena Amberdale."

She said it plainly, the way you'd report a sandbar ahead. It still cost her something to say aloud, even here, even to these two—but less than she'd feared. The name came up out of her like a splinter long buried, and the flesh around it had mostly healed.

"My name is Elena Vasquez-Amberdale. My mother was from the coast trade families; my father kept books for a shipping concern in Port Moresby before he drank himself into the ground. I grew up with a ledger in one hand and a pencil in the other. Numbers were the one thing I was ever good at. They don't lie, and they don't ask where you're from, and if you add them correctly nobody can take anything away from you. That was the whole of my philosophy at nineteen years old, and I built a life on it. Clerk. Registrar's office. Civil records, court filings, verdict documentation for the district judiciary." She laughed, short and without humor. "You see the irony. I spent three years filing other people's convictions. I stamped them. I archived them. I thought the paper was neutral. Paper doesn't care who wins; paper just remembers."

"Until you read something you weren't supposed to," Tomas said.

"Not wasn't supposed to. Wasn't *able* to. There's a difference, and Hessler understood the difference, and that's why he's dangerous." Lena crouched by the fire, holding her hands to the warmth out of old habit, though the wound in her arm ached worse with heat. "The verdict archive was supposed to be sealed. Final judgments, signed by the presiding magistrate, locked away. But the seal on the eastern cabinet had been broken for months—replaced badly, with wax of the wrong color—and I noticed, because noticing is what clerks do. And one evening, staying late to reconcile a quarterly tally, I opened it."

She could see it still, with terrible clarity: the smell of the archive room, camphor and damp paper; the single bulb swinging slightly in the draft; the ledger bound in government-blue cloth with the district crest stamped in gold. She had expected misfilings. Clerks always expect misfilings. What she had found instead was a second book running parallel to the official record—a private ledger, in Hessler's own hand, of payments received.

"There were forty-one entries," she said. "Forty-one verdicts in six years where money had moved before the judgment was signed. Land clearances granted to syndicate proxies after 'donations' to the magistrate's discretionary fund. Convictions overturned—for a price—for men who'd beaten laborers nearly to death in the mine camps. Sentences imposed on villagers whose only crime was refusing to sell their land. And in the margin of one entry, dated four years back, a notation I've never been able to forget. *Karawari Bend — settled.*"

Mara went very still. "That was the year of the burning," she said. "The village my father—" She stopped. Started again. "That was the massacre."

"That was the massacre," Lena said. "I didn't know it then. I knew it as a line item. Forty thousand kina, entered as 'consultation fees,' against a case file about a land dispute that officially ended with no casualties. Officially." She looked at Mara directly. "Your father's map proves what actually happened. My testimony proves who signed for it. Between the two of us, we have the whole machine."

"And Hessler found out you'd seen the ledger," Tomas said.

"He found out because I was a fool." The shame rose in her then, the old shame, the one she'd carried downriver and upriver and through six years of false names, and she let it rise, because she had promised herself on the mountain trail that she would not flinch from it this time. "I didn't copy it. I didn't photograph it. I didn't take it to anyone. I told myself I needed to verify the entries first—cross-reference the case files, confirm the amounts, build an airtight dossier. That's what a clerk does. That's what a clerk *is*. I spent eleven days verifying. Eleven days. On the twelfth day I came in to begin copying the ledger properly, and the cabinet was empty, and Mr. Calloway—young then, already smooth—was sitting in Hessler's office with two constables, and between them on the desk was a stack of bank drafts bearing my own signature."

The fire hissed as a drop of condensation fell from the overhang above.

"They'd forged withdrawals from the judiciary benevolent fund in my name. Fourteen thousand kina over eight months. Enough, with the right witnesses primed, to hang me. And the witnesses were primed—the tribunal had three testimonies, all consistent, all damning, all from men I later learned were on Hessler's payroll. I stood in that tribunal room and I watched them read out my crimes, and I kept thinking: *the numbers don't lie. If I can just get to the numbers, show them the real ledger, prove the arithmetic—*" She shook her head. "But the real ledger was gone. Vanished. And a clerk without her evidence is just a thief with good handwriting."

"They convicted you," Mara said quietly.

"Convicted, sentenced, and shipped to a coastal work camp inside a month. Fast, even for them. Because I hadn't just seen the ledger—I'd seen *Hessler* see me see it. A week before the frame, he passed me in the corridor outside the archive and stopped, and smiled, and asked after my mother's health. My mother had been dead two years. He knew that. It was a message. *I know exactly who you are and what you know, and your life is now whatever I decide it is.*" Lena turned her cup slowly on her knee. "I served fourteen months of a five-year sentence before the camp overseer—another man on the take, but careless with his keys—found his storeroom short and needed a scapegoat who could swim. I swam. Changed my name at the first mission station down the coast. Took my mother's family name forward and dropped the rest, because Amberdale sounded like nobody, like a woman who'd always been here, like weather. Bought passage up the coast. Found the Sepik. Found the Kestrel rotting at a mooring with a debt on her and no captain. And I became her."

"The boat," Tomas said.

"The boat. The name. The whole flat gray life of it." She looked up at him. "Six years, Tomas. Six years of dodging officials, paying harbor bosses, keeping my head down and my papers clean and my past locked in a box I never opened. Do you know what I told myself? I told myself I was free. I told myself the river was freedom." Her voice caught—not dramatically, just a hitch, a stone in the current—and she pushed through it. "It wasn't freedom. It was exile with scenery. I wasn't living. I was *hiding*, and I'd gotten so good at it I'd mistaken hiding for a personality."

Iri came down the slope then, quiet-footed, with two small animals over his shoulder and rain-wet hair plastered to his forehead. He looked at the three of them—at the shape of the silence, which children and exiles both learn to read—and he sat down by the fire without asking, and began preparing the catch, and listened.

"You said you verified for eleven days," Iri said, not looking up from his work. "That's not foolish. That's careful. You wanted it to be true beyond any argument."

Lena blinked at him. Out of the mouths of teenagers and river orphans.

"That's one way to see it," she managed.

"It's the correct way to see it," Mara said. She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her face in the firelight was her father's face—Elias Voss worn down to essentials, all softness burned away. "Listen to me, Elena. You've spent six years telling yourself a story, and it's the wrong story, and it ends tonight. The wrong story is: *I was a fool, I got caught, I ran, I am guilty of something—of slowness, of carelessness, of surviving when I should have fought.* That story is a weight, and you've been hauling it up this mountain along with everything else." She tapped the oilskin satchel. "The right story is this: a clerk found the single most incriminating document in the district's history, alone, with no allies, no protection, and no way to secure it—and the machine crushed her for seeing it, exactly as it has crushed everyone who has ever seen it. Your father's mapmaker among them." Her voice roughened. "My father paddled down from Karawari Bend with survey instruments and came back with a map and a bullet hole in him, and he lasted long enough to give it to a stranger because he had no one else. You are not the exception to what Hessler does to people who see clearly. You are the rule. And the fact that you're alive, sitting at this fire, with the knowledge intact in your head—forty-one entries, dates, amounts, case numbers—is not your failure. It's your *cargo*."

Lena stared at her.

"You memorized them," Tomas said. "Didn't you. In the camp. Fourteen months with nothing to do but survive, and you recited them to yourself like prayers."

"Every night," Lena said. Her throat was tight. "Dates, amounts, file references. All forty-one. I thought—I don't know what I thought. That if I kept them, they hadn't won completely. That somewhere the truth was still filed correctly, even if only in one woman's head."

"That's not guilt," Tomas said. "That's evidence. You've been carrying evidence up this mountain and calling it shame." He poked the fire, sending up a spiral of sparks. "I falsified nothing, and they ruined me anyway, and I spent four years drinking because I thought the disgrace was mine. It took a dying prospector and a stubborn boat captain to teach me otherwise. So believe me when I tell you how this works: the day you speak your testimony out loud, under your own name, in front of people—the shame stops being yours. It transfers. It goes where it belongs. Onto the man who forged the drafts and bought the witnesses and burned the villages. Shame is a load meant to be delivered, Lena. You've just been carrying it to the wrong address."

The mist drifted through the firelight. Down in the valleys the thunder rolled again, closer now, the monsoon gathering itself for another night's work.

"Elena Vasquez-Amberdale," Mara said, testing it, giving each part its weight. Then, softer: "My father trusted a stranger with his life's work because she had honest eyes. He was dying and feverish and he chose you out of every boat on that stretch of river. Men like Elias and men like you—we're what's left when the law sells itself. We're the backup system." She reached across the fire and gripped Lena's unwounded forearm, hard, the way river people grip—brief, certain, binding. "At the confluence, when you stand up, you won't be confessing. You'll be testifying. There's a difference, and every village at that gathering will hear it."

Lena looked around the fire—at Iri carefully turning meat on green sticks, at Tomas feeding the flames another handful of fern, at Mara settling the satchel against her side like a sleeping child—and felt something shift in her chest that she had no nautical term for. For six years she had been ballast and bilge and hidden cargo. She had named herself after a bird that hunts alone over water. But birds, she remembered suddenly, absurdly, also return. Birds have territories. Birds, when the season turns, go home.

"All right," she said, and her voice came out level and clear, the captain's voice, the voice that carries across water. "Then we plan. Two days to the high pass, if Iri's shortcut holds. After that, the confluence plateau—three days' walk, four in this rain. Which means Calloway knows our route, Hessler knows our faces, and we arrive with nothing but testimony, a map, and whatever proof the healer's brother smuggled out of Ambunti." She pulled the wrapped pages from her pack—the healer's gift, thin sheets covered in cramped handwriting, witness names and dates, the silenced made suddenly audible. "So we sleep in shifts, we walk fast, and nobody talks to strangers with clean boots."

Iri grinned. Tomas laughed, low and rusty, like a hinge finding its swing again.

But Mara was looking past the fire, up the trail they would take tomorrow, toward the high pass, and her expression had gone taut and listening.

"Lena," she said. "How long since you heard the birds?"

Lena stilled. The mist hung motionless between the trees. No pigeons winging through the canopy. No honeyeaters working the flowering scrub. Only the fire, and the thunder, and—

Far up the slope, faint but unmistakable, the crack of a boot on wet stone. Then another. Then the metallic whisper of something being drawn from a sheath.

Someone was already on the mountain.
