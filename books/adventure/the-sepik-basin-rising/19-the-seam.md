## 19. The Seam

Mara Voss had imagined this valley a thousand times, and in every imagining it glittered.

She had built the seam in her mind the way her father used to build things in his workshop—carefully, lovingly, out of whatever scraps were at hand. A vein of gold threading through grey quartz like a vein of light through stone. Her father's hands sketching it in the margins of her schoolbooks when she was nine. Her father's voice, hoarse with fever in the last letter she ever received: *There's something at the head of the Bakari tributary, Mara. Something worth more than what they think it is.*

She had thought he meant gold. She had carried the thought like a coal in her chest for eleven years, warming herself on it through the worst seasons of the waystation—the nights the syndicate recruiters came with their contracts and their threats, the mornings she counted the laborers who hadn't come back from the mines. Somewhere in the world there was a seam with her family's name on it, unclaimed, and if she could just reach it—

The Kestrel was dead, scuttled in the Gorge of Echoes, and the last three days they had come up out of the river country on foot, over ridges slick with monsoon runoff, through forest so wet the air itself seemed to drip. Iri went first, light as a bird on the wet stone. Tomas followed with the instruments he'd salvaged in a waterproof satchel he guarded like a child. Lena brought up the rear, her healed arm still stiff, her eyes never still.

And now, at midday, they crested the final ridge, and Mara looked down into the valley her father had died to map.

There was no glitter. There was no gold.

There was a wound.

* * *

The valley ran perhaps two kilometers east to west, a bowl of hills that should have held, by every law of the country she knew, a river running clean through standing forest. Instead the forest stopped at the rim like a crowd stopping at the edge of a fire. Inside the bowl there was mud—grey, ochre, a sick chemical white—and terraces cut into the hillsides in great raw steps, tailings piled in cones that no rain had yet softened because nothing grew on them to hold the rain. Water moved through the valley in channels that had been dug and abandoned and dug again, braiding and rebraiding, and where it pooled it lay the color of weak tea gone wrong, filmed at the edges with a rainbow sheen that Mara recognized from the waystation, from the drums the laborers were forbidden to touch.

The smell reached them before they'd descended a hundred meters. Sulphur and diesel and something sweeter underneath, something that made the back of the throat close.

"No," Iri said. Just that. The boy had not spoken much since the gorge, since the Kestrel went down and the river closed over her, and when he did speak it was usually practical—*the trail forks here, the stone is slick there*. Now he stood at the rim with his mouth open and said *no* the way a man says it to a storm.

Tomas had gone very quiet in the way Mara was learning meant the surveyor in him had taken over. He crouched, pulled a folded paper from his satchel—his own old government survey of this very district, the one that had cost him his career—and spread it on the wet stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. His finger traced the contour lines.

"The fish," he said. "The elders at the village told us the fish died in the lower Bakari three seasons ago. Everyone blamed the monsoon." He looked up, and his face was grey as the tailings. "It wasn't the monsoon. It's this. It's been this. The whole watershed drains through that gorge."

Lena stood a little apart, arms crossed, looking at the valley the way she looked at a sandbar she had to read before committing the boat to it. "Test-mining," she said. "Calloway said the seam was unclaimed. Voss's map was supposed to be the claim."

"It was never a claim." Mara heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "My father wasn't a prospector. He was a surveyor—like you, Tomas. He mapped. That's all he ever did. Everyone in the highlands called him the mapmaker." She started down the slope before she'd decided to move, boots sliding on wet clay, and the others followed because there was nothing else to do. "He told me once that a map is an argument. I thought it was just something fathers said."

* * *

They found the first dead fish forty minutes later, at the edge of a settling pond the size of a chapel floor.

It lay on its side in the shallows, belly up, and it was not rotting so much as it was *wrong*—its scales dulled to a chalky white, its fins stiff, the water around it so still it might have been posed. Then Mara saw the second one. And the third. And then she stopped counting, because the pond's entire margin was a fringe of them, hundreds of fish that had come up the poisoned channels from the lower river or been born in them and never had a chance, and the monsoon rains had washed them into windrows along the banks like leaves after a storm.

Iri turned away and was sick against a tailings cone. No one remarked on it. Mara's own stomach had clenched into a knot she suspected would not come undone for days.

"They knew," she said. She was kneeling at the pond's edge, not touching the water, just looking at her own reflection floating above the dead fish, distorted by the chemical film. "The syndicate knew. You don't build settling ponds this size for a survey. You don't dig test adits—" she pointed across the valley at the dark mouths cut into the far hillside, timbered with fresh-cut lumber that had no business being this far from any road— "unless you've already decided to mine. The seam isn't unclaimed. They claimed it years ago. They just never filed it, because filing it means inspections, and inspections mean someone sees *this*."

"Voss mapped it anyway," Tomas said quietly. "Mapped the seam, mapped the tailings, mapped the ponds. Filed nothing. Sent the map downstream to be hidden, and the syndicate chased him into the river for it." He was unpacking his instruments as he spoke, and his hands were steady, but his voice had a crack running through it that Mara recognized, because she'd heard it in her own voice for eleven years. "They didn't kill him for a gold claim. You don't murder a man over a claim you could contest in a court you already own. They killed him because of what else is on that map."

Mara unslung the oilskin roll from her back.

She had carried it every step since Ambunti, since the medicine woman had pressed it back into her hands after Lena's fever broke—*it was his trust to give and yours to carry, child, and now it's yours to open*—and she had not opened it since Karawari Bend, since the night she and Lena had sat up by lamplight and she had finally, finally shown Lena what her father had actually drawn.

She unrolled it now on the flattest stretch of ground she could find, weighting it with stones, and the four of them knelt around it in the mud like mourners at a grave.

Her father's hand was still there. That was the thing that undid her, every time—the small, precise, patient hand of it, the contour lines drawn with a ruling pen, the little annotations in his cramped script: elevations, flow rates, dates. And threaded through the gold seam itself, the seam she had dreamed of for half her life, the annotations she had not understood until this valley taught her the vocabulary:

*Arsenic signature in headwater sample — confirm.*
*Tailings discharge unpermitted — third channel.*
*Village of Wamaku downstream. 214 souls. Water source at risk.*
*If I do not return, take this to Mara. Take it to the confluence. Let the river people judge.*

"He wasn't claiming the gold," Mara said. Her voice cracked and she let it. "He was documenting a crime. The seam's coordinates—the elders were right, Tomas, look—" Her finger moved to the map's eastern margin, where her father had drawn, in his careful way, the old village site. Wamaku. The village Hessler's men had burned. The massacre site. "It's the same ground. They put the test adits into the hillside above the burned village because no one living would go there. The dead don't file complaints."

The wind came down the valley and it smelled of sulphur, and for a long moment none of them spoke.

Then Lena straightened, and Mara saw the change come over her the way weather comes over water—the captain's stillness, the pilot reading a reach of river and deciding. "Then we do what he asked," Lena said. "All of it. Tomas—can your camera survive the damp?"

"It survived the gorge. It'll survive this."

"Then we work fast. Weather's building in the west, and if Calloway's gunboat crew found the gorge, they'll find this valley by tomorrow at the latest. We photograph everything. We copy the map. And the originals—" she looked at Mara— "the originals go somewhere no one will ever find them unless we lead them there."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the clouds stacked themselves against the western ridges like a debt coming due.

Tomas photographed. He was transformed by the work—Mara watched it happen with something like grief and something like relief. The sodden, self-mocking drunk she'd pulled out of a trading post argument was gone; in his place was the government surveyor he must once have been, methodical and exact, working his way across the valley in a grid, photographing the adits, the ponds, the dead fish windrows, the rainbow sheen, the fresh-cut timber with whatever brand the syndicate's lumber suppliers stamped into it. He shot the map itself in overlapping frames, top to bottom, margin to margin, so that every annotation could be reconstructed. He shot his own old survey beside it, the fraudulent land-claim overlays the syndicate had demanded he sign, and his face as he did it was the face of a man exhuming his own grave.

Mara copied. She had her father's hand—she'd traced his letters as a child the way other children traced pictures of birds—and she sat under an overhang at the valley's rim with a waterproof notebook and a pencil stub and copied the map line by line, contour by contour, annotation by annotation, while the poisoned valley spread below her. It took hours. Her father's script was small and she refused to paraphrase a single word, because she understood now that the words were the weapon. *214 souls. Water source at risk.* Somewhere in a magistrate's office in the lowlands there was a ledger with Hessler's name on it—Lena's ledger, the one that had ruined her—and somewhere in this valley there was a map with her father's name on it, and between the two of them they would hang the whole rotten structure if the river people got the chance to see them.

Iri ranged the valley's edges like a scout, because that was what he was now, and it was Iri who found the camp.

He came back at a run, silent on the wet ground, and crouched beside them with his eyes wide. "Smoke," he said. "Old smoke, two days maybe. Downstream side, past the third adit. Tents. Tins. And—" he swallowed— "more dead fish. Fresh ones. They've been running the pumps again."

Lena was on her feet before he finished. "Then we're done photographing. We cache and we go."

* * *

The cache was Tomas's idea, and it was a good one, because it was a surveyor's idea.

Two hundred meters below the rim, on the northern side where the forest still held, there was a limestone overhang behind a curtain of hanging roots—the kind of place the old people had used for burials before the missions came, Iri said, a place you did not go unless you were dead or grieving. Inside, dry as a held breath, Tomas had spent the previous evening preparing a cavity: a niche in the rock behind a slab that could be levered out and replaced, invisible unless you knew the slab was not the wall.

Into it went the oilskin map, rewrapped in fresh oilcloth with the medicine woman's herbs packed around it against the damp. Into it went the notebook of witness statements—the healer's brother's list of silenced names, the elders' testimony of the burning of Wamaku, everything they'd gathered since Ambunti, each page copied twice. Into it went Tomas's film canisters, the exposed negatives, sealed in wax. And into it, last, Mara placed a single sheet of her own writing, in her father's hand, addressed to no one and everyone:

*If you have found this, you were led here by someone the river trusts. The map of the Bakari seam documents the poisoning of the watershed and the desecration of Wamaku. The originals must go to the confluence gathering, to be judged by the river communities. My name was Elias Voss. My daughter carries this. Judge it true.*

She wrote *my daughter carries this* and her pencil hesitated over it, because it was past tense and present tense at once—her father had written it eleven years ago in a letter that reached her after he was already dead, and she was writing it now with his hand, alive, carrying it. Both of them carrying it. She sealed the sheet in oilcloth with the rest and helped Tomas lever the slab back into place, and Iri swept the ground before it with a palm frond until the limestone showed no mark of their passing.

"Only four people in the world know where it is," Tomas said, stepping back.

"Five," Mara said. "My father knew this country better than any of us. If there's a way for a dead man to point at a rock, he'd have found it." She touched the slab once, lightly, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. "Goodbye, Papa. I'll finish the argument."

* * *

They climbed out of the valley as the light failed, taking the northern trail Iri knew, and from the ridge above the rim Mara stopped once and looked back.

The valley lay below in the last grey light, terraced and poisoned and still, the settling ponds holding the sky in their film of rainbow. Somewhere down there the syndicate's pumps were running, had been running for years, would run tomorrow, pouring their slow arsenic into the water that two hundred villages drank. Her father had seen this from the outside, from maps and samples, and it had killed him. She had seen it from the inside, and it had done something else—it had burned away the last of the coal she'd carried in her chest for eleven years, the dream of the glittering seam, the inheritance of gold. What was left after the burning was harder and lighter both.

The gold was never the point. It had never been the point. The point was that a magistrate had burned a village to hide a massacre, and a company had poisoned a river to hide a mine, and a surveyor had died trying to write it all down, and the writing was the treasure, and the treasure was in a hole in a rock, and she knew the way to it.

"We'll make the river by tomorrow night," Lena said beside her, reading the trail ahead the way she read water. "Two days after that to the confluence, if the monsoon gives us a day's grace."

"It won't," Iri said.

"Then we move tonight."

They moved. And Mara, climbing, kept her hand flat against the notebook in her jacket—the copy, the argument, her father's voice in her own handwriting—and below them, in its dry and patient dark, the originals waited for the river people, and the first rain of the next storm came down out of the west like a summons.
