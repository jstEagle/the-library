## 22. Downstream, Under Her Own Name

The monsoon ended the way it had begun—not gradually, but all at once, as if the sky had simply grown tired of weeping and shut its great grey eye. One morning the rain was hammering the tin roof of the Ambunti mission shed; by noon the clouds had torn open to reveal a blue so clean it looked newly made, and the whole basin steamed and glittered like a thing being born.

Lena stood on the mud flats below the wreck of the *Kestrel* and felt the sun on her face for the first time in weeks.

The boat lay on her side in the shallows of the gorge's lower reach, half-buried in silt, her stern still wedged against the rock shelf where Tomas had scuttled her. She looked less like a vessel now than like the ribcage of some great drowned animal, her hull breached, her wheelhouse caved in, her name—*Kestrel*, painted in Lena's own hand six years ago, in another life—faded to a ghost of white.

"You're going to try to save her," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. He came down the bank with his boots squelching, a surveyor's roll of paper under one arm, and stopped beside her in the wet sand. "I've seen that look before. On men staring at mine shafts that had already killed their partners."

"She saved us first."

"She sank for us, you mean. There's a difference." He tilted his head, studying the wreck with the same flat professional gaze he gave river channels. "She's done, Lena. Even if you raised her, the hull's rotten to the waterline. You'd be ferrying a coffin."

Lena said nothing. She walked out to the wreck, boots sinking, and laid her palm flat against the cold timber just below the waterline, where the wood was still sound. She had bought the *Kestrel* with money she'd saved under a name that wasn't hers, in a port where no one asked questions, because a woman with no name needs a vessel that can't be traced. The boat had been her anonymity as much as her livelihood. Her hiding place. Her shell.

And shells, she was learning, were meant to be outgrown.

"She's done," Lena agreed at last. "But she's not useless."

Tomas raised an eyebrow.

"Salvage," Lena said. "There's a winch at the fuel depot, and Iri knows the boys who work it. The engine might be flooded, but engines forgive. And there's timber in the highlands—Mara's waystation sits on a stand of kwila nobody's claimed since Hessler's men scared everyone off it." She turned to look at him, and found she was smiling, and was surprised by it, the way you're surprised by your own reflection in a shop window. "I'm not saving the *Kestrel*, Tomas. I'm building what comes next."

* * *

It took eleven days.

They worked from first light until the heat drove them into the shade, and then worked again in the long gold evenings while the river ran low and glassy and the birds came down in screaming clouds to feed on the exposed mudflats. Iri proved to have a genius for engines that no one had suspected; he stripped the *Kestrel*'s motor down to its bones on the depot's concrete apron and had it running again in four days, coughing blue smoke, then purring. The depot boys—skinny, sharp-eyed, all of them cousins of somebody Iri knew—helped drag the hull onto rollers and winch it upright, and when it came free of the mud with a great sucking sigh the whole crowd cheered as if a patient had sat up on the operating table.

Mara arrived on the sixth day with three flat-bottomed punts loaded with kwila planking and a carpenter named Old Beni who had once built trade boats for the mission fleet and had opinions about hull design that he expressed freely and without mercy. Mara herself had changed since Karawari Bend—or rather, Lena thought, watching her direct the unloading with crisp economy, she had stopped changing, stopped spending energy on being wary. The map was delivered. The promise was kept. What remained was work, and work, Mara understood, was its own kind of peace.

They rebuilt the boat on the flats, in the open air, in full view of anyone who cared to watch. That was the point, though Lena didn't say it aloud. For six years she had worked at night and in fog and behind tarps, a woman patching a hull by lantern-light because daylight meant witnesses and witnesses meant records and records meant Magistrate Hessler's clerks finding a name that didn't hold up. Now there was no hiding. Hessler had fled Ambunti the week after the confluence gathering, so the story went, taking nothing but a leather case and whatever gold he could carry, his authority stripped by the assembled villages before half the basin had even finished hearing the testimony. The colonial administration, embarrassed and scrambling, had opened an investigation into the syndicate's land claims; Calloway sat in a holding cell in Wewak awaiting transport, his smooth talk finally useless against men who had watched their own fathers testify.

There was nothing left to hide from. So they built in the sun.

Lena learned to let them help her. That was the hardest part, harder than the carpentry. Old Beni would take a chisel out of her hand mid-cut and say, "You're rushing the scarf joint, girl, the river will find every flaw you leave," and she would stand there with her empty hands and her pride stinging, and then she would breathe, and watch, and learn. Tomas surveyed the rebuilt hull the way he surveyed channels, walking its length with a level and a notebook, muttering figures. Iri painted. He had asked for the job, solemn as a magistrate, and spent two full days on the stern with brushes and a tin of white enamel, and when he stepped back at last there it was, fresh and bright and impossible to look away from:

*Amberdale*.

Lena stared at it for a long time.

"It's your name," Iri said, uncertain. "The one everyone knows you by. I thought—"

"It's half my name." She touched the wet paint, then wiped her finger on her trousers, leaving a white smear. "The half I made up."

* * *

The registry office at Ambunti occupied a stilted building near the government wharf, and it had not changed since the last time Lena had stood in it, six years and a lifetime ago. Same sweating clerk. Same ceiling fan turning with the lazy conviction of a thing that has never once been asked to hurry. Same smell of ink and mildew and old paper—the smell, Lena had once thought, of the machinery that ground people up.

She climbed the steps with the papers in a leather folder under her arm. Tomas and Iri waited at the bottom. Mara had wanted to come; Lena had asked her not to. This was something a woman had to do alone, the way she'd had to do everything else alone, only this time alone was a choice instead of a sentence.

"Vessel registration," the clerk said, not looking up. "Name of vessel."

"*Amberdale*."

He wrote it. "Owner's name."

And there it was. The whole basin balanced on the point of the clerk's pen, all the years of it—the conviction, the false testimony, the ledger she'd found in Hessler's office with its columns of bribed verdicts, the theft she'd been framed for, the night she'd walked out of the courthouse with her old life confiscated and a new name stitched together from nothing. *Amberdale* because amber was what resin became under pressure, and *dale* because she'd liked the sound of a valley. She had told herself the name was a disguise. She had told herself that for six years.

But the name had carried her. The name had pulled a dying man from the reeds and kept a promise across three hundred miles of floodwater. The name had stood up in front of the confluence gathering and said, *My true name is Elena Sandoval, and I was a clerk in the magistrate's office, and I am here to testify*—and the river country had listened, and the river country had judged, and the river country, in its slow immense way, had decided that names were less important than what a woman did with them.

Elena Sandoval had been a clerk who found a ledger. Lena Amberdale was a captain who had come home.

Both of them were her. She was done choosing.

"Lena Amberdale," she said. "A-m-b-e-r-d-a-l-e."

The pen scratched. The fan turned. Somewhere out on the water a fish hawk screamed and dove.

"Port of registry?"

"Ambunti."

The clerk's head came up at that, finally, and he looked at her—a weathered woman in a salt-stained shirt with a scar on her forearm and river mud still under her fingernails—and something like recognition passed over his face, the recognition of a man who had stamped a thousand papers and understood perhaps one. "Ambunti," he repeated. "Not Wewak?"

"Here." She tapped the counter. "This is home port."

He stamped the form. The thump of it sounded, to Lena, like a door opening rather than closing.

* * *

They launched her on a morning of impossible clarity, the kind of morning the basin allowed itself perhaps twice a year, when the air was rinsed clean and the mountains of the highlands stood up blue and sharp beyond the floodplain as if you could walk to them by lunchtime.

Half of Ambunti came down to the flats. The medicine woman from upriver came, wrapped in her red trade cloth, and pressed something into Lena's hand—a woven charm, small and tight, that smelled of smoke and bark—and said only, "For the arm. For the boat. It is the same thing now, I think." The depot boys came and shouted advice nobody asked for. Old Beni came and inspected the launch with the expression of a man prepared to be disappointed and secretly, deeply pleased not to be. The elders of the river villages came, and one of them, an old man with a voice like gravel in a current, stood at the water's edge and spoke a blessing over the hull in his own tongue, and then, because he was a man who understood what he was blessing, added in trade pidgin: "Go and come back. That is the whole of the law."

The *Amberdale* slid off the ways and took the water like she'd been born to it—which, in a sense, she had, being half the *Kestrel*'s bones and half new kwila, old wood and new, a salvage and a beginning.

Lena stood at the wheel. Tomas took the bow with the lead line, out of habit, calling depths he didn't need to call, grinning. Iri perched on the cargo hatch with his legs dangling. Mara stood beside Lena at the helm, arms folded, watching the channel open ahead of them, and after a while she said, quietly, "My father would have liked this. He always said a map was just a promise you hadn't kept yet."

"You kept his," Lena said.

"We kept it together." Mara unfolded her arms. "There's a difference. I'm only recently learning it."

They ran downstream with the current, past the trading posts and the stilt villages and the drowned forests where the floodwater still stood silver among the trunks, and at every village they stopped, because that was the contract now. The restoration commission—the first thing the river communities had built together since the gathering, without asking anyone's permission—had hired the *Amberdale* to ferry witnesses to the inquiry at Wewak, and seedlings and tools back, and surveyors and teachers and, once, a very nervous colonial auditor who spent the whole trip gripping the rail and asking whether the river was always like this.

"Only in the wet season," Lena told him.

"And when's that?"

"Most of it."

But the truth was that the river was gentler now, or she was; the channels Tomas had charted through the gorge held, the markers the villages had set themselves—white-painted poles, no syndicate steel—guided them true, and the *Amberdale* handled like a boat that had been loved in the building. In the evenings they tied up at villages where people knew Lena's name, both of her names, and neither one made them flinch. Children ran along the banks beside the wake. Old women traded fish for kerosene and news. And every night, somewhere between the last pipe and the first stars, Lena found herself standing at the rail doing nothing at all—no charts to memorize, no escape route to plan, no alias to rehearse—and discovering that nothing at all, done in safety, under her own name, was the finest cargo she had ever carried.

* * *

On the last evening of the first run, coming back upriver with the holds empty and the light going amber on the water, Iri appeared at the wheelhouse door holding a letter.

"From Wewak," he said. "The inquiry clerk gave it to the mission boat."

Lena took it. The paper was official, the handwriting not. She read it twice.

"What is it?" Tomas asked.

She handed it to him and watched his face as he read. The colonial administration, formally and with the maximum of bureaucratic embarrassment, was restoring what it called *legal standing* to one Elena Sandoval, née of the district, her conviction of eleven years ago vacated on the evidence presented at the confluence gathering. There was language about *miscarriage of justice*. There was language about *restitution*. And at the bottom, in the clerk's careful hand, a single line that had clearly been added after the form was finished:

*The magistrate Hessler has been sighted in Port Moresby. He is said to be seeking passage abroad. The commission requests your advice on the matter of pursuit.*

Tomas read it aloud, slowly, and when he finished, the wheelhouse was quiet except for the engine and the water.

"He's running," Iri said. "Good. Let him run."

"He'll surface somewhere," Mara said. "Men like that always do. Somewhere with weak courts and gold."

Lena turned the letter over in her hands. Eleven years ago, a clerk named Elena Sandoval had found a ledger and been destroyed for it, and she had spent every day since learning how to disappear. She could disappear again. She could fold this letter into a pocket and let the world keep its own accounts, and pilot her boat up and down her river until her hands gave out, and that would be a life—maybe even a good one.

But she was not that woman anymore. That woman had been a hiding place too, like the *Kestrel*, like the name. And hiding places were meant to be outgrown.

"He's not running," Lena said at last. "He's relocating. There's a difference." She looked out at the river, at the long amber water running home, and felt the old fear rise in her and pass through her and leave something harder behind. "And I know exactly which harbors take men like him, because I spent six years learning every harbor that would take me."

She took the wheel. The *Amberdale* answered her hands, steady and true.

"Tomas," she said, "chart me a course to Port Moresby."
