## 1. The Last Charter

The season was dying the way it always did on the lower Reckoning—slowly, then all at once. Jude Thackeray felt it in the water first: the current gone thin and glassy, the glacial milk of high summer settling into the cold clarity of September. The *Maren B* sat low at her mooring with her holds empty and her paint peeling in long gray ribbons, and Jude stood at her rail with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand, watching the last charter of the year walk down the dock toward him.

She didn't walk like a tourist. That was the first thing he noticed. Tourists came down that dock the way they did everything—loudly, off-balance, cameras swinging. This woman came down it like she'd been born on pilings, boots finding the gaps between boards without looking, a canvas duffel over one shoulder and a hard case in her hand that had the particular scuffed dignity of equipment carried a long way.

"Captain Thackeray?" Her voice carried flat across the still water, no question in it.

"That depends on what you're buying."

"Passage." She stopped at the gangway and looked up at him, and he got his first real look at her face—thirty-something, sunburned across the cheekbones, eyes the pale unimpressed gray of river fog. "Upriver. As far as you'll go."

Jude set his coffee down on the rail. "Lower runs close out next week. I've got two days open before I pull the boat for winter. Kettle Falls, maybe the old trading bend if the water's kind."

"I don't want Kettle Falls." She said it without heat, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "I want the Gorge of Bells."

The name landed on the deck between them like something dropped from a height. Down the dock, old Anders paused in his painting of the fuel shed, brush hovering, listening without turning his head. Everyone on the lower river listened when someone said *Gorge of Bells*. It was the way people went quiet around a grave.

"No," Jude said.

"You haven't asked what I'm paying."

"It's not a price problem, Doctor—" he read the lanyard half-hidden under her jacket collar, "—Marsh."

Something flickered through her face at the name, quick as a fish under ice. She let the duffel slide off her shoulder to the dock boards. "Then let me make it a different kind of problem."

* * *

He made her coffee anyway, because refusing a person passage and refusing them warmth were different things, and because the *Maren B*'s galley was the only place on the dock with a stove worth the name. She sat across from him with her hands wrapped around the mug, not drinking, and laid it out the way he supposed she laid everything out—in order, without decoration.

Her father was Dr. Elias Marsh. Glaciologist. He'd gone upriver eleven weeks ago with an eight-person expedition, licensed for survey work above the gorge, and the license itself should have been the first warning, because nobody surveyed above the Gorge of Bells. The upper river wasn't water anymore up there; it was braided shallows and dead channels and meltwater running off the face of the Cape Reckoning summit, and past a certain point it wasn't navigable by anything with a keel or a motor or sense.

Six weeks ago, the expedition's scheduled check-in hadn't come. The satellite phone rang into nothing. A flyover found their base camp stripped, the tents gone, no sign of movement. The second flyover found nothing at all. The university wanted to wait for spring. The provincial office wanted paperwork. Elena Marsh had instead come three thousand miles to a river town at the end of the season to find the one captain who ran the last stretch of navigable water—and every one of those captains had already told her no.

"They told me you were the stubborn one," she said. "That you'd at least hear it out."

"Anders tell you that?"

"Anders told me you were the only one who'd ever taken a boat above the falls and brought it back."

"Once," Jude said. "Fifteen years ago. And I didn't bring back everyone who went up with me."

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere below the hull, the river moved against the pilings with its endless patient sound, the sound Jude had slept inside of for fifteen years, the sound that measured his life now in miles instead of years. He looked at her hands around the mug—knuckles scarred, nails cut short, a rope burn healing on the left wrist—and understood she was not a woman who spent her life in laboratories.

"My father," she said, "knew your father."

There it was. He'd been waiting for it since she said *Marsh*, since the moment the name had snagged on something deep in him like a hook in a sunken log. Thackeray and Marsh. Two names from another lifetime, spoken together in chart rooms and mess tents, in the years before the upper river took Nathaniel Thackeray and the world decided he'd drowned and Jude decided to stop arguing with the world.

"Elias Marsh bankrolled my father's last run," Jude said carefully. "Or half of it. Everyone says so."

"He did more than bankroll it. They were partners. Friends." She turned the mug a slow quarter-turn on the table. "My father has spent fifteen years trying to prove Nate Thackeray reached the source. Not died near it. *Reached* it. He believed there was evidence up there—your father's records, his findings, something worth more than anyone's willing to say out loud. And now my father is missing above the same stretch of water, and the province wants to file forms until the snow closes the passes, and I am done waiting for institutions to grow a spine."

"So this is a rescue."

"This is a rescue." Her eyes came up and held his, and they were not soft eyes. "Whatever else it is can wait until we find them alive."

*If*, said the part of Jude's mind that had learned its arithmetic on this river. *If we find them alive.* Eleven weeks. He'd seen what eleven days did to men caught above the falls in a bad August, and that had been summer, that had been with gear, that had been luck.

"No," he said again, but it came out quieter this time, and they both heard the difference.

* * *

He walked her off the boat at dusk and stood at the rail after she'd gone, watching her cross the lot toward the boarding house with her duffel over her shoulder, and hated how much the sight of her walking away felt like watching a door close on something he'd been pretending wasn't knocking.

Because here was the truth he kept nailed shut in the lowest hold of himself, tarred over like a seam against the water: he didn't believe his father drowned. Fifteen years, and he had never once believed it. Drowned men floated downstream. Drowned men washed up in the reeds at Kettle Falls or came around the bend in the spring thaw, and none of them ever came, and a boy of nineteen had stood on this same dock scanning the current until his mother led him inside, and some part of that boy had never come back in either. What if Marsh was right? What if there was something above the gorge—not just a body, not just a camp, but a *reason*, a record, a truth his father had climbed toward while the whole lower river wrote him off as a fool swallowed by white water?

And what if reaching it meant learning the thing Jude had spent fifteen years not knowing?

He slept badly and woke before light, which was normal, and stood on the foredeck in the dark, which was also normal, and found himself doing the thing that was not normal: reading the river. The gauge showed the level dropping ten centimeters a day. Calving season on the upper glacier would start within the month, maybe sooner—the summit shedding its edges into the headwaters, choking the channels, sealing the route until June. If a window existed, it was closing. It was always closing. That was the river's oldest lesson.

He was still standing there when he heard her boots on the dock.

Elena Marsh came down the gangway carrying a document case, and she didn't ask permission this time either. She set the case on the chart table in the wheelhouse and opened it, and inside, under a waterproof sleeve, was a photograph gone soft at the corners and yellowed the way paper yellows when it has been carried against a body for years.

Two men stood on a riverbank in the picture, arms slung around each other's shoulders, laughing at whatever the camera had said. One was young Elias Marsh, recognizable despite the decades—the same sharp bones under the weathered skin. The other was tall and lean and grinning like the river belonged to him, and Jude's chest did something painful and involuntary, because he knew that grin. He saw it in the mirror some mornings and hated it.

"Nate Thackeray," Elena said, "and my father, the day they signed the partnership papers. My father kept it in his kit on every expedition since. He told me once—if anything happens to me up there, find Thackeray's son. He'll say no. Make him say yes."

Jude looked at the photograph a long time. The wheelhouse clock ticked. Below them the river ran dark and patient, carrying everything downstream except the things that mattered.

"What's your cargo weight limit?" he heard himself say.

Her head came up sharply. "You're—"

"Don't make me repeat it. I'll change my mind." He was already moving, pulling the winter tarp ropes loose, his hands working ahead of his better judgment the way hands do when the heart has finally lost an argument it was never going to win. "We load tomorrow. Fuel, food for three weeks, cold-weather kit. You're paying for the inflatable too—I'm not taking the *Maren B* above the falls, and if we go past where she can float, we go light."

"We?"

"You're not rowing yourself." He yanked a knot free and stood there a moment in the pre-dawn gray, breathing the cold river air, feeling fifteen years of nailed-shut silence groan somewhere in the timbers of him. "One condition, Dr. Marsh. Whatever your father thinks is up there—whatever he told you about mine—you tell me everything before we pass the falls. No secrets on my deck. I've buried enough surprises."

For the first time, something that was almost a smile crossed her face, tired and fierce at once. "Everything," she said. "I promise."

He almost believed her. Then she turned to look upriver, north, toward the pale line of the summit barely visible beyond the tree line, and Jude watched her watch it, and thought: *she knows more than she's saying.*

Above the Gorge of Bells, faint as memory, the wind came down the valley carrying a sound he hadn't heard in fifteen years and had never forgotten—the long, ringing toll of ice shifting in the high country, like bells underwater.

The river was calling in its debts.
