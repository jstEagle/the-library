## 2. Miles, Not Years

Jude Thackeray measured his life in miles, not years.

Fifteen years since the river took his father, and he had never once counted the seasons. He counted instead the distance from Fort Meridian to the lower rapids—forty-one miles, forty-two in low water when the channel shifted east of Gravel Bar. He counted the run from the trading post to the mouth of the Gorge of Bells, sixty-eight miles of brown water and deadfall, and he knew every one of those miles the way other men knew their own hands. A year was an abstraction. A mile was a thing you could feel under your boots, could read in the color of the current, could trust or distrust with your life. His father had taught him that before the river taught him anything else, and it was the one lesson Jude had kept after everything else went under.

He was thinking about this at four in the morning, loading the *Cormorant* by lamplight while the mist stood on the water like something waiting.

The dock planks were slick with frost. His breath came out white. Around him the town was still asleep, except for the gulls working the fish offal behind the cannery, and the only sounds were the creak of rope, the slap of water against pilings, and the steady rhythm of his own work—which was, he supposed, its own kind of prayer. Fifteen years of mornings like this one. Fifteen years of loading gear for men who wanted the upper river and didn't understand it, and turning most of them away, and taking the money from the few who insisted, and telling himself each time that it meant nothing, that the past was a drowned country and he had no business mapping it.

Now he was running north anyway. Because of a debt. Because of a woman who watched summits the way his father used to.

He stowed the fuel drums amidships, lashed them twice, checked the lashings a third time. He loaded rope, anchors, the spare propeller wrapped in canvas, crates of hardtack and tinned meat, the coffee Elena Marsh had bought without being told to—good coffee, not the tin-shed swill the survey crews drank—and he found himself arranging her equipment more carefully than he'd arranged a client's cargo in years. Her cases were aluminum, military surplus, stenciled with someone else's expedition numbers ground half away. Heavy. He'd lifted one the night before and felt instruments shift inside like sleeping birds.

She appeared at the end of the dock as the sky went from black to iron, carrying two more cases and a rifle-bag she set down without explanation.

"You're early," he said.

"So are you."

"River men sleep badly before a charter." He took the cases from her, noting again how she carried weight correctly, balanced, no wasted motion. "You've done fieldwork."

"Glaciology is fieldwork." She stepped down into the boat and began stowing her gear without asking where things went, which he both resented and respected. Within minutes she had made herself useful in ways that were almost offensive—coiling line properly, checking the drum of the winch, reading the boat's trim by eye. "Four seasons on the Greenland margin. Two in the Karakoram. I can run a camp, splice a rope, and I won't be sick in your gorge."

"The gorge makes grown guides weep."

"Then I'll weep quietly." She looked up at him across the cargo. "You don't have to test me, Captain. I tested myself years ago. What you need to decide is whether you're going to tell me what's actually up there, or whether we're both going to pretend this is a simple search-and-rescue."

Jude tied off a lashing harder than he needed to. "It's a rescue."

"It's a rescue," she agreed, too easily. "And my father's expedition carried eleven hundred pounds of scientific equipment to measure ice depth. That accounts for maybe six hundred pounds of what I'm shipping. The rest—" she patted one of the aluminum cases, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure you wanted opened, "—the rest is mine to explain when there's time. There isn't time now. The calving window closes in nine days."

"Eight," Jude said. "I've watched that glacier spit since I was six years old. It's closing early this year. The bells started ringing last night."

Something moved behind her eyes at that—the same flicker he'd seen on the dock when she'd hired him, quick and quickly buried. "You heard them?"

"Everyone hears them. Most people have the sense to call them weather." He cast off the bow line. "Board properly. We're burning daylight I told you we didn't have."

* * *

The first thirty miles were water he could have run blindfolded, and mostly did, standing at the wheel with one hand on the throttle while the *Cormorant* shouldered through the morning current. Elena sat forward with the charts open on her knees, but she wasn't reading them; she was watching the banks slide past with the expression of a woman counting something no one else could see.

Jude used the quiet to hate himself efficiently and thoroughly, the way he did most things.

He had nailed that door shut fifteen years ago. He could name the day: the third of September, the year the salmon ran late, when the word came downriver with a trapper named Aldous that the Thackeray expedition had gone silent above the Gorge of Bells, and that the search parties had found nothing but a cache torn open and a stretch of dead water where the maps stopped meaning anything. His father had gone up with six men and come back as nothing—a rumor, a legend, a story the barkeeps told about the forbidden source. *Old Thackeray found the head of the river and the river kept him.* They'd said it almost fondly. Jude had left town within the month and hadn't gone above the gorge since, and the miles below it had been enough, had been plenty, had been a whole life measured out in safe brown water.

Except that Elena's father, Marcus Marsh, had been his father's partner on that last run. Had come down alive when Nathaniel Thackeray had not. Had spent fifteen years writing letters Jude never answered, and funding searches Jude never joined, and finally—last winter—had died with the question still open. And his daughter had walked into Jude's dock with that question in her bag like ballast, and he had looked at her and seen the debt he owed, and hated it, and said yes.

"You're doing the thing," Elena said, without looking up from the charts.

"What thing?"

"Where you answer questions nobody asked and steer very precisely so your hands have an excuse." She turned a page. "My father did that. Drove my mother insane."

"I'm steering because it's my job."

"You're steering around a bend that isn't there for another mile." She looked up then, and her eyes were gray and level and entirely without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You don't have to talk about him. But you should know I'm not going up there for a body, Captain. Whatever you think I'm hauling in those cases—it isn't grief. Grief I already did. This is something else."

"And what's that?"

"That's mine to explain," she said again, and went back to her charts, and Jude steered around the bend that wasn't there yet, and said nothing, and added the exchange to the long ledger of things he would deal with later.

* * *

They made the narrows below Gravel Bar by midafternoon, and Jude put in at the old survey cabin to check the drum bearing—a real errand, or half of one—and while he worked, Elena climbed the bank with her binoculars and swept the valley north, and he let her, because a man couldn't guard his own history forever.

It was while he was elbow-deep in grease and bearing race that the radio woke up.

The set was an old Marconi rig bolted under the dash, tuned by habit to the expedition band, the way he always did, the way he'd done for fifteen years without ever admitting why—some part of him leaving a light on in a window no one was coming home to. Static, mostly. The upper country ate signals alive; even in good years the gorge swallowed transmissions whole. But the static had texture today, a rhythmic pulse under the hiss, and Jude wiped his hands slowly on his rag and leaned toward it the way you lean toward a sound in the dark.

*...—ssshhhk—...any station...—ssshhk—...position—*

A voice. Thin, shredded, riding the static like a leaf rides floodwater. Male. Exhausted.

Jude's hands went still.

*...—kkkhhh—...if anyone—...the dam—* a burst of pure noise, long, five seconds, ten, *—...listen to me, the dam is—*

Then nothing. Carrier wave, empty, humming. Someone transmitting silence into the teeth of the mountains.

Elena came down the bank at a run. "That was—did you get a position? Did you get anything?"

"No position." Jude's voice came out flat and strange, belonging to someone else. "Signal's bouncing off the gorge walls. Could be anywhere above Mile Sixty."

"But you heard it. The dam—" She grabbed the edge of the dash, leaning in, and for the first time since the dock her composure cracked clean through. "Captain. *Thackeray.* You know these frequencies. Whose voice was that?"

And here was the thing, the thing he would carry upriver with him like a stone in his boot: he didn't know. And he did. The voice was ruined by static and distance and fifteen years, worn down to wire and gravel—but underneath all of it, in the cadence, in the particular way the man clipped his words short when he was frightened and trying not to show it—

"I need to check the bearing," Jude said, and turned back to the drum, and greased a bearing that didn't need greasing, and listened to the empty carrier wave hum, and thought about a man frozen upright somewhere in the high white country holding a radio that transmitted nothing, and thought about his father's voice saying *listen to me* across fifteen years of dead water.

"Get aboard," he said finally. "We're running the narrows tonight. Full throttle, no stops."

Elena didn't argue. She cast off the stern line herself, and as the *Cormorant* swung out into the current and turned her bow north, toward the pale line of the summit and the bells tolling faintly beneath the wind, Jude understood that the miles ahead were ones he'd been measuring his whole life without knowing it.

Sixty-eight miles to the gorge. Then the country where the maps stopped meaning anything.

Then whatever was left of the truth.
