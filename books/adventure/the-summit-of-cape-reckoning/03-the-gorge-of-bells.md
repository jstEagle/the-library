## 3. The Gorge of Bells

Elena had read about the Gorge of Bells in her father's letters, and like everything he wrote, the description had been both precise and useless. *The walls narrow to forty meters at the pinch,* he'd written, eleven years ago, in a letter that was mostly logistics and only a little love. *The echo carries for a full minute. Bring earplugs if you're sensitive to your own heartbeat.* She had smiled at that once. Now, standing at the bow of Thackeray's boat as the walls rose on either side like the pages of a closing book, she understood what he'd been telling her without telling her: that some places make you listen whether you want to or not.

The river ran green-gray and fast here, braided with shadow. The granite went up and up, streaked white with old flood lines, hung with ice teeth that would not melt until August—if they melted at all anymore. The air coming off the water was cold enough to ache in the fillings of her teeth. She kept her hands on the rail and did not look down.

Thackeray stood at the wheel behind her, and she could feel him reading the water over her shoulder, the way a man reads a face. He hadn't spoken in twenty minutes. Neither had she. The silence between them had changed texture since the cache at Mile 41—since the radio static, since the fragment of voice that had made him go still in a way she was beginning to recognize as his version of shouting.

"Pinch ahead," he said finally. "Hold the rail. When I say lean, lean left."

The boat swung into the narrows and the world contracted. The walls came so close she could have touched them—could see the garnet flecks in the stone, the thin veins of quartz running through like frozen lightning—and the sound arrived all at once: the river's roar doubled, tripled, folded back on itself until it wasn't sound anymore but pressure, a physical weight against the chest. And beneath it, faintly, absurdly, something that rang. Water finding hollows in the rock and blowing across them like fingers across bottle mouths. Dozens of hollows. A choir of them.

*There it is,* she thought. *The bells.*

She leaned when he told her to lean, and the boat threaded the throat of the gorge with perhaps a meter to spare on either side, and then the walls fell away and the noise broke apart into ordinary wind and water, and Elena discovered she had been holding her breath for the better part of a minute.

"You've run this before," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Twice." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. Fifteen years of silence had grown into him like grain into wood, and she was starting to understand that every word he gave her cost him something. "Your father ran it four times. He used to say the gorge was the river's way of asking if you were sure."

"And were you sure?"

His jaw worked. "Ask me again on the way down."

* * *

They found the cache an hour past the pinch, on a gravel bench where the river widened into a slow, glassy reach—the kind of water her father had called *dead man's mirror*, flat enough to show you your own face and shallow enough to kill you anyway.

Thackeray saw it first. He always saw things first; she was learning that too. He cut the engine and let the boat drift, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say. The cache was exactly where the expedition's filed coordinates said it would be: two aluminum supply drums and a rubberized crate, half-tucked under an overhang, marked with a strip of orange survey tape that had faded to the color of old skin.

But the drums were open.

Not open the way weather opens things—not rusted, not split by frost-heave, not pried by bears looking for rendered fat. Opened. Cut. The lids had been removed with a blade worked carefully around the sealing gasket, clean and deliberate as surgery, and set aside beside the drums in neat order, as if whoever had done it had intended to come back and seal them again and then thought better of it.

Thackeray grounded the boat and stepped out onto the gravel without waiting for her, and she followed because following was the only thing worse than following.

"Bear?" she asked, though she already knew.

"Bears tear." He crouched at the near drum, not touching it yet, his eyes moving over it the way he'd moved over the water in the gorge. "Bears don't unscrew."

He was right. It was worse than tearing. Someone had opened these drums with patience, with care—with a knife and time and a reason. Inside the first: freeze-dried stores, mostly intact, rifled but not taken. Rope. A Primus stove, its fuel bottle gone. The second drum held the medical kit, and someone had gone through it bandage by bandage, folding each item back into place, taking only the broad-spectrum antibiotics and—she counted twice—the entire supply of morphine ampoules.

"Someone was hurt," Elena said. Her voice sounded strange in the wide quiet of the reach. "Badly enough that they needed the whole kit."

"Or someone wanted to be ready to hurt somebody else." Thackeray straightened. He was looking at the rubberized crate, the one that sat apart from the others, the one her father's manifest had listed simply as *SURVEY — DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT E.M.*

Her initials. Her father had sealed it with her initials, eleven years ago, before any of this, back when the expedition was still a dream he described to her over Sunday phone calls. *I'm leaving something for you in the field, Lena. So you'll have to come find it yourself someday.* She had thought he meant a rock sample. She had been twenty-three and foolish about her father, which is to say she had believed he was invulnerable and slightly boring, a man who loved ice more than people.

The crate's zipper had been cut and restitched with wire. Thackeray looked at her. "Yours," he said. Not a question either.

"It's addressed to me."

"Then you open it."

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas against eleven years of cold, she found her father's field journals—or one of them, volume three of what the spine told her had been five—and beneath it, a sealed foil packet labeled in her father's blocky hand: *LENA. READ THIS FIRST. THEN DECIDE IF YOU STILL WANT THE SOURCE.*

And beneath that, at the bottom of the crate, a partial page torn from another journal entirely—a page she recognized, because she had spent fifteen years of her life trying to forget its handwriting. Ragged-edged, water-stained, the ink feathered where meltwater had found it. Jude Thackeray's father's handwriting.

She didn't tell Thackeray what it was. Not yet. She held it in her gloved hands and felt the paper's fragility and felt, underneath that, the older and heavier thing: the weight of a secret she had carried since she was nine years old, the summer the river took Elias Thackeray and gave her family a debt no one ever named out loud.

"Elena." Thackeray's voice was careful. "What does it say?"

* * *

They made camp on the bench rather than push on—the light was failing early behind the gorge walls, and Thackeray had gone quiet in a way that meant the river owned his attention now—and while he rigged the stove among the dead expedition's leavings, Elena sat in the tent with the page and her father's journal and read by headlamp until the words stopped being words.

Her father's entry was dated June 9th, from a camp two miles above where she now sat:

*Voss insists the source is reachable before the melt. He may be right, and God forgive me, part of me wants him to be right. But the sonar returns don't lie. There is a cavity under the dam—a void in the ice the size of a cathedral, filling with water that has nowhere to go. Elias knew. Elias tried to tell us. His last notes from the source describe the chamber singing—the same bells we heard in the gorge, but louder, structural, the sound of ice under strain. He believed something is buried in the glacier, something old, something the melt is uncovering, and he believed the dam will not hold another season. Voss says Elias was mad with altitude. I say Voss wants the discovery more than he wants his crew alive. I have hidden half our evidence here, under my daughter's name, because I no longer trust the men I hired. If you are reading this, Lena, then I did not come back, and you must decide what kind of truth is worth carrying downstream.*

Elena sat with the page a long time after she finished it. Outside, the stove hissed. The river murmured over its gravel in the dark, patient as a debt collector.

There it was, then. The thing she had flown nine thousand miles not to say. Her father had known Elias Thackeray had reached the source. Had known it and buried the knowledge in a box with her initials on it, because Elias had sent his findings to her father alone—trusting the one man upstream who might believe him—and her father had sat on that truth for fifteen years while a boy grew up fatherless on the lower river, counting miles.

And now she had brought that boy—no, that man, that hard, silent, competent man thirty feet away—to dig the truth up with his own hands.

She thought about the cache. About the careful knife-work, the surgical opening, the morphine taken and everything else left. About the wire-stitched zipper, undone and redone. Someone had come through here recently—days, not years, ago. Someone who knew what her father had hidden and had gone through it piece by piece, looking for something specific, and had restitched the crate closed when they didn't find it.

Which meant the missing expedition hadn't just vanished. Someone had come back for the evidence. Or come back to make sure no one else ever read it.

She crawled out of the tent. Thackeray sat by the stove with a tin cup he wasn't drinking from, watching the black line of the water. The firelight moved on his face and made him look briefly younger, briefly like the boy in the photograph her father had kept in his desk drawer all those years—two boys on a dock, one grinning, one solemn, neither knowing.

"The page," he said, without turning. "It's his handwriting, isn't it. My father's."

She could lie. She was good at lying; she'd had fifteen years of practice, the long slow lie of omission, the lie that lets everyone keep their comfortable shapes. She could tell him it was survey data, coordinates, anything.

Instead she held it out to him. "Read it yourself."

He took the page in both hands, the way a man takes something that might burn him, and turned it toward the firelight, and Elena watched his face as he read his own father's handwriting for the first time in fifteen years—watched the stillness take him, that terrible controlled stillness, and watched something underneath it begin, very quietly, to break.

"He reached the source," Thackeray said. His voice was almost steady. Almost. "He was *alive*. He sent word, and Marsh—" He stopped. Started again. "Your father knew. All this time, your father knew, and he never—" The page trembled in his grip. "What else are you not telling me, Dr. Marsh?"

Above them, far up the dark throat of the gorge, faint and impossible and unmistakable, the ice began to ring.
