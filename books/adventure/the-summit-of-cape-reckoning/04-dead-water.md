## 4. Dead Water

The river died a mile above the gorge.

Jude felt it before he saw it—the way the current went slack beneath the hull, the way the *Kestrel's* wake spread out behind them in lazy fans instead of folding into a clean V. Fifteen years on this water had taught him to read it like a face, and this morning it wore the expression of a man losing his argument with gravity.

"Shoaling," he said, and cut the engine.

The sound of silence came down hard after three days of motor. It was not a peaceful silence. Up here, above the Gorge of Bells, quiet had texture—a thin ringing quality, like the air itself remembering that they were close to ice. The river ahead of them split and split again, braiding into a dozen silver threads that wandered across a gravel plain so wide it looked like the sky had fallen and been pressed flat.

Elena Marsh stood at the bow with her binoculars raised, her breath pluming white. She'd barely slept since the cache—since the ransacked crates, the deliberately cut ropes, the journal page she'd shown him by lamplight and then taken back as if she regretted the showing. She hadn't spoken much about it. But she'd stopped sleeping with her back to the riverbank, and Jude understood what that change meant better than any confession would have.

"How far can we push her?" she asked without lowering the glasses.

"Maybe another half mile before we grind paint off the keel." He let the *Kestrel* drift, hands light on the wheel out of habit more than need. "After that, she's a very expensive lawn ornament."

"And the inflatable?"

"The Marsh expedition left one." He nodded toward the eastern bank, where a scatter of orange had caught his eye an hour ago—tarps, maybe, or a boat turned hull-up against the weather. "If they didn't cut it open too."

Her jaw tightened at that. Good, he thought. Let it tighten. Anger was easier to carry than dread, and God knew they'd both been hauling enough dread upriver to sink a barge.

He brought the *Kestrel* alongside the gravel bar with the kind of care his father had beaten into him before the man became a ghost: nose into the current, throttle feathered, lines ready. The shallows here were glass-clear over pale stone, so transparent the boat seemed to float on nothing, held up by its own reflection. In fifteen years of running freight and fishermen and the occasional government survey crew, Jude had never once come this far up. There was a reason the maps called everything above the gorge *dead water*—not because it didn't move, but because nothing that went up it came back worth the name.

They waded ashore through ankle-deep meltwater cold enough to make his bones ache. Elena carried her pack like she'd been born to it—she'd proven that much on the gorge portage, sure-footed and silent where he'd expected a scientist's stumbling—and she was already scanning the bank for the orange when he finished tying off.

"There." She pointed. "That's a boat. That's definitely a boat."

It was. The inflatable lay upside down on a shelf of gravel above the high-water line, its hull furred with frost, its transom cracked along one seam but repairable. Beside it sat two aluminum cases, a coil of rope gone stiff as wire, and a fuel can that sloshed hollow when Jude kicked it—nearly empty, which told him something about how desperate the last days here must have been. You don't abandon fuel unless you've got none to spare, and you don't spare any unless you're walking into country you don't expect to come back from.

Or unless someone made sure you couldn't.

He crouched by the transom and ran his thumb along the crack. Clean break, stress fracture, no blade work. Whatever had happened to the supply cache below hadn't happened to this boat. Small mercies. He'd learned to count them in units this small or not at all.

"She'll hold," he said. "Patch kit?"

Elena was already digging through the nearest case. Her hands paused over something inside, and for a moment her whole body went still in a way that made the hair rise on his neck.

"What is it?"

She lifted out a photograph, bent soft at the corners from handling. Even from where he knelt he could see it: a group shot, eight people in expedition gear arranged on a dock somewhere green and southern, squinting into sun. One of them was older than the rest, gray-bearded, standing slightly apart the way men do when they're used to giving orders and tired of it.

"My father," she said quietly. "Taken the day they launched."

Jude looked at the faces. He didn't know any of them. But he knew the type of every one of them—the hungry ones, the dutiful ones, the one grinning too wide at the edge of the frame like a man who already knows he'll be first to break. Expeditions were crews, and crews were just families with a deadline, and Jude had buried enough of both.

"We should move," he said, and hated how flat his voice came out. "We've got maybe six hours of light, and I want to be well clear of the open flats before dark. Ice country at night is a different animal."

Elena folded the photograph away without a word, but he saw her tuck it into the inner pocket, against her chest, where a person keeps the things they're not ready to look at again.

* * *

They ran the inflatable up the eastern channel, the only thread of the braid deep enough to trust, and the dead water lived a little under their keel. Jude worked the small outboard with two fingers, reading the shallows by color—tea-dark meant depth, milk-pale meant gravel, and the green-glass stretches meant glacial silt over drop-offs that could swallow a man whole. Elena handled the patch job on the transom while they drifted, working epoxy with cold-stupid fingers until he made her put her hands inside his jacket to warm them, an act so practical and so strangely intimate that neither of them mentioned it afterward.

The valley narrowed as they climbed. The walls came in closer, darker, streaked with the long gray scars of old avalanches, and the river between them grew quieter and stranger—pools connected by riffles, all of it running milky with rock flour, all of it smelling faintly of stone and something older underneath. Stone and, very faintly, iron.

It was Elena who smelled it first, or thought she did. It was Jude who saw why.

"Stop," he said.

He killed the motor and let the boat glide. Ahead, on a gravel spit where the channel bent hard around a fallen wall of moraine, something stood upright.

At fifty meters it might have been a cairn. At thirty, a survey marker. At twenty, there was no pretending anymore.

A man stood on the spit, facing upriver, frozen into the posture of a man still walking. His parka was expedition-grade, blue once, bleached gray now at the shoulders. Frost had built on him in layers, thickening his arms and hood until he seemed carved rather than clothed, a figure of packed snow with a man inside it. His legs were planted apart. His right arm was raised—not waving, not reaching, just raised, bent at the elbow, holding something against his chest.

A radio.

"Oh God," Elena whispered. "Oh God, oh God—"

"Elena." Jude's voice came out low and even, the voice he used on panicking passengers in white water, and he was grateful for it because his own pulse was anything but even. "Stay in the boat."

"He's—he's been standing there—" Her breath was coming fast and shallow. "How long does it take to—"

"Years," Jude said. "Could be years."

They grounded the boat and he went to the figure alone, boots crunching on frost-glazed gravel, each step feeling like a trespass. Up close the horror resolved into terrible detail. The man's face was a mask of ice, featureless except where wind had scoured it thin over the cheekbones. His eyes were closed. Whatever he'd been looking at, he'd chosen to stop looking at it—or been unable to keep looking—and turned his face slightly upward instead, toward the glacier at the head of the valley, toward the source.

Toward whatever he'd been trying to call on that radio.

Jude reached out and touched the radio set. It came free of the frost with a sound like tearing skin, and he held it in both hands: an expedition transceiver, heavy, professional, its casing scarred, its antenna snapped off short. He turned it over. The transmit key was depressed—jammed down, actually, wedged by a sliver of gravel driven into the mechanism, so that the set had been keyed to send, continuously, for however long this man had stood here dying.

Sending nothing. No antenna. No power. A radio screaming into a void, forever, for no one.

"Why would he—" Elena had come up beside him despite the warning; he hadn't the heart to repeat it. Her voice shook. "Why stand? Why not walk down? The boat was right there, the river was right—"

"Because he wasn't trying to leave." Jude heard himself say it before he understood it. "Look at his feet. Look which way he's facing."

She looked. The frozen man's boots were planted pointing upriver. Toward the source. He had walked up from the boat, not down from the ice. Whatever had killed him had found him coming back for something—or going forward to warn someone—and he had stopped here, in the middle of nowhere, raised his broken radio, and tried to shout a message he never got out.

Jude knelt. There was a name tape on the parka's chest, iced over. He worked it free with his thumbnail, chipping frost, careful as an archaeologist, though his hands were not steady.

The letters read: OKONKWO, D.

The world tilted sideways.

Not the world—the past. Fifteen years of it, all at once. A galley on a riverboat in another lifetime, steam rising off a pot of rice, a big laughing man with a scarred chin teaching a fourteen-year-old boy to juggle oranges because the boy's father was drunk below decks again and somebody had to keep the kid's mind off it. *Easy does it, little Thackeray. Eyes on the orange, not on your hands. Your hands already know what they're doing.* Danny Okonkwo. Cook, deckhand, the gentlest giant the upper river ever carried, who'd signed onto Elias Thackeray's last expedition because Danny never could say no to a friend, and who had vanished into the same white silence as everyone else aboard, and whose name Jude had spent fifteen years not saying aloud.

"Danny," he said. Just the name. It came out of him like something surfacing from deep water.

Elena looked at him sharply. "You know him."

"I knew him." Jude stayed kneeling. The frost mask regarded him with closed eyes, patient as the glacier that had kept him. "Danny Okonkwo. He cooked on my father's boat. He taught me to juggle." A sound came out of him that was almost a laugh and was nothing like one. "He used to say the river gives back what it takes, eventually. Just not to you, and not in order."

The wind moved down the valley then, a long cold exhalation off the ice, and far above them—so far it was more felt than heard—the glacier groaned. A deep, structural sound, the sound of a thing vast and old shifting its weight. Both of them went still and listened until it faded.

"That's the third time today," Elena said quietly.

"I know."

"Jude." She crouched beside him, close enough that he could see her breath trembling. "My father's journal said the source held evidence of something buried in the ice. Something Elias Thackeray found. If Danny Okonkwo was walking toward it—if he died trying to reach it or warn about it—then whatever's up there isn't just a discovery anymore. It's a reason. People have been dying on a straight line toward it for fifteen years."

Jude rose. He took the broken radio and stowed it in the boat, gently, the way you stow a man's ashes. Then he stood over Danny Okonkwo a moment longer, this man the river had finally given back, not to Jude, and not in order, exactly as promised.

"We bury him proper on the way down," he said. "I'm not leaving him standing."

"Yes," Elena said. "Of course."

"But first we find out what he was walking toward." Jude turned upriver, toward the narrowing valley, toward the pale wall of ice at its head that had already begun, in the failing light, to glow a faint and terrible blue. "Because Danny didn't freeze standing still. Men don't die facing what they fear. He died facing what he was trying to save us from—and I mean to know what that was before it decides we're next."

Above them, the glacier groaned again—longer this time, lower, like a bell struck underwater.

And somewhere in the ice ahead, faint as memory, a second sound answered it.
