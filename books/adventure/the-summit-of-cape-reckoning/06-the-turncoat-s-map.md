## 6. The Turncoat's Map

We found the first smoke at dusk on the fourth day above the gorge, a thin gray thread rising off the eastern bank where the valley pinched between two shoulders of black stone. I killed the outboard and let the inflatable drift, because smoke in that country meant one of two things: someone who wanted to be found, or someone who wanted to be found by no one. Either way, you didn't announce yourself with forty horsepower.

Elena crouched low in the bow, binoculars steady against the swell of the current. "Tarp," she said. "Orange. Ours."

"Expedition issue?"

"Everything we carried was expedition issue." She passed the glasses to me without looking away from the bank. "But that's not what I'm watching for."

I found it in half a minute: a figure moving behind the tarp, quick and purposeful, then gone. Then a second figure, further up the shingle, standing very still, facing us.

Facing us with a rifle slung across his back.

"There," Elena said quietly, though she knew I'd seen it. Her voice had gone flat in the way voices do when they're holding something down. "That's Halloran. He was our medic. And the other one—"

"I can see him."

"No. You can't see him properly yet." She took the binoculars back. "That's Voss."

The name landed in the boat like ballast. I'd heard it twice before on this trip—once from her father's radio logs, once from the frozen man in the blue ice who'd been clutching a transmitter set to send nothing to anyone. *Voss has the route,* the dead man's journal had said, in handwriting that got shakier as the pages turned. *Voss says the window holds.*

The window hadn't held. The man in the ice was proof of how it had closed.

"We should talk to them," Elena said.

"One of them is armed."

"One of them is my father's colleague, Jude. They both are. Whatever happened up here—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way you step onto river ice. "Whatever happened up here, they're the only people alive who were there when it happened. That makes them worth more than the risk."

I looked at the rifleman on the shingle, small against the gray rock, and thought about the supply cache in the gorge—cut open, not torn, blades taken to canvas with deliberation. Someone had wanted those supplies and hadn't wanted anyone following them to know the taking had happened. I thought about the body frozen upright like a man waiting for a bus that never came, his radio transmitting silence into silence.

"Okay," I said. "But we come in slow, and we keep the water at our backs. Nobody corners me on land."

Elena almost smiled. It wasn't much of one. "You sound like my father."

"He sounds smarter than me, then."

She didn't laugh. Neither did I. We turned the bow toward the smoke.

* * *

Halloran met us at the waterline with his hands visible and his rifle still slung, which was its own kind of message. He was gaunt in the way men get when they've been eating expedition rations and burning expedition calories for too long, cheekbones like tent stakes, eyes red-rimmed but level. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and something sour underneath it—the smell of fear that's been worn so long it stops being an event and becomes a climate.

"Thackeray," he said, reading the name stenciled on the boat's hull before I could offer it. "You're Marsh's boat man. The old charter."

"The old charter," I agreed.

"You look like him."

"I get that a lot lately."

His gaze flicked past me to Elena, and something moved through his face—relief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Dr. Marsh. Thank God. When we heard a boat—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "You shouldn't be here. Neither of you. You need to turn around."

"That's the second time this week someone's told me that," I said. "The first one's dead."

Halloran flinched. Behind him, at the edge of the firelight, the other man stood up.

Voss was shorter than I'd built him in my head, which surprised me. In the journals and the dead man's accusations he'd grown enormous—a giant of bad decisions, a colossus of ambition. In person he was a compact, weathered man in his fifties with a climber's forearms and a scholar's stoop, frost-nipped at the fingertips, and he looked at us the way a man looks at a bill he's been dreading. Not guilty, exactly. Accounted-for.

"Dr. Marsh," he said. His voice was quiet and precise, an accent I couldn't place under the cold. "You have your father's stubbornness. I want you to know I recognized it from the air, three weeks ago, when I last saw him alive."

Elena went very still beside me. "You saw him alive? Three weeks ago?"

"I did." Voss held her stare without flinching, and I'll give him this: it cost him. I watched it cost him. "And I've spent every day since knowing exactly what I did when I walked away from him."

* * *

They fed us tea that tasted of spruce needles and old metal, and around the fire Voss told it straight, no varnish, which was either honesty or a performance so good it amounted to the same thing.

"We were nine when we left the gorge," he said. "My father's maps put a meltwater channel through the upper braids—a shortcut, days saved. I believed the window would hold. Dr. Marsh believed the ice was telling us otherwise." He turned his mug slowly in his ruined fingers. "He was right. Of course he was right. But I had funding deadlines and a board of trustees who'd already written the press release, and I made the arithmetic come out the way I needed it to. I split the party. Took four willing ones onward. Left five at the bivouac with most of the radios."

"And then?" Elena asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't; I saw her fold them into her sleeves.

"Then the route worsened, exactly as your father predicted. A serac field came down across the channel on day six. We lost Rasmussen. The others refused to go on—I don't blame them; I wouldn't go on now—and I..." He exhaled. "I went on alone. Two days. Far enough to see the source lake through the pass, far enough to understand that everything my maps promised was a lie drawn by a man who'd never stood where I was standing. Then I came back. And the bivouac was empty. Torn down, gear scattered, tracks heading downriver. I followed the tracks to the gorge cache and found it cut open. After that I stopped following tracks and started surviving. Halloran found me ten days ago. He'd gone back for the wounded and gotten cut off when the ice shifted."

"Empty," Elena repeated. "You're telling me you came back and my father was simply *gone*?"

"I'm telling you what I found. I've had three weeks to imagine the rest, and every version I imagine is worse than the last." Voss reached inside his jacket and drew out a folded sheet of waterproof paper, handled like scripture. "This is half the survey map. The western half. Your father kept the east. Without both halves there's no route to the source that doesn't cross the dam itself, and nobody crosses the dam. Not now. Not after what I heard it doing."

The map was beautiful even in firelight—contour lines fine as hair, the meltwater channel inked in confident blue, annotations in two hands. One was Voss's. The other I knew without being told, because I'd grown up watching it get written across grocery lists and chart margins and the flyleaves of books I still owned.

My father's hand. Except it wasn't—it was Elena's father's, and the shock of how much the two scripts resembled each other hit me somewhere below the ribs. Two men who'd learned their letters on the same river, thirty years apart.

"He was trying to turn everyone back," I said. "Before the split. Wasn't he."

Voss looked at me for a long moment. "You're Thackeray's son."

"He was trying to turn everyone back," I said again.

"Yes." Voss set the map on the crate between us, smoothing it flat with both palms, an offering or a surrender. "Every morning, another reason. The ice was singing—that's what he called it, singing—hollow notes under the surface where there shouldn't have been hollows. He said the dam was rotten from the inside. He said if it let go, everything downstream to the gorge would go with it, and the gorge would funnel it straight into the lower villages." His voice dropped. "I told him discovery didn't wait for caution. I told him the board had invested too much. I used words like *legacy*, God help me. And he looked at me the way your father must have looked at men like me his whole life, Mr. Thackeray—with pity—and he said, *the mountain doesn't care about your legacy.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere up-valley, faint as a struck bell, the glacier rang one long note and fell silent.

Elena's hand found the map's edge. "Then we need the eastern half."

Something changed in Halloran then. He'd been sitting apart, feeding sticks to the fire, and he stopped mid-motion, and when he raised his head his face had closed like a door.

"No," he said.

"Halloran—"

"You don't understand what he's asking." The medic stood, and his hand wasn't near the rifle, but it wasn't far, either. "You don't know what's up there. I do. I carried the eastern half for six weeks, and I listened to your father beg us to turn around, and I watched Voss walk anyway, and I stayed with the ones who stayed—and then the ice started *moving*, Doctor. Not groaning. Moving. Whole sections of the bivouac slope sliding like tide. Your father ordered us downriver. Ordered us. And half the party wouldn't go without the map, and he wouldn't give up the map, because he said the map was the only thing that might stop anyone else from going up." Halloran's breath came ragged. "There was a fight. There was a rifle. I'm not going to tell you who pointed it, because it doesn't matter anymore, because afterward your father took the eastern half and the last working radio and he went *up*, alone, toward the dam—to prove it, I think, or to die proving it—and the rest of us scattered, and I have spent every night since hearing that glacier sing."

Silence held the camp. Even the river seemed to lean in.

Elena rose slowly, her eyes wet and blazing at once. "He went up alone."

"He went up alone," Halloran said. "And the eastern half of the only safe route went with him. Which means the only way to follow him is the way he forbade us all to go." He looked at me then, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Across the dam. Across the thing he died trying to warn us about."

Up the valley, the glacier answered him—one long, hollow note, then another, closer together, like a bell learning to toll.

Voss laid his half of the map gently on top of the crate, weighting the corner with a stone.

"Then we'd better decide fast," he said, "which of us is going to listen to it."
