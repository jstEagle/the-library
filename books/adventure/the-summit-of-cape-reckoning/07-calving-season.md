## 7. Calving Season

The mountain spoke first.

Elena heard it before she was fully awake—a sound like a cathedral door swinging on rusted hinges, deep and groaning, rising from somewhere beneath the glacier itself. She lay in the dark of the tent, breath fogging in the cold, and listened to the ice complain. It had been talking all night in small voices: ticks, cracks, the whisper of settling snow. But this was different. This was a voice with weight behind it, the kind of sound that made her think of her father's journals and the word he'd underlined twice in the margins of his last entry—*unstable*—as if once hadn't been enough to hold the meaning.

Beside her, Jude was already awake. She could tell by his breathing, which had gone shallow and deliberate, the breathing of a man listening with his whole body.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since maybe three." He didn't move. "It's getting worse."

Outside, the light through the nylon had turned that strange glacial gray that wasn't quite dawn. Elena crawled out into it and stood blinking at a world remade overnight. The sky was low and bruised, pressing down on the valley like a lid. And the glacier—the great blue-white shoulder of it that shouldered up from the head of the valley toward the source—had changed its posture. She couldn't have said how she knew; she only knew it the way you know a person is angry from across a room. The seracs near the summit wall leaned differently. Fresh fracture lines scored the face above them, hairline white scars against the deeper blue.

Jude came out beside her, pulling on his gloves. He studied the ice the way he studied water—with that flat, patient attention that missed nothing—and what she saw settle over his face frightened her more than the sounds had.

"That window we were counting on," he said. "It's closed."

"It can't be. Voss's notes said—"

"Voss's notes were written by a man who wanted the route to stay open." He turned to her. "Elena. Listen to it. That's not settling. That's a dam telling you it's failing."

* * *

They broke camp fast, and it was Voss who made them break it faster.

He'd been different since Dead Water—quieter, watchful, the half-map folded into an inner pocket of his parka like something alive he was keeping warm. All morning he'd been ahead of the others on the rope, setting a pace too hard for the terrain, and when they stopped at the base of the icefall to re-rig, he stood with his hands on his hips and looked up at the route with an expression Elena recognized from conference rooms and grant committees: the look of a man calculating how much he could get away with.

"We go now," he said. "Today. Straight through the icefall while it's still frozen solid. Cold holds it together. We climb in the cold, we're past the seracs by noon."

"The seracs are *moving*," Jude said. He kept his voice level, but Elena heard the iron under it. "You've been listening to the same mountain I have. That icefall is calving apart in slow motion. We wait for the next cold snap—two days, maybe three—and go through at night when it's locked down."

"Two days." Voss laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of frayed contempt. "Two days is how long my brother spent waiting at this latitude. You found what's left of him holding a radio." He stepped closer, and Elena saw that his eyes were red-rimmed, sleepless, burning. "I have spent six weeks watching people die politely, Thackeray. Waiting. Being careful. I am done being careful. The source is four miles up that valley, and I am going to stand where your father stood, and I am going to see what he saw, and anyone who doesn't like the pace can turn around."

"You'd kill us all to get there first."

"I'd *arrive*," Voss said, "which is more than your father managed."

Elena saw it happen in Jude then—saw the change come over him like weather. His hand moved toward the ice axe at his belt, not fast, just inevitably, and his voice dropped into something quiet and terrible.

"Say that again."

But it was Elena who stepped between them, hand flat against Jude's chest, feeling the hammering beneath the layers. "Not here. Not like this." She looked at Voss. "You want to move? Fine. We move. But we take the low line around the icefall, along the moraine, like Jude says. And if the mountain tells us to stop, we stop."

Voss held her gaze for a long moment. Then he shrugged, shouldered his pack, and started up the moraine without another word—which was its own kind of answer, and none of them believed it would hold.

* * *

The low line was slower and, for two hours, safer. They climbed the lateral moraine in single file, boots grinding on stone and dirty ice, the icefall towering to their left like a frozen waterfall caught mid-plunge. Elena kept her eyes on the route and her ears on the mountain, cataloguing sounds the way her father had taught her to catalogue data: the tick-tick-tick of expanding ice in the sun, the distant artillery of rockfall off the headwall, and underneath everything, continuous now, that deep tectonic groan, like the glacier clearing its throat.

Twice they stopped and simply stood, silent, until it passed.

The second time, Voss didn't stop.

She saw him peel away ahead—saw him cut left, off the moraine, angling up onto the glacier's flank where the route ran straight and white and fast toward the col below the source. "Voss!" she shouted, but the wind took it, and he didn't turn, just kept climbing with that terrible efficiency, rope trailing loose behind him because he'd refused to tie in with them days ago.

"He's going through the icefall," Jude said. "Right through the middle of it."

"Then we follow the edge and cut him off at the col—"

The mountain answered before she finished.

It began as a crack—a single report, sharp as a rifle, from high on the summit wall. Elena's whole body understood it before her mind did; she was already moving, already diving for the convoluted blue shelter of a crevasse lip as the crack became a roar and the roar became the world falling. The serac went like a building demolished, hundreds of tons of blue ice detaching from the wall and hitting the glacier slope below, and the impact came through the ice into her bones, and then the surface she'd been running on simply opened.

She was falling.

Blue swallowed her. The world rotated—sky, ice, sky—and then rope snapped taut at her waist and the fall became a swing, her shoulder wrenching, her boots skating down a wall of glassy ice, and she stopped, hanging, thirty feet down inside the throat of a crevasse with snow-dust sifting past her like ash.

Above her, daylight. A jagged rim of it. And Jude's face, framed there, wild with something she'd never seen on him before.

"ELENA." His voice bounced down the blue walls. "Talk to me. Are you hurt?"

"My shoulder—I'm okay—" Her own voice sounded far away. She looked down. Below her boots the crevasse went on into darkness, depthless, breathing cold. She pressed herself against the ice wall and did not look down again. "Jude. Voss. Where's Voss?"

Silence above. Then, quieter: "He was on the slope. Directly under it."

She closed her eyes. In the dark behind them she saw the man's red-rimmed burning eyes, his brother's radio transmitting nothing, six weeks of dying politely. She thought: *he got exactly what he was racing toward.* And she thought: *God forgive us, we let him lead.*

"Come on," Jude called down, and his voice had changed—hardened into pure function, the voice of a man hauling nets in a storm. "Rope's good. I've got you anchored. Climb, Elena. Hand over hand. Don't stop, don't look down. I've got you."

She climbed. Her shoulder screamed. The ice wall was wet glass under her front-points, and twice her footing sheared away and she swung free over the dark, and both times the rope held and Jude's voice came down steady as a metronome—*again, again, again*—until her glove broke the surface snow and his hand closed around her forearm, iron-hard, and hauled her out onto the solid rock of the moraine.

For a moment she just lay there, gasping, the sky wheeling overhead, snow-dust still drifting down like the aftermath of an explosion. Then strong arms gathered her up and she was against Jude's chest, and she felt him shaking—not the controlled tremor of cold but something deeper, something he couldn't govern.

"I thought—" he started, and stopped. Started again. "When the rope went tight. I thought I'd lost—" He didn't finish it. He pulled back and looked at her, and whatever was in his face, he let her see all of it. "I spent fifteen years telling myself the river takes what it takes and you don't fight it. You just count the miles and keep moving." His grip tightened on her arms. "I'm done counting. Do you hear me? Whatever's up at that source, whatever my father found—it isn't worth you. Nothing up there is worth you."

Below them, where the serac had landed, the glacier smoked with pulverized ice. Somewhere under that white shroud was what remained of Marcus Voss, and with him, probably, the half of the map folded in his inner pocket.

And beyond the debris field, up the valley, the route to the source lay open—unguarded now, unargued, the mountain having settled the debate in the most final way it knew.

Jude followed her gaze upward. When he spoke again, his voice was quiet and stripped bare.

"He's gone. And the map's gone with him." He looked at her. "So it's just us now. Whatever we decide, we decide together."

Elena turned toward the source. The groaning had stopped—for the moment—but the silence felt worse, like the held breath before a cough. High on the summit wall, fresh scar tissue gleamed where the serac had torn free, and she understood with cold clarity that the mountain had given them a warning shot, not a pardon.

Her father had come up this valley believing the ice was hiding something worth dying for. Her father had been wrong about a great many things, and dead six weeks before she ever hired a riverboat captain to find him.

But the truth was still up there. And now, finally, so was nothing else.

"Together," she said, and shouldered her pack, and started climbing.
