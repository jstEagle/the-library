## 8. The Source

The ice opened like a throat.

Jude had seen the glacier do many things in his life—shrug off seracs, swallow crevasses whole, groan through the night like a ship working loose from its moorings—but he had never seen it *invite*. Yet that was what the mouth of the meltwater cavity looked like: an arch of blue glass taller than a man, breathing out air that smelled of stone and deep cold and something older than either. The melt season had eaten into the dam's flank and found a hollow there, and the hollow had been waiting.

Elena stood beside him at the threshold, her breath pluming, her face lit from within by the strange submarine light that poured out of the dark. Behind them, Voss hung back with the gear, saying nothing, which for Voss was a kind of prayer.

"It's stable," Elena said, though her voice made it a question. She had spent three days reading the recovered journals by headlamp, and she knew this place the way you know a house you've only seen in photographs. "Thackeray's notes say the chamber runs two hundred meters under the ice. He called it the Blue Room."

*My father named it,* Jude thought. *He was down here, in the dark, naming things.*

"Rope," he said aloud, because his voice needed a job or it would break. "Twelve meters between anchors. We go slow."

They went slow.

The light inside was the color of deep water over white sand—not blue exactly but the memory of blue, filtered through forty meters of compressed ice that had swallowed every wavelength except the one the glacier wanted to keep. It fell in shafts where the ceiling thinned, and in those shafts the air itself seemed to have texture, motes of ancient dust drifting like plankton. The floor was bare ice, scalloped by meltwater into long smooth channels, and their boots rang on it faintly, like footsteps on the deck of a sunken ship.

Jude had imagined this moment fifteen years' worth of ways. In most of them he was angry. In some he was triumphant. In none of them did he feel what he actually felt now, which was smallness—the specific smallness of a son standing inside the last room his father ever occupied, knowing the man had walked these same channels, touched these same walls, and then sat down in this cold and never got up.

"There." Elena's lamp swung left. "That's a camp."

It was tucked against the chamber wall where an ice ridge offered shelter from any drip or shift: a tent gone stiff as sheet metal, a stove, two crates, a sled frame stripped of its runners. And beside it, weighted down with stones against a wind that could not possibly reach this deep, a scatter of papers.

Jude knelt. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Fifteen years of river work had taught his hands to be steady while everything above them drowned.

The top page was a journal entry, the handwriting slanted and hurried, the ink browned with age but legible:

*Day 41. The cavity is larger than we hoped—large enough to be a route, large enough to be a warning. The dam is rotten. I've said it until Marsh won't listen. There is water moving under the summit that has no business moving, and the ice above us is thinner than any of them want to believe. I am sending this downstream with Rourke tomorrow. If I don't come out, whoever finds this should know: I reached the source. It was never treasure. It was a clock, and it is running down.*

Jude read it twice. Then a third time, his lips moving without sound.

*Rourke.* He knew the name. Everyone on the upper river knew the name of the man they'd found frozen upright in the shallows, radio in his hands, set to transmit nothing. So his father had sent the warning out. It had gotten as far as Dead Water, and no farther. The message had died ten miles short of anyone who could have carried it home.

"He tried to stop them," Elena said quietly. She was crouched across the camp from him, lifting a oilcloth packet from one of the crates with the care of a surgeon. "My father. You understand that, don't you? Thackeray wasn't defying the expedition. He was trying to save it."

"I know." Jude's voice came out rougher than he intended. "I always knew. I just didn't know if I knew it or if I'd invented it."

She looked at him across the blue light, and whatever she saw in his face made her turn back to the crate and keep working, giving him the privacy of tasks.

There were photographs. Dozens of them, developed in the field from a battered camera, pinned under the same stones. The chamber in every season of light. The channels of meltwater, mapped in pencil directly onto the prints. And in several of them, a man in a red parka with a beard going gray at the chin, squinting into the lens with an expression Jude knew from mirrors and from other people's memories—a look that was half laughter and half apology, the look of a man who was always about to leave.

His father. Not a legend. Not a ghost. A tired man in bad light who had been right about everything and been unable to make anyone listen.

Jude picked up the closest photograph and held it a long time. He did not cry. He had done his crying years ago, alone in the wheelhouse of his boat somewhere below the Gorge of Bells, and what was left now was drier and stranger: a feeling like a knot untying so slowly he could feel each fiber let go.

"You're not drowned," he said to the photograph. "You were never drowned. You were just *here*, being stubborn, being right." He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "God help me, you were right."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the daylight, cataloguing by lamplight while the glacier ticked and settled around them like a house at night. Journals—four of them, complete. Maps drawn on tracing paper, annotated in two hands: his father's tight slant, and another hand Jude recognized now from the ransacked cache, Marsh's, arguing in the margins. *Overstated. The readings don't support it.* And beneath that, later, in different ink: *Understated. God forgive me, he was understating it.*

Elena held that page for a long moment before she passed it to Jude.

"He knew," she said. "At the end, my father knew Thackeray was right, and he kept pushing anyway, because the source was *there*, because turning back meant admitting—" She stopped. Breathed. "Because men are fools, Captain. Even good ones."

"Especially the ones with something to prove."

"Is that what you think he had?"

Jude considered it honestly, which was more than he'd managed in fifteen years. "A name. A discovery. Same thing, maybe, to a certain kind of man." He nodded at the journals. "And my father had the opposite problem. He had the truth and nobody wanted it. Turns out both kinds of man die on this river."

"Not today," Elena said. "Today we take it all out. Every page, every print, the recordings—"

"The what?"

She lifted the last item from the crate: a field recorder, heavy, analog, its casing furred with frost. "There's a reel inside. It's labeled." She turned it so his lamp caught the masking-tape label, written in the red parka man's hand:

*FOR WHOEVER COMES UP. PLAY THIS BEFORE YOU GO HIGHER.*

Voss appeared at the edge of the lamplight, drawn by something in their silence, and for once even he had nothing cynical ready. "Play it," he said simply.

Elena checked the mechanism, blew warmth onto the transport with cupped hands, and pressed the key.

Tape hiss first, long and patient. Then a voice—and Jude's whole body recognized it before his mind did, the way a riverbed knows water. Low. Roughened by cold. Unhurried, the voice of a man who had decided that panic was a waste of calories.

*"This is Elias Thackeray, recording at the source, day forty-three. If you're hearing this, you came up past the gorge, past Rourke, past every warning I left, so I'll assume you're the stubborn kind and skip the pleasantries.*

*"Here's what matters. This dam is failing. Not someday—this season, maybe this month. The meltwater's cut a channel under the summit wider than the gorge itself, and the ice holding it back is thinner than my arm in places. When it goes, everything below Dead Water goes with it, and that includes your boats, your camps, and the town, if the surge reaches the lower bends.*

*"I'm staying to finish the survey so the warning is precise enough to be believed. Precise is the only language bureaucrats speak. Rourke took the journals down yesterday. If he made it, you already know all this, and you can laugh at an old man talking to a machine.*

*"If he didn't make it—if you're standing where I'm standing and nobody warned you—then hear me now. Turn around. Take the records. Run the river ahead of the flood, because there will be a flood, and the only people who survive it will be the ones already moving when it starts.*

*"One more thing."*

The tape hissed. When the voice came back it was closer to the recorder, quieter, and Jude closed his eyes because he could see the man leaning in, red parka, gray beard, half laughter and half apology.

*"To my boy, if it's my boy listening. I know what I cost you. I know you learned rivers because I left you one. That's not the inheritance I wanted, but it might be the one you needed—you'll run water no one else can, and one day you'll need to. Maybe today. Be steadier than me, Jude. Be smarter than me. And when you get clear of this mountain, stop counting the miles. They were never the point.*

*"End of record."*

The tape ran on into silence. Nobody moved. The blue light lay over them like water over stones.

Jude opened his eyes. The knot in his chest had finished untying; in its place was something load-bearing, structural, a beam set true after fifteen years of sag. His father had known. Had spoken to him across the ice and the years, had asked him for exactly the thing he was already becoming: a man who could run the break.

"He recorded this four days before he died," Elena said softly, checking the survey dates. "The cold took him here, in his sleep, after he finished the measurements. He never tried to come down."

"Because coming down meant leaving the numbers unfinished." Jude gathered the journals, the photographs, the recorder, wrapping each in oilcloth with hands that did not shake. "He stayed to make the warning believable. That was the whole man, start to finish."

Voss cleared his throat. "Then we'd best make him believable too. All of it comes out. Every page."

"Yes," Jude said. "All of it."

He was reaching for the last crate when the sound came.

Not a groan this time. A *crack*—deep, single, absolute, like the mountain clearing its throat to speak—and the floor of the Blue Room shivered under their boots. Somewhere far above, a seam that had held since before human memory let go of its neighbor. Fine snow sifted down through a shaft of blue light, glittering, unhurried, beautiful, falling like the first flakes of something enormous beginning.

Elena looked up. Voss looked up. Jude looked at the recorder in his hands, at the label in his father's writing—*PLAY THIS BEFORE YOU GO HIGHER*—and understood that the old man's clock had not merely been running down.

It had struck.

"We're leaving," Jude said. "Now. Everything we can carry, nothing we can't—and pray the river's still a river when we get to it."
