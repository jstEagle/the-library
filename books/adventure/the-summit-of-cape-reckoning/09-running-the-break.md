## 9. Running the Break

**JUDE**

The mountain spoke first.

Not a groan this time, not the deep bell-tone they'd been hearing for two days, but a crack like the sky splitting along a seam—a sound Jude felt in his back teeth, in the soles of his boots, in the old scar tissue along his ribs. He was halfway down the blue chamber's slope with his father's journals sealed in a dry bag against his chest when the second crack came, and then a third, each one closer together, like a drummer losing patience.

"It's going," Elena shouted from above. Her voice bounced off the ice walls and came back doubled, tripled, as if the glacier itself were repeating the news to every chamber of its body.

Jude didn't answer. There was nothing to say that the ice wasn't already saying better. He ran.

They came out of the source into light so white it hurt—the sun had broken through at last, cruel and indifferent, and it lay across the ice field like a sheet over a corpse. Below them, maybe four miles down the valley, the meltwater lake that had backed up behind the dam for who knew how many years sat fat and gleaming, and along its near shore he could see the water already dropping, pulling back from the ice like a tide going out.

That was the worst sign of all. Water that retreats is water gathering itself.

"The boat," he said. "Everything we can carry. Now."

Voss was already moving. Whatever else the man was—turncoat, coward, the reason three good people were frozen into this valley—he had come out of the source with his arms full and his mouth shut, and he hadn't stopped since. He'd carried the last of the expedition's gear down the moraine without being asked, twice, and when Jude caught his eye across the ice the man only said, "I know what I did. Let me carry something."

So Jude let him.

The inflatable lay where they'd cached it below the serac field, half-buried under snow they'd shoveled over it against the wind. But the portage from the source had torn it—one long rip along the starboard tube where it had dragged over rock, and a valve weeping air in a thin silver whistle that sounded, in the middle of everything, almost peaceful.

Jude knelt in the snow and opened the repair kit with hands that wanted to shake and refused to.

*Fifteen years,* he thought, spreading the patch cement with the little spatula, *and it all comes down to rubber and glue.*

He thought about his father teaching him to read water on the lower river, how the old man would put his hand flat on the surface and say, *the river tells you what it's going to do before it does it, boy, you just have to listen.* He thought about the recording still playing in his head—his father's voice, hoarse and calm, describing the cavity under the dam, the melt rate, the arithmetic of collapse. *If anyone finds this, do not come up here. And if you're up here already, go down fast and don't stop.*

His father had died trying to warn people away. The least Jude could do was listen.

Behind him the mountain cracked again, longer this time, a sound like a train falling off the world.

"Talk to me," Elena said, crouching beside him, pressing tape into his palm before he asked for it.

"Patch holds or it doesn't." He smoothed the rubber down, working from the center outward, chasing every bubble of air to the edge. "Valve seat's scored—I can wrap it but it'll seep. We'll be bailing."

"How long?"

"To fix? Four minutes. To run the break?" He looked downriver—at the braided shallows they'd labored up, now filling, the channels finding each other, joining, becoming one brown muscle of water. "Nobody's ever done it. Nobody's ever tried."

Elena was quiet a moment. Then: "My father used to say you were the best boatman on this river. That you had your father's eyes for it."

"My father's eyes are buried up there," Jude said, jerking his chin toward the source. "Let's hope he left me the rest."

* * *

**ELENA**

She catalogued what they had because cataloguing was what she knew how to do, and because her hands needed work or they would start trembling and she could not afford trembling. Not now.

One inflatable, patched, rated for a load it would be carrying double. Three paddles. Two dry bags of records and instruments—her father's data, Thackeray's final testimony, the photographs of the blue chamber that would end the argument about the dam forever, assuming anyone lived to publish them. One rifle, unloaded; she'd made Voss leave the bolt in the ice. One radio, dead. One flare gun with two flares. Three people.

Three people, and a river coming down to meet them.

She stood at the top of the bank and watched the flood arrive. It came the way disasters do—not all at once but in stages, each stage worse than the last. First the side channels went, then the gravel bars vanished, then the standing waves in the main channel grew heads, crests curling back on themselves, whole trees turning end over end in the current like matchsticks in a drain. The roar of it filled the valley floor to wall, a sound with no space left around it, and the spray hung in the air so thick the far shore blurred.

"We launch from here?" Voss asked. He had the gear staged in a neat line, everything lashed, everything balanced. His hands were steady. She noticed that. Whatever broke in this man above the gorge, whatever made him split the party and walk away from the dying, it had re-knit into something hard and useful. Guilt, maybe, doing the work courage couldn't.

"No." Jude came up beside her, and she saw him reading the water the way she'd seen him read it every day since the gorge—head tilted, eyes moving, lips slightly parted, listening. "Here it's all debris. First mile is a strainer—we'd be wrapped around a spruce inside ninety seconds. We drop down to the cutbank past the slide." He pointed. "There's an eddy behind the point. If it hasn't flooded out, we launch there, ferry across the tongue, and take the right channel through the boulder garden while it's still deep enough to float."

"And if it has flooded out?"

"Then we launch into the throat of it and pray the patch holds." He glanced at her, and for a moment the river-boat captain was gone and someone younger looked out—someone fifteen years old, watching a plane fly away without his father. "Elena. Once we're in, I can't stop. Not for anything. You understand? If someone goes in the water, I can't turn around. The river won't let me."

She understood. She thought of her father somewhere in this valley, or under it, or scattered along it, and understood that grief was a luxury the next hour would not sell her.

"I understand," she said. "Go."

* * *

**JUDE**

The eddy behind the point held. Barely.

It was maybe twelve feet of slack green water behind a shelf of collapsed bank, and the flood tore past its lip close enough to touch. Jude backed the boat in, and for thirty seconds—thirty seconds of silence except for their own breathing—they floated in a pocket of stillness with hell running alongside.

He checked everything twice. Patch: holding. Valve wrap: seeping, slow, manageable. Load: lashed low and centered. Paddles: Voss bow, Elena mid-thwart on bail duty, Jude stern. Life jackets cinched. Flare gun in his jacket, records bag strapped to his chest.

He looked upstream once. The lake was nearly empty now. What remained of the dam stood in the channel like a rotten tooth, and even as he watched, a slab the size of a house leaned, sighed, and folded into the current. The wave that came off it was coming for them.

"Okay," Jude said. "When I say go, paddle like the dead are chasing you. Because they are."

He dug the blades in. "Go."

The eddy peeled them out and the river took the boat the way a fist takes a bird.

There was no word for what the first minute was like. The water was not water anymore—it was a solid thing with moods, and it slammed them sideways, lifted the stern until Elena screamed something that might have been a prayer, and drove them down the tongue at a speed Jude had never coaxed out of any boat he'd ever built or bought. Spray hit like gravel. The cold went through the wetsuit in one breath and found the bone.

"Bow! Left channel—HARD left!" He saw the boulder garden opening ahead, black teeth in brown foam, and beyond it the gorge walls narrowing where the river funneled into the run they'd poled up two weeks ago in another life. "Voss, with me—now!"

Voss paddled like a man paying a debt, which he was. Every stroke was clean, deep, timed to Jude's count without being told. Twice the boat hit glancing blows that spun it half around, and twice Voss hauled it straight again before Jude finished calling the correction. Somewhere in the chaos Jude felt something loosen in his chest—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe never—but recognition. This was what the man should have been all along.

"Elena!" he shouted. "Bail!"

She was already bailing. The valve wrap had given up its argument; the starboard tube was soft, sagging, the boat listing and taking water over the gunwale in sheets. She scooped with the bail bucket and threw it overboard with a rhythm as steady as a metronome, hair plastered to her face, eyes wide and fixed on him, and she was smiling. God help him, she was smiling—wild and white-eyed, terror and joy fused into one expression, and Jude realized he was wearing the same one.

This was it. This was the thing the river had been teaching him his whole life and he'd refused to learn: that fear and love were the same water, and you rode both or you drowned.

The gorge swallowed them.

Walls rose on either side, close enough to scrape, and the flood compressed between them into a single standing wave train taller than the boat—hills of water marching downstream, and the boat surfing down their backs, weightless, airborne, then slamming down into the troughs hard enough to compress spines. Jude steered by instinct older than memory, hips and blade and prayer, threading the gaps between holes that could hold a school bus underwater. A log swept past close enough to touch. He didn't look at it. Looking at things was how you hit them.

Halfway through the gorge the starboard tube gave a final silver whistle and folded.

The boat lurched, slewed, began to spin. Water poured across the thwart. For one black instant Jude saw the whole arithmetic of it—three people, one swamped raft, a gorge with no eddies—and then his hands moved without him, years of river living in them, and he was shouting, "Weight right! Everyone right! Paddle RIGHT!"

They threw themselves starboard. The listing tube kissed the wall of water and the current, instead of capsizing them, caught the high side and rolled them upright-ish, half-swamped but driving, and the gorge spat them out into the widening valley below like a seed.

* * *

**ELENA**

They came out of the gorge into a world remade.

The river spread wide and shallow and fast, braiding again, and the boat—half-deflated, riding on its floor and one good tube—slowed enough to steer, slowed enough to breathe. Elena bailed with arms that no longer belonged to her, and laughed, actually laughed, at the absurdity of it: alive, soaked, half a boat, four miles of flood behind them and the whole lower river ahead.

Then she turned and looked upstream.

Above the gorge, where the summit had stood for ten thousand years, a column of mist and ice-dust was rising into the sunlight, vast and slow and terrible, unfolding like a white flower blooming in reverse. The sound reached them a full minute later—a boom, then a long rolling thunder, then silence, then the river beneath them shuddering as the flood pulse arrived.

The source was open to the sky now. Whatever else the glacier had kept, it kept no longer.

Beside her, Jude stared at the rising cloud, and she watched him say goodbye to something—to the myth, to the man, to the fifteen years of counting miles—and when he finally looked away, his eyes were wet and clear at the same time.

"That's him done," Jude said quietly. "Now it's just us and the water."

Ahead, the lower river unrolled—wide, brown, and still rising, carrying whole islands of debris, and somewhere down there, days away, the last charter town, the dock, the world. The boat sat low in the water. The patch was holding by a memory. And the river, which had taken everything it wanted, was not yet finished giving.

Jude set his blade and pointed them downstream.

"Hold on," he said. "It gets worse before it gets home."
