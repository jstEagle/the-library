## 10. Downstream Light

The river gave them back gently, the way a fist opens.

Jude felt it before he understood it—the water smoothing beneath the hull, the roar behind them thinning to a long gray murmur, the spray turning from sleet to rain to nothing at all. The inflatable rode low and crooked, one tube half-flattened where a boulder had kissed it in the last mile of white water, but it rode. That was all he'd asked of it. That was all he'd asked of anything for three days.

He looked back once.

The summit was going. He could see it even from here, even through the haze that hung over the lower gorge like breath on glass—a column of dust and shattered ice rising off the high country where the dam had stood for ten thousand years, standing up out of the earth the way smoke stands up from a doused fire. It didn't look violent from this distance. It looked almost ceremonial. As if the mountain had waited until they were clear, until the last of his father's records were lashed under the thwart, until Elena's hand had stopped bleeding, and only then allowed itself to fall.

"Is it—" Voss started, from the bow, and couldn't finish.

"Yeah," Jude said. "It's done."

Nobody said anything else for a long time. The river carried them. It was brown now with everything it had been given—silt and gravel and the ground-up bones of a glacier older than any name anyone had ever put on it—and it moved with a kind of purpose Jude had never felt in it before, as if it too were delivering something, as if it too had cargo.

Elena sat beside him at the stern, her shoulder against his, her father's journal sealed in the dry bag between her boots. She hadn't let go of that bag since the source. Not through the portage around the first breach, not through the night they'd spent wedged in a scree gully listening to the ice die above them, not through any of it. Her fingers rested on it now the way a person rests their hand on a sleeping animal.

"How far to the lower takeout?" she asked.

Jude did the arithmetic without thinking—fifteen years of arithmetic, miles logged like coins in a jar—and then, for the first time he could remember, he stopped. He let the number go unfinished.

"Not far," he said.

* * *

They came off the water at Miller's Bend in the last hour of light, where the river widened into the slow green reach Jude had run a hundred times and where, fifteen years ago, a different boat had put in without its captain and a boy had stood on this same gravel bar waiting for a father who was never coming down.

The wrecked state of them announced itself before they did. Voss went ahead, limping, waving both arms at the fuel dock until old Sam Petrie came out with a shotgun already broken open, and then lowered it, and then just stood there staring—at Voss's frost-split face, at the torn raft, at the two figures in the stern who looked less like rescuers and rescued than like people who had been somewhere no one comes back from. Word went up the hill on the radio. By dark there were forty people on the bank with lanterns, and someone had a fire going, and someone else had coffee, and the questions came at them like hail—*were there others, how far up did you get, is it true about the dam*—and Jude answered what he could and deflected the rest, because some of it wasn't his to tell yet, and some of it belonged in an envelope, and some of it belonged to a dead man who had earned the right to speak first.

They took Elena to the clinic in Thackeray's Landing—his landing, named for a grandfather he'd never met, the whole town built on the trade his family had run for four generations—and Jude sat outside the door on a bench with peeling green paint while a doctor he'd known since childhood stitched her forearm and checked her ribs and asked her, twice, if she wanted anything for the dreams that would come. She said no to the pills. Through the window he watched her sit very still on the paper-covered table, holding the dry bag in her lap, and when she caught him looking she didn't look away.

Voss slept in the church basement. He'd tried to say something to Jude on the walk up from the dock—something with *sorry* shaped inside it, maybe something with *your father* too—but Jude had stopped him with a raised hand. Not because he hadn't earned the words. Because Jude wasn't ready to hear them yet, and Voss deserved better than a half-listened apology. There would be time. The man had hauled gear through a flood for them; whatever debts remained between them would keep.

Jude walked home along the levee road in full dark, past the boatyard, past the shuttered cannery, past the house he'd grown up in and sold eleven years ago and now rented rooms above, and he found himself doing the thing he always did—counting. Miles to the bend. Miles to the mouth. Miles his father must have covered, upstream, alone, carrying whatever he carried toward the source. He caught himself at it halfway across the footbridge and stopped, both hands on the rail, the black water sliding underneath.

Fifteen years of measuring. Fifteen years of keeping the ledger so the loss would stay tidy, stay finite, stay a thing with edges instead of a hole. And now here was the truth of it, sitting in a dry bag fifty yards away: his father hadn't drowned. Hadn't vanished into legend or cowardice or bad luck. He had climbed to the forbidden source, found the cavity under the dam, understood what it meant, and spent the last weeks of his life trying to turn men back—writing warnings, recording warnings, sending his notes downstream in a waterproof case addressed to a partner who never got them because the river took the messenger instead. Dying not running from something, but toward everyone.

You couldn't put a mileage figure on that. Jude had tried for fifteen years and the books had never balanced, and now he understood why. Some distances aren't measured in miles.

He stood on the bridge until his hands were cold, and then he went up, and slept without dreaming for the first time since spring.

* * *

The records went out on Thursday.

Not to a university, though three had called. Not to the government office that had faxed twice and phoned six times, wanting the survey data, the dam assessment, the photographs of the cavity—all of which mattered, all of which might buy the towns downstream another decade of warning systems and evacuation plans. Those copies went where they had to go; Elena handled that, tireless, precise, her father's discipline in her spine. But the originals—the journals, the film, the tape of his father's voice recorded in a blue chamber under a mountain—those Jude carried himself, in person, on the morning mail boat, sixty miles downriver to the regional archive where a woman named Okafor received them with white gloves and a silence that told him she understood exactly what she was being handed.

He'd listened to the recording once, the night before, alone in his kitchen. His father's voice, older than Jude remembered, rougher, wind-burned even through the microphone:

*"If anyone finds this, don't come up. Whatever you've heard about the source, whatever it's supposed to prove—it isn't worth the road in. The dam is hollow. I've seen it. Tell Marsh I'm sorry I doubted him, and tell my son—"*

A pause there. Eleven seconds of wind against fabric. Jude had timed it.

*"—tell my son the river was right. It goes where it goes. Don't make it carry what it can't."*

Jude had sat in the dark kitchen after the tape ran out, and he had not counted anything at all.

At the archive, Dr. Okafor catalogued each item aloud as she received it, and when she reached the recording she looked up and said, "Would you like to hear it played here, Mr. Thackeray? For the record."

"No," Jude said. "I've heard it. He said what he needed to say. Now it says itself."

She nodded, and wrote something in the margin of her log, and closed the box, and that was that. Fifteen years, delivered. He walked out into a bright cold morning with his hands empty for the first time in longer than he could calculate, and discovered that empty hands, given long enough, start to feel like open ones.

* * *

Spring came early that year, or late—he'd lost track of which, and found he didn't care. The upper river stayed closed; the new channel was still sorting itself out, the maps useless, the gorge renamed by consensus as simply the Break. Survey crews went up in summer and came back quiet and respectful. The town adapted, the way river towns do, folding catastrophe into routine the way the river folds silt into its banks.

Jude kept the boat. He refitted her himself through the winter—new tubes, new transom, the scarred old nameplate sanded and repainted letter by letter: *Cape Reckoning*, after the headland you could see from the source on a clear day, his father's favorite word for the place where looking stops and going begins. He ran charters again, lower river only, easy water, teaching kids to read current and elders to stop apologizing for their fear of it.

And on the first warm evening of the new season, with the boat tied up at Miller's Bend and the light going gold across the water, Elena came down the levee road carrying two cups of coffee and the expression of a woman who has made a decision and intends to be argued with about it.

"They offered me the glaciology chair," she said, sitting on the gunwale. "Back east. Tenure, funding, the whole cathedral."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the most important glaciological site in the hemisphere just relocated itself sixty miles from this dock." She handed him a cup. "I told them I'd consult. In the winters."

"Winters," Jude repeated.

"I'm told the summers here are navigable." She looked out at the river, at the far bank where the willows were breaking bud, and her voice went quieter. "My father used to say he'd retire here someday. He never said it to me directly. I found it in the margins of his old charts—this bend, specifically. He'd drawn a little house on it. Ridiculous little house with a ridiculous porch." She paused. "I bought the parcel in March."

Jude set his cup down on the thwart, carefully, because his hands were not entirely steady and he refused to spill anything in front of her.

"Elena Marsh," he said. "Are you asking me to help build a porch?"

"I'm asking you to stop counting miles," she said, "and start counting seasons. There are fewer of those, and they matter more."

Out on the water, the current slid past, brown and patient, going where it went. Somewhere far upstream, under new ice that would take centuries to grow, a blue chamber lay open to the sky at last, and a man's warning had finally finished arriving everywhere it needed to arrive. Jude thought about his father's voice saying *the river was right*, and understood, finally, that it hadn't been a resignation. It had been an instruction.

He took her hand, and the light came down the valley the way it always did, and for the first time in fifteen years, Jude Thackeray was exactly where he intended to be—not passing through it, not measuring it, not logging the distance to anywhere else.

Staying.

The river went on without needing him to count it, and that, he decided, was the whole point.
