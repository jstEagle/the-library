## 1. The Man Who Owes Nothing

The trouble with Cinder Flats was that it remembered everything.

That was the trouble with most border towns, in Elodie Ashgrove's experience. They sat at the hinge of two territories, too small to matter and too stubborn to die, and they kept accounts the way other places kept weather — constantly, obsessively, with a kind of grim pride. Cinder Flats had three saloons, one assay office, a water tower that groaned like an old man climbing stairs, and a population of perhaps four hundred souls, every one of whom could tell you what every other one of them had done since the last monsoon. It was, in short, the worst possible town for a man whose entire life's work was being forgotten.

Elodie had been working on it for six years.

He'd arrived the first time under a different name, which was standard practice. He'd left under a different name still, which was also standard practice. The trick, he'd learned, was not to be interesting. Interesting men got remembered. Interesting men got described — *tall fellow, quiet, scar on the left forearm, paid in silver* — and descriptions traveled faster than horses. A man who wanted to vanish had to be aggressively, relentlessly dull. He had to be the human equivalent of a fence post: present, functional, impossible to recall with any affection or detail.

He was on his fourth visit to Cinder Flats now, and he was pleased — insofar as he permitted himself pleasure — to note that he was achieving fence-post status beautifully. The barkeep at the Drowned Cactus, where he took his meals, called him "ranger" without ever appearing to wonder which ranger. The liveryman knew his horse's name better than his own. When he walked down the main street, conversations did not pause so much as fail to register him at all, the way they'd fail to register a change in the wind.

It was, he told himself, exactly what he wanted.

Mostly he believed it.

* * *

The contract came to him on a Tuesday, because contracts in Cinder Flats always came on days when the heat made everyone slightly stupid and therefore agreeable.

The man offering it was named Osgood Pell, and he was the closest thing the town had to a magistrate, which meant he owned the assay office and had once shot a claim-jumper and been very sorry about it afterward. He was a round, damp man who sweated through his vest even in October and who conducted all business as though the business were doing him a personal favor.

"You'll want to hear this," Pell said, waving Elodie into the assay office and shutting the door against the street's dry glare. "Somebody's robbed me."

"Robbed you of what?"

"That's just it." Pell lowered himself into his chair with the sigh of a man bearing the world's weight and several extra pounds besides. "I don't rightly know. That's what makes it a job for you and not for the sheriff."

Elodie leaned against the wall by the window, where the light wouldn't put his face in shadow but also wouldn't feature it. "Start from the beginning."

"The beginning." Pell mopped his forehead with a handkerchief that had seen better decades. "Three weeks back, a government survey outfit comes through. Corvus Hale's people — you know Hale? Territory Survey Division. Big reputation, bigger budget. They were headed south into the basin. Devil's Throat."

"I know the Throat."

"Course you do, you're a ranger." Pell said this as if it explained everything, which in fairness it usually did. "Well. They camped here a week, buying supplies, hiring mules, generally throwing money around like it grew on creosote bushes. And one of my clerks — young fellow, Tomas Merrick, good family, bit dreamy — he up and quits. Leaves a note saying he's taken a position with the survey. Fine. Good wages. I wasn't pleased, but I wasn't surprised; a boy like that doesn't belong counting ore samples his whole life."

Pell paused. Elodie waited. Waiting was most of his profession.

"Two nights ago," Pell went on, "somebody breaks into my back office. Doesn't take the strongbox — there was four hundred dollars in it, untouched. Doesn't take the deed files, the assay records, nothing with value. Takes one thing only. A case. A sample case, brass-cornered, about so long—" he held his hands a foot apart— "with a survey division stamp on the lid. Left behind it a note in Merrick's handwriting saying he was sorry and please don't look for him."

"And the case?"

"Belonged to the survey team. They left it in my keeping when they passed through — said they'd collect it on their way back north. Hale's own instruction. Said it held instruments too delicate for the field." Pell's voice developed a wheedling quality that Elodie recognized as the sound of a man about to overpay. "Now the survey's gone south and not come back, and my clerk's run off with their property, and Mr. Hale has sent word that if the case isn't recovered, there will be consequences of a professional nature. For me. Personally."

"So hire the sheriff."

"The sheriff is my wife's cousin and dumber than a sack of hammers. You, Master Ashgrove—" Pell pronounced the name carefully, having clearly rehearsed it— "come recommended. Quiet. Thorough. Find things that don't want finding."

"What's in the case?"

"If I knew that, I'd know what I was robbed of, wouldn't I?" Pell spread his damp hands. "Instruments. Delicate ones. That's all anyone's told me."

It was a lie. Elodie had spent six years getting very good at knowing when men were lying, mostly because he needed them to keep lying to him convincingly enough not to look closer. Pell didn't know what was in the case, but he suspected, and the suspicion frightened him more than the theft did. Men who were merely angry bargained hard. Men who were frightened bargained fast.

"Rate's four dollars a day," Elodie said, "plus expenses, plus fifty on recovery of the case."

"Thirty-five."

"Fifty. Devil's Throat in monsoon season is fifty."

Pell winced at the words *monsoon season*, which confirmed he'd done his arithmetic. "Forty-five, and I'll throw in the use of my field glasses."

"Done." Elodie pushed off the wall. "One condition. Nobody hears I took this. No notices posted, no talk in the saloons. As far as this town knows, I rode out toward the salt flats checking fence lines."

"Ashgrove." Pell looked genuinely puzzled, sweat beading along his hairline. "Why would anyone care?"

"No reason," Elodie said. "Habit."

* * *

He spent the evening preparing, which meant spending an hour confirming how little there was to prepare.

His kit fit in one saddlebag with room left over for regret: bedroll, tarp, water skins, a week of jerky and hardtack, a tin of coffee he guarded like scripture, fishing line, a small medicine roll, flint and steel, and forty feet of good rope. His weapons were a rifle older than he was and a knife he'd rehandled twice. His total liquid assets, counted twice on the livery floor by lamplight, came to eleven dollars and sixty cents, of which nine had just technically become Pell's advance.

Six years of work, and this was the estate. He found he didn't mind. Minding was for men who expected to be somewhere long enough to accumulate things. Elodie had structured his life on the principle that a man should be able to walk away from everything he owned in under a minute, and he tested the principle regularly, the way other men tested their fences.

There was one item that didn't fit the principle, and it lay at the bottom of his shirt roll wrapped in oilcloth: a letter.

He took it out now, because he always took it out the night before a job, and read it, because he always read it, and hated himself a little, because that was also part of the ritual.

The paper had gone soft and yellow at the folds. The handwriting was his father's — a big, confident, schoolmaster's hand, the hand of a man who'd believed the world arranged itself for the benefit of those who wrote clearly. The letter was twelve years old. It said, in summary, that the compass was ready, that a boy ought to have something of his own before he set out, that the debt could wait until Elodie was established, that debts between family weren't debts at all really, and that Elodie should write more often.

The compass had been collateral from the day Elodie took it. His father had insisted on framing it that way — *collateral, not a gift, a loan against your future success* — because his father had never in his life given anything without attaching terms, and because, Elodie suspected now with the unfair clarity of hindsight, his father had understood that a boy leaving home needs to believe he'll come back, and would believe it more firmly if he owed something on the return trip.

The debt had never been money. That was the joke of it, the bitter private joke Elodie had carried across half a territory: everyone assumed he was hiding from creditors, from some ruined ledger, from a woman or a crime or a gambling hole. Let them assume. The truth was smaller and heavier. The truth was that he owed a man his future, and the man had died before collecting, and there was no way on this earth to pay a dead man, and so Elodie had spent six years practicing not existing, because a man who doesn't exist can't be asked to settle up.

Except that wasn't quite true either, and he knew it, and the letter knew it, sitting there in its oilcloth like a small patient judge.

He read it through once. Then, because tomorrow he was going into Devil's Throat in monsoon season after a thief who'd stolen something worth frightening Osgood Pell, and because a man carrying a letter is a man carrying evidence of a self, he did the thing he'd been circling for six years.

He burned it.

Not dramatically. There was no ceremony. He fed it to the little stove in the livery loft a corner at a time, holding each piece until the flame took it, watching his father's clear confident handwriting curl and go black — *the debt can wait* — then — *debts between family aren't* — then nothing but ash, which he stirred into the stove's old ash so thoroughly that no line of it survived.

He felt lighter. He felt gutted. Both sensations arrived together, like twins who don't get along, and he sat with them for a while in the lamplight, listening to the water tower groan out its slow complaint across the dark.

"Well," he said to the empty loft, in the tone of a man concluding unprofitable negotiations. "That's that."

It wasn't, of course. Nothing was ever *that's that*. But a man could practice.

* * *

He rode out before dawn, ahead of the heat, and the town let him go without ceremony, which was precisely the send-off he'd designed for himself.

The trail south dropped off the mesa in long switchbacks and then ran out onto the hardpan — a hundred miles of baked earth and salt scrub with Devil's Throat cut across the bottom of it like a wound nobody had bothered to dress. The early light came up red behind him and turned the basin the color of an old bruise. Somewhere out there, a dreamy young clerk named Tomas Merrick was running with a brass-cornered case, and a government survey team had gone silent, and Osgood Pell was sweating over consequences of a professional nature.

Elodie checked his backtrail twice in the first hour, out of habit, and then stopped checking it, out of a newer habit he was trying to build: the habit of not looking behind for a life he'd already burned.

By midmorning the heat had flattened the horizon into a shimmering lie, and he'd picked up the track.

It was a good track — a boy's track, hurrying, uneven, the left heel striking harder than the right the way it does with a man carrying weight on one shoulder. The thief had come this way maybe ten days ago, alone, on foot, which was either brave or desperate and was usually both. Elodie followed at a walk, reading the ground the way other men read letters, and for a while the work was simple and the world was simple and he was nobody at all, just motion and attention moving south across the empty basin.

The track held steady for two days. Water stops at the tinajas. Camp sites chosen with more haste than sense. One place where the boy had dropped and retrieved something small — a button, Elodie guessed, or a coin — and stood turning in a full circle before committing to his heading, as if the basin itself had made him unsure.

On the third morning, the track bent east, toward the Throat's upper rim, and began to behave strangely.

First the stride shortened. Then the prints deepened, as though the load had grown heavier. Then, cutting across a flat of pale clay, Elodie found where the boy had stopped and knelt — both knees down, deliberate, unhurried — and done something with his hands that had left no mark Elodie could read.

And past that point, the government stakes began.

They were iron pins with orange paint, driven every quarter mile in a dead-straight line running southeast, straight as a sermon, straight toward the throat of the Throat itself. Fresh. Weeks old at most. Each one stamped with the same mark: a triangle over a bar, and beneath it the letters T.S.D. — Territory Survey Division. Corvus Hale's outfit, marching its neat mathematical line into the one basin every map Elodie had ever seen marked with a warning rather than a name.

Elodie sat his horse at the edge of the stake line for a long moment, working through the arithmetic of wrongness. A survey crew mapping the Throat in monsoon season was odd. A survey crew whose line ran unerringly toward the deepest, least accessible part of the basin was odder. And a thief fleeing north who had knelt at the exact point where the survey line crossed his path — knelt, deliberately, as if marking something, or leaving something, or taking something from the ground —

That wasn't flight anymore. That was purpose.

He dismounted and walked the clay flat slowly, and it was on the far side, tucked into a fold of ground below a shelf of rock, that he found the camp.

It was a survey camp, and it was abandoned, and it was wrong in every particular.

The tents stood pitched and staked, guy lines taut, as if the owners had stepped out to check a reading and meant to return before supper. Instruments sat in their cases, lids closed, latches shut. A cookfire — and this was the detail that lifted the hair on Elodie's neck — a cookfire held a bed of coals that was still, faintly, warm. Not warm the way old ashes stay warm. Warm the way a fire stays when it has been fed within the hour.

Around the camp, the ground told its story plainly: dozens of boot prints arriving, mule tracks, the scuffed geometry of daily work. And leading out of it — nothing. No departing trail in any direction. Six men's worth of coming, and no going. The hardpan around the camp was smooth as a table, unbroken in a half-mile circle except by his own horse's prints, arriving.

Elodie stood at the center of it with his hat in his hand, feeling the desert's silence press in with a new quality — not empty, but attentive. The coals ticked softly in their bed. The wind moved through the tents, stirring papers on the mess table, and among the papers he caught a glimpse of charts, ink lines, a cartographer's fine sure hand drawing terrain he did not recognize.

He looked south, down the line of orange stakes, toward where the land fell away into the blue haze above Devil's Throat.

Somewhere down there was a thief, a missing survey team, a brass-cornered case full of whatever frightened Osgood Pell — and now, apparently, a camp full of men who had vanished from inside their own boots.

Elodie put his hat back on.

"Well," he said again, to nobody, in the tone of a man whose negotiations had just gotten considerably less profitable. "That's that."

It wasn't. He was beginning to suspect that nothing down here would be.
