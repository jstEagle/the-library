## 2. Tracks That End

The thief's trail was three days old and honest about it.

Elodie Ashgrove appreciated that in a trail. Some men walked like they were apologizing—weight rolling to the outside of the boot, hesitations at every fork, a whole confession written in dust. This one walked like a man who knew where he was going and resented the fact. Long stride, even gait, no wandering. The prints said *government issue* boots, size ten, left heel worn on the outer edge the way a man's wears when he carries something heavy on his right side.

A sample case, probably. Elodie had read the contract twice by lantern light in Cinder Flats, and the client—a lawyer with a voice like dry paper and a handshake like a wet fish—had been careful not to say what was stolen. Only that it was government property, small, portable, and that the man carrying it had gone into Devil's Throat, which was either very brave or very stupid, depending on how much you believed the stories.

Elodie believed maybe forty percent of the stories. He'd spent enough years in empty country to know that deserts collect rumors the way dry washes collect tumbleweeds: they blow in from somewhere else, pile up against anything that will hold them, and mostly they're hollow.

Still. Forty percent was enough to keep his hand near his canteen instead of his rifle.

He walked through the morning with the sun climbing his back like a slow animal, following a line of prints across hardpan so flat and baked it rang faintly underfoot, like the lid of an enormous pot somebody forgot about. The basin ahead of him opened out in long pale shelves, and the Throat itself lay beyond them—a seam of shadow in the earth, visible only as a change in the color of the heat haze, the way you can see a river from a distance before you can see water.

"Three days old," he told the trail conversationally. Talking to tracks was a habit he'd picked up somewhere around the time he'd stopped talking to people, and he'd never seen a reason to break either habit. "You're walking fast for a man who doesn't want to be followed. Either you don't know I'm behind you, or you don't care."

The trail didn't answer, which was typical.

Around noon he stopped in the shade of a leaning slab of basalt and ate half a strip of dried goat and let the other half think about whether it wanted to be lunch or dinner. His provisions were the kind of arithmetic nobody taught in ranger school: nine days of food if he stretched it, seven if he was honest, five if the Throat turned mean. The reward on the contract would fix a great many things, most of them involving numbers written down in places he preferred not to think about.

He did not think about them. He was very good at not thinking about them. It was, in fact, the single most developed skill he possessed, and he'd had years of practice.

The trail bent north in the afternoon, angling toward a line of fresh-cut stakes that caught the light wrong—too bright, too new, too straight. Elodie crouched over the first one and frowned at it the way a man frowns at a word spelled correctly that still looks misspelled.

Survey stakes. Iron, galvanized, stamped with a serial number and a department seal he recognized without wanting to: the Bureau of Territorial Survey, the branch of government that existed primarily to make maps of places nobody went, which struck Elodie as a reasonable use of public funds. Somebody had driven these stakes recently. Recently enough that the packed earth around their bases hadn't yet cracked into its usual mosaic.

That was odd, but it was the ordinary kind of odd—the kind that came with paperwork attached. Government survey teams came through the borderlands every few years, measured things, argued about elevations, and left. What was strange was that the stakes ran *toward* the Throat, in a neat surveyed line, and the thief's prints ran alongside them for two hundred yards like a man using someone else's road.

Then both stopped.

Not faded. Not scattered. Stopped. The last print of the left boot sat complete in the hardpan, every tread edge crisp, as though the man had been lifted off the earth mid-stride by a considerate giant. Beside it, the right. And past them, nothing. No scuff, no drag mark, no sign of a turn. The hardpan continued on toward the canyon rim, smooth and blank as a page someone had licked their thumb and wiped clean.

Elodie stood over those two prints for a long time.

He'd tracked men through flash floods, through sandstorms, through a night of freezing rain once in the northern ranges that he still brought out at low moments to remind himself things could be worse. Trails broke. Trails lied. Trails got eaten by weather and rock and time. But trails did not simply *end*, in good condition, on ground that held a print like wax holds a seal.

"All right," he said to the desert. "You've got my attention."

The desert, being a desert, declined to elaborate.

* * *

The camp was half a mile further on, tucked into the lee of a low ridge where any sensible person would put a camp, and it was the neatest camp Elodie had ever seen abandoned.

That was the phrase his mind kept circling back to, turning it over like a stone looking for the cool side. Abandoned camps were never neat. Abandoned camps were scatterings and leavings—broken crates, a mule shoe, tin cans with their lids peeled back like flowers, the archaeology of hurry. People leaving a place in a rush left a mess, because fear is untidy and even relief is careless.

This camp looked like the owners had stepped out to check a gauge and meant to be right back.

Four tents, ridge-poled and guyed taut, flaps buttoned. A instrument table under an awning, brass fittings winking. A tool rack with picks and shovels hung in descending order of size, which appealed to some part of Elodie that he didn't examine too closely. A stack of supply crates, roped and labeled in a clerk's careful hand. Everything squared, everything staked down against wind, everything *waiting*.

And the cookfire—this was the part that raised the hair on his forearms despite the heat—was warm.

Not warm the way ash stays warm for a day. Warm the way a banked fire stays when someone has raked coals together and covered them, meaning to come back within hours. He held his palm above it, then closer, then winced and pulled it back. An hour. Maybe two. Someone had made coffee here this morning, in the middle of nowhere, beside a trail that ended in nothing, and then had not come back to drink it.

There was a tin cup sitting on a flat rock by the fire. Full. Cold now, but full, a skin of dust just beginning to settle on its surface. Nobody abandons a full cup. Even fleeing men take their coffee; Elodie had known outlaws who'd gone back through gunfire for a hat, and hats are not coffee.

He drew his rifle anyway, because the alternative to being paranoid out here was being surprised, and surprised men in empty country tended to become dead men in empty country, and dead men were terrible at collecting rewards.

"Census," he announced clearly, moving into the open with his hands where they could be seen. "Ranger service. If there's anyone here who can hear me, now's a fine time to say so."

The camp said nothing. The wind moved a tent flap with a sound like a slow sigh, and somewhere far off a hawk said one contemptuous thing about the general state of the world.

He worked the camp the way he worked everything: edges first, then center, then the details nobody thinks matter until they're the only thing that does. The tents held bedrolls, rolled tight. Personal effects—boots, a spare shirt, a shaving kit, a paperback novel with the cover gone soft from handling. Five bedrolls. Five kits. Whatever had happened to this team, it had happened to all of them at once, and none of them had packed so much as a water skin.

The instrument table was worse. Charts under glass weights, ruled and annotated in a fine, confident hand. A theodolite under an oiled cloth. Journals—three of them, stacked square—and Elodie flipped the top one open with a pencil stub rather than his fingers, an old habit from a life he also didn't examine.

The last entry was dated four days ago. It was a routine elevation reading. Below it, in the same handwriting, someone had begun a second line—"At dawn we move to the lower seep to—" and stopped. Mid-word. The pencil stroke trailed off into a faint gouge, as if the writer's hand had been taken away from them, or as if they'd simply forgotten, between one letter and the next, what writing was for.

Elodie closed the journal gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"Five people," he told the fire. "Five beds, five kits, one cup of coffee, and a sentence nobody finished." He crouched and studied the ground inside the camp proper, working outward. Plenty of prints here—boot traffic, mule signs, the churn of daily life. But the perimeter was another matter. He circled the whole site twice, widening each ring, reading the ground with the particular patience that was the one thing he'd never been able to unlearn no matter how hard he'd tried to unlearn everything else about himself.

No trail out. Not one direction. As though five grown people had evaporated between the coffee pot and the horizon.

He found the boat tracks at the bottom of the ridge, though, and those at least obeyed physics. Wheel ruts, heavy ones, running down toward the lower canyon where—according to the charts he'd allowed himself one more look at—there shouldn't have been enough water to float a rowboat, let alone whatever they'd hauled down there. The ruts were recent. The rain-swollen season was coming; you could smell it under the dust, a green metallic thread in the air that the old-timers called *the river clearing its throat*, which everyone agreed was an unpleasant name for it and used anyway.

Elodie stood at the top of the slope with his thumbs in his belt and considered his options, which were, in order: go home, go home, or go down.

Going home meant reporting a vanished survey team and a vanished thief and a trail that ended in two perfect footprints, and watching some desk-bound official decide he was either lying or mad, and neither verdict paid out. Going home also meant the debt stayed where it was, growing interest in the dark like something buried that hadn't finished dying.

Going down meant finding out why the hair on his arms hadn't settled since noon.

"Well," he said, because the desert liked a decision spoken aloud—it seemed to hold you to it, somehow. "Nobody ever drowned following a dry wash."

Which was, he would reflect later, exactly the sort of thing a man says right before the universe takes it personally.

He shouldered his pack, gave the neat little camp one last look—five beds, zero people, a sentence ending mid-word—and started down the slope toward the lower canyon, where the wheel ruts pointed and the air smelled, faintly and increasingly, of water.

It took him the rest of the afternoon to reach the flood channel, and the light was going copper and long when he heard it: a sound carried up the gorge on the evening wind, thin and rhythmic and unmistakably human.

Someone, down in the darkening throat of the canyon, was calling for help.
