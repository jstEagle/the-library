## 3. The Stranger in the Boat

The lower canyon had drowned overnight.

Elodie stood on a shelf of red sandstone and looked down at water where there had been no water yesterday—brown-green, slow-moving, filling the narrows like something poured from the sky's own canteen. It wasn't monsoon yet; he'd have smelled it coming days ahead. This was seepage, spring melt backed up behind some rockfall downstream, turning the throat of Devil's Throat into a gullet.

Which meant his thief was either upstream of it or under it.

He worked the problem the way he worked all problems: quietly, with his hands in his pockets and his opinions to himself. The trail had ended two miles back beside those government survey stakes, neat as fence posts at a church social. Stakes meant a crew. A crew meant boats, maybe. And boats meant that if anyone was still alive down there, they'd be floating.

He didn't much want company. Company asked questions. But a dead survey team would bring helicopters, and helicopters brought men with clipboards who wanted to know why a man with no registered address was wandering the most classified canyon in the territory.

So he went down toward the water for entirely selfish reasons, and told himself that was the only reason.

* * *

The flooded narrows were a maze of drowned cottonwoods, their crowns sticking up like drowned men waving politely. Elodie moved along the shelf until he found a place where the rock stepped down in terraces, and there he found the boat—or heard it first, which amounted to the same thing out here.

A sound. Rhythmic. Metallic. Someone bailing.

He eased along the terrace, boots silent on stone, and looked down through a screen of willow at a gray survey skiff drifting in an eddy behind a dead tree. A woman knelt in the bilge with a dented pot, scooping water over the side with the grim patience of someone who had been doing it long enough to develop opinions about it. She wore a survey team jacket two sizes too big, sleeves rolled six times. Her hair had given up on any particular style and settled for survival.

There were no supplies in the boat. No pack, no rifle, no canteen, no rations crate. Just the woman, the pot, and maybe three inches of standing water she was losing to very slowly.

Elodie considered his options. He could watch her drift past and continue his search unencumbered. He could announce himself and risk whatever she was running from. Or he could do the arithmetic: one leaking boat, one woman bailing, one canyon about to get considerably wetter.

He sighed, because nobody was around to hear him not sigh, and stepped out onto the open rock.

"Boat's taking on faster than you're pouring," he said.

She came around fast, pot raised like a weapon, and he noted approvingly that her stance was decent—weight low, pot held at an angle that would do real damage to a jaw. Then she saw him: dusty coat, weathered face, no visible weapon, hands loose at his sides. Her eyes did the same inventory his had done a hundred times, and apparently concluded he was more tired than dangerous.

"Where did you come from?" she demanded.

"Upstream."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the honest part of one." He crouched at the waterline, keeping his distance respectful. "You're from the survey camp. Two miles back. Gear stacked neat as a general's footlocker, fire still warm, and not one set of tracks leaving. You want to tell me how you got in that boat?"

Her mouth opened. Closed. She looked at the pot in her hands as if it might contain the answer, then at him again, and something in her face shifted—the confidence draining out and leaving something younger and much more frightened underneath.

"I don't know," she said.

"You rowed here."

"I don't remember rowing here. I don't remember—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started over with the careful diction of someone reading off an internal list. "My name is Sable Vane. I'm the cartographer for the Basin Survey Expedition. We've been in this canyon eleven days. I know that because I wrote it in my logbook, and I remember writing it. I do not remember the last—" her jaw worked— "I don't know. Days. A week? I woke up in this boat with a hole in the hull and no idea which direction the sun rises."

"It rises east," Elodie said. "Behind you."

"Thank you," she said, with such fierce sincerity that he almost smiled.

"And your crew?"

"They were at camp. I think. I remember breakfast. I remember arguing with someone about contour lines—I'm always arguing with someone about contour lines—and then I remember cold water and this pot." She lifted it slightly, as if presenting evidence. "That's the whole story. That's everything I have."

Elodie studied her. Liars had tells, and he'd spent enough years being lied to by himself to recognize them in others. She wasn't performing confusion. She was drowning in it, and doing it while bailing, which took a certain grit.

"What's your name?" she asked. "Since we're trading."

He weighed it. Names were currency and he was deliberately bankrupt. But she was sitting in a sinking boat in a canyon that ate people, and refusing to give a name to a drowning woman seemed like the kind of pettiness that came back around.

"Elodie Ashgrove."

"That's a girl's name."

"It's my mother's fault. She wanted a daughter first and got me instead, and she was not a woman who wasted a good name."

Something flickered across Sable Vane's face—not quite a laugh, but the place where a laugh would live if she could afford one. "Ashgrove," she repeated. "Like the trees."

"Nothing grows in an ashgrove. That's rather the point of them."

* * *

The boat was holed below the waterline on the port side—a crack in the planking, old wood split along the grain, patched with survey tape and what looked like a strip of chart paper smeared with pitch. Somebody had done emergency work with whatever was at hand. The work was holding. Barely.

"That patch is mine," Sable said, watching him examine it. "I mean—I know it's mine. That's my handwriting on the chart. My knots. I tie a left-handed bowline, everyone on the team used to mock me for it." She frowned at her own hands. "So I was in the water, I got back in the boat, I fixed the hull. None of which I remember doing. Isn't that strange? My hands remember things I don't."

"Hands are loyal like that," Elodie said. "They'll keep working long after the rest of you has quit."

He got the skiff tied off to a dead snag and climbed down to it, because the arithmetic hadn't changed: she couldn't stay adrift, and he couldn't carry her out overland before the water rose further. The sensible move was to get the boat to the abandoned camp, patch it properly with the gear there, and wait out whatever was coming with supplies and high ground.

"The camp," he said. "Can you walk to it from here?"

"Probably. Can you tell me why the whole canyon is underwater?"

"Spring melt, backed up behind a slide downstream. It'll rise another few feet when the monsoon comes, and when the monsoon comes, this whole basin turns into a lake with opinions."

"When does the monsoon come?"

"Two days. Maybe three."

Sable looked up at a sky that was still insultingly blue, then at him, clearly deciding whether he was joking. He kept his face neutral. Desert men learned early that the funniest thing you could do was be right.

"Two days," she said flatly.

"Give or take. The clouds will come over the western rim looking like nothing, and then they'll come over everything else looking like the end of the world. I've seen this Throat swallow a herd of cattle in an afternoon. The rancher cried. The cattle didn't even notice."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

It was, in fact, not a little funny, and they both knew it, and somehow that made it easier to keep moving.

* * *

Getting the boat to camp took the better part of the afternoon. They poled it along the shelf where the water ran shallow, Elodie pushing off the bottom with a dead cottonwood limb while Sable bailed in shifts, and somewhere in the second mile she stopped treating him like a stranger who might rob her and started treating him like equipment—useful, slightly unreliable, to be monitored.

"You walk like a cat," she observed, as he picked a line across slick rock without seeming to look at it.

"Cats are smarter than me. I just don't waste steps."

"Why not?"

"Because every step you take out here is a step somebody can read. Walk heavy, you leave a story. Walk light, you leave a rumor." He said it lightly, but she caught the edge under it—he saw her catch it, saw her decide not to poke at it, which he appreciated more than she could know.

At camp, the wrongness waited for them exactly as he'd left it. Six tents, stakes driven square. Instruments crated and lashed against rain. The cookfire ring swept clean, ashes cold now but organized. And the charts.

Sable went to the charts the way a thirsty woman goes to water. She spread them on a folding table with hands that shook only a little, and Elodie watched her read her own cartography like a letter from a stranger.

"This is the lower gorge," she said, tapping. "This is where you found me. This is—" She stopped. Turned a sheet. Stopped again. "This canyon isn't on any map I've ever seen. And I've seen all of them. It's my job to have seen all of them." Her finger traced a tributary drawn in her own confident hand, annotated in her own cramped script: *flow increases after dark — test again.* "Why would I write that? Water doesn't increase after dark."

"Springs do strange things in limestone country," Elodie said, though he didn't believe it and neither did she.

"There are six tents," she said quietly. "And I've counted the bedrolls twice. There are five."

Elodie had counted too. He said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

Sable sat down slowly on a supply crate and put her face in her hands. For a moment she was just a tired woman in a borrowed jacket, and the canyon held its breath around her. Then she straightened, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand—briskly, businesslike, the way people do when crying is a scheduled activity and time is short—and looked at him.

"All right, Ashgrove. You found me. You clearly know this country. I can't remember my own week, but I can remember that you're the first person in it who hasn't lied to me, and I intend to hold you to that standard." She stuck out her hand. "Partners. Temporarily. Until I figure out what happened to my crew and why there's a canyon on my maps that doesn't exist."

He looked at her hand. Small, ink-stained at the fingers, rope-burned across the palm. A working hand. A loyal hand, probably, like its owner's memory.

Partners meant questions. Partners meant someone noticing if he slipped away in the night. Partners meant becoming, however briefly, a person who existed to somebody else.

But the monsoon was two days out, the boat leaked, five bedrolls waited for six tents, and the alternative to partnership was watching her drown alone in a canyon full of water that remembered things she didn't.

He shook it.

"Temporarily," he agreed.

Her grip was stronger than he expected. "Good. First order of business: I'm starving, and I don't suppose you remembered to bring food, since you seem like the type who travels with nothing."

"I travel light," Elodie said.

"You travel empty," Sable Vane said, with the unerring accuracy of a cartographer reading terrain, and went to see what the missing survey team had left in their stores.

* * *

They ate beans from a tin heated over the revived cookfire, sitting on opposite sides of it like two people negotiating a border. The light went amber, then rust, then gone, and the drowned canyon turned to black glass below them, reflecting stars.

"Tell me something true," Sable said suddenly. "Since we're partners. One true thing. Fair trade for the name."

Elodie fed a stick to the fire and considered the exchange rate. Most of his true things were expensive.

"I burned a letter this morning," he said finally. "Before I left town. It was the last piece of mail anybody ever sent me, and I'd been carrying it four years."

"Why burn it?"

"Because carrying it meant I was still the person it was sent to. And I've been trying very hard not to be that person."

Sable watched him across the flames. She didn't ask who sent it, or who he'd been, which he catalogued as either kindness or strategy and couldn't decide which unsettled him more.

"My turn," she said. "I'm afraid that whatever happened to my memory, I did it to myself. Volunteered for it. Signed something." She turned her hands over, studying the ink stains. "Because when I try to reach for the missing week, it doesn't feel like a hole. It feels like a door I closed from the inside."

The fire popped. Down in the black water, something moved—slow, heavy, indifferent—and neither of them mentioned it.

"Tomorrow," Elodie said, "we inventory the camp, patch the boat properly, and get above the flood line. In that order."

"And after that?"

"After that, we find out what your team was doing in a canyon that doesn't exist." He looked east, where the sky was already beginning to stack up its first thin, innocent-looking clouds, two days early. "Because I don't think it was mapping it."

Sable followed his gaze, and her face, lit sideways by the fire, went carefully blank—the blankness of a woman reading her own handwriting on a map of nowhere and hearing, faintly, water rising where no water should be.

"No," she agreed quietly. "I don't think so either."
