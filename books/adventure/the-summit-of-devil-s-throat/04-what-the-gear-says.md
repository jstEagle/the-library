## 4. What the Gear Says

The first thing I knew for certain was the smell of the coffee tin.

Not coffee — the tin had been empty long enough to grow a fine dust of rust inside — but the smell that clung to it anyway, burnt and bitter and stubborn, like a memory of a memory. I held it under my nose and breathed in and felt something in me lean toward it the way a plant leans toward a window. I had drunk from this tin. I had burned whatever had been in it. My hands knew this. The rest of me was taking notes.

"Find anything?" Elodie asked from the other side of the camp.

He was doing his ranger thing, which as far as I could tell consisted of crouching over the ground looking at it as though it were about to confess to something. He'd been at it for an hour. Occasionally he'd say "huh" in a tone of deep professional suspicion, which I was beginning to understand was how he said *this is impossible* without wasting breath on the whole word.

"Not yet," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because I had found the tin, and the tin had found something in me.

I set it down and turned to the instrument cases.

There were six of them, arranged in a neat row against the canyon wall, latches facing out, handles all turned the same direction. Somebody had organized them. Somebody tidy. Somebody who alphabetized their spice rack and folded maps along their original creases. I looked at my own hands — small, ink-stained at the cuticles, a callus on the middle finger of my right hand exactly where a pen rests — and thought: *that somebody is probably you.*

It's a strange thing, suspecting your own hands of being competent. You'd think it would be comforting. It wasn't. It was like finding a stranger's diary in your nightstand and recognizing the handwriting.

I opened the first case.

A theodolite, brass and glass, packed in wool with the tenderness people usually reserve for infants or instruments they love more than infants. Underneath it, a leather notebook. I opened it to the first page and there it was:

*Site 14 — approach from the west rim is mislabeled on the regional sheet. The draw is 40 meters deeper than charted. Corrected below.*

My handwriting.

I want to be clear about what that sentence does to a person. It isn't like reading a note you left yourself before bed. It's like hearing your own voice on a recording — that jolt of *that can't be me, that's not what I sound like* followed immediately by the sickening certainty that it is, that it always has been, that everyone else has been listening to that voice your whole life while you got some flattering internal version instead.

Except worse, because I couldn't remember writing it. Couldn't remember the draw, the west rim, the regional sheet. Couldn't remember being the kind of person who noticed things like that and bothered to correct them.

"You've gone quiet," Elodie said. He had materialized beside me the way he did — no footsteps, just a gradual increase of Elodie, like weather coming in.

"I found my handwriting."

"That's usually good news for a cartographer."

"It would be," I said, "if I recognized the terrain."

He looked at the notebook over my shoulder. He read the way he walked, I was learning — efficiently, missing nothing, pretending to be casual about both.

"That's decent survey work," he said. "Careful. Fussy." A pause. "You strike me as fussy."

"I don't strike myself as anything. That's rather the problem."

* * *

Here is what I knew, and I want you to appreciate how short this list is:

My name was Sable Vane. I knew this the way you know your own name when someone wakes you at three in the morning — instantly, but with no sense of why it should be yours. I was the cartographer of a government survey team. I knew this because Elodie had found the team manifest in the command tent, laminated against the damp, and my photograph was on it, and the face in the photograph was making the exact expression I make in photographs, which is the expression of a woman who has been told to smile and is negotiating terms.

Beyond that: fog. Not the gentle kind. The kind with teeth.

The week before the boat — gone. The week before that — soft shapes, impressions, nothing I could press my thumb into. I remembered a city, I thought. Rain on canvas awnings. A woman laughing in a doorway, though whether she was laughing with me or at me I couldn't say. I remembered hating olives, which felt like the kind of fact that survives anything, and I clung to it shamelessly. A person who hates olives is still, in some essential way, herself.

Everything else, I was reconstructing from equipment.

So I opened the second case.

Charts. Dozens of them, rolled and sleeved in oilcloth. I spread them across the camp table — a slab of plywood on trestles that someone, probably me, had leveled with shims, because two of the table legs sat on flat stones and I could tell the stones had been chosen with care.

The first charts were ordinary. Basin topography, contour lines marching across hardpan like rings in a tree. Drainage patterns. A stretch of the old freight road with its grades noted in margins. This was standard work, good work, the work of someone who took pride in her line weights. I ran my finger along a contour and felt the small vanity of craft: *yes, I drew this well, whoever I am.*

Then I unrolled the fifth chart, and the room tilted.

It was a canyon. A deep one, branching, drawn in detail so dense the page went dark in the middle where the walls crowded together. Every bend surveyed. Every side channel named — and here was the thing, here was the thing that made me sit down on a supply crate harder than I meant to — the names weren't official designations. They were personal. Someone had named the side channels after things. *Kettle.* *Lantern.* *The Long Argument.* And in the lower right corner, in the neat block letters I now understood were mine:

*S.V., del.*

S.V., delineator. Me. Signing my work like a schoolgirl signs a drawing of a horse.

"Elodie," I said.

He came over. He looked at the chart for a long time. Longer than the chart deserved, if it were merely a chart.

"This canyon," he said slowly, "isn't on any map I know."

"It's on mine," I said. "That's the problem. Look—" I pulled the regional sheets from the third case and spread them beside it, the official surveys, the ones with government stamps in the corners. "This is the basin. This is everything the government thinks exists down here. And this—" I tapped my canyon, my beautiful, impossible canyon, "—is not on any of them. Not as a blank space, even. There's no gap where it should be. The surrounding terrain just... flows across it. Like it was never surveyed at all. Like someone decided it didn't exist and redrew the world around the decision."

Elodie was quiet. Then he said, in the voice of a man testing ice with one boot: "Canyons don't get unsurveyed by accident."

"No," I agreed. "They get unsurveyed on purpose."

We looked at each other. It was the first time we'd looked at each other properly, I think — not the wary sizing-up of two strangers sharing a disaster, but an actual look, two people arriving at the same bad idea from opposite directions and meeting in the middle of it.

"So either I imagined a canyon," I said, "in extraordinary detail, with consistent elevations and hydrology that actually makes sense—"

"Which you didn't," Elodie said. "Imagined canyons don't have working drainage. Trust me. I've met people who imagine canyons."

"—or I mapped a real place that someone wanted off the record. And then someone made sure I forgot mapping it."

The wind moved through the camp, flipping the corner of a chart. Somewhere up-canyon, water dripped with the patience of a thing counting down.

* * *

I worked through the afternoon. It was the only thing I knew how to do — the one skill the fog hadn't taken. Give me paper and instruments and I stopped being a ghost and became a professional again, and I'll tell you honestly: I have never been so grateful for a job in my life.

The fourth case held the field journals. Seven of them, dated across five weeks. Mine, presumably, or partly mine — the early entries were in my hand, brisk and dry, with occasional marginal doodles of a bird I kept drawing without meaning to. Some kind of thrush. Apparently I had opinions about this thrush. Apparently it had been stealing my pencil shavings.

*Day 9,* one entry read. *The thrush has escalated. H. says we should name it and adopt it. T. says naming it will only encourage it. I have hidden my pencils in the theodolite case, which it cannot open, having no thumbs.*

H. Hale, I learned later, from the manifest. Director Corvus Hale. T. — Tomas Reyes, junior surveyor. Five names total. Five people who had stood where I was standing, cooked at that fire ring, argued about a bird. And every trace of them ended mid-sentence.

Because that was the other thing about the journals. They didn't stop dramatically. They thinned. Around day twenty-six, the entries got shorter. Day twenty-eight was four lines. Day twenty-nine was two:

*Foggy today. Slept badly. H. says the water here is worth studying.*

And then nothing. No final entry, no *last day, packing out.* Just a journal closed and stacked with the others, waiting for a hand that never came back for it.

I sat with that for a while. The light was going amber in the canyon mouth. Elodie had built the fire up and was heating water for the tea he'd found in a ration crate, and he did it without asking if I wanted any, just made two cups, and handed me one, and that small unprompted act of assumption — *you exist, you drink tea* — nearly undid me more than the journals had.

"The water here," I said. "Hale said the water here is worth studying."

"Springs are worth studying," Elodie said. But he said it carefully, the way he said most things, like each word had been weighed on a scale he kept behind his sternum.

"There's a spring marked on my canyon chart." I pulled it back out and found it — yes, there, in the deepest branch, a little blue symbol and my own annotation: *flow constant, temp steady, taste — ?* I had written *taste — ?* Like I'd meant to come back and finish the thought. Like I'd tasted it and the tasting had interrupted itself.

"Taste — question mark," Elodie read aloud. "That's an unusual review."

"I gave the canyon names like 'The Long Argument.' I think I was the sort of person who wrote down what things were actually like." I stared at the annotation. "So why couldn't I finish this one?"

He didn't answer. He was looking at the fire, and his face had done something complicated — the look of a man recognizing a shape in someone else's story that matches a shape in his own, and deciding not to mention it yet. I was learning to read those looks. I expected it would take practice. I suspected I'd have plenty of time.

* * *

The last case I opened at dusk, mostly because I'd run out of daylight courage for anything bigger.

It was smaller than the others. A personal case, scuffed, with initials scratched into the lid: S.V. My initials, scratched with a knife point, unevenly, the V too wide — the scratch of someone doing it quickly, maybe proudly, maybe the first day of the assignment.

Inside: a spare fountain pen (of course; apparently I was that person), a tin of pencils, a hair ribbon, a paperback novel with the cover torn off, and a bundle of letters tied with string.

I untied the string with fingers that had decided to shake without consulting me.

The top letter was addressed to me. *Sable* — in handwriting I didn't know, round and warm, a mother's hand or an aunt's, the kind of hand that dots its i's with circles. I read it twice. It was about nothing. Weather, a neighbor's dog, a recipe, a complaint about my brother's stubbornness — *he gets it from your grandfather's side, don't let him tell you otherwise* —

A brother.

I had a brother. I had a family that wrote to me about dogs and recipes and called me stubborn-affectionate things. I pressed the letter flat against my knee and read it a third time, hunting my own life in it like a woman searching a crowd for her own face.

And that was when I found the second letter, underneath the first, and understood that the fog had teeth after all.

It was also addressed to me. Same round hand. But this one had been opened already — and resealed, clumsily, with a different adhesive, the flap crooked. Someone had read my mail. And inside, in the margins, in a narrow, precise, bureaucratic script that I already hated on sight, someone had made annotations.

Corrections.

*Incorrect — see revised family history, attached.*

*Subject does not have a brother. Confirm subject accepts this.*

*Retention of olive aversion: acceptable. Trivial attachments may remain.*

I read it three times. The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, thunder cleared its throat, far off, like a giant turning over in bed.

"They rewrote me," I heard myself say. My voice sounded strange — level, almost conversational, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "Someone went through my mail and edited my life. Crossed out my brother. Kept the olives." I laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken sound. "Kept the *olives*, Elodie. Because hating olives is trivial. Trivial attachments are acceptable. But a brother — a brother is load-bearing."

Elodie had gone very still. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice of a man standing very close to the edge of something. "What do you remember about him? Your brother?"

Nothing. That was the horror of it. I reached for him and found a shape — tall, stubborn, laughing in a doorway — and the shape dissolved when I grabbed it, like river silt in a fist. Except. Except.

"He laughs," I said slowly. "In a doorway. In the rain. I remembered rain on awnings, earlier — I thought it was a city. It might have been him. Standing in a doorway, laughing at me." My eyes stung. "They left me the laugh and took the man. What kind of monster leaves the laugh?"

Elodie didn't answer right away. He fed the fire a stick, and the sparks went up, and I watched him decide how much truth to spend.

"Whoever wrote in that margin," he said at last, "knew what they were doing. That's not vandalism. That's procedure." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not pity, thank God, pity I couldn't have survived — but a kind of grim recognition. "I've spent years making records forget me. You're telling me someone made you forget yourself. We're not in different businesses, you and I. We're just on opposite sides of the ledger."

Before I could answer — before I could decide whether to be offended or comforted, which was going to be a genuinely difficult choice — he straightened, abruptly, his head tilting toward the canyon mouth like a compass needle finding north.

"What?" I said.

He was sniffing the air. Actually sniffing it, like a man reading a document written in scent.

"How deep is that canyon of yours?" he asked. "The one that doesn't exist. Does it flood?"

I looked at my chart. At the contours crowding dark together. At the little blue spring symbol, patient and unanswered, with its unfinished question.

"Elodie," I said, "it's a canyon. Everything floods."

He was already moving toward the high ground, scanning the sky, where the last light was dying behind clouds that had begun, quietly and without any announcement, to organize themselves into something with intent.

"Then we have a problem," he said, "bigger than your handwriting."
