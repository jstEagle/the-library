## 5. Monsoon Warning

The morning Elodie understood they were going to die walking, the sky was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges.

He'd been awake since before dawn — a habit so old it had worn grooves in him — and he'd spent an hour doing what he always did when something was wrong: nothing, visibly. He sat on a flat rock above the flooded lower canyon with his hat tipped forward and his hands loose on his knees, and to anyone watching he would have looked like a man enjoying the view. Inside, he was counting.

Clouds stacked over the western rim like plates nobody had washed. The air had that thick, sweet, green smell that desert country only got when it was about to drown. And the wind — the wind had turned. For six days it had come out of the east, steady and dry as an old argument. Now it came from the southwest, warm and wet and heavy, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet but intended to.

"Elodie." Sable's voice came up the slope behind him, careful on the loose rock. She'd gotten better at moving over stone in the past two days. Not good. Better. "You've been sitting like that for an hour. Either you're praying or you're constipated."

"Reading," he said.

"Reading what? There's no book."

"The sky."

A pause. Then she climbed up beside him and lowered herself onto the rock with the particular gracelessness of someone whose body still didn't entirely believe it belonged to her. She'd been that way since he'd pulled her out of the leaking boat — moving like a woman borrowing her own limbs, checking her hands as if expecting them to be returned to someone else.

"All right," she said. "Read it to me."

Elodie pointed with his chin, keeping his hands where they were. "Those clouds. Cumulonimbus building over the high mesa. That means the storm system isn't local. It's coming off the coast, dragging half the ocean with it. Monsoon pattern. When that breaks over the Throat—" he traced a line down the canyon wall with one finger, "—every drop of water in four hundred square miles of hardpan comes down this gorge inside of thirty-six hours. The river rises forty feet. Sometimes sixty."

"And us?"

"Us is the interesting part." He finally turned to look at her. Her hair had dried into a salt-stiff tangle, and there was a scrape healing along her jaw from the night she'd spent adrift, and she was watching him with the level, unblinking attention of someone who had decided — provisionally, grudgingly, the way you decide to trust a rope bridge — that he knew things worth knowing. "We're eleven miles below the survey camp. Eleven miles of canyon with no exit. No side drainages we can climb. I checked yesterday while you were napping."

"I wasn't napping. I was lying down with my eyes closed thinking."

"That's napping with extra steps."

"Elodie."

"Eleven miles," he repeated, "and no way up. If we try to walk out ahead of the flood, we die tired instead of drowned. The flood moves faster than a horse can gallop through the narrows. Faster than anything."

Sable was quiet for a moment. Below them, the river ran brown-green and deceptively calm, the flooded lower canyon holding its breath. Somewhere downstream, around the third bend, the survey boat sat beached on a gravel bar where they'd dragged it after patching the worst of its leaks — patched with tar from the camp stores and a strip of canvas and, at Sable's insistence, one of her own shirts, which she claimed she didn't remember buying anyway.

"So we don't walk," she said.

"We ride."

* * *

The problem with the plan, Elodie explained as they packed the camp, was that the plan was terrible.

"It floats," Sable said, hauling the last of the salvaged gear toward the boat in a tarp dragged over gravel. "That's more than most plans manage."

"It barely floats. It leaks in three places I know about and probably four I don't. It has no rudder worth the name, no anchor, and enough fuel for maybe six hours of running time if the engine doesn't quit out of spite." He crouched by the hull and pressed his thumb against the patched seam; the tar held, grudgingly, the way old men hold their tempers. "But it floats, and it's big enough to survive being thrown around, and the Throat has high shelves and sea caves along the middle reaches where a boat can hide from the worst of it. So we ride it up-canyon until the flood crests, tie off somewhere defensible, and wait out however long the water stays high."

"How long?"

"Weeks." He said it plainly because he'd learned that plainness hurt less than softening did. "Could be three. Could be five. The Throat fills like a bathtub and drains like one with the plug stuck."

Sable stopped dragging the tarp. "Weeks. With how much food?"

Elodie did the arithmetic he'd already done twice, hoping the answer would improve with repetition. It didn't. "The camp gave us canned goods for maybe ten days, generous portions. We have line for fish, though what lives in this water in monsoon season is anybody's guess. There's cactus. There's whatever grows on the shelves."

"Wonderful. I'll look forward to weeks of cactus and mystery fish."

"You say that now," Elodie said, shouldering his pack, "but give it nineteen days. You'll dream about cactus."

The strange thing — the thing he kept turning over in the back of his mind like a stone in a pocket — was that planning out loud with another person felt less like a burden than he remembered. He'd been alone so long that other people had become weather: something that happened near him, occasionally inconvenient, never engaged with directly. But Sable argued. She asked questions he hadn't thought of. When he said *we tie off somewhere defensible*, she said *defensible against what*, and when he said *against everything*, she said *that's not an answer*, and she was right, and the arguing itself was somehow steadying, like a handrail in a dark stairwell.

They pushed the boat off the gravel bar mid-morning. The engine caught on the third pull, coughed, caught again, and settled into a ragged idle that sounded like a smoker climbing stairs.

"Talk to it nicely," Sable advised from the bow, where she'd appointed herself lookout.

"It knows what it's done."

* * *

They made nine miles that day, running slow against the current, hugging the shaded wall where the water ran slack. Elodie kept glancing at the sky between bends, and each glance told him the same story a little louder. The cloud deck had thickened. The light had gone strange and metallic, the kind of light that made shadows look cut out of paper. By early afternoon the wind had picked up enough to carry grit off the rims, and once, far to the west, he heard thunder — a single long roll, like furniture being moved in a room overhead.

Then the helicopter came.

It came out of the south, low and fast, a government gunmetal shape with survey markings stenciled along the tail. Elodie killed the engine and let them drift while it passed over the canyon mouth a mile or so behind them, close enough to see the rotor wash flatten the water. It didn't slow. It didn't circle. It flew straight up the throat of the basin, over the rim, and away north, and the sound of it faded until the loudest thing in the world was the river.

"They saw us," Sable said quietly.

"Maybe."

"They didn't stop."

"No," Elodie agreed. "They didn't."

He watched the empty sky a while longer. A rescue craft stopped. A supply run circled. A craft that passed over two people stranded in a flooded canyon without so much as dipping a wing was either blind or busy, and Elodie had spent enough years around government work — most of it avoiding it — to know that *busy* meant *scheduled*, and *scheduled* meant somebody upstream had a list, and lists like that rarely ended well for the people on the ground.

"Keep your head down next time one comes," he said, restarting the engine. "And keep mine down too, apparently."

"You're getting sentimental."

"I'm getting practical. Sentimental is just practical with better lighting."

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf forty feet above the waterline, under an overhang that would shed rain. Elodie built a small fire in a ring of stones, small enough to smother in seconds, and they ate beans out of a can split between them and drank coffee boiled strong enough to stand a spoon in.

Sable sat with her knees drawn up, staring into the flames. He'd learned her silences by now — there was the silence of someone trying to remember, which had a strained quality to it, like a person pressing on a bruise to see if it still hurt; and there was the silence of someone who had given up trying for the evening, which was softer, almost peaceful. This was the second kind.

"Ask me something," she said suddenly.

"What?"

"When I can't sleep, I try to answer questions about myself. Name, age, hometown. Most of them come back blank or wrong-feeling. But some things stick." She turned her coffee tin slowly in her hands. "So ask me something. Maybe we'll find out what's still in here."

Elodie considered. He was not, by nature or long practice, a man who asked questions; questions invited answers, and answers invited history, and history was the one cargo he'd spent a decade unloading. But she was looking at him across the fire with that borrowed-limbs expression, and the wind was rising outside the overhang, and it seemed suddenly cruel to leave a person alone with her own missing week.

"What's your favorite color?" he asked.

She blinked. "That's your question? I hand you my whole scrambled head and you ask me about colors?"

"Start small. Build up."

"Blue," she said, after a moment, sounding surprised. "Dark blue. Like deep water." She frowned at the fire. "I know that. I don't know how I know it, but I know it."

"There. One fact recovered. At this rate we'll have you fully reassembled by spring."

"What's yours, then? Favorite color."

"Tan," Elodie said.

"Tan."

"Everything out here is tan. It's the honest color. Nothing in the desert can lie to you for long — it all fades back to tan eventually."

"That's the bleakest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once worked for a government agency."

He laughed — short, rusty, surprising himself. Sable smiled at her coffee, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the silence was the good kind, the shared kind, the kind that needed no filling.

Then the first raindrop hit the overhang above them, fat and deliberate, like a knock at the door.

* * *

It rained all night, a steady drumming that turned the world beyond the firelight into a curtain of gray noise. They slept in shifts — Elodie insisted, and Sable, who had begun picking up his habits the way travelers pick up accents, didn't argue. In the gray hour before dawn he woke her, and she took the watch without complaint, and he lay listening to the rain and doing the final arithmetic.

Two days. Maybe less. The storm system was bigger than he'd first read — the rain had weight to it, the steady kind that came from a system parked overhead and pulling moisture up from the coast in a long wet ribbon. Upstream, somewhere over the high mesas, that ribbon was dumping inches per hour onto ground too hard to absorb any of it. All of it was coming here. All of it was coming soon.

At first light the rain thinned to mist and he stood at the edge of the shelf and read the sky one more time, and the sky confirmed it: the western clouds had gone black-green, stacked and boiling, and beneath them the air had that pressed-flat stillness that old desert men called *the held breath*. His father had called it that. His father had known a hundred things about weather and water and none at all about staying solvent, and Elodie pushed the thought away before it could grow roots.

"Sable," he said. "We move now. Full speed up-canyon. We need to be past the narrows and tied off in a cave before the main wall of water comes through."

"How long do we have?"

"If we're lucky, tomorrow afternoon. If we're not, tomorrow morning." He started breaking camp with quick, economical movements. "Either way, today is the last easy day either of us sees for a month."

She didn't waste time asking if he was sure. That was the thing about her he was beginning to rely on, and relying on things was dangerous, and he relied on it anyway: she met bad news the way she met rapids, with her weight forward.

They loaded the boat in the rain-mist, and Elodie pulled the starter cord, and the engine — faithful, spiteful, half-drowned thing — caught on the first pull for the first time since he'd known it.

"See?" Sable said, taking her post at the bow. "You talked nicely to it."

"I threatened it."

"Same thing, in your voice."

They ran upriver into the thickening day, the canyon walls closing in green and dripping, and Elodie kept his eyes moving — water level, sky, water level, sky — and tried not to think about the helicopter, and failed. Somebody had flown over this canyon yesterday and hadn't stopped. Which meant somebody knew what was down here. Which meant somebody might be coming back, and not with ropes and rations.

Around the fourth bend, the mist lifted for a moment, and he saw it: far upstream, where the canyon narrowed to the first gorge, the river had already begun to rise. He could tell by the watermark lines on the rock — fresh ones, darker than the old ones, climbing.

"Sable," he said quietly. "Hold on to something."

She looked back at him, and then at the water, and he watched her do the same math he'd done, watch it land in her eyes.

"How fast can this thing go?" she asked.

"Not fast enough," Elodie said, and opened the throttle.
