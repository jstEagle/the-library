## 6. Downriver

The sky had been lying to them all morning, and Elodie Ashgrove had finally stopped believing it.

It looked like a fine day. That was the trouble with the desert in monsoon season—it wore its best face right up until the moment it tried to drown you. The air was warm and gold, the canyon walls glowed like honeycomb, and the river ran calm and green between the gravel bars. Any fool would look at it and think: *what a lovely morning for a boat ride.*

Elodie looked at it and thought: *we have maybe four hours.*

He'd spent the last two hours patching the survey boat with everything the abandoned camp had offered—a tin of pitch, a strip of canvas, a buckle off Sable's own belt, which she'd surrendered with the expression of a woman donating a kidney. The hull was a patchwork of hope and tar. It would float. Probably. He'd stopped saying "probably" out loud after he caught Sable's face doing something complicated at the word.

"Tell me again," she said now, standing at the bow with her arms crossed, "why we can't just walk out."

"Because walking out means crossing forty miles of hardpan in front of a flash flood." He tested one of the seams with his thumb. "Water goes down this canyon faster than a man can run downhill. You'd be a rumor by nightfall."

"And the boat?"

"The boat is a rumor that floats."

She considered that. "That's not comforting."

"No," Elodie agreed, hauling the patched hull down the shingle toward the waterline. "But it's true, and I find truth more comforting than comfort. Comfort lies. Truth just hurts you honestly."

"You're a very strange man, Ashgrove."

"I'm a very *dry* man," he said. "Ask anyone who's ever been rained on near me."

* * *

They launched at noon.

The first mile was easy—slow water, wide channel, Sable sitting primly amidships with her hands folded as though she were being rowed to church. Elodie steered from the stern with a broken oar and an opinion about everything.

"Lean left when I say left," he told her.

"I know how boats work."

"Do you? Because ten minutes ago you asked me if the pointy end was the front."

"That was a *clarifying question*."

"It was a cry for help."

Sable opened her mouth to argue further—and then the sky ended.

There was no graceful way to describe it. One moment the sun was on the water; the next, the western rim of the canyon simply went dark, as if someone had pulled a curtain across half the world. The wind arrived first, hot and smelling of wet stone, then the sound—a low rolling roar upstream, like the throat of the desert clearing itself.

Elodie's hands went still on the oar for exactly one heartbeat. Then they didn't stop moving again for six hours.

"Left!" he barked.

Sable leaned left. The boat swung. And the rain came down all at once—not falling so much as *arriving*, a gray wall of water that erased the canyon walls, the sky, the difference between up and down. In three seconds Sable's hair was flat to her skull. In five, there was an inch of water in the bilge.

"Bail!"

"With what?"

Elodie threw her the dented cooking pot from the camp supplies. She caught it, stared at it, stared at him—and started bailing.

The river changed under them. It thickened, browned, began to move with the muscular purpose of something that had somewhere to be. Debris came past them—a tumbleweed riding high and fast, a dead mesquite branch the size of a fence post, and then, horribly, a survey stake, snapped clean off, spinning in an eddy like a drowning man's finger pointing downstream.

"How far is this gorge?" Sable shouted over the rain.

"Two miles!"

"Of what?"

"Of *everything*!"

The first rapid took them before she could answer. It wasn't a rapid so much as a staircase the river had built in a fit of temper—three drops in quick succession, water stacking up in white ridges. Elodie read it in a glance, the way other men read a page: *left side open, right side a hole, thread the needle.*

"Left! HARD left!"

Sable threw her weight over. The pot flew out of her hands, clanged off the thwart, and vanished overboard. She made a small sound of pure outrage and grabbed the bail bucket instead—the old canvas hat of some long-gone surveyor, stiff with tar, which held perhaps a quart and leaked perhaps a pint.

"This bucket leaks!"

"So does the boat! You're breaking even! BAIL!"

They hit the first drop. The bow rose, hung, and slammed down into the trough with a crack that Elodie felt in his teeth. Cold water came aboard in a green sheet. Sable went down on her knees in it, kept bailing. The second drop spun them; the third nearly put them sideways, and for one long, glittering moment the whole world was wall-to-wall white water and Elodie's voice saying *lean, lean, LEAN* like a prayer with profanity in it.

Then they were through, and the river widened, and they were both gasping and soaked and alive.

"We lost the pot," Sable said.

"We lost the pot."

"I liked that pot."

"You donated your belt to this boat an hour ago. Your loyalty has no spine whatsoever."

"My belt wasn't *bailing*."

* * *

The gorge proper began an hour later, and it was not a place the word "rapids" did justice. The canyon pinched down until the walls leaned overhead, black with wet, and the river filled every inch of the space between them like a piston in a cylinder. There was no eddy, no slack water, no place to pull over. There was only forward.

The roar ahead sounded like a train in a tunnel.

"Sable." Elodie kept his voice level, because he'd learned long ago that fear was contagious and calm was too, and he might as well infect her with the useful one. "When we go through this, the boat is going to do things you won't like. It may spin. It may stand on its nose. At some point, water will come over the sides in quantities you will consider personal."

"Noted."

"If we capsize, get downstream of the wreck, feet up, and don't grab me unless I grab you. A drowning man holding another drowning man is just a heavier drowning man."

"You've done this before."

"I've done stupider things than this before. This is practically a retirement activity."

The gorge swallowed them.

What followed, neither of them could afterward have described in proper order. There was a drop where the boat went weightless and Sable screamed—not in terror, she insisted later, but in *surprise*, a distinction she defended fiercely. There was a wave that hit them broadside and put the river briefly inside the boat with them, waist-deep, cold as a grudge. There was a moment—Elodie would remember it always—when the current slammed them toward a rock the size of a chapel, and he dug the oar in with everything his shoulders had left, and the boat answered, grudgingly, the way an old mule answers, and they slid past the rock close enough to touch it.

And through all of it, Sable bailed.

She bailed with the leaking hat, with her cupped hands, with a boot she'd pulled off her own foot when the hat went flying again. She bailed while the boat spun. She bailed upside down, nearly, when the stern lifted. She never once stopped, never once screamed at him except to shout coordinates of water coming in—"STARBOARD SEAM!"—like a gunner calling targets, and Elodie, wrestling the oar through chaos, found himself thinking, with a clarity that cut through even the adrenaline:

*I'd trust this woman at my back.*

He hadn't trusted anything at his back in eleven years.

Then the rudder bracket—already cracked from the first drop—gave up entirely with a bang he felt through the deck plates, and the steering oar's collar sheared, and suddenly the boat was a sled on ice with a madman dragging an oar behind it.

"Rudder's gone!"

"Wonderful! What does that mean?"

"It means we steer with prayer and profanity!"

"You're good at both!"

The worst of the gorge came at them then—one final chute where the walls narrowed to less than twice the boat's length and the water dropped away into froth. There was no line to pick. There was only pointing the wrecked thing straight and holding on. Elodie wedged the broken oar against his hip, aimed the bow at the one gap that looked less murderous than its neighbors, and said, conversationally, to the boat, to the river, to whatever was listening:

"Not today."

They went through like a cork out of a bottle—slapped, spun, buried to the gunwales, and spat out the far end into a long, wide, roaring flood-plain of a reach where the canyon opened its jaws and let them breathe.

* * *

They drifted a mile before either of them spoke.

The rain had softened to a steady drumming. The walls stood back, red and dripping. Ahead, the water ran big and brown but honest—no more hidden holes, no more chutes. They'd cleared the worst of it. Elodie knew it in his bones, the way he knew weather and trail and the exact weight of his own silence.

Sable sat amidships in a puddle of her own making, wringing out her hair with both hands. She looked like a drowned cat who had won the fight anyway.

"The rudder," she said finally.

"Gone."

"The pot."

"Gone."

"The hat-bucket."

"Still with us. Barely. Like everything else on this boat."

She looked down at herself—at the ruined boots, the beltless waist, the hands scraped raw from the thwart. Then she looked at him, and something in her face had shifted since noon. Not warmth, exactly. Recognition.

"You steered us through that," she said. "On instinct. With a broken oar and no rudder."

"You bailed us through it. With a hat."

"A *leaking* hat."

"A leaking hat and a boot. I saw the boot. That was inspired."

"It was my *boot*, Ashgrove."

"Yes," he said gravely. "That's what made it inspiring."

She laughed then—a short, startled bark of a laugh, as if it had escaped custody—and pressed her hand over her mouth like she could shove it back in. Elodie pretended not to notice, and found he was smiling at the river instead.

"Why did you come looking for me?" she asked, after a while. "In the flooded canyon. You could have taken the gear, reported the camp, gone home. Nobody pays extra for strays."

Elodie watched the water slide past for a long moment before answering.

"Habit," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I own. I spend a lot of time making sure nobody notices me, Sable. Finding somebody else nobody noticed felt—" He turned the broken oar over in his hands. "Familiar."

She studied him with those unmoored eyes, the ones that held no past week and possibly no past at all, and he braced for the follow-up question—the one about the debt, the records, the erasure. Everyone always asked eventually.

Instead she said: "Well. Next time we survive a gorge together, I expect you to buy me dinner."

"There's a restaurant about ninety miles downstream. I hear the service is flooded."

"Booked," she said. "Naturally."

* * *

They made camp at dusk on a gravel shelf above the flood line, hauling the wounded boat up on rollers of driftwood. Elodie got a fire going under an overhang, using wood Sable gathered without being asked—she was learning the desert faster than she knew, picking dry stuff from under ledges the way he'd shown her that morning, or maybe the way her body remembered and her mind didn't.

They ate the last of the hardtack softened in river water, which tasted exactly as bad as it sounds, and drank tea boiled from water they'd strained through Sable's spare shirt, which she refused to discuss further.

Above them, the storm moved off eastward, trailing lightning like a torn hem. But upstream, far back along the canyon rim, the clouds were still stacked and black and patient. More was coming. There was always more coming, in monsoon season. Days of it. Weeks, maybe.

Elodie did the arithmetic without wanting to: food for four days if they stretched it. Boat barely floating. Rudder gone. And somewhere behind them, a government survey team's equipment sitting in an empty camp, waiting for whoever had flown over yesterday without stopping to come collect it—or collect *them*.

"You're doing the face again," Sable said.

"What face?"

"The one where you count things nobody can see."

"Floods," he said. "Food. Flares. Men in boats."

Her eyes sharpened. "You saw something today. Before the storm. You went quiet when the helicopter passed."

He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"When the flood seals this canyon," he said, "nobody comes in. And nobody leaves. Whatever's happening upstream—with your team, with that camp—it'll happen without witnesses." He glanced at her. "Unless we're the witnesses."

Sable pulled her knees up to her chest. The firelight moved on her face, and for a moment she looked very young and very tired and very much a woman deciding something.

"Then we'd better remember everything," she said quietly. "Before someone decides we shouldn't."

Downstream, around the first bend, the river made a sound Elodie didn't like—a deep, hollow booming, like water pouring into a place with no bottom. He'd heard it once before, years ago, in a canyon that ate a survey crew whole.

He looked at the fire. He looked at Sable.

"Get some sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we find out what this river's hiding."
