## 7. The Spring Beneath the Water

The shelf they'd washed up on was maybe eight feet wide and made of rock the color of old teeth. Below it, the river ran brown and furious, hauling whole trees past them like a crowd shoving toward an exit. Above it, the canyon walls went up so far that the rain came back down as mist.

Sable Vane sat with her back against a boulder and tried to remember her own mother's face.

She got the hat. Her mother had owned a hat, wide-brimmed, the color of eggplant, with a feather that had lost its argument with the wind years ago. She could see the hat perfectly. The face beneath it was a smudge, like a thumbprint on wet ink.

"You're doing it again," Elodie said from the far end of the shelf, where he was rigging their one dry blanket into something between a shelter and a flag of surrender. "That look. Like you're reading a book someone tore pages out of."

"Maybe I am." She turned her palms up. They were raw from bailing. Everything about her was raw—the throat, the eyes, the place behind the ribs where panic lived like a tenant who paid no rent. "I know I had a mother. I know she wore a ridiculous hat. I can't get under the hat."

Elodie considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, which she was beginning to suspect was a kind of armor. "My father had a compass," he said. "Brass case, glass cracked in the northeast corner. I can tell you every scratch on it." A pause. "I couldn't tell you his voice for about four years after he died. Then one day it came back mid-sentence, saying something dull about fence posts. Memory's like that. It comes back when it wants, not when you call it."

"And if it never comes back?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer, and she loved him a little and hated him a little for it.

They had been stranded on the shelf since dawn, when the flood had lifted their patched boat, thought better of it, and set it down again against the rocks hard enough to stove in what remained of the hull. Elodie had hauled her out of the water by the collar like a cat. Now the boat lay wedged and dying at the shelf's edge, and the rain kept up its gray arithmetic, and there was nothing to do but wait and not drown.

* * *

It was thirst that did it, in the end.

By late afternoon the rain slackened to a whisper and the sun cut through low and gold, lighting the canyon walls until they glowed like the inside of a kiln. And Sable saw it—a thread of water, thin as spider silk, sliding down the rock face forty feet above them, gathering in a pocket of stone before spilling over in a bright bead of a waterfall.

"Seep," she said, and heard herself say it in a voice that knew the word too well. "Rain-fed seep off the caprock. It'll be clean. Better than clean—caprock filters it through a hundred feet of sandstone."

Elodie looked at her. "You said you didn't know this canyon."

"I don't." Her mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the desert. "I don't. But apparently my mouth does."

They climbed anyway, because thirst is a tyrant and because neither of them could bear another hour of sitting still. The route was a crack in the wall, a chimney of handholds worn smooth by centuries of other thirsty creatures. Elodie went first, testing each hold with the patience of a man who had decided long ago that dying was possible but hurry was optional. Sable followed, and her hands found the holds without being told where they were.

That should have frightened her more than it did. At the time, she was thinking only of water.

The seep gathered in a basin no bigger than a dinner plate, cupped in the stone, ringed with a crust of white mineral like lace. It was the clearest water she had ever seen. It didn't look like water at all; it looked like a piece of sky that had gotten lost and decided to stay.

Sable knelt and drank.

* * *

The memory did not come gently.

It came the way the flood had come down the gorge—all at once, with debris in it, taking doors off their hinges.

*Sunlight on red water. Not this water—water below, in a slot canyon, a pool the size of a courtyard, so clear the fish seemed to swim through air. Around it, six people in survey khakis, and herself among them, kneeling at the edge with a sample vial, and her own voice saying: "It's not mineral. There's almost nothing in it. That's impossible. Water doesn't come up this clean unless—"*

*Unless.*

*The taste of it. She had tasted it. Of course she had tasted it; everyone had; Dr. Okafor had laughed and said what happens in Devil's Throat stays in Devil's Throat, and they'd all drunk from cupped hands like children at a stock trough.*

*And then—*

*Then the maps. Herself at the camp table at midnight, drawing by lamplight, drawing and drawing, and the strange joy of it, lines flowing out of her pen like the water flowed out of the rock, canyons she'd never walked appearing under her hand complete and correct, and somewhere in the joy a small cold voice asking: how do I know this? Who drew this first? Whose hand am I copying?*

*Hale.*

*Corvus Hale standing at the edge of the pool with his hands clasped behind him, a neat man, a tidy man, silver at the temples, smiling the way men smile when the thing they've wanted has finally walked into the trap. "Seal the site," he'd said. "Nothing goes out. No samples, no charts, no radio traffic. This is now a classified hydrological anomaly." And Priya Okafor—kind, rumpled Dr. Okafor, who had cried at her sister's wedding and shared her coffee ration with everyone—Priya had said, "Classified? Corvus, this is the greatest discovery in the history of— "*

*"Managed," Hale had said. "The team will be managed."*

*And the look on his face when he said it. Not cruelty. Worse than cruelty. Bookkeeping.*

Sable came back to herself on her hands and knees at the edge of the seep, gasping, water dripping from her chin, and Elodie gripping her shoulders and saying her name—her name, Sable, Sable—in a voice pitched carefully flat, the way you talk to a spooked horse or a person on a ledge.

"I remember," she said. Her voice came out shredded. "God help me, Elodie, I remember everything."

* * *

She told him all of it, there on the shelf above the drowned river, while the light went amber and then rose-colored and then gone. How the survey team had found the spring nine days ago—or was it twelve; time had gone soft at the edges—how they'd mapped it, sampled it, celebrated around the cookfire like children. How the water had done its quiet work that very night: Priya weeping over a letter from a brother she'd quarreled with a decade ago, suddenly able to recall the exact words of the quarrel and, more strangely, the exact words of the reconciliation that had followed and been forgotten. How young Tomas Reyes had remembered his grandmother's lullabies in a language he'd been told he'd never learned. How Sable herself had woken at 3 a.m. with the entire layout of a city she'd visited once at age seven drawn in perfect detail behind her eyes.

"It gives back what you lost," she said. "That's what I thought, at first. That's what we all thought. It felt like grace."

"And then?"

"And then I started mapping places I'd never been." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I told myself it was intuition. Surveyors develop instincts. But it wasn't instinct, Elodie. I was remembering other people's surveys. Surveys that were destroyed, or buried, or—maybe people who drank from that spring generations ago and left their memories in the water the way you leave footprints in mud. The spring doesn't just keep water. It keeps *everything the water ever touched.*"

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. Downriver, the flood muttered to itself like a giant turning over in sleep.

"Hale figured that out," he said.

"Hale figured that out before we did." The words tasted like iron. "There was a memo. I wasn't supposed to see it—I saw it because I'm nosy and Priya left her folder open. Preliminary applications. Applications, plural. Therapeutic reclamation of traumatic memory—that was the public face, the grant language. But there was a second column. Selective suppression. Targeted restoration. He'd written in the margin—it was his handwriting, I'd know it anywhere—he'd written *'the asset is not the recovery. The asset is the choice of what is recovered.'*"

She watched Elodie absorb that. He was good at absorbing things; he took information the way sandstone took water, slowly, holding it, letting nothing run off.

"He dosed us," Sable said. "That's the part I keep—keep hitting, like a bruise. After the memo, Hale brought out a cask of 'purified reserve' for the cookfire coffee. We were so tired. We'd been mapping eighteen-hour days. Nobody questioned it. And the next morning—" Her breath caught. "The next morning none of us could remember finding the spring. We woke up knowing we were a survey team in Devil's Throat, knowing our jobs, our names, our skills—and the site itself was a blank. He'd wiped the last four days. He stood there watching us stumble around the camp like sleepwalkers, checking our own instruments, and he said, 'Gentlemen, we appear to have lost the trail. We'll begin again from the top,' and we *believed* him. Why wouldn't we? He was the director."

"But you got away."

"A piece of me did." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Whatever he put in that cask, it wasn't strong enough, or the spring water was still in our blood fighting it, or—I don't know. Bits kept surfacing. I'd find myself drawing the canyon on the corner of a chart and not know why. Tomas started having nightmares he could describe in detail. Priya wrote a note and hid it in her boot. Hale noticed. Of course he noticed—he watches people the way I watch contour lines. So he announced a personnel rotation. Two boats downriver to the junction station. 'For resupply.'"

"There was no resupply."

"There was a deep pool below the third gorge," Sable said, "and a boat that took water fast, and a director who waved goodbye from the rim." Her voice cracked clean through. "Priya couldn't swim. She was from the interior. She used to say deserts were honest and rivers were liars. And he put her in a boat he'd already—"

She stopped. Elodie waited. He was, she was learning, very good at waiting—not the empty waiting of a man with nothing to say, but the full kind, the kind that held space open like a door.

"She grabbed my arm as we pushed off," Sable managed finally. "Last thing she ever said to me. She said, 'Draw it anyway. Whatever they take out of your head—your hands remember. Hands are stubborn.'" A wet, broken laugh. "So I drew it. In the boat, going down the gorge, with water coming over the gunwales, I drew the spring from memory on the back of a chart and sealed it in the map tube and—"

"—and the tube is in the survey camp," Elodie said quietly, "with all the rest of the gear we walked past yesterday."

"With all the rest of the gear," she agreed, "sitting in a neat little camp, waiting for whoever Hale sends to burn it."

* * *

Full dark came down the canyon like a lid. They had a fire the size of a cupped hand—Elodie's doing, built in a hollow so its light wouldn't leak—and tins from the boat's ruined stores, and the blanket-shelter, and the enormous indifferent stars.

Sable lay on her back and let the memories settle in her like sediment in a jar. Some of them were terrible. Some of them were wonderful. All of them were hers, returned without warning, without apology, like a thief breaking back into your house to return what he stole.

"Priya's brother's name was Dev," she said to the sky. "I know that now. I didn't know it this morning. She forgave him at that spring, or the spring let her forgive him, and then Hale erased it, and now she's dead, and I'm the only person alive who remembers that she ever made peace with anyone." Her eyes stung. "Is that a gift or a punishment?"

"Yes," Elodie said.

She turned her head. In the firelight his face was all planes and patience, and there was something in it she hadn't seen before—or had seen only in glimpses. Not softness exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own private arithmetic spoken aloud by someone else.

"You knew," she said slowly. "Before I drank. You knew something was wrong with that water. With me."

"I knew a woman who navigated a flooded gorge in a leaking boat using channels she claimed she'd never seen." He fed a twig to the fire. "I've spent ten years making sure nobody knows anything about me. I know what it looks like when someone's history is sitting on them like weather." A pause. "I should have stopped you from drinking."

"You'd have had to tackle me."

"I considered it."

Despite everything—despite the dead woman named Priya and the brother named Dev and the neat-handed monster named Hale—Sable felt her mouth twitch. "You considered tackling me and decided against it."

"You bite," Elodie said. "Noted during the rapids."

She laughed then, really laughed, and it hurt and helped in equal measure, and for a moment the shelf above the drowning world felt almost like a home.

Then the laughter drained out of her, because the sediment settled and the shape at the bottom of the jar became clear, and it was this:

"Hale will have sent men already," she said. "The moment the boats didn't report in. He won't send rescuers, Elodie. He'll send erasers. And when they're done with the camp and the charts and the tube with Priya's map in it, they'll come downriver looking for bodies." She sat up. The firelight caught the mineral crust on her scraped knuckles. "Or they'll come looking for witnesses. And they'll find two."

Elodie was silent long enough that she thought he might be doing his slow sandstone thing, absorbing, holding. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, flatter, the voice of a man consulting an old ledger.

"The patrol will come by water," he said. "Flood season, it's the only way in. Which means they'll pass this shelf." He looked up at the black walls, the blind stars, the river muttering in the dark like something rehearsing. "Which means tomorrow, for the first time in ten years, I'm going to teach someone how to make a campsite disappear."

Sable pulled the blanket around her shoulders and stared into the small defiant fire, and somewhere far upstream, faint as a lie, she thought she heard the sound of an engine.
