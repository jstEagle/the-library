## 8. Hale's Men

The morning after the first storm, the Throat smelled like wet stone and iron, and Sable Vane was trying to teach me a song.

"It's not a song," I said. "It's a list."

"It's a mnemonic," she corrected, chin up, hair still stiff with river silt. "There's a difference. A song has rhythm. A list is just tyranny."

"Then what you're humming is tyranny with a tune."

She hummed it again anyway — the order of the survey team's names, which she'd reconstructed from her own handwriting on the equipment crates and now recited like a rosary. Reyes. Okafor. Lindqvist. Tomas Merrick. Four names plus hers, five people who had walked into Devil's Throat, and so far we'd found one boat, one camp, and zero cartographers who could remember arriving. The arithmetic of that was not comforting, and she knew it, and she sang it anyway, because singing was how she kept the numbers from eating her alive.

I let her. I was busy watching the water.

The flood had come up overnight the way I'd promised it would — not gradually but all at once, the way a debt collector arrives. The brown water stood six feet over the gravel bar where I'd found her boat, and it carried things: a dead javelina spinning slowly, a fence post, somebody's hat from some upstream town that would never see it again. The canyon walls narrowed ahead into the gorge we'd run yesterday, and the roar coming out of it said the worst was still behind us, still coming down, still hungry.

We'd pulled onto a shelf of rock about forty feet above the current, tucked under an overhang, with the patched boat hauled up on rollers of driftwood and everything we owned — which fit in two canvas bags and my coat pockets — stacked against the wall. It wasn't shelter. It was a delay.

"You're doing the thing," Sable said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at the horizon like it owes you money." She lowered herself onto the driftwood bench we'd improvised and patted the space beside her. "Sit down, Ashgrove. If the sky's going to fall, you can watch it from a seated position like a civilized person."

I sat. That was the trouble with Sable Vane, or one of them: she made sitting down feel like a decision I'd wanted to make myself.

For a while we ate. Breakfast was hardtack softened in creek water caught from a seep above us — I'd learned by day three not to drink anything that touched the main channel, because the flood turned the whole river into a moving argument between every farm, mine, and outhouse upstream. Sable chewed thoughtfully and hummed her tyrannical little list, and I almost — *almost* — relaxed.

That's when I heard the engine.

* * *

You have to understand what an engine means in Devil's Throat during monsoon. It means someone spent real money to be somewhere no sane person would go. It means horsepower, and horsepower means payroll, and payroll means government.

I was on my feet before my brain finished the sentence. I put two fingers to my lips, and Sable — credit where it's due — went silent mid-note and followed my eyes downriver instead of asking questions. Three days ago she'd have asked questions. We were making progress.

The sound came around the bend below the gorge, muffled by the roar of the rapids but unmistakable: the steady diesel knock of an outboard running heavy, pushing against current. Then it came into view, and I pulled us both back under the overhang before I'd even fully processed what I was seeing.

A patrol boat. Aluminum hull, center console, twin outboards, flying survey colors off a short mast — the dusty blue-and-white pennant of the Territorial Survey Office, same as the flags on the abandoned camp. Two men visible at the console, a third in the bow with something long and tubular across his knees. A flare gun. And along the gunwales, stacked with the neatness I'd already learned to hate, evidence bags. Dozens of them.

They weren't running the rapids. They were working the eddies — nosing into every side channel, every slack-water pocket, every place debris collects and, more to the point, every place a body or a boat might collect too. At each stop, one man would get out, walk the bank with his head down, and come back either carrying something bagged or carrying nothing and looking annoyed about it.

"They're looking for survivors," Sable whispered. Her hand found my sleeve. "Elodie, they're looking for—"

"No," I said.

It took me another minute to say more than that, because the shape of it was ugly and I wanted to be sure before I handed it to her. I watched the bow man fire a flare — not a distress signal, not fired upward, but flat across the mouth of a side canyon, a white star that hung there and lit up the slot like a lamp in a drawer. Checking it. Sweeping it. The way you sweep a room you intend to empty.

"They're collecting," I said finally. "Gear, charts, anything with writing on it. And they're checking every hole where a person could hide." I nodded at the flare drifting down as ash. "Survivors get pulled out of the water and wrapped in blankets. This isn't rescue. This is inventory."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Below us, the patrol boat nudged into an eddy fifty yards downstream, close enough that I could hear the men talking — casual, bored, the tone of men doing a chore, not a mission of mercy. One of them laughed at something.

"That's Hale's operation," she said. Her voice had gone flat in a way I was starting to recognize as worse than tears. "Those are his colors. He told the team — he told *me*, whatever's left of me knowing it — that resupply would come after the flood crest. Nobody comes during the crest. Nobody comes *into* this." She looked at me. "Unless they're not worried about the flood."

"Why wouldn't they be worried about the flood?"

"Because they know exactly when it crests." She said it simply, the way you'd report a measurement. "Because they planned this season. Because the camp was stocked for a monsoon extraction and nobody came, because the plan was never extraction." Her fingers tightened on my sleeve. "Elodie. They're not cleaning up gear. They're cleaning up *witnesses*. We're not lost people to them. We're loose ends."

Downstream, the bow man fired another flare into a second side channel. White light. Hanging. Dying.

And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because a man ought to keep honest books even when the ledger shames him: for the first ten seconds, all I thought about was myself.

Ten seconds of pure old habit. *Stay low. Stay small. Let the boat pass. A man who erases himself survives; a man who waves drowns.* Ten seconds of the arithmetic I'd been doing for years, the cold little sums that kept me breathing and kept me nothing. The patrol boat was between us and any route out. If they found us, they'd find a deserter from his own life traveling with a woman whose memory was a crime scene. They'd ask questions I couldn't answer without unburying everything I'd buried. Every instinct I owned, every trick that had kept me invisible from Cinder Flats to the border, said the same word: *stillness.*

Then Sable's hand was still on my sleeve, and she was watching those flares with an expression I can only describe as someone reading their own obituary in a language they used to speak, and the arithmetic changed.

It changed the way water changes — not gradually but all at once.

Because if they were sweeping side channels, they'd sweep this one. Sooner or later, today or tomorrow, a flare would hang over our shelf and light up two people, a boat, and a stack of survey equipment with a missing cartographer's handwriting all over it. Hiding *myself* was a craft I knew. But there were two of us now, and one of us couldn't remember enough to hide, and the other one — I checked inside myself and found this to be true, strange and inconvenient and true — the other one I did not intend to give up.

"All right," I said. "Listen carefully, because we have maybe twenty minutes before they work their way up this far, and then we're going to be very quiet for a very long time."

* * *

Hiding tracks in flood country sounds impossible until you realize the flood does most of the work — the trick is hiding everything the flood *misses*.

Our shelf was connected to the high ground behind it by a goat path through broken scree, and scree records visitors like a ledger. I sent Sable up it first with instructions to walk on stone, never on dirt, and to step where I stepped, and she did it so well I briefly wondered if she'd been a ranger in the week she couldn't remember. Then I went through behind her with a dead juniper branch, dragging it sideways across every disturbed patch, erasing our passage the way you'd erase a chalkboard — not perfectly, but perfectly enough for tired men in a boat who expected to find corpses, not careful ones.

The boat was the harder problem. Canvas and wood, patched with tar and hope, sitting on driftwood rollers forty feet above the waterline. From the air or from the river it was a flag. I couldn't sink it — we'd need it when the flood dropped, if we lived that long — so I did the next best thing. There was a shallow overhang at the back of the shelf, a fold in the rock draped with dead brush, and I rolled the boat under it and built the brush back over the hull until it looked like exactly what this canyon was full of: flood trash. A man sees what he expects to see. Tired men in aluminum boats expect driftwood.

The equipment was hardest of all, because Sable wouldn't let me bury it.

"The charts are in there," she whispered, crouched beside the canvas case, holding it shut like it might bolt. "My handwriting. Proof the team was *here*, proof we mapped—" She stopped. Recalculated. I watched her do it, watched the fear arrive and then get shoved into a drawer. "Proof we existed," she said, quieter. "If they take it, it's like we were never—"

"If they take it, you're easier to erase," I said. "And I'm not having that."

She stared at me. I busied myself with the tarp, because apparently my mouth had started saying things my policy manual hadn't approved, and it was easier to look at knots than at her face.

In the end we compromised, which is to say I got my way dressed up as teamwork. We split the load: the instruments and the sample cases went into a crack in the rock wall behind a slab I could shift and replace, sealed in oilcloth. The charts Sable insisted on keeping went into the waterproof tube strapped inside her coat, against her body, where a search party would have to be extremely rude to find them. Everything else — the tent fly, the cook kit, the odds and ends of camp life — stayed visible on the shelf, arranged to look like a camp that had been washed out and abandoned. Let them read a story. Just not ours.

"Where did you learn this?" Sable asked as I smeared wet ash over the fresh scratches on the rock where we'd dragged the boat. "Not the tracking. The *un*-tracking. You move like someone who's been erased before."

"I move like someone who reads a lot," I said.

"You move like someone who's practiced."

"I read a lot *and* practice."

She let it go, which was its own kind of mercy, and I let it go, which was my own kind of cowardice, and together we finished the job just as the diesel knock rounded the last bend below us.

* * *

What follows, I set down in detail, because detail is the only weapon I had that day and I've learned not to waste ammunition.

The patrol boat came up the channel slow, both outboards idling, the bow man standing with the flare gun resting on the rail. Forty yards out. Thirty. Close enough to count the evidence bags — I counted eleven full ones — and close enough to hear one of the men say, clear across the water:

"Another hat. Third one today. Flood's giving up its dead, I guess."

"There's no dead," the helmsman said. "That's the point. Hale wants *confirmation* there's no dead. Sweep the slots, bag the paper, and we're drinking in Cinder Flats by Friday."

"And if there is a dead?"

"Then there's a dead, and we make it look like drowning, and we're still drinking in Cinder Flats by Friday."

Sable's breath went out of her in a thin stream. I put my hand flat on the rock between us — not touching her, just there, the way you'd brace a wall — and she pressed her palm down on top of it, and we both stayed very, very still.

The bow man raised the flare gun toward our side channel.

I had done everything right. Tracks brushed, boat hidden, ash smeared, story staged. And none of it mattered, because a flare doesn't read tracks. A flare just looks. And we were thirty feet up a shelf behind a lip of rock, and if that white star rose over our position it would pour light down into our pocket like water into a cup, and there was nowhere to be that light wouldn't reach.

Except one place.

I grabbed Sable's collar and pulled us both backward off the shelf's lip into the hollow beneath the overhang — the same fold of rock where the boat lay hidden — and I mean *into*, flat on our backs in six inches of flood seep and grit, the dead brush closing over our faces like a lid. Through the twigs I could see the sky, a gray slit of it, and the bow man's silhouette against it, raising his arm.

He hesitated.

I will think about that hesitation for the rest of my life. Something made him pause — the angle, the wind, the simple human laziness of a man who'd already fired nine flares at empty holes and wanted his Friday. His arm came down an inch. The helmsman shouted something about the current pulling. And the flare went up crooked, arcing wide, bursting fifty yards south of us over bare rock, lighting up nothing but stone and spray and the indifferent brown water.

"Empty," the bow man called down. "Next one."

The engines knocked. The boat slid past our shelf, so close I could have spat into its wake, and moved on upriver toward the gorge, hunting emptiness and finding it, and Sable Vane lay in the mud beside me shaking — not with fear, I realized after a moment, but with fury, silent and total, her jaw clenched so hard I could hear her teeth.

"*Confirmation,*" she breathed, when the sound had faded. "They're confirming. Like we're weather. Like we're a spill." She rolled her head to look at me, mud to the eyebrows, eyes bright and terrible. "Whoever this Hale is, he didn't lose a survey team. He *spent* one. And now he's sending men to pick up the change."

"Yes," I said.

"And you knew. On the shelf, before they got here. You knew and you hid *us* — not yourself, us, you didn't even hesitate—" She stopped. Studied my face in the twig-filtered gloom, and whatever she saw there made her frown. "Why? You don't know these people. You don't know me. Your whole — your entire *posture*, Ashgrove, is a man whose survival plan fits in one coat pocket. Why break it for mine?"

I should have lied. I'm good at lying; it's the one trade I've kept current. Instead I looked up through the dead brush at the gray slit of sky where a flare had failed to find us, and I heard myself say:

"Because somebody has to be worth remembering, and it might as well start with you."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Somewhere above us, water dripped off the overhang in a slow count. Sable opened her mouth, closed it, and finally said, with the gentle precision of a person defusing something:

"That's the saddest sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I once woke up in a boat with no past."

"I'm aware."

"We're going to work on it."

"Noted."

But she squeezed my hand once, there in the mud, before letting go, and neither of us mentioned it again, and I found that I didn't mind the mud nearly as much as I should have.

* * *

We gave the patrol boat two hours' lead, then climbed out of the hollow and assessed our kingdom: one shelf, one hidden boat, one crack full of instruments, and a canyon that was now actively hostile in two directions — the flood below us, and Hale's cleanup crew working its way upriver above us. Trapped between the water and the water's owners.

It was Sable who spotted it first, sweeping the binoculars salvaged from camp across the far wall while I rechecked the boat's patch. She went very still, the way she did when memory knocked instead of when danger did.

"Elodie. Look. Upstream, past the gorge — the tributary on the north wall. There's a mark on the scree. Fresh. Someone climbed out of the water there, recently, and went up."

I took the glasses. She was right: a smear of displaced stone, a broken cobweb line, the faintest suggestion of a handhold polished by a desperate grip. Someone had come out of the flood within the last day and climbed for their life. And below the mark, snagged in a drowned willow, turning slowly in the current, was something small and dark and rectangular.

Even at distance, I knew the shape. Everyone in the territory knew the shape of a survey sample case.

"Someone made it out of the water," Sable whispered beside me. "One of the team. They're *alive*."

I kept the glasses on the willow, on the climbing mark, on the tributary slot winding away north into country the flood couldn't reach — and I thought about flare guns and evidence bags and men drinking in Cinder Flats by Friday, and I understood that whoever had climbed that wall wasn't just a survivor.

They were the last loose end. And so was the case in the willow.

"We rest tonight," I said, handing back the glasses. "First light, we cross."

Sable was already humming. Not the list of names this time — something new, quick, climbing, like a footpath going uphill.

"That's not a mnemonic," I said.

"No," she agreed. "That's a plan."
