## 10. The Thief Revealed

The tributary announced itself before we saw it—a green thread of willow and tamarisk stitching down the canyon wall, the first living color Devil's Throat had offered us in two days. Sable spotted it from the shelf where we'd made camp, and something in her face changed, some cartographer's instinct firing in a brain that kept misfiling its own history.

"That's Cottonwood Wash," she said. "It's on my charts."

"You've never been here," I reminded her gently.

"I know." She frowned at it the way a person frowns at a stranger wearing their coat. "But I drew it anyway. Look at the meanders—I wouldn't invent those. Nobody invents meanders that ugly."

It was as good a reason as any to go look, and better than most. We'd spent three days in the sea cave bailing and trading truths, and I'll be honest: I was tired of my own truths. A man can only hear himself confess to cowardice so many times before he starts wanting to do something about it. So when the water in the cave receded enough to show us a game trail along the tributary's edge—worn by bighorn, cut fresh in one place by a boot heel—I didn't pretend to deliberate.

"Boot print," I said, crouching. "Left foot dragging. Somebody hurt."

Sable came up beside me. "Tomas?"

"Maybe. Maybe Hale's man checking channels." I touched the print's edge. The clay held the impression clean, no crumble, no curl. "No. Hours old, not days. And this drag—" I moved ahead ten yards to the next visible print. "Same drag. Same boot. One person, going upstream, falling down here." There was a body-shaped smear in the mud where someone had caught themselves on hands and knees. "And getting back up."

"Stubborn," Sable said.

"Or scared. Scared and stubborn is the most dangerous combination there is, and also the only kind worth following."

We went up the wash single file, me first, reading the story in the mud the way Sable read her own handwriting on charts of country she couldn't remember. That was the thing about tracking that nobody who isn't a tracker understands: it's not seeing, it's listening with your eyes. And the wash was talking. It said: one man, injured, moving in bursts. It said he'd stopped twice to drink and once to vomit. It said that near a bend where the water pooled deep and dark, he'd sat for a long time—there was a flattened patch of sedge, a ring of disturbed pebbles—and then stood up and kept going, which told me more about him than anything else. A man who sits down beside deep water in his condition and gets up again is either very brave or has argued with himself about it and won.

I didn't say that part out loud. Some readings you keep to yourself until you know how they end.

* * *

We found him an hour past noon, in a hollow where the wash bent against a sandstone wall and the willows grew thick enough to make a green room of shade.

He was half in the water. Not drowned—half-drowned is its own condition, a limbo between the river and the world, and he hung there like a man who hadn't decided which side he belonged to. His legs were still in the shallows. His upper body was sprawled across a gravel bar, one arm flung out, and clutched in the fist of that arm, gripped with the desperate strength of the nearly gone, was a metal sample case.

He was maybe twenty-five. Younger than I'd expected. The thief I'd been hired to hunt had grown enormous in my imagination over the weeks—a phantom with my contract attached to him, a figure worth money, worth the trouble of crossing Devil's Throat. This was just a boy with river-mud in his hair and lips gone gray-blue with cold, shivering even in the heat, which is the worst sign a body can give you. There was a gash above his ear, crusted brown, and his breathing came in wet whistles that I did not like at all.

Sable knelt first. She put two fingers at his throat and looked up at me, and her whole face had changed—the wariness gone, replaced by something raw.

"He's alive."

"Barely." I got my hands under the boy's arms and hauled him up the bar, out of the reach of the water. He weighed nothing. Monsoon country does that to people; it gives and takes with the same hand. I got my bedroll under him and my spare shirt over him, and Sable built a small fire from driftwood while I checked him over the way I'd check any found animal: broken bones, none obvious; the head wound; feet cut to ribbons, meaning he'd walked a long way barefoot or near it; and the case.

The case I set aside without opening it. Habit. You don't open other people's cases. But I noticed my hands wanted to, and I noticed something else too, something that sat in my chest like a stone dropped into a well: I had crossed a desert to find this boy. My contract was to run him down, take back what he stole, and bring proof. Every mile of the last weeks had pointed here, to this gravel bar, to this fist around this case. And now that I'd arrived, all I could think was *get him warm.*

That should have worried me more than it did.

* * *

He surfaced out of delirium near dusk, and what came up first was not his name.

"No—" He thrashed, nearly scattering our fire, and I put a hand flat on his chest—not restraining, just anchoring, the way you'd steady a spooked horse. "No, you can't pump it, you can't—it's not yours, it doesn't belong to—"

"Easy," I said. "Nobody's pumping anything. You're at Cottonwood Wash. You're safe."

His eyes found mine and focused with an effort that cost him visibly. They were pale gray, fever-bright. "You're not survey," he rasped.

"No."

"You're not Hale's?"

"No."

Something in him let go then, a tension I could see leaving his shoulders like water out of a cracked boat. He started to cry, quietly, the way exhausted young men cry—without any sound, just the shaking and the wet eyes, embarrassed even through the delirium.

"I'm Tomas," he managed. "Tomas Reyes. Junior surveyor. Third season." As if the credentials might protect him. As if anyone outranked by Hale could be protected by a title.

"Sable Vane," Sable said, and the boy's eyes went wide.

"You're dead," he whispered. "You fell. At the spring. You fell into the—into the water, and Hale said—" He stopped. Looked at her properly. Looked at me. "You're *alive.*"

"Apparently," Sable said, dry as hardpan, but her voice wasn't steady, and I understood why. Somewhere behind those scrambled memories of hers, this boy knew things she didn't. He was a page torn out of her own book.

"Tomas." I crouched so we were eye to eye. "Three weeks ago a man rode into Cinder Flats and hired me to track a thief who stole from a government survey. Sample case, sealed instruments, the lot. That thief is supposed to be you."

He flinched. "I didn't steal—"

"I know," I said. "I'm telling you what the paper says. Now tell me what actually happened."

He looked at the case in the firelight. His hand crept toward it and stopped, hovering, the way you hover over something both precious and cursed.

"They sent me down to collect," he said. "Water samples. Routine, they said. Except the spring isn't routine, and everyone on the team knew it and nobody would say it out loud, because Dr. Vane—" he glanced at Sable, apologetic, grieving, "—because she figured out what it does first, and then Hale sealed the site and started dosing the coffee, and people started forgetting things. Little things. Then big things. Then Rennick forgot he had a daughter. Just—stood there one morning with her photograph in his hand and asked whose kid it was." His voice cracked. "Hale called it containment. Called *us* containment. And when Dr. Vane fell into the source pool and came up remembering everything, clearer than any of us, Hale decided she was a liability, and I—" 

He stopped again. Swallowed.

"I took the case because it's proof," he said. "Samples, sealed, dated, chain-of-custody logged in my own hand. Water that does what that water does, documented properly, can't be waved away. I ran at night. Downriver. Hale's men chased me into the gorge and I lost the boat and I lost—" he touched the gash above his ear, wonderingly, as if meeting it for the first time, "—I don't remember much after that. Waking up in the shallows. Walking. Keeping it dry." His fist tightened on the case again. "Keeping it dry. That was the whole job. Keep it dry, keep walking."

"And you walked on a ruined head and half-drowned lungs," I said, "for three days."

"It was the whole job," he repeated, stubborn as his own boot prints, and despite everything—despite the hunt dissolving under me like salt in water—I felt the corner of my mouth move.

* * *

Later, when Tomas slept a fevered sleep against the sandstone and Sable sat turning the sample case over in her hands, studying the seals, the dates, the neat chain-of-custody entries in a junior surveyor's careful script, I walked to the edge of the wash and looked at the water running dark under the last light.

I had a contract in my pack. Paper, signed, witnessed. Find the thief. Recover the property. Deliver proof of capture to the survey office in Cinder Flats, where Corvus Hale's signature waited at the bottom of the payment line.

Every professional instinct I owned—the ones that had kept me fed and invisible for years—said the same thing: deliver the boy, deliver the case, collect the money, disappear. That was the arrangement. That was the shape of my life, worn smooth as a river stone by repetition.

Except the arrangement was with a man who dosed his own team's coffee. Except the property was proof of a crime. Except the thief was a boy who'd walked three days on a cracked skull to keep evidence dry, and the woman who'd hired-adjacent wandered into my life with no past and a leaky boat, and somewhere in the bailing and the arguing and the long confinement of the flood, I had stopped being a man alone in the desert and become half of something.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Sable said, coming to stand beside me. She didn't look at me. She watched the water. "I can tell. You get a line right here—" she tapped her own brow, "—when you're calculating whether a thing is survivable."

"The contract pays enough to clear a third of what I owe."

"And?"

"And delivering Tomas to Hale delivers a witness to the man who wants witnesses erased." I turned the thought over and found it had no honest bottom. "And the case goes back into the hands of the man who poisoned a spring so he could decide what a whole basin full of people remembers."

"So the contract dies here."

"Contracts die all the time," I said. "Mostly of natural causes." I looked at her. "You understand what that means. The debt stays. The invisibility stays. Everything I've spent six years building—this careful nothing of a life—keeps costing me."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then she said, "You hid your tracks for me. Back in the canyon, when the patrol came. You didn't have to. You were the invisible man, and you reached out and made two sets of footprints instead of zero, and you didn't even hesitate." She finally turned to look at me, firelight catching the edge of her face. "Elodie. You've already paid more than a third of a debt tonight. You just haven't sent the payment anywhere yet."

I didn't have an answer for that. Trackers learn to sit comfortably inside silence, so I did, and she let me, and below us the tributary ran on toward the main channel, carrying its small cargo of night toward the flooded Throat.

Behind us, Tomas cried out in his sleep—one word, over and over, thin as a whistle:

"*Remember. Remember. Remember.*"

Sable and I looked at each other.

"Tomorrow," I said, "he tells us everything. All of it. While he still can."

Because I'd heard that note in a dying man's voice before, and I knew what it meant: the boy was afraid not of dying, but of being forgotten mid-sentence—of his testimony drowning with him in that gray space between the river and the world.

And far downstream, faint but unmistakable on the night wind, came a sound that turned my blood to ice water: engines. More than one. Coming upriver.
