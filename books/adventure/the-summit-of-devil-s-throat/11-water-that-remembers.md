## 11. Water That Remembers

The sample case had spent a night drying by the fire, and by morning it looked less like evidence of a crime and more like something a careful person had loved very much.

Sable turned it over in her lap while the sea cave dripped its slow arithmetic around them. Brass corners, leather gone soft with river water, a latch that opened only if you pressed left first, then right — someone's private grammar. Inside, nested in wool, sat six vials of water so clear they seemed to disappear against her palm, each stoppered and waxed and labeled in a hand she recognized before she recognized anything else about it.

Her own.

*Seep A — primary. Do not dilute. Do not ingest without baseline documentation.*

She read it twice. The handwriting was hers — the same impatient loop on the *g*, the same habit of pressing so hard on downstrokes that the paper nearly gave way. She had written those words. She could not remember writing them, could not remember the seep, could not remember deciding that anyone who drank this water should be documented first, like a patient, like a subject.

"Found your penmanship?" Elodie asked from the mouth of the cave, where he was doing something patient and useless-looking to the boat's ruined rudder. He had a way of asking questions that sounded like he already knew the answers and was simply giving you the dignity of arriving there yourself.

"It's mine," she said. "The labels. All of them."

"And?"

"And I don't remember it." She heard how flat her voice came out and hated it a little. "You'd think that would stop being surprising. It doesn't."

Tomas slept against the far wall, wrapped in every dry thing they owned, his breathing doing that stuttering thing it had done since they pulled him out of the tributary. Fever, Elodie said. Delirium. But when Tomas woke in his lucid stretches, he looked at Sable like a man looking at a door he was afraid to open, and she was beginning to understand why.

She uncorked one vial and held it to the gray cave light.

It was just water. That was the maddening thing. No color, no shimmer, no cinematic glow. If you set it beside the canteen water Elodie had boiled that morning, you couldn't have told them apart. And yet somewhere downstream, beneath the flood, a spring had filled these vials, and a survey team had carried them up out of Devil's Throat, and Corvus Hale had decided the whole canyon needed to be erased for their sake.

"You're not going to drink it," Elodie said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm going to test it," Sable said. "There's a difference."

"There usually isn't."

She smiled despite herself. Two weeks ago — or what she believed was two weeks; time in the Throat had gone soft as wet paper — she would have bristled at being managed. Now she found she liked being argued with by a man who argued the way other people hummed: absentmindedly, and mostly to keep himself company.

"Baseline first," she said, and took out one of the camp's field notebooks, the ones she'd sorted on the second day, the ones full of her own handwriting describing terrain she'd never seen. She wrote the date. She wrote *Subject: S. Vane.* Under it, because it felt important, she wrote everything she currently knew about herself, in order:

*Name: Sable Vane. Cartographer, Basin Survey. Brother: Merrick. Mother taught me stars. I am afraid of deep water and I don't know why. I like the smell of creosote after rain. I once —*

The pen stopped.

*I once* what? There should have been more. There should have been years of *once*. A childhood. A first job. A reason she feared deep water. She stared at the blank space after the dash until the letters seemed to swim, and then she wrote, honestly, because the notebook deserved honesty:

*I once had a life and cannot produce it on demand.*

Elodie had come over quietly and was reading upside down, which apparently was a ranger skill along with tracking and looking unimpressed. "That's a good list," he said.

"It's a short list."

"It's an honest one. Those are rarer." He crouched beside her. "What's the test?"

"Sips," she said. "Small ones. Documented. We see what changes." She tapped the vial label. *Do not ingest without baseline documentation.* Past-me knew something. Past-me, whoever she'd been out here with Hale's team, had been frightened enough to write instructions to herself in capital letters.

"Past-you sounds smarter than present-you," Elodie observed.

"Present-you can carry past-me back to the boat if this goes badly."

"That's the plan? Carry you?"

"I'm lighter than I look."

"You look exactly as heavy as you are. It's one of your better qualities."

She laughed — actually laughed, which startled them both — and then, before the laughter could talk her out of it, she touched the vial to her lips and took a sip.

It tasted like nothing. Like cold. Like the inside of a stone.

For a long moment, nothing happened at all, and she felt almost embarrassed, sitting in a dripping cave waiting for magic from a mouthful of groundwater. Then—

The cave went away.

Not darkness. *Elsewhere.* She was standing ankle-deep in a pool lit green from below, and around her stood people in survey khakis, and one of them was saying something about flow rates, and the pool was fed by a seam in the rock that wept water in threads fine as hair, and someone — Hale, she knew it was Hale even though his face wouldn't resolve, his face kept sliding like a fish under ice — someone said, *"Bottle the primary. Everything else gets diluted. And nobody drinks on site again, do you hear me? Nobody."*

And a woman's voice, her own voice, younger somehow, asked: *"Why? What does it do?"*

And Hale laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound she had ever heard, warm and reasonable, the laugh of a man explaining arithmetic to children. *"What does it do? Ashford, tell her what it does."*

And a man named Ashford — she could see him clearly, could see the ink stain on his left thumb, could see that he was afraid — said, *"It gives back what's lost. That's the finding. Memory loss, trauma suppression, induced forgetting. You drink, and what was taken comes home."*

*"And if we control the supply?"* Hale said, still pleasant, still gentle. *"If we can give back whatever we choose — and take away whatever we choose? Think of it as editing, Vane. Not erasure. Editing. A man confesses to the wrong crime; we correct his memory. A witness remembers too much; we help him forget. Whole districts, if we scale it. Populations that remember only what we've approved. It's not a weapon. It's a service."*

The vision broke.

Sable came back to herself on the cave floor with Elodie's hand hard on her shoulder and her own name in his mouth, said three times, getting louder. The vial lay empty beside her. She didn't remember drinking the rest of it. Her body had done that on its own, greedy, reaching for the rest of the scene the way a drowning woman reaches for light.

"How long," she croaked.

"Four minutes. You were talking."

"What did I say?"

"'Green pool,' 'flow rates,' and then—" He hesitated, and Elodie hesitating was worse than any amount of bad news. "Then you said, in a voice that wasn't yours, *'It's not a weapon, it's a service.'* Then you started crying and asked for your mother, and then you went quiet."

Sable sat up slowly. The cave reassembled itself around her: the drip, the fire, Tomas's breathing, the boat skeleton at the entrance, the rain-hammered world beyond. All of it real. All of it *still there*, which suddenly felt like a miracle she'd never earned.

"It shows you things," she said. "Lost things. I saw the spring. I saw the team. I saw—" Her throat closed. "I saw Hale explain what he wants to do with it."

"And?"

"He doesn't want to destroy it, Elodie. Destroying it would be mercy. He wants to *own* it." She grabbed the notebook, hands shaking, and wrote as fast as the words would come. "The water restores lost memory — that's true, that part's even beautiful. But restoration means access. If you can put memories back, you can choose *which* memories. You can take a man who remembers a crime and make him remember an alibi. You can take a town that watched something happen and give them a version where it didn't. It doesn't erase. It *overwrites*. The old memory doesn't come back wrong — it comes back replaced, and the person can't tell, because to them it was always that way."

Elodie had gone very still. In her experience — her short, rented, unreliable experience — Elodie's stillness meant he was either planning something or remembering something, and both wore the same face.

"You felt it," he said quietly. "Just now. The rewrite."

"No. What I got was recovery. My own memories, coming home." She stopped. Looked at the empty vial. Looked at her notebook, at the short sad list of who she was. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless some of what just came back wasn't mine to recover." Her voice dropped. "Unless the reason my week is missing, the reason my *life* is missing in pieces, is that someone already edited me once — and what I'm calling recovery is just the water restoring whatever version was last saved." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I can't tell anymore which memories are original equipment. I remember my mother teaching me stars. Do I? Or did someone decide I should remember that, because it made a cleaner story?"

"Sable."

"Because that's the product, don't you see? That's what Hale was selling upstairs, to whoever he reports to. Not truth serum. Not amnesia drug. *Certainty.* People who are certain their memories are real. You never have to fight a population that's certain. They'll defend the edit themselves. They'll love it. They'll call it healing."

The drip counted. Somewhere outside, the flood spoke in its low constant voice, the voice of a thing with all the time in the world.

Elodie picked up the case and turned it in his big careful hands, the way he handled everything — as if objects might be more fragile than they appeared, himself included. "Six vials," he said. "He sent a junior surveyor to steal six vials of the most dangerous substance in the territory, and the thief ran *toward* the wilderness instead of out of it."

"Tomas."

"Tomas. Who, when he could talk, kept saying the same thing over and over." Elodie's eyes went to the sleeping man. "'Don't let him have the primary source. Don't let him have the primary source.' I thought it was fever talk."

"It's not fever talk." Sable felt the pieces click together, cold and clean, like a lock accepting its key. "Six vials is a sample. Samples go to labs. Labs mean replication, dosage, scaling. Hale let the theft happen, Elodie — or arranged it — because a stolen sample gives him deniability. *Look, thieves hit our survey. Tragic.* While the territory chases a ghost through the Throat, he brings the real operation downriver and takes the spring itself." She looked up. "How long has it been since the helicopter?"

"Nine days. Maybe ten."

"And the flood seals the canyon for weeks. Nothing comes in or out except by water." She was standing now, she realized, pacing the small bright circle of the fire. "He's not sending patrols anymore. Patrols were cleanup. Next comes the *expedition*. Pumps. Casks. Enough containers to drain the seep and enough poison to salt whatever's left. He'll wait for the flood to drop just enough for heavy boats, and he'll come take his service national."

"You got all that from four minutes and a sip?"

"I got it from Hale's own mouth. He narrates. Men like that always narrate — they think a good explanation is the same thing as permission." She stopped pacing. "There's one thing I didn't understand before. One thing I do now."

"Say it."

"The team. Twelve people, gear stacked neat, no tracks out. I kept thinking *massacre*, or *abduction*, or something with bodies." Her stomach turned. "No. He dosed them. He sat them down at that camp, one by one, and he edited them — gave them new weeks, new reasons, new names if he wanted — and walked them out the top of the canyon as different people who'd never seen a spring. That's why the trail ends. The people who made that trail stopped existing. Their replacements walked a different direction entirely." She hugged her arms against a chill that had nothing to do with the cave. "I might be the only one the water spat back out unfinished. Adrift in a leaky boat with a hole in my week, because something interrupted the edit."

"Or because you fought it," Elodie said.

She looked at him.

"Past-you," he said, tapping the vial label, "*do not ingest without baseline documentation.* Capital letters. That's not a lab note. That's a woman leaving herself instructions because she knew what was coming. Whatever else got edited out of you, the part that fights stayed." He shrugged, awkward, a man distributing compliments like they cost him money. "I've been tracking one kind of person my whole career. The kind who runs. You're not that."

"Don't make me like you," she said thickly. "I'm having a crisis."

"Noted. I'll be unlikeable after breakfast."

She laughed again — wetter this time — and then Tomas made a sound.

They both turned. He had surfaced from sleep into one of his lucid stretches, and his eyes, fixed on the open brass case in Elodie's hands, had gone wide and terrible with recognition.

"That's it," Tomas whispered. "That's the primary. Where did you—" His gaze snapped to Sable, to the empty vial, and something in his face crumpled. "Oh no. Oh, no, no. How much did you drink? How much did you *drink*?"

"One vial. Why? What happens—"

Tomas pushed himself up on his elbows, and the fever in his eyes burned through the lucidity like sun through fog, and what he said next was perfectly, horribly clear:

"Then you need to tell me everything you remember right now. *Everything.* Before it finishes settling. Because the primary doesn't restore, cartographer — it *replaces* — and if Hale's version goes in deeper than yours, you won't know whose memories you're living in." He grabbed her wrist with shocking strength. "Start with your name. Tell me your name, and tell me you're sure."

Outside, beneath the rain, far down the drowned throat of the canyon, Sable thought she heard — faint, impossible, or perhaps only wishful fear — the low knock of engines against water.

Someone was coming early.
