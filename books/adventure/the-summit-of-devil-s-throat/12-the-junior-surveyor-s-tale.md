## 12. The Junior Surveyor's Tale

The fever had a geography all its own.

Tomas Rehnquist drifted through it the way he'd once drifted through the salt flats of the northern basin: by trusting that the ground would hold him even when he couldn't see it. Sometimes the fever was a red canyon with walls that breathed. Sometimes it was his mother's kitchen in Harrow Bend, steam on the windows, his father's voice reading survey coordinates aloud like scripture — *bearing two-seven-zero, distance four hundred links* — because his father believed a boy should fall asleep to numbers the way other boys fell asleep to songs.

Sometimes it was the spring.

He surfaced from one of those dives to find a canvas roof over his head and the smell of wet stone and someone's boot pressed against his ribs, shaking him with what could only be described as diplomatic rudeness.

"Alive?" said the boot's owner.

Tomas opened his eyes. The world swam, then steadied into a sea cave lit by a single oil lamp, water talking to itself somewhere in the dark behind them. A lean man crouched over him with the weathered face of somebody who'd been rained on professionally for years. Behind him sat a woman Tomas knew — knew the way you know a word you've read a thousand times but can't define.

"Sable," he croaked.

Her head came up sharply. "You know me?"

"You're the cartographer." He tried to sit up and discovered his body had filed a formal complaint against the entire concept. "Sable Vane. You draw better contour lines than anyone I've ever — you hum when you ink. You don't know you do it. You hum."

Something crossed her face — hunger and fear sharing the same plate. "I don't remember humming."

"Well," Tomas said, and then, because fever makes philosophers of us all, "there's a lot of that going around."

* * *

The lucid stretches came like weather. He learned to feel them arriving — a cooling behind the eyes, the world snapping into focus like a lens finding its subject — and he learned to spend them wisely, the way a man spends the last of his money.

The first thing he did with a good stretch was drink the water Elodie held to his lips. Plain water, boiled. He made sure to ask.

"Just rain off the rock," the ranger said. "We've been careful."

"Good." Tomas laughed, and it hurt, and he kept laughing anyway, because the alternative was something worse. "Careful. Yes. We were careful too, at the end. Careful as church mice. It didn't matter."

"Tell me," Elodie said. Not gently, exactly. Honestly. There was a difference, and Tomas had come to prefer it.

So he told him.

* * *

"It started as the best posting of my life," Tomas said. "I want you to understand that part first. Everyone's going to want to hear the horror story, but the truth is it began like a dream job. Corvus Hale personally requested me for the Devil's Throat hydrographic survey. *Personally.* Do you know what that means for a junior surveyor? Hale was famous. His reports went straight to the capital. Men built careers just carrying his instrument cases."

"He recruited you himself," Sable said quietly.

"He shook my hand. He remembered my thesis title. 'Alluvial Memory Structures in Closed-Basin Aquifers.' He said, 'That's exactly the kind of mind this expedition needs,' and I floated out of his office like a parade balloon." Tomas's mouth twisted. "I thought I'd been chosen. Later I understood I'd been *selected*, which is a different word wearing the same coat."

Elodie fed the lamp a little more oil. "Selected how?"

"Nobody on that team had strong family. Nobody had a supervisor who'd raise questions if we went quiet. Sable —" he glanced at her, apologetic — "you'd been in the field eleven months. Your file said 'no next of kin reachable.' That wasn't true, was it?"

Sable's jaw tightened. "My brother is reachable. My brother just never writes."

"There it is," Tomas said softly. "Hale reads files the way you read maps. Every gap is a door."

* * *

The second lucid stretch, three days later, he spent on the spring itself.

"We found it in the third week," he said. "A seep, high on the north wall where the canyon bends — you'd never find it from the river, it's tucked behind a shelf of travertine. The water comes up warm and tastes faintly of iron, like a coin held in your mouth. Dr. Okafor tested it six ways. Minerals, nothing exotic. Nothing that should do what it does."

"And what does it do?" Elodie asked, though Tomas suspected he already half-knew; the ranger had the look of a man assembling a picture from pieces he didn't enjoy holding.

"I drank it on a dare." Tomas smiled at the ceiling of the cave. "Pell — our instruments man, big fellow, afraid of nothing except moths — dared me, because I'd been complaining I couldn't remember my grandmother's face. Just her hands. Isn't that strange? Twenty-six years old and my grandmother was a pair of hands kneading dough. I drank maybe a cup of the seep water to shut Pell up, and that night I dreamed her whole kitchen. The blue jar on the shelf. The crack in the east window shaped like a running dog. Her voice, saying my name wrong, the way she always did — *Tomás*, with the accent, though we weren't Spanish, she'd just decided I deserved one."

His voice caught. He let it.

"The water gives back what you lost," he said. "That's what Okafor wrote in her log, before the logs were taken away. And Hale stood at the seep with his hat in his hands like a man in church, and he said — I'll never forget it, I wish to God I could forget it — he said, '*Do you understand what we're standing on? This isn't a spring. This is an eraser.*'"

Sable made a small sound. Elodie looked at her, and she shook her head — *later* — and Tomas understood there were things being carried in that boat besides bailing buckets.

* * *

The worst stretch of telling was the dosing. He saved it for his clearest hour, mid-morning, when the light came gray through the cave mouth and his head felt almost like his own.

"Hale started with the tea," he said. "Every morning, a thermos of it. 'Field blend,' he called it. Bitter. We joked about it — Pell called it swamp juice, Sable used to —" he stopped, because Sable had gone very still. "You used to trade yours to me. You hated it. You'd say, *Tomas, drink my sins.*"

"I remember drinking something," Sable whispered. "I remember being angry about the taste and not knowing why I kept accepting it."

"Because Hale watched us drink it. And because the first dose only takes the edges." Tomas flexed his fingers, watching them tremble. "Little things go first. What you ate Tuesday. Which fork of the canyon you scouted. Then bigger things. Okafor forgot her husband's name for a full day and cried without knowing why. And here's the devilish part — the elegant, evil part — when your memory comes back wrong or doesn't come back at all, you doubt yourself. You think, *I'm tired, I'm confused, the field's gotten to me.* You police yourself. Hale never had to threaten anyone. He just kept pouring tea."

"How long?" Elodie asked.

"Six weeks. By the end, none of us could have drawn a reliable map of our own childhoods. Except—" Tomas turned his head carefully toward Sable. "Except you. You drank more than any of us. Pell figured it out — he was the one who tested the thermos, hid samples in a flask. The spring water in you fought the tea. Whatever that seep does, it *holds*. You were the control group, Sable. You were the proof the whole project worked. That's why Hale kept you closest. That's why you can't remember arriving anywhere — you've got two chemicals fighting a war inside your skull, and the battlefield is you."

She put her hand flat on the stone, as if checking that the world was still solid. Elodie moved — not quite touching her shoulder, just near it, the way a man stands near a wall in an earthquake.

"What did Hale intend?" Elodie asked. "Exactly. No summaries."

Tomas took a breath and gave it to him straight, because a witness who softens things is a witness wasting everyone's time.

"The survey was the cover. The deliverable was a method. Dosing protocol, dosage tables, a map of every seep and feeder channel feeding the main spring. Hale's report wasn't going to the geological service. It was going to people who liked the idea of a town that could be given tea on Monday and remember nothing by Friday. Strikers. Witnesses. Whole valleys with inconvenient histories." He swallowed. "And the final field test — the one he needed before the report went out — was to wipe the site itself. Wipe *us*. Team drinks the deep-dose blend, wakes with no memory of the Throat, gets extracted as confused survivors of a flood. Tragic accident. Nobody looks for a spring nobody remembers."

"But you ran," Elodie said.

"Pell ran first. Pell stole the sample case — the concentrated extract, Okafor's notes, the dosage tables — and went downriver at night in the survey boat. He was forty-eight years old and he rowed like a man possessed, because he was. He'd been the one who figured out the tea, so he'd been skipping doses for a week, pretending to swallow. When they caught him at the lower gorge—" Tomas closed his eyes. "I'm not certain they caught him. I heard shouting. I heard the boat. Then Hale announced Pell had deserted with government property, and that desertion in the field carries a summary judgment, and we all drank our tea that morning because what else do you do?"

"And you?"

"I ran two nights later. Same route. I got the case as far as the tributary." He gestured vaguely at his own ruined body, the bandage on his head, the fever banked behind his eyes like a fire in a wet stove. "The river did the rest. I remember the water taking the case, and me going after it, because Okafor's notes were in it — her handwriting, her life's work — and I remember thinking, with great clarity, *someone has to be able to prove this happened.* Then the rocks. Then nothing much."

* * *

There was a long silence after that, filled by the cave's slow dripping and the distant argument of the flooded river.

It was Sable who broke it. She'd been quiet through most of the telling, and when she spoke her voice had a cracked, sanded-down quality.

"Tomas. The others. Okafor, the rest of the team — are they still up there?"

"They were, when I left." He met her eyes and made himself not flinch. "Hale moved them to the upper camp. He said they were being 'stabilized' before extraction. But he'd started burning the equipment, and he posted guards on the seep, and I don't think stabilization is the word for what happens next. I think we were always meant to be part of the site. Part of what gets erased."

Sable stood abruptly and walked to the cave mouth, where the gray light took her and made her silhouette into something anonymous. Elodie watched her go, then looked back at Tomas.

"You understand what you're asking," the ranger said. It wasn't a question, quite.

"I'm not asking anything yet," Tomas said. "I'm too tired to ask anything. Ask me tomorrow, when I'm lucid, and I'll tell you where Okafor hid her duplicate notes — yes, there's a duplicate set, the woman trusted no one, bless her paranoid heart. Ask me tomorrow and I'll give you the seep's bearing to the second decimal. Ask me tomorrow." His voice frayed. "But tonight I'm going to ask for one thing, and it's small, and you can say no."

"Say it."

"When I sleep tonight — and I'm going to sleep, the fever sees to that — talk to me. Tell me what day it is. Tell me where we are. Tell me your names, over and over, until I've got them stitched down somewhere the tea can't reach." He managed a shaky grin. "Consider it fieldwork, ranger. You're mapping me. And if I wake up tomorrow and don't know my own thesis title, I want to at least know there's a man named Elodie Ashgrove sitting nearby who owes nobody nothing, and a woman named Sable Vane who hums when she inks."

Elodie was quiet a moment. Then he pulled his bedroll closer to the lamp and settled in like a man planning to be there a while.

"Elodie Ashgrove," he said. "Ranger, fourth district. Currently unemployed by choice. Owes nobody nothing — that part's aspirational."

"Sable Vane," came the voice from the cave mouth, and she came back into the lamplight and sat down on Tomas's other side, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her. "Cartographer. Hummer. Apparently a control group."

"See?" Tomas murmured, sinking. "Stitched down. Both of you. Right next to the blue jar and the dog-shaped crack."

He slept, and the fever took him out onto the flats again, but this time the ground held.

* * *

In his best hour of the following afternoon — clear as glass, sharp as a freshly cut stake — Tomas made them prop him upright against the pack and gave them everything, fast, the way a dying man dictates a will.

Okafor's duplicates: sealed in waxcloth, cached under the third cairn on the bench above the upper camp, marked with a broken arrow scratched into the stone. The seep's true source: higher than anyone guessed, above the flood line, which meant the poison Hale carried couldn't reach all of it — *remember that*, he said, gripping Elodie's wrist with fever-hot fingers, *the mountain keeps some back, the mountain always keeps some back*. Hale's schedule: the main expedition would come downriver within days, with pumps, with drums, with enough chemical to make the whole watershed forget itself.

"And when they come," Tomas said, "you must not be heroes. Heroes get erased. Be witnesses. Be boring. Be *documented*." He laughed, coughed, laughed again. "God, listen to me. Twenty-six years old and giving survival advice from a bed. My father would be so proud. Bearing two-seven-zero, distance four hundred links, Papa."

Then the clarity drained out of him like water through sand, and he slumped, and the fever came back up around him like a rising river.

But at the edge of it, going under, he caught Elodie's sleeve one more time.

"Someone remembers," he said. It wasn't a question either. "Promise me. Not the report. Not the capital. *Someone.* One person, years from now, standing at that seep, knowing what happened here. That's all a witness is for. That's the whole job."

Outside, far up the canyon, a sound rolled down the water — engines, many of them, still distant but no longer hypothetical.

Elodie looked toward it, then back at the sleeping man, and his jaw set in a way Sable had not seen before.

"They're coming," she said.

"Sooner than he thought," Elodie said. "We need to move tonight."
