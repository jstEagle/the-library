## 13. Patience of the Flood

The monsoon did not arrive so much as move in.

That was the thing nobody told you about the wet season in the Throat — that it wasn't weather at all, really, but a tenant. It took up residence in the canyon the way a squatter takes a house: first a few damp belongings left about, then a smell, then a presence you stopped noticing until it was under everything you owned. For eleven days it rained without pause. On the twelfth it rained harder. By the third week I had stopped counting days and started counting other things instead: how many handfuls of meal remained in the sack (nine), how many hours the sea cave's ceiling dripped between drops (fewer each day), how many times Tomas had asked me my name (fourteen, and every time I gave it he nodded like a man confirming a reservation).

The cave sat forty feet above the flood line on the canyon's north wall, wide enough for three people and one dying boat, which we'd hauled up on rollers of driftwood in the last sane hour before the river swallowed the banks. The boat was beyond saving now. The patch Sable had sewn into its belly in the rapids held against calm water and nothing else, and the hull had begun to soften the way old fruit softens — quietly, from somewhere inside, so that pressing your thumb to the planking left a dimple that stayed.

"Your boat is composting," Sable observed one gray morning, standing over it with her arms crossed.

"It's resting."

"It has mushrooms."

I looked. It did. Small pale ones, pushing up through a seam near the stern like questions raised by an audience that had given up on us.

"Well," I said. "At least something down here is thriving."

She almost smiled. She'd been doing that more lately — the almost-smile, the half-turn of her mouth that she caught and corrected before it could commit to anything. I understood the instinct. Committing to joy felt reckless when Tomas lay shivering under both our blankets, his memory going out room by room like a house with a failing generator.

* * *

Tomas had worsened in the way water worsens a wall — not all at once, but in a slow widening of cracks. Some mornings he woke clear-eyed and hungry and full of stories, and those mornings we hoarded like the last of the meal. Other mornings he looked at Sable with polite blankness and asked if she was the doctor, because there had been a doctor, hadn't there, back at the camp? And Sable would say yes, she was the doctor, and give him water, and turn away so he wouldn't see what it cost her to lie.

The cruelest part was the ordering of it. The poison Hale had dumped into the upper seep didn't take memories in the order they were made. It took them in reverse of importance, or some order with no logic I could find. Tomas lost the names of rivers before he lost the name of his mother. He lost the taste of coffee before he lost the face of the woman he'd been engaged to marry — though he kept, stubbornly, the fact of the engagement itself, a wedding ring of pure information with no bride attached to it.

"She liked the coast," he told me once, apropos of nothing, staring at the rain. "I remember that she liked the coast. I don't remember her."

"I know," I said.

"Is that worse or better?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty. "Worse," I said. "Better would be forgetting the liking too. Then it wouldn't sit there being true with nothing attached."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd said something wise, and maybe I had, though it came from a bad place in me — from a man who had spent years sanding himself down to nothing and knew exactly how much of a person you could remove before what remained stopped answering to their name.

* * *

It was during one of Tomas's sleeping stretches — long, merciful sleeps that we encouraged with willow bark tea brewed from the canyon's scrub — that Sable found me teaching myself patience.

There wasn't much else to teach myself. Food was the arithmetic of the hour: nine handfuls of meal became eight, then seven, then a day of nothing but cattail root and the two fish I'd taken from an eddy with a bent pin and a length of line salvaged from the survey gear. The fish were small and muddy-tasting and I was proud of every one of them. Pride, I've found, scales down remarkably well. A man can be proud of a fish the size of his hand if he's hungry enough.

I was working on a snare — desert craft, the kind of thing you learn when the land gives you nothing but thorns and lizards — when Sable settled onto the rock beside me and watched me bend a green branch into a loop.

"What's it for?" she asked.

"Nothing, right now. Everything, later." I tied off the loop. "It's a snare. You set it where an animal has to choose between going around and going through, and you make going through look easier."

"That seems rude."

"It is. That's why it works." I handed her the branch. "Here. Your turn."

She took it the way she took most things — with a cartographer's precision, turning it over, studying the knots. Her fingers were clever. They'd been clever since the first day I pulled her out of the river; whatever else the spring had scrambled in her, it had left her hands fluent.

"Why are you showing me this?" she asked.

"Because in three weeks the flood goes down, and we walk out of here, and walking out takes four days across country with no water in it. If something happens to me, you'll want to know how to find water where there isn't any."

Her head came around. "Something happening to you isn't part of the plan."

"There's always a version of the plan where something happens to me. I've been the something-happens in enough plans to know how they go." I showed her how to read the sand at dawn for the stitched lines of lizard tracks, how to follow a dry wash downhill because water runs down even when it hasn't run in years, how to split a barrel cactus without losing a finger or a friendship. "Desert rule one," I told her. "The land isn't trying to kill you. It just doesn't care. Once you stop taking that personally, you can start learning from it."

"That's terrible advice for people."

"It's excellent advice for people. Half of what makes people miserable is thinking the world's indifferent is aimed at them."

She worked the snare loop in silence for a while. Rain drummed the cave mouth. Somewhere below, the river moved its heavy furniture around — a groan of stone, a slap of debris against the far wall.

"My father taught me stars," she said suddenly. "Not maps. Stars. He said maps lie because someone drew them, but the sky never got a chance to be dishonest."

I looked at her. She was watching the gray ceiling of cloud as if she could see through it.

"He was a ferryman," she went on. "Before the survey. Before—" She waved a hand that took in the spring, the forgetting, the whole drowned architecture of the last months. "He ran night crossings on the Verrow River. Said you could lose the bank and keep the sky. He taught me the navigators' stars before he taught me letters."

"Teach me," I said.

She blinked. "You're a ranger. You know stars."

"I know the ones you use to not get lost. North star, and the two bright ones you draw a line down from. That's survival. That's not navigation." I shrugged. "You said it yourself. Maps drawn by hand lie. I'd like to learn the kind that didn't get a chance to."

* * *

So began the trade.

Every evening the clouds broke for an hour or two — the monsoon breathing out before breathing in again — and we climbed to the shelf above the cave where the sky opened like a wound, black and enormous, dripping light. Sable taught me the navigators' constellations, the ones ferryman fathers pass to daughters: not the pretty named ones from storybooks but the working stars, the honest laborers of the sky. She taught me to find south from a star's swing around the pole, to tell time by which constellation stood over the canyon rim, to hold my fist at arm's length and count hand-widths from horizon to star and know, from that, how many hours of dark remained.

"One fist is an hour," she said, guiding my arm up. "Two fists above the horizon means—"

"Two hours till it sets."

"And if you know when it sets, you know where you are in the night, and if you know where you are in the night, you know when to move and when to sleep." She lowered my arm herself, both hands around my wrist, brief and businesslike and warm. "My father used to say a man who can read the sky is never actually lost. He might be somewhere unfortunate. But he's never lost."

In exchange I taught her the desert's own astronomy — the ground-bound kind. How to read a ridge line for the shadow pockets where water pools after rain. How to tell a flash-flood year from a drought year by the depth of the packrat middens. How to walk in heat (slow, stupid, steady — the desert punishes hurry twice) and how to make a still from plastic sheeting and a tin cup and your own desperation. How to listen to silence, real silence, the kind that means something with teeth has stopped moving because it's watching you.

"You're very good at this," she said one night, after I'd shown her how to find water by following the bees home.

"I'm very good at being alone in bad places. It's the same skill wearing different clothes."

"That's the saddest sentence anyone's ever said to me."

"It's also the truest, so let's call it even."

But she laughed — actually laughed, not the almost-smile — and the sound went up into all that dark air and I thought: well. There's a thing I haven't heard myself cause in a while.

* * *

The food ran low the way rivers run low — not empty, but thinner every day, the current of it weakening until you were scraping the bed. Seven handfuls of meal. Five. Three, stretched with cattail pollen and the inner bark of willow, which tastes like punishment but keeps a body moving.

We divided it with a precision Sable insisted on charting. She drew the ration schedule on a scrap of survey paper — her handwriting, that strange familiar stranger's hand — and pinned it to the cave wall with a fishhook. Portions for Elodie, portions for Sable, double portions for Tomas, marked in her neat cartographic script: *the patient eats first.* I argued once that the healthy should eat first, that starving guards protect nobody, and she looked at me with such flat finality that I let it go. Some battles you lose on purpose because winning them costs more than the ground is worth.

Tomas, in his lucid stretches, watched us trade skills across the fireless cave — we'd stopped burning wood except to boil water; smoke was a signal and signals were invitations — and one evening he said, "You two are getting married, I think."

"We're not," Sable said.

"Sharing food and knowledge. In my village that's most of it." He smiled vaguely at the ceiling. "The rest is arguing about whose family hosts."

"We argue plenty," I offered.

"There," said Tomas, satisfied. "Nearly done, then."

Sable threw a pebble at him, gently, the way you'd toss bread to a bird, and he laughed until he coughed, and we pretended not to worry about the cough.

* * *

On the thirty-first night — I'd started counting again, because the rain had begun to slacken and hope makes a man numerical — the clouds tore open properly for the first time in weeks, and the sky came out in full.

Sable and I climbed to the shelf. The whole wheel of it turned overhead, washed clean, brilliant past decency. The Milky Way ran corner to corner like spilled flour, and the cold stars of the navigators stood in their stations, and below us the flooded Throat reflected it all upside down so that we sat suspended between two skies, one of them moving.

"South-southwest," Sable said, pointing. "See the pair there, low? Tomorrow they'll be a fist higher at dusk. The season's turning."

"The flood follows the season."

"The flood is the season. Give it six days. Maybe five." She hugged her knees, chin down, watching the river glimmer. "When it drops, we walk out. Four days east to the ranger station at Cinder Flats. We take Tomas. We take the charts. We take the sample case." She said it like a route she'd already surveyed, which, knowing her, she had — probably twice, in her head, with elevation profiles.

"And then?" I asked.

"And then we testify. All three of us. Hale answers for the team, for the seep, for Tomas." Her voice didn't shake. It had stopped shaking somewhere in the last weeks, the way a new rope stops shedding. "And then I find out who I am when I'm not missing anything."

I wanted to say something to that. I had a whole speech somewhere in me about identity and choice and how a person is the sum of what they refuse to forget — but speeches are heavy cargo, and I've always traveled light. So instead I said, "One more lesson tonight. Last one."

"Which star?"

"Not a star." I pointed downriver, into the black throat of the gorge. "Listen."

She listened. Wind. Water. The tick of cooling stone.

"Now tell me what's missing."

She frowned, listening harder. The wind shifted. And then I saw her hear it — the absence underneath the sound, the hole in the noise where noise should be.

"Frogs," she whispered. "There were frogs. Every night since the flood. Thousands of them."

"And tonight there aren't."

Frogs go quiet for storms. Frogs go quiet for cold. But frogs go silent and stay silent when something big moves through the water — something that pushes a bow wave and doesn't care who hears it.

We sat very still on our shelf between the two skies, and far down the black throat of the canyon, around the bend where the current slackened, a lantern came into view. Then another. Then the low, patient shape of a barge riding the floodwater, running lights hooded, moving upstream against everything natural — toward the spring.

Sable's hand found my sleeve in the dark.

"They're early," she said.
