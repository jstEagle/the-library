## 14. Hale Comes Downriver

The flood taught Sable Vane a new unit of time. Not hours, not days—*bails*. A bail was the interval between times the water in the boat's belly rose past her boot soles and she had to scoop it out again. In the sea cave where they'd holed up, a bail was roughly forty minutes, which meant her life had been divided into forty-minute portions, like a loaf of bread cut by someone who didn't much care about even slices.

She was on her hundredth-or-so bail when Elodie appeared at the cave mouth with that particular set to his shoulders she'd learned to read as *news, probably bad, possibly interesting*.

"Boats," he said.

"How many?"

"Four. Big ones. They're coming up the main channel, slow, like they own it."

Tomas stirred on his bedroll of folded survey tarps. His fever had broken days ago, but something else had taken its place—a softness behind his eyes, a habit of asking questions he'd already asked. "Are we there yet?" he said, which was his word for everything now. Are we at the spring yet. Are we safe yet. Are we who we were yet.

"Not yet," Sable said gently, as she always did. "Sleep, Tomas."

He slept. He was very good at sleeping lately. It frightened her more than the fever had.

Elodie crouched beside her at the cave lip, below the shelf of rock that hid them from the channel, and passed her the binoculars—the one piece of salvage from the abandoned camp that had proven worth its weight in water. Brass-cased, salt-spotted, with a crack across the left lens that split every image into a clean half and a smeared one. Sable had gotten so used to the crack that clear vision now felt slightly indecent.

Through the good half of the lens, she watched the lead boat come around the bend of the Throat.

It was not a patrol skiff like the ones from before, the ones Elodie had hidden their tracks from with a patience that had awed her—him, the man who'd spent years perfecting the art of leaving no sign, spending that art on keeping *her* signs covered too. This was a barge. Flat-decked, diesel-throated, flying the government survey pennant she knew from her own—apparently her own—charts. On its deck: pumps wrapped in canvas, drums stenciled with hazard diamonds, crates of glassware packed in straw. And men. A dozen at least, in matching gray coveralls, moving with the unhurried confidence of people being paid well to do something terrible.

"They're going for the spring," she said.

"That'd be my guess." Elodie took the binoculars, looked, and handed them back. "Pumps to draw it off. Drums to—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. They'd both read enough of Tomas's fragmented testimony, and of the charts in Sable's own handwriting, to know what went in drums like those.

"Poison," Sable said, because somebody should say it plainly. "He's going to poison the only water in the world that gives people back to themselves."

"And pump what's left into tanks before it kills the source." Elodie's voice stayed level, but she'd learned his levels. This one had a hairline fracture running through it, like the lens. "Hale doesn't want to destroy the spring. He wants a monopoly."

They watched the barge nose toward the wide pool where the spring seeped up through the canyon floor, invisible under the flood except to those who knew—and now, apparently, to those with charts. A second boat began rigging an anchor. A third lowered a gangway. The fourth hung back, engines idling, and Sable understood it was the guard boat, the one whose job was to notice things like two castaways in a sea cave.

"We can't fight twelve men," she said.

"No."

"With what? Good looks and a bail bucket?"

A ghost of a smile crossed him. Even now, especially now, Elodie Ashgrove rationed his smiles like a man crossing a desert with a canteen he didn't trust. "I've survived worse odds with less charm."

"You've survived worse odds by *hiding*, you mean."

He didn't answer, which was an answer. She regretted it immediately—not the truth of it, but the timing. There would be time later, if there was a later, to keep cracking him open like a geode. Right now the flood was rising and the drums were coming ashore and somewhere in her scrambled head was a map she'd drawn herself of exactly this canyon, and she could not remember drawing it.

That was the thing about the spring's gift, she'd come to understand. It gave back, but it never gave back whole. Her memories returned in bursts, like monsoon rain through a torn roof—one moment dry, the next drowning. She remembered Hale's voice saying *seal it* and *manage them*. She remembered the taste of the seep water, cold and mineral-bright, like licking a struck match. She remembered crying without knowing why. The connective tissue between the moments was gone, dissolved, and she was learning to live as a series of vivid fragments strung on a wire of will.

* * *

By afternoon the expedition had made itself at home on the shelf above the spring pool, and Sable had made herself sick watching.

Not from fear. From recognition.

It started small. A man on the barge deck coiling rope with a peculiar overhand flourish she found oddly familiar, the way you find a stranger's laugh familiar until you realize you've heard it at every family dinner of your life. Then a gesture—hand flicking at his ear, brushing away a fly that wasn't there. Then, when he turned to shout an order down the gangway, the set of his jaw, stubborn as a door swollen shut in wet weather.

Her hands were shaking before she let herself raise the binoculars all the way.

The cracked lens split him in two. In the clean half: a young man, mid-twenties, sunburned, gray coveralls too big in the shoulders. In the smeared half: her brother.

*Aldous.*

His name arrived in her mouth whole, unbroken, no fragment about it—the spring's strangest mercy, or cruelest joke, that the memory of Aldous Vane had survived every wiping intact. Older brother. Four years older. The one who'd taught her to swim in a reservoir and to lie to their mother about where they'd swum. The one who wrote letters full of jokes and never once asked how she really was. The one she hadn't seen in three years, since—

Since when? The wire slipped. The fragment ended. But the feeling remained, and the feeling was enormous, and it was grief wearing anger's coat.

"He knows me," she whispered.

Elodie, who had been studying the guard boat's patrol pattern with a ranger's patient arithmetic, turned. "What?"

"The tall one. Rope coil. That's—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish, because Tomas had begged them to make sure someone remembers, and remembering started here, now, with her own name for her own blood. "That's my brother. Aldous Vane. He's on Hale's crew."

Elodie took the binoculars without a word, found the man, studied him for a long moment. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain it's him. I'm not certain he's certain of anything." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "God, Elodie, I don't even know if *he* remembers me. If Hale dosed the crew—if that's what happened to the survey team—maybe Aldous is down there right now with a hole in his week and orders in his pocket and no idea why his hands shake every time he passes the water drums."

"Or maybe he signed on," Elodie said quietly. "People do. For money, for fear, for the belief that following orders is the same as being good."

"Don't." She said it sharper than she meant to. "Don't you dare hand me the comfortable version where I get to hate him cleanly. Let me sit with the other version first."

He sat back on his heels. In the weeks of the flood he'd changed in ways he probably couldn't see—filled out a little on short rations, as if scarcity agreed with him; laughed twice, both times at himself; stopped flinching when she said his name. But he still did this: offered the hard truth flatly, then waited to see if you wanted it taken back. It was, she'd decided, either the most honest or the most cowardly habit she'd ever encountered, and she still hadn't settled which, and she suspected the answer mattered more to her than she wanted it to.

"All right," he said. "Sit with it. I'll watch the boats."

So she sat with it, and it was worse than danger. Danger was simple; danger said run or fight. Betrayal just stood there, holding a coil of rope, looking like home.

Because here was the thing she kept circling: she could not remember whether Aldous had ever known about the spring. Had he been on the original survey? Was that how Hale had recruited him—or was it the reverse, had *Aldous* brought Hale the site, sold his own sister's discovery up the chain? The fragments wouldn't line up. Every path her memory tried to walk ended at the same washed-out bridge. She remembered arguing with someone about ethics in a tent lit by a lantern, and the someone had Aldous's voice, and she could not remember which side of the argument she'd won, or lost, or whether anyone had.

"You're doing the thing," Elodie said.

"What thing?"

"Where your face goes somewhere I can't follow and you stop blinking."

She blinked. "Rude of you to notice."

"It's a useful skill. Noticing." He nodded at the shore. "Your brother just went into the survey tent with Hale. Alone. If you want to know which version he is, that's where the answer's going to be written."

Sable raised the binoculars and watched the tent flap swallow her brother, and thought: *I have spent weeks getting myself back, piece by piece, and now the last piece is down there, and it might be rotten.*

* * *

Evening came down the Throat like a lid closing. The expedition lit lamps along the barge rail, and the light lay gold on the brown floodwater, almost beautiful, the way a trap is almost a shelter. Sable counted sentries—two on the guard boat, one walking the shore shelf, one at the tent. Hale's silhouette moved among them, directing, a man arranging furniture in a house he intended to burn.

Tomas woke at dusk, lucid in the way the evenings sometimes granted him, and asked for the story. It had become ritual: each evening, Sable told him the day's events as though telling a child about a country he'd once visited. Today she told him about the boats, and about the pumps, and left out the part about her brother, because some stories you weren't ready to tell out loud even to yourself.

"There were six of us on the survey," Tomas said suddenly, staring at the cave ceiling. "Six. I keep counting five. Who am I losing?"

Sable's chest tightened. "There's you, and Dr. Imre, and Priya, and Okonkwo, and Ferro, and—"

"And the cartographer." He turned his head and looked at her with eyes gone strangely clear. "You. You were the sixth. I keep forgetting you were there, and that's the worst one to lose, because you're *here*." He smiled, apologetic, heartbreaking. "Tell me tomorrow too. Promise."

"I promise."

Elodie, at the cave mouth on watch, said nothing, but she saw his shoulders do the thing they did when he was carrying something heavy and refusing to put it down.

Later, when Tomas slept, she joined Elodie at the entrance. Below, the lamps burned. Somewhere under that gold-lit water, the spring breathed out its impossible gift, and above it men stacked drums of poison with the tidy efficiency of people who believed the world was a ledger to be balanced.

"I need to go down there," she said.

Elodie didn't look surprised. She suspected nothing about her surprised him anymore, and she'd stopped being sure whether she liked that. "To do what?"

"To see him. To find out which version he is." She hugged her knees. "And because those are our charts in that tent, Elodie. My handwriting. The only proof outside of Tomas's head that any of this happened, and Hale is sitting on it, and tomorrow he'll burn it with the rest."

"Tomorrow," Elodie repeated. He was doing his arithmetic again—she could practically hear the abacus. "The pumps start at first light. Which means tonight, the camp is busy and the sentries are bored and everyone's watching the water, not the dark." He was quiet a moment. "There's a gravel bar fifty yards downstream of their anchor line. Flood debris piles against it. A swimmer could reach it without showing against the current."

"A swimmer," Sable said. "In monsoon water. At night."

"In monsoon water, at night, carrying a knife and a flare." He finally looked at her, and in the dimness his face was unreadable, which she knew by now meant it was very readable indeed. "I was going to suggest I go alone."

"Of course you were."

"But you'd only follow, and you swim better than I do, and you know the camp layout from charts you drew yourself." He exhaled through his nose—not quite a sigh, not quite surrender. "So. Both of us. We cut the mooring lines of the guard boat, drift it into the channel, and while they chase their own boat we get into the tent and get the charts."

"And I find my brother."

"And you find your brother." He paused. "Sable. If he's the version we don't want him to be—"

"Then I'll deal with it."

"If he raises the alarm—"

"He won't." She heard how certain she sounded and marveled at it, because underneath the certainty was nothing but a rope-coil flourish and a hand flicking at a fly that wasn't there. "He's my brother. Whatever else is true, that's true. I'm going to hold onto that until it's either load-bearing or it breaks."

Elodie considered this. "That's either very brave or very foolish."

"I learned it from watching you hide your tracks for a stranger. Seems to be contagious."

Below them, a lamp flickered. A sentry laughed at something. And in the survey tent, silhouetted against the canvas for one unguarded moment, her brother stood talking to Corvus Hale, nodding, nodding, agreeing—and then, just before the shadow swallowed him, he glanced out across the black water toward the sea caves, toward the dark, toward her, as if he could feel her looking.

Sable's heart stopped, started, and refused to settle.

"He saw me," she breathed. "Elodie—he looked right at me."

"Or he looked at the dark," Elodie said, already reaching for the knife. "Either way—we move tonight."
