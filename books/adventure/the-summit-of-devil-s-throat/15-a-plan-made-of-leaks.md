## 15. A Plan Made of Leaks

The boat was dying. That much had been true for a week; what changed on the morning of the ninth day was that it started dying *loudly*.

Elodie woke to a sound like a man clearing his throat underwater—a long, wet gurgle from the bilge—and lay still in the sea cave's grey half-light, listening the way he'd once listened for rattlesnakes under bedrolls. The gurgle came again, then a third time, and then the boat said something no boat should say: a soft, structural sigh, like an old woman settling into a chair she intended never to leave.

"Well," Sable said from across the embers. She was already awake, knees drawn up, chin on them, watching him with the particular patience of someone who had spent weeks learning to read his silences and had gotten annoyingly good at it. "I heard it too."

"Heard what? I didn't hear anything."

"You flinched."

"I was stretching."

"At dawn. In a cave. After you'd been asleep for six hours." She tipped her head toward the boat, beached on the shelf at the cave mouth where they'd hauled it after the rudder went. Water was coming through the seam below the waterline in a thin, steady thread, catching the light like a silver wire. "It's talking."

"Boats don't talk."

"This one does. It's saying goodbye."

Elodie got up, crouched at the hull, and pressed his thumb against the seam. The wood gave under the pressure with a softness that made his stomach drop—the rot had gone past the surface planks and into the frame. He'd known it was bad. He hadn't let himself know *how* bad. There was a trick to surviving out here that he'd practiced for years: hold the facts at arm's length, examine only the ones you can use, and set down the rest before they get heavy. He was getting worse at it. He suspected he knew whose fault that was.

Behind him, Tomas coughed.

The sound pulled both of them around at once. The junior surveyor lay in the driest corner they could make, wrapped in every scrap of canvas they owned, and he was thinner than when they'd pulled him half-drowned from the tributary, though he'd barely had flesh to lose. The poison in the upper seep was working through him slowly, the way silt works through clear water—not dramatic, just steady, taking things. Yesterday he'd asked Sable who she was. This morning, when Elodie checked on him, Tomas smiled up with the polite blankness of a man greeting a stranger at a well.

"Morning," Tomas said. "Are we nearly there?"

"Nearly," Elodie lied, because there was nothing else to say. Nearly where, he couldn't have told you. Tomas didn't seem to need a destination. He seemed to need only the reassurance that there was one, and then he'd close his eyes again, satisfied, and drift back under.

Sable knelt beside him, took his wrist without being asked—she did this now maybe twenty times a day, a small ritual neither of them commented on—and counted his pulse against her own breathing. Then she rose, walked to the cave mouth, and stood looking downriver with her arms crossed, and Elodie knew before she spoke that whatever she was about to say would rearrange his morning entirely.

"They're moving," she said. She handed him the binoculars—salvaged from the abandoned camp, one lens cracked so the world came at you slightly doubled, which Elodie had decided was either a flaw or a philosophy. "Look past the second bend. The big raft."

He looked. Downriver, where the canyon widened into the flooded basin around the spring, Hale's main expedition had anchored four days ago: two pontoon rafts lashed together, a pump rig, generators, tarps, men in survey colors moving with the unhurried confidence of people doing a job they expected to finish. Elodie had counted eleven. Eleven against two and a half—one and a half if you were honest about Tomas, zero-point-five if you were honest about the boat.

This morning the rafts were repositioned. Closer to the spring channel. And on the near raft, half-hidden under tarpaulin, sat shapes Elodie recognized with a cold professional clarity: fuel drums. A crate of signal flares. The pump rig's secondary engine, still crated.

"They're staging for the poisoning run," Sable said. "Upstream seep first, then the main source. Once the flood peaks, everything mixes. Whatever they put in the water will carry through the whole basin before anyone could ever—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "You know what it'll do."

He did. They'd tested the stolen sample enough times to stop pretending they didn't understand. The spring didn't restore memory so much as it *edited* it—dilute, it returned what was lost; concentrated, dosed, controlled, it could take things away, or put other things in their place. Hale hadn't come to map a wonder. He'd come to build a faucet.

"How long?" Elodie asked.

"Before they start? Two days. Maybe three. The flood tops out day after tomorrow—that's when the channels all connect and anything dumped upstream spreads everywhere." She turned to face him fully. In the cave's dimness her eyes were very dark and very steady, and Elodie felt the familiar pull of them, the way you feel a current under apparently calm water. "So. We have two days, one rotten boat, no weapons, and eleven men. I've been doing the arithmetic since dawn. It keeps coming out badly."

"It comes out badly every way except one," Elodie said.

And then he stopped, because he hadn't actually known he was going to say that. The sentence had been sitting in him somewhere below thought, the way the plan itself had been sitting in him, assembling itself out of pieces he'd been staring at for days without seeing: the leaking boat, the fuel drums, the flares, the flood, the mooring lines he'd watched Hale's crew tie off with the lazy overconfidence of men who'd never had anything come at them out of the dark.

Sable waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the many things about her that had gone from infuriating to essential without asking his permission.

"The boat," he said slowly, working it aloud, testing each piece as it left his mouth. "She's done as a boat. But she's not empty. We've got the reserve fuel canister from the survey stores—four gallons, maybe five. Flare locker's still half full. And she's got a hull that wants to sink, which right now is the most useful thing about her."

He crouched and drew in the sand with a stick: the basin, the bend, the moored rafts, the current running green and fast between them.

"Flood's peaking. Current through the mooring channel will be running hard—hard enough that anything loose goes exactly where the water says. So we let the water say it." He dragged the stick along the channel line, up from their shelf, down toward the rafts. "We load her with the fuel cans, strap the flare crate amidships, pack the whole bilge with anything that burns, and we give her a hole big enough to guarantee she goes down—but slow. Slow enough to reach them. No one aboard. We send her down alone, riding the current straight into Hale's moorings, and when she gets there, she opens up. Fuel on the water. Flares cook off. And their pontoons, their pumps, their generator, their nice dry tarps are sitting ten feet away, tied fast, unable to move."

Silence. Somewhere above them, water dripped from the cave ceiling onto stone, patient as a clock.

"That's not a plan," Sable said. "That's an arson."

"It's a plan *made* of arson."

"It's a plan made of leaks," she corrected, and despite everything—a dying man behind them, a brother downriver among enemies, a canyon full of poisoned water—a smile flickered across her face, quick and unwilling, like a fish rising. "You want to float a bomb at eleven armed men and hope they're too stupid to cut a rope."

"I'm hoping they're too busy to notice one more piece of debris. Every river in this canyon is full of drifting junk right now—branches, drums, dead stock. One more waterlogged boat coming down on a flood crest won't look like anything until it's too late to look like anything else." He turned the stick in his fingers. "It doesn't have to destroy them. It has to break their moorings and burn their fuel. After that they're not an expedition anymore. They're eleven wet men in a flooded canyon, and wet men in a flooded canyon start making mistakes."

"And us?"

"We're downstream of the mistake. In the water, in the dark, away from the light of their fire. By the time anyone thinks to look for whoever sent the boat, we're a mile gone."

Sable looked at the drawing in the sand for a long moment. Then she said, in the tone of someone setting down a card she'd been holding since dawn:

"I'm going with it."

Elodie closed his eyes briefly. "No."

"Yes."

"Sable—"

"There's someone on that raft." Her voice didn't rise. That was the thing about her anger and her grief; they never shouted. They just got denser, like water going deep. "My brother is on that raft, Elodie. He's been down there for days, helping that man pump poison into the only thing in the world that ever gave me back myself. And I have spent every one of those days telling myself that when the moment comes, I'll think of some clever way to reach him from a distance. Some plan where nobody has to stand in front of anybody and say the hard thing." She shook her head. "There isn't one. There's never one. You taught me that—you're the one who keeps saying distance is a lie trackers tell themselves. Well. I'm done lying. When that boat hits their lines and everything comes apart, I'm going to be in the middle of it, and I'm going to find Merric, and I'm going to look him in the face while the world burns around us, and one of us is going to remember who we used to be. Even if it's only me."

"Merric," Elodie repeated. It was the first time she'd said the name aloud. She'd only ever called him *my brother*, as if naming him might spend something she couldn't afford. Hearing it now, it landed in the cave like a dropped tool.

"Merric Vane," she said. "Two years older than me. Taught me to swim in a stock tank. Cried at my wedding, and I cried at his, and then our parents died and he went one way and I went another, and the last time I saw him before all this he told me I was wasting my life on maps of places nobody goes." Her jaw tightened. "He wasn't wrong about the places. He was wrong about the waste."

Elodie looked at her—at this woman who had washed into his life in a leaking boat with no past she could finish, who had rebuilt herself out of her own handwriting and a stranger's campfire, who was now asking to walk into fire to save a man who might turn her in—and felt something in his chest do a thing he didn't have a name for. It wasn't quite fear and it wasn't quite admiration. It was closer to recognition. Here was someone else practicing, in her own way, the same hard craft he'd been practicing for years: the craft of deciding who you were on purpose instead of by accident.

"If he gives you up," Elodie said quietly, "we lose everything. The evidence, the recording gear, the testimony—all of it's tied to us staying free until the authorities come up-canyon."

"I know."

"If he hesitates even a breath—"

"I know." She met his eyes. "Do you want to know why I'm sure? Because he wavered. On the patrol boat, the day they swept the canyon—he was at the rail with the searchlight, and it passed over our channel twice, and both times it slowed. Both times, Elodie. Men don't slow a searchlight over nothing twice unless something in them is arguing." She exhaled. "He's already halfway to turning. All I have to do is arrive at the exact moment he finishes."

"And if he doesn't finish?"

"Then I'll finish for him." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone to the survey knife at her belt, and Elodie chose—with wisdom hard-won—to interpret that as resolve rather than threat.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the work had a strange holiness to it, the way last things often do.

They stripped the boat of everything that mattered: the patched sail, the bail bucket, the food tin, the tools, the coil of rope Elodie had braided and repaired so many times it was mostly his own handiwork holding hands with itself. What remained was cargo. Five fuel cans, lashed low and centered. The flare crate, waterproofed under tarp, wired shut. The lamp oil. The pitch pot. Everything that burned, packed into a hull that leaked, weighted to ride low and stable, rigged with a slow-opening seam below the waterline that Elodie cut and plugged and re-cut three times, muttering calculations about current speed and burn time that Sable eventually took over because, as she pointed out, she was the cartographer and hydrology was just maps that moved.

"Current through the mooring channel at peak flow," she said, chalk-scratching numbers on a board they'd salvaged, "call it eight feet a second. Distance from the bend to their anchor point, six hundred yards. So your fuse"—she tapped the plug—"has to fail somewhere between seventy and ninety seconds after release. Any sooner and she sinks mid-channel, harmless. Any later and she drifts past them and blows up a rock formation, which would be very dramatic and accomplish nothing."

"So seventy seconds."

"Seventy-five. Give the current its due." She glanced up. "You realize we're building the most complicated way possible to say hello to eleven men."

"I've always believed in making an entrance."

"You hid from a helicopter for six hours last month."

"Different circumstances."

Tomas watched them from his corner, drifting in and out. At one point, surfacing, he squinted at the loaded boat and said, with sudden terrible lucidity: "That's how I came downriver. In a boat like that. Hale said it was a supply run." His eyes found Elodie's, and for a moment the fog lifted entirely and Elodie saw the young man underneath, sharp and scared and carrying something heavy. "You're going to make them remember. You promised."

"I promised," Elodie said.

"Good." Tomas lay back. "Because I'm starting to forget it for both of us."

Nobody spoke for a while after that.

* * *

Night came down the canyon like a lid. Upstream, at Hale's anchorage, lanterns bloomed—warm yellow points strung along the rafts, and beyond them the bigger glow of a generator lamp, throwing long shadows of men at work. Pumping. Preparing. Elodie watched through the cracked binoculars and counted again: eleven, minus one asleep on watch he shouldn't be sleeping, plus one figure standing apart at the stern of the far raft, not working, just looking upriver. Tall. Shoulders Sable would have known anywhere.

He lowered the glasses and found her beside him. She'd dressed for the water—every layer she owned, knife at her belt, hair bound back, the small waterproof packet of charts and evidence strapped tight against her ribs. She looked, Elodie thought, like exactly what she was: a person about to do something enormous and pretending it was ordinary.

"Last chance to tell me it's a bad plan," she said.

"It's a terrible plan."

"Good. I'd worry about us otherwise." She was quiet a moment. "When I go over the side, don't follow. Your job is the boat. Let her run true, and then get clear and stay clear. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Liar."

"Completely."

She laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, echoing off the cave wall—and then pressed her forehead briefly against his shoulder, the way you'd touch a doorpost leaving home. Elodie stood very still and let it happen, and understood that he was memorizing it, the weight and warmth of it, the way he'd once memorized escape routes.

"One more thing," he said. "If it goes wrong—if the boat sinks early, if they catch you, if any of the forty ways this dies happens—remember that the evidence matters more than either of us. Get the charts out. Get yourself out. Someone has to testify."

"I know."

"And Sable." He paused. The words he wanted were large and he was a man who had spent a lifetime packing light. "Whatever happens down there. Whoever your brother turns out to be tonight. You're not the version of yourself anyone tried to write. You're the one holding the pen. Don't forget it in the dark."

She looked up at him, and in the lantern-glow from far upstream her eyes were bright and absolutely certain, and she said, "Fifty seconds after you cut her loose, start counting my strokes. I'll see you on the water."

Below them, the flood roared through the dark like a freight train that had lost its track, and the little boat sat loaded and leaking at the shelf's edge, patient as a held breath, waiting to become a weapon.

Elodie put his knife to the mooring line.

"Seventy-five seconds," he said. "Give the current its due."
