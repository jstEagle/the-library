## 16. Night Run

*Elodie*

The boat was dying anyway. Elodie had known it for three days, had watched the hull drink the river through its seams and give it back in a slow, apologetic weep that no amount of pitch could shame into stopping. So when he laid out the plan — *we let her go, we let her go straight at them* — Sable looked at him the way a person looks at a doctor who has just suggested amputating something that was already gone.

"You want to turn our lifeboat into a bomb," she said.

"I want to turn a sinking boat into a bomb. It's a promotion."

Tomas, propped against the cave wall with his blanket around his shoulders like a man attending his own funeral and finding it dull, laughed until it turned into coughing. "He's got you there."

They spent the afternoon preparing, which mostly meant lying to themselves about how much time they had. The fuel they'd salvaged from Hale's abandoned supply cache went into every container they owned — jerricans, water bottles, a tin that had once held peaches and now held their futures. The flare stores went in last, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged amidships where the fire would find them fastest. Elodie worked with the calm of a man who had done dangerous things alone for years and was only now discovering how different it felt to do them with witnesses.

"Say it back to me," he told Sable.

"The boat drifts on the flood current at slack of night. We ride behind it in the raft. When it touches their moorings, we cut the lines, the chaos does the rest." She paused. "And I go ashore first, because my brother is on that bank, and I'm not watching him burn from a safe distance."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what I heard."

He should have argued. He knew all the arguments; he'd built most of them himself over the years, load-bearing walls of caution, a whole architecture of staying alive by staying away. Instead he found himself looking at her face in the cave's gray light and understanding that some arguments are just doors, and she had already walked through this one.

"Stay low," he said. "Stay near the water. And if it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"—if it goes wrong, you swim downstream and don't look back."

"No," Sable said pleasantly, checking the knot on her pack strap. "I really won't."

*Tomas caught Elodie's arm as they loaded the raft.* His voice came out thin but level, the way it did now, rationing itself. "The case," he said. "The sample case. If the fire gets near the spring water—" He stopped, and Elodie saw him wrestle with the thought, saw him lose. "It doesn't matter. Burn it. Burn all of it. Better nothing than that in anyone's hands."

"Nothing," Elodie agreed, "is looking like one of the better outcomes lately."

Tomas smiled, and for a second he was the junior surveyor again, the man who'd run downriver clutching stolen truth like a life vest. "Make it loud," he said.

* * *

*Sable*

The river at night was a black animal breathing under them. Sable lay flat in the raft beside Elodie and watched their doomed boat drift ahead, riding the flood current with the eerie grace of something that didn't know it was dead yet. A single lantern burned aboard it — her idea, so the sentries would see a boat, a crew, a target worth shooting at instead of asking questions about.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere below the steadiness, grief sat doing quiet arithmetic: *my brother is down there, my brother helped them, my brother is going to watch me come out of the dark and he will either raise the alarm or he won't.*

She'd rehearsed both endings all day. Neither rehearsal had prepared her for the actual moment, forty minutes later, when the drifting boat kissed the mooring line of Hale's lead vessel with a sound like a knuckle rapped on a door, and the camp woke up shouting, and she slipped out of the shallows onto the bank ten yards from a figure holding a lantern.

Merrick Vane had gotten taller since she'd last seen him. Or she'd forgotten. Memory was an unreliable country these days; she tried not to hold it against him.

"Merrick," she said.

He turned. The lantern shook. For three full seconds neither of them moved, and the camp roared around them — men running toward the waterline, someone screaming about fuel, someone else screaming about fire — and her brother stood frozen in the middle of it like a man who had stepped out of time.

"Sable." Her name came out of him cracked down the middle. "They said — Corvus said you were dead. He said the river took you."

"The river doesn't take people, Merrick. People put them in the river." She kept her voice low, kept walking forward, one step, two. "You were on the survey. You drank from the seep same as me. Do you remember? Do you remember any of it?"

His face did something terrible then — a flicker, a reaching, a hand closing on smoke. "There was a canyon," he whispered. "There was — Sable, there was a map, I remember your hands drawing a map, and then it's just — it's fog, it's all fog, and Hale says the fog is normal, Hale says—"

"Hale is going to poison the only thing in this desert that could give you back everything he took." She was close enough now to touch him. She didn't. Touching him would end her. "You're standing guard over the man who erased you. Think about that. Really think."

Behind him, at the moorings, the world caught fire.

It started as a bloom of orange light on the water — the drifting boat finally surrendering, or finally arriving, depending on how you counted — and then the flare stores went, and the sound arrived a half-second late like thunder remembering its job. The shockwave shoved Sable sideways into her brother, actually into him, his arms coming up around her out of pure older-brother reflex older than memory, older than betrayal, and for one heartbeat they were just two kids in a storm again.

Then he pushed her back and looked at her over the flames, and she watched the war happen behind his eyes.

"Go," he said.

"Merrick—"

"*Go.*" His voice broke on it. He turned his face away from her, toward the burning moorings, toward the men shouting Hale's name, and when he spoke again it was barely air. "I didn't see you. Whatever happens next — I never saw you. But Sable—" He looked back once, and his eyes were wet and furious and young. "Come back for me. After. Come back and tell me who I was."

She ran. There was no other answer she could give him that wasn't a lie, and she had sworn off those.

* * *

*Elodie*

From the raft, mid-river, the explosion was almost beautiful — which Elodie noted with professional detachment and immediately distrusted, because beautiful things in his experience were usually the expensive part. The pumps were the prize: Hale had anchored a pumping rig at the spring's lower outlet, a scaffold of pipe and canvas that now listed hard to starboard, one leg folded, steam and black water geysering from a ruptured joint. Men swarmed it with buckets. Buckets. Against a fuel fire on open water.

"Amateurs," Elodie murmured, with genuine feeling.

Sable hit the water beside the raft a minute later, gasping, and hauled herself aboard, and he pulled her in without a word because some things don't need narrating. She was shaking. He pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"Well?" she managed.

"Pumps are done. Rig's crippled. They'll be days repairing it, and by then—" He glanced upriver, where the flood still rose, patient as arithmetic. "By then the Throat finishes the job for us."

"And Hale?"

Elodie raised the binoculars. On the far bank, apart from the chaos, a tall figure stood perfectly still, facing the water, facing *them*, though there was no way he could see the raft in the dark. Even at this distance, even across firelight and river-noise, Elodie felt the weight of being looked at — a sensation so foreign to him that it took a moment to identify. He had spent nine years making himself unnoticeable. Corvus Hale found him anyway, across half a mile of burning water, the way a magnet finds iron filings.

"He knows," Elodie said quietly.

"Knows what?"

"That it was us. That it was *me*." He lowered the binoculars. "Nobody's looked at me like that since before I can remember wanting them to."

Sable took the binoculars, found the figure, and went very still. "Then we'd better be worth looking at," she said.

They paddled hard for the upstream channel, the fire shrinking behind them, and the last thing Elodie saw of Hale's camp was his own reflection in it — two small figures in a raft, unmistakably, deliberately, on the map at last.

Behind them, faint over the water, a bell began to ring. Not an alarm.

A summons.

Hale was calling his men together — and turning upriver.
