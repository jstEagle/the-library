## 17. The Spring Runs Red

The flood gave us three days of grace, and we spent them like misers.

We went upstream in the patched boat, Tomas lashed amidships under a tarp I'd rigged from the survey camp's groundsheet, Sable at the bow reading the water the way she read her own charts—which was to say, better than anyone had a right to. The Throat was draining itself in slow stages, the red walls sweating, the current dropping an inch an hour and leaving tidelines of silt on the stone like rings on a bath tub. Somewhere behind us, downstream, Hale's expedition was burning or sinking or both. We'd heard nothing since the explosion except one long helicopter pass at dawn that hadn't stopped, and I'd decided—provisionally, the way I decided everything—to take that as good news.

"Left channel," Sable said. "There's a snag."

I saw it. A dead cottonwood, root ball the size of a wagon, turning slowly in the eddy like something deciding whether to commit. I swung us right, and the boat scraped gravel, and we were through.

"You know," she said, "for a man who spent six years making sure no map ever contained him, you're awfully good at reading them."

"I contain multitudes," I said. "Mostly sand."

Tomas laughed from under the tarp—a thin, cracked sound, but a laugh. He'd been doing more of that since the night run, riding the adrenaline like a wave, asking questions about the explosion with the bright-eyed hunger of a man who'd been very quiet for a very long time and had finally found his voice. *Did it really blow the pumps? Did you really cut the lines with a fish knife?* Sable answered most of them. She was better at it than me. She could tell a story without checking over her shoulder halfway through.

We made camp that first evening on a gravel bar below the tributary fork, and I let myself feel something dangerously close to optimism. The poison drums were gone—we'd seen their cradles empty on Hale's barge before it burned. The pumps were scrap. The confession was recorded, broadcast, and sitting in the ranger network's queue like a letter with our names on it. All we had to do now was keep Tomas alive, keep ourselves fed, and wait for the water to drop enough for the authorities to come upriver and do their slow, official work.

I have since learned never to trust optimism in Devil's Throat. It is a basin built on the principle that everything good comes with a bill attached.

* * *

We found the seep on the second day.

It was where Sable's recovered memory said it would be—a fracture in the north wall, forty feet above the dropping floodline, seeping through a lip of travertine into a basin no bigger than a wash bucket. The spring's upper vein. In her memory of it, the water there ran clear and cold and tasted faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath, like honey left in a tin. It was the water that had given her back her week. It was the water Hale had wanted to own entire.

We climbed to it because Tomas asked.

He'd been restless all morning, pacing the gravel bar, and when he wasn't pacing he was standing very still with his brow furrowed, which was worse. He'd look at Sable and I could see him reaching for something—the name of the survey camp, the route they'd taken downriver—and watch it slide away from him like a fish in shallow water. He covered it well. Junior surveyors are trained to cover things; it's half the job. But I've spent years watching people hide what they've lost, and I knew the posture.

"The seep," he said at breakfast, pointing up the wall with a confidence he didn't quite own. "That's where we sampled first. Before the main pool. I remember—" He stopped. Started again. "I remember being told I remembered."

Sable and I looked at each other across the cookfire, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: that *being told you remembered* was not the same as remembering, and that a man dosed and wiped by Corvus Hale might have been handed his memories back like counterfeit coins.

But he wanted to see it. And seeing it seemed harmless. The seep was above the flood line, upstream of everything Hale had touched—or so we believed. So we climbed, all three of us, Sable going first because she'd mapped this wall in another life, me last because someone had to carry the rope and the water skins and the general weight of other people's hope.

The seep was running red.

Not blood-red. Not dramatic. Just a faint rust stain down the travertine, a discoloration in the moss, and a smell—I caught it ten feet out—that I knew from old range work. Copper salts. Mining runoff. Except there was no mine within two hundred miles, and this stain was fresh, days old at most, laid down by a careful hand with a careful jug.

Hale's failsafe. His spiteful little insurance policy, planted before the night run, before the explosion, maybe before he'd even come downriver at all. A man like Hale doesn't build one plan. He builds a plan, and then a smaller meaner plan inside it, like a nest of boxes, and the innermost box is always just cruelty, plain and simple. If he couldn't own the spring, no one would drink from it clean again. Not the government. Not history. Not us.

"Oh," Sable said. Very quietly. That was all. Just *oh*, in the voice of a woman watching a door close.

Tomas was already kneeling at the basin.

"Tomas." My voice came out harder than I meant it to. "Don't."

But he'd already cupped his hands. He'd already drunk. Of course he had—he was thirsty, we were all thirsty, and the water looked like water, clear as glass over the stained stone, and why would a junior surveyor who'd lost a week of his life suspect the one spring in the world that gave memory back? Why would any of us? We'd climbed to it believing exactly that.

He drank twice, three swallows, before I got a hand on his collar and hauled him back. He fought me for a second—an honest, startled second—and then went limp and stared at the basin, and then at his own wet hands, and I watched the understanding arrive in his face the way weather arrives over hardpan: slowly, and then all at once.

"That's not—" he said. "That's not how it tasted. Before. It tasted sweet. This tastes like—" He licked his lips and his face changed. "*Coins.*"

"It's poisoned," Sable said. "Seepage compound. Copper salts, probably, and something else. Something designed to—" She stopped herself. None of us wanted to say the word *overwrite* out loud next to a man who'd already swallowed it.

"How much?" Tomas asked. His voice was very level. Surveyor-level. The kind of level you learn for reporting instrument errors. "How much did I just drink?"

"Three swallows," I said.

"And the dose that wiped the whole team was—" He looked at Sable.

"One cup," she said. "Over a week, in the coffee."

Tomas sat down on the travertine very carefully, the way a man sits when he doesn't trust his knees, and looked out at the canyon, and said nothing for a long time. The water dripped. Somewhere below us the river muttered over its gravel. I stood there holding a rope nobody needed anymore and felt the rage come up my throat like heat off rock—not the hot kind, the useful kind, but the cold kind, the kind with nowhere to go. Hale was ash somewhere downstream or a prisoner-in-waiting, and it didn't matter, because the man had already won this particular round from a hundred miles away and weeks ago, with a jug and a ladder and five minutes of spite.

You can track a thief across hardpan. You can track a lie through a bureaucracy. But some losses don't leave tracks at all. They just drain quietly out of a person while everyone watches, and there is nothing on earth you can do about it.

* * *

We got him back to the boat and got water into him—clean water, from the skins, though I confess I watched every swallow after that like a hawk watching a field mouse—and by afternoon he was himself again. Mostly. He told us about his sister in Cinder Flats, whom he'd mentioned before, and got the name right, and only hesitated once.

It was the hesitation that scared me. Not the forgetting. The *hesitation*—the small pause before a fact that should arrive without tolls, like a road with a new gate on it.

By evening it was worse, and by morning it was different, and different was worse than worse.

He woke us at gray light, sitting bolt upright in the boat, and said, "Where's the survey director? I need to file my report with Director—" and then his face went smooth as sanded wood, and he said, "Who runs the survey?" and Sable said, "Corvus Hale," and he nodded, satisfied, and lay back down. As if the question had been routine. As if the answer hadn't been the name of the man who'd dosed him, erased him, and hunted him down a canyon.

"He remembers Hale fine," Sable whispered to me over the cookfire. Her hands were shaking around the tin cup. "He remembers the wrong version. The version before everything. The spring doesn't just take the new memories—it *rewrites backward*. It's filing him down toward childhood."

"Can we stop it?"

"I don't know. Water dilutes. Time—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Elodie, I drank from the clean seep and got a week back. One cup, one week. He drank three swallows of the *poisoned* seep, and the poison isn't just erasing, it's—it's active. It's still working. Whatever Hale mixed in, it keeps dissolving him."

That day Tomas forgot the name of the boat. Not a tragedy; we didn't have a name for it. But he walked its length twice looking for something, and when Sable asked what, he said, "My kit. I had a kit," and she said, "It's here, Tomas, everything's here," and he looked at the neat stack of salvaged gear—his own instruments among them, his own handwriting on the labels—and said, politely, "This isn't mine," and smiled at her the way you smile at a kind stranger.

Gentle humor has always been my armor, and God knows I reached for it that week. I told him the boat was named the *Improbable Circumstance*, and he laughed, and for an hour it held. I taught him knots. Knots live in the hands, deeper than the head, and his fingers remembered the bowline even when his mouth couldn't find the word for it. "Muscle memory," I said. "Cheapest real estate in the body. Nobody's figured out how to poison it yet." He grinned at that, and tied the knot again, faster, showing off, and for that hour he was a twenty-six-year-old junior surveyor with a future.

Then he'd surface from a nap and ask me if I was the ranger sent to find him, and I'd say yes, and he'd say, "Good. Good. There's something you need to know about the spring," and begin telling me, with tremendous earnestness, the exact story he'd already told me four times—each time a little shorter, a little smoother, the rough edges of lived experience worn down into a polished anecdote. By the fifth telling he no longer mentioned the fear. By the seventh he no longer mentioned the escape. He told it like a brochure.

Sable took it hardest. She sat with him for hours, prompting him—*the tributary fork, the second camp, the night you ran*—writing down everything he still had, building a ledger of Tomas the way she'd build a chart, fixing positions before they drifted. Once he caught her writing and read it upside down and said, wonderingly, "Is this me?" and she said, "Yes," and he said, "It's a good likeness," and turned away to look at the river, and she put her face in her hands where he couldn't see.

I did what I could, which was the desert thing: I kept the fire, kept the water, kept the count of days. And I wrote too, in my ugly ranger shorthand, because somebody had to hold the version with the fear in it. Somebody had to be the deep archive. I thought about my father's compass, riding in my pocket all these years like a debt with a needle, and I thought: *this is what it's actually for. Not finding north. Finding your way back to a thing you refuse to lose.*

On the fourth day after the seep, Tomas forgot his sister's name entirely.

He didn't notice. That was the worst part. He talked about her happily—"my sister, back in town, she'll want to hear about all this"—and when Sable gently asked her name, he paused, that new gate swinging shut on an empty road, and said, "It'll come to me," and smiled, and moved on to describing her garden, which he described beautifully, in detail, a garden that may or may not have existed, grown entirely from whatever the poison had missed.

Sable walked to the far end of the gravel bar and stood there a long time. I went and stood beside her, because that's mostly what comfort is in the desert: standing beside someone where the wind can't get between you.

"We can't fix this," she said. "Can we."

"No," I said. Because lying to her would have been its own kind of poison. "Some losses don't leave tracks. You can't follow them home."

She was quiet. Then: "When he's done forgetting—if he forgets everything—what's left? Who do we deliver to his sister? A stranger with his face?"

"A man who was brave," I said. "That part seems to stick. Everything else drains out, but the brave stays. I've seen it go the other way too—men who lose everything else and the cowardice stays. It's the one thing the water can't seem to touch."

She almost smiled. "That's a terrible theory."

"It's the only one I've got."

Downstream, faint on the wind, came a sound we'd been waiting three days to hear and dreading anyway: an engine, low and steady, working upriver against the current. Not Hale's barge—that was scrap. Something official. The authorities, coming at last to do their slow paperwork over our ruins.

And Tomas, hearing it too, stood up on the gravel bar and shaded his eyes and said, with the bright relief of a man greeting a rescue he'd forgotten he needed:

"There's the survey launch! Director Hale must have sent them. I should report in."

Sable looked at me. I looked at Sable. And the engine sound grew closer, and the question of what we were going to tell the authorities about the man standing on the bar—who was, at that moment, waving cheerfully at the people sent to arrest his tormentor—became suddenly, urgently ours to answer.
