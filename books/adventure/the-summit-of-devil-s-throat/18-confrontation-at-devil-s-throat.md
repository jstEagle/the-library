## 18. Confrontation at Devil's Throat

**Elodie**

The canyon head was a dead end wearing a pretty face. Sheer walls funneled down to a slot barely wider than the boat, and behind it the flood had been climbing all morning—brown water chewing at ledges that had been dry stone since before either of them was born.

"They'll come up here," Elodie said. He crouched at the lip of a granite shelf, watching the far bend where Hale's boats would appear. "It's the only high ground left. They have no choice."

"And we do?" Sable lay beside him, binoculars pressed to her eyes, her braid stiff with dried mud. "Because from where I'm sitting, our choices are 'cornered' and 'cornered with a view.'"

"That's two more than they'll have." He checked the flare stores one last time. Three flares, a leaking fuel drum lashed to a rock, and Tomas's sample case repurposed as a toolbox. It wasn't an arsenal. It was an argument.

Sable lowered the binoculars. "You're smiling. Why are you smiling? Nothing about this is smile-shaped."

"First time in eleven years I've planned to be seen on purpose." He shrugged. "Feels like trying on boots that actually fit."

She looked at him for a long moment, then shook her head, but she was almost smiling too. "You're a strange man, Elodie Ashgrove."

"I'm not sure I'm anyone, strictly speaking. That's rather the point of today."

The first boat came around the bend at midday.

* * *

**Sable**

There were six of them, and her brother was third in line, rifle slung, eyes fixed anywhere but the water. Sable watched him through the glass and felt the old wound open again—not betrayal this time, exactly. Something more tired. He looked like a man doing arithmetic he hated: six against three, orders against conscience, and the flood rising under all of it.

Hale stood in the bow of the lead boat in his immaculate survey jacket, the beacon case strapped beside him like a pet. Even now, even here, he looked like a man about to give a lecture.

They beached below the shelf. Hale's voice carried off the canyon walls, pleasant as a weather report. "Vane! Ashgrove! I know you're up there. Come down and we'll discuss severance packages."

"He thinks we're stupid," Elodie murmured.

"He thinks everyone is stupid. It's his whole personality."

"VANE." A pause. "I have your brother. That should interest you."

"It doesn't," Sable called back, and was surprised by how steady it came out. "He made his choice. So did I."

Her brother's head came up. Across the water, across forty yards of brown flood, their eyes met, and he didn't deny it, and he didn't raise his rifle either. He just stood there, a man caught between two sisters—the blood one and the truth one—and for the first time since she'd seen his face through the binoculars, he looked like he might cry.

Elodie touched her elbow. "Beacon," he breathed. "That's the play. His own equipment. He brought it to scrub our signals—we turn it around and broadcast everything instead."

"The ranger network won't reach this deep."

"It reaches the relay on Cinder Flats ridge. And Cinder Flats has ears everywhere." He grinned. "Including, I happen to know, a ranger named Odile who owes me a favor and hates Hale's department with the passion of a woman who's read his expense reports."

"You've been planning this since the cave."

"I've been planning things since the cave. Mostly bail schedules and soup. But yes. This too."

Below, Hale's men were spreading out along the shelf, and the water kept rising, patient as a creditor.

* * *

**Elodie**

The trap worked because Hale was smart, and smart men assume traps are beneath them.

Elodie let them advance up the shelf until the flood had sealed the slot behind them—until retreat meant swimming in water moving fast enough to strip paint. Then he stepped out from behind the granite with the flare gun held loose and friendly, like a man greeting neighbors.

"Afternoon," he said. "Terrible weather we're having."

Hale stopped. To his credit, it took him only a heartbeat to read the geometry—the rising water, the fuel drum lashed above the channel, the small figure of Sable Vane already crouched at the abandoned survey station with its recording equipment uncovered, cables in her hands like a surgeon deciding where to cut.

"Ashgrove." Hale's voice stayed pleasant. "I did wonder what happened to you. The man with no records. Do you know how difficult you've been to threaten? You can't fire someone who doesn't exist. You can't ruin a man with no name."

"Funny thing about that," Elodie said. "Turns out being no one means there's nothing left to take. Very liberating. You should try it."

"I don't think so." Hale glanced at the water, calculating. Elodie could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes: six rifles against one flare gun, high ground against higher ground, and time—time on the side of the flood, which answered to neither of them. "Whatever you think you're doing with that equipment, Miss Vane, no signal leaves this canyon. That's rather why I brought the beacon."

"Oh, I know," Sable called down, cheerful as anything. "That's why I'm using *yours*. Corvus, sweetheart, you really should read your own manuals. The beacon doesn't just jam. It rebroadcasts on every emergency frequency in the region when you flip the auxiliary switch. Page forty-one. You wrote the manual."

For the first time, something moved behind Hale's composure. Not fear yet. Annoyance. The look of a man discovering a typo in his own masterpiece.

"There is no auxiliary switch."

"There absolutely is. Tomas found it." Her voice cracked slightly on Tomas's name, and Elodie saw her steady herself. "He found a lot of things. He recorded a lot of things. Would you like to hear what your junior surveyor has to say about what you did to his team?"

Hale's hand drifted toward his sidearm, casual as adjusting a cuff.

And that was when Elodie fired the flare—not at Hale, but at the fuel drum.

* * *

**Sable**

Later, she would remember it in pieces: the orange bloom against gray stone; the drum splitting with a sound like the world clearing its throat; burning fuel sheeting out across the flood in a widening ring of fire that turned the whole canyon amber. Not an explosion—Elodie had measured it too carefully for that—but a wall of flame between Hale's men and the boats, between them and any thought of shooting first.

"Hold!" someone shouted, and it might have been her brother, and the rifles wavered.

In the chaos, Sable flipped the switch.

The survey equipment hummed, caught, and began to speak. Tomas's voice, thin and delirious and utterly clear, poured out of the little speaker into the canyon air: *"Day nine. Director Hale administered the compound without consent. Marta forgot her daughter's name. Okafor forgot he could swim. He calls it asset management. He calls us assets. If anyone hears this—"*

"Shut it off," Hale said. Quietly. Which was worse than shouting.

"No," said Sable.

"Miss Vane." He started up the slope toward her, and his men, God help them, actually hesitated—because the recording was still talking, because the fire was still burning, because somewhere above them all a beacon was singing their crimes into the sky on every frequency a government owned. "You are a woman with no verifiable past. Whatever you claim to remember, no one can confirm it. You are testimony without a witness."

"I'm my own witness," Sable said. "That's the entire point of me."

He lunged for the equipment, and Elodie hit him from the side like a landslide with opinions.

* * *

**Elodie**

They went down the rocks together, Hale's elbow catching Elodie's ear, the world ringing white. They crashed through shallow water at the shelf's edge, and Hale was stronger than he looked—all that confidence had muscle under it—and his hands found Elodie's throat with the precision of a man who had ruined careers and knew exactly where to press.

"You are *nothing*," Hale snarled, and shoved.

The water took Elody whole.

The flood wasn't water so much as moving earth, cold and muscular, and it rolled him under before he could find down. For one lucid instant he thought, with absurd calm: *this is fitting. The invisible man, erased at last.* His father's compass was years gone, surrendered already, and there was nothing in his pockets, nothing in his hands, nothing anywhere that proved Elodie Ashgrove had ever—

A hand closed on his collar.

Not Hale's. Smaller. Certain.

Sable hauled him up through the brown roar with a grip learned from hauling herself out of rivers she couldn't remember falling into, and dragged him gasping onto the shelf while Hale floundered in the shallows, screaming orders no one obeyed. Above them, the beacon sang on. Below them, the fire ring burned itself out against the wet stone. And her brother stood at the waterline with his rifle grounded, guarding nothing, protecting no one's secrets anymore, just watching his sister save a man's life and quietly, visibly, choosing a side.

Elodie coughed half the Throat out of his lungs and lay on his back on the granite, blinking at a sky gone suddenly, enormously public.

"Sable," he wheezed.

"Don't talk."

"He tried to drown me."

"Yes."

"In front of witnesses."

"Yes." She was grinning now, mud-streaked and wild-eyed, wringing out her braid. "Six of them. Plus whatever's listening on the beacon. Congratulations, Ashgrove. You're officially unforgettable."

Downstream, Hale dragged himself onto a rock, soaked, stripped of dignity, his men standing in a loose and mutinous semicircle that no longer faced quite the way he faced. The recording played on—Tomas's voice, steady now in its repetition, saying *remember, remember, remember*—and the flood rose another inch, indifferent, taking its time, holding everyone exactly where they stood.

Elodie started to laugh. It hurt his ribs and his throat and possibly his soul, and he laughed anyway.

"What?" Sable demanded.

"Eleven years," he managed, "of practicing being no one. And it turns out all I had to do was get famous in front of the right people."

"Get up," she said, hauling on his arm, her voice softening into something that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite tears. "The authorities will have heard all this by nightfall. And when they come down that canyon—" she glanced at the water, at the wrecked boats, at her brother standing very still, "—someone is going to have to tell them where the spring is. And someone is going to have to decide what happens to *him*."

Elodie got up. The flood kept rising, patient as ever, carrying them all toward whatever came next.
