## 19. Testimony

The water went down the way a crowd leaves a funeral: slowly, reluctantly, in stages, each one revealing something you'd rather not have seen.

Sable stood on the shelf above the spring and watched the flood peel back from the canyon walls like a bandage coming off a wound. First the high-water mark of silt and debris, a brown tideline forty feet up the rock. Then the terraces, emerging one by one, dripping. Then, at last, the channel itself, running low and clear and ordinary, as though it hadn't spent three weeks trying to kill them.

"You're doing it again," Elodie said behind her.

"Doing what?"

"Watching the water like it owes you money."

She almost laughed. That was the strange arithmetic of the last few weeks — that a man who had practiced being no one for years had become the person whose jokes she waited for. "It might," she said. "It took my whole life and gave it back in pieces. I'm itemizing."

Behind them, in the shade of the overhang, Tomas slept under a survey tarp, his breathing shallow and even. He had good mornings and bad mornings now, and they had all agreed — without ever saying it aloud — not to keep score of which was which. Every morning Sable sat with him and told him his own stories: how he'd joined the survey straight out of the academy because he believed maps were a form of honesty; how he'd been the one who noticed the water samples didn't match the official assay; how he'd run. He listened the way you listen to a story about a stranger you're fond of, and sometimes, at the end, he'd say, "He sounds brave," and she'd say, "He was," and mean it in both directions.

The beacon crackled twice from its perch on the rocks where Elodie had lashed it — the same beacon Hale had brought to erase signals, turned now into a lighthouse. The ranger network had answered within a day of his confession going out. Within three days, the first patrol skiff had appeared at the mouth of the Throat. Now there were four of them moored below the spring, and men and women in gray-and-green moved through the camp Hale's crew had abandoned, cataloguing pumps and poison drums with the grim efficiency of people who had seen this before and hated it.

"Sable." Elodie's voice had changed. She turned. He was looking down at the landing beach, where a skiff was coming up the channel with a woman standing in the bow — tall, gray-haired, wearing the crossed-arrows insignia of the Ranger General's office. "That's Commandant Reyes. She doesn't come to places. Places come to her."

"Then I suppose," Sable said, "we're a place now."

* * *

They met on the flat rock above the landing, because Reyes declined the overhang — "I've been in enough caves this year to last a career" — and because, she said, she wanted to see the spring herself before anyone told her what to think of it.

It was running low and clean in its basin, catching the morning light. Reyes crouched at its edge for a long moment, didn't drink, and stood up with the expression of a woman filing something away that she intended to worry about for the rest of her life.

"Vane," she said, consulting a slate. "Sable Vane, cartographer, Hale expedition."

Sable felt Elodie go very still beside her. She understood why. She'd felt the same stillness in herself all morning, the way you feel it standing at the edge of cold water.

"No," Sable said.

Reyes looked up.

"My name is Sable Vane," she said, "and I'll answer to it, and I'll sign with it. But I'm not testifying as Sable Vane." She took a breath. The canyon was quiet except for the water and the far-off sounds of the evidence teams. "The spring scrambled me. Hale dosed the whole team, and the water did the rest — it took a week of my life and handed it back in pieces, and for a while I couldn't tell which memories were mine and which were ones the water had *edited*. That's what it does. That's what you're going to find in every report, and I need you to understand it before you drink anything, before your superiors drink anything, before anyone gets the bright idea that this is a *tool*."

"I've read your charts," Reyes said. Her voice was dry as the hardpan outside. "The ones in your hand. The ones Hale tried to burn."

"Then you know I can draw a canyon I have no memory of seeing. My hand remembers what my head lost." Sable held up her fingers, ink-stained, cracked from bailing and rope work and three weeks of survival she had no stories for. "So when I testify, I want it on the record which parts came from where. What I *saw* since the flood, I saw. What I *remember*, I remember — but I'm choosing which memories to stand behind. That's the difference. Hale wanted to decide what people knew. I'm deciding for myself. It's the whole point."

Reyes studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, the corner of her mouth moved. "The academy trains rangers to be instruments," she said. "It's rare to meet one who insists on being an author."

"I was never a ranger. I'm a cartographer. We're worse."

"Deponent's counsel will be thrilled," Reyes said, and Sable realized, with a small warm shock, that the woman was joking.

* * *

The testimony took two days, and it was held, at Sable's request, at the long survey table they'd dragged out of the ruined camp, with the charts spread across it — her charts, in her hand, the canyon that appeared on no other survey drawn in ink so confident it seemed to dare the world to contradict it.

She told it in order, or as close to order as the pieces allowed. The team finding the seep in the third week of the survey. The samples. Tomas's quiet discovery that the assay reports were being falsified upstream. Hale's word — *managed*, that soft, terrible word — and the first dosing, done through the canteens, done while they slept. The escape downriver. The flood.

She told the parts she remembered and flagged the parts she'd reconstructed, and when Reyes's clerk asked her how she could be certain of a reconstructed memory, she said, "Because I checked it against my own handwriting," and the clerk wrote that down as though it were a normal sentence, and Elodie, sitting at the end of the table where witnesses weren't supposed to smile, smiled.

When it was done — when she had signed the deposition *Sable Vane* with a flourish she did not remember learning and did not need to — Reyes gathered the pages and said, "There will be a tribunal. Hale's crew will be tried. Some of them will name names, and some of those names will be inconvenient. You should know that testifying makes you a person of record. There's no more erasing you."

Sable looked across the table at Elodie, who was examining the grain of the wood with the concentration of a man being discussed.

"Good," she said.

* * *

That evening, the light came down the canyon the way it did in the dry season — long and amber and forgiving — and Sable found Elodie sitting on the shelf where they'd first drunk the seep, turning his compass over in his hands. His father's compass. She'd seen it a hundred times now; he handled it the way other men handled photographs of the dead.

"You were quiet in there today," she said, sitting down beside him. The rock was still warm.

"Wasn't my testimony."

"No. But you were quiet in a *specific* way. I've learned your quiets. That one had a door in it."

He huffed — almost a laugh. "You have learned my quiets. That's either a compliment or a warning."

"Both. I'm a cartographer. We deal in features." She nudged his boot with hers. "What's behind the door?"

He was quiet a while longer. The spring murmured below them. Somewhere down the canyon, the evidence teams were striking their lamps for the night, and the first stars were coming out over the rim, the ones Sable had taught him to steer by, one at a time, on nights when the boat was rotting under them and the food was down to a tin of beans and hope.

"The man I owe," Elodie said at last. "The debt. He'll have heard by now. Hale's confession went out on every beacon in the network, and my name was attached to it — my *real* name, the one I spent years burying." He turned the compass over again. "He'll come, or he'll send for me. And he'll offer me the same trade he always offers: silence for erasure. Stay quiet about where the spring is, and the debt dies."

"What will you say?"

He looked at her then, and she saw something in his face she'd never seen there — not the practiced blankness, not the weary self-containment, but something younger and more frightened and more alive underneath it, the way a canyon looks when the flood finally drains and you can see the shape of the thing that made it.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the honest answer. Three weeks ago I'd have said yes before he finished the sentence. Invisible and debt-free — that was the whole dream." He turned the compass in his fingers one more time, and then, deliberately, he put it away in his coat, the inner pocket, the one over the heart, and buttoned it. "But it turns out being no one is just another kind of drowning. Slower. Drier. But the same."

Sable reached into her satchel and drew out a folded square of paper — one of the last clean sheets, salvaged from the survey stores. She'd been working on it in the evenings while Tomas slept, and her hands had known what to do even before she'd decided.

"What's that?" Elodie asked.

"Coordinates." She unfolded it and held it out. "A spring in the Coahuila breaks, up north. Clean water, as far as I can tell — my hand drew it, so it's real whether I remember it or not. Nobody's mapped it properly. It'll take a month to get there and a month to survey, and the pay is nothing, and the company is—"

She stopped. She'd meant to say *the company is tolerable*, which was the joke they'd been circling for weeks, the joke that wasn't a joke.

Elodie took the paper. He looked at it for a long time — at the coordinates, at the small, precise, unmistakable signature in the corner. Hers. Chosen.

"You're asking me to be on a map," he said quietly.

"I'm asking you to be *on* a map. With me. There's a difference, and it's the whole point." She smiled. "You can say no. You're allowed to be no one if that's what you choose. But I've seen what you choose when it matters, and it isn't that."

Below them, the spring ran clear in the last light. Down the canyon, a skiff's lantern came up the channel toward the landing — a visitor, arriving late, a tall figure in the bow with the posture of a man who had never once been refused. Sable watched Elodie see it, watched his hand go to the inner pocket of his coat, watched him stand.

"That'll be him," Elodie said. "The debt."

"Elodie—"

"It's all right." He said it the way he'd said *trust me* on the river, in the worst of the rapids, and she had learned by now that when he said it in that voice, it was true. "Whatever he offers — I already know what I'm going to say." He glanced back at her, and the ghost of a grin crossed his face. "But I'd like to say it out loud, once, where someone's listening. Would you—"

"I'm coming," Sable said, already on her feet, folding her charts. "You're on my map now, Ashgrove. Nobody erases my work."
