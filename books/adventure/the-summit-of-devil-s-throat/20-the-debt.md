## 20. The Debt

The authorities came upriver on the third morning after the flood began to let go of Devil's Throat, and they came in numbers that made the canyon feel briefly like a town.

There were rangers from three districts, a magistrate's clerk with ink-stained cuffs and an expression of permanent apology, two engineers who spent most of their time arguing about pumps, and a photographer who took forty-one pictures of Sable's charts and then asked her to hold still while he took forty-two. There was a doctor for Tomas, which was late but not too late, and there were handcuffs for Corvus Hale, which were neither.

Elodie watched them load Hale onto a patrol boat at midday. The director went quietly, chin high, the way men go when they are already composing the version of events they will tell in court. He looked, as he passed Elodie on the gangplank, directly at him — one long, measuring look, the look of a man filing something away — and then he was gone downriver, and the canyon exhaled.

"You're staring," Sable said. She had come up beside him with two cups of the terrible coffee the rangers brewed, and she handed him one without asking, which he was learning was simply how she did things.

"I'm counting," Elodie said. "Boats. Men. Guns."

"And?"

"And none of them are his." He drank the coffee, which tasted like boiled rope. "Hale doesn't travel without a second purse. Somewhere in that fleet is a man whose job isn't surveying or arresting or pumping. It's paying."

Sable considered this over the rim of her cup. "You think someone's going to try to buy you."

"I think," Elodie said, "that I've been bought before. Cheaply. I know what the transaction looks like from the seller's side."

She didn't say anything to that. She just stood next to him in the sun, shoulder almost touching his, watching the last of Hale's expedition break camp below. After a while she said, "Whoever it is — you don't have to take the meeting."

"I know."

"You could just not be where the meeting happens. You're good at that. You told me. You're *very* good at that."

It was said gently, but it landed where she meant it to land, because Sable Vane had a cartographer's instinct for terrain, including the internal kind. Elodie looked out at the water, brown and slowing now, dropping inch by inch off the canyon walls like a curtain being lowered on everything that had happened here.

"Ambrose Kell," he said.

"Should I know that name?"

"Everyone in the basin knows it eventually. He owns half the freight lines out of Cinder Flats and the other half of the water rights. Twenty-two years ago he was a young man with one fast boat and a cargo manifest that didn't exist, and I was nineteen years old and hungry, and a ranger captain offered me a month's wages to tell him what was in the hold." Elodie turned the cup in his hands. "I told him. They seized the boat. The cargo turned out to be medicine bound for a fever camp upcountry, and by the time anyone understood the mistake, people had died waiting. Kell lost everything except his life, and he spent twenty years building it back tenfold. And I got my month's wages, and a debt I've never once explained to anyone, because explaining it would mean admitting what I was worth."

Sable was quiet for a moment. "A month's wages," she said.

"The captain rounded up. He was generous that way."

"That's the debt. That's what you've been erasing yourself over. Not money."

"It's all money, in the end. It's just that some of it gets paid in other currencies." He shrugged, the old shrug, the one that closed doors. "Kell kept something of mine as collateral. My father's compass. Held it against the loss he suffered, on the theory that a boy who sells a man out once will sell anything, and a compass is a thing a man's son can't easily replace. He's right about the second part."

"He's had your father's compass for twenty-two years."

"He's had the *debt* for twenty-two years. The compass is just how he keeps it warm."

Sable set down her cup. "And you think he's coming here."

"I think the spring restores memory," Elodie said, "and the government wants it erased, and the one thing more dangerous than a witness is a witness who owes somebody. Kell didn't get rich missing arithmetic like that."

* * *

He came on the fifth day, when the river had dropped enough to show the scars of the flood — the stripped banks, the trees bent downstream, the survey stakes standing in rows like teeth. He came alone, which was its own kind of message: *I am not afraid of you, and I do not need men to handle what we are going to discuss.*

Ambrose Kell was sixty now, silver at the temples, built like a man who had been strong once and had since discovered chairs. He wore clothes that cost more than a ranger made in a year and managed, somehow, to look comfortable in them, which was worse. He stepped off his launch onto the shingle below the camp and stood there a moment, looking up at the canyon walls, and Elodie watched him take in the scale of the place with a businessman's eye — the water, the rock, the seep somewhere above that could rewrite what a person knew.

"There he is," Kell said warmly, when Elodie came down to meet him. "Elodie Ashgrove. You know, I've had people looking for you for two decades. Off and on. Never hard enough to find you, I'll admit. You're a difficult man to locate."

"That was the general idea."

"It's a good idea. It's a *discipline*. I respect it." Kell clasped his hands behind his back and rocked slightly on his heels, surveying the camp, the rangers, the distant figure of Sable sorting charts under a tarp. "Terrible business with Hale. I knew him, you know. Slightly. Enough to have declined dinner. A man who talks about populations the way one talks about inventory — you learn to hear it early or you end up owning stock in it."

"What do you want, Mr. Kell?"

"Straight to it. Good. I remembered you as a boy who'd learned to be careful first and honest second, so I'll match the pace." He turned back to Elodie, and his eyes were pale and patient and entirely without malice, which somehow made them harder to face than anger would have been. "Here is the situation. In a week, maybe two, the hearings begin. Everyone in this canyon will be asked where the spring is. Most of them won't be able to answer — the water saw to that, ironically. But you can answer. And you, I'm told, are a man with a reputation for finding things and keeping his mouth shut, both of which are services I have historically valued."

"You want the location."

"I want the *silence*, Ashgrove. The location is incidental. Anyone can find a seep; the government has already found it, and the government is the problem. What matters is what happens after. There will be a decision — sealed site, guarded site, drained site, or some committee's compromise — and decisions are made by the people in the room, and rooms are small, and I am very good at small rooms." He smiled, thin and reasonable. "All I ask is that you not testify to the precise location. Let the engineers argue over their guesses. Let the maps stay vague. In exchange—"

"In exchange the debt is erased."

"In exchange the debt is *forgiven*, which is better. Erasure implies forgetting, and I don't forget. Forgiveness means I remember the whole account and mark it settled." Kell reached into his coat, unhurried, and produced a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age. He held it out. "Twenty-two years I've carried this around, more or less. Moved house four times. It's always been the last box packed and the first unpacked. Do you know why?"

Elodie didn't take it. "Because it's leverage."

"Because it's the only thing I own that I never wanted." For a moment — just a moment — the warmth dropped out of Kell's voice, and underneath it was something older and tireder. "Your father was the only man who ever beat me honestly, Ashgrove. Did you know that? Before the boat, before any of it. We raced freight up the flats road, his mule team against my first engine, and he beat me by four hours and then helped me fix the engine when it cracked. He laughed at me the entire time. Kindly. The way you laugh at family." Kell looked down at the bundle in his hand. "Then his son sold me to the rangers, and I learned the other lesson, the profitable one, and I kept the compass to remind myself which lesson to trust. And I have hated carrying it every single year, because every year it asks me a question I can't answer."

"What question?"

"*Which son did I lose?*" Kell said. "The one who fixed my engine, or the one who sold me?" He held the bundle out again. "Take it. Take it, and take the forgiveness, and walk away from these hearings rich in the only currency that ever mattered to either of us — unwatched. Nobody looking for you. Nobody remembering you. You can go back to being no one. Isn't that what you wanted?"

The canyon was very quiet. Down by the tarp, Elodie could see Sable pretending very badly not to watch, her head bent over a chart she was holding upside down.

And Elodie thought about the question. Honestly, carefully, the way he'd learned to read sign — not the track he wished was there, but the one that was.

For twenty-two years he had practiced being no one. He had burned letters, scrubbed records, walked out of towns ahead of his own reputation. He had told himself it was penance, and penance had been easier than the truth, which was that invisibility was also a hiding place — from the fever camp, from the dead, from the arithmetic of what a month's wages had purchased. He had spent two decades paying a debt in the currency of absence, and absence, it turned out, compounded.

And then a woman had come adrift out of the flooded lower canyon with no past and steady eyes, and she had accused him of choosing cowardice, and she had been right, and being right had felt less like an insult than like a door finally unlocked from the outside.

"No," Elodie said.

Kell's eyebrows rose a fraction. "No?"

"I'm going to testify. Full location. Bearings, elevations, the seep line, all of it, in front of the magistrate and whatever committees you fill your small rooms with. And I'm going to testify about Hale, and about the survey team, and about a junior surveyor named Tomas Reyes who is going to need his story told for him every morning for a long time, and I intend to help tell it." Elodie held out his hand — not for the compass, but open, palm up, flat. "But you're right about one thing. The debt ends today. So pay it."

"This *is* the payment," Kell said slowly. "Forgiveness plus the collateral. That was the offer."

"No. That was the trade — silence for the compass. I'm not taking the trade." Elodie kept his hand out. "Give me the compass, and I give you the testimony instead. All of it. That's the settlement. You carry that thing twenty-two years because it asks you a question you can't answer — fine. Here's your answer: you lost neither son. You lost one boy who did a cheap, cowardly thing, and what grew out of him is standing in front of you trying to do an expensive, difficult one. Settle the account against *him*. Not against silence. Silence is how the fever camp happened in the first place."

For a long moment Ambrose Kell did not move. The river muttered over the shingle. Somewhere upstream a ranger shouted something about a winch, and the sound echoed and died.

Then Kell laughed — short, startled, genuine, a sound that seemed to surprise him more than anyone. "You know," he said, "your father would have said almost exactly that. Different words. Worse grammar. Same spine." He set the bundle in Elodie's palm, and it was heavier than Elodie remembered and lighter than he feared. "Settled, then. Witnessed by a canyon. God help me, I feel *lighter*."

"You'll still work the hearings," Elodie said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'll still work the hearings. I'm sixty years old, Ashgrove; I'm not going to reform in a riverbed." Kell straightened his coat, and the businessman returned to his face like a shutter closing — but not all the way. Something stayed open behind it. "But I'll tell you what a man with a settled account can afford to do that a creditor can't. I can lose a vote. Once. Quietly. If the room splits wrong, I won't be the one who mends it." He turned toward his launch, then paused. "The compass works, by the way. I had it serviced. Seemed criminal to let it die in a box."

He went down the shingle, boarded, and the launch pulled away upriver, leaving a long wake that slapped against the stones for a minute afterward, like slow applause.

* * *

Elodie sat on a rock above the waterline for a long time with the oilcloth bundle in his lap, not opening it.

It wasn't sentiment, exactly. Or it was, but mixed with something more practical — the superstitious caution of a man who has learned that objects can change weight depending on what you decide they mean. As long as it stayed wrapped, it was still Kell's collateral, still the debt, still the shape of the old life. Opened, it became something else, and he wasn't sure yet what.

Sable found him there eventually. She sat down beside him without asking, which was how she did things, and looked at the bundle, and looked at him.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me everything I've spent twenty-two years wanting. Total invisibility. Debt forgiven, name unwritten, nobody looking." Elodie turned the bundle over. "And the strange thing is, for about ten seconds, I wanted it. It fit like an old boot."

"And then?"

"And then I thought about Tomas asking us every morning to tell him his stories, and how he says *thank you* after, every time, like each one is a gift he didn't expect to survive the night. And I thought about you, testifying under your real name next week. And I thought—" he huffed, almost a laugh, "—I thought, hell, if she can stand up in front of a magistrate and claim a self the water scrambled, the least I can do is claim one I've been hiding in plain sight for two decades."

Sable nodded slowly. Then, with the tactlessness that Elodie had come to rely on the way other men relied on a good knife: "Are you going to open it, or are we going to sit here admiring the wrapping?"

He untied the cord. The oilcloth fell away.

The compass was smaller than memory. Brass, dented at the bezel where his father had dropped it off a pack mule in '41 and lied about it for a year. The glass was new — Kell's servicing — and beneath it the needle swung, hesitated, and settled, pointing north with the calm certainty of a thing that had never once doubted itself.

Elodie realized his eyes were wet and did nothing whatsoever to hide it.

"My father bought this the week I was born," he said. "Told me it was so I'd never be lost. I used to think that meant the needle." He closed his hands around it. The brass was cold and then, slowly, wasn't. "Turns out it meant the man holding it."

Sable said nothing. She just leaned over, briefly, until her shoulder rested against his, and stayed there, and the river ran on below them, lower every hour, uncovering the world.

After a while Elodie stood, and slipped the compass into his shirt pocket — over the heart, though he'd have denied choosing the spot — and looked up the canyon, toward the trailhead that the drying season was already opening, mile by mile, like a letter unsealed.

"Come on," he said. "We've got hearings to ruin."

Sable grinned and got up beside him, and together they climbed toward camp, two people with names, walking north by a dead man's needle — which, Elodie noted with something that felt dangerously like hope, pointed exactly the direction they were already going.
