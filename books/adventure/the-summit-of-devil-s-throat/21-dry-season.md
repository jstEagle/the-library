## 21. Dry Season

### Sable

The dry season came to Devil's Throat the way forgiveness comes to a marriage—slowly, and with a great deal of dust.

For weeks the canyon had been a brown river wearing a disguise. Now the water was dropping a hand's width every morning, leaving terraces of red mud on the walls like the rings of some enormous, patient tree. The air smelled of wet stone drying in the sun, of creosote, of beginnings.

Sable Vane woke before dawn in the survey camp they'd reclaimed—the same camp where she had once found her own handwriting on maps of places she couldn't remember, back when she had been a stranger to herself. That seemed like years ago. It had been one season.

She built the fire first. Then she filled the tin cup from the good seep—the clean one, upstream, the one Elodie had marked on his own crude map with a small careful star—and carried it to the tent at the edge of camp where Tomas slept.

He was already sitting up. He was always already sitting up now, as if sleep were a country he could only visit briefly before the border guards noticed him.

"Good morning," he said. Then, the way he said it every morning now, with the particular politeness of a man asking for something he's ashamed to need: "Could you—"

"I know," Sable said. "I'm here."

She sat down on the upturned crate beside his cot and began, as she did every morning, to tell him his stories.

"You were born in Marrow Bend," she said, "where your father ran a ferry that mostly ferried goats. You were the youngest of four and the only one who could read, which made you insufferable by age six."

"That tracks," Tomas said gravely.

"You won a scholarship to the Survey Academy on the strength of an essay about why maps are moral documents. Your examiner wrote in the margin: *This boy will either change the profession or be expelled.*"

"He got both," Tomas said, and smiled, and then the smile wobbled, because somewhere behind his eyes the essay itself was gone—he knew about it only because she'd told him—and Sable reached over and squeezed his wrist until it steadied.

"You joined Corvus Hale's expedition because you believed the government should know what's out here," she went on. "You were the best junior surveyor on the team. When you found the spring, you took samples without being asked, because that's what a moral map requires."

"And then I stole the case and ran downriver like a rabbit," Tomas said.

"And then you did the bravest thing anyone in this story has done," Sable corrected him, "which is run *toward* help instead of away from trouble. You just happened to do it in a flood."

Every morning the stories were a little longer, or rather the telling of them was, because each day there was more scaffolding required—more explanation of who Hale was, who she was, who the quiet man mending nets down by the boat landing was. Some mornings Tomas remembered her between dawn and breakfast. Some mornings he didn't remember her at all until she said his name twice.

The poison in the upper seep worked backward through a life, Elodie had observed. It ate the newest memories first and worked toward the oldest ones, the way rot works into a fence post. Which meant that someday—not soon, but someday—Tomas would forget the ferry and the goats and the essay, and there would be nothing left of him but whatever Sable could carry.

So she carried it. Every morning. She had written it all down too, in her own hand, in a ledger she kept wrapped in oilcloth, because paper was a kind of memory that even poison couldn't reach. But she told it aloud anyway, because Tomas said the words sounded different coming from a person than off a page. He said when she told them, he could almost feel them happening.

Today, when she finished—with the explosion, with Hale's confession crackling out over the ranger network, with the authorities poling their way up the receding river—Tomas was quiet for a while.

"You're leaving," he said. Not a question. He'd learned to read her the way she read terrain.

"In a few days," Sable said. "When the trailhead opens."

"And Elodie?"

Sable looked out the tent flap toward the river, where a lean figure was standing hip-deep in the shallows, doing something inscrutable with a length of rope and a great deal of concentration.

"He hasn't said," she said.

"Hm," said Tomas, in the tone of a man who had, in another life, been an excellent surveyor and therefore an excellent judge of what the data actually showed.

* * *

### Elodie

The rope was not going to cooperate, and Elodie Ashgrove had made peace with that.

He was trying to rig a drying rack for the last of their smoked fish, and the rope—salvaged, sun-rotted, opinionated—kept slipping its knots. He was aware, in the way a man becomes aware of weather, that Sable was watching him from the tent.

He had spent most of his adult life being watched by nobody, and he had gotten very good at it. Being watched by Sable Vane was a different discipline entirely. She had a cartographer's eye; she saw the lines you didn't draw.

The knot held on the third try. He stood there a moment longer than necessary, ankle-deep in the cold clear water, looking up the canyon.

The Throat in dry season was almost unrecognizable. The flood had scoured it and then withdrawn like a tide, and what it left behind was raw and new—fresh gravel bars, exposed rock faces the color of embers, pools holding themselves in hollows like coins. Somewhere above all of it, past the poison-stained upper seep, past the shelf where Sable had drunk and remembered, the true source waited under the summit. Clean. Unmapped. They had agreed not to go up yet. Some things deserved a proper expedition, with proper gear, and neither of them had the heart for it alone.

That was the thought he kept stepping around, the way you step around a snake on a trail: *alone.*

Elodie had been alone so long it had stopped being a condition and become a craft. He had erased himself from ledgers and rosters and other men's memories with the patience of a man sanding a floor. And then a woman had drifted out of a flooded canyon with no past and no supplies, and somewhere between the rapids and the sea cave and the night they'd turned a dying boat into a weapon, he had stopped practicing being no one and started being—something else. Someone whose tracks were worth hiding because someone else was walking beside him.

He wrung out his trouser cuffs and walked back up the bank.

"Breakfast?" he said.

"Fish again," Sable said.

"Fish again," he confirmed.

"We are two people of extraordinary resourcefulness," she said, "eating fish again."

"The desert provides," Elodie said solemnly, "fish."

She laughed—that quick, surprised laugh she still seemed slightly startled by, as if it belonged to someone she used to know—and handed him a plate, and they ate sitting on the gravel bar with their backs against the overturned hull of the old survey boat, watching the light come down the canyon wall by wall.

It was, Elodie reflected, the finest meal of his life, and it was fish again.

* * *

### Sable

On the third morning, the trailhead opened.

You could tell because the mud at the canyon mouth had finally stiffened enough to hold a hoofprint, and because a supply rider came down from Cinder Flats with mail, flour, and news. The news was that Corvus Hale had been formally charged with eleven counts, that the hearings would be held in the capital in the autumn, and that the ranger network wanted testimony from "the Vane woman, the Ashgrove man, and the surveyor Reyes, if he's able."

Tomas Reyes. Sable had almost forgotten his surname fit him.

"I'll testify," Tomas said, when she read it to him. He was getting better at deciding things quickly now, before the ground shifted under him. "I want it in my voice. While I still—" He stopped, and started again. "While it's mine."

"It will be recorded," Sable said. "All of it. Every morning's telling. Even after."

"Even after," Tomas agreed, and looked at her with such plain trust that she had to turn away and fuss with the fire for a moment.

The rider also brought a letter for her, forwarded through three offices, postmarked from a town she'd never visited. Her brother's handwriting.

She didn't open it by the fire. She walked up the gravel bar, past the drying rack, past Elodie—who glanced up, read the envelope in one look, and with the tact of a man who had spent years making himself invisible, became very interested in his knots again—and stood at the canyon mouth where the wind came through.

*I didn't turn you in,* the letter said, in her brother's cramped, apologetic script. *I keep telling myself that counts for something. I know it doesn't count for enough. I've asked to be assigned anywhere they'll send me that isn't a map room. I don't know how to make this right. I don't suppose memory-water works on guilt. I'm sorry, Sable. I remember everything, and I'd give anything to be able to forget, and I know now that's the coward's wish. You were always the brave one. — D.*

She read it three times. Then she folded it along its original creases, which felt like the right thing to do with a letter from family—putting it back the way it was made—and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where she kept things she wasn't finished with.

When she walked back, Elodie had, impossibly, made progress on the knots.

"Trailhead's open," she said.

"I heard." He straightened, wiped his hands on his thighs. "Rider says the pass to Cinder Flats is dry all the way. Two days' walk."

"Mm," said Sable.

They looked at each other across the gravel bar, two people who had survived a flood together and were now discovering that the flood had been the easy part.

"I'll get my pack," Elodie said.

* * *

### Elodie

They broke camp the way they'd done everything that season—without discussing it much, in the wordless choreography of people who had bailed the same boat. Sable inventoried the charts and sealed them in oilcloth for the authorities. Tomas sat in the sun with the ledger on his knees, reading his own life like a stranger's novel, occasionally saying "I did *not* say that" and being assured by both of them that he absolutely had.

At midday they walked out.

The trail climbed along the old survey line, past stakes that Hale's crew had driven and that no one would ever bother pulling up, and the canyon fell away below them, red and gold and rinsed clean. Elodie walked last, as he always did, checking their backtrail out of habit—and caught himself, halfway up the switchbacks, deliberately setting his boot beside Sable's print instead of erasing it.

Old habits. He let this one live.

They made the rim by late afternoon, and there was the trailhead: a stone cairn, a leaning signpost with three destinations and one blank board where a fourth name had never been painted, and the long dusty road running west toward Cinder Flats and everything after.

Tomas would come this far with them tomorrow, riding the supply mule back to the ranger post, where there were doctors and record-keepers and a job waiting for a man who mapped things. Tonight there was still one more evening at the waystation, one more pot of terrible coffee, one more morning of stories.

But the parting that mattered, somehow, was this one—at the cairn, in the long gold light, with the road unrolling.

Because Sable was not going west.

"I've been offered the resurvey," she said. "The whole basin. Officially, this time. My name on the title block." She said it lightly, but her hand was flat against the pocket where the letter lay, and Elodie understood that the offer meant more than maps. It meant a name, chosen. A self, authored. "I leave in ten days. From the north trail."

"North," Elodie said. "That's good country. Bad water, good country."

"You'd know."

"I would."

A silence. The wind moved the dust along the road in long slow ropes. Elodie looked at the signpost—at the blank board, waiting for a name—and thought about a man who had spent twenty years removing himself from every record, every roster, every map, and who had recently, in a flooded canyon, drawn his first map ever and signed it, and had found that the world did not end. That in fact the world had barely noticed, in the best possible way. That being seen was survivable. That it might even be—

Sable reached into her coat and held out a folded square of paper.

"What's this," Elodie said, though he could see perfectly well what it was. Coordinates. Neat, precise, in her unmistakable hand.

"The source," she said. "Above the flood line. Where the spring actually starts. We agreed we'd go up properly someday, with real gear and real time." She tilted her head. "I've been given real gear and real time. Ten days from now, I'll be at those coordinates. There's a plateau up there big enough for two camps." A pause, delicate as a compass needle settling. "Or one."

Elodie took the paper. His hands, which had steered a boat through a monsoon gorge and cut mooring lines under gunfire, were not entirely steady.

"Sable," he said. "I owe a debt. I paid it, but the man I paid—it's not the kind of thing that stays paid. There may be consequences. There may be—"

"There may be," Sable agreed. "And there may be a plateau with good sightlines and two exits and a man on it who taught me how to read a dust devil. I've done my arithmetic, Elodie Ashgrove. I'm a cartographer. I don't draw lines I haven't measured."

He looked at the coordinates a long time. North. Up. Above the flood line, where the water came out of the earth clean and remembering, and no one had ever thought to write it down.

"You know what this is," he said finally. "This is a beginning. This is you handing me a beginning."

"No," Sable said, and smiled, and the evening light caught her face the way it catches the rim of the canyon at the end of a hard day—warm, and earned. "It's me handing you a *map*. What you do with it is your own survey."

Behind them, up the trail, Tomas called out that the coffee was on and that it was terrible, and that they'd better come drink it while there was any left, and the ordinary music of an ordinary evening rolled down off the rim.

Elodie folded the coordinates with great care and put them in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept the things he wasn't finished with.

"Ten days," he said.

"Ten days," said Sable. "Don't be late. I hear the view from up there is unforgettable."

And far above them both, past the poisoned seep and the drowned camps and all the wreckage of the season, the true spring ran quietly out of the summit stone, keeping every story it had ever been told, waiting for someone to come and write it down.
