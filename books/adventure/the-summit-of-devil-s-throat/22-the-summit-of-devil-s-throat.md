## 22. The Summit of Devil's Throat

The trail up the eastern wall of Devil's Throat did not exist, which was exactly how Elodie liked his trails. It was the sort of path that only appeared to a person who had spent eleven years learning to read country the way other people read letters — the faint shelf of stone where the flash floods had never quite reached, the juniper growing at a lean that said *follow me, the wind and I have an arrangement*. He climbed it in the blue hour before dawn, with Sable three switchbacks above him and gaining ground the way she always did, cheerfully, unfairly, as though gravity were a rumor she'd declined to believe.

"Keep up, ranger," she called down, not turning around. "The sun's coming and I want to be there before it is."

"You said that an hour ago."

"And I've been right the whole hour."

He let himself smile, which was still a new thing, still slightly stiff in the hinge, like a door in an old house that had been shut for years. Eleven years of practicing being no one, and it turned out a face could forget how to do anything else. Some mornings he caught his reflection in a water tin and had to consciously arrange it into something a person might wear. Other mornings — most mornings now — it arranged itself.

The monsoon had come and gone since that terrible green season in the Throat. Hale was in custody, his confession crackling out of his own beacon to every ranger station in the basin while he stood ankle-deep in floodwater shouting about classified work. Tomas was in a sanatorium in the high country with a window that faced the sunrise, and Sable wrote to him every week, and every week she told him his own stories in the morning light of her letters, because she had promised. The spring's lower seep was fenced now, government men with honest faces and honest clipboards cataloguing it, though everyone privately agreed that a spring that remembered things would probably remember being catalogued and hold it against them.

But the source — the true source — had never been found. The surveys mapped it to the flood line and no further. The flood line, in Devil's Throat, was a wall of scoured stone forty feet tall, and above it the canyon narrowed into country so broken that the government mappers had simply drawn a hachured blob and written *impassable* in the confident handwriting of people who had never tried.

Sable had looked at that blob for a long time one evening in Cinder Flats, over a supper of beans and the good bread from the bakery that Elodie had started frequenting because the baker remembered his name and he was practicing letting people do that.

"Nobody's ever been up there," she'd said.

"No."

"So it's clean." She'd said it the way other people said *holy*. "Unmapped. Unpoisoned. Unwritten." She'd looked up at him with that expression she had, the one that made him feel like a locked door being gently picked. "Elodie. We have to go."

And so they had gone, and here they were, climbing in the dark toward a place that had no name because no one had survived the naming.

* * *

The sun found them at the top of the wall, and the Throat opened below like a held breath let out.

Elodie had seen the basin from above before, in the years when he crossed it alone, but he had never seen it like this — washed clean by the flood season, the water gone, the sand laid down in long pale sheets, the channel braiding silver and gold in the early light. Somewhere down there was the shelf where Sable had drunk and remembered. Somewhere down there was the sea cave where he had told her about the man he'd informed on, the worst hour of his life given away like a stone from his pocket, and how she had not forgiven him but had *argued* with him, which had turned out to be better than forgiveness. Somewhere down there was the black scar of Hale's burned pumps.

"Look," Sable said, and pointed.

Above the flood line, the canyon did something no map had recorded. The walls pinched to a slot barely wider than a doorway, and through it — audible before it was visible — came the sound of water. Not the roar of a river. Something quieter. A sound like a room where someone was speaking kindly.

They went through the slot sideways, packs scraping stone, and came out into a chamber the size of a chapel, open to the sky, the walls striped red and cream and a green that came from some mineral Elodie had no name for. And at the center, rising out of a basin of white stone, was the spring.

It was small. That was the first thing. After everything — the floods, the deaths, the war Hale had made of it — the source of all that trouble was a pool you could have stepped across, fed by a thread of water sliding down the far wall, catching the light and throwing it back in slow coins against the stone. Ferns grew in the crevices, impossible ferns, green as a promise. The air smelled wet and mineral and faintly, absurdly, of juniper.

"It's above the line," Sable said softly. "The poison never reached here. The flood never reached here." She crouched at the pool's edge but did not touch the water. "It's been up here the whole time, running clean, waiting for somebody to climb."

"Most people don't climb," Elodie said.

"Most people," Sable said, "are missing the entire point of the world."

She did not drink. He noticed that. She knelt there with her hands in her lap and her face full of longing and she did not drink, and after a moment he understood why, and understood also that she was letting him see her not drink, which was its own kind of gift.

"I spent a week not knowing who I was," she said. "I don't recommend it. And I've spent months since then knowing exactly who I am, and some of what I know is hard." She looked up at him. "The water gives you back what you lost. But it doesn't sort it. It doesn't choose. You get all of it, the way it was." She smiled a little. "I think I'd rather earn my memories the slow way. One morning at a time. Like Tomas. Like telling him his own stories until some of them stick."

"You're the one who sticks," Elodie said. It came out before he could inspect it, the way the truest things always did.

Sable looked at him for a long moment, and then, mercifully, she laughed — that bright, unguarded laugh that had gotten them through a flooded canyon and a night attack and a season of beans. "That was almost romantic, ranger. For you, that's a speech."

"It *was* a speech. I've been working on it since the slot canyon."

"I know. You've been rehearsing at your campfire. You talk to the fire when you think I'm asleep." She stood, and brushed the white dust from her knees. "The fire thinks you're very eloquent."

* * *

They ate breakfast on the chapel floor with their boots off, because Sable insisted that a place like this deserved bare feet, and Elodie discovered that stone worn smooth by ten thousand years of water was, in fact, a reasonable substitute for a floor. The light moved down the striped walls as they ate, the green mineral glowing, the pool throwing its slow coins.

It was while they were eating that Elodie took out the paper.

He had bought it in Cinder Flats from the same shop that had once sold him the rope for his first solo crossing of the basin, back when he was a different man with a different name and a father's compass in his pocket. The shopkeeper had asked what it was for, and Elodie had said, *mapping*, and the word had felt enormous in his mouth, the way a first word feels to a man learning to speak late.

Sable watched him unroll it. She said nothing, which was her way of saying a great deal.

"I've been thinking," he said, "about what you said that night in the cave. About choosing cowardice." He smoothed the paper on the stone. "You were right. Mostly. I hid because hiding was the penance I'd decided I deserved. But there was a second reason, and I never said it out loud, because saying it would have meant admitting I wanted the opposite." He turned the paper so she could see it. Blank. Clean. "Nobody maps a place they plan to leave. Nobody signs a map they mean to disappear from."

Sable had gone very still.

"I gave up the compass," he said. "I gave up the debt. I'm not hiding anymore. And I've been trying to figure out what a man does with himself when he stops hiding, and I keep coming back to the fact that I know this country better than any survey that's ever been run through it. Eleven years of knowing it. Alone. For nothing." He took out the pencil — her pencil, the one she'd pressed on him in the boat, *a cartographer's tool, ranger, treat it with respect* — and found that his hand was not quite steady. "So I thought I'd start."

He began to draw.

He was not good at it. That was the second thing, after the smallness of the spring. His lines wobbled. His scale drifted. He drew the slot canyon too wide and had to scratch it out and draw it again, and Sable made a small strangled sound of professional agony and he said, "You're a witness now, Vane, no taking it back," and she said, "I'm a *witness*, I'm not a *miracle worker*," and he kept drawing.

He drew the chamber. He drew the pool and the thread of water on the far wall and the ferns in their crevices. He drew the light, or tried to — a few strokes at the top of the page that he suspected no cartographer had ever put on a map and that he suspected Sable would permit, just this once, just this page. He drew the trail up the eastern wall, the one that did not exist, and labeled it in his careful ranger's hand: *the way that appears to those who look*.

Sable read that label and pressed her lips together and looked away for a moment, out through the slot at the basin below, and when she looked back her eyes were bright.

"Sign it," she said.

He hesitated. Eleven years was a long time to practice being no one. A name was a hook in the water; a name was a debt; a name was a door that anyone could knock on. He had burned a letter once in a stove in Cinder Flats and watched his old self curl up into ash, and he had thought that was the end of it.

But that had been the end of the *old* name. This was something else. This was the first page of a new book, and a book's first page wants a signature the way a trail wants a walker.

He wrote it. *Elodie Ashgrove.* And beneath it, because the moment seemed to ask for it, because the chamber seemed to ask for it, because the whole impossible green season of his life seemed to ask for it: *Surveyed at last. By the man who was here.*

Sable leaned over and read it, and read it again, and then she did something he did not expect. She took the pencil from his hand, and beneath his signature she wrote, in her quick cartographer's script: *Verified. S. Vane.* And beneath that, smaller: *He was here the whole time. We just had to climb up and find him.*

"There," she said. "Now it's a real map. Two witnesses. That's the standard." She handed the pencil back. "Also your scale is terrible and I'm teaching you contours before we map anything else."

"Anything else," Elodie repeated.

"Well." She gestured with her chin at the basin spread below them, the whole silver-and-gold sweep of it, the braided channels and the unmarked side canyons and the high country beyond, all of it half-known, half-guessed, waiting. "You know this country better than any survey ever run through it. Your words. I'm holding you to them. There's a canyon two days west that appears on no map at all, and I found a reference to it in Hale's own files — a place the old ranchers called the Singing Stones, and nobody living knows why." She was grinning now, that grin that had gotten them through everything. "I thought maybe a ranger and a cartographer could go and find out."

Elodie looked at the map in his hands — his map, the first thing he had ever made instead of erased — and at the spring running clean and small and patient in its chapel of stone, and at the woman who had pulled him out of a flooded canyon and out of himself, and he found that the answer was already in his mouth, had been there for months, waiting for him to climb high enough to say it.

"When do we leave?" he said.

"Tomorrow," said Sable Vane. "The country's not going to map itself."
