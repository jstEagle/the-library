## 1. The Compass in the Post

The dig at Port Merrow was a grave that had already been robbed twice—once by Victorian antiquarians with their trowels and their certainty, and once by the sea itself, which took its time about it but never left anything behind. Noor Oyelaran had been kneeling in the same trench for eleven days, teasing out the ghost of a Roman salting hut from a cliff face that surrendered another foot to erosion every winter. The work was honest. That was the most anyone could say for it.

He felt the rain before he saw it, a fine salt haze rolling off the water and settling on the back of his neck, and he straightened up slowly, one vertebra at a time, the way his body had begun insisting on lately. Twenty-nine years old and he creaked like a ship's rigging. His knees had opinions. His hands, when he flexed them, smelled permanently of earth and something faintly chemical—the consolidant they sprayed on the exposed bone of a child's skeleton two trenches over, a find that had made the local paper's third page under the headline ROMAN MYSTERY DEEPENS, which was the kind of journalism that could only flourish in a town where nothing had happened since 1847.

"Oyelaran." Dr. Fenwick stood at the trench edge with her hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had outlived three governments. She was sixty, built like a groyne, and she looked at him the way she looked at everything: as though it had personally disappointed her. "Post came. There's a parcel. Heavy one."

"I didn't order anything."

"No," she said, and there was something in her voice—not kindness, Fenwick didn't do kindness, but a kind of careful neutrality—that made him look up. "You didn't."

The site hut smelled of wet canvas, diesel from the pump, and the instant coffee that had achieved a state of geological permanence in the bottom of the urn. The parcel sat on the folding table between a crate of bagged sherds and someone's abandoned lunch. It was wrapped in brown paper gone soft at the corners, the way paper gets when it has traveled a long way through a lot of hands, and it was addressed in handwriting Noor knew better than he knew his own.

He knew it the way you know a scar. The tall ascenders. The way the *r* hooked forward like something reaching. He had spent nine years not looking at that handwriting, and here it was anyway, arriving on a Tuesday, in a coastal town he'd chosen precisely because it was nowhere.

His father's handwriting.

Noor didn't touch it for a long moment. He stood with his hands hanging at his sides and let the hut's small sounds go on around him—the drip at the seam of the roof, the wind working at the door latch—and understood that some part of him had been waiting for this parcel for nine years without ever admitting it, the way you wait for a tooth to finally break.

"Want me to open it?" Fenwick asked. She hadn't moved from the doorway.

"It's mine."

"That it is." She turned and left, boots ringing on the duckboards, and Noor was alone with the thing.

He cut the string with his pocketknife. Inside the brown paper was a second layer—a plastic mailer, the kind used for international freight, stamped with customs declarations in two languages and a franking mark from a country he'd last seen at fourteen years old, from an airport window, watching his father get smaller on the tarmac. And inside *that*, packed in wood shavings that still smelled of some equatorial hardwood, was a compass.

Brass. Old brass, the kind that doesn't shine so much as glow, dully, like a lamp behind fog. It was heavier than it looked—heavier than any modern instrument had a right to be—and the casing was battered almost past recognition. Dents everywhere, deep ones, the kind metal takes from stone. One side was scoured nearly smooth, as if it had been dragged across rock. The hinge of the lid worked but complained, a gritty whisper of metal on metal, and when he thumbed it open the needle swung once, hard, and settled pointing north-northwest with the small confident tremble of a thing that has done this ten thousand times.

There was no note. No letter. No card. Just the compass, nested in its shavings, and beneath it, when he tipped them out into his palm, a single strip of paper maybe two inches long, torn roughly from the edge of a field notebook. On it, in that same scar-handwriting, four words:

*Bring it when you come.*

Noor read it three times. Then he set it down on the table very carefully, the way you set down something venomous, and walked to the window, and stood there with his arms folded so tightly across his chest that his own shoulders ached.

*When you come.* As if coming were a simple thing. As if nine years were weather. As if there weren't a whole archaeology of silence between them—every unanswered email, every birthday acknowledged by neither party, every conference talk Kofi Oyelaran gave to standing ovations while his son learned about his father's life from journals and press releases, the way strangers did. *Bring it when you come,* written by a man who had never once, in nine years, asked where his son was living or whether he was eating or whether the hole in his chest where a father should have been had filled with anything besides work.

And yet.

And yet the compass. Noor picked it up again despite himself. Turned it over. The baseplate was engraved, worn nearly flat, but he tilted it against the grey light from the window and caught it: a maker's mark, and below it, in script finer than the dents should have permitted to survive—

*To K.O., who walks straight lines through crooked country. — A.S.*

A.S.

He stared at those letters for a long time. They meant nothing. They meant nothing, and still his pulse ticked up, because his father had kept this thing—carried it, by the look of it, carried it for decades, until it was worn to the shape of his pocket—and then, at the end, whatever the end was, he had mailed it to the son he never wrote to. With no explanation. With four words on a scrap of notebook paper.

That was Kofi Oyelaran entire. A man who could spend forty minutes explaining the hydraulic properties of pre-Columbian drainage systems to a lecture hall of first-years and could not, apparently, compose a sentence to his own child that began with anything other than an instruction.

Noor put the compass in his jacket pocket, because leaving it in the hut felt wrong, and went back out into the rain, and dug for another four hours, badly, thinking about nothing else.

* * *

The bunkhouse was a converted fishing shed at the top of the harbour road, six bunks, a stove that smoked unless you fed it exactly the right kind of coal, and a radio on the shelf above the sink that belonged to Wojtek, the Polish surveyor, who listened to the shipping forecast the way other men prayed. By nine o'clock the site crew had filtered in—Wojtek, Priya from the finds lab, old Tam who ran the dumper and had opinions about everything—and the stove was lit and the rain was doing its patient, total work against the windows, and Noor sat on his bunk with the compass open in his lap, turning it slowly, letting the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, while the others argued about whether the salting hut had a second room or just a very ambitious cupboard.

It was Priya who turned the radio dial. She was flicking through stations half-listening, static and Portuguese football and a man selling boats, and then she stopped, because the news bulletin was leading with something, and the word *expedition* has a way of cutting through a room.

"—authorities confirm that the Oyelaran archaeological expedition, operating on the upper Kessler River, has missed two scheduled check-ins with its support base. Dr. Kofi Oyelaran, the renowned archaeologist and veteran of more than thirty major expeditions, was leading a survey team into remote highland territory. A spokesperson described the loss of contact as, quote, 'not yet cause for alarm,' citing poor weather in the region. A relief assessment is reportedly being considered. More on this story as it develops—"

Priya turned it down. The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone is pretending not to look at one person.

Noor didn't move. He sat on the edge of his bunk with the compass in his lap, and the needle trembled at north-northwest, and somewhere in his chest a feeling he had been carefully maintaining for nine years—this dry, well-constructed resentment, load-bearing, familiar—began, horribly, to take on water.

They would all have heard the name. Of course they would. Everyone in the discipline knew Kofi Oyelaran; you couldn't study a shovel without tripping over the man's bibliography. Wojtek whistled low through his teeth. Tam said something about monsoon season and how no check-in for two days in that country wasn't necessarily anything, radios failed, satellites failed, men forgot. Priya said nothing at all, but she was looking at Noor, and Noor understood that she had already done the arithmetic—same surname, same profession, the way he'd flinched at the parcel that afternoon—and had decided, generously, to say nothing.

"Oyelaran," Tam said. "Any relation?"

"Distant," Noor said. The lie came out smooth and cold and tasted exactly as bad as lies always taste. "I've never met him."

Tam nodded and went back to his newspaper. Wojtek fed the stove. The rain worked at the windows. And Noor sat in the lamplight with his father's compass open in his hands, reading the engraving again in the small yellow light—*who walks straight lines through crooked country*—and thought: you absolute bastard. You couldn't write to me for nine years, and now you send me a dead man's instrument and four words, and now you're missing in a river valley, and I'm supposed to feel something, and the worst part, the genuinely unforgivable part, is that I do.

Because here was the thing he could not say aloud in the bunkhouse, the thing he barely admitted in the privacy of his own skull: worry had arrived before he'd finished hearing the bulletin. It had come in ahead of the resentment, quick and uninvited, the way a dog comes in ahead of its owner, and it was already moving through the house of him, sniffing at everything.

Two missed check-ins. In monsoon country. On a river he knew from childhood maps spread across a kitchen table he'd been banished from at nineteen—maps his father had annotated in that same scar-handwriting, *rapids, unreliable*, *village, good wells*, a whole geography of a place Noor had never seen but somehow knew.

He looked down at the compass. The needle held steady. North-northwest. Toward nothing, from here—an ocean lay in that direction, and beyond the ocean a coastline, and beyond the coast a river running up into mountains where an old man was either fine, or late checking in, or dead in the water.

"You alright?" Priya asked quietly, when the others had turned in.

"Fine."

She looked at him a moment longer, then nodded, and clicked off the lamp, and the bunkhouse settled into the sounds of breathing and rain.

Noor lay awake in the dark with the compass on his chest, feeling its weight rise and fall with his breath. Bring it when you come. The needle pointed north-northwest, patient as gravity, and Noor stared at the ceiling of the fishing shed and understood, with the flat clarity of two in the morning, that he had already decided. He simply hadn't told himself yet.

In the morning, he would go and find Captain Reiner.
