## 2. Salvage Terms

The *Maren K* sat low in the water at the end of the commercial pier, and she looked like exactly what she was: a boat that had been worked hard and apologized to never. Noor had walked past her twice already, once at dawn and once now, mid-morning, with the rain coming down in the fine, needling way that coastal rain did — not enough to soak you all at once, just enough to remind you it could. Her hull was the color of an old bruise, green-black fading to rust at the waterline, and her name had been painted on the stern by someone with more confidence than skill, the letters running slightly downhill as if even the paint had given up on the journey.

He stood at the top of the ramp for a long moment, hands in the pockets of his jacket, feeling the compass press against his hip where he'd transferred it from the tin box to a canvas pouch that morning. It was a small thing, that pressure. It shouldn't have felt like a hand on his shoulder, steering him down the ramp.

The deck was a chaos of honest labor. Crates lashed under tarps, a generator coughing somewhere below, coils of rope coiled with the particular indifference of rope that had been thrown down rather than stowed. A woman was at the rail amidships, working a splice into a hawser with her bare hands, and she did not look up when his boots hit the deck planking. She was broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair cut short as a hedge, and she moved with the economy of someone who had long ago stopped performing effort and simply performed results.

"Deckhand position's filled," she said, without looking up.

"I'm not looking for a deckhand position."

"Then you're lost."

"Captain Reiner?"

Now she looked up. Her eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of sea glass, and they went over him the way a surveyor goes over land — quick, thorough, unimpressed. "Depends on who's asking and what for."

"Noor Oyelaran. I want passage upriver."

The splice work stopped. Not dramatically — she simply set the hawser down across the rail and turned to face him fully, and Noor understood that he had just become a problem she was deciding whether to solve or throw overboard.

"Upriver," she repeated. "The Kessler."

"The Kessler."

"The Kessler that's running at flood stage. The Kessler where the monsoon's due in—" she glanced at the sky as if it owed her money, "—four days, if the weather bureau's feeling optimistic, which it generally isn't. The Kessler that just swallowed an entire archaeological expedition." She wiped her hands on her thighs, leaving darker streaks on canvas that was already beyond dignity. "That Kessler."

"You're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway because the Oyelaran expedition owes me eleven thousand, four hundred and sixty dollars, and I intend to collect it from whatever's left of their camp. Radios, generators, satellite equipment — archaeological expeditions travel heavy and expensive. Salvage rights on abandoned equipment in a flood zone are a gray area, and gray areas are where I make my living." She said it all without heat, the way you'd read a grocery list. "So unless you've got eleven thousand dollars and a strong back, I don't see what you bring to this conversation."

Noor had prepared for this. He'd prepared on the walk over, running the conversation the way you'd run a trench — carefully, layer by layer, knowing that one wrong stroke could bring the whole wall down on you.

"I've got a strong back," he said. "And I've got something better. I know the expedition."

That got her attention. She tilted her head, and he saw her recalculating him, the way the needle of a compass swings when you bring iron near.

"Know it how?"

"I'm a digger. Field archaeologist. Ten years in the ground — coastal surveys, rescue digs, contract work. I know how an expedition camp is laid out. I know where they'll have kept their equipment, what they'll have brought, what's worth taking and what's just weight. You want to salvage eleven thousand dollars of gear off that riverbank, you'll want someone who can tell the difference between a theodolite and a paperweight."

"And you want passage. Just passage."

"Passage and a share. Small share. Ten percent of whatever you recover."

She laughed — one short, hard bark, no humor in it. "Ten percent. Of equipment I'm financing the fuel, the crew, and the risk to recover."

"Five percent of the gross, then, and I work. Full shifts. I'm not cargo, Captain. I'm crew."

The rain picked up, drumming on the tarps, and somewhere below deck the generator changed its cough to a wheeze. She let the silence sit between them for a while, and Noor let it sit too. He'd learned a long time ago, in the trade, that silence was a tool like any other. Most people couldn't bear it. They'd fill it with concessions.

Ilsa Reiner, he was beginning to suspect, could bear a great deal.

"The last man who told me he was crew," she said finally, "was a customs inspector with a badge and a warrant. So before we go any further — why does a field archaeologist with ten years in the ground want to run up a flood-stage river toward a missing expedition, in monsoon season, on a salvage boat, for five percent?"

And there it was. The question under the question. Noor had known it would come, and he had decided, walking down the ramp, exactly how much truth he could afford.

"Because I've worked contracts that went bad," he said. "Not like this. Nothing like this. But I know what it is to be owed money by an operation that folds, and I know what it is to watch the people who owed you sail off up a river and stop answering their radio." He kept his voice level, kept his eyes on hers, and gave her the truth with the center cut out of it, the way you'd hand someone a map with the capital city inked out. "I want to know what happened up there. And I want to be paid what I'm owed. Those two things have gotten tangled up in me, and I don't expect you to care about the first one. But the second one, you can use."

It was, he reflected later, a good answer because most of it was true. Just not the part that mattered.

Reiner studied him for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and produced a folded sheet of paper, soft with handling, and held it up between two fingers.

"You know what this is? It's a manifest of the Oyelaran expedition's equipment, as declared to the river authority when they got their permits. I paid a clerk forty dollars for a copy." She unfolded it, and Noor's stomach tightened as her finger came down the list — tents, generators, radios, theodolites, sample cases, a satellite uplink. "Eleven thousand, four hundred and sixty. That's what they owe me for fuel and port fees from their staging here, plus interest I've decided to charge myself, because no one else will." Her finger stopped. "You notice what's not on this list?"

"Personal effects."

"Personal effects. Which means if I find a man's letters, his photographs, his—" she paused, and something moved behind the sea-glass eyes, there and gone, "—his family's things, those aren't mine to sell. Those go to whoever comes asking. And someone always comes asking, eventually. Usually with a lawyer." She folded the manifest away. "So here's my counteroffer, Oyelaran. You come aboard, you work, and you get passage. No share. In exchange, when we find that camp — and we will find it, dead or alive, because the river doesn't lose things, it just moves them — you help me sort what's salvage and what's personal, and you keep your mouth shut about the gray areas. That's the deal. Take it or walk back up the ramp."

"No share," Noor said. "But I keep anything addressed to me."

Her eyebrows went up a fraction. "Anything addressed to you."

"Expeditions carry mail out with the supply runs. If there's a mail sack in that camp, and there's something in it for me, it's mine. That's my price. That and the work."

It was a strange thing to ask for, and he watched her understand that it was a strange thing to ask for. A man bargaining for salvage rights on a drowned camp, and what he wanted was mail. But the compass sat against his hip like a second opinion, and he thought: *bring it when you come*. If his father had sent letters upriver — if there was anything in that camp with his name on it — he would not leave it to a salvage manifest and a gray area.

Reiner looked at him for a long time. The rain came down. The generator wheezed. Somewhere behind them, on the pier, a gull screamed at nothing.

"Mail," she said at last, as if tasting the word. "You're either the most sentimental salvage man I've ever met, or you're running from something, or you're running toward something, and in my experience the second and the third look identical from the outside." She stuck out a hand, rough as dock rope. "Passage and wages — no wages, passage and food — in exchange for work and silence. Mail addressed to you is yours. Everything else is mine. We shake on it, and if you lie to me about anything that matters, I'll put you ashore wherever we happen to be, and the river can have its own opinion about that."

Noor took the hand. Her grip was exactly as strong as he'd expected and exactly as unpleasant.

"Deal," he said.

"Deckhand Chandra will show you where to stow your kit. We cast off at six tomorrow, on the tide and the flood both, because if I'm going to be stupid enough to run this river, I'm going to be efficient about it." She turned back to her hawser, then paused. "Oyelaran."

"Yes?"

"You said you knew the expedition. How?"

And there it was again — the question under the question, surfacing like something dead coming up out of a flooded trench. Noor had known it would come back. He'd bought himself a night, maybe. A day.

"Permit records," he said. "The river authority posts the expedition rosters. I read the same list you did." It was almost true. He had read the roster — had read it a dozen times the night before, by lamplight, looking for a name he both dreaded and needed to find: *K. Oyelaran, expedition lead.* It was there, printed in bureaucratic black, and it had hit him like a fist made of paper.

Reiner grunted, which from her he was beginning to understand could mean anything from *acceptable* to *I'll be watching you*. She went back to her splice.

Noor found Dev Chandra amidships, a wiry man with a sunburnt face and the restless hands of someone who couldn't stop working even when there was nothing to work on. Chandra looked him over with frank, cheerful curiosity, the kind Noor hadn't been on the receiving end of in years.

"You're the new hand," Chandra said. It wasn't a question. "Captain said to give you the forward locker. Fair warning — it leaks. Everything on this boat leaks. The boat itself leaks, a little, in a philosophical sort of way."

"Then we're well matched."

Chandra laughed, delighted, and showed him the locker, and the narrow bunk in the fo'c'sle that Noor would share with the anchor chain and a smell of diesel and old fish that no amount of time would ever fully evict. Noor stowed his kit — one bag, the tin box, the canvas pouch with the compass — and stood a moment in the dim, rocking dark of the fo'c'sle, listening to the hull breathe against the pier.

He had done it. He was aboard. And he had done it by cutting his father out of the story entirely, the way you'd amputate a limb to save the body — cleanly, at the joint, with a tourniquet of half-truths. *I read the roster. I read the same list you did.* Technically true. Functionally a lie.

He thought about Reiner's handshake, and her terms: *if you lie to me about anything that matters.* He thought about how it was possible to lie without ever saying anything untrue — how you could build a whole architecture of concealment out of load-bearing omissions, and how it would stand right up until the day someone leaned on it.

* * *

The evening came down gray and early, and Noor worked until his hands were raw — coiling rope, lashing crates, learning the boat's grammar the way you learned any new site, by touch and error. Chandra talked the whole time, a running commentary on the river, the boat, the weather, the cook's crimes against rice, and Noor let the talk wash over him, answering in monosyllables, cataloguing.

"You've done river work before?" Chandra asked, as they wrestled the last of the fuel drums into the hold.

"Coastal. Digs, mostly. Some survey."

"Ah, a dirt man." Chandra grinned. "You'll hate the river, then. The river doesn't respect the stratigraphy. It mixes everything. You dig a site on the Kessler, you get 1990 and 1400 in the same layer, all jumbled up, and no way to tell which grief came from when."

Noor paused, a coil of rope in his hands. "You talk like you've dug."

"Me? No. My grandfather did, though. Downriver, before the mining company came through and rearranged everything." Chandra's grin faded into something older. "The Kessler valley's got a long memory, friend. Longer than the river. You'll see."

They ate that night in the cramped galley — Reiner, Chandra, a taciturn engineer named Vasquez who ate like it was a chore to be finished quickly, and Noor, the stranger at the table. Reiner laid a chart of the Kessler out on the table and weighted the corners with mugs, and for an hour the four of them bent over it while she talked distances, fuel, water levels, the two checkpoints where the expedition had missed its radio call-ins.

"They went silent here," she said, tapping a point where the river bent north, "and here, six days later. That's not equipment failure. Equipment fails once. Two missed check-ins means either they're past caring about protocol, or they can't radio. Either way, we'll know which inside a week."

"And if they're dead?" Vasquez asked, not looking up from his food.

"Then their equipment's still worth eleven thousand dollars," Reiner said, "and their families still deserve to know. Both things are true. I've never found a reason to pretend otherwise." She rolled up the chart and looked at Noor across the table, and for a moment he felt seen straight through, sea-glass eyes going all the way down to the tin box and the canvas pouch and the name he wasn't saying. "Get some sleep, Oyelaran. Six o'clock comes early, and the river doesn't wait."

* * *

He didn't sleep, or hardly. He lay in the fo'c'sle with the compass open on his chest, listening to the hull knock against the pilings, and he turned the day over the way you'd turn a find over in your hands, looking for what it really was.

He had a berth, a deal, and a lie. The lie was load-bearing. *I read the roster.* He'd told it smoothly, and Reiner had half-believed it, and the half that didn't believe was going to sit at the back of her sea-glass eyes for the whole voyage, waiting.

And beneath that — beneath the arithmetic of it, the five percent he'd talked himself down from, the mail sack he'd bargained for instead of money — beneath all of it was the thing he hadn't said to anyone, not to Priya, not to himself, not to the compass: that when he'd read the roster and seen *K. Oyelaran, expedition lead*, his first feeling had not been worry. It had been a fierce, shameful *there you are*. There you are, up a river, missing, silent, possibly dead, and finally — *finally* — impossible to ignore.

He'd spent nine years learning to need nothing from Kofi Oyelaran. Nine years of returned letters and unanswered calls and a mother who'd said, near the end, *he loves you in his way, Noor, it's just that his way is a hole in the ground somewhere.* And now the old man had sent a compass with no note and gone silent up a river, and Noor had discovered that the need had never died at all. It had just gone feral. It had been living in him all this time like something in a cave, blind and hungry, and the compass was the light at the cave mouth, and he hated it, and he had followed it down to this pier anyway.

The needle pointed north-northwest. Toward the bend in the river. Toward the silence.

Outside, the rain kept on, and the tide turned, and somewhere in the dark the pilings groaned. Noor closed the compass and stowed it in the canvas pouch, and lay listening to the boat breathe, and rehearsed, one more time, the small smooth architecture of his lie — testing each joint, each load-bearing omission, the way you'd test a rope before trusting it with your weight.

At six, they cast off on the tide and the flood both, and the *Maren K* turned her blunt bow upriver, into the brown, swollen, patient water, and Noor stood at the rail and watched the town shrink behind them and did not look back.

The Kessler ran high and fast beside them, carrying everything toward the sea — silt, branches, rain from mountains he couldn't see. Somewhere up that water, past the bend, past the checkpoints, was a camp, and a silence, and a man he had spent nine years trying to stop being the son of.

The river didn't care. The river just moved things.

Noor gripped the rail and let it carry him.
