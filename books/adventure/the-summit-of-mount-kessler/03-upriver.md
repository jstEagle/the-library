## 3. Upriver

The Kessler did not want them.

Dev Chandra had worked the river for eleven years, and he had learned to read its moods the way a farmer reads clouds — but this season the river was not in a mood. It was in a rage. The monsoon had come early and hard to the high country, and all that rain came down to meet them now, the whole watershed's grief funneled into one brown, muscular channel that carried entire trees like matchsticks. The *Marta B* — Ilsa Reiner's salvage boat, forty years old and held together by welds and stubbornness — climbed each wave as if scaling a wall, hung at the top with her propeller churning air, then slammed down into the trough hard enough to rattle teeth.

Dev stood at the bow rail with his hands wrapped around the cold steel and watched the water try to kill them.

He was not afraid, exactly. Fear was a thing you got used to on the Kessler, the way you got used to blisters or bad coffee. What he felt was older than fear: a kind of respect that bordered on dread, the feeling of being watched by something vast and indifferent. His grandmother used to say the river remembered everyone who ever drowned in it, and that on certain nights you could hear them counting. Dev had never heard the counting. But he believed in the remembering.

"Chandra!" Ilsa's voice cut through the engine roar from the wheelhouse behind him. "Get off the rail before the river gets a free meal!"

He grinned and gave her a lazy salute, though he didn't move until he'd finished looking. Ahead, the valley narrowed. The walls rose up steep and green on both sides, forested so densely the trees seemed to be climbing over each other's shoulders to get a look at the water. Mist clung to the upper slopes like gauze over a wound. Somewhere up there, past the treeline, past the icefalls, was the glacier, and past the glacier whatever it was Dr. Oyelaran's expedition had gone chasing after and then gone silent about.

Dev turned and made his way aft along the gunwale, one hand always on the rail, stepping over coiled line and fuel drums lashed under tarps. The deck was crowded now. Salvage runs were usually lean affairs — Ilsa, Dev, the twins Ferro and Sanka down in the engine room, and whoever needed moving upriver badly enough to pay. But this run had grown passengers like a wound grows infection. There was the quiet one, Noor, who'd bought his way aboard with cash and a story about a survey contract that fooled nobody, least of all Dev, who had watched the man stare at the water for six hours without speaking. And there were the two men in matching gray jackets who called themselves a relief party sent by Halloran Mining, though they'd arrived at the dock in a truck with no company markings and asked questions about the Oyelaran camp that relief parties don't ask.

That was yesterday. That was downstream. For now it was just the river, and the river took up all the attention there was.

He found Noor aft, sitting on a crate of chain with his back against the cabin bulkhead, the brass compass open in his palm. He wasn't reading it. He was just holding it, the way a man holds a letter he can't bring himself to open.

"You're going to lose that over the side," Dev said, dropping onto the crate across from him. "River takes everything eventually. Might as well not hand it a head start."

Noor closed the compass. "You've worked this water long?"

"Eleven years. My father worked it twenty before me. Before that, my grandfather poled timber rafts down from the high valleys when the forests still belonged to themselves." Dev lit a cigarette, cupping the flame against the wind, and offered one. Noor shook his head. "So I suppose you could say I've been coming up this river my whole life, one way or another."

"And the people here?" Noor asked. "Along the banks. We passed three villages since dawn and didn't see a single person."

"They saw us." Dev exhaled smoke. "They just don't wave anymore."

"Why not?"

Dev looked at him for a while. The boat groaned through a set of standing waves, and spray came over the bow and spattered them both, cold as thrown gravel. Downstream villages, low villages, they still traded with the boats, sold fish and fuel and gossip. But up here, above the confluence where the Kessler met its dark tributary, the villages turned their faces away from the river traffic. He'd never quite understood why until he was a boy and his father finally told him.

"You know why we're running salvage instead of cargo?" Dev said. "Why Ilsa's owed money by half the expeditions that have come through in ten years? Because everybody comes upriver hunting something. Gold, timber, ruins, glory. And every few years somebody finds it, and somebody else dies finding it, and the ones who die get blamed for it." He tapped ash into the water. "There's a name people say on this river when they want to curse something. You heard it yet?"

"No."

"You will." Dev settled back against the bulkhead. The engine thundered beneath them, and the mist came down the slopes in slow fingers, and he found himself talking the way men talk in high places — because the altitude loosens something, because the story wanted telling, because the passenger had the kind of face that made you want to warn him about something you couldn't name.

"Thirty years ago," he said, "there was an expedition. Big one. Sponsored money, satellite phones before anybody had satellite phones, the works. Halloran Mining paid for it — same company whose jacket those two gentlemen in the wheelhouse are wearing, if you look close enough at the stitching. They were surveying the upper Kessler for a road and a mine head. Needed a route through the high passes before the ice closed."

"The guide," Noor said quietly. "Amara Sesay."

Dev stopped. Looked at him properly for the first time. "Where'd you hear that name?"

"I don't know. It was in something I read. Years ago." Which was a lie, Dev was fairly sure, but it was the kind of lie a man tells when the truth is too heavy to lift alone, so he let it stand.

"Amara Sesay," Dev repeated, and saying the name aloud on the water felt like dropping a stone down a well and waiting for a splash that never comes. "Best guide these mountains ever produced. My grandfather knew her — poled rafts alongside her, said she could read the ice the way priests read scripture. She knew where the corn snow lay thin and where the seracs leaned, knew the weather three days out from the smell of the wind off the glacier. Fourteen seasons guiding, fourteen seasons bringing everybody home. And then one autumn she took a Halloran survey team up high, and four of them came down dead, and the company needed someone to blame."

"The weather turned," Noor said. "Or the route—"

"The report says she took a shortcut." Dev's voice went flat, the way it did when he recited things he'd been made to memorize against his will. "Says she pushed the team onto the icefall late in the season against her own judgment, to save time, to save her fee. Says four men died in a collapse and she walked away. That's the official version. It's written down in a report some junior researcher signed off on, and it's been repeated so many times it's worn smooth as river stone." He flicked the cigarette into the wake and watched the water take it. "My father said the report was bought. Said Amara warned them — that she told the team lead the route was rotten, that she refused to go the shortcut way, and that the team lead ordered it anyway because Halloran's schedule mattered more than Halloran's people. And when the mountain did what mountains do, the company needed the blame to land somewhere it wouldn't cost them anything."

"So they buried her."

"They buried her twice." Dev held up two fingers. "Once in the ice — she went back up the next spring to recover the bodies, alone, because none of the hired men would go with her, and she never came down. And once in the story. Because a dead woman can't defend herself, and a dead woman with no family left to speak is easiest of all. Her daughter left the valley. Her husband died within the year. And the name Sesay became the word mothers here use when they want to frighten children away from the glacier. *Don't go up high, the ice will take you like it took her.* Thirty years of that." He shook his head. "I grew up believing she was a murderer. Believed it the way you believe the sun rises. Then my father got drunk one night — the only night I ever saw him drunk — and told me the other version. And I've been carrying both versions ever since, and I'll tell you what I've learned: the heavy one is almost always true."

The boat slammed a wave. Spray sheeted over them. Neither man moved.

"What happened to her granddaughter?" Noor asked. "If she had one."

Dev went still. Outside, the mist had thickened until the valley walls were only suggestions, gray shapes breathing beyond gray water. "Now that," he said slowly, "is a strange question to ask a stranger on a boat."

"It's a strange river," Noor said. "Strange questions seem appropriate."

Dev studied him — the careful face, the compass-shaped bulge in his jacket pocket, the way he sat like a man braced for news. Eleven years on the water taught you to read passengers, and this one read like a sealed letter. "Nobody knows," he said finally. "Some say the daughter took the child south. Some say there never was a child. But the old people in the high villages — my aunt among them — they say a girl comes down sometimes when the salmon run, buys salt and lamp oil, pays in old coins, and won't give a name. And they say she has her grandmother's eyes, which is to say eyes that look at you like the ice looks at you: patient, and certain, and not impressed." He smiled thinly. "Superstition, probably. But on this river I keep my superstitions fed. They're cheaper than insurance and more reliable."

Ahead, through the murk, a shape resolved: a bend in the valley where the cliffs pinched close and the water compressed itself into a single furious throat of white. Ilsa's voice carried from the wheelhouse, sharp with command, and the twins answered from below decks, and the whole boat became a single animal straining toward the gap.

Dev stood, knees flexing with the deck's motion. "Hold on to something," he said. "And hold on to your questions too. We'll be passing the lower camps soon — the good flat water above the gorge. That's where the Oyelaran lot staged from, if Ilsa's chart is honest. Whatever silence swallowed them, it started there." He paused at the rail, looking back at Noor, at the closed compass in his fist. "One more thing about Amara Sesay. When my grandfather spoke of her — and he only ever spoke of her once — he didn't call her a guide. He called her *the one who listened*. Said the mountain talked to her, and the shame of this valley is that when she tried to tell us what it said, we covered our ears and called it murder." He gripped the rail. "Remember that, upstream. If you hear something listening back."

The gorge took the boat then, and there was no more talk — only the roar of white water, Ilsa's voice hauling commands like rope, the hull shuddering between rock walls close enough to touch, green water exploding over the bow in freezing sheets. Dev worked the forward line with the twins' cousin, hauling and cleating and hauling again, his body doing what eleven years had trained it to do while his mind stayed with the story, circling it, the way his thoughts always circled it, a stone worn smooth by thirty years of the valley's turning.

They came through. Of course they came through; the Marta B always came through, that was her one virtue. Beyond the gorge the valley opened and the river gentled into long dark reaches, and the mist lifted just enough to show the first ridgelines going white above the treeline — the glacier country beginning, the high silent country where the expedition had gone to stop answering its radio.

It was Ferro who spotted it first, shouting from the wheelhouse roof: smoke. A thin gray thread rising from the far bank, maybe two kilometers on, where a shelf of flat ground met the water — the site of the expedition's lower camp, if Dev's memory of Ilsa's charts served.

Ilsa cut the engines to half. The sudden quiet rang in Dev's ears. Everyone came to the rails — Noor, pale and gripping the compass; the two gray-jacketed men with new alertness in their posture; Sanka with a pair of binoculars that he passed hand to hand without a word.

Through the glasses, Dev found the thread of smoke and followed it down to its source. Not a cookfire. Too much smoke, too black, too steady. Something burning that shouldn't burn — canvas, maybe, or paper, or worse.

"Well," Ilsa said beside him, her voice stripped of everything except arithmetic. "Either our missing doctors are alive and cooking lunch—" She lowered the binoculars. "—or somebody's cleaning up after them."

The Marta B pushed on toward the smoke, and Dev Chandra stood at the bow and felt the valley watching them come, the way it had watched thirty years ago, the way it would watch long after all of them were stones in its water. The river remembered. He only hoped, looking at the black thread rising against the gray sky, that it hadn't already begun writing down their names.

* * *

They made the bank a mile below the camp, in a lee of boulders where the current slackened, and Ilsa ordered the anchor set and the engines silenced. In the hush that followed, the valley's sounds arrived one by one, as if summoned: water worrying the hull, wind combing the high pines, and beneath it all — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable — the sound of something burning.

Dev went ashore with Ilsa and Noor, leaving the gray jackets aboard under Sanka's watchful eye and a pretext about inventory that fooled no one. The trail up from the bank was old and overgrown, a porter's path from some earlier era, and it climbed through wet fern to the shelf of flat ground where the expedition had built its lower camp.

They smelled it before they saw it. Burnt canvas. Burnt plastic. Underneath, faintly, something sweeter and far worse.

Then the trees opened, and the camp was there.

Tents slashed — not collapsed, not blown down, but cut open with deliberate strokes, their contents strewn and trampled. Crates split, their labels facing the sky like the bellies of dead fish. A radio mast leaned against a stump, snapped at its base. Ash everywhere, drifting in the light wind, settling on fern and stone and boot alike. At the center of it, a fire pit still smoldered, heaped with the blackened remains of notebooks, maps, film canisters, a typewriter ribbon pulled out and burned like a garland.

Noor made a sound Dev had never heard a grown man make — small, involuntary, torn out rather than spoken — and went down on his knees in the ash beside the fire pit, reaching into the wet outer ring of the burn where the flames hadn't fully taken. He came up with a charred notebook, its cover half gone, and opened it with hands that shook.

Dev watched him read. Watched the color leave his face and something else arrive in its place — recognition, raw and total, the look of a man seeing his own name written in a place it should never be.

"Noor," Ilsa said carefully. "What is it? What does it say?"

But Noor wasn't listening. He was staring at the handwriting on the ruined page, and when he finally spoke, his voice came out cracked and quiet, meant for no one and everyone:

"That's my father's hand."

The ash drifted down around them. Somewhere upslope, in the standing silence of the trees, a branch cracked — once, deliberately, like a door closing.

Dev's hand went to the machete at his belt, and the valley held its breath.
