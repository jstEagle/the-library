## 5. Monsoon Locks the Door

Ilsa Reiner had a rule about weather, and the rule was simple: you never argue with a river. You could argue with clients, with customs officers, with the drunk first mate of a rival salvage outfit who owed her four hundred dollars and his left ear. But the Kessler in monsoon season was not an argument she intended to lose, because losing it meant drowning, and drowning was bad for business.

She stood at the wheel of the *Marenka* at dawn and watched the sky do something she had only seen twice in twenty years on this water. The clouds did not roll in from the west, as they should have. They came down. They came off the high valleys like smoke poured from an overturned chimney, gray-green and boiling, swallowing the ridgelines one by one until the whole world above the river was simply *weather*, and the river below it went the color of wet iron.

"Dev," she called. "Get the tarp over the forward hold. Now."

Dev Chandra was already moving. That was one thing she'd say for him — he read her voice the way other men read instruments. He came aft along the gunwale with the rolled tarp on his shoulder like a coffin he'd gotten used to carrying.

The boy — Osei, or whatever his name was; the quiet one who'd bought his way aboard with cash and a story about a missing survey team — stood amidships holding the rail with both hands, watching the water rise up the banks. Ilsa had been watching him too, all week. Watching how he looked at the current. Watching how he flinched when they passed the second set of rapids. A man who knew rivers didn't flinch at rapids unless he was thinking about something else entirely.

She hadn't hired him for his honesty. She'd hired him because he had money and muscle and didn't ask questions, and because a boat running light ran stupid. But now, with the sky coming down like a lid, she found herself wishing she'd asked one question after all: *Who are you really running from, boy? Because somebody's secrets are about to cost me my hull.*

The rain began at seven minutes past six by her father's old watch, which she still carried though it hadn't run since the year she buried him. It did not begin the way rain begins in civilized places, with a few polite drops and a warning. It began the way a door slams. One moment the air was thick and breathless; the next, the river's surface was being hammered flat, millions of fists striking it at once, and the sound was so total that Ilsa's own thoughts had to shout to be heard inside her skull.

"Down to half throttle!" she roared at Dev. "We're not fighting this current blind."

"You want to anchor?" Dev shouted back, already hauling the throttle back.

"I want to *think*." Which was a lie. What she wanted was to be forty kilometers downstream with a dry deck and a bottle of arrack and no archaeologists' debts anywhere in her ledger. But the Kessler had already made that decision for her. The banks had gone soft and vertical, the side channels were torrents, and any stretch of gravel that might have taken her hull two days ago was now three meters underwater and moving at the speed of a freight train.

They nosed into the lee of a rock shelf around noon — if noon could be said to exist under that sky; it was more a rumor of light than light itself — and Ilsa killed the engine and listened.

Every captain knows her boat has a voice. The *Marenka* was thirty-one years old, flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, built for exactly this kind of lying river, and she talked constantly: the tick of the cooling engine, the creak of the cargo strapping, the small complaints of wood and rivet against water. Ilsa stood barefoot on the deck plates — she always went barefoot in bad weather, so the boat could speak through her feet — and what the *Marenka* said to her, low and urgent, was: *I am taking on water.*

Not much. Not yet. A whisper of it, seeping somewhere forward of the midships bulkhead, cold against the plates where cold shouldn't be.

"Dev. Forward compartment. Check the bilge."

He was back in ninety seconds, rain streaming off him, and his face told her everything before his mouth opened. "About ten centimeters. Rising slow. There's a seam weeping just past the fuel tank — I can't see it proper without pumping out."

"How long since she was surveyed?"

"Before I signed on. Captain, she held fine on the way up—"

"She held fine on the way up because the river wasn't trying to *bend* her." Ilsa wiped the rain out of her eyes with the heel of her hand and stared upstream, where the gorge narrowed toward the confluence and whatever waited there. "This water's got weight behind it now. Whole mountainsides draining into her. Every hour she pushes harder on the plating, and every hour that seam opens a little wider."

"So we turn back."

Isla laughed, and it came out ugly. "Turn back through *that*?" She gestured at the channel they'd come up, where the rapids they'd run that morning were now nothing but brown chaos, boulders surfacing and vanishing like animals in a fight. "You want to go down that backwards, in the dark, with a leaking hull? The river doesn't care which way we're pointed, Dev. Downstream is just a different way to die."

The boy had come aft without either of them noticing. He stood with the rain hammering his shoulders, and he said, quietly, in that careful accent of his, "There's a village. Sesay Landing, the journals called it. Maybe eight kilometers up. Deep-water bank, stone quay — the old expedition maps showed it."

"The old expedition maps," Ilsa repeated. "Which you have memorized. Of course you do."

Something flickered across his face — guilt, or its cousin — and Ilsa felt her fury find its target and sink its teeth in. Not at the river. Rivers were honest. At *this*. At the boy with his hidden reasons, at the dead expedition with its unpaid invoices, at the whole chain of other people's silences that had towed her, boat and crew and ledger, into the mouth of a monsoon.

"Here's what happens now," she said, loud enough to carry over the rain. "We run for this landing at quarter throttle, pumps going, everyone bailing who isn't steering. And when we get there, whoever knows something they haven't told me is going to tell me. Because I am done being the last person to find out things on my own boat. Are we understood?"

Neither of them answered, which was answer enough.

* * *

They reached Sesay Landing at dusk, or what passed for dusk, the sky so low and dark that the lamps they lit seemed to make the night deeper rather than less. The stone quay was older than anything else on the river — black blocks fitted without mortar, furred with moss, the steps worn into long shallow curves by generations of feet that had nothing to do with her or her problems. Above it, the village clung to the terraces: a scatter of tin roofs and drying racks, most of them shuttered against the rain, smoke standing straight up out of one chimney before the wind shredded it.

The *Marenka* came alongside with a groan Ilsa felt in her teeth. Dev threw lines to two men who materialized out of the rain in oilskins, and the boat settled against the stones, and Ilsa went below with a lamp to look her ship in the face.

The forward bilge had gained another four centimeters. She lay down on the deck plates, cheek almost to the wood, lamp held low, and found it: a seam just aft of the bow locker, maybe forty centimeters long, weeping in a thin silver thread that pulsed gently with the river's breathing against the hull. Not a gash. A wound going septic. The caulking had dried and shrunk sometime in the last few years — someone had skipped maintenance, and she had a sick suspicion she knew whose signature was on the invoice that said otherwise — and the monsoon had found the weakness the way water always finds weakness, patiently, without malice, absolutely without mercy.

She sat back on her heels in the lamplight and let herself feel it, just for a moment, all of it at once: the fear, cold and specific, sitting in her chest like ballast. Twenty years. Twenty years of reading this river, of knowing every sandbar between here and the delta the way she knew the scars on her own hands, and it could still reach up and put a finger through her livelihood in an afternoon. The *Marenka* wasn't just a boat. The *Marenka* was the only thing she had ever built that had stayed built. Her marriage hadn't. Her partnership with Vasquez hadn't. Her father's faith in her had gone down with his own hull in the flood of '09, and she had pulled herself out of that water alone, and everything she owned that mattered fit inside these thirty-one years of tarred planking and stubborn oak.

And now some seam nobody had caulked was going to take it away from her, forty centimeters at a time, while she sat trapped in a gorge full of other people's unfinished business.

She heard footsteps on the deck above and knew both sets: Dev's heavy, the boy's careful. She climbed up into the rain and faced them under the awning they'd rigged over the cockpit.

"Report," she said.

"Pumps are keeping pace," Dev said. "Barely. If the seam opens faster, we've got maybe—" he did the arithmetic with his eyes on the water, "—six hours of margin. Less if the river keeps rising."

"It'll keep rising." Ilsa looked upstream, where the gorge walls vanished into the rain. "Three days minimum before this system crests. Could be five. The old-timers say when the monsoon comes down the valleys instead of across them, it means the high passes are already socked in, and everything those mountains are holding is coming down here whether we like it or not."

"So we're locked in," the boy said.

"We're locked in." She said it flatly, and watched him absorb it, and noticed — not for the first time — that he didn't look afraid so much as *confirmed*, as if some private forecast of his had finally verified. That frightened her more than the leak did. People who expected disaster had usually arranged to meet it.

"Then here's how it's going to be." She planted her feet on her own deck, captain's stance, the one that had ended a hundred arguments before they started. "At first light, Dev strips that seam and re-caulks it with everything we've got — oakum, pitch, the epoxy from the dive kit, I don't care if he has to sew it shut with his hair. You—" she jabbed a finger at the boy, "—you're on bailing and supply inventory, and you're going to count every can and every liter twice, because if we're stuck here, we're stuck on what's aboard. And the villagers will want payment for moorage and water, and we will pay it, because I don't burn bridges in places where I might need to swim."

"And then?" the boy asked.

"And then," Ilsa said, "you're going to tell me why a man with survey-team money and expedition maps in his head flinches at rapids and goes pale at the word *Oyelaran*."

She saw it land. Saw it hit him square between the shoulder blades, saw his hands close on the rail, saw Dev turn to stare at him with the slow dawning of a man doing long-overdue math.

"I don't know what you mean," the boy said.

"Three nights ago, in the cook tent, Dev mentioned the missing expedition. Said the lead man's name. Dr. Kofi Oyelaran." She stepped closer, rain drumming on the awning above them like applause for a verdict. "You dropped a mug. Broke it clean in half. And you've been sleeping in your boots ever since, like a man who expects to run. So. One more time, and then I start making decisions without your input: *who are you?*"

The rain filled the silence. Downriver, unseen in the dark, the Kessler kept rising, patient as a creditor.

The boy looked at Dev, and Dev looked back with no help in his face at all. Then he looked at Ilsa, and she watched him arrive at the edge of something — she'd seen that look before, on men about to confess to crimes and men about to propose marriage, and it was the same look, because both were gambling everything on how the truth would be received.

"My name is Noor," he said. "Noor Oyelaran. Dr. Oyelaran is my father."

* * *

Later — much later, after Dev had finished swearing and gone below to sulk at the bilge pump, after the boy had told them the rest of it in a flat, stripped-down voice: the compass in the mail, the missed check-ins, the money owed, the lie at the hiring dock — Ilsa stood alone at the stern with her father's dead watch in her fist and the rain coming down like the sky meant to finish the job the river had started.

She ought to put him ashore. That was the sane move, the safe move, the move the woman she'd been at twenty-five would have made without blinking. A stowaway of secrets, a liar with a famous surname, tied to a hunt that had already swallowed one expedition whole — cut him loose, let the valley sort him out, keep her hull and her crew clear of it.

But the quay stones were slick and the village doors were shuttered and the nearest town with a road was nine days downstream through water that would kill her to cross. And beneath all of it, under the fury like a coin at the bottom of a well, was the thing she couldn't say aloud: that the boy had lied to her for exactly the same reason she'd once lied to a customs officer in a storm, and a debt collector in a flood, and her own dying father — because sometimes the truth costs more than a person can pay on the spot, and you promise yourself you'll settle up later, and later becomes never, and the interest compounds in the dark.

She knew about interest compounding in the dark. She was still paying hers.

Below her feet, faint through the deck plates, she felt the *Marenka* whisper again — the seam, working, opening by hairs. Six hours of margin, Dev had said. Maybe less.

Ilsa closed her fist around the dead watch and looked upriver, into the black throat of the gorge, where the rain fell without end and the water climbed the stones centimeter by centimeter, and somewhere beyond the dark, past the confluence, past the icefall, a shrine or a grave or a fortune was waiting with her name on none of it and her boat's fate written all over it.

"Fine," she said to the rain, to the river, to the boy asleep in her fo'c'sle with his father's compass. "But everybody settles up. Everybody. Even the dead."

Behind her, at the landward end of the quay, a lantern came alight in a window that had been dark all evening — and beside it, briefly, unmistakably, the silhouette of a figure standing motionless on the ridge above the village, watching the boat, before the rain took even that away.
