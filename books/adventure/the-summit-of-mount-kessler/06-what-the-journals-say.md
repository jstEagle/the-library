## 6. What the Journals Say

The rain came down like a verdict.

It hammered the tarp Ilsa had rigged over the fire pit until the canvas drummed a rhythm no drummer could sustain, and beneath it the salvaged journals lay spread out like patients on a ward — propped open with sticks, fanned toward the heat, their swollen pages steaming faintly where the flames licked close enough to dry but not close enough to burn. Noor had counted them twice: eleven field notebooks, four data ledgers, one leather-bound volume that was older than the rest and heavier, its spine cracked into segments like the vertebrae of some small animal. That one he had set apart from the others without quite deciding to. His hands had made the decision for him.

The lower camp had given up its dead quietly. Radios with their casings pried open and their guts gone green with water. A mess tent collapsed under the weight of standing water, its table still set, plates furred with silt. And in the supply tent, sealed inside an ammunition box that someone — his father, almost certainly; the packing was obsessive, each notebook wrapped in waxed cloth, each bundle labeled in that upright, unhurried hand Noor had spent nine years trying to forget — someone had preserved the expedition's mind if not its body.

Eleven notebooks. He turned the first one over. The cover read, in block capitals: *OYELARAN EXPEDITION — KESSLER SOURCE SURVEY — DAY 1*.

Day 1. Which meant there were more somewhere, or fewer than there should have been, or — he refused to finish the thought — none at all beyond this camp.

"You going to read them or baptize them?" Dev said, dropping onto the log beside him with two enamel mugs. The tea was terrible, overboiled and tasting faintly of the river it had been drawn from, which meant it tasted of everything the river carried: glacier flour, pine needles, and higher up, Noor didn't let himself think what else. "Ilsa says two hours by the fire before they're safe to touch. She also says we're burning diesel to dry paper while our hull sits on a gravel bar with a hole in it, so drink fast."

Noor drank fast.

The fire cracked a knot and threw sparks against the underside of the tarp. Beyond the ring of light the camp dissolved into rain and black trees, and beyond the trees the Kessler spoke to itself in the dark, low and continuous, a voice that never needed to breathe. Somewhere upstream — three days' climb, maybe four, if Sena-whatever-her-name-was's ridge line held — his father was either alive or he wasn't, and every hour the river rose was an hour subtracted from the answer.

He opened Day 1.

The handwriting hit him first, before any words did. It hadn't changed. That was the obscene thing about handwriting, Noor thought — faces aged, voices thickened, whole philosophies rotted through, but the hand stayed itself. The same forward-leaning capitals. The same habit of crossing his *t*'s with a single impatient stroke that overshot the letter entirely, as if even his alphabet were always leaving somewhere in a hurry. Noor had grown up watching that hand move across kitchen tables, across grading sheets, across the backs of envelopes on which his father sketched stratigraphy instead of writing birthday cards. Nine years of silence, and here it was again, leaning toward him out of the steam, asking to be read.

*Day 1. Base established below the confluence. Reiner's invoice settled at last — she drove a hard bargain and deserved every cent of it. Crew morale good. Weather window narrow; we move tomorrow.*

No mention of a son. No mention of the compass. Noor told himself he hadn't expected one and found that his chest disagreed.

* * *

By midnight the fire had been fed twice and the rain had settled into something steadier, less furious, as though the sky had made its point and now intended to keep making it indefinitely. Ilsa slept in the boat, or pretended to, one boot hanging off the gunwale like a man who could be moving in four seconds. Dev sat across the fire sharpening a machete that did not need sharpening, because Dev processed worry through his hands the way other people processed it through prayer.

Noor had reached Day 19.

The early entries were exactly what he'd expected, and hating them had been almost comfortable: survey grids, sediment samples, the dry liturgical language of archaeology, *context* and *provenience* and *in situ*, his father hiding inside his vocabulary the way he'd always hidden inside it. But around Day 12 the vocabulary began to crack, and through the cracks came a different voice.

*Day 12. Showed the crew the 1994 survey maps today. Mbeke asked the question I knew someone would ask eventually: why here? Why does a man spend thirty years and his own money looking for a shrine nobody can prove exists? I gave him the funding answer. Halloran legacy grant, cultural remediation, etc. He accepted it the way you accept weather. I am becoming a liar of the first order, and the worst kind — the kind who lies fluently to men who trust him.*

Noor read that entry three times.

Then he read it aloud, without meaning to, half a whisper, and Dev's machete stopped moving.

"Legacy grant," Dev repeated. His voice had gone flat in a way Noor was learning to recognize as Dev's version of alarm. "Halloran Mining gives money to study the mountain they gutted? That's like the wolf endowing a scholarship for sheep."

"Keep reading," Ilsa said from the boat. She hadn't been asleep after all. Her voice came out of the dark with the mildness of a drawn blade. "Whatever it is, I want all of it."

So Noor kept reading. By the light of a fire that hissed whenever the rain found a gap in the tarp, he read his father's secret out loud to strangers, night by night, day by day, and the expedition on the page peeled away from the expedition he'd assumed, the way bark peels from wood, revealing something underneath that was both stranger and sadder.

*Day 15. Found the first marker stone above the second gorge. Sesay carvings — the spiral-and-river motif documented once, in 1961, and dismissed as decorative. It is not decorative. It is a boundary sign. The shrine tradition marks its approaches. Amara Sesay knew this route intimately, which means her accusers never understood what they were condemning her for knowing.*

*Day 17. Mbeke found a second stone. I sat with it for an hour. Thirty years I have told myself the 1994 report was defensible — that the evidence was ambiguous, that I merely declined to tilt at windmills. Sitting here, I know better. I have always known better. Ambiguity is a luxury. Someone paid me to afford it.*

Noor stopped reading.

The fire popped. Downriver, impossibly far, thunder rolled along the valley floor like furniture being dragged across heaven.

"Paid you," Dev said softly. "Paid *him*. To do what?"

"I don't know yet." Noor's voice came out wrong, scraped. He wet his throat with tea that tasted of river. "Keep — I'm keeping reading."

But his hands weren't steady, and the next pages blurred, and he had to hold the journal at arm's length the way his father held menus, an inheritance of gesture Noor had mocked for years and now performed without noticing, and the words swam and then resolved:

*Day 21. Rain. Wrote letters tonight I will not send. One to my son. Got as far as "I owe you an explanation" and understood that explanation is precisely what I am owed nothing of. Burned it. The valley has enough smoke in it already.*

Noor closed the journal.

For a long moment he simply breathed, and the rain breathed with him, and the fire leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered, like something trying to stand up in a wind only it could feel.

"There's a letter," he said finally. "There was a letter. He burned it."

Dev said nothing. Ilsa, from the darkness of the boat, said nothing. The silence of people who understand that some sentences are not addressed to them.

* * *

He read through the small hours. The others slept in shifts — Dev first, curled under a groundsheet with the machete against his palm like a child with a toy soldier, then Ilsa, who slept the way she captained, economically, waking at every change in the rain's intensity as though the sky were reporting to her personally. Noor did not sleep. Sleep felt like a country he'd lost his passport to. He fed the fire and turned pages and watched his father's soul come apart on paper, entry by entry, and what frightened him most was not what the journal confessed but how familiar the confession sounded — because it was his own voice, thirty years older, saying the things Noor had said about him for nine years in bars and dig-site tents and the privacy of his own skull: *he's a coward, he hides behind work, he'd rather excavate a dead civilization than face a living son.* Every sin Noor had catalogued in his father, the journal catalogued too, in the father's own hand, with a precision Noor had never managed.

*Day 26. Crossed the third gorge. Lost a week to the collapse — the route Amara used is gone; the mountain has eaten its own memory. But the letters describe a high path, a shepherd's way, and if the stones mark it correctly we may still reach the shrine before the monsoon seals everything. I am racing a calendar I helped write. There is a sentence I never thought I'd say.*

*Day 28. Told the crew the truth tonight. All of it. Sat them down at this same fire and read them the 1994 report — my report — and told them what Halloran paid for and what I signed. Mbeke looked at me for a long time and then said, "So we are not finding a shrine. We are returning one." I could not have said it better. I would have given anything not to need it said at all.*

Noor's breath caught audibly. Across the ashes, Ilsa opened one eye.

"The 1994 report," she said. Not a question. She'd been listening longer than he'd known.

"You knew the name Sesay," Noor said. "On the boat. You said the valley curses it."

"I said the valley curses it." Ilsa sat up, and the firelight found the grey at her temples and the lines around her mouth that were not age so much as weather. "I didn't say what for, because it wasn't mine to tell, and because I've run cargo on this river eleven years and learned that everyone's secrets are cargo too, and you don't open other people's crates." She nodded at the journal in his hands. "But yours appears to be open."

So he told them. Not everything — not the compass, not yet, not the nine years, not the birthday cards that never came — but the shape of it: that the missing archaeologist whose debts had bought Noor's passage was Dr. Kofi Oyelaran, and that Dr. Kofi Oyelaran was his father, and that he had boarded this boat lying about all of it, and that the lie had died in a flooded tent three days ago when he'd recognized a *t* crossed too long.

Dev whistled, low and tuneless. Ilsa stared into the fire for a long moment, doing arithmetic Noor could almost see — salvage claims, sunk costs, a hull with a hole in it, a client who was now a fugitive's son.

"You lied to me for a week," she said at last.

"Yes."

"On my boat. In my water."

"Yes."

She considered this with the gravity of a woman weighing anchor. Then: "You dried my journals." A pause. "We'll call it even when we're off this river. Until then, read."

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it the last entries.

The final notebook was different from the others — smaller, older, its cover soft with handling. Noor had saved it deliberately, the way you save the last of anything, and now, with the rain thinning to a grey whisper and the fire down to coals, he opened it and understood within a sentence that it was not an expedition journal at all.

It was a copy. Page after page, in his father's careful hand, transcribed from something else: a series of letters, dated across decades, written in a voice that was nothing like his father's — warmer, rougher, full of valley idiom, the sentences running like the river itself, taking the long way around every obstacle. At the top of the first page, a heading:

*Letters of Amara Sesay, as preserved by E. Vann, 1971–1989. Copied at the National Archive, with permission of the Sesay family, [date illegible].*

And below it, the first letter, and Noor read it and the morning stopped.

*They ask me why I go up when the ice opens. I tell them the mountain keeps accounts. My mother kept accounts for the whole valley — births, debts, whose cow ate whose hay — and when she died the book went with her, and since then the mountain keeps them. Somebody has to go up and check the ledger. Somebody has to remember what the ice is holding.*

"She was real," Noor said. His voice sounded strange to him, young. "Not a name. Not a curse. She wrote letters. She — she checked the ledger."

"That's what shrines are," Dev said quietly. He had woken without Noor noticing and was sitting with his knees drawn up, watching the coals. "Everywhere my grandmother's people walked, there's a stone like that. Not worship. Bookkeeping. The mountain remembers so we don't have to carry it alone." He glanced at Noor. "Your father copied her letters by hand. Archives don't allow photocopies of family papers sometimes. So he sat there and copied every word."

"Why?" Ilsa said. "Why does a man do that?"

Noor turned to the back of the notebook, where the transcription ended and his father's own hand resumed — not an entry, but a single page, written differently, slower, the letters pressed deep enough to emboss the following sheet. A draft. Never sent, never burned. Kept.

*I have begun to understand what I am actually doing here. Everyone believes I am searching for a shrine. I am not searching for a shrine. I am searching for the moment in 1994 when I read Amara Sesay's field notes — notes the company confiscated within a week of the disaster, notes I was told were "inconclusive" — and I signed my name anyway. Four men died on a shortcut Halloran ordered to save eleven days of haulage. Amara went up ahead of them to turn them back. She did not come down. And I took Halloran's fee and wrote that she had misjudged the route, that the deaths were the result of guide error, and I buried the truth in the same grave as her body, and I built a career on top of it, and I called the building scholarship.*

*The shrine holds her field notes. The valley tradition is explicit: what is given to the mountain is kept by the mountain. If her notes survive — if any of it survives — then the ledger can be corrected. Not in a court. Courts want witnesses alive and evidence notarized, and Halloran owns both markets. But a valley is not a court. A valley is a memory with borders. And memories can be corrected by anyone willing to speak.*

*I sent my son a compass because I could not send him the sentence I just wrote. If he is reading this, then he came anyway, without being asked, which is more than I deserve and exactly what I hoped.*

*If I do not come down, the notes must. Whatever happens to me, the notes must come down.*

Noor lowered the page.

The rain had stopped. He hadn't noticed when. The valley stood dripping and enormous in the early light, the river running high and brown and patient, and above the treeline, far up, a smudge of white that might have been cloud or might have been the glacier, holding whatever it held.

"He knew," Noor said. "Before all this. Before the camp was hit. He knew they'd come for it — Halloran — and he packed the journals in the ammo box and—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "He planned for us to find this. Or anyone. Anyone but them."

"And them," Ilsa said, rising, nodding downstream.

Noor followed her gaze. On the far bank, small with distance, two figures stood at the water's edge where no figures should have been — still, deliberate, facing the camp across the swollen river. Even at that distance Noor could see the glint of optics raised to study them.

One of the men lifted a hand. Not a wave. A measurement.

"They're counting us," Dev whispered. "Why would they count us unless they're coming across?"
