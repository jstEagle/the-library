## 7. The Stranger on the Ridge

Dev Chandra had grown up believing that rivers had moods the way people did, and that a river's mood was never really about water. The Kessler in flood was not angry; it was *occupied*, preoccupied, thinking about something else entirely, something enormous and slow that no one standing on its banks could see. His grandmother had told him this when he was small enough to sit inside the curve of her arm while she shelled beans on the veranda. The river is listening to the mountain, she'd said. We only hear what spills over.

Thirty years later, standing at the rail of the *Marianne B* as she wallowed upriver through brown chaos, Dev thought his grandmother had undersold it. The Kessler wasn't spilling over. It was everywhere. It had eaten the landing stages, drowned the fish traps, turned the terraced fields along the banks into brown lakes with the tops of banana trees poking out like drowning men's fingers. And still it climbed.

He worked the winch because working was better than thinking. Ilsa had them running shifts around the clock now — two hours on the engine, two hours on the pumps, four hours trying to sleep in a cabin that smelled of diesel and wet canvas — because the leak they'd patched below the waterline was patching itself slower than the river could find new ways in. The boat groaned constantly, a low animal complaint from her keel, and every crew member had developed their own private superstition about which groan meant what. Dev's was simple: if the groan came with a shudder, you moved. If it didn't, you let her talk.

"You're staring at the ridge again," said Marta, coming up beside him with two tin mugs of tea that tasted mostly of tannin and rust. She handed him one. "Third time today."

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring."

He drank. Above the western bank the ridgeline ran green and knife-edged, black forest climbing into cloud, and where the cloud broke there was grass — high meadow country, the kind of ground shepherds used in the dry season and nobody used at all during the monsoon. Nobody human, anyway. Dev had been running this river for eleven years and he knew the rules of the valley the way he knew the rules of cards: the lowlands belonged to the water, the midlands to the towns, and the heights to whatever the towns preferred not to name.

"Oyelaran," he said — the younger one, the quiet one who'd come aboard claiming to be a digger for hire and whose hands were too clean for it — "asked me yesterday whether the Sesay shrine was a real place or a story."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That it's both." Dev watched the ridge. "Everything up there is both. That's the whole trouble with this valley."

* * *

It happened on the fourth day above the last village, in the grey hour before full light, when the mist off the water was thick enough to chew.

Dev had the dawn watch. He liked it best — the river at its most honest, before the sun burned the mystery off everything — and he stood at the bow with his tea going cold, watching the current sort itself into channels around the drowned snags, when the mist thinned for perhaps ten seconds against the eastern slope.

The figure stood on the ridge shoulder where the forest gave way to scree. Black against grey-green, upright, still. Not moving the way animals move, hunched and busy. Standing the way people stand when they have stopped to look at something — and the something, Dev understood with a coldness that started in his scalp and worked downward, was the boat. Was him.

Ten seconds. Then the mist closed like a door.

He did not shout. That was the first strange thing, and he knew it even as it happened: an eleven-year river man should have bellowed, woken the deck, pointed. Instead he stood with his mug halfway to his mouth and his heart going like a drum in a procession, and he said nothing, because some old instinct older than sense told him that naming it would make it real, and making it real would make it *near*.

When the mist opened again the ridge was empty.

* * *

He lasted until noon before he told anyone, and then he made the mistake of telling everyone at once, because Ilsa insisted on full crew briefings and because keeping it in was like swallowing a stone that grew.

"A person," he said. "On the ridge. Watching us. Gone when the mist shifted."

They were crowded under the awning aft, rain drumming on the canvas overhead like applause from a very large, very indifferent audience. Ilsa sat with the chart across her knees, pencil behind her ear. Marta was mending a net she hadn't used in weeks. Noor Oyelaran sat slightly apart, as he always did, a notebook open on his knee that he pretended wasn't a journal.

"Poachers," Ilsa said, without looking up. "Or smugglers. There's a route crosses the high country this time of year — jade, sometimes tiger parts, sometimes people. They watch traffic. It doesn't mean they're interested in us."

"It stood," Dev said. "For ten seconds. Animals don't stand like that. Poachers don't either — poachers crouch, poachers move. This stood like it was *waiting*."

"Then it waited, saw we were a leaking salvage tub with nothing worth stealing, and left." She looked up then, and her eyes were kind in the hard way that all her kindness was hard. "Dev. I need your head working on bilge schedules, not ghosts."

"It's not a ghost," Dev said. Then, because the valley got into your blood whether you were born in it or just ran its river long enough: "But you should know what the villages say. About Amara Sesay."

Noor's pen stopped.

"They say she walks the high ground," Dev went on, quieter, because saying it aloud in daylight felt like whistling past a graveyard. "Not her body — her *watching*. Thirty years ago they blamed her for those deaths on the Halloran expedition, and the valley has been cursing her name ever since, and the story grew a shadow. People say if you go up past the treeline without leave, someone stands on the ridge and watches you. Counts you. And if your business up there is dishonest—" he shrugged, "—the weather finds out before you do."

Silence, except the rain.

"That's a story mothers use to keep children off the icefalls," Ilsa said finally. But she said it carefully, the way you handle something you suspect might be load-bearing.

"Most true things are," Dev said.

* * *

That evening the mist behaved strangely.

Dev noticed it first as a wrongness in the sound — the river noise flattening, the rain softening, the whole world going acoustically close, as though the valley had drawn a curtain. Then the mist itself came down off the slopes in ropes and banners, not rising off the water as it should but descending, pouring downhill like something spilled, and within twenty minutes the *Marianne B* sat inside a white room with walls you couldn't see but could feel.

Ilsa anchored her mid-channel rather than risk the snags blind. Watches doubled. And it was during the second watch, near midnight, that Dev heard the whistle.

Three notes. Low, then lower, then a third that bent upward at the end like a question. It came from the east bank, from somewhere upslope, and it was answered — this was the part that put ice in his spine — by nothing, by silence, by a silence so deliberate it functioned as a reply.

He woke Noor.

He didn't fully decide to. He simply found himself crouched in the corridor outside the younger man's bunk, shaking his shoulder, and Noor came awake the way soldiers wake, instantly and silently, which told Dev something about him that his clean hands never had.

"The ridge," Dev whispered. "There's someone on the ridge. Whistling."

Noor dressed without a word. They went up together, and the mist had thinned to rags, and the moon was a smear of silver behind moving cloud, and the ridge stood revealed in bands — black forest, pale scree, black sky — and there, at the seam between forest and scree, a shape. Closer tonight. Lower on the slope. Still.

Then it raised one arm, and Dev's breath stopped — but it wasn't a weapon, wasn't a signal. The figure held something up toward the boat, held it out at arm's length as if offering it, or showing it, and held it there for the length of three heartbeats, and then the mist took the whole picture away like a hand closing a book.

"What did it hold up?" Noor asked. His voice was steady. His hands, Dev noticed, were not.

"Small. Dark. Flat-ish." Dev rubbed his face. "A slate? A piece of bark?"

Neither of them slept after that, and neither of them said anything more about it either, which was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

In the morning the mist lifted all at once, the way it does when the wind changes its mind, and the valley appeared scrubbed and glittering and innocent, and Ilsa ordered the boat underway before the fog could reconsider. They made good distance through the morning — six, seven miles of grinding progress against a current that fought like a live thing — and it was Noor who found the sign, because Noor had spent the morning doing what he'd quietly done every day since the silent camp: reading the banks.

He called Dev over to the port rail around midday. Below a scree fan where a side-stream cut down to the river, a shelf of flat ground ran along the waterline — old shepherd path, probably, the kind that stitched the high valleys together before the roads died. Mud lay on it in a broad ribbon where the stream had flooded over, and the mud was printed.

One print. Just one, at the edge of the ribbon where the flood hadn't reached. A boot, Dev saw when they beached the skiff and waded in to look — a proper boot, hobnailed or lugged, larger than Dev's own, planted deep at the toe as if the wearer had paused there facing the water. Facing the boat's exact line of travel.

"One man," Noor said, crouching. "Heavy. Stood here recently — the edges haven't slumped. Hours, not days." He looked up the slope, following the line of the print's gaze, and Dev followed his look and found what Noor found: a thread of flattened grass angling up through the scree, a path worn by feet but not by hooves, disappearing into the cloud.

"Poachers don't leave prints pointing at passing boats," Dev said. "Poachers hide their tracks. This is someone who wanted to be *known*."

Noor didn't answer. He was scanning the mud around the print in slow arcs, and Dev watched his face change — watched the archaeologist surface through the worried son, the professional attention narrowing everything else away — and then Noor went very still and reached out and lifted something from the mud with both hands, careful as a man lifting a bird with a broken wing.

It was a wooden token, palm-sized, carved from dark dense wood gone silver-grey with weather. A disc, with a spiral cut into its face — not decorative spiraling but deliberate, coiling inward toward a center that had been drilled with a single hole, and around the rim, tiny figures: stick-people, stick-mountains, a stick-river rendered in strokes so fine they might have been done with a blade the width of a hair.

Dev knew it. That was the terrible thing. He knew it the way you know a face in a funeral photograph.

"Sesay work," he said. His voice came out wrong. "My grandfather traded with the Sesay family — guides, carvers, the whole clan worked the high routes. They cut tokens like that for trail-markers. You hang them at a junction and the spiral points the safe way." He swallowed. "That carving style died with Amara. Everyone says so. Her whole family scattered after the scandal, the craft went with them—"

He stopped, because Noor had gone white.

"What?" Dev said. "What is it?"

Noor turned the token over. On the reverse, scratched shallow and recent, cutting through the old silvered patina so the raw wood showed pale beneath: a single letter. *K.*

"My father," Noor said. "Kofi. He had tokens like this — I've seen them in his photographs, decades old, from his early fieldwork. He kept one on his desk my whole childhood. I used to think it was a coaster." His thumb hovered over the fresh scratch without touching it. "This mark is new. Days old. Someone carried this token up that path, stood here, and left it where a boat heading upriver couldn't miss it."

"Or dropped it," Dev said, without conviction.

"No." Noor's voice had gone quiet and certain in a way that frightened Dev more than the ghost stories had. "Dropped things fall. This was placed. Face-up. At eye-line from the water." He looked up the slope again, at the cloud, at the invisible ridge. "Someone knows we're coming. Someone knows *who* we are. And they're not hiding anymore."

Above them, at the top of the scree, the mist tore for a moment — and Dev, following the pull of it with his eyes, saw the figure again, or believed he did: upright, patient, at the exact place where the grass-thread path crossed the ridgeline. It did not move. It did not need to. It had shown them the token; the rest was courtesy.

Then the cloud sealed, and the ridge was only a ridge, and the river went on roaring past with all its occupied, enormous indifference, carrying them upstream toward whatever was waiting at the top of the spiral.

Dev stood in the mud with his heart hammering and understood, with total clarity, that he no longer believed in ghosts. Ghosts were easier. Ghosts didn't leave boot prints, and ghosts didn't know your passengers' names, and ghosts — whatever the villages said about Amara Sesay walking the high ground — did not scratch a living man's initial into thirty-year-old wood.

Whatever watched them from the ridge had flesh, and a purpose, and a family grievance older than Dev's entire career on this river.

"We should show Ilsa," he said.

Noor closed his fist around the token. "Yes," he said. "And Dev — when we do, there's something else I have to tell her. Both of you. All of it."

Downstream, faint through the roar of the water, came three notes of a whistle. Low, then lower, then bending upward at the end.

Like a question. Like an invitation. Like a door, opening.
