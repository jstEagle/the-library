## 8. Halloran's Men

Ilsa Reiner had learned to read weather the way other people read faces, and for three days the sky had been telling her a lie. It sat there above the Kessler gorge, pale and reasonable, promising nothing, while behind the western ridgeline it stacked its real intentions in towers of bruised cloud that she could feel pressing on her ears like a change of depth. The river had come up another half meter overnight. She'd marked it with grease pencil on the pilothouse glass — a habit from her father, who'd run timber down the Elbe and believed every river kept honest books if you checked them daily. The Kessler's books were getting ugly.

She stood at the wheel now with coffee gone cold in her fist, watching the bank slide past: black basalt, hemlock dripping, the water so high it had drowned the willow scrub and turned it into a field of green heads bobbing in the current. Somewhere below her feet the patched hull was doing its work, tarp and epoxy and prayer holding back the gray pressure of the river. The pump cycled every four minutes now instead of six. She had not told anyone that.

Behind her, in the wheelhouse's cramped aft corner, Dev was rewrapping his hands with the methodical patience of a man who'd grown up watching his mother do it before temple. Noor Oyelaran sat on the engine cover with the salvaged journals stacked on his knees like a sleeping child he didn't dare wake. He'd been reading them for two days, and Ilsa had noticed what she suspected no one else had: the boy read the way a man reads letters from home when home is a place that hurt him. Slowly. With his thumb pressed flat against the page as if to keep the words from moving.

"You're going to wear through that page," she said.

Noor looked up. He had his father's face, she'd decided — the same long jaw, the same deep-set eyes — though he seemed to have made an agreement with himself never to use it for the same purposes.

"I'm going to know what's in it," he said.

"That's what worries me." She nodded at the journals. "Men who go looking for truth upriver usually find something they wish they'd left downstream."

Dev snorted softly. "Captain talks like the valley," he said. "Everything is a warning."

"The valley is mostly warnings." Ilsa lifted her binoculars and swept the bend ahead, where the river narrowed between walls of stone and the current stood up in long gray ridges like hackles. "The rest is history. Same thing, really."

That was when she saw the smoke.

It rose thin and civil from the gravel bar at the mouth of Sengal Creek — one fire, built small and neat, the kind of fire a survey crew or a fishing party makes. Not the sprawling smudge of poachers, who burn big and don't care who sees. This fire wanted to be seen. It wanted, specifically, to be seen by her.

"Dev," she said quietly. "Get the rifle out of the locker under the chart table. Don't make a show of it."

Dev's hands stopped mid-wrap. He looked at the smoke, then at her face, and whatever he found there made him move without another word.

Noor stood. "What is it?"

"Company," Ilsa said. "Or something wearing company's clothes."

* * *

They came around the bend slow, engine at idle, because Ilsa Reiner did not approach strange fires fast. The gravel bar resolved out of the mist in pieces: the fire, a canvas fly strung between two alders, two boats pulled up on the shingle — aluminum, new, outboard motors still caked with shipping grease. And two men standing at the waterline with the unhurried posture of people who have been waiting exactly as long as they intended to wait.

One was tall and gray-templed, with the build of a man who'd been athletic once and had since converted all that muscle into authority. He wore a rain shell with the hood down despite the drizzle, and beneath it a shirt too clean for the bush. The other was younger, wide through the shoulders, standing slightly behind and to the left in a way that said bodyguard without ever having to say it aloud. Both had rifles slung across their backs — carried openly, muzzle-down, the way you carry a weapon when you want everyone to see it but nobody to worry.

"Ahoy the boat!" The tall man raised an arm. His voice carried across the water easily, trained, warm at the edges. "Halloran Mining relief party! We're looking for survivors off the Oyelaran expedition!"

Ilsa brought the boat alongside the bar with the current quartering her stern, killed the engine, and let the momentum carry them in. She stepped down onto the shingle with the rifle broken open over her forearm, shells visible, the universal grammar of *I see yours, I'm not afraid, let's all be adults.*

"Reiner," she said. "Salvage. Out of Karst Harbor."

"Voss." He extended a hand. His grip was dry and firm and lasted exactly one beat longer than friendly. "Marcus Voss, Halloran Security and Logistics. This is Adeyemi." The younger man touched his hat brim. "We've been running the river since the check-ins went silent. You're the first vessel we've seen with expedition gear aboard."

"We pulled salvage off the lower camp," Ilsa said. "Journals, some instruments. Tents were cut. Radios dead."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — quick, professional, filed away. "Cut tents. Interesting. Any sign of the team itself?"

"No bodies, if that's what you're asking."

"It's always what we're asking." He smiled, and it was a good smile, white and even, the kind that has been practiced in mirrors until it forgot how to mean anything. "You understand our position, Captain. A famous archaeologist goes silent in Halloran's concession territory. The press will be here inside a week asking why the company let him die. Our job is to make sure that when they arrive, there's a tidy story waiting."

"And what story is that?"

"Rescue." Voss spread his hands, encompassing the bar, the fire, the river, the whole gray theater of it. "Halloran spares no expense. Halloran moves mountains. Halloran finds Dr. Oyelaran alive and brings him home." He tilted his head. "You have fuel?"

"Enough."

"Then here's my proposal. We travel together. Your boat draws shallower than ours; you know this water. We provide security, satellite comms, medical if you need it." He gestured at the patched hull, and Ilsa felt the observation land exactly where he'd aimed it — he'd seen the tarp, seen the pump marks, done the arithmetic of her situation in the time it took to shake her hand. "Nobody comes down this river alone in monsoon season. That's just sense."

It was good sense. That was the trap of it. Every word the man said was reasonable, generous even, and every word of it was also a hook set quietly in dark water.

Ilsa glanced back at the boat. Dev stood in the doorway with his wrapped hands folded, watching the younger one, Adeyemi, the way a dog watches a stranger's hands. Noor had come down to the rail, and she saw Voss register him — saw the tall man's gaze snag on the journals under the boy's arm and stay there a half-second too long.

"Those are company property concerns," Voss said pleasantly. "Expedition records. Halloran sponsored the original survey work thirty years ago; we have archival obligations."

"They're waterlogged paper," Ilsa said. "Salvage rights attach to recovered property. Ask any harbor court."

"I intend to avoid courts entirely." Still smiling. "That's rather the point of cooperation."

* * *

They ate together because refusing would have been its own answer. Voss produced tinned ham, real coffee, chocolate — supplies that said *we planned for weeks, not days* — and talked while they ate, a low steady stream of it, about river levels and concession boundaries and the price of copper, and underneath the talk, like a current under a calm surface, ran the questions. Where had they found the camp. What condition were the journals in. Had anyone mentioned the Sesay name. That last one he asked lightly, almost idly, spooning sugar into his cup, and Ilsa watched Noor's face across the fire and saw nothing there at all, which was itself an answer, and she saw Voss see it too.

When the meal was done, Voss walked the bank with her, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting an orchard.

"Your hull," he said. "I heard the pump when you came in. Four-minute cycle?"

"Five."

He smiled at the lie, kindly. "Captain. Let me tell you how this river works. Above the confluence there's a gorge section where the water doubles its gradient. In monsoon, no boat your size goes up it. No boat comes down it either. Which means everything north of the confluence is effectively closed until the rains end — closed to you, closed to us, closed to whoever is up there right now with your missing archaeologist." He stopped walking and turned to face her, and for the first time the warmth drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and more patient underneath. "Unless someone controls the confluence. Then the river isn't closed. It's owned."

"You're offering me partnership."

"I'm offering you honesty, which is rarer." He looked out at the water. "My employers spent a great deal of money on this valley thirty years ago, and something went wrong, and they have spent thirty years making sure the wrong thing stayed buried. I don't pretend to know all of what's up there. But I know this: whatever Dr. Oyelaran went looking for, Halloran wants it found by Halloran, or not found at all. And you, Captain, are a practical woman in a leaking boat with two passengers and a debt of your own. So I'll ask plainly. What's your price for the confluence?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked on the alder leaves. Downriver, somewhere beyond the bends, the monsoon was gathering itself like a debt coming due.

"My price," she said, "is that you get off my river."

Voss sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor conceding the scene. "I was afraid you'd say that." He didn't raise his voice. He simply turned his head slightly, and Adeyemi moved — not fast, not threatening, just inevitable, stepping to a position where his rifle hung between himself and Dev, and where the boat, with its three souls and its leaking hull, lay squarely within easy reach. "Then let me offer terms instead. We'll escort you upriver. For safety. The water's bad ahead, and we'd hate for anything to happen to your vessel." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Or anyone aboard her."

"And if I refuse the escort?"

"Then we'd be obliged to travel separately," Voss said, "and separately is dangerous, and accidents happen on rivers. Especially to boats with old patches and short fuel." He smiled again, and this time he let it be what it was. "Captain, I've read your file. Reiner Salvage owes the Oyelaran expedition eleven thousand against a charter that never completed. You're not up here out of charity. You're up here because you're owed. Halloran settles debts. All of them. Think about which side of the ledger you'd rather stand on."

* * *

They cast off at first light, and Ilsa stood at the wheel with Voss beside her in the wheelhouse — *invited*, as if it were his boat — while Adeyemi rode the bow with his rifle across his knees and Dev steered under watchful instruction and Noor stayed below with the journals, out of sight, out of reach, out of mind. She hoped.

The river rose to meet them, swollen and brown and full of the mountain's runoff, and the rain began again around midmorning, soft at first, then with intent. Ilsa drove through it with her jaw set, feeling the shape of the trap close around her with every kilometer: the escort ahead, the escort behind, the confluence waiting somewhere in the gray ahead like a door only one side gets to lock.

Voss drank her coffee and praised her boat and told stories about Lagos and Antofagasta, and between the stories he watched her hands on the wheel, and she understood that he was learning her — her tells, her habits, the exact limits of what she'd risk — the way a locksmith learns a lock.

"You're angry with me," he observed at one point, mildly.

"I'm angry with myself," Ilsa said. "I let you aboard. That was the mistake. Everything after is just consequences."

"Captain." He set down the cup and looked at her with something almost like respect. "In thirty years of doing this work, I've learned one thing worth passing on. Everyone believes they're the hero of their own river. They're not. They're cargo. The only question is who's carrying them, and toward what." He settled back, satisfied, watching the rain stitch silver threads across the brown water. "You're carrying something valuable, whether you know it yet or not. My job is to make sure it arrives."

Below decks, Ilsa knew, Noor Oyelaran was reading his father's handwriting by lamplight, learning things a corporation had already killed to keep quiet once. Ahead, the confluence waited, where two rivers met and became one loud, unownable voice.

She marked the rising water on the wheelhouse glass with her grease pencil, one more line in the Kessler's honest books, and began — quietly, in the privacy of her own skull, where no locksmith could reach it — to plan.
