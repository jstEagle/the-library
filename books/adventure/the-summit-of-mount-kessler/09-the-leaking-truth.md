## 9. The Leaking Truth

The rain had found a rhythm, and Noor had learned to hate it.

It came off the ridge in sheets, drumming the tarp Ilsa had rigged over the boat's midsection, filling the pauses between Halloran's men's voices with a sound like static. The two of them — Voss, the wide one with the rifle slung casual as a shoulder bag, and Marek, thin and precise, with the satellite phone he checked every hour on the hour — had made their camp twenty meters upstream, under a green tarp that snapped and cracked in the wind like something trying to escape. They were escorting the crew upriver. That was the word they used. *Escort.* As if the crew were dignitaries instead of hostages who hadn't been told yet.

Noor lay awake in the stern beneath the tarp, listening to the river gnaw at the bank, and told himself he was only getting up to check the mooring line.

The mooring line was fine. He went anyway, barefoot in the mud, because Dev was asleep and Ilsa slept like a cat with one ear open, and because there was a shape to the night — the way the rain thinned for a moment, the way the fire upstream had been fed recently, dry wood burning bright — that said someone was still awake over there. He crouched behind the boat's hull where it listed against the bank, close enough to the water that the cold came up through his knees, and he listened.

"—not a relief party," Marek was saying, voice pitched low but carrying anyway, the way voices do across water. "Nobody thinks we're a relief party. We're an assessment team."

"Assessment." Voss laughed without much sound in it. "That's what corporate called it?"

"That's what I'm calling it. There's a site upriver. Old man Oyelaran was looking for it before he went into the ice. If he found it, there'll be material. Documents. Carvings, maybe. Anything with dates or names on it."

"And if he didn't find it?"

"Then we find it first, and there's nothing to assess." A pause; the fire popped. "Halloran's position is simple. Thirty years ago some guide died up here and the company took the blame in the press for a season. It cost them the concession. Now a famous archaeologist goes missing hunting proof of — what? That she was framed? You know what that does. One headline. *Mining Giant Framed Local Guide, Records Show.* And suddenly every concession Halloran holds on this continent gets audited by every government with a grudge. It's not about one shrine. It's about the whole ledger."

Noor's hands had gone cold against the hull. Not from the water.

"So we burn it," Voss said. It wasn't a question. He said it the way a man says *so we eat.*

"We document nothing, we remove everything removable, and anything that won't come out of the rock—" Marek paused, and Noor heard him choose the word carefully, the way careful men do when the word is ugly, "—we make unrecordable. Fuel accelerates stone weathering. There are methods. The point is that by the time anyone credible reaches that valley, there should be no *there* there. Just another legend the mountain ate."

"And the crew?"

"What crew?" Marek said mildly. "We're escorting four civilians upriver for their own safety. If the river takes the boat, that's a tragedy. Rivers up here are dangerous. Everyone knows that."

The rain thickened again, mercifully, drowning the rest. But Noor had heard enough. More than enough. He stayed crouched in the mud until his legs cramped, watching the firelight waver on the far bank, watching the silhouette of Marek check his satellite phone — 0300, on the hour — and understanding, finally, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what kind of animal had hold of the boat's towline.

His father was alive somewhere above the treeline. Or he wasn't. Either way, Halloran's men intended to reach the shrine first, and leave nothing behind but a story with no evidence — and if four civilians became inconvenient, the river would take the blame, just as it always did, just as it had taken the blame for Amara Sesay thirty years ago while men with ledgers signed reports in warm offices.

*Amara Sesay.* Even the name arrived differently now. In the journals, dried page by page over Ilsa's stove, his father's handwriting had grown smaller and more urgent as the expedition climbed: *the Sesay shrine, if it exists, holds her field notes — she kept notes, Kessler's own letters confirm it — and her notes will show the shortcut was ordered, not chosen.* His father had been chasing redemption up this river like a man chasing a debt. And Noor had boarded this boat hiding behind a false name, ashamed of the old man, and now the shame had turned inside out and become something worse: he was sitting on the truth while armed men planned to erase it.

He went back to the stern and sat in the dark with the compass in his hand until dawn came gray through the rain.

* * *

He told them at first light, while Halloran's men were breaking their camp downstream of the smoke haze, out of easy earshot.

Ilsa stood at the wheel with her arms crossed, rain running off the brim of her cap, and Dev sat on the supply crates with his bandaged forearm cradled — the gash from the salvage winch, three days old, angry red at the edges now — and Noor stood in front of them both feeling like a man stepping off a ledge.

"My name isn't Noor Vance," he said. "It's Noor Oyelaran. Dr. Kofi Oyelaran is my father."

The silence lasted long enough for the rain to fill it twice.

"You're kidding me," Dev said.

"I've owed him money for nine years. He owed me an explanation. Neither of us ever collected." Noor pulled the compass from his jacket and held it out flat on his palm, brass worn to shine at the edges, the hinge loose, the needle trembling as if it could feel the confession too. "Three years ago this arrived in the post. No note. Nothing. I thought it was a taunt. An old man reminding me he knew where north was and I didn't." His voice caught and he hated it. "Last night I understood. He wasn't taunting me. He was asking. The only way he knew how."

Ilsa hadn't moved. Her eyes were the color of the river after a storm, and they did not blink. "You lied to me on my deck. For six days."

"Yes."

"I bargained with you. I gave you terms. I staked fuel and food and my hull on a stranger, and the stranger was the son of the man whose debts I'm chasing up this river in monsoon season."

"I know what it's worth," Noor said. "Deduct it. All of it. From whatever we find, from my father's estate, from my own hide if it comes to that. But hear the rest first, because the rest is why I'm telling you."

And he told them. The camp — the slashed tents, the dead radios, the journals drying over the stove, his father's handwriting marching up the pages toward the shrine. The figure on the ridge. The boot print. The carved wooden token, the same one from the photographs in his father's study a lifetime ago, the one Amara Sesay had carried. And then last night: Marek's voice across the water, level and unhurried, laying out the arithmetic of erasure. *Anything that won't come out of the rock, we make unrecordable.* The river taking the blame for four more bodies.

Dev had gone very still. When Noor finished, the younger man looked down at his bandaged arm as if seeing it fresh. "My grandmother used to spit when she said the name Sesay," he said quietly. "Cursed guide. Killed four people. That's the story everyone tells. That's the story *I* tell." He looked up. "And you're saying the story is a receipt somebody wrote."

"I'm saying my father signed part of it," Noor said. The words came out of him like splinters, each one leaving a hole. "There's an entry in the early journals — dated 1994, when he was twenty-six, a junior researcher on the Kessler survey. Halloran paid for the report. His name is on the section that attributed the deaths to guide error. Amara Sesay's error." He made himself keep going. "Everything since — thirty years of career, the expeditions, the papers — I think it was all one long attempt to unsign his own name. And when he couldn't do it alone, he mailed me a compass."

Ilsa finally moved. She uncrossed her arms, took the compass from his palm, and turned it over once, twice, reading it like a chart. Then she looked at him, and what was in her face wasn't forgiveness — it was recalculation, cold and fast, a captain replotting a course around a new reef.

"Here's what your confession buys you, Oyelaran," she said. "One hearing. You get it because the alternative is sailing upriver under the guns of men who intend to burn the thing your father spent thirty years looking for, with a crew that doesn't know why. That's not a crew. That's cargo." She handed the compass back. "But understand the position. Two rifles against our zero. A leaky hull. A river that wants us dead. And a passenger manifest that now includes the son of the man those rifles were sent to manage."

"They're not going to let us reach the shrine," Dev said. "Whatever we decide."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "They're not."

The rain hammered the tarp. Downstream, faint through the noise, Noor could hear Marek's voice on the satellite phone, clipped syllables, reporting on schedule. Somewhere above the treeline, in a cave or a crevasse or a grave, his father was either waiting or beyond waiting, and the only people standing between Halloran Mining and a clean slate were a disgraced salvage captain, an injured deckhand, a liar with a compass, and a valley that had cursed the wrong dead woman for thirty years.

"So we don't sail up their river," Noor said slowly, and the idea was arriving even as he spoke it, assembling itself out of everything Dev had told him around the cookfire, every story of the ridge-watcher, the boot print, the token. "We go where the boat can't. Someone has been watching us from the heights for days. Someone who knew to watch. If there's a path up there that Halloran's maps don't have—"

"The shepherd routes," Dev breathed. "The old ones. My grandmother said the high valleys were all walked, once, before the mine roads."

Isla was already shaking her head, but Noor saw her stop herself — saw her look at the leaking hull, at the rising brown water swallowing the bank, at the two men downstream breaking camp with the unhurried competence of people who owned the route — and he watched the arithmetic turn over behind her eyes and come up with a number she hated.

"If we run," she said, "we run tonight, in the worst of it, when their glasses can't see across the water. And we abandon the boat above the gorge and climb. In monsoon. With his arm." She jerked her chin at Dev. "Anyone who dies, I'm billing your father's estate."

"He'd pay it," Noor said. "He's good for debts. It's explanations he's cheap on."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Ilsa Reiner's face and drowned. She turned to the wheel, to the gray wall of rain, to the river running high and fast toward the confluence where Halloran's men would expect them to be tomorrow morning, obedient and escorted.

"Then pray the watcher on the ridge is friendlier than the ones with guns," she said. "Because at first light we're gone — and God help us, we're climbing into the clouds to find a ghost."

Above the ridgeline, unseen through the rain, the clouds parted for the space of ten seconds — and on the black spine of rock, a figure stood watching the boat, still as a carving, before the weather closed like a door.
