## 10. Mutiny at the Confluence

Ilsa Reiner had run the Kessler for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above every other rule: never let anyone else hold your tiller. Not a paying passenger, not a port official, not a man with a rifle and a corporate lanyard and a smile like a signed contract.

She was breaking that rule now, and it sat in her chest like swallowed river stone.

The Halloran men — Voss, the tall one with the quiet voice, and Brandt, who chewed the same toothpick until it was a splinter of gray wood — had installed themselves at the stern of her boat as though they'd bought her at auction. They rode upriver behind the lead skiff, their outboard droning through the rain, and Ilsa stood at her own wheel with their orders in her ears: *Confluence by tomorrow night. No stops. No detours.*

The Kessler did not take orders. That was the thing about rivers, she thought, watching the water climb another hand's width up her hull. You could command men all day long. The river only listened to itself.

* * *

They made camp that second night on a gravel bar shaped like a broken tooth, wedged between two walls of jungle that leaned over the water so far they seemed to be whispering to each other across it. The rain came down in ropes. Ilsa posted Dev on wood detail because his hands needed something to do besides shake, and put Noor on tarp duty because he was the only one aboard who could rig a shelter that didn't collect water like a cupped palm.

Kofi Oyelaran sat under the gunwale awning, wrapped in two blankets, looking like something the river had coughed up and hadn't finished deciding about. He hadn't spoken more than ten words since the cave. His eyes followed his son around the camp with an expression Ilsa recognized from thirty years of watching men look at things they'd lost and couldn't afford to want back.

Voss and Brandt ate separately, at the far end of the bar, under their own tarp, rifles across their knees. Voss had offered them rations from Halloran stores — good rations, sealed foil, coffee — and Ilsa had taken them because pride didn't float a boat. But she'd counted the packets. She always counted.

That was when she saw it. The arithmetic of the camp, laid out plain as a manifest:

Two armed men. One skiff, one leaky salvage boat. Four of hers, if you counted the old man, which after the cave she no longer did — not as cargo, anyway. And a storm coming down the valley behind them that Voss's radio had been chattering about all evening, a system the pilot in the skiff clearly wanted to outrun.

You couldn't outrun weather on the Kessler. She'd told Voss so twice. He'd smiled both times, the way a bank smiles.

Ilsa turned her tin cup slowly in her hands and began, very quietly, to plan a mutiny against the men holding guns, using nothing but a rotten boat, half a fuel drum, and the worst storm in nine years.

It was, she reflected, exactly the kind of bargain she'd sworn off making. And yet here she was, making it again, because the people on this boat had stopped being passengers somewhere above the Silent Camp and started being hers.

* * *

She found Dev first, stacking wet wood under the overhang where it would never dry, cursing at it in three languages.

"Your arm," she said quietly, nodding at the sling. "How bad?"

"It's fine."

"Dev."

He exhaled through his nose. "Torn, not broken. I can row if I have to. Badly."

"You won't have to." She crouched beside him, keeping her voice under the rain. "Listen. Tomorrow we hit the confluence. Big water, two rivers arguing, and the storm hits us there or just before. Voss wants to push through it because his boss wants that shrine before anyone else breathes on it."

"And you don't want to push through."

"I don't want to die for a mining company's conscience." She glanced toward the far end of the bar, where Brandt's silhouette hunched over the fire. "Here's what I see. Their skiff draws shallow. Ours draws deep but carries everything. In floodwater at the confluence, nobody's boat does what they promise. If we're separated —"

"You mean if we make ourselves separable."

"I mean," Ilsa said, "if the river decides things, which rivers do, then whoever's ready for the separation comes out of it owning their own route. I've run that confluence eleven times. There's a chute on the north side, past the drowned snag, that looks like death and isn't. Nobody takes it unless they know it. Their pilot doesn't know it."

Dev was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the tarp like fingers on a desk. "And us? We take the death-looking chute in a boat with a patched hull?"

"We take it slow, engine half-throttled, lines rigged, everyone in life vests, and we pray to whatever god patches hulls." She looked him dead in the eye. "Or we ride with them to the shrine and watch them burn whatever's up there and put a bullet in the story. Your choice too, Dev. Everyone's choice. I'm not commanding a mutiny. I'm asking for crew."

Dev looked down at his sling. Then at the fire, where Noor was feeding sticks with the careful, stubborn patience of a man who had decided something and was feeding the decision stick by stick.

"They saved my life at the icefall road," Dev said finally. "The old man dragged me out of the water when I went under at the rapids. Did you know that? He's seventy years old and half-starved and he went in after me without thinking." He flexed the fingers of his good hand. "So yes. I'm crew."

* * *

Noor came to her last, near midnight, when the rain had softened into that steady gray whisper that meant the real storm was still hours out. He sat beside her at the wheel without being asked, which she appreciated; there was a time for explanations and a time for simply standing next to someone.

"You've been doing sums all evening," he said. "You do this thing with your jaw."

"My father did it too. It means the numbers are ugly."

"How ugly?"

Isla told him. All of it — the chute, the timing, the storm window, the division of supplies, the fact that they'd be leaving the skiff and its radio and most of the food to two men with rifles who would be furious enough to shoot at a departing boat.

Noor absorbed it the way he seemed to absorb everything: silently, completely, like ground taking rain. Then: "My father can't run. He'll slow the whole thing."

"He can sit low in the bilge and hold a bail bucket. That's all I need from him."

"And if Voss shoots at us?"

"Then he shoots at us." She said it flatly, because sugarcoating got people killed. "But mark me — Voss is a company man, not a killer. Company men shoot at last resorts. If we're gone, gone clean, into a storm, with their skiff damaged in the confluence so they *have* to stop and deal with their own boat first —" she tapped the wheel — "we become a problem for later. Later is a country company men never travel to."

Noor almost smiled. "You've thought about this a lot."

"I've been owned before," Ilsa said. "Once was educational."

She told him the rest: the signals, the positions, the moment. He listened, and then he said the thing she'd been waiting for him to say, the thing that told her the boy had become crew.

"The compass points northeast," he said. "Sena's valley. Whatever happens at the confluence — we're not running away. We're running *at* something. There's a difference, and everyone on this boat needs to feel it."

Ilsa looked at him a long moment. Somewhere behind them, thunder cleared its throat in the high country.

"Aye," she said. "That difference will keep us afloat."

* * *

The storm arrived an hour before dawn, and it arrived like a debt collector.

The sky over the eastern ridge went the color of a bruise going bad, and the wind came down the valley ahead of the rain in a single flat shove that bent the jungle and flattened the river to hammered tin. Ilsa woke with her hand already on the wheel cover, heart going like a bilge pump.

By first light the Kessler had risen two feet and turned the color of milk chocolate, carrying whole trees sideways. The gravel bar was shrinking. Voss stood at the waterline shouting into the wind at his pilot, and his voice carried in pieces: *— now — before it —*

Ilsa gathered her four with a look, nothing more. Dev at the bow line. Noor amidships with the bail bucket and the spare fuel. Sena aft, coiling rope with the economy of someone raised on this water. Kofi Oyelaran lowered himself into the bilge like ballast and gripped a thwart with both knotted hands, and when his eyes met his son's, something passed between them that Ilsa deliberately did not watch.

"Engine check," she barked, loud enough for the camp. Routine. Boring. A captain fussing.

Brandt waved her off without interest, toothpick working. Voss was still shouting at the skiff.

The diversion, when it came, was small and perfect and cost them nothing but a coil of rope and a lie. At Ilsa's signal, Dev stumbled down the bank toward the skiff's mooring line, calling out that it was fouling, dragging, that the current was swinging their boat into theirs — and in the two seconds Voss and Brandt spent turning to shout at him, Ilsa threw off her own lines, gunned the engine, and swung the *Marta-K* hard upstream into the brown chaos of the confluence.

The world became noise.

The two rivers hit each other fifty meters out like rams, and the standing waves there were greyhounds, long and leaping, and the *Marta-K* climbed the first one with her screws singing and slammed down the far side hard enough to crack a rib of spray across the deck. Ilsa heard Noor shouting — counting strokes, counting seconds — heard Sena's voice cutting through it all, steady as a bell: *"North! North side! Trust the chute!"*

Behind them, muffled by wind and water, a rifle cracked. Once. Twice. The rounds went wide and low, slapping the river, and then the sound was swallowed, because the storm closed behind them like a door.

The drowned snag appeared exactly where her memory kept it, a black forked arm rising from the boil, and beyond it the chute — a slot of green-white water between rock walls barely wider than her beam, looking precisely like death.

"Hold!" Ilsa roared, and took it.

For six seconds the *Marta-K* was less a boat than a bullet. Rock scraped along the starboard planking with a sound like a giant filing his teeth. Cold water geysered over the bow and found every seam the tarp patch had ever doubted. Dev, lashed at the bow, was thrown against the rail and Ilsa heard the crack of it even through the roar — heard his cry, short and bitten off — and there was nothing, absolutely nothing, she could do about it except keep the wheel true and the throttle honest and pray to the god of patched hulls.

Then the walls fell away, the water spread, gentled, went green — and they were through, into the flooded channel of the north branch, alone, running free, the storm at their backs and the whole hidden valley open ahead.

* * *

They ran until noon, putting kilometers under the keel while the storm walked behind them, and only when Ilsa found a cutbank eddy deep enough to hide in did she let the engine die and the shaking start.

Dev lay in the shelter with his arm cradled wrong and his face the color of tallow. It wasn't the sling arm. It was the ribs — two of them, by the way he breathed, maybe three, cracked where the rail had caught him. Sena splinted what could be splinted and bound his chest with strapping tape and a strip of sailcloth, and Dev bore it grinning white-faced, making jokes in Hindi that nobody laughed at, which he seemed to appreciate.

"You should've stayed on the skiff," Ilsa said, crouched beside him. Guilt tasted like bilge water. She knew its flavor well.

"And miss the mutiny?" He winced through a breath. "Captain. I've read about you. Whole bars full of stories. Half of them are even true." Another breath, thinner. "This is the best bad bargain I've ever been part of. Don't you dare apologize it into something worse."

Ilsa pressed his good shoulder once, hard, because words were failing her, and went topside.

The others were there: Noor wringing out the bail bucket, Sena studying the gray wall of the valley ahead, Kofi Oyelaran sitting very straight despite everything, holding the compass in his ruined hands and staring at it as though it were a letter he'd written and never sent.

"We lost the skiff, the radio, and most of the food," Ilsa said to all of them. "We kept the boat, the fuel, the tools, and each other. I'm calling that a win, and I've won uglier."

"And Voss?" Noor asked.

"Alive, angry, and anchored to a damaged boat in a flood. He'll come. Men like him always come. But he'll come days late and a river wrong." She turned to face them all, rain streaming off her oilskin, and felt the strange lightness that came after a betrayal done for the right reasons — the feeling of a knot cut rather than untied. "From here we're not salvage anymore. We're an expedition. Small, wet, broke, and illegal in at least two jurisdictions. But ours."

Sena lifted her chin toward the heights, where the clouds tore briefly and showed a shoulder of black rock and, above it, the white gleam of the glacier country.

"Three days' climb from where the river ends," she said. "If the ice holds its schedule."

Ilsa looked at Dev below, breathing in sips. Looked at the compass in the old man's hands, needle trembling toward the northeast. Looked at the mountain.

"Then we'd better move," she said, "before the mountain changes its mind."
