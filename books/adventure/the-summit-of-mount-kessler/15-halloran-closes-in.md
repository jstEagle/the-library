## 15. Halloran Closes In

Ilsa Reiner had spent thirty years reading water, and she read ice the same way — by its silences, its hesitations, the places where it held its breath before it lied to you. The icefall above them was doing a great deal of lying that morning. It creaked and groaned and settled like an old house at night, and every sound it made was a promise it had no intention of keeping.

She stood at the lip of the moraine with her binoculars pressed to her eyes, watching the blue chaos of the seracs tumble down the valley wall like the wreckage of a cathedral. Somewhere in that frozen ruin was the route Sena had described — a corridor through the icefall that opened only when the glacier began to calve, when the summer melt undercut the lower shelf and whole sections of the ice sheared away into the meltwater lake below. Days, Sena had said. Maybe a week. The mountain would open its throat and then close it again, and anyone caught between those two moments belonged to the mountain.

"Anything?" Dev asked. He'd propped himself against a boulder with his bandaged leg stretched out before him, his face the color of old tallow. The wound had gone bad at the edges, a hot red bloom spreading beneath the dressing, and he was burning through his fever in waves — lucid one hour, muttering the next. Right now he was lucid, and his eyes were fixed on her with the particular attention of a man who knew he was becoming cargo.

"Movement," Ilsa said. "Two o'clock. Below the rock band, moving fast."

She passed him the glasses. He found the figures quickly — three of them, dark against the grey scree, roped together and climbing with the unhurried competence of men who had done this kind of work before. Not soldiers. Worse. Soldiers followed orders; these men followed a paycheck, and a paycheck never told them when to stop.

"They found our trail at the confluence," Dev said. "I told you the storm wouldn't cover us."

"The storm wasn't meant to cover us. It was meant to slow them." Ilsa took the glasses back. "It slowed them eleven hours. I counted."

Behind them, in the shallow cave they'd reached at dawn, she could hear the low murmur of the Oyelarans — father and son, sitting apart the way magnets sit when the poles are wrong, close enough to touch and refusing to. Kofi had the journals wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a man carrying an infant through a firefight. Noor was sharpening the ice axes with a whetstone, the sound thin and rhythmic as a heartbeat. And Sena — Sena was out on the ice.

That was the thing Ilsa kept coming back to, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed hook. Sena had gone out onto the icefall at first light to scout the route, alone, because she was the only one who could read it, and because she trusted the ice more than she trusted any of them. Ilsa understood that trust. She shared it. But trust was not the same as safety, and the icefall did not care who had walked it before.

The first shot cracked across the valley at 0940.

Ilsa was moving before the echo came back — that was the sailor in her, the body deciding before the mind had finished the arithmetic. She hit the moraine flat, rolled behind a boulder the size of a truck, and came up with her field glasses instead of a weapon, because the shot hadn't come from the climbers below. It had come from above. From the rock spur that overlooked the icefall's lower gate, where she had watched a single figure move an hour ago and told herself it was a chough, a shadow, a trick of the light.

Through the glasses she saw the muzzle flash again, and then she saw Sena.

The girl had come out of the ice corridor onto the flat shelf below the spur, and she had done the worst possible thing: she had stopped. She was standing very still with her hands away from her body, and two men in Halloran's grey-and-orange shells were coming down toward her from the rocks, moving the way men move when they are certain of their position — no hurry, no cover, rifles slung loose.

"Contact," Ilsa said, and her voice carried flat and cold down the moraine the way a bosun's call carries across a deck. "Sena's taken. Two shooters on the spur, three climbers coming up the valley. They've boxed her."

Noor appeared at her shoulder, breathing hard. "How many total?"

"Five that I can see. Could be six. There was always a sixth in the boat, back at the confluence." She lowered the glasses. "And now they've got the one person who can read that ice."

Below them, Kofi Oyelaran had come out of the cave, and the old man looked smaller than he had yesterday, the altitude and the hunger and the nine years of guilt all compressing him at once. "They want the journals," he said. It wasn't a question.

"They want the journals, your signed statement, your testimony, and your silence," Ilsa said. "In whatever order gets them off this mountain fastest. And they're going to use her to get it, because they know you'll trade everything you have for her." She looked at him steadily. "The question is whether they're right."

Kofi's jaw worked. Noor stepped between them, and Ilsa saw the son's body answer the question before his mouth could — the set of the shoulders, the half-step toward the icefall. Yes, the body said. They're right. We'd trade it all.

"Nobody's trading anything yet," Ilsa said. "Get down. All of you. We're going to talk about the glacier's timetable, and you're going to listen, because it's the only weapon on this mountain that outranks theirs."

* * *

They talked in the cave, in the blue half-light that came through the ice at its mouth, with Dev propped on a coil of rope and his fever-bright eyes following every face.

Ilsa drew in the frost-scum on the stone with her fingertip: a rough line for the glacier, a jagged mass for the icefall, the melt lake below it like a held breath.

"Here's what I know about calving glaciers," she said. "I ran supply boats on the Sermilik for two seasons, before the Kessler. When a glacier calves, it doesn't give you warning. It gives you a schedule, and then it breaks the schedule. But there are signs. The meltwater rises. The ice groans in a particular register — low, like a ship's hull in a storm. And the bergs start going before the big shelf does, small pieces first, like a crowd leaving a theatre before the curtain."

"So we wait for the curtain," Noor said.

"We wait for the curtain, and we use the crowd." She tapped the melt lake. "When that shelf goes — the lower serac field, the one that seals the high route — it's going to be the loudest thing this valley has heard in a hundred years. The report will carry for kilometers. The lake will surge. Everything on the ice will run for cover, including five men with rifles, because no paycheck in the world is worth standing on an icefall when it decides to die."

Kofi frowned. "You're proposing to cross during a calving event."

"I'm proposing to make them think we're going to cross during a calving event, which is worse for them, because they'll have to decide whether to believe it." She looked around the cave. "Here's the shape of it. They've got Sena at the lower gate. They'll send someone to parley — they always do, they want the handover to look clean, in case anyone ever asks questions. While their talker has our eyes, the other four will be watching the route. But nobody — nobody — watches an icefall during the crowd-leaving hour. Their discipline will break. It always breaks. And in the minute it takes to break, we take her back."

"Take her back," Noor repeated. "With what? We have two axes, a flare gun, and a cook's knife."

"We have the mountain." Ilsa held his gaze. "Boy, I've been outgunned my whole life. Every boat I ever sailed was outgunned by the sea. You don't win by matching weapons. You win by choosing the moment. The sea gives you a moment — one, if you're lucky — and the glacier's about to give us ours."

"And if you've misread it?" Dev asked quietly. His voice had the flat clarity that fever sometimes brings, all the ornament burned away. "If the shelf doesn't go when you think it will?"

Ilsa was silent for a moment. Outside, the icefall groaned — that low register she had described, the hull-in-a-storm note — and she let the sound finish before she answered.

"Then I've bet all our lives on a bad schedule," she said. "Same as I did at the confluence, same as I did when I patched a leaking hull with tarp and prayer. I've been wrong before. But I'll tell you what I know in my bones, the way I know a river's going to flood before the rain starts. That ice is done holding. You can hear it. It's been saying goodbye to itself for two days." She wiped her map from the stone. "The mountain's going to render its verdict whether we're ready or not. All we get to choose is where we're standing when it does."

* * *

The parley came at noon, as she'd predicted.

A single man came up the moraine with his rifle slung and his hands visible, an easy smile on a hard face. Ilsa knew the type from a hundred ports — the negotiator, the one whose job was to make the surrender feel like a business meeting. He stopped at a respectful distance, close enough to talk and far enough to be a difficult target, and he looked past her to Kofi with the smile never moving.

"Dr. Oyelaran. Mr. Halloran's compliments. He'd like this handled quietly. The journals, your statement, and your cooperation going downriver, and the young woman walks free. Nobody has to get hurt, nobody has to get cold, and nobody has to explain anything to anybody." He spread his hands. "You're a reasonable man. You've spent your whole career being reasonable."

Ilsa watched Kofi's face while the man talked, and what she saw there frightened her more than the rifles. She saw a man already counting the cost and finding it acceptable. She saw nine years of guilt reaching for the one trade that would feel like absolution: himself for the granddaughter of the woman he'd helped destroy. It would be the most reasonable thing he'd ever done, and it would hand Halloran everything.

She stepped into the negotiator's sightline.

"Here's our counteroffer," she said. "You tell your people on the spur that this shelf is coming down inside forty-eight hours — maybe sooner — and that when it goes, everything below the ice gate goes into the lake. You tell them to look at the water line. It's risen a meter since yesterday. You tell them to listen." She paused, and pitched her voice to carry, the way she'd once shouted through gales off Sermilik. "And you tell them that when it starts — and it will start without knocking — there won't be time to be heroes or employees. There'll only be time to run."

The negotiator's smile flickered. Just at the edges. He glanced up at the icefall — he couldn't help it; nobody could — and Ilsa watched him do the same arithmetic she had done, weighing a paycheck against a mountain, and she watched the mountain win.

"I'll pass that along," he said, and his voice had lost some of its warmth.

"Do that," Ilsa said. "And tell them the captain's advice is free. It's the last free thing they'll get."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and the mountain spent it talking.

Every hour the groans came lower and closer together, the register dropping like a ship settling by the bow. The melt lake rose in visible increments, its grey water climbing the ice walls and leaving a clean bright band of fresh calving. Small bergs began to go — a crack like a rifle shot, a splash, a slow roll — and then more of them, at shorter intervals, the crowd rising from its seats.

Ilsa stood watch with the glasses and counted. She counted the shooters on the spur: two, then one, then two again as the negotiator's message landed and one man climbed down to look at the water and climbed back up looking wrong. She counted the bergs: eleven by midafternoon, seventeen by dusk. She counted Sena, visible at the lower gate in the failing light, sitting with her hands bound in front of her and her eyes on the ice — and Ilsa saw something in the set of the girl's shoulders that made her grin despite everything. The girl wasn't watching her captors. She was watching the glacier. Counting with it. Waiting with it.

She's a river-runner's granddaughter, Ilsa thought. She knows a schedule when she's standing on one.

At dusk she gathered them in the cave and laid it out, and her voice was the one she used in weather — no ornament, no apology, just the shape of the thing.

"Tonight we move to the moraine above the lower gate. All of us. We sleep in shifts with the axes in reach. Tomorrow, when the shelf starts — and it will start, you'll hear it in your teeth before you hear it in your ears — their discipline breaks, and in that break we go down the moraine and take her back. Noor, you're fastest on the broken ground. Dev, you're on the flare gun; you put one behind them, not at them — we want their eyes on the fire, not on us. Kofi—" she looked at the old man, and softened nothing, "—you stay with me, and you keep those journals inside your coat, and you do not trade them, do you hear me? Not for her, not for anyone. She'd spit in your face if you did. I've known her four days and I know that."

Kofi held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, and it was the first nod she'd seen from him that didn't look like surrender.

"And if the shelf doesn't come tomorrow?" Noor asked. "If your timetable's wrong?"

Ilsa looked out the cave mouth at the icefall, black now against the last grey of the sky, groaning its low hull-note into the dark, shedding another berg with a crack and a splash and a long rolling echo.

"Then we stand on that moraine and we wait," she said, "because there is no version of this where we go back down that river, and no version where we hand those journals over. The mountain's the only door out, and it's opening. It's just opening the way mountains open." She picked up her axe. "Loud, and late, and all at once."

Above the valley, the first stars came out, and the glacier talked to itself in the dark — and somewhere below the ice gate, a girl with bound hands was listening to the same voice, and counting.
