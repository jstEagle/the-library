## 16. The Calving Season Opens

The mountain woke them at dawn, and it did not knock.

Dev Chandra came up out of a thin sleep with the sound already inside his chest — a groan so deep it seemed to come from under the world, from the roots of the range itself. The cave walls shivered. Grit sifted down from the ceiling and ticked against the tarp they'd strung over their gear. For one blind moment he thought the whole slope was coming down on them, that the mountain had finally decided to shrug them off like fleas, and he grabbed for the nearest solid thing — which happened to be Noor's ankle — and held on.

Then the sound resolved into something almost like language. A long, grinding exhalation. Ice moving against ice.

"It's starting," Sena said. She was already at the cave mouth, wrapped in her grandfather's old wool coat, watching the glacier below through the grey half-light. Her breath smoked. "Listen to it. It's been holding its breath all season, and now—" She stopped, head tilted, listening the way other people read.

The calving had begun.

Down in the valley of ice, the glacier's snout was shedding itself. They couldn't see it yet from this angle, but they could hear everything: the deep internal thunder of shelves splitting, the boom and echo as house-sized blocks sheared off and went down into the meltwater lake, the water answering in slow, heavy slaps. The sound rolled up the valley and came back changed, layered, until it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

Ilsa Reiner appeared beside Dev, still buckling her pack straps, and stood listening with her hands on her hips like a woman appraising an engine. "How long?" she asked Sena.

"Days. Three, maybe four before the route closes again." Sena's face was unreadable in the dawn light. "My grandmother's letters said the ice opens like a door in calving season. All the pressure releases, the seracs along the high flank shift back, and for a little while there's a corridor." She looked at Dev, then away. "And then it shuts."

"Then we walk through a door that's closing," Ilsa said. "Of course we do. Why would the mountain make anything easy?"

Dev tried to sit up and the world tilted sideways, patient and enormous. His leg — the one crushed between the hull timber and the rock shelf during the mutiny at the confluence, eleven days ago now, though who was counting, everyone was counting — sent up its white flare of pain. He breathed through it the way Sena had taught him, short sips, counting, and after a while the flare banked down to its usual dull furnace.

He'd stopped pretending, somewhere in the last week, that the leg was getting better. It wasn't. The swelling had gone down and come back twice. The skin above the ankle had gone a mottled, worrying color, and when Sena unwrapped it each evening she pressed her lips together and said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said. Infection, probably. Or worse than infection. Out here, without proper medicine, without a hospital within a hundred kilometers of honest road, the difference hardly mattered. What mattered was that every day the leg carried him a little less, and every day the rest of him had to carry more.

"I can walk," he said, because he said it every morning, and because saying it was a kind of prayer.

Nobody answered, which was also a kind of prayer.

* * *

They left the cave as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the glacier, and the whole valley lit up like something newly made. Dev had grown up in towns where mountains were things you saw from a distance, blue and decorative, and nothing in his life had prepared him for this: ice that was not white but blue and green and bruise-purple, shot through with bands of grey rock dust, cracked into towers and bridges and crevasses that glowed from inside like lanterns. Beautiful, yes. But beautiful the way a river rapid is beautiful, or fire — beauty with an appetite.

Sena led them down onto the lateral moraine, picking a line among the boulders with the certainty of someone walking a path she'd been shown in dreams. Behind them, Kofi Oyelaran moved slowly, leaning on a staff Ilsa had cut for him, his strength only partially returned after they'd dug him half-starved out of the cave. And behind Kofi came Noor, carrying Dev.

That was the arrangement now, and no one had discussed it; it had simply assembled itself, the way the true arrangements always do. On the second morning of the climb, when Dev's leg had finally refused him halfway up a scree slope and he'd sat down in the middle of the trail with the particular humiliation of a man whose body has broken a contract, Noor had simply crouched, said "Up you get," and hauled him onto his back. Piggyback, then fireman's carry, then a kind of improvised sling across the shoulders using a rope and a folded tarp — they'd refined the method over days, the way you refine any tool. Now Noor could carry him for an hour at a stretch before they traded off with Ilsa.

"You're heavier than you look," Noor said now, by way of good morning.

"And you're uglier than you look, which is an achievement," Dev managed. His mouth was dry and his voice came out papery. "Put me down for a bit at the next flat place. I want to try walking."

Noor didn't argue, which Dev appreciated. Arguing cost energy nobody had.

The first hours were the best hours. The cold kept the pain down, and the rhythm of movement — Noor's boots crunching, the creak of the rope sling, the far-off artillery of the calving glacier — gave Dev something to count besides his own pulse. He watched Sena work ahead of them and marveled, not for the first time, at what she actually was. Not a guide the way Amara Sesay had been a guide, exactly — though the blood showed, God, the blood showed, in how she read the ice, in how she knew where the moraine would be stable before her feet told her — but something new. A granddaughter of a curse, walking toward the thing that might unmake the curse.

He thought about what the valley said about Amara Sesay. He'd grown up on those stories too, two valleys over — the guide who led the '94 expedition astray, who took the shortcut that killed four men, who vanished into the ice rather than face what she'd done. He'd said the name himself, as a boy, spitting it like the other boys did, not knowing what he was spitting at. And now here he was, being carried up the very mountain on the back of the son of the man who had signed the report that made the curse stick.

There was a symmetry to it that Dev found darkly funny, in the way that only a man with a crushed leg and a fever can find things funny.

At midmorning they reached the flat bench of rock Sena had promised, and Dev walked.

It went well for ten minutes. He set his jaw, took Ilsa's trekking pole in his right hand, used the slope itself as a crutch, and made himself move — heel, roll, push, the bad leg mostly ceremonial, doing its small share of the work while the good leg and the pole did the rest. The sun was warm. The glacier boomed below them like distant festival drums. For ten minutes he was a man walking up a mountain, and not cargo.

On the eleventh minute the leg informed him, with great clarity, that the arrangement was over.

He didn't fall so much as fold — knee buckling, the slope tilting up to meet him, and then Noor's arm was already around his chest, catching him, lowering him onto a boulder with a care that was somehow more humiliating than the fall.

"Ten minutes," Dev said, laughing, or making a sound adjacent to laughter. "New record. Write it down."

"We'll write it down," Noor said. His face was close, sweat-streaked, dead serious. "In the book. *Chandra walks eleven minutes, day forty-one.* Future generations will study it."

"Future generations," Dev panted, "will study your father's confession, and my leg will be a footnote."

"Footnotes matter," Noor said. "Ask any archaeologist."

And Dev laughed then, really laughed, until the laughing hurt the leg and the leg's hurting made him laugh more, a stupid feedback loop, until Ilsa looked back and snapped "Save it for the shrine" and the moment broke and they moved on.

But something had settled in him during those eleven minutes, some small hard seed of a thing. He understood, lying on the sling with Noor's shoulder blade pressing against his chest, hearing the man's heart working through his back like a drum, that this was what the old stories meant when they talked about bearing one another. Not metaphor. Weight. Actual kilograms, actual sweat, actual breath. Somebody's brother on somebody's back, up a mountain, because the alternative was leaving him, and they were not people who left people. Whatever else happened at the shrine — whatever truth came out of the ice, whatever Halloran's guns did or didn't do — Dev Chandra intended to be upright for it if it killed him.

Which, he reflected, was a real possibility.

* * *

The pursuers showed themselves at noon.

Ilsa spotted them first — she had the eyes of a woman who'd spent thirty years reading water and had simply transferred the skill to terrain. She said nothing at first, just altered their pace slightly, took them behind a ridge of moraine, and passed the word back hand to hand: *Two of them. Below and behind. Moving fast.*

Halloran's men. The ones they'd slipped at the confluence, plus reinforcements — three figures now, maybe four, roped together, moving with the efficient arrogance of men paid by the kilogram. They'd lost a day finding the route, Sena guessed. They wouldn't lose another.

"They'll catch us by tonight if we keep this pace," Ilsa said quietly, crouched beside Dev where he lay propped against his pack. "And we can't fight them. We can't outrun them. So we have to be somewhere they don't want to be."

"The corridor," Sena said.

"The corridor," Ilsa agreed. "We go through the ice door while it's open, and we let it start closing behind us. They either follow us into a calving zone — which even mercenaries have opinions about — or they take the long way around and lose two days." She looked at Dev, and her eyes did the thing they did when she was measuring a man against a task. "Can you be quiet and brave for six more hours?"

"I've been quiet and brave for eleven days," Dev said. "It's basically my entire personality now."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Ilsa's face. "Good. Because the entrance to the corridor is that notch—" she pointed to a saddle of ice high on the glacier's flank, blue-white and impossibly steep-looking, "—and the approach is exposed. If they see us crossing it, they'll shoot. So we cross in the noise."

"The noise," Dev repeated.

"When the big shelf goes," Sena said softly, understanding first, as she always did. "When the main calving happens, the sound will cover everything. Even gunfire, maybe. We cross during the thunder."

They waited two hours in the shadow of the moraine, and those two hours were among the strangest of Dev's life. The whole team lay flat among the boulders, watching the enemy crawl closer below, watching the glacier build toward its climax. The air filled with smaller reports — cracks like rifle shots, whumps of settling snow — each one making Dev flinch, each one just the overture. Sena watched the ice with total attention, and once she whispered, "Soon. She's leaning," and Dev realized Sena talked about the glacier the way sailors talk about ships, as a living thing with moods and intentions, and decided not to ask.

The main shelf went at a quarter past two.

There was no preparing for it. The sound began as a crack beneath the world and rose through every register Dev owned — a shriek, a roar, a concussion he felt in his teeth and his ruined leg and the backs of his eyes — and the whole valley shook, and somewhere below and to the east a piece of the mountain the size of a cathedral fell into the lake, and the spray, they learned later, went eighty meters up.

"Now," Sena screamed, and they ran.

Or they did what running was, at their altitude, in their condition. Ilsa hauled Dev up onto Noor's back, Kofi clutched his staff and moved with a surprising, desperate economy, and Sena led them across the exposed slope in the rolling thunder, small avalanches hissing off the seracs above them, the whole world roaring so completely that Dev couldn't hear his own screaming leg, which was the only gift the mountain gave him that day. The notch approached. The blue door of ice opened ahead of them, a corridor between walls of serac taller than churches, glowing from within.

They went through it as the echo died.

Behind them, faint through the settling silence, Dev heard a shout — Halloran's men, arrived at the base of the exposed slope, finding it empty. Finding the door.

Let them choose, Dev thought, as the corridor swallowed them. Let them stand there with their guns and do their arithmetic. The mountain's timetable versus the company's money. He'd bet on the mountain. He'd seen it work.

* * *

The corridor was everything and nothing like Dev had imagined.

Inside, the light came down through the ice in shafts of green and gold, and the walls rose sheer on either side, streaked with a century of summers, and the floor was wind-polited blue glass. It should have felt holy. Some of it did. But the calving never fully stopped — the deep groans ran through the ice continuously now, like the breathing of something enormous sleeping badly — and every few minutes a section of wall shed itself somewhere ahead or behind with a report that bounced down the corridor like a billiard ball.

They moved fast and afraid and awed, all three at once.

Dev, on Noor's back, watched the ice walls slide past and thought about doors. About how his whole life had been a series of them — the door out of his village at nineteen, the door into Ilsa's crew, the door of the salvage contract that had seemed like such a simple piece of arithmetic back in the river town. *Debt owed, debt collected.* He'd boarded that boat for money. He'd stayed for this — for these specific, impossible people, for the son carrying him, for the captain betting their lives on a timetable, for the granddaughter walking into her grandmother's story with her chin up.

Somewhere in the middle of the corridor, the fever he'd been fighting all week finally won a round. The corridor tilted. The green light smeared. He heard himself say, from a great distance, "Noor. I think I need—"

"I've got you," Noor said, and his voice came from right beside Dev's ear, steady as a metronome. "I've got you, Dev. Stay with me. Tell me about the boat. Tell me about the day we met."

"The day we met," Dev murmured. The words came slow and syrupy. "You lied to me. You said you were a surveyor."

"I did lie to you."

"Worst surveyor I ever met. Couldn't read a depth gauge." A pause. The ice boomed, far off. "Why'd you stay? After the camp. After we knew who you were. Why'd Ilsa keep you?"

Noor was quiet for three steps, four. "Because I carried you," he said finally. "That was the interview. That was the whole exam." His voice roughened. "Everything else is negotiable. That part isn't."

Dev wanted to answer. He wanted to say something worthy of it — that he'd carried Noor too, in the ways that didn't show, that the whole crew had been carrying each other since the silent camp, that this was what Ilsa's bad bargain had actually bought. But the fever pulled him under before he could find the words, and the last thing he registered was the corridor's light going gold, then green, then the warm dark of Noor's shoulder, and the sound of the ice breathing around them like a giant deciding whether to let them pass.

* * *

He woke to firelight and arguing.

They'd made camp in a hollow the ice had carved — a natural room, walled in blue, roofed with a bridge of ancient snow that glowed faintly even in the dark. Sena had pronounced it stable. "For tonight," she'd added, in a tone that made clear the *for tonight* was load-bearing.

"—not a debate," Ilsa was saying as Dev surfaced. She stood over the little stove, arms crossed, addressing Kofi Oyelaran. "We rest four hours, no more. Your son agrees with me. Sena agrees with me. Even the fever case agrees with me, and he's unconscious, which makes him the most reasonable person present."

"He needs proper rest," Kofi said. His voice was hoarse, worn down to gravel. "Real rest. Look at him."

"I am looking at him. I'm also looking at the calendar." Ilsa jerked her chin upward, toward the invisible exit of the corridor. "This door closes, Doctor. In three days, maybe less. And when it closes, the only way out of this valley is past Halloran's guns. So we move while the moving's possible, and we get your evidence, and we get down, and *then* everybody rests for a month. Until then, rest is a luxury item and nobody's buying."

Kofi looked at Dev — really looked, the way he hadn't, Dev realized, since the cave. The old man's eyes were wet in the firelight.

"I signed a report once," Kofi said quietly, to no one in particular, "that weighed a man's life against a schedule. I've spent thirty years learning what that costs." He turned back to Ilsa. "Four hours. But I take first watch. And I carry him tomorrow."

"You can barely carry yourself," Ilsa started—

"I carry him tomorrow," Kofi said, and there was something in his voice Dev had never heard there before. Not the famous authority. Something older. "It's my turn. It's been his turn for eleven days."

Dev found his voice. It came out as a rasp. "Doc. No offense. You weigh ninety kilos of good intentions and bad knees."

Kofi smiled — a real smile, crooked and tired. "Then we'll be slow together," he said. "Slow is a speed. Sena says the mountain accepts all speeds. It only refuses stopping."

From across the fire, Sena nodded once, not looking up from rewrapping Dev's leg. Her hands were gentle and unhurried. When she finished, she caught him watching her.

"How is it?" she asked.

"Honestly?"

"Always honestly. That's the family business now."

Dev considered the leg, the mottled skin, the deep hot throb that had become as constant as his heartbeat. He considered lying, and found he didn't have the energy, and found, underneath that, that he didn't want to. Not anymore. Not with these people.

"It's bad," he said. "Isn't it."

Sena held his gaze. Outside, the glacier groaned its long sleepless groan, and somewhere in the dark a serac fell, and the light through the snow-bridge flickered like a candle in wind.

"It's bad," she agreed quietly. "But you're stubborn, and stubbornness is a treatment. The best one we've got." She tucked the blanket around his shoulders. "Sleep, Dev Chandra. Tomorrow you ride on the back of the man who signed the report — and you'll be the heaviest thing he's ever carried, and he knows it, and he'll carry you anyway. That's the whole religion, right there. Everything else is commentary."

Dev slept. And if he dreamed, he dreamed of doors — blue ones, opening — and of a valley that had waited thirty years to listen, and of the sound of footsteps behind him in the corridor that were not his own, and were not friendly, and did not stop.

Because far back down the blue throat of the ice, faint under the glacier's breathing, he had heard it in his last waking minute and filed it away as fever:

Footsteps. Following.

Halloran's men had chosen the door.
