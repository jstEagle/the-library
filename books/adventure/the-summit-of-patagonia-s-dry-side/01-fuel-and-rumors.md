## 1. Fuel and Rumors

The wind on the dry side of Patagonia did not blow so much as insist, and by the time Yara Almeida brought the Cessna down onto Estancia Los Álamos's dirt strip, it was insisting at forty knots across her left wing. She held it off a heartbeat longer than felt comfortable, let the mains kiss the gravel, and rode the bucking ground effect until the plane forgave her for landing in weather that had no business being flown in.

Medicine didn't wait for weather. That was the whole of her business model, and some days it was the whole of her philosophy.

She taxied to the windsock — standing straight out, snapping like something furious — and cut the engine where the strip widened near the shearing sheds. The silence afterward was enormous, the way it always was out here: a silence with weight, pressing against the windows, filled only by wind and the far-off complaint of sheep.

Doña Elvira was already coming across the paddock in her truck, dust pluming behind it, before Yara had even finished her post-flight ritual of touching the cowling and muttering thanks. Ten years of this route had taught the estancias her rhythms the way they'd learned the migrations of birds. They watched for her shadow the way their grandmothers had watched for rain.

"Señora Almeida." Doña Elvira climbed down from the truck cab with the stiffness of a woman who had been riding horses since before trucks were an insult rather than a convenience. "You came through that."

"The medicine would have come through worse."

"And you?" The old woman's eyes, pale as meltwater, went over Yara's face with the frank appraisal of someone who had buried three husbands and did not waste sentiment.

"I'm fine."

"You're thin."

"I'm always thin."

"You're thinner." She took the cooler box from the passenger seat without waiting for permission, hugging it against her coat. Inside were the antibiotics for young Nico Herrera, who had picked up a chest infection shearing in September and whose lungs, according to the radio consultation Yara had relayed to the doctor in Comodoro, were not forgiving him for it. "Come up to the house. There's mate. There's stew. There is," and here her mouth did something dry and complicated, "news."

Yara should have said no. Every minute on the ground was fuel burned against tomorrow's margin, and her margins these days were measured in things thinner than paper. But Doña Elvira's news had a particular frequency, and after ten years Yara could hear the shape of it before a word was spoken.

* * *

The kitchen at Los Álamos smelled of woodsmoke and lamb and the faint mineral tang of water hauled from a well everyone pretended wasn't dropping. Yara sat at the long table under the photograph of Doña Elvira's sons — one dead, one gone north, both staring at the camera with the flat patience of men who knew they'd end up framed — and drank mate until the bitterness found the bottom of her empty stomach and settled there like a tenant.

"So," Doña Elvira said, refilling the gourd. "You've heard."

"Heard what?"

"Your radio people talk too much." The old woman smiled without warmth. "Comodoro especially. Everyone on the band this morning talking about Tomás Reyes."

The name landed in Yara's chest the way a stone lands in a still stock tank — no splash, just the water displaced, rising silently toward the rim.

"Reyes," she repeated, keeping her voice level, keeping her hands easy around the gourd. "What about him?"

"That he's back. That he flew into Trelew on the company jet two days ago. That he's bought permits — concessions, they're saying — somewhere west." Doña Elvira stirred the yerba with the bombilla, slow, deliberate, watching Yara over its rim. "That he's asking after pilots who know the dry side."

The wind worked at the kitchen window, worrying the frame the way it had worried it for eighty years. Yara looked at the photograph of the dead son and thought about nothing at all, which was a skill she had practiced for ten years and never mastered.

"There are plenty of pilots who know the dry side," she said.

"There are plenty of pilots," Doña Elvira agreed. "There are very few who know the Sierra del Silencio."

The mate went cold in Yara's hands. Outside, a dog barked once at the wind and thought better of it.

"That range has nothing anyone wants," Yara said. "No minerals worth the hauling. No grass. No water anyone can reach. Old Ferro used to say—"

She stopped. Too late. The old woman's face softened by a single degree, which from Doña Elvira was a flood of tenderness.

"Ferro's been dead ten years," she said gently. "And the man who walked away from that mountain has been rich ever since. Funny arithmetic, isn't it? The one who stayed loyal is flying antibiotics in a thirty-year-old Cessna with fabric peeling off the ailerons. The one who lied his way off the ice owns half the coast."

"You don't know what happened up there."

"No," Doña Elvira agreed. "Nobody does. That's the whole trouble with it." She set the gourd down and folded her hands, and for a moment she looked every one of her seventy-odd years, and every one of them hard-won. "I'll tell you what I know, girl, since your radio friends won't say it plainly. Reyes is putting together an expedition. Big money. Helicopters for the heavy freight, maybe, but he wants fixed-wing support, someone local, someone who can land on strips like mine and worse. And he wants them soon. Before the season turns."

"Why soon?"

"That," said Doña Elvira, "is the question everyone on the band is asking and nobody is answering."

* * *

Bruno was waiting at the hangar when she got back — Bruno was always waiting at the hangar; the man had the soul of a lighthouse — and she read his mood from fifty meters by the way he stood under the open door of the shed, arms crossed, weight on his heels. Bruno had two moods regarding airplanes: satisfaction and grievance. Today he was wearing grievance like a borrowed coat.

"She's leaking again," he said, before she'd shut the engine down. "Number three cylinder. I've been telling you for two months."

"You told me. I heard you."

"Did you hear the part where we don't have a replacement cylinder? Because the part where we don't have a replacement cylinder is the part that matters." He wiped his hands on a rag that had started life white sometime during the previous government. "I can keep her running. I can't keep her honest."

Yara climbed out and stood beside him, both of them looking at the Cessna the way farmers look at a drought-stricken field — with love, and with arithmetic. The paint was sun-scoured to the color of old bone. The tires were bald as monks. Somewhere under the cowl, engine number LV-KIL ticked as it cooled, patient and accusatory.

"How much is left?" she asked.

"In the account?"

"In everything."

Bruno was quiet for a moment. He was a big man, slow-moving, with a beard going iron-grey and hands so large they made tools look like toys, and he had been with her since the beginning — since before the beginning, since Ferro, since the mountain — and he knew better than most what questions cost her.

"After we pay the fuel bill from today, and the hangar rent, and what we owe the parts place in Trelew?" He rubbed the rag between his fingers. "We can fly for maybe six more weeks. Seven if the weather's kind. Eight if we stop eating."

"We won't stop eating."

"Sofía already has. Have you seen her? She's giving her rations to her mother's neighbor. She thinks nobody notices." He shook his head slowly. "The village aquifer's failing, Yara. Her whole valley. Wells going brackish. They're hauling water now, in trucks, like it's the desert. She hasn't said it to you because she knows what it costs you to hear it."

Yara said nothing. The wind moved through the hangar door and riffled the pages of the maintenance logbook on the workbench, flipping through years of entries in Bruno's careful block capitals, each one a small act of faith: *repaired, patched, deferred, deferred, deferred.*

"So," Bruno said. "Tomás Reyes."

Her head came around. "How do you—"

"The whole band's talking about it. Third-hand by now, fourth. Reyes is back, Reyes has money, Reyes is buying concessions on the cordillera and asking after dry-side pilots." He looked at her steadily. "He'll come here, Yara. You know he will. Where else would he go? There's exactly one outfit that knows that country, and it's us, and he knows it, and he hates that he knows it, but he'll come anyway."

"Then he can keep driving past."

"With what money?" Bruno gestured at the hangar, at the plane, at the whole rusting apparatus of their lives. "Triple rates, they're saying. Maybe more. One job. One season. We fix the cylinder, we clear the debt, we put fuel in Sofía's valley's water trucks if it comes to that. One job, and then we never have to see his face again."

"You didn't see his face."

"No," Bruno admitted. "But I saw yours, afterward, for ten years. And I saw Ferro's grave, and I helped you carry the stone." He put the rag down and faced her fully, and there was no anger in him, only the terrible patience of a man who had watched his friend bleed pride for a decade. "Yara. Pride doesn't buy avgas. I love your pride. I've defended your pride in three bars and two police stations. But pride is a luxury, and we stopped being able to afford luxuries around the time the bank started calling twice a week instead of once."

"They'll talk," Yara said. "The estancias. If I fly for him, after everything—"

"The estancias love you because you show up. In weather, in trouble, with medicine, with news. You think they'll stop loving you because your employer's name changed on a flight plan?" He almost smiled. "Half of them will be glad. Old Víctor at Río Seco says Reyes owes him an apology and he'd like to deliver it personally, but even Víctor says take the money."

Yara turned back to the plane. Her reflection floated ghostlike in the cabin window, superimposed over the empty seats, over the place where Ferro's kit bag used to ride, strapped in like a passenger, all those years ago. She had never moved the straps. She had never quite known why.

"If he comes," she said slowly, "he lies. He'll tell us it's survey work, or mining, or science. It'll be none of those things. A man doesn't come back after ten years with helicopters and permits unless he's going back for something he left behind. Or something he took."

"Then we'll know it when we hear it," Bruno said. "And knowing it, we can price it accordingly." He shrugged, huge and unbothered. "I'm not saying trust him, Yara. I'm saying *invoice* him."

Despite herself — despite the cold stone of the name still sitting in her chest — Yara laughed. It came out rough, disused, like an engine turning over after a long winter.

"Invoice him," she said.

"Triple rates. Fuel surcharge. Hazard pay for working with a man with his face." Bruno counted on his thick fingers. "And a clause in the contract: we fly where we say, when we say, and if he so much as looks at my engine wrong, we walk. Money or no money."

The wind had dropped toward evening, the way it sometimes did, as if apologizing. Beyond the hangar door the sky over the steppe was doing its nightly performance, all rose and copper and violet, beautiful in the indifferent way of things that don't care whether you survive them. Yara looked west, toward the horizon where the land lifted into blue distances and then, farther than any of it, into the white shoulders of ranges that appeared on no map she trusted.

Somewhere out there was the Sierra del Silencio. Somewhere out there was a col where she had dug a grave in frozen scree with her bare hands, and a promise she had made over it that she had kept for ten years the way you keep a wound clean — painfully, daily, without letting yourself think about the scar underneath.

"He won't come himself," she said. "Men like that send lawyers first."

"Maybe." Bruno began stowing tools, each one into its outlined place on the pegboard, the small liturgy that ended his days. "But lawyers don't know the strips. And whatever he's buying permits for, he'll need someone who can put wheels on gravel at eight thousand feet in afternoon wind. There's you, and there's nobody."

Yara stood a while longer in the doorway, watching the light go out of the steppe degree by degree, feeling the debt and the pride and the old grief arrange themselves inside her like cargo that wouldn't balance.

Six weeks of fuel. Sofía's valley going dry. A cylinder that couldn't be replaced. And on the open band, all day, the same name moving from radio to radio across a thousand kilometers of emptiness, growing larger with each retelling, the way storms do.

She was still standing there when the HF set crackled behind her — the little radio in the corner of the hangar that caught everything, gossip and weather and prayer, the true nervous system of the dry side. A voice from Comodoro, faded and doubled by skip, saying words she couldn't quite make out, except for the ones she could.

*...Reyes... chartering out of Trelew... asking specifically for... Almeida...*

Bruno's hands went still on the pegboard.

Yara reached over and turned the volume up.
