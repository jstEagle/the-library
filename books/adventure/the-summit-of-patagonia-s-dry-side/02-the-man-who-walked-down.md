## 2. The Man Who Walked Down

The wind off the steppe carried the smell of dust and diesel that evening, the way it always did when a storm was building somewhere over the cordillera, and Yara Almeida was under her Cessna with a torque wrench in her hand when she heard footsteps on the gravel apron — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a man who had never once needed to hurry in his life.

She knew before she looked. Ten years is long enough to learn a man's footfall the way you learn a song you hate.

"Still flying that old girl," Tomás Reyes said from behind the wing strut. "I'd have bet on it."

Yara did not come out from under the aircraft. She finished the turn on the bolt, felt it seat, and let the silence stretch until it became an answer of its own. Only then did she slide out on the creeper, lie back against the concrete, and look up at him upside down: the good wool coat, the city boots already scuffed wrong for this country, the face older but arranged the same way — all confidence, no apology.

"You're trespassing," she said. "This hangar belongs to the bank, technically, but the airspace belongs to me."

"I brought coffee." He held up a thermos like a peace offering, as if coffee could bridge ten years and one dead man. "Real coffee. Not whatever Bruno's been passing off."

"Bruno makes fine coffee."

"Bruno makes boiled regret." He smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because some traitor muscle in her own face wanted to answer it. That was the thing about Tomás Reyes that no one who hadn't flown with him could understand: he was easy to hate at a distance and hard to hate at arm's length, and he knew it, and he used it the way other men used a compass.

Yara stood, wiped her hands on a rag that was dirtier than her hands, and walked out into the open where the light was honest. The Sierra del Silencio stood far to the west, blue-white above the brown plain, catching the last sun. She looked at it deliberately, so he would know she knew why he'd come.

"Mineral survey," she said. "That's what the radio chatter says. Some new company with permits and money."

"Not chatter anymore." He set the thermos down on a fuel drum, unscrewed the cap, poured two cups anyway. Steam rose and shredded in the wind. "The concession's mine. Thirty days on the clock. I need an expedition flown in — three ferry runs to a basecamp on the eastern glacier, then resupply, then extraction. Fixed-wing only. There's no strip closer than yours."

"There are strips in Coyhaique."

"Coyhaique is four hundred kilometers north and full of pilots who've never crossed the dry side in weather." He handed her a cup. She didn't take it. He set it on the drum between them, a small white flag neither of them would acknowledge. "I need the one pilot who knows every downdraft between here and the ice."

"You need a lot of things, Tomás."

"Triple rates." He said it flatly, no theater, which was how she knew how badly he wanted it. "Per flight hour, plus hazard pay, plus a retainer that clears your debt with the bank in Comodoro. All of it. The hangar, the Cessna, the parts Bruno keeps ordering secondhand from Chile. Yours by the end of the month."

The number landed in her chest like ballast dropped from altitude — sudden, heavy, welcome. She thought of the invoice taped inside the hangar office door, the one she turned face-to-the-wall every morning. She thought of the strip, cracked and heaving with frost, that the municipality had promised for six years to repave. She thought of Sofía's mother's medicine, of Bruno's daughter's school fees in Esquel, of the way poverty in Patagonia doesn't knock — it just moves in quietly, like cold, through every gap.

And she thought of Ferro.

* * *

Old Ferro had died in October, eleven years ago now, in the bad spring when the Río Sengerr froze late and everyone's lambs came early. He had not died on the mountain — that would have been too clean, too legible. He died in his bed at Estancia La Quijotada, an old man's heart giving out, but his mind had been on the Sierra del Silencio the whole last year, circling it, refusing it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

They had buried him on the rise behind the estancia house, facing west, because even dying he wouldn't turn his back on the range that had taken everything from him. And at the grave, in front of the whole valley — the estancieros in their funeral coats, the shearers, the schoolchildren bussed in from Gobernador Gregores — Ferro's widow had put her hand flat on the coffin and said what everyone knew Ferro had said a hundred times alive:

*Nobody goes back into the Silencio. Nobody. Not for money, not for glory, not for science. What's up there killed my husband's soul twenty years before it stopped his heart.*

Yara had been thirty then, furious at the world, furious mostly at Tomás, who had not come to the funeral at all. She had made the promise standing on that frozen ground with her breath steaming, and she had meant it the way young people mean things — completely, and without understanding what they're promising, or what it will cost to keep.

Now Tomás stood in her hangar with steam rising off a cup of coffee and said, "It's mineral survey work, Yara. Copper signatures in the eastern batholith. Cores, assays, a report. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," she repeated.

"Nothing more."

"You expect me to believe that you flew eight hundred kilometers to hire the pilot whose career you ended, to revisit the range your own survey got Ferro blacklisted over, and it's about *copper*?"

Something moved behind his eyes — fast, quickly shuttered, like a bird crossing a window. For half a second she saw it: not guilt exactly, but calculation caught mid-turn. Then the confidence reassembled itself, seamless as lake ice.

"The original survey wasn't mine alone," he said quietly. "You know that."

"I know what I was told."

"And what were you told? By whom?" He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, though there was no one for a mile in any direction except Bruno, who was mercifully still in town buying spark plugs. "Ferro told you a story, Yara. A story where he was the honest man and I was the ambitious one, and the data was falsified, and the valley lost its water rights because of me. One version. Told by a grieving man to two grieving kids who worshipped him. Did it ever occur to you to check?"

"Check against what? You disappeared. You went north and got rich while we buried him and spent a decade being known as *the ones from the failed expedition*. Do you know what that did to us? Sofía couldn't get charter work for three years. Bruno drank through two seasons. And you want to stand here and tell me the story needs checking?"

"Yes," Tomás said. "That's exactly what I'm telling you."

The wind gusted along the apron, rattling the loose tin on the hangar roof, and somewhere beyond the fence a horse complained at the weather. Yara looked at the mountains again. The Silencio had gone pink with alpenglow, beautiful and remote as a photograph of something you'd lost.

Here was the truth she kept in the same locked drawer as the bank invoices: she had never stopped wanting to go back. Ten years of flying the dry slope, ten years of medicine runs and mail and gossip, and every clear day the range sat on the horizon asking its question. Why did the survey fail? Why did the water rights end up upstream, held by men who'd never seen the glacier? Why did Ferro, honest Ferro, sign the report that ruined the valley? She had asked these questions into her pillow at nineteen and at twenty-five and, if she was honest, last Tuesday. The promise over the grave had not buried them. It had only taught her to stop saying them aloud.

Tomás watched her look at the mountains, and he was smart enough — he had always been smart enough — to say nothing at all while she did it. He just picked up his coffee and drank, patient as geology.

"Triple rates," she said finally. "Retainer clearing the debt. Hazard pay. Contracts in writing, signed tonight, no verbal anything."

"In writing. Tonight."

"And my crew votes. Bruno and Sofía get a real vote, not a formality. If either of them says no, the answer is no, and you drive back to Comodoro and find your copper somewhere else."

"Agreed." He set down the cup. "One condition of mine."

"Of course there is."

"We fly the same route Ferro planned. Eastern approach, the high col. His route." He met her eyes, and for the first time since he'd walked down the apron, the confidence flickered and she saw underneath it — not remorse, nothing so useful as remorse, but hunger. A man who had wanted something for twenty years and could finally taste it. "He had the right line, Yara. Whatever else happened, he had the right line. Let me prove that much."

She should have asked him then. Right there, with the alpenglow dying on the Silencio and the wind dropping the temperature toward frost, she should have asked the question that had lived in her for a decade: *Why do you care about Ferro's route, Tomás? Why does a mineral man care about a dead glaciologist's approach line?*

But the invoice was taped inside the office door, face to the wall, and Sofía's mother needed medicine that cost more than a good horse, and the strip was failing, and poverty doesn't negotiate — it only waits, patiently, for a man with money to walk down the apron.

"All right," Yara said. "Crew votes tomorrow. Bring your contracts at noon."

Tomás smiled, and this time she let herself not smile back. He gathered his coat, nodded once — the old expedition nod, the one that used to mean *wheels up at dawn* — and walked back across the gravel toward the truck he'd left by the fence, his silhouette going long and thin in the last light.

Yara stood alone on the apron until the taillights vanished down the road toward town. Then she went into the hangar, took the invoice off the door, and looked at it properly for the first time in months. The figure at the bottom had grown another zero's worth of interest since winter.

Above the door, tacked where Ferro's widow had nailed it after the funeral, hung the old expedition pennant — sun-bleached, torn at the fly end, the silhouette of the Silencio printed on it in ink gone almost gray. *Nobody goes back,* the promise said, in a dead woman's voice, on frozen ground, eleven years ago.

Yara reached up and took the pennant down. She didn't throw it away. She folded it carefully, the way you fold a flag, and put it in the drawer with the invoices, and closed the drawer, and told herself — in the voice she used on passengers in turbulence, calm and certain and sometimes true — that it was just flying. Three ferry runs. Cores and assays. Nothing more.

Outside, the first stars came out over the steppe, and far to the west the Sierra del Silencio faded into darkness, keeping whatever it kept.
