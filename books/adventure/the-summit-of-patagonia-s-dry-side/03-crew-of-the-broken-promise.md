## 3. Crew of the Broken Promise

The vote happened in the hangar, because everything in their lives happened in the hangar — around the scarred pine table where Bruno kept his parts catalogs and Sofía pinned her charts, under the bulb that swung on its wire whenever the wind found a gap in the corrugated wall. Yara had called it a meeting. Nobody was fooled. Meetings at Almeida Aéreo were how Yara let other people say out loud what she had already decided not to decide.

Bruno went first, as everyone knew he would. He sat with his forearms flat on the table, hands like cracked leather, and he didn't hedge.

"Yes," he said. "Triple rates. Thirty days of work. I don't care if the man is the devil's own cousin — we need the strip graded before winter, and we need the compressor, and Sofía needs her radio replaced, and none of that buys itself." He looked down at his hands. "I voted yes for Ferro too, back then. I know what that cost. But Ferro isn't here to feed us."

The silence after his name did its usual work, settling over the table like dust.

Sofía spoke second, and she said no. She said it quietly, the way she said most things, but she said it twice so there could be no mistaking it. "No. And no again." She stood, because sitting felt like agreeing. "You want me to navigate for Tomás Reyes into the Sierra del Silencio? The range Old Ferro died keeping us out of? The man lied about the first survey, whatever exactly happened up there — Yara won't even tell me all of it, which tells me enough — and now he comes back with money and permits and a smile, and we're supposed to fly him in like a taxi?"

"Nobody's asking you to like him," Bruno said.

"I'm asking you to remember him," Sofía said. "There's a difference."

She looked at Yara then, and Yara looked back, and between them passed the whole unspoken ledger of ten years: the flights, the debts, the grave they'd dug above the estancia road with Ferro's compass buried in his jacket pocket because he'd have wanted it that way. Yara said nothing. That nothing was an answer, or would become one, and Sofía hated her a little for it, and loved her more.

* * *

It was two days later that the aquifer news came, and it came the way bad news always came to El Rincón: sideways, half-heard, riding in on someone else's radio traffic.

Sofía was in the hangar loft running drift calculations when she caught the tail of a conversation on the municipal band — the co-op engineer in town talking to a well contractor, voices frayed with the particular exhaustion of men repeating themselves to people who don't listen. She turned the gain up.

"—third test this season. It's not seasonal. The water table's dropped four meters since spring. Four meters, Daniel. The shallow wells are already brackish. If it keeps this rate, by next summer you're drilling past anything the village can pay for—"

She sat very still. Outside, the wind worried the tin roof the way it always did, a sound she'd slept under her whole life and never once heard properly until this moment, when it sounded like something being taken away.

El Rincón ran on that aquifer. Her mother's garden, the school's pump, the co-op's alfalfa, the church baptistery — all of it drinking from the same slow underground river that nobody had ever seen and everybody had always assumed, the way you assume your own heartbeat. Four meters. She tried to make four meters mean something concrete and couldn't; it was an abstraction wearing the clothes of a catastrophe, and abstractions were easy to survive until suddenly they weren't.

Tomás Reyes arrived an hour later. Of course he did. Later, Sofía would wonder whether he'd known about the radio call, whether he'd timed it, whether a man like that ever stopped calculating long enough to simply be somewhere. He came into the hangar with a folder under his arm and mud on his boots from the strip, and he didn't open with money this time.

"I heard about your wells," he said.

Sofía kept her face still. "News travels."

"It travels faster than water, apparently." He set the folder on the workbench, unhurried, and opened it to a map — not the Sierra charts she'd expected but a hydrological survey of the valley, contour lines in blue threading beneath El Rincón like veins. "I've been reading these since yesterday. Your aquifer feeds off the eastern recharge zone. Which sits," and his finger moved up the page, "inside the concession boundary I'm holding. Thirty days of access, and part of my contract obligation is infrastructure assessment. Wells, cisterns, a pipeline spur if the data supports it. I can't promise the village water, Sofía. But I can promise that for the first time in fifty years, somebody with equipment will be looking under your feet instead of over your head."

"You could just give us the money," she said. "If you're serious."

"I could. And then the money ends, and you're back here next year listening to the same conversation." He closed the folder gently, almost tenderly. "I'm offering you a reason, not charity. There's a difference."

*There's a difference.* His phrase, thrown back at her own words from two days ago, and she felt it land exactly where he intended. That was the thing about Tomás Reyes she'd forgotten and was remembering now: he didn't argue with you. He arranged the world until arguing seemed unreasonable.

That night she walked out past the last house of El Rincón to the old stock tank, dry-rimmed and cracked, where she and Yara had caught tadpoles as girls before Yara left for flight school and Sofía stayed to learn maps. The stars over the steppe were merciless, the way they always were, beautiful the way things are beautiful when they owe you nothing. She thought about Ferro, who used to say that a navigator's job was to know where she was, always, and to admit it. *Know where you are,* he'd say, tapping her temple. *Not where you wish you were.*

Where she was: standing over a dying aquifer, holding a vote she hadn't yet changed, telling herself that changing it wasn't betrayal but arithmetic. Need dressed as duty. Duty dressed as love of the valley. Love of the valley dressed as — what? She couldn't find the bottom layer. Maybe there wasn't one. Maybe people were just reasons all the way down, and the only sin was pretending otherwise.

In the morning she told Yara: "Change my vote."

Yara studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her jaw tighten. "Because of the wells."

"Because of the wells."

"And if I said no anyway?"

"Then I'd go anyway," Sofía said. "As his consultant, maybe. Or I'd stay and hate you. One of those."

Something moved behind Yara's eyes — not anger; recognition, terrible and tired. "All right," she said. "Two yeses, one no. My vote decides." She looked out at the strip, at the Cessna sitting on its gear like a patient animal, at the sky building itself gray in the west. "We fly him in. Survey work only. We come home every night we can. And Sofía—" her voice dropped, went private, "—if he asks us to do one thing Ferro would have hated, we walk. All three of us. Deal?"

"Deal," Sofía said, and heard herself believe it, or believe in believing it, which in the steppe counted for nearly the same thing.

* * *

They loaded the Cessna under a sky the color of tin.

It was the kind of sky Old Ferro used to call *plomo* — lead — and he'd taught them both to respect it: not dangerous in itself, but a lid, a held breath, weather deciding what it wanted to be. The light came down flat and shadowless, erasing the horizon, so that the plane and the mountains and the men loading drums of fuel all seemed cut from the same gray cloth.

Bruno worked the cargo without speaking, which was how he prayed. He strapped the drill casing Dr. Ibarra had shipped down, lashed the fuel drums, wedged the food boxes, checked each tie-down twice and the critical ones three times. Sofía sat in the right seat with her new charts spread across her knees — Tomás's charts, precise beyond precision, every ridge and col drawn with a confidence that should have pleased her and instead itched somewhere below thought, like a word on the tip of the tongue that turns out to be in a language you never learned. She'd navigated this country for ten years and her own maps had gaps. His had none.

Tomás climbed in last, settled into the middle seat among the crates, and gave the cabin a satisfied look, as if the plane were already his.

"Weather window closes Thursday," he said. "We'll want three runs minimum."

"We'll want what the weather gives," Yara said, running her preflight from the left seat, voice level, hands busy. "Same as always."

"Of course." He smiled. "Same as always."

Sofía watched him in the small chrome of the mirror mounted by the cargo net — watched the smile sit on his face a moment after it was needed, like a man who has practiced expressions the way other men practice golf swings. Then she looked down at her chart, at the blue contour lines of her own aquifer threading toward the Sierra del Silencio, and told herself the story one more time, the good version, the version she'd chosen: *this is for the wells, this is for the village, this is duty, this is duty.*

The engine coughed, caught, steadied into its familiar roar. Yara taxied them out onto the failing strip, gravel ticking against the struts, and swung the nose into the wind.

"Sierra del Silencio," Yara said into the intercom, and it wasn't a destination so much as a sentence being pronounced. "Thirty days."

The Cessna lifted, heavy-laden, and the tin-colored sky took them.
