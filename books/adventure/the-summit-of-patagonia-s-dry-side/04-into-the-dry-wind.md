## 4. Into the Dry Wind

The wind on the dry side of the cordillera did not blow so much as insist. It came off the ice fields in long, patient shoves, bending the coihue scrub flat, herding dust along the strip in ropes that looked almost solid, and it had been doing this, Yara thought, since before there were names for any of it. She stood at the open door of the hangar with a mug of mate cooling in her hand and watched the tin-colored sky decide what kind of morning it intended to be.

Three flights. That was the arithmetic Tomás had laid out on her chart table two nights ago, his finger tapping the map of the Sierra del Silencio like a man counting money he already owned. Gear to basecamp at the foot of the glacier — tents, drills, fuel drums, the crates of instruments belonging to Dr. Ibarra, who had arrived with the gentle, distracted air of a man who loved ice more than people. Three loads in the Cessna, maybe four if the weather held its temper. Triple rates per hour, paid half up front into an account that had been gasping since winter.

Yara had said yes because Bruno had counted the numbers aloud and Sofía had not met anyone's eyes, and because poverty, she had learned over ten years of flying this slope, was a creditor that never slept and never forgave. But she had made herself one promise at Ferro's grave, kneeling in the gravel above Puerto Chacabuco while the wind read the eulogy for him: *never again into the Silencio.* And now here she was, checking tie-downs under that same broken sky, about to break it.

"You're doing the preflight twice," Bruno said, coming around the wing with the torque wrench still in his hand. He was a broad, slow-moving man whose silences were more eloquent than most men's speeches. "I checked everything last night."

"I'm checking it again."

"The engine doesn't get more honest the second time."

"Everything gets less honest," Yara said, "the closer we fly to that range." She pulled the oil dipstick, wiped it, seated it, drew it again. Full. Clean. The one thing in her life currently telling her the truth.

Bruno leaned against the strut, unhurried. "You want to talk about it?"

"There's nothing to talk about. It's a freight run."

"It's a freight run into a place Old Ferro wouldn't let us look at from the air." Bruno's voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, the way water moves under ice. "You've flown angry before, Yara. You're good at it. But you haven't flown scared, and I don't like guessing which one this is."

She screwed the cap down and looked past him at the strip — the strip that was failing, cracks spidering through the packed earth where the spring thaw lifted it every year, money for repair existing nowhere in any ledger she kept. "It's just flying," she said, and heard how much it sounded like a prayer repeated until it wore smooth.

* * *

The first run went easy, the way the mountain lets you believe things.

They left at first light with the Cessna heavy — drums of avgas lashed in the cabin, Ibarra's instrument crates wedged between them, Sofía in the right seat with the charts spread across her knees. The dry wind pushed them south-southeast along the valley towns, each one a smudge of poplars and tin roofs, each one a place that trusted Yara's radio voice more than any ministry's paperwork. She flew low enough to see laundry on lines, dogs trotting fence lines, the green insistence of irrigated plots that existed only because some distant snowfield deigned to melt in their direction.

Then the land began to climb and dry out all at once, the way it does east of the divide — grass giving up to bunchgrass, bunchgrass giving up to scree, the whole world turning the color of old bone. And ahead, rising out of the haze like something the continent had tried to swallow and failed, the Sierra del Silencio.

Nobody spoke. Sofía traced their position with one finger, not looking up. In the back, Ibarra pressed his palm against the window like a boy at an aquarium glass.

The glacier appeared exactly when Tomás's map said it would appear — a white tongue hanging out of a cirque of black towers, crevassed blue at its snout, moraine ridges braiding below it like the roots of something upside down. There was no marked strip, of course; there never was, out here. Yara picked her line along the outwash plain, judged the wind off the dust streams, and put it down on the gravel with the particular tenderness she reserved for landings far from help.

They were on the ground forty minutes, long enough to unload and for Ibarra to walk the moraine with his head tipped back, reading the ice like scripture. Then they were airborne again, climbing out over the tongue of the glacier, and Yara felt the Silencio slide beneath her wings with all its old weight — the weight of a man's voice saying *we do not go back there*, the weight of a grave, the weight of whatever it was that had ended the first expedition and sent her and Tomás home without their mentor.

Tomás had not come on the first run. He was waiting at basecamp when they returned for the second load, standing among the drums with his collar turned up, watching them land with an expression Yara couldn't file anywhere. Not gratitude. Not relief. Something closer to recognition — the look of a man seeing a room he'd been in before.

* * *

The second run was the one that nearly killed them.

They went in heavier — the drill rig in sections, the last of the fuel, camp stores for ten days. The Cessna grumbled up through the valley passes with its load sagging in the performance charts, and by the time they turned into the high country the tin sky had lowered itself another thousand feet and begun to think seriously about weather.

Sofía called the ridge line first. "Wind's backing," she said, her voice clipped into its working register. "West-northwest, gusting. That col ahead is going to be spilling."

"I see it." Yara did see it — the spindrift lifting off the crest in long gray banners, streaming east like smoke from a fire on the other side of the world. Banners meant wind over the top, and wind over the top meant rotor and sink on the lee side, and the lee side was where they had to go.

"We can go around," Sofía said. "South, add twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes puts us on the ground after the light goes." Yara weighed it the way she weighed everything, quickly and without sentiment: fuel, daylight, the load, the crew waiting on the moraine with no stove and no shelter. "We go through. High and close to the rock, where the sink can't get a grip on us."

"That's where the turbulence lives."

"That's where the exit lives too."

They crossed the first saddle clean. The second was where the mountain changed its mind.

It happened without announcement, the way the dry wind always did its real work. One moment they were in rising air, the Cessna buoyant, the cirque opening ahead of them in a bowl of blue shadow and white ice — and then the downdraft took them, a fist of cold descending air that shoved the altimeter down through three hundred feet in as many seconds. Yara's hands were already moving, nose down to keep flying speed, full power, the engine howling its protest at the altitude and the load, and the ridge — the black fang of it — swelled in the windshield with a horrible, patient slowness, the way disasters move when they are certain of you.

"Ridge!" Sofía's voice cracked across the cockpit.

"I have it." Yara did not have it. The Cessna was mushing, sinking, the air simply refusing to hold them, and the fang grew, and somewhere behind her she heard Ibarra say nothing at all, which was worse than screaming. She banked hard toward the gap she'd memorized on the way in — the notch in the ridge line, the one gap — and fed the aircraft every ounce of dignity it had left, trading altitude for airspeed, airspeed for distance, the whole miserable algebra of mountain flying done at the edge of the envelope.

They cleared the fang by what she would later estimate, honestly, at eighty feet. Maybe sixty. The rotor caught the tail as they crossed and slapped the Cessna sideways like a hand correcting a child's grip, and then they were through, into the smoother spill on the far side, sinking toward the glacier's outwash with the engine still roaring and Yara's heart going like a loose tool in a metal box.

She landed harder than she liked. Nobody commented. Ibarra climbed out with his legs unsteady under him and went to look at the ice, because that was the only language he had for shock.

* * *

It was afterward, sitting in the Cessna with the engine ticking itself cool, that the thought arrived — and once it arrived, it did not leave.

Because she had cleared the fang through the notch. The notch. And she had aimed for it without thinking, without consulting the chart, because Tomás's map showed it. A thin, penciled annotation in the corner of the survey copy he'd handed her: a gap in the ridge line at 2,140 meters, marked with a small cross and the notation *paso viable con viento del oeste.* A viable pass with west wind.

But nobody had ever mapped that notch. Yara knew the official surveys — she'd flown this slope for a decade, she'd bought every sheet the Instituto Geográfico ever printed, and the Silencio was a blank on all of them, a hatched area labeled *sin levantar*, unsurveyed. Ferro's expedition had gone in with sketch maps drawn from memory and rumor, and Ferro had died keeping whatever was in there unrecorded. So where did a penciled cross come from? Where did a wind notation come from — *with west wind*, as if someone had sat in that notch, or flown through it, in a west wind, and written down what they found?

And the glacier. On the approach she'd matched Tomás's contour lines against the actual ice and they had agreed — not approximately, not the way secondhand maps agree, but exactly, the snout where the snout was, the moraine loop where the loop was. Ice moves. A survey even ten years old drifts out of truth. These contours were current. These contours were *fresh*.

Yara sat in the cooling cockpit and let the certainty assemble itself, piece by piece, the way she'd once assembled a carburetor in the dark with numb fingers — by feel, by fit, by the click of each part seating where it belonged.

Tomás Reyes had been here. Recently. Secretly. Had stood in that notch with the west wind pouring over him, had flown or walked this cirque, had put his pencil on the ice field's living shape — and had told them it was mineral survey work, virgin country, a first look. He had lied with maps, which was the most efficient kind of lying, because maps carried no tone of voice to catch them in.

She thought of Ferro forbidding them the range. She thought of the way Tomás had watched them land yesterday, recognizing the ground. She thought of the grave, and the promise, and the triple rates sitting in an account that could fix the strip.

Through the windshield, across the moraine, Tomás stood talking to Ibarra beside the drill rig, gesturing at the glacier with the easy authority of a man pointing at his own property. And when he turned — perhaps feeling her attention the way animals feel weather — he looked directly at the Cessna, directly at her, across two hundred meters of gravel and wind, and smiled.

Yara did not smile back. She reached for the chart on the seat beside her, folded it closed over the penciled notch, and said nothing.

Some truths, she told herself, you carry until you know their weight. She started the engine for the third run, and the dry wind took the propeller wash and tore it apart over the ice, and the Silencio kept whatever else it was keeping.
