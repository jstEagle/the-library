## 5. What the Ice Keeps

The wind on the glacier spoke in a language Sofía had spent her whole life learning not to hear. It was the same wind that had scoured her village at the edge of the dry slope, the one that came off the ice fields carrying nothing — no moisture, no mercy, just the long hiss of air moving over stone. Here, though, at the foot of the Sierra del Silencio, it sounded different. Thicker. As if the mountain itself were breathing through its teeth.

She stood at the edge of the drill site with her hands jammed in her parka pockets and watched Dr. Ibarra work.

The glaciologist was a small, precise man, older than he first appeared, with forearms like knotted rope and the patient hands of someone who had spent decades persuading ice to give up its secrets without breaking. The thermal drill hummed against the glacier's skin, sinking centimeter by centimeter, and every so often Ibarra would pause, tilt his head as if listening to something beneath the noise, and adjust a dial by a fraction.

"Careful there," he murmured, to no one in particular. "Careful."

Sofía understood why Tomás had hired him. A man like Ibarra didn't ask questions about permits or concessions or why a mining survey needed core samples from a glacier no map had ever named. He asked questions about isotopes and accumulation layers and the chemistry of air trapped two centuries ago, and those questions kept him too busy to wonder what the answers were *for*.

What she couldn't understand was Tomás.

He had barely slept since they'd landed. She'd seen him at three in the morning, standing outside the tents with his arms crossed, watching the ice field the way other men watched a sleeping enemy. During the day he hovered at the perimeter of the drill site, and when Bruno wandered too close to the sample cases, Tomás moved between them with a speed that had nothing casual in it.

"They're just cores," Bruno had said last night, laughing, holding up his hands. "I'm not going to steal your ice, Reyes."

"You'd be surprised," Tomás had said, and hadn't smiled.

Now Sofía turned away from the drill site and walked the line of moraine where the glacier's debris had piled itself into a low gray ridge. It was the kind of errand she gave herself when she needed to think: check the cache markers, walk the perimeter, be useful somewhere Tomás wasn't looking. Her village's aquifer was failing — she had known it for years in the way you know a slow illness, in the taste of the water, in the orchards dying row by row — and Tomás had promised pipes and pumps and a treatment plant with his name on a plaque. She had voted yes because of that promise. She repeated it to herself some mornings like a prayer, though lately the prayer had begun to sound more like an excuse.

The moraine ridge curved along the eastern flank of the ice field, and she followed it until the camp was out of sight behind a shoulder of rock. The silence here was total except for the wind and the occasional deep groan of the glacier settling, a sound like a door opening very slowly in a house with no one in it.

That was when she saw the cairn.

It was old — that much was obvious even before she got close. The stones were furred with lichen, gray-green and orange, and they had been stacked by hands that knew what they were doing, each rock seated firmly on the one below, built to outlast weather. Not a natural pile. Not recent. Someone had stood exactly here, twenty years ago or more, and built this marker deliberately, facing the ice field.

Sofía's mouth went dry.

She looked back toward camp. The drill hummed faintly, distant now, and Tomás's figure was a dark smudge against the white. He hadn't seen her. The shoulder of rock hid her completely.

She should have gone back. She told herself that later, many times — that the sensible thing, the loyal thing, was to turn around and log the cairn as a survey point and say nothing. But her hands were already moving, lifting the top stones, and once the first one came free the rest seemed to remove themselves, as if the cairn had been waiting all these years for someone curious enough and foolish enough to open it.

Beneath the stones, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff and brown with age, was a tin.

It was the size of a bread box, rectangular, rusted at the corners but sound. And stamped into the lid, struck deep enough to survive two decades of frost, was a mark she knew better than her own signature: a stylized condor rising over a chevron, with the letters **E.F.** beneath.

Ferro's mark. Old Ferro's expedition stamp, the one he'd punched into their gear, their crates, the tail of the first Cessna. The one Yara still wore on a leather cord around her neck, pressed from the original die.

Sofía sat down hard on the moraine, the tin in her lap, and did not move for a long moment. The wind hissed. Somewhere under her boots the glacier groaned again, that slow opening door.

*The past isn't buried,* she thought. *It's preserved.*

That was the thing about ice that Ibarra talked about endlessly at dinner, waving his fork: ice doesn't forget. Every year's snowfall seals the year's air inside it, layer upon layer, an archive written in cold. Nothing rots. Nothing escapes. Whatever happened on this glacier was still here, held perfectly, waiting for someone with a drill or a shovel or simply bad luck to come and read it.

She pried the lid. The hinges fought her, flaking orange rust onto her gloves, then gave.

Inside, wrapped in waxed paper that crackled like old leaves, were photographs. Dozens of them. Black and white, then faded color, edges soft with age. Sofía lifted them out one at a time with the care Ibarra used on his samples.

The first showed a camp — tents, a drill rig, men in parkas of a cut twenty years out of date — pitched on this exact ice field. She recognized the profile of the cirque above it, the notch of the high col, the moraine ridge she was sitting on. The second was a close-up of a core sample being lifted from the barrel, gleaming like a spine pulled from the mountain's back. The third was a group shot, six men squinting into the sun, and Sofía's breath caught, because standing at the center, younger by twenty years but unmistakable, was Ferro himself — broad-shouldered, gray-bearded, his hand resting on the shoulder of a young man beside him.

The young man was Tomás Reyes.

She stared at it until the figures blurred. Ferro smiling. Tomás smiling. Standing together on this ice, in this place Ferro had forbidden them ever to return to, in the years before everything went wrong. Before the first expedition. Before the crash of reputation and the death on the col and the silence that had fallen between Ferro and the world.

She flipped the photographs faster now. Survey grids drawn over the ice field, annotated in a handwriting she recognized as Ferro's — tight, slanted, precise. Depth measurements. Dates. And then, near the bottom of the tin, a folded sheet of heavier paper, and beneath it a smaller envelope with no writing on it at all.

Her fingers hesitated over the envelope. Something in her — the part of her that had grown up reading weather, reading water, reading the silences adults left behind them — told her that whatever was in it would change the shape of everything. That some tins are sealed for a reason.

But the past isn't buried, she thought again. It's preserved. And preserved things are meant, eventually, to be read.

She opened the envelope.

Inside was a single photograph, color, better preserved than the rest, and a strip of paper torn from a notebook. The photograph showed a document — a survey report, photographed page by page, four pages captured in a grid — bearing official letterhead she half recognized from her village's water cooperative files. The notebook strip carried five words in Ferro's hand:

**"If anything happens — tell Yara."**

Sofía sat motionless on the frozen moraine, the photographs spread across her knees, and felt dread bloom in her chest like ink dropped in water, spreading outward through everything, staining it all.

Tomás had been here before. Twenty years ago, side by side with Ferro. Which meant the maps he'd brought — the maps too accurate for a stranger — weren't purchased or stolen. They were remembered. And which meant that whatever Ferro had found on this ice, whatever he'd photographed and hidden in a cairn facing the field like a man leaving a letter for the future, was important enough that a dying man had walked up here — or sent word up here — to make sure it survived him.

And Tomás had guarded this site obsessively, day and night, since they landed. Had she assumed it was secrecy? Paranoia? The caution of a man hiding mineral data from rivals?

Or had Tomás known the cairn was here all along? Had he been waiting, watching, counting the days until someone else's hands opened it — so that the finding would never be his?

The wind shifted, and Sofía heard, faintly, voices from camp. Ibarra's drill had stopped. She looked down at the photographs, at young Tomás smiling under Ferro's arm, at the five words in the dead man's handwriting, and made herself move — folding the papers back into their wrappings, lowering them into the tin, pressing the lid down until the hinges clicked.

She rebuilt the cairn stone by stone, seating each rock as carefully as the original builder had, and only when it was whole again did she allow herself to look toward camp.

Tomás stood at the edge of the drill site, exactly where he'd been, a dark figure against the white. But he was no longer facing the ice field.

He was facing the moraine. Facing her.

Even at three hundred meters, across the blue glare of the glacier, Sofía could feel the weight of his gaze settle on her — patient, unblinking, the look of a man who has been waiting a long time for something to surface, and is in no hurry at all to see what it turns out to be.

She picked up her pack, tucked her hands into her pockets to hide their shaking, and started down toward camp, toward the questions she could not yet ask and the answer she could no longer unknow, while behind her the glacier groaned its slow, patient groan — the sound, she thought, of an archive turning its pages.
