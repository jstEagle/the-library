## 6. The Concession Clock

Bruno heard the plane before he saw it, the way you hear anything out here first — a mosquito whine swelling against the silence of the ice, growing teeth. He was elbow-deep in the generator's carburetor, fingers black with grease, and he didn't bother pulling them out. Supply runs came on schedule. This one was early.

The Twin Otter — not theirs, a charter out of Comodoro with a fat-bellied cargo look — swung wide over the moraine and settled onto the flagged strip with a cough of snow and gravel. Bruno wiped his hands on his thighs and walked out to meet it, boots crunching, breath steaming in air that still carried October's edge even at midday.

The pilot leaned out, a young woman with wind-chapped cheeks and a courier's pouch slung across her chest like a bandolier.

"Bruno Ferreyra?" she called over the dying engine.

"Who's asking."

"Company mail." She tossed the pouch down before he could decide whether he wanted to catch it. "And a message for Reyes. Verbal. I'm supposed to say it to him direct."

"He's up at the drill site."

"Then you're going to want to hear this too." She climbed down, stamped feeling back into her legs, and lowered her voice as though the mountain itself might be listening. "Reyes's permit's been challenged. A company called Hidroandina filed a counter-claim in Esquel ten days ago. They've got lawyers, they've got senators, and they've got inspectors. Two men from the provincial water authority, flying in with a survey team. Word is they'll be at your basecamp inside two weeks, maybe less if the weather holds."

Bruno felt the words land somewhere below his ribs, where he kept the things he didn't say out loud. "Two weeks," he repeated.

"That's what I'm told. There's paperwork in the pouch. Signature required." She held out a clipboard with the cheerful indifference of someone delivering other people's disasters daily.

He signed. His hand left a smear of grease across the carbon copy, a black thumbprint like a confession.

* * *

Tomás took the news standing, which Bruno had expected. Tomás took everything standing — meetings, betrayals, weather. He read the inspector's notice twice, folded it along its creases until it was small enough to disappear into his fist, and then stood looking north toward the col where the glacier bent around the shoulder of the Sierra del Silencio like water bending around a stone.

"Two weeks," he said. It wasn't a question. It was arithmetic.

"The Otter can make two more supply runs," Bruno said. "Three if we push the weight limits, and I'd rather not push them with this wind pattern coming down off the icecap. Yara will tell you the same."

"We won't need three." Tomás turned, and there was something in his face Bruno hadn't seen since the old days — not the polished calm he wore like a good jacket, but the thing underneath it, quick and bright and slightly wrong. "We accelerate. Dr. Ibarra drills around the clock. I want the full core sequence out of the summit site within nine days."

"Nine days." Bruno laughed, and it came out ugly. "Ibarra told me himself the deep cores need slow extraction or they fracture. You'll get ice full of cracks and call it data."

"I'll get ice, and it will be enough." Tomás was already walking toward the drill tent, talking faster now, the words tumbling like scree. "Ibarra works nights, Sofía logs, you keep the generator fed and the Otter schedule tight. Nobody leaves camp. Nobody radios out except scheduled check-ins. When the inspectors come, we are a routine mineral survey that has nothing to hide and nothing to show them but rock samples."

"And the cores?"

Tomás stopped. For a moment the wind filled the silence, moving through the camp with its long dry hiss, the sound the valley towns had named the range after — *el silencio*, the silence that wasn't silence at all.

"The cores go in sealed cases," Tomás said. "Chain-of-custody seals. Only I hold the keys."

* * *

They built the crating that evening in the mess tent, by lamplight, because Tomás wanted it done before dark and done his way. Aluminum flight cases, the kind used for instruments, lined with foam cut to fit each core section. Ibarra worked silently, sliding the day's ice into its slots with the tenderness of a man laying out the dead, while Tomás fitted the hasps and snapped the numbered seals shut, one after another, each click sharp as a bone breaking.

Bruno watched the keys. There were six cases and one ring, and the ring went into Tomás's breast pocket, and Tomás patted it flat the way a man pats a wound to be sure it isn't bleeding.

"You realize how that looks," Bruno said quietly.

Tomás glanced up. Around them the lamplight made amber pools, and beyond the tent walls the blue dusk of the glacier was settling into true dark. "How what looks."

"A man sealing evidence in boxes only he can open." Bruno set down the foam knife. "That's not chain of custody, Tomás. That's a lockbox. Inspectors aren't stupid. They see one man holding all the keys, they start asking why."

"They'll see proper procedure." Tomás smiled, and it was the smile from the hangar, from the day he'd come walking down the strip with money and permits and an old debt dressed up as opportunity. "You signed on for triple rates, Bruno. Triple rates buy discretion."

"Triple rates buy my labor. Not my eyes closed."

For a moment neither of them moved. Ibarra looked from one to the other and found something fascinating to study in his sample log. Outside, the generator throbbed, and somewhere Sofía's voice drifted faintly from the comms tent, reading coordinates into the static.

"You backed me," Tomás said. Softer now. That was the dangerous register, Bruno knew — the voice Tomás used when he wanted to remind you of something rather than argue about it. "In the vote. I remember. You told Yara the past stays buried and the living eat. Your words."

"My words." Bruno straightened. His knees cracked; he was fifty-one years old and every winter of the dry slope lived in them. "And I meant them. But I backed a mineral survey. A survey doesn't need prisoner crates. A survey doesn't need a man guarding ice like it's gold bullion. So either you tell me what's in those cores worth hiding, or—"

"Or what?" Tomás stepped closer. Up close, in the bad light, he looked older than his years and younger than his sins, both at once. "Or you'll do what? Walk out? We're four hours' flight from anywhere, and the Otter flies Yara's schedule, and Yara signed with me same as you. You'd walk down the valley on foot and explain to your creditors why the triple rate wasn't enough?"

It was the creditors that did it. Not the threat — Bruno had been threatened by better men in worse bars — but the casualness of it, the way Tomás reached into Bruno's life and turned over the stones he kept his shame under, without even looking, the way you'd flip a stone to check for scorpions.

Bruno's fist was moving before he decided anything. Tomás caught it on the forearm — he'd always been fast, that was the unforgivable thing about him, fast and lucky — and then they were grappling among the crates, knocking a lamp so it swung wild shadows across the tent walls, two grown men breathing hard through their noses like bulls, Ibarra shouting *basta, basta* and throwing himself between them with his thin arms spread wide.

They broke apart. Bruno's chest heaved. Tomás straightened his collar with two precise tugs, and there was a red mark on his forearm already rising through the fabric, and his eyes had gone very calm, which was worse than anger.

"Nine days," Tomás said. "Then the cases fly out under seal, the inspectors see rock samples, and everyone gets paid. Including you." He picked up the key ring, weighed it once in his palm, and pocketed it. "Think about whose side hunger is on, Bruno. It's always been on mine."

He ducked out into the dark. The tent flap fell shut behind him, and the cold rushed in to fill the space he'd occupied, and Bruno stood there with his knuckles aching and the swinging lamp slowly steadying above six locked cases of ice.

* * *

He couldn't sleep. He told himself it was the altitude, the light, the generator's rhythm through the tent wall — the honest excuses — and then gave up on honesty and sat outside in the cold with a tin cup of coffee gone bitter, watching the stars wheel over the col.

Yara found him there near midnight. She came out of the dark without a sound, the way she did everything, and sat down on the supply crate beside him without asking. For a long time neither spoke. That was the luxury of twenty years of friendship: silence that needed no explanation.

"Sofía says you nearly broke his arm," Yara said finally.

"He caught it. Mostly." Bruno turned the cup in his hands. "Yara. The maps he brought. The ones he claims came from a survey office in Buenos Aires."

"What about them?"

"They've got contour lines on the east cirque that don't exist on any government sheet I've ever flown. I checked, back at the hangar, before we voted. I checked and I said nothing, because the money was good and the strip is failing and I told myself a map's a map." He stared into the cup as if the coffee might show him something. "Nobody draws contours like that from a desk. Somebody walked that cirque. Somebody stood where Ferro died and wrote down what he saw, and then came home and lied to our faces about never going back."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Above them the Sierra del Silencio held its shape against the stars, black on blacker, keeping whatever it kept.

"Why tell me now?" she asked.

"Because today he locked the ice in boxes and put the only keys in his pocket." Bruno set the cup down. "Because I voted yes, Yara. My vote tipped it. And because in nine days the inspectors land, and Tomás hands them sealed cases and a story, and if that story is a lie, then my name is on the manifest next to his, and I'm too old and too broke to start over twice."

The wind moved down the glacier, patient as time. Yara pulled her collar up and looked toward the drill tent, where a light still burned — Ibarra working his night shift, coaxing the mountain's memory out in cylinders of ice.

"Two weeks," she said at last. "Maybe less."

"Maybe less," Bruno agreed.

She stood, brushed frost from her trousers, and paused. In the starlight her face was unreadable, but her voice, when it came, was quiet and level and it frightened him more than Tomás's smile ever had.

"Get some sleep, Bruno. Tomorrow I'm going up to the summit site. I want to see what the ice keeps — and I want to see it before Tomás decides we've seen enough."

She walked back toward the tents, a dark figure against the pale field, and Bruno sat alone with the cold and the ache in his knuckles, listening to the silence that wasn't silence, and understood that whatever they had flown into this range to find, they were no longer the ones doing the finding.
