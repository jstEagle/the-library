## 7. Summit of Silence

The col had no name on any map Tomás carried, which was itself a kind of confession. Yara saw it first from two hundred meters below — a saddle of wind-scoured ice between the two highest towers of the Sierra del Silencio, a notch of gray sky where the mountain seemed to have been cut open with a knife. Twenty years ago, Old Ferro had pointed at it from the valley floor and said, *There. That's where I'll stop.* He hadn't meant it as prophecy. He'd meant it as a boundary, the line past which he would not take two young climbers he loved too much to bury.

Now Yara stood at the base of that line with an ice axe in her hand and Tomás Reyes three meters behind her, breathing hard in the thin air, and she understood that boundaries are only real for people who intend to keep them.

The final ascent party was four: Yara, Tomás, Sofía, and Dr. Ibarra, who moved across the glacier like a man visiting a library he'd only ever read about. Bruno had stayed at basecamp with the radio and the core cases, watching them the way a man watches a dog he no longer trusts around children. The morning had begun clear and windless, which on the dry side of Patagonia was not a blessing but a held breath. By nine the wind had arrived from the west, steady as a freight train, carrying ice crystals that stung the face like thrown sand.

They climbed roped, spaced wide. Yara led because her body still remembered this mountain the way a tongue remembers a burned palate — automatically, unwillingly. Every crevasse bridge they crossed, every slope angle that steepened past forty degrees, her hands knew before her eyes did. She had spent ten years telling herself she'd forgotten the Sierra del Silencio. The mountain had spent ten years waiting to correct her.

* * *

They reached the col at noon, and the wind dropped all at once, the way it does in these ranges — not easing but simply ceasing, as if the mountain had finished saying what it came to say. The silence that followed was so complete Yara could hear the tick of the ice settling beneath their boots, the slow conversation of a glacier moving at the speed of grief.

Sofía sank onto a rock at the saddle's edge, cheeks bloodless above her balaclava. Ibarra immediately began photographing the stratigraphy of the serac wall, muttering about accumulation layers, already somewhere else in his head. And Tomás stood looking north, toward the summit dome, his gloved hands on his hips, wearing the expression of a man surveying property he already owned.

Yara did not look at the summit. She looked down.

Twenty years ago she had stood on this same saddle, nineteen years old and invincible, while Ferro's rope team went up into the whiteout above. She remembered the exact sound of the mountain taking him — not a roar, nothing dramatic, just a deep percussive *whump* rolling down from the fog, and then the long, idiotic quiet afterward, the quiet of people standing in snow listening for a voice that was never going to answer again. They had searched for six days. The mountain had given back nothing. The inquiry that followed had given back plenty: Ferro's route log falsified, his survey data inconsistent, his judgment called into question by a report signed by his own protégé. Tomás Reyes had written that report. Yara had signed herself as witness, nineteen and hollowed out, believing what the paper told her because the alternative — that the man beside her had built the lie — was unthinkable.

She had carried the blame for twenty years anyway. That was the thing about guilt: it doesn't check whether it belongs to you. It moves in like weather.

"Yara." Tomás's voice, careful. "We should push for the dome before the wind returns. Two hours up, two hours down."

"I want to check the crevasse field east of the col first," she said. "Ferro's camp was there."

Tomás went very still. It was a small stillness, the kind most people wouldn't catch, but Yara had been reading Tomás Reyes since they were both teenagers stealing fuel for joyrides over Lago Buenos Aires. Stillness was what he did instead of flinching.

"There's nothing there," he said. "Twenty years of accumulation. It's buried."

"Then it'll take ten minutes to confirm that."

Ibarra glanced between them, sensing old voltage, and wisely turned back to his seracs. Sofía rose slowly, brushing rime from her knees, her eyes on Yara with the particular attention of someone who has learned, over thirty days of expedition, when her pilot is about to do something irreversible.

* * *

The crevasse field lay a quarter hour east, a chaos of blue fractures where the glacier poured over a buried step in the bedrock. Yara navigated by dead reckoning and memory, counting moraine ribs, and stopped where her body told her to stop — above a crevasse whose lip had partially collapsed inward, forming a shallow depression like a cupped hand.

She knew before she saw it. Some part of her had always known.

The snow bridge had sagged but not sealed, and through the gap, wedged in the blue shadow below, she could see fabric. A triangle of faded orange — tent nylon, the color Ferro had insisted on because, he said, orange is the color men can find when God turns off the lights.

"Sofía. Rope."

They anchored and Yara went down into the crevasse fifteen meters, into air that smelled of stone and centuries, her headlamp cutting a cone through darkness that had never once been disturbed. The shelter was intact. Ferro's emergency camp — tent, stove, a coil of rope, everything stowed with the methodical care of a man who expected to come back for it. He had crawled in here during the storm, Yara realized, hurt or simply pinned by the whiteout, and he had organized his own dying. That was Ferro. Even the mountain couldn't make him untidy.

The journal was in the tent's inner pocket, wrapped in a plastic bag sealed with tape, and beneath it a second bundle: his original survey notebook, the one the inquiry had declared lost.

Her hands shook so badly she could barely work the zipper.

* * *

She read it sitting in the snow at the crevasse rim, back to the wind, Sofía reading over her shoulder with her breath held. The handwriting was Ferro's — blunt, slanted left, the letters of a man who wrote the way he climbed, without ornament.

The first pages were routine. Barometric readings, route notes, complaints about the freeze-dried food. Then, dated the third day of the storm:

*T. came up yesterday against my orders. He brought the revised survey sheets and wanted my signature. The numbers are wrong. I ran the flow gauges myself in the valley for eleven years and I know what the Río Blanco gives and what it doesn't. These sheets show triple the yield. Someone downstream is selling water that isn't there.*

*I told him I would report it. He smiled at me. That smile. Nineteen years I've watched that boy lie with his whole face and call it charm.*

*He took the sheets back down. If anything happens to me, look at the chain of custody on those surveys. Look at who countersigned. I am an old man and my name is worth more than my life now, and I think he knows it.*

The last entry, pencil pressed so hard it had nearly torn the page:

*Storm again. In the tent. If I don't come down, tell Yara the fault was never hers. Tell her she signed what she was shown. Tell her the shame belongs to the man who showed it.*

Yara closed the book.

For a long moment she could not breathe, and it was not the altitude. Something that had lived in her chest for twenty years — a cold, dense knot she had mistaken for part of her own anatomy — loosened all at once, and the pain of its leaving was worse than the pain of its presence. Grief, she discovered, does not end when you learn the truth. It changes state. It stops being a weight you carry and becomes a blade you hold, and you must decide, very quickly, what to do with your hands.

"Yara." Sofía's voice was barely audible. Her dark eyes were enormous above her balaclava. "This is — this says he sold the valley's water. Our water. Before any of us were born, almost. He let Ferro die with everyone thinking—"

"He didn't let him die." Yara heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "He made sure the mountain got the credit."

Below them, at the edge of the crevasse field, Tomás stood alone, facing the summit dome, his back to them. Whether he had seen them descend, whether he had counted the minutes and done the arithmetic, Yara couldn't say. But he stood the way men stand when they know the ground under them has changed composition.

Sofía touched her arm. "What do we do?"

Yara looked at the journal, at the survey notebook with its honest numbers, at the orange scrap of tent still visible in the blue dark below. She thought about the estancias waiting for their medicine flights. About Sofía's village and its failing aquifer. About Bruno's vote, cast in good faith, and the hangar with its cracked apron and its ledger full of red.

She thought about a pen hovering over a chain-of-custody form, somewhere in the next few days, waiting for her signature.

"We go down," Yara said. "All of us. Together. And nobody says a word until I decide how this ends."

She tucked the journals inside her jacket, against her skin, where the cold would not get them and neither, she prayed, would Tomás Reyes.

Above them, the wind returned all at once, screaming down from the summit dome like something released from custody — and far below, faint through the roar, Yara thought she heard the engine of a supply plane arriving early, carrying inspectors, deadlines, and the last hours of Tomás's thirty days.
