## 8. Whiteout Bargain

The storm did not arrive; it was simply *there*, the way a verdict is there, sudden and total. One moment Tomás Reyes had been watching the last light fail on the western ridgeline, calculating hours, and the next the world had been erased — summit dome, col, sky, all of it swallowed into a white so dense it seemed less like weather than like absence. The wind came through the fabric of the tents not as sound but as pressure, a fist that never finished closing.

He sat in the larger of the two dome tents with his back against the gear duffels and listened to the mountain try to unmake them. Somewhere beyond the nylon, Yara Almeida was in the other tent with Sofía and Bruno, and somewhere between the two shelters — no more than four meters of screaming white — lay the journal he needed to talk about.

He had known this conversation was coming since the crevasse. He had watched Yara's face when she'd come out of the ice shelter with Ferro's journal pressed inside her jacket, and he had read her the way he read everything: quickly, coldly, completely. She hadn't looked at him with hatred. Hatred would have been manageable. She had looked at him with *recalculation*, with the terrible arithmetic of a woman discovering that a debt she had carried for twenty years had never been hers to pay — which meant someone else owed it, and she was deciding what to charge.

Tomás poured himself two fingers of the pisco he'd carried up in a vacuum flask and did not drink it. He held the warmth of it in his palm instead, an anchor.

*You are not corrupting her*, he told himself, the way he had told himself every night for twenty years. *You are rescuing her. There is a difference, and the difference is that you know where the money is.*

* * *

He waited until the third hour of full dark, when even the storm seemed to tire, the wind dropping to something merely murderous. Then he clipped into the fixed line between the tents and crossed the four meters with his head down, and when Yara unzipped the flap at his knock, the lantern light fell across her face and showed him exactly what he expected: exhaustion, suspicion, and beneath both, the old loyalty, still alive, still stubborn, still the thing he was counting on.

"Five minutes," he said.

"You get three."

Inside, Sofía was asleep — or performing sleep, curled in her bag with her back to them, close enough to the tent wall that Tomás noted it without noting it, the way he noted everything. Bruno was on watch rotation in the cook tent. It was as private as this mountain allowed.

Yara sat with her arms around her knees. The jacket she wore was the one with the journal in it. She wanted him to know that. She wanted him to see that she knew he knew.

"Twenty years ago," Tomás began quietly, "Ferro stood where you're sitting and made me the same offer I'm about to make you. Did you know that?"

Yara's eyes didn't move. "Ferro didn't make offers. Ferro made promises."

"He made me an offer," Tomás said. "And I refused it, and then I made a different choice, and you've spent twenty years believing that choice killed him." He leaned forward. "It didn't, Yara. Listen to me. Nothing on this mountain killed him except the mountain. What killed your *faith* — that was paperwork. That was me, at a desk in Río Gallegos, signing a survey amendment that moved the watershed boundary eleven kilometers upstream. Eleven kilometers. Enough to put three valleys' water under a concession title that didn't exist yet." He spread his hands. "I was twenty-six years old and the company owned my signature the way they own everyone's. Ferro found out. Ferro took the blame rather than name me, because naming me would have ended the expedition funding and stranded us all on this glacier. And you —" his voice caught, and he let it, because the catch was true, or true enough — "you were nineteen, and you decided your mentor's disgrace was your inheritance. You built a whole life out of carrying it. A decade of medicine runs and gossip and goodwill, flying for estancias because you couldn't stand to fly for anyone who might ask about Sierra del Silencio."

"Don't," Yara said. Her voice was very level. "Don't you dare use him to sell me something."

"I'm not selling. I'm *offering*. There's a difference, and the difference is —"

"You said that already."

"The concession expires in—" he checked his watch, an absurd gesture in a whiteout, and knew it was absurd, and did it anyway because ritual steadied him— "eleven days. The inspectors arrive early. When they do, I present sealed cores, a chain of custody, and a pilot-in-command signature. Yours. In exchange: partnership. Not employment — partnership. Fifteen percent of the concession, Yara. Do you understand what these water rights will be worth in ten years? The aquifers below this range are the only ones in the province that aren't failing. Every town from here to the coast will drink from what we hold. Your hangar gets rebuilt in stone. Bruno retires with a pension. Sofía's village gets its infrastructure — pipelines, treatment, the works — funded by the concession itself. We don't steal from the valley, we *own* the valley's water honestly, on paper, and we spend it back into the ground it came from." He heard his own voice rise with conviction and let it, because the conviction was real. That was the thing no one understood about him. The conviction was real. "Keep the journal quiet. That's all. One document, kept quiet, and everyone lives better than any of you ever dreamed. Ferro forgave me before he died. I know he did. Why can't you?"

The lantern hissed. Wind leaned on the tent.

"Ferro is dead," Yara said at last, "because of a lie you signed. And now you want me to sign the next one."

"I want you to sign the truth," Tomás said. "The truth is complicated. The journal is just grief. Grief makes bad law, Yara. I'm offering you the chance to be *right* instead of *vindicated*. They're not the same thing. Vindication is what people chase when they'd rather burn the house down than fix it."

For a long moment she said nothing, and he watched her, and he believed — he genuinely, catastrophically believed — that he saw the arithmetic working behind her eyes. The failing strip. The accounts. Sofía's aquifer. Bruno's hands, ruined by decades of engine work. He had chosen his numbers well. He always chose his numbers well.

"Three minutes are up," Yara said finally. "I'll think about it."

"That's all I ask."

She turned away, and he zipped himself out into the dark, and the storm took him back across the line like a tide taking a swimmer.

* * *

In the tent he did not sleep. He sat with the pisco untouched and rehearsed the inspectors' meeting, the forged custody forms, the exact pressure of Yara's pen on paper, and he felt — this was the remarkable thing, the thing he would remember later — he felt *good*. Clean, almost. He had laid the whole rotten architecture bare to her. No more lies between them, only a shared silence, and shared silences were the strongest structures he knew. Empires were built on them.

What he did not hear — what no one could have heard through the wind but what traveled anyway, the way all secrets travel on the dry side of Patagonia, by radio, by rumor, by ear pressed to canvas — was Sofía, awake in her bag with her cheek nearly touching the tent wall, memorizing every word.

Eleven kilometers. Upstream. Twenty years ago. *Ferro took the blame.*

She lay in the dark and thought of her village's dying wells, of Tomás's generous promise of pipes, and she understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, exactly how the offer worked: he wasn't buying their silence. He was buying their *complicity* — making them co-owners of the theft so that exposing him would mean exposing themselves.

Outside, the storm began, very slowly, to die.

And far below, at the glacier's toe, lights moved among the supply caches where no lights should be — early, official, patient — while in the largest tent Tomás Reyes finally slept, dreaming of signatures, unaware that by dawn he would need three of them, and that only one would ever be given.
