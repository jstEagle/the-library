## 9. Descent

The storm broke the way storms do on the dry side — all at once, like a hand opening. One hour the world was white and screaming, and the next the wind had spent itself against the cordillera and gone east to die over the steppe, leaving behind a sky scrubbed clean and cold enough to make the metal of the Cessna's struts sing.

Yara stood at the edge of basecamp and watched the peaks emerge from the cloud one by one, white shoulders first, then the black teeth of the ridgelines, then finally the summit itself — the high col where Old Ferro had died, where she had knelt in a crevasse shelter two days ago with his journal open in her mittened hands and read her own history rewritten in his careful, freezing script.

*The survey was falsified. The rights were sold upstream. I signed nothing. They made certain my name was on everything.*

She had carried those words down off the ice in the breast pocket of her flight jacket, close to her heartbeat, and they had not grown lighter with the altitude. If anything they had grown heavier, the way stones do when you carry them out of a river and into the dry air, all their water gone, nothing left but weight.

Behind her, the camp was coming apart in efficient silence. Bruno and Sofía struck tents and coiled rope with the economy of people who had done this a hundred times, and Dr. Ibarra packed her drilling equipment with the tenderness of a woman saying goodbye to something she loved. The core samples sat in their aluminum cases near the tent Tomás had claimed for himself, each case locked, each key living in the pocket of Tomás Reyes's parka.

He emerged from that tent now, zipping it closed behind him as if sealing something inside, and crossed the trampled snow to where Yara stood. Up here, with the wind gone, his voice carried easily, and he kept it low anyway. He always kept it low when it mattered.

"Weather window holds until tomorrow noon," he said. "Three flights. Cores go first, with me. Then Ibarra and the heavy gear. You and your crew last."

"I know how to load my own plane."

"I know you do." He looked out at the mountains, at the range he had once sworn he'd never seen and which Yara now knew he had walked in secret, mapping, measuring, selling. "It's good flying weather. Clean air after a storm. You'll want to be in it."

What he meant was: *stop looking at me like that.* What she heard was: *you signed nothing yet either.*

"Tomás." She turned to face him fully. At this altitude, this close to the col, it felt right to say what she said plainly, without the armor of sarcasm she'd worn for ten years. "I found Ferro's journal."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. She'd expected surprise and been denied even that. What moved there was calculation, quick and cold as meltwater finding a crack in rock.

"I know," he said. "Sofía told me."

Sofía. Of course. The girl who had voted no until her village's dying aquifer was dangled in front of her, who had listened through tent canvas because listening was the only power she had. Yara filed the betrayal away — or maybe it wasn't a betrayal; maybe Sofía had simply decided, as Yara herself was deciding, whose side the truth belonged to.

"And?" Yara said.

"And we'll talk about it on the ground. There's a time for everything, Yara. On a mountain is not the time." He put his hand briefly on her shoulder, an old gesture from an old life, and she let it sit there for exactly as long as it took her to decide not to shrug it off. "Fly well. That's all I ask today. Fly well and land safe, and let tomorrow be tomorrow."

He walked back toward the cases, and Yara watched him go, and above them both the summit stood in its silence, keeping what it kept.

* * *

The first flight out was smooth as poured cream. The second was rougher — turbulence spilling off the warming slopes in brown rivers of air — but by then Yara flew on instinct older than thought, and the Cessna rode the chaos the way a gull rides a squall. She ferried Ibarra and the drills down to the strip at Río Blanco, went back for the last of the gear, and on the final run she took Sofía and Bruno and the journal and herself, and left the Sierra del Silencio behind her in the mirror, white and blameless-looking, the way guilty things always look from a distance.

They came down the eastern slope into light the color of weak tea, the steppe unrolling beneath them in its endless tan stitching of guanaco trails and fence lines. Estancias passed below like islands. She knew them all by name, by windsock, by the particular way each owner waved — or didn't — when she thundered overhead. A decade of medicine and gossip and engine parts, stitched together with fuel receipts. It wasn't much. It was everything. And every mile of it, she understood now, had been built on ground Tomás had sold out from under it twenty years ago.

"You're quiet," Bruno said from the right seat. He'd insisted on riding along, though the Cessna didn't need two up front for a ferry leg. His hands were folded on his knees, which was what Bruno did instead of wringing them.

"Flying," Yara said.

"You've been flying for eleven hours. You're quiet *at* me."

She glanced at him. His face was weathered and honest and afraid, and she realized he already knew — had probably known since the crevasse, since the night Tomás sealed the cores, since the fists that had almost flown at basecamp. Bruno had backed Tomás in the vote. Bruno had argued for the money. And loyalty, once cracked, doesn't heal clean; it heals around the crack, and you feel the seam every time you flex.

"Bruno," she said, "when we land — whatever happens — remember that you voted yes because we were starving, not because you were wrong about anything else."

"That's not—"

"It is. It's the only reason any of us are on this mountain's payroll. Hunger votes yes. It always has." She banked gently around a column of dust rising off the plain, a whirlwind walking east on its own errands. "The question isn't whether we needed the money. The question is what we were paid to carry."

Bruno was silent a long moment. Below them the fuel stop appeared — Estancia La Querencia's dirt strip, a windsock hanging limp in the evening stillness, a single fuel bowser parked beside a shed with a corrugated roof. Routine. She'd stopped there forty times.

Except the shed wasn't empty.

Two vehicles stood beside it, white with government plates, and four figures in dark jackets stood in the windless gold light, and even from three hundred feet Yara could see the clipboard in the tallest one's hands.

"They're early," Sofía said from behind her. Her voice was very steady, which was how Yara knew she was terrified. "Inspectors don't come early. Inspectors come late, with apologies. These came early."

Yara keyed the radio, called the strip, got a bored confirmation of wind and surface, and brought the Cessna down onto the dirt with the tires kissing rather than landing, because some animal part of her already understood that this arrival was being watched, measured, recorded. The inspectors did not wave.

* * *

Tomás met them on the ramp with the aluminum cases already unloaded and arranged in a neat row, and he was smiling, and the smile was the most frightening thing Yara had seen in thirty days on the mountain.

"Gentlemen," he said, extending his hand to the tall inspector. "Reyes Exploraciones. You're ahead of schedule — welcome. Everything is prepared."

"Señor Reyes." The inspector did not smile back. His name, according to the laminated badge, was Ferrer. "We received notice that samples would be moving under permit SR-114. We're here to verify chain of custody before transport."

"Of course." Tomás produced a folder from his expedition bag — a folder Yara had never seen, thick with carbon copies and official stamps, documents that must have been printed in some town office while they were all freezing on the glacier, documents that existed only because Tomás Reyes had spent twenty years learning how paper could lie. "Manifest. Custody log. Sealing certificates from the site, countersigned by the drill supervisor. Dr. Ibarra can confirm—"

"Dr. Ibarra confirmed by radio this morning," Ferrer said. "What we require now is the pilot-in-command signature. All transport under concession permit requires it. The aircraft operator assumes legal custody of the samples in transit." He turned to Yara, and held out the clipboard, and on it was a form with a line at the bottom, and beside the line, printed in small bureaucratic type:

*Piloto al mando — firma.*

Her name was already typed above it. ALMEIDA, Y. Not requested. Assumed. As if it had never been a question, as if her hand belonged to Tomás the way the plane did, the way the crew did, the way the whole valley's water did.

The pen was clipped to the board. A cheap ballpoint, blue ink, the kind that skipped in cold weather. The evening was cooling fast. The pen would skip.

"Yara." Tomás's voice, low again, pitched for her alone while the inspectors examined seals and Bruno stood very still by the fuel bowser and Sofía stood very still by the tail. "Look at me."

She looked at him. Ten years fell away and there he was at twenty-six, standing over Ferro's grave with snow in his hair, promising never again, and she had believed him because grief makes believers of us all.

"The cores are legitimate," he murmured. "The science is real. All this does is move ice from one freezer to another. But if you refuse to sign, the permit dies tonight, the rival company takes the concession, and every peso we haven't yet earned disappears — including the infrastructure contract for Sofía's valley. Including your hangar. Including Bruno's shop. You sign, we fly home, and tomorrow we talk about the journal like adults. You have my word."

"Your word," Yara said quietly, "is the most expensive thing in Patagonia."

His jaw tightened. "Yara—"

"Because everyone who ever bought it paid with someone else's life."

Ferrer cleared his throat. "Señora Almeida? The light is failing. We'd prefer to complete this before dark."

The pen hovered over the line. Blue ink. Cheap ballpoint. Her whole life balanced on the width of a signature — the hangar, the strip, the accounts Bruno kept worrying like a bone, the work that fed eleven estancias and their dogs and their children and their radios. All of it hers to keep, if she wrote her name. All of it complicit, if she wrote her name. There was no third line on the form. There never was. The forms only ever offered you the two ways to be owned.

And underneath the fear, calm as deep water, the other thing — the thing Ferro had put in her hands in a crevasse two days ago, written in pencil because he'd known ink freezes: *Tell them what was taken. Tell them who took it.*

The pen touched paper.

She wrote.

One letter. A single, deliberate stroke — the beginning of her name, the beginning of anything — and then she lifted the pen, and looked up past Ferrer's shoulder at the east, where the first star was coming out over the steppe, over the villages, over the aquifer dying under Sofía's home, and she said:

"Actually — no. I need to check one document first. My aircraft's custody log. Regulations require me to verify manifests against my own records before signing." She handed the clipboard back with the half-letter drying on it, blue and unfinished, like a door left open. "Give me until morning. The permit runs thirty days. It runs one more night."

Ferrer frowned, consulted his watch, and nodded slowly. "First light, then. We'll be here."

Tomás was looking at her with an expression she had never seen on him before — not anger, not charm, but something naked and young and cornered. "Yara," he said, just her name, just the sound of it, the sound she'd trusted across a decade of dead reckoning.

She picked up the journal-weight against her chest and walked toward the hangar shed, toward the radio that lived inside it, toward the open band that reached every estancia in the valley.

"One more night," she said to no one, to everyone, to the mountain still visible in the west, going purple in the last light. "Then everyone hears."
