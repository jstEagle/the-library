## 10. Broadcast

The hangar smelled of kerosene and old canvas, the way it had for ten years, and Yara stood in the middle of it knowing that by morning it might not be hers. The permit expired at midnight. Tomás's lawyers had called twice that afternoon — she'd let the radio crackle and go unanswered — and the inspectors' report was due to be filed with the sealed cores as evidence of a clean survey. Her signature sat on the chain-of-custody form like a brand on a steer's flank. All she had to do was nothing, and the lie would become law, and the hangar would keep its roof, and Bruno would keep his wages, and Sofía's village would get its pipes.

All she had to do was nothing.

She opened Ferro's journal instead.

It lay on her workbench under a hanging lamp, its cover soft as chamois from twenty years of being carried, its pages swollen where meltwater had found them in the crevasse. She had read it three times since the descent, each pass stripping something away from her — first the hope that she'd misread it, then the hope that it mattered less than it did, and finally the last comfortable fiction that this was Tomás's sin alone and not a debt the whole valley had been paying interest on ever since. The numbers were all there, in Ferro's cramped left-slanting hand: flow rates measured at the headwaters of the Río Blanco that matched no survey ever filed, aquifer draw-downs recorded season after season while the towns downstream were told their wells were simply failing, old and dry, unlucky. And beside them, in different ink, the corrections — the true figures, the ones Tomás had replaced before the expedition returned, the ones that had made Ferro's name a synonym for fraud in every ministry office between here and Buenos Aires.

*They will say I falsified it,* Ferro had written, in the last entry, dated four days before he died on the col. *Perhaps they already do. But ice keeps what men discard, and one day someone will drill down far enough to read it.*

Dr. Ibarra had drilled down far enough. The cores in Tomás's sealed cases told the same story the journal did, layer by annual layer, dust and chemistry written in frozen water. Two records, independent, agreeing. Yara had spent the evening convincing herself that was enough.

She looked at the Cessna. It sat in the open hangar mouth like a patient animal, fuel tanks full — she'd filled them herself at dusk, telling Bruno it was for a dawn ferry run to El Maitén. He had believed her, or had been kind enough to seem to. That kindness sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone.

Ten o'clock. She switched on the radio set and let it warm, the tubes glowing amber in the dark hangar, and dialed through the bands out of habit. Estancia chatter, the way it always was at night: the Roca place reporting a lost ewe, someone two hundred kilometers south complaining about wind, a voice from Gobernador Costa reading prices. The whole dry slope of Patagonia, strung together on frequencies nobody official bothered to monitor, talking to itself in the dark because it was the only company it had.

Yara had spent a decade being part of that conversation. Medicine and gossip, that was her cargo. Tonight she would carry something heavier.

She picked up the handset. Her hand was steady. She noticed that, and was grateful for it, and understood that the steadiness was not calm but decision — the body finally agreeing with something the mind had settled days ago, up on the mountain, kneeling in the crevasse shelter with the journal open across her knees while the wind sang over the col where Ferro died.

"This is Charlie-Alfa-Bravo," she said, "Yara Almeida, airborne service, calling all stations on the open band. If you can hear me, stay with me. This will take a while."

Static, then silence, then silence deepening as stations stopped transmitting to listen. She could feel them out there — the estancias, the villages, the kitchens where radios glowed on shelves next to saints and photographs. Every settlement whose well had gone brackish. Every rancher who'd been told the water table was dying of natural causes. Every family that had paid Tomás Reyes's company for water pumped from rivers that had been theirs.

She began with Ferro's name, because he deserved to have it said correctly first. Then she read the journal. Not all of it — some pages were too water-damaged, some griefs too private — but all of the numbers, every figure, every date, reading slowly so that anyone taking notes could take them. Her voice sounded strange to herself in the hangar's iron quiet, flat and clear, a pilot's voice, a voice built for weather reports and mayday calls. She read until midnight came and went and the permit lapsed somewhere overhead in the dark, unmarked by anything but the second hand of her wristwatch.

Then she read the discrepancies. The filed survey against Ferro's measurements, line by line, the forgery laid bare in arithmetic that no lawyer could argue with and no inspector could ignore. She named the ministries. She named the company. She named Tomás Reyes, and said his name the way you say the name of the dead, without hatred, because hatred was a luxury the truth didn't need.

Somewhere past two in the morning, other voices joined her. A woman from Cushamen confirming that her village's aquifer tests, never released publicly, matched the journal exactly. An old shepherd who remembered Ferro, who had flown with him once, weeping openly on frequency and apologizing for it. A clerk from the water authority, whispering, saying only: *I have copies. God forgive me, I have had copies for years.* The band that had been hers became everyone's, and the story traveled down the dry slope faster than any aircraft could carry it, station to station, kitchen to kitchen, the way news had always traveled out here — by trust, which was the only infrastructure that had never failed them.

At four, a new voice cut in, clipped and official: aviation authority, ordering her to cease transmission, citing an unlicensed broadcast. She read the last page of the journal instead, Ferro's final words, and signed off with her full name and her license number, giving them everything they needed to take it from her.

"Let the record show," she said, "that Yara Almeida, pilot-in-command, made this statement freely."

She shut off the radio. The hangar was very quiet. Outside, the eastern sky had begun to think about light.

* * *

She flew anyway. There was nothing to fly for — no cargo, no contract, probably no license by noon — but she could not sit in the hangar waiting for the knock, and the plane wanted the air the way she wanted it, and dawn on the steppe was a thing she had never once refused. She lifted off into a sky rinsed pink and gold, the Sierra del Silencio standing blue and enormous behind her, and flew the old mail route low and slow, over country that had listened to her all night.

She thought about what it would cost. The hangar, certainly; Tomás's lawyers would strip the business in a week of countersuits. The license, likely. Maybe worse — obstruction, defamation, whatever charges could be assembled by men who owned the paperwork. She turned each loss over in her mind like a stone, testing its weight, and found that none of them outweighed the image of Sofía's village drawing clean water from a river that belonged to it again. Some debts you pay in money. This one she was paying in everything else, and it felt, strangely, like being paid.

She landed at the strip outside town just as the sun cleared the horizon, the Cessna settling onto the dirt with the soft crunch of tires on frost.

Two figures stood at the edge of the field. Sofía, arms crossed against the cold, and Bruno beside her, cap in his hands, both of them facing the plane as it taxied in — not waiting for her, she realized, the way you wait for news, but waiting for her the way you wait for someone coming home.

She shut down the engine and climbed out into the freezing gold light. Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Bruno put his cap back on, which from Bruno was a speech, and Sofía crossed the frost and took her arm.

"The radio at my mother's house played you all night," Sofía said. "Half the valley stayed up. They're saying the concession's dead — the ministry pulled it an hour ago. The rights revert." Her voice caught. "They're saying the water is ours."

"It always was," Yara said. "Ferro just wrote it down."

Bruno cleared his throat. "There's a man at the hangar," he said carefully. "Been waiting since before dawn. Says he flew all night to find you. Says—" he hesitated, glancing at Sofía, "—says he knew Old Ferro. Says he has the *other* half of the journal. The pages Ferro mailed south before the expedition ever went up."

Yara felt the ground tilt gently beneath her, the way it did when a new wind arrived. Twenty years she had thought the record ended in a crevasse. It seemed the ice had kept more than she knew — and so, apparently, had someone else.

She looked east, toward the hangar, toward the stranger, toward whatever came next.

"Well," she said, unbuckling her watch and turning it in the light. "Then we're not finished."
