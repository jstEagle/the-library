## 1. Fuel, Rumors, and Rain

The trouble with flying medicine into river villages is that the medicine is never the cargo. The medicine is the excuse. The cargo is gossip, and gossip weighs nothing and takes up all the room.

I learned that my first year in the Triangle, hauling penicillin up the Serawak tributaries in a Cessna 206 that had been old when I was young and was now older than both of us combined. A headman would meet me on the mudbank with a handshake and a list of what his people needed, and by the time the drums were loaded he'd be leaning in the door of the plane telling me about his cousin's wife, the Dutch survey team, the ghost light on the point, the man in the next valley who'd found something in the reef and wouldn't say what. I'd fly home with a full tank and a head full of other people's lives, and it kept me aloft in every way that mattered.

Ten years of that. Ten years of never landing twice in the same place.

It wasn't a superstition, whatever the fuel men at Kudat said. It was arithmetic. A bush pilot's margin is thin — weather, fuel, parts, the temper of officials — and every return visit thickens the margin with obligation. Stay the night once and you're a guest. Twice, a friend. Three times, and somebody's asking you to carry something you shouldn't, or asking why you never stay, or asking your name in a way that means they've heard it somewhere before. The Triangle is a big place full of small water, and the only way to stay free in it is to be a rumor yourself. The woman who lands once. Never the same strip twice. Ask around; nobody can tell you where she sleeps.

I was refueling at the Serawak mouth that morning, which broke my own rule by a hair — I'd been through in March — but the drum prices at Batu Tiga had gone criminal and the river strip at Kuala Serawak still charged like it was 1998, so I forgave myself. Rules are ballast. You jettison them when the load demands it, and you know exactly how much you've dropped.

The rain came in sideways off the estuary while old Pak Mat and I hand-pumped the first drum, and he talked the whole time, the way he always did, about his grandson's exam results and the price of coconut and the government boat that hadn't come in eleven weeks. I answered in the half-sentences of a woman watching a fuel gauge and a sky at the same time. The sky was the one to watch. Quake season building. The barometer on my panel had been twitchy for a week, dropping in little stutters like a heart with a murmur, and the long swell running into the river mouth had that wrongness to it that old fishermen read and pilots should learn to.

"Your medicine," Pak Mat said, when the drum was empty and the second one was open. "The fever village up past the bend. The quinine."

"All of it's for them. Two hundred tablets, and the syringes are in the blue case, and don't let anyone tell you the blue case is theirs because it isn't, it's the clinic's, I'll know."

He grinned with the four teeth he had left. "You'll know. You know everything, Crane."

"I know where my cases are."

"And the lights?" he said, casual as a man asking about weather, and my hands went still on the pump handle for exactly one beat too long.

"What lights?"

"On Karakel." He nodded north, where the horizon was a bruise under the rain. "The fishermen say before the last quake, before the season, there were lights out on the water by Karakel. Green-white, under the surface, moving like something swimming. And now with the season coming again, they're saying it's starting early. My cousin's boy saw it two nights past."

"Fishermen," I said, and started the pump again. "They see mermaids and Chinese submarines and the ghost of every man ever lost off that reef. Lights under water are plankton. It's always plankton."

"Maybe," Pak Mat said, in the tone of a man who has outlived every expert he ever met and intends to outlive me too. "But the boy said the light was in a line. Like a lamp in a hallway. Under the island."

I pumped fuel and let the rain hammer the wing above us and did not say what I thought, which was nothing, which was *plankton, old man, plankton*, and which was also, in some cellar of myself I kept locked, a picture of a green-white line under black water that I had not looked at in ten years and was not going to look at now.

Karakel Island sits alone off the eastern shoulder of the Triangle, forty miles from anything, a green fist of limestone with a reef collar and a reputation. Divers used to go out there before the accident season made the crossing ugly, and before — well. Before. There was a Spanish wreck story attached to it the way there's a Spanish wreck story attached to every island in this sea, and a survey team went out once, years ago, and came back with nothing and a dead man.

Not nothing. A dead man.

I finished the fuel, thanked Pak Mat, overpaid him because overpaying is cheaper than owing, and walked the quinine up the mud track to the fever village with the rain coming down my collar. The clinic was a room with a roof that worked and a nurse named Siti who worked harder than the roof. She counted the tablets twice, signed my book, and then held the pen a moment and looked at me with the particular expression of a woman about to hand me gossip of a higher grade than usual.

"You're going north?" she said.

"I'm going wherever the wind and the money say."

"Karakel way?"

"Why does everyone keep saying Karakel to me today?"

"Because of the lights," she said. "And because of the boat."

"What boat?"

"The salvage boat." She wrote something on her pad, tore the page off, and gave it to me, which is the kind of thing nurses do when they want a thing to be true. "It came past three days ago, going east, big and black, with cranes. The *Sable Fortune*. It stopped at the fuel depot at Tawau and the men there said the crew paid cash and asked questions about Karakel. About the reef. About whether anyone lived out there." She paused. "About whether anyone flew out there."

The rain got louder, or I did.

"Flew," I said.

"You're the only one who does, Coral. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knowing that was, I will note for the record, a failure of my entire system. Ten years of never landing twice, of being a rumor, and I was still *the only one who does*. A bush pilot's anonymity lasts exactly as long as she's the only pilot, and I had been the only pilot for a long time, because the Triangle eats bush pilots — eats their planes, their money, their nerve — and I had stayed eaten-resistant out of what I told people was stubbornness and what I knew, in the locked cellar, was something else.

"Salvage boats go where wrecks are," I said. "There are wrecks everywhere. It's a sea."

"Mm," Siti said, the way women in this country have said *mm* to evasive men since before the Spanish lost their galleon. "Fly careful, Coral Crane. The season's coming early this year. The birds went inland a month ago."

The birds going inland early is the oldest forecast in the Triangle and the only one that's never wrong. I took my receipt, took my empty blue case, and walked back down the mud track through rain that was beginning to have intentions.

* * *

The Cessna sat on the strip at the river's elbow with water beading on her wings and her tail tied to a coconut stump, and I walked around her the way I always do, the full inspection, hand on every surface, a ritual I could perform blind and sometimes essentially had. Her name is *Wren*, painted small under the port wing where the paint has faded to a whisper, and she is the last thing in my life I have never lied to. She shakes when the air is wrong. She tells me the truth about my landings. She has never once pretended to be fine.

I untied the tail, drained the sumps, checked the fuel caps twice because a capped-but-not-sealed tank in rain is how bush pilots die stupid, and climbed in, and the cabin smelled of fuel and quinine and the ghost of a hundred cargoes, and I sat there a moment before engine start the way I always do, hands in my lap, doing the arithmetic that keeps me alive.

Wind: east-southeast, fifteen, gusting twenty-five off the estuary, which meant a crosswind on this strip and a tailwind going north. Ceiling: low and lowering, but the rain cells were moving, there'd be gaps. Fuel: full, six hours with reserve. Quake season: building, the swell wrong, the birds gone inland, the barometer stuttering.

And Karakel forty miles north of my next stop, with lights in a line under the island and a black salvage boat asking after me by reputation.

I could go south. There was always a south. There was always a village with a fever and a mud strip and a man with a drum of fuel, and I could spend the whole season flying circles in the southern Triangle and never once point the nose at that green fist of limestone. I had, in fact, done exactly that for ten years. Karakel was the one place in this sea I had drawn a line around on my charts — not with a pen, nothing so honest, but with the way my hands moved the yoke, the way my flight plans bent, the way "wherever the wind and the money say" always somehow never said Karakel.

The trouble with a line drawn around a place for ten years is that it stops being a line and becomes a wall, and walls have doors, and the door in mine was standing open this morning with green-white light coming through it.

I started the engine because not starting it was a decision and I wasn't ready to make decisions, only motions. The Wren coughed, caught, settled into her old idle, and I ran up the engine watching the gauges do their honest work, and by the time I'd finished the runup I had constructed, out of nothing, a perfectly reasonable flight plan: fuel delivery to the trading post at Tanjung Laut, which is real and paid and forty miles *south* of Karakel, and then, if the weather allowed and the mood took me, a wide, innocent, entirely professional dogleg north on the return leg, because the direct route home passed within twenty miles of the island anyway, and twenty miles is nothing, and looking at an island from twenty miles is not landing on it, and looking is free.

This is how I talked to myself. Ten years of practice. I could build an alibi out of weather and waypoints the way Pak Mat built fish traps — quickly, and out of whatever was lying around.

I took off into the rain, crosswind correction in, wheels up — she's a fixed-gear, but in my head the wheels always come up, ten years of wishing they would — and turned north, because the wind and the money said, and because I said, and I have never once in my life confused the two.

* * *

The flight north was the kind that reminds you why you do this. The rain cells broke apart over the coast and the light came down in columns, silver on green water, and a pod of dolphins ran my shadow for two miles off the point, and the Wren flew like she was glad to be out of the river mouth, and for a while I was nothing but a woman in a working airplane in beautiful weather, and the cellar stayed locked.

Tanjung Laut is a stilt town over a brown bay, a trading post, a church, a football pitch that floods on spring tides, and a strip of crushed coral on the headland that I have landed on exactly once before, four years ago, which by my rules meant never again. I landed anyway. The delivery was real — outboard parts and antibiotics for the post — and I told myself the rule was about *staying*, not landing, which was a lie, and I filed the lie with all the others.

The post's manager was a Malay named Osman who had the eyes of a man who reads everything and repeats only what pays.

"Crane," he said, shaking my hand on the strip. "You're a rumor. We thought you'd retired."

"I'm a rumor who delivers outboard parts."

"So you are." He helped me unload, and then, while I signed his book, he said, in the tone of a man opening a door he'd been asked to open, "You came the long way."

"Fuel stop at the river."

"The long way," he said again, "is the way past Karakel."

I handed him the pen. "I came the way the weather allowed."

"Of course." He looked north, where the sky over the sea had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong, yellow-green under the black. "Then you'll have seen it."

"Seen what, Osman."

"The lights. The whole coast has seen them. Green-white, under the water, in a line, like a lamp in a hallway. My uncle fished off Karakel for forty years and he says he saw it once, the year of the big quake, the year the sea went out. And now it's back, and the season hasn't even turned." He gave me back my book. "And the salvage boat is anchored in the lee of the island since yesterday. Black hull. *Sable Fortune* on the stern. They sent a skiff in this morning asking for fuel and fresh water, and paying for it in cash, and asking, very friendly, whether any planes came this way."

I looked at my plane. He looked at my plane.

"And what did you tell them?" I said.

"I told them planes come and go," Osman said. "I told them the truth, which is that I couldn't say." He smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile, it was a careful one. "I like you, Crane. You brought my parts in weather no one else would fly. So I'll tell you something for free. The man from the boat — the one who paid — had a list. A real list, on paper. And the second thing on the list, under the island's name, was a date. Ten years old. And the third thing was a name."

The rain started again, soft on the coral strip, and I stood there with my book in my hand and my heart doing something I made it stop doing.

"Whose name?" I asked, and my voice came out level, which cost me.

"I can't read the writing well," Osman said. "It was a foreign name. Short. But the man said it like it mattered, and he looked at my face when he said it, to see if I knew it." He paused. "I didn't. But you do. I could see it land on you just now like a bird on a wire."

I got in the Wren. I started the engine. I taxied out to the head of the strip with the rain coming harder and the light going strange and green, and I sat at the threshold doing my arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out like this: a black salvage boat anchored off the one island I had spent ten years flying around, asking after a name, and lights in a line under the water like a lamp in a hallway, and a quake season coming early, and the birds gone inland a month.

I took off into it, north, no dogleg this time, no alibi, the island dead ahead under its bruised sky, and I told myself it was to have a look, just a look, from a safe altitude, professional caution, and the Wren flew through the rain toward Karakel and I let her, because the truth — the cellar truth, the locked-door truth — was that I had been flying toward that island for ten years, in circles, and today the circles simply got smaller.

The sea did something wrong about six miles out.

I felt it before I saw it — a shudder through the airframe, a flutter in the engine note like a skipped heartbeat, and then the water below changed. The swell, which had been running long and heavy from the east, went suddenly, horribly slack, and then it began to run the *other way*, back out toward open sea, and the water level in the bays along the coast started dropping as I watched, reefs surfacing that had no business surfacing, brown mudbanks emerging and steaming, the whole sea exhaling.

I had seen it once before, ten years ago, from the air, from a distance, on the worst day of my life. The sea going out. The ocean holding its breath.

"Earthquake," I said aloud, to the Wren, to nobody, and my hands were already moving — throttle, mixture, scanning the gauges — and the engine, which had fluttered, caught its rhythm again, and then, three miles from Karakel, with the tide running out of the world like water out of a broken bowl, it missed again, harder, and didn't fully come back.

The Wren and I looked at each other, the way we do.

"Well," I said. "Never land twice in the same place."

There was one place to land.
