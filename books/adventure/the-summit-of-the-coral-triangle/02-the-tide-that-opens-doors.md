## 2. The Tide That Opens Doors

The sea went out from Karakel Island like a guest leaving without saying goodbye.

I was eleven miles south when it happened, droning along at nine thousand feet with a load of veterinary antibiotics and a crate of someone's mail-order espresso machine — because even the archipelago's most remote missionaries have priorities — and the whole horizon shivered. Not the plane. The world. I'd felt tremors before; they come up through the stick as a faint buzzing, like the airframe clearing its throat. This one came down through my seat instead, which is backwards and wrong and the kind of thing a bush pilot files under *later*.

Below me, the water around Karakel pulled back.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've kept. The reef flats that normally sat under six feet of water stood bare and gleaming, fish flopping in the tide pools like an audience that had been told the show was cancelled. The channel where I'd once measured forty feet on the sounder looked shallow enough to walk across. And there — north shore, in rock I had photographed a dozen times over ten years of flying this coast — was a black opening in the cliff face that had never been there before.

A cave mouth. Big enough to fly a Cessna through, if Cessnas were stupid enough to try.

I did what any sensible pilot does with a phenomenon: I circled it twice, took photographs I'd never develop, and put it out of my mind, because Karakel Island is not on my list of places to linger. Nowhere is on my list of places to linger. That's the whole architecture of my life — I land, I unload, I take on fuel or passengers or gossip, and I leave before anyone can ask me a question I'd have to answer honestly. Never land twice in the same place. It's not superstition. It's policy. Policies are how you survive when your judgment can't be trusted.

The earthquake didn't care about my policies. Neither did my engine.

* * *

It started as a hiccup over the Sula Strait — a stutter in the RPM gauge that I first blamed on the gauge, because that's always step one of aircraft troubleshooting: assume the instruments are lying. Step two is assuming the fuel is lying. I'd taken on drums at a river village the day before, drums that had been sitting under a tarp next to a man who swore on his mother's grave they were clean. His mother may well be alive and honest. The fuel was neither.

By the time I was over open water with Karakel at my back, the engine was running rough enough that I could feel each cylinder making its own individual argument about whether to fire. The needle on the fuel pressure gauge sagged like a tired shoulder. I switched tanks, leaned the mixture, said several words I wouldn't repeat in front of the missionaries.

Here's the arithmetic nobody teaches you in flight school but every bush pilot knows by heart: with a sick engine, you don't cross water. You find dirt. And the only dirt within gliding distance — the only landmass for thirty miles in any direction — was Karakel Island. The island I had just spent two circles admiring from a professional distance. The island whose new cave mouth I had photographed and deliberately forgotten.

The universe has a sense of humor. It's just never been about me.

I put her down on the exposed reef flat on the north shore, which until four hours ago had been seafloor and was now a broad, wet apron of coral sand and dead coral heads, firm enough to hold a wheel if you picked your line. I picked my line. The engine quit entirely on short final, which saved me the trouble of shutting it down myself, and I rolled out in a long hiss of spray and sand and sat there in the sudden silence listening to my own heartbeat file a complaint.

Then I got out and looked at the cave.

* * *

Up close it was bigger than it had looked from the air — a vaulted mouth maybe sixty feet across, cut into volcanic rock that the retreating sea had stripped of twenty feet of water and ten thousand years of concealment. Water still poured out of it in a slow, steady exhale, draining off the island's interior through some drowned passage, and the smell coming out was old. Not rotten-old. *Old*-old. Stone and salt and time, the way a church smells, if churches were built underwater by people with secrets.

I should have camped on the beach. I had a tent, a sleeping bag, a thermos, and a decade of hard-earned wisdom about not going into holes in the ground on islands with bad reputations. Instead I stood at the mouth of the thing while the light went amber behind me, and I heard myself think: *just far enough in to get out of the wind.*

That's how it starts. That's how everything starts. Nobody ever falls into anything; they lean.

I went in with my headlamp and a flare gun, because I'm reckless, not stupid, and there's a difference, though I'll admit the border has moved around over the years.

The floor sloped gently upward, dry above the tideline, and the walls began almost immediately to do something walls aren't supposed to do. They were carved. Not naturally eroded, not barnacled accident — carved. Rows of figures in relief, worn soft by centuries of salt water, marching along the stone at chest height: ships with high stern castles, men with halberds, waves rendered in a style that wasn't Spanish exactly and wasn't local either but some negotiated language between the two, the way all trade becomes eventually a kind of conversation. A galleon's crew had been here. Or galleons' crews. And someone after them — much later, judging by the tooling — had added their own marks alongside, spirals and star-charts and a repeated symbol like a bird diving.

I stood there with salt water dripping somewhere in the dark ahead of me and felt the specific vertigo of standing inside a sentence someone started four hundred years ago and never finished.

"Okay," I said aloud, to no one, because the cave deserved at least one human voice acknowledging it. "Okay."

* * *

The chest was fifty yards in, past a chamber where the ceiling opened into a shaft of dying daylight, wedged into a niche above the old high-tide line as if whoever left it had known exactly how high the water would reach and not one inch higher. Rotted oak, iron bands gone to orange lace, lid collapsed inward like a ribcage. I didn't touch it at first. I crouched next to it for a full minute, telling myself I was assessing structural integrity, when what I was actually doing was having an argument with myself that I was losing.

Because here's the thing about me and chests and caves and wrecks: I used to do this for a living. Not salvage — science, sort of, adjacent to it. For three years I flew supply runs and survey flights for an expedition working the Triangle's wreck sites, and the man in the right-hand seat of most of those flights was Mateo Vidal, and the last dive Mateo ever made was a wreck site I can still draw from memory, and I have spent ten years building a life with a floor plan that has no room in it for chests in caves.

I touched the lid anyway. Of course I did. The wood came apart like wet cardboard, and underneath the collapse, wrapped in tarred canvas that had done its job better than anyone had a right to expect, was a bundle.

Lead sheets. Thin ones, the kind they stamped for manifests on treasure ships because paper doesn't survive salt water and captains wanted records that would. There were three of them, bound with cord that disintegrated when I breathed on it. I unwrapped the top sheet with hands that were steadier than they had any business being, and held it up to my headlamp.

And there, stamped into the lower corner — a maker's mark, an expedition mark, pressed deep and confident into soft metal — was a diving helmet over crossed fins, with the letters *MV* beneath and a serial number I knew.

Not recognized. *Knew.* Because I'd painted that stencil onto equipment crates with my own hands, in a boatyard in Zamboanga, on an afternoon I remember completely, including the radio playing and Mateo laughing at my lettering and saying *your M looks like a seagull, Coral, nobody will respect our expedition.*

MV. Mateo Vidal. My partner. Dead eleven years this October, officially, in a dive accident off a wreck called the Santa Inés, in water I was standing on the surface of when he stopped answering his regulator.

His expedition mark was stamped on a manifest sealed inside a cave that did not exist yesterday.

I sat down on the cold stone. Not gracefully. My legs made an executive decision without consulting me, and I let them, because the alternative was finding out what they'd do instead.

* * *

Here is what I know about shock, from professional experience: it arrives like weather, and it lies to you. It tells you the impossible thing is fine. It hands you a cup of tea in the middle of a house fire. So I sat on the floor of a cave that shouldn't exist, holding a lead sheet stamped with a dead man's mark, and my mind — helpful, organized, deeply committed to self-preservation — began generating explanations the way a wounded squid generates ink.

Coincidence. Someone reused the stencil. Expeditions borrow marks, copy marks, homage marks—

Except the serial number. Except the seagull-M, which I would know anywhere, because I drew it, badly, on purpose, as a joke between us, and jokes don't get copied by strangers.

Salvagers found his gear later and stashed it here. Except this cave was under the ocean until this afternoon, and the sealant on the canvas wrapping predates modern expeditions by a century at least, and I know canvas, and I know sealant, and I know — God help me — I know Mateo's mark the way you know your own handwriting.

Which left the explanation I had flown eleven years to avoid, the one I'd buried so deep and so thoroughly that I'd genuinely believed the grave was finished:

Mateo had been here. Before he died. He had stood in this chamber, or one like it, and handled these sheets, and marked them, and the official story — the story I helped build, the story I swore to, the story that put his brother Rafe in prison for negligence — the story was wrong. Or incomplete. Or a lie with my signature on it.

The tide was coming back. I could hear it — a change in the breathing of the cave, the exhale at the entrance slowing, the water voice in the deeper passages rising a half-step. Earthquake tides run strange; the sea had gone out like a liar and it would come back like a debt. I had maybe an hour of margin before the reef flat I'd landed on became a reef again, and a floatplane pilot I am not.

I should have left the sheets. I knew that even then, some part of me knew it — knew that whatever was stamped into that lead belonged to the dark, that some doors the ocean opens are tests and not gifts. But my hands were already rewrapping the bundle, already tucking it inside my jacket against my ribs, already committing the act, and I watched them do it the way you watch weather come in: aware, resigned, and complicit.

At the mouth of the cave I stopped once and looked back. The carvings ran off into the blackness, ships and men and waves, and somewhere among them that diving-bird symbol repeated, patient as a signature waiting to be read.

"I'm coming back tomorrow," I told the dark, and hated myself for it, and meant it.

* * *

Getting out was ugly. The tide had reclaimed half my runway; I waded to the plane through shin-deep water, hand-propped the engine through sheer spite, and got airborne off eighteen inches of remaining sand with the stall horn singing hymns. The engine ran rough all the way to the anchorage at Pulau Tiga, coughing silt and probably prayer, and I set her down on the mission strip at last light with both hands shaking on the yoke.

The missionary doctor who met me took one look at my face and asked if I'd hit a bird.

"Worse," I said. "I hit a memory."

He laughed, because missionaries are trained to laugh at pilots. I helped him unload the antibiotics and the espresso machine, drank the coffee he offered, and declined the bed he offered harder. There was a spare room with a real mattress and a mosquito net and the particular terrible kindness of people who feed strangers, and I turned it down with a smile I've practiced in mirrors, and slept instead under the wing with the lead sheets zipped into my flight bag and one hand on it like a dog on a bone.

Never land twice in the same place. I broke the rule before dawn, backtracking to Karakel with a borrowed fuel filter and a heart rate I chose not to measure, and if you'd asked me why — if anyone had been awake at that hour to ask — I'd have said engine verification. Follow-up maintenance. Duty.

I'd have been lying. But then, I've had practice.

The tide was out again at Karakel, and the cave mouth stood open in the gray morning light like a held breath. I anchored the plane on the beach, checked my flares, checked my nerve, and walked back into the dark with the manifest sheets, because there were things stamped into that lead I hadn't let myself read the night before.

Three sheets. I'd only truly looked at one.

Under the headlamp, in the cold breathing silence, I flattened the second sheet against the stone and read the first line of the cargo list — and stopped breathing.

Because there, item fourteen, catalogued in a secretary's careful antique Spanish, was a description I knew. An astrolabe. A brass ship's compass with an ivory face and a maker's mark from Seville. Recovered, the entry said, from the wreck of the Santa Inés.

The compass I had sworn, under oath, eleven years ago, was lost with Mateo on the night he died.

The compass my testimony said never existed.
