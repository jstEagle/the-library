## 3. What the Manifest Says

The headlamp beam trembled. I told myself it was my hand, and then I told myself it didn't matter which of us it was.

Item fourteen. *Brújula de latón con esfera de marfil, marca del artífice en Sevilla.* A brass compass with an ivory face, maker's mark Seville. Recovered from the wreck of the Santa Inés, logged, catalogued, and — according to this sheet, this rot-stiffened, salt-bitten sheet of lead that had been waiting in the dark longer than I'd been alive — *placed in the expedition strongbox for transfer to port authority.*

Expedition strongbox. Expedition mark. The stamp beside the entry was a circle bisected by a wave, with three letters worked into the curl of the water: **M.V.D.**

Mateo Vidal Delgado. My Mateo. The man whose handwriting I'd know blindfolded, whose initials I'd traced on fogged windows in another life, stamped here in lead like a brand on a hide.

I sat down on the cave floor without deciding to. The stone was cold through my flight suit, and the sea breathed somewhere behind me in the dark, patient as a creditor.

"Okay," I said aloud, because the silence had teeth. "Okay."

The compass had an ivory face. I knew that the way you know your own phone number — not because anyone told me twice, but because I'd held it. Three weeks before the dive that killed him, Mateo turned it over in his palms in the wheelhouse of the *Perla Negra* and said, "Coral, look. Ivory. They don't make them like this anymore. This thing survived two hundred years at the bottom of the sea and some archivist in Manila kept it in a drawer." He'd laughed. "Some things refuse to stay lost."

I remember thinking: he says things like that so they come true.

I remember everything. That's the disease. Other people get to forget their worst night; mine plays back in full fidelity, no static, every time I let the engine idle too long.

* * *

Eleven years ago. The monsoon season that never quite arrived, the season of glassy water and bad decisions. The *Perla Negra* rode at anchor off Karakel — Karakel, always Karakel, the island keeps its hooks in me — and we were running the last survey dives of the season on a wreck the charts politely ignored.

Mateo went down at dusk against every rule he'd ever taught me. The viz was collapsing, the current had turned, and Dez Okafor — our third, our careful one — had already surfaced, shaking his head, pointing at his gauge. Half a tank. Not enough.

But Mateo had found something on the sonar that afternoon. A return he didn't like. He said the site had been disturbed — "somebody's been digging, Coral, somebody who isn't us" — and he wanted one more look before the tide changed. He kissed me through the regulator, which is a stupid romantic gesture and also the last time his mouth touched anything of mine, and he rolled backward off the gunwale into water the color of steeped tea.

Rafe was on the boat. Rafe, twenty-four years old, Mateo's little brother, who'd joined the expedition that season because his brother asked and because Rafe would have followed Mateo into a volcano. Rafe ran the surface line. It was Rafe's rope, Rafe's knot, Rafe's watch on the depth gauge while I handled comms.

Forty minutes. Then fifty. Then the line went slack in a way lines shouldn't — not cut, not fouled, just *slack*, like the tension had been let out of the world.

What came up wasn't Mateo. What came up was Rafe, gray-faced, hauling on a line that ended in a frayed end and nothing else, screaming that the current had taken him, that the knot held, that he'd done everything right. And I believed him. God help me, in that moment I believed him completely.

It took two days to find the body. He was wedged in a coral shelf at forty meters, mask gone, tank empty, and clipped to his buoyancy vest — this is the detail, this is the detail that has lived under my ribs for eleven years — was nothing. No compass. No slate. No artifacts. His collection bag was empty except for silt.

The inquiry wanted a reason. Investigations are machines that run on reasons; feed them one or they'll build their own. The financier — Aldous Wren, silver-haired, soft-voiced, owner of the permit and the payroll — gave them a reason. He produced expedition logs showing Mateo had been warned about his air management. Showed maintenance records suggesting the regulator had been serviced improperly. Pointed, gently, at the surface line, at the knot, at Rafe.

And the logs were wrong. I knew they were wrong. I'd written half of them myself, in my own square pilot's handwriting, and the pages Wren presented bore dates from days we'd spent becalmed in port playing cards. But when the investigator slid the statement form across the table to me and asked whether the surface procedures had been followed, I looked at Rafe — twenty-four, hollow-eyed, holding his brother's folded dive flag like a folded flag at a funeral — and I said:

"Yes. As far as I observed."

As far as I observed. Four words. The most expensive four words ever spoken in a humid government office, and I have flown eleven years of medicine runs and mail runs and rumor runs trying not to hear them again.

They convicted Rafe of negligent operation resulting in death. Two years suspended sentence, then the second investigation — the one after the audit of Wren's finances went sideways — stretched it out, deepened it, made it permanent. And I flew away. Because that's what I do. That's what I'm *for*. I put distance between myself and things.

Except the compass. The compass was the one thing I never lied about, because nobody asked. The inquiry assumed it went down with him. Lost with the body, lost with the site, lost with the *Santa Inés*'s other treasures — scattered, sunk, gone. Item fourteen existed nowhere in any record because I testified, implicitly, by my silence, that it had never been recovered.

And here it was. Catalogued. Stamped with his initials. *Placed in the strongbox.*

Which meant it had come up. Which meant it had been recovered — recovered *before* the dive, or on a dive I never knew about, and logged, and stored. Which meant the story I helped build — the story of a reckless diver and a negligent brother — was built on a foundation I had personally poured concrete over.

Or it meant someone had forged this sheet. Someone had carved a lie into lead and left it in a sea cave for me to find, which was impossible, which was paranoid, which was —

Which was exactly the kind of thought you start having when you've spent a decade lying to yourself about one specific thing.

* * *

I did what pilots do with problems too big for the cockpit. I made a list.

One: the sheet was old. The corrosion pattern on the lead didn't lie — I've hauled enough salvage junk and enough genuine articles across the Triangle to know the difference between metal that aged underwater and metal that aged in a drawer. This had been down here decades. Whatever was written on it was written long before Mateo died.

Two: the handwriting wasn't Mateo's. The entries were in a formal secretary's script, antique Spanish, the kind of hand a ship's clerk learned with a ruler and a beating. But the annotations — small marks in the margin, checkmarks, a notation beside item fourteen that read *verificado* — those were different. Newer ink, though still old. And the checkmark beside item fourteen was a distinctive little hook, drawn left to right, like a bird's foot.

Mateo checked things off exactly like that. Bird's feet. I used to tease him that his lists looked like crow tracks.

Three: if Mateo verified the recovery of item fourteen, then the compass existed. It existed after the *Santa Inés* survey began, and the manifest said it went into the strongbox, and the strongbox — where was the strongbox? Was it on the *Perla Negra* the night he died? Did it go down with the boat? The *Perla Negra* was sold for scrap two months after the funeral by a grieving estate that Wren administered, and I never once asked what happened to its contents, because asking questions is how you find answers, and I had arranged my entire life around not finding this one.

Four: I was sitting in a cave that hadn't been charted, holding proof that my sworn testimony was false, and outside the tide was coming back in.

I got up. My knees complained. Eleven years of rudder pedals.

The third sheet was still folded where I'd left it, and I realized I'd been avoiding it the way you avoid the last page of a medical report. I picked it up anyway. Pilots check the fuel even when they don't want to know.

This sheet was different. Less cargo, more correspondence — or the ghost of correspondence, since whatever had been written on the original had been transferred onto lead in summary. Shipping instructions. A date. A name.

The date was six weeks before Mateo died.

The name was not Wren's.

It was a single word, stamped deeper than the rest, deliberate, the way you strike a seal when you want it legible for centuries: **KARAKEL**. And beneath it, in that same secretary's hand, a line that translated itself in my head with the slow cold turn of a bilge pump:

*Transfer of recovered items to be effected at the island anchorage, upon arrival of the designated vessel. Items to include all catalogued artifacts of the Santa Inés, per inventory. The undersigned confirms receipt of advance payment and the discretion agreed.*

Discretion agreed. Payment received. Artifacts recovered — including item fourteen — scheduled for transfer off Karakel six weeks before the dive that killed the man whose bird's-foot checkmarks sat beside them.

So the compass had been here. Or bound for here. Or hidden here. Either way, it had been *here*, on this island, in the window of time when Mateo was diving these waters and finding sonar returns that showed somebody had been digging.

*Somebody's been digging, Coral. Somebody who isn't us.*

He knew. Oh, God, he knew. He found the disturbance on the site, he found evidence that the artifacts were being moved — sold, brokered, laundered through some arrangement with *discretion agreed* — and instead of going to the authorities he went back down for one more look, alone, at dusk, against every rule he'd ever taught me. To document it. To prove it. Mateo didn't confront; Mateo *catalogued*. He was going to bring them the evidence, all of it, neat as a museum drawer.

And the current took him. Or the frayed line. Or — and here was the thought I'd been outrunning for eleven years, finally catching me in a cave with nowhere left to fly —

Or somebody made sure the line was slack.

I put the sheets down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. The headlamp lit the chamber in a trembling cone, and the carved coral stared from its niche in the wall — saints and waves and a ship with broken masts, some drowned sailor's votive, centuries old and utterly indifferent to what I'd just done to my own history.

Because here's the arithmetic I couldn't escape, standing in the breathing dark doing sums with my whole life:

If the compass was recovered and catalogued, then the expedition logs Wren presented to the inquiry — the forged logs, the ones with the wrong dates, the ones that damned Rafe — weren't just sloppy. They were *constructed*. Built to fit a story. And the story needed a reckless diver, because the truth was a salvage fraud, and the truth would have pointed at the money, and the money was Wren's.

And I had stood in that office with the truth sitting in my own memory — the becalmed days, the real logbook pages, the compass with the ivory face turning in Mateo's hands — and I had said *as far as I observed*, and signed, and flown away, and let a twenty-four-year-old kid carry a lie that weighed as much as a ship.

My testimony wasn't just false. It was load-bearing. Take it out and the whole structure comes down — Wren's story, the conviction, my clean hands, all of it.

Guilt doesn't hit you like a wave. I'd always imagined it would, all these years — a big cinematic breaker, knock you flat, done. It doesn't. It comes up like a body you buried too shallow, in ground that erodes. One tide, one earthquake, one cave mouth opening where no cave mouth should be, and there it is. Faceup. Recognizable. Waiting for you to admit you always knew exactly where you'd put it.

I stood in that cave and I let it look at me.

Then I heard it — faint, above the sea-breath, above my own pulse: an engine. Distant, low, throttled way back. Not the wind. Not a boat generator. An aircraft, circling wide, the way you circle when you're looking for a strip or a wreck or a woman's plane parked where no plane should be.

I killed my headlamp and stood in absolute blackness, listening, with three sheets of lead in my arms and eleven years of lies rearranging themselves around a new and terrible shape.

Someone else knew about this cave.

And whoever it was, they hadn't come for the coral carvings.
