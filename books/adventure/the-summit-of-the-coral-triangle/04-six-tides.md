## 4. Six Tides

The radio at the Sorong field office had a voice like a man gargling gravel and opinions, and by seven in the morning it was telling me everything I didn't want to know.

"—buoy array four-seven confirms pressure drop of eleven millibars over eighteen hours," it crackled, while I stood there with a cup of coffee I'd already stopped drinking because my hands had other plans. "Sea surface temperature anomaly persists. All stations advise: monsoon trough consolidation expected within six tidal cycles. Six tides, people. After that, nobody flies anywhere for a week, maybe two."

Six tides.

I did the arithmetic the way you do when you're trying not to do the arithmetic — fast, sloppy, hoping to get a number that lets you off the hook. Six tidal cycles was roughly three days. Three days until a wall of weather came down on the Coral Triangle like a door slamming, and after that door slammed, the sea would spend a week shoving itself into every low place it could find, including — my brain supplied helpfully, uninvited — a certain uncharted cave on Karakel Island that I had walked out of two days ago with sand still in my boots and a lead sheet stamped with Mateo's expedition mark folded into my jacket like a letter addressed to a ghost.

I put the coffee down. It had gone cold anyway, or I had.

* * *

Here is what I told myself, standing in that office while the gravel-voiced forecaster droned on about wind shear vectors: I told myself I was going back for the medicine.

This was almost true. It was true in the way a chart is true — accurate within known error, useless if you trust it with your life. There *was* medicine involved. Old Pak Darma in the river village where I'd landed last week had asked me to bring back penicillin for his granddaughter's ear infection, and I had promised, because promising things to grandfathers is easy and cheap and I do it all the time. The penicillin was real. It was sitting in a cold box in the back of my plane right now, wrapped in a towel, waiting.

What the penicillin was not doing was sitting in Karakel Island, which lay ninety minutes southeast of here across open water, in exactly the wrong direction from the river village, past fuel range margins I would have to fudge, into an island group that was about to become the least hospitable square of ocean in Indonesia.

But the lie needed a spine, so I gave it one: I'd fly the medicine first, swing wide, refuel at the coastal strip at Tanjung Merah — which may or may not have fuel this month; the drums there were older than some of my regrets — and then, since I would *already be in the area*, since the weather window was closing and a responsible pilot checks her options, I would overfly Karakel. Just to look. Just to confirm the cave mouth I'd sheltered in was going to flood, so that someday, when someone asked, I could say I'd seen it with my own eyes.

Nobody was going to ask. That was the beauty of the alibi. Nobody ever asks the bush pilot why she takes the long way. They assume it's weather, or fuel, or a woman, or all three. My whole career ran on the assumption that people who fly alone are running from something or toward something, and everyone politely declines to find out which.

I signed the weather sheet. My signature looked steadier than I felt, which is the only trick I've ever learned that matters.

* * *

The walk out to the plane took four minutes and cost me more than the flight would.

She sat on the apron in the early heat — a 1978 Cessna 206 the color of a bruise healing, tail number worn soft as an old tattoo — and she was the only honest relationship of my adult life. Ten years we'd been together. She'd never once asked me who Mateo was. She just wanted clean fuel, a preflight done properly, and me sober enough to land her, and in exchange she carried me over water that has swallowed better planes than hers.

I walked the preflight slow, because slow hands keep lies from shaking. Fuel caps. Oil. Control surfaces free and correct. Tires — good, no slashes, no surprises; paranoia is just maintenance with feelings. I drained the sumps and held the sample up to the light the way Mateo used to, checking for water, and caught myself doing his little wrist-flick afterward, the one that scattered the drops, and stood there for a second with my arm out over the concrete like a man proposing to nobody.

Ten years, and the dead man still had better posture than I did.

"Where today?" asked Yusuf, the line boy, who was sixteen and thought my life was romantic. He lugged the chocks like they were made of lead and dreams.

"River run first. Then south." I said it lightly. Lightness is load-bearing; I'd built my whole personality on it.

"South? Weather's coming south."

"Weather's coming everywhere, Yusuf. That's what weather does." I tossed him the tow bar. "If a woman comes looking for me — archaeologist type, sharp eyes, probably asking questions — tell her I went north."

"You don't know any archaeologists."

"I know one now." I climbed in before he could ask the obvious follow-up, which was *why*, which was the question I'd been refusing to answer since the tide dropped the sea off Karakel and showed me a door I'd spent a decade pretending wasn't there.

* * *

The river leg was easy and I hated how much I loved it.

Ninety minutes of green corrugations, mist peeling off the ridgelines like bandages, the river braiding silver through it all — flying like that is the closest thing to forgiveness I've found, and I've looked. I set the 206 down on the grass strip at Long Naga with the gentleness I only manage when my conscience is loud, and old Pak Darma came hobbling out of the longhouse before my wheels stopped rolling, because in the villages the airplane is the most interesting thing to happen since the last airplane.

"You have it?" His hand was on my arm before I was out of the door. His granddaughter hung back behind him, one ear crusted and angry, eyes too big for her face.

"Cold box, wrapped in blue. Two weeks, full course, even if she feels better — especially then." I crouched to the girl's level. She was maybe eight and regarded me with the flat suspicion children reserve for adults who arrive bearing both gifts and questions. "You take every pill. Even the ones that taste like regret."

"I know what regret tastes like," she said solemnly, and I laughed harder than the joke deserved, because apparently I was the only one at that airstrip who hadn't figured out the theme of the day.

Darma pressed a woven bracelet on me — payment, refusal of which would have been an insult — and then held my wrist a moment longer than ceremony required. He was old enough to read weather in bones and trouble in faces, and he was reading mine.

"You look like a woman carrying something heavy in a small bag," he said.

"It's a light bag."

"Heavy things weigh the same in small bags." He let go. "Fly careful. The sea is pulling its breath in. When the sea pulls its breath, it means to shout."

I thanked him, lied beautifully about my route home, and took off with the penicillin delivered and the alibi half-spent, which meant the other half was now naked and squirming in the seat beside me, wearing my face.

* * *

Tanjung Merah was a strip and a rumor: coral grit runway, a fuel drum farm rusting under a tarp, a shack where a man named Benny sold cold beer, warm diesel, and information in whatever order you paid for. I got the drums rolled out — two of them, and the fuel inside was the color of weak tea, which is either fine or catastrophic depending entirely on faith — and while the pump wheezed I bought a beer I didn't want and paid for the third item.

"Karakel," I said. "Anything?"

Benny's shack had a radio, and Benny's radio had been listening to the same forecast I had. He wiped the counter with a rag that had given up its career. "You heard the buoys. Six tides, they say. Fishermen pulled in yesterday — all of them, every boat, even the Bajau, and the Bajau don't scare." He leaned in. "And there's a ship."

"A ship."

"Salvage flag. Big, black hull, working lights like a city. Came through the strait two nights ago heading your way — well, heading *Karakel* way, if you follow." He shrugged with the elaborate innocence of a man who has decided not to wonder why a bush pilot cares about a salvage vessel. "Name on the bow. *Sable Fortune*. Sounds like a poker hand, eh?"

It sounded like a knife. I kept my face doing nothing, which is my second-best trick. "Probably chasing the same rumor everyone chases. Quake dropped the sea, someone saw something shiny."

"Someone always sees something shiny." Benny capped my beer. "You want the truth about treasure, sister? The treasure is always already claimed. Only the dying is unclaimed."

I flew out of Tanjung Merah with tea-colored fuel and a new weight in the small bag. A salvage fleet doesn't go somewhere for nothing. Somebody else had the manifest — or part of it, or word of it — and they were moving with the same clock I was. Six tides. Maybe fewer, if their captain was the kind who counted in hours instead of moons.

* * *

Over the water, alone at nine thousand feet with the monsoon building on the horizon like a country declaring war, I finally let myself stop lying, because there was no one up there to sell it to.

Here is the ledger, then. The truth I wouldn't say out loud at Sorong, wouldn't say to Yusuf, wouldn't say to Darma, wouldn't say to Benny, wouldn't say to the girl with the sore ear:

I was going back to Karakel because of a compass.

Not the lead manifest — well, yes, the manifest, but the manifest was just paper, and paper can be burned, lost, denied. What couldn't be denied was the object it listed. Item forty-one, recovered from the wreck site, logged and dated: one brass surveyor's compass, maker's mark Halloran & Voss, serial number I knew better than my own birthday, because I'd bought it for Mateo with my first year of real flying money and had it engraved on the back where he never let anyone see it. *For M., who always knows north.* 

That compass was supposed to be at the bottom of the Selayar Strait, inside a dive harness, inside a drowned man, lost forever on the night I gave testimony that ended a different man's freedom. That compass was the linchpin of my lie. If it was in a rotted chest in a cave on Karakel — if it existed anywhere but the grave — then the manifest I swore by was false, the dive log was false, and the negligence I'd pinned on Rafe Mateo's brother like a medal made of chain was false, and I had spent ten years flying away from a crime whose evidence had just been handed to me by an earthquake.

So: six tides. After the monsoon, the cave floods and stays flooded, and the proof drowns a second time, deeper, and this time the sea keeps it. Which meant I had three days to decide whether I was the woman who let the truth drown twice, or the woman who swam down and got it.

I checked my watch against the tide table taped to the yoke. Slack water at Karakel: tomorrow, 0440. Low, calm, the cave mouth breathing out instead of in. I could be on the water by first light, in and out before the fleet's divers finished their coffee — assuming the *Sable Fortune* hadn't already posted a anchor watch, assuming Benny's rumor was a day late instead of a day early, assuming the tea-colored fuel didn't eat my engine alive halfway across the strait.

Assumptions. The currency of the doomed.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the engines, the thing I would not say even to myself at altitude: *Mateo wrote to someone, before he died. Someone knew the logs were forged. And if Mateo knew — if Mateo suspected — then the night he died might not have been an accident at all, and I have been mourning a tragedy that was possibly a murder, and testifying for the man who might have arranged it.*

No. Too far. That way lay madness, and madness doesn't fit through a cockpit door. One lie at a time. First the compass. First the proof. First the alibi of duty, which was holding, barely, like a rope bridge in wind.

* * *

Karakel rose out of the afternoon haze the way it always did — abrupt, green-toothed, indifferent — and my stomach dropped before the plane did.

There was a ship at anchor off the northern point.

Black hull. Working lights already lit though the sun hadn't set. A crane on her deck stood folded like a sleeping mantis, and two rigid inflatables bobbed alongside her like ticks. Even at this distance, even through a scratched windshield and two miles of salt air, I could see she was not a fishing boat and not a government vessel and not anything that belonged in a bay that appeared on no chart.

*Sable Fortune.*

Benny's rumor, punctual after all.

I throttled back and let the 206 drift wide, high, lazy — a sightseeing pilot, nothing more, just another pair of eyes on a pretty bay — while my heart hammered out flight-plan calculations against my ribs. Their position. The cave mouth on the southern shore, hidden below the headland, invisible from the anchorage unless you knew where to look. Did they know? Did they have the manifest, or just the rumor of it? Was there a watch on the southern water?

A diver surfaced near the stern of the big ship, tiny as a comma, and paused — I swear it, paused — treading water, face turned up toward the sky. Toward me. You can't see a face at that distance. But I felt it anyway, the way you feel a draft under a door: somebody down there had looked up at a small bruise-colored plane crossing the bay, and recognized it, and remembered.

I banked south, casual as Sunday, and put the headland between us.

Below me, hidden, the cave waited with its rotted chest and its impossible list and whatever else the earthquake had pried loose from the dark. Ahead of me, the tide table said slack water at 0440, and the barometer said I had six tides, and the fuel gauge said five hours and change of tea-colored prayer.

The alibi of duty had gotten me here. It would not get me further. Somewhere between Tanjung Merah and this bay it had quietly died, and what was left in the cockpit with me was just a woman and a decision, and the decision had been made days ago in a flooded cave when I picked up a lead sheet and read my own name in the wreckage of my own lie.

I found the little strip of beach on the island's lee side — shell grit, firm enough, the kind of landing you make once and pray you never have to explain — and brought the 206 down in a long flat slide of spray and coral dust.

One landing. I'd promised myself one landing, wheels down, wheels up, no attachments.

The sun was going into the water red as a warning flare. Four hours to slack tide. Somewhere behind the headland, a salvage crew was eating dinner off steel trays, and somewhere ahead of me, in the dark under the island, a compass engraved *for M., who always knows north* was pointing at the one direction I'd spent ten years refusing to fly.

I killed the engine and listened to the silence fill up with surf, and started walking toward the cave.
