## 5. The Salvage Fleet

There are three kinds of men who end up captaining a salvage vessel in the Coral Triangle: the ones who love the sea, the ones who hate the land, and the ones running from something with better legs than they have. I am the third kind, though I tell my crew I'm the second, because "running" invites questions and questions invite mutiny.

The *Sable Fortune* is forty meters of bad decisions welded to good steel. She was built as a coastal freighter in 1979, converted to salvage in Jakarta by a man who cut corners like he was trimming his own hair, and bought at auction by Meridian Deepwater Resources — which is to say, by men in Singapore who wear watches worth more than my boat and have never once been wet past the cuffs. My orders come to me encrypted, unsigned, and always phrased as suggestions. *Suggest* you proceed to grid Karakel-7. *Suggest* you complete recovery operations before the end of the quarter. *Suggest* you avoid contact with any government survey vessel.

In eleven years of this work I have learned that a suggestion from Meridian is a bullet with polite wrapping paper.

We were three days out of Bitung when the manifest reached me.

* * *

It came through the satcom at 0400, which is when all bad news arrives, because whoever schedules these things believes that a man reading a screen alone in the dark is more likely to do what he's told. The file was ninety-one pages, scanned crooked, water-stained along one edge — someone had photographed it off a table in a hurry, or off a dead man's desk, and hadn't bothered to be neat about it.

A lead sheet. A Spanish galleon's cargo manifest, recovered from a sea cave on Karakel Island, listing artifacts raised and catalogued across four seasons of diving. Silver bars. Church plate. An astrolabe stamped Toledo, 1602. And item after item with catalog numbers matching an expedition that officially never happened — because the expedition's logs had been sealed, its financier had gone quiet, and two of its divers were dead.

I knew the expedition. Everyone in this trade knew of it, the way everyone knows of a house fire on their street. Seven years ago. Karakel waters. One diver dead on the bottom, one brother jailed for negligence, one expedition member vanished into the kind of silence you don't ask about at parties. The official story was a regulator failure and a panicked ascent. The unofficial story, in the bars where people talk too loudly after the third beer, was that nobody who'd actually seen the logs believed a word of them.

I read the manifest twice, then a third time with the chart spread out beside me, and I did what I always do with Meridian's gifts: I asked myself what they weren't telling me.

Here is what a man learns after a decade of stripping wrecks for other people's profit: the work itself is honest. The sea buries things; we dig them up; the ledger balances. It's the paperwork that rots you. Every job I've ever taken has arrived wrapped in a story, and every story has had a hole in it exactly the size of whatever somebody upstairs needed disappeared. You learn to put your finger in the hole and feel the shape of the thing they're hiding. This time the hole was shaped like a question: *why does a mining conglomerate care about a two-hundred-year-old manifest?*

Answer: because the manifest wasn't the treasure. The manifest was the receipt. And receipts, in my experience, are only valuable to people who need to prove something happened — or prove it didn't.

I closed the file. I set our course for Karakel. I told myself it was just another job.

I have been telling myself that for eleven years. I'm very good at it. Practice, mostly.

* * *

My chief diver is a Fijian named Joeli Tuinaceva who weighs as much as my anchor winch and moves like smoke over water. He came to me out of a pearl farm in the Torres Strait with no references and a scar running ear to collarbone that he has never explained, and in nine years he has never once lied to me, which makes him unique among human beings I have employed.

He found me on the aft deck at dawn, drinking the coffee that tastes like it was brewed in the bilge, because it was.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I slept."

"You're standing the way a man stands when he hasn't slept." He leaned on the rail beside me and looked at the water the way he always does, like it owes him money. "New orders?"

"Karakel."

Joeli was quiet for a moment. "That's quake country. Season's turning."

"The season's turning everywhere." I handed him the tablet with the manifest open. He read the way he dives — slow, thorough, missing nothing. When he got to the expedition numbers his thumb stopped moving.

"These are the Halloran-Mateo numbers," he said. "The dive that killed—"

"I know what they are."

"And Meridian wants us there before—" He looked up. "Before who?"

"Anyone official."

"That's not an answer, Vikram."

"It's the answer I have." I took the tablet back. Below us, the Flores Sea did its patient blue nothing. "We go in, we strip the cave, we're gone inside five days. No government survey, no archaeologists, no press. In and out before anyone knows the site exists."

"And if someone already knows?"

I didn't have an answer for that either, so I drank my terrible coffee and let the silence answer for me, which is a captain's oldest trick.

* * *

Karakel rose out of the sea on the fourth day looking like nothing much — a green fist of volcanic rock with a fringe of white sand, uninhabited, unloved, uncharted in any way that mattered until last month. That was when the earthquake came, the one that dropped the sea off its western shoulder like a pulled tablecloth, and somewhere under all that receded water a cave mouth opened that had been shut since before anyone was keeping charts.

Meridian had known about it within seventy-two hours. Think about that. Think about what it means that a company whose business is nickel and dredge leases had eyes on a drowned Spanish galleon faster than any navy in the region. Somebody fed them the location. Somebody with access to hydrographic data and no patience for procedure. I have my suspicions about who, but suspicions are like ballast — useful, heavy, and best kept below the waterline.

We anchored in the lee of the western point at 1530, and by 1700 Joeli's first team was in the water.

I'll say this for the cave: it earns its mystery. The entrance sits below the low-tide line, a black slot in the limestone maybe four meters wide, breathing with the swell — exhaling a slick of disturbed water on each wave, inhaling it back. Joeli went in first with a reel and a slate, and I stood on the deck watching his bubbles march into the dark and counting seconds the way I always count, because a man who counts seconds underwater is a man who expects to stop counting someday.

Forty-one minutes. He surfaced in the gathering dusk with his light still working and his face doing something complicated.

"Well?" I said.

"It's real." He spat his reg and grinned, and for a second he looked twenty years younger and considerably more foolish. "Vikram, it's *real*. Chambers behind chambers, air pockets, the whole cathedral. There's concretion over everything but I can see hull timbers, ballast stone, and—" he held up his slate, "—somebody's already been in here. Recently."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. "Recently how?"

"Fresh fin wash in the silt. A tie-off line, modern braided core, cut clean — not ours. And this." He passed me a scrap of coral, carved, deliberate, the kind of marker a diver leaves to say *I was here, I'm coming back.* "Whoever it was, they weren't salvaging. They were *looking* at things."

Looking. Not taking. That distinction mattered more than I could yet say why.

"Could be a fisherman," said Priya, my deck boss, who had wandered over with the inevitable clipboard.

"In a cave at depth with a reel line?" Joeli shook his head. "This was technical. Twinset or rebreather. This was a *diver* diver."

I looked at the little carved coral in my palm and felt the first cold thread of a feeling I would spend the next several days refusing to name. Somebody had beaten us to a site that had been sealed for centuries and opened for weeks. Somebody who dove like a professional and marked their way like a person planning a return trip.

Somebody, in other words, who intended to come back. Just like us.

"Double the watch tonight," I said. "Nobody works the site after dark. And Joeli — if you see so much as a kayak, you sing out."

"Aye, Captain." He paused at the ladder. "You know what this means, right? If someone else found the cave, someone else might have the manifest too. Might know what Meridian's really after."

"I know what it means."

"Do you?" He smiled without much humor. "Because from where I'm standing, Captain, it means we're not the hunters anymore. We're just the ones who arrived second."

* * *

The diver who recognized the plane was named Marcus Webb, and I want it on the record that I never liked him.

Webb came up from the second dive at midday on our second day on station, hauling himself over the gunwale with the graceless urgency of a man with news, and found me on the bridge wing with the binoculars doing what I'd been doing since dawn: watching the empty sky over Karakel for reasons I couldn't have defended to a court of inquiry.

"Captain." He was breathing hard, and not from the swim. "There's a plane."

"What kind of plane?"

"Bush strip on the island's east side — old war-era cut, half overgrown. There's a floatplane parked on it. High-wing single, red and white, tail number patched over but I got close enough on foot to see the paint scheme." He wiped salt spray off his face. "Captain, I know that plane. I've *seen* that plane. Three years ago, Ambon harbor — she was running medevac runs out of the islands. Everybody in the trade knows her pilot. Coral Crane."

And there it was. The name I'd been circling for two days without letting myself land on it, delivered by the last man aboard I'd have chosen to deliver it.

Coral Crane. Bush pilot, ferry runner, ghost of the archipelago routes. And — according to every bar-side historian from Sorong to Dili — the surviving witness of the Karakel dive. The woman whose testimony put Mateo Halloran's brother in prison. The woman who, if the manifest in my cabin was what it appeared to be, had testified to the loss of a compass that was quite possibly sitting in a rotted chest forty meters inside the cave I was contracted to empty.

"You're sure," I said, and it wasn't a question, and Webb heard that it wasn't.

"Positive. Red and white Cub derivative, floats, the dented cowling port side. She flies medicine and mail and doesn't land anywhere twice." He grinned, and I remembered again why I didn't like him: he grinned at other people's trouble like it was entertainment. "Small world, eh, Captain? The witness shows up at the wreck."

"Get off my bridge wing."

He went, still grinning. I stayed where I was, glasses up, scanning the eastern ridge where a red-and-white speck sat parked on a scar of runway like a heron that had decided to stay.

Here is the thing about Coral Crane that the bar historians get wrong. They tell it like gossip — the pilot who testified, the pilot who ran. But I'd crossed her wake twice over the years, once in Ambon and once at a fuel dock in Kupang, and both times what struck me wasn't the legend. It was the tiredness. A woman flying medicine into river villages in a plane older than she is, never sleeping in the same place twice, never staying for the second drink. That's not a life anybody chooses for fun. That's a sentence being served in installments, in the air, where nobody can serve the papers.

I knew the type. I *was* the type, with worse fuel economy.

So now the board looked like this: a leaked manifest of dubious provenance, orders to strip a site blind before any survey, a carved coral marker left by an unknown diver, and the one witness alive who could testify to everything the manifest called a lie — parked on the same island, in the same weather window, ahead of us.

Either she'd found the cave herself, or she'd been drawn to it the way we had, by the same invisible hand that had fed Meridian. And either way, the arithmetic was ugly. Because if the manifest proved her testimony false, then it also proved Rafe Halloran innocent — and it proved that somebody, seven years ago, had built a lie solid enough to send a man to prison, and had built it on top of a drowned ship that they'd known about all along.

Meridian didn't spend charter money chasing silver bars. Meridian spent it burying receipts.

I lowered the binoculars. The barometer on the bulkhead had begun, very quietly, to fall.

"Mr. Rao." Priya at the bridge door, clipboard against her chest. "Satcom for you. Encrypted. Marked priority."

Of course it was. Bad news keeps office hours too.

I took the printout she handed me and read it twice. The gist was simple, the way bullets are simple: accelerate extraction. Full recovery within four tidal cycles. And a new paragraph, added since the last transmission, in the same polite unsigned voice:

*Suggest you treat any independent parties on site as a security concern.*

I folded the page and put it in my pocket, and out on the water the tide was turning, and the cave mouth breathed in, and somewhere on the far side of the island a red-and-white plane sat on its scar of runway with its wheels — its floats — chocked, waiting for whatever came next.

Four tides. Then the monsoon. Then none of this would matter to anyone but the lawyers.

I went down to the mess, poured the coffee that tasted like bilge, and did not sleep.
