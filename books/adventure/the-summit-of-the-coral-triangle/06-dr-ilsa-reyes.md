## 6. Dr. Ilsa Reyes

There are three ways to find a bush pilot in the islands, and I had exhausted two of them by the time the monsoon season made up its mind.

The first way is paperwork, which in this part of the world is a euphemism for bribery, and which got me a list of forty-one licensed aviators, none of whom admitted to flying the Karakel run. The second way is harbors — sit in a bar with a view of the water, buy drinks for men who smell of diesel and salt, and listen. That got me a name spoken twice, both times by people who immediately regretted saying it: *Coral Crane*. Fly her if you must, they said, but don't ask her about the past, and don't ask her to wait.

The third way is to simply stand at the end of her airstrip looking like a problem she can't ignore. Which is how I spent the better part of four days, on a shelf of crushed coral and old asphalt that someone with more optimism than engineering training had once called an airfield, on the coast of an island whose name I'll omit because the people there were kind to me and deserve better than to be dragged into this.

I am a marine archaeologist. People hear that and picture scuba diving over pretty ruins, and some of the time that's even true. What it actually means, most days, is that I am professionally trained to look at something everyone else has decided is finished — a wreck, a ruin, a closed case — and ask what's still missing from the story. It turns out I can't turn that off. Not for wrecks. Not, as I was about to learn, for people.

* * *

She landed at 6:40 in the morning, out of a sky the color of wet slate, in a plane so old it should have been in a museum and so well-kept that the museum would have been embarrassed. A high-wing taildrifter, paint sun-bleached to the color of bone, tires kissing the coral strip with a delicacy that told me everything about how many thousands of times she'd done exactly this.

I stepped out from under the palm where I'd been waiting, and watched her go through post-flight like a surgeon scrubbing out: walk-around, fuel check, a hand trailing along the wing strut as if greeting an animal. She saw me. I knew she saw me, because her pace changed — not much, just enough. A woman who never lands twice in the same place learns to read strangers fast, and I could feel myself being read: hat, boots, field kit, the university sticker half peeled off my equipment case. Categorized. Filed under *trouble, academic, probably broke*.

"You're blocking my runway," she said. Her voice was flat and pleasant the way a machete is flat and pleasant.

"Dr. Ilsa Reyes." I didn't offer a hand; pilots on preflight don't want hands. "Marine archaeology, National Institute. I'm hoping to charter you."

"I'm not a charter service."

"Your plane says otherwise. So does the manifest I pulled from the coastal authority — medicine runs, mail, the occasional passenger who pays cash and doesn't ask questions." I let that sit. "I'm the occasional passenger. Cash. No questions."

That earned me the first real look. Up close she was younger than her reputation and older than her face, if that makes sense — a woman who'd been weathering herself deliberately, like timber left out to gray. Her eyes did a thing I'd later understand: they went somewhere else for half a second, checked on something locked in a room behind them, came back.

"Where?"

"Karakel Island."

The change was small. Most people wouldn't have caught it. But I've spent fifteen years reading sediment layers and shipworm damage and the tiny lies of provenance papers, and small changes are my entire profession. Her jaw set a quarter-millimeter differently. Her weight shifted off her back foot.

"Nothing's on Karakel," she said.

"There wasn't. Then there was an earthquake, and a tide that dropped the sea off its west side like someone pulling a tablecloth, and now there's a cave mouth that appears on no chart I can find, going back to the Spanish surveys." I kept my voice level, conversational, the tone you use for discussing weather. "And I have reason to believe there's material inside it connected to the Meridian Expedition."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the theme of everything that follows: I said that last part to hurt her. Or not to hurt her — to *test* her. To see what moved. Because I'd spent three weeks pulling threads before I ever stood on that airstrip, and every thread led through the same knot: the Meridian Expedition, five years ago, six divers, one drowned man, one convicted brother, one vanished financier's money, and one witness whose testimony put Mateo Vidal's own flesh and blood in prison.

Mateo was my research partner for four years. Before that he was the person who taught me that archaeology isn't about objects, it's about what people do to each other around objects. He wrote that in the margin of one of my papers, in green ink, with an arrow, like everything he said was urgent. He was brilliant and careless and funny and he trusted too easily and I loved him in the way you love a colleague when you're both too busy and too careful to call it anything else, and then one night off Karakel his regulator failed — or the dive plan failed, or the logbook lied, depending on which version of events you believed — and he died, and I was on the other side of the world, and by the time I got home there was a trial and a verdict and a coffin and a hole in my life shaped exactly like a man in green ink.

Three weeks ago, going through his effects — his sister finally sent me his boxes; grief takes years to become logistics — I found a letter. Unsent draft, saved file, whatever you want to call it. Dated nineteen days before he died. In it, Mateo wrote that the expedition's recovery logs were forged. That artifacts listed as lost in earlier seasons were listed as recovered later, with dates that didn't match tides or weather or physics. That he suspected why, and that the why involved money, and that he was going to pull the thread.

Nineteen days later he was dead, and the official story was a tragic accident, and his brother went to prison for negligent operation, and the expedition's logs were accepted as clean by every institution that reviewed them, including mine, including me.

So yes. I said *Meridian Expedition* to a stranger on an airstrip, and I watched her face, and what moved in it was not surprise.

It was recognition. And under the recognition, guilt — the specific, terrible fluency of a person hearing their own crime described in a language they thought no one else spoke.

* * *

"Coffee," she said abruptly, and walked toward the lean-to shed at the strip's edge that served, apparently, as terminal building, café, and chapel. There was a rusted percolator on a camp stove and two enamel cups, one chipped, and she poured without asking whether I wanted any, which I took as either hospitality or surrender.

We sat on a bench beneath the shed's corrugated awning while the first fat drops of the morning rain began ticking on the tin. She held her cup in both hands and looked at the runway instead of at me.

"How did you find me?" she asked.

"A fisherman on Talise described your plane. Said it lands like a heron and leaves like one's after it."

A breath through the nose that might have been a laugh. "He's not wrong." Then, quieter: "You said the logs were forged. That's a serious accusation."

"It's Mateo's accusation. I'm just the one delivering it late." I took out the printout — I'd learned not to carry the original anywhere near salt water — and laid it on the bench between us. "Unsent. Nineteen days before he died. He names the discrepancies: recovery dates that fall on neap tides when the site was undivable, artifact numbers reused across seasons, a compass logged as recovered in year two that was reported lost in the final dive. He writes, quote, 'the logs are a ledger, not a record — someone is balancing accounts.'"

She read it the way I'd seen her read the wind a few minutes earlier: completely, once, without moving her lips. When she finished, she didn't touch the paper. She looked at it for a long moment as though it might detonate.

"The compass," she said.

"You know it?"

"I know of it." Her voice had gone carefully neutral, a diver equalizing. "Everyone in the salvage world knows the Vidal compass. Gimballed brass housing, claimed to be off a Manila galleon, worth more than most boats in this archipelago. Logged lost with the body. Never recovered." A pause. "If it turned up somewhere, it would mean the loss report was false. And if the loss report was false—"

"Then the negligence case built on it collapses. And Rafe Vidal walks." I said it plainly because I wanted to watch her take the blow. "His conviction rested heavily on the expedition's documentation. Forged documentation, if Mateo's right. Your testimony included it."

For the first time she looked directly at me, and what was in her eyes was so raw and so fast that I understood, in one lurching instant, that I had gotten her entirely wrong. I had come prepared for evasion, maybe hostility, maybe the smooth denials of someone paid to forget things. I had not come prepared for a woman who looked like she'd been waiting five years for someone to say this sentence to her, the way you wait outside a door you're afraid to open.

"You think I don't know that?" she said. Low. Controlled the way a pressure hull is controlled. "You think I flew ten thousand hours over this ocean without doing that arithmetic every single day?"

"Then help me finish it."

"Why me?"

"Because you fly the Karakel run. Because you were on the island within a week of the quake that opened that cave — I checked the coastal authority's flight logs, Dr. Crane, you filed a weather diversion to Karakel nine days after the event, and you haven't flown that route since, which for a woman who supposedly never goes anywhere twice is itself a confession." I let that land. "And because whoever forged those logs is going to be very interested in what's in that cave, and I need a pilot who won't spook, and won't sell me out, and can get us out ahead of a monsoon."

The rain thickened against the tin roof. Somewhere down the strip a rooster made an argument nobody was entertaining. She sat very still, holding her coffee, and I watched her weigh things I couldn't see — and I realized, with the particular loneliness of the recently widowed-by-circumstance, that we were both doing the same calculation with the same dead man at the center of it.

"Mateo Vidal," she said at last, "was the best diver I ever flew for. And the worst liar I ever met. He couldn't keep a straight face through a birthday surprise." Her thumb traced the chip in the enamel cup. "If he found something rotten in those logs, he'd have confronted it head-on. Loudly. With witnesses."

"And instead he's dead, and the logs are clean, and his brother is in Suva Correctional."

"Yes." Flat. Final. A depth sounding.

"So you understand why I can't stop."

"I understand why you *shouldn't*." She set the cup down and stood, and for a moment I thought that was the whole conversation, that I'd flown halfway around the world to be given coffee and a door closing. She walked three steps toward her plane, stopped, and stood with her back to me in the rain-shadow of the awning, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — stripped of the machete, down to something tired and young.

"There are lights on Karakel at night," she said. "Boats that don't answer hails. I've seen them. I've heard other pilots talk about them. Someone is already working that site, Dr. Reyes, and they aren't doing it with brushes and grid squares." She turned. "You want me to fly you into the middle of whatever killed him."

"I want you to fly me to the truth. What's in the middle is negotiable."

"That's the most academic thing anyone's ever said to me." And there it was again — the almost-laugh, gone as fast as it came, but this time it stayed a half-second longer, and something in it wasn't hostile. Something in it was *recognized*: two women standing on opposite rims of the same crater, agreeing that the bottom needed visiting.

She looked at the sky, reading the monsoon the way I read a stratigraphy profile, top layer to bedrock. Then she looked at me for a long, unhurried moment, and I had the distinct sensation of being re-categorized — moved out of *trouble, academic* and into some new file whose label I couldn't yet read.

"Fuel's paid through Thursday," she said. "I carry one passenger. You ride up front, you touch nothing without gloves, and if I tell you we're leaving, we're leaving — mid-sentence, mid-discovery, no debate. Those are the terms."

"Accepted."

"Don't accept yet." She picked up her cup, drained it, and set it upside down on the bench — a pilot's superstition or a scientist's, I couldn't tell. "Because I need to tell you the last part, and then you can decide what kind of fool you're climbing into my plane."

The rain drummed. She folded her arms, and I watched her decide to hand me something heavy.

"Nine days after the quake, I put down on Karakel with engine trouble. Sheltered overnight in a sea cave on the west side — the one your charts don't show. And I found a lead manifest sheet stamped with the Meridian Expedition's mark." Her eyes came up to mine and held there, unflinching, daring me to flinch first. "It lists artifacts recovered from that cave. Including a gimballed brass compass. Artifact number MV-114."

MV. Mateo Vidal. My stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"If that compass is in that cave," Coral Crane said quietly, "then it was never lost with him. Which means the report I gave under oath — the one that helped convict his brother — was wrong. Or was made wrong." She exhaled slowly. "So before you ask me to fly you there, Dr. Reyes, you should know that I'm not taking you to a site. I'm taking you to my own crime scene. And I genuinely don't know yet which side of it I'm on."

I stood up. Rain ran off the awning between us in a silver curtain, and beyond it her plane sat on the strip like a patient animal, and beyond that the sea lay flat and gray and full of everything neither of us had said yet.

"I know exactly which side you're on," I told her. "I've been on it for five years. It's called the truth, and it's a rough crossing, and I'd rather not make it alone."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up my equipment case, carried it to the plane, and strapped it into the forward seat.

"We lift at first light," she said. "Pray the fleet doesn't beat us there."

"*Fleet?*" I said. "What fleet?"

She swung up into the cockpit and looked down at me with an expression I would come to know very well indeed — the look of a woman sharing bad news strictly on a need-to-know basis.

"The one that's been asking the same questions you have," she said. "Only they're asking with cranes and cutting torches. Get in, Doctor. We've got six tides."
