## 7. Unwelcome Passenger

I have a rule about passengers, and the rule is: no. Passengers are cargo that talks. Cargo that talks asks questions, and questions are how you end up with your name in someone's logbook and your business in someone's mouth. Ten years of flying medicine and machine parts across the Coral Triangle had taught me that the safest seat in any aircraft is an empty one.

So naturally I broke my own rule at first light, for a woman who'd spent the previous evening dismantling my life one careful sentence at a time.

Ilsa Reyes climbed into the forward seat like she'd been born there — which, to be fair, she probably had been. Some people are born into houses. Ilsa was born into field stations and research vessels and the particular brand of chaos that comes from being the smartest person on a boat full of academics. She stowed her kit without being asked twice, buckled in, and put her hands in her lap like a woman attending church.

"Rule of mine," I said, running the preflight. "No talking during takeoff."

"Noted."

"No touching anything."

"Noted."

"And if you're going to be sick, aim out the window, not at the manifest."

That got a look from her. Not offended — cataloguing. She was doing what she'd been doing since she walked onto my airstrip: filing me away somewhere. I didn't love it. People who file you away usually come back to retrieve something.

The engine caught on the second try, which was two tries more than I liked, and we lifted off the strip into air so thick with moisture it felt less like flying than swimming upward. The monsoon season was still three days out by the buoys, but you could taste it already — that metallic pressure behind the sinuses, the sea holding its breath.

Karakel lay two hundred and ten nautical miles east-southeast. Three hours in the Cub, four if the headwinds were feeling personal. I'd flown the route enough times to do it blind, which was fortunate, because Ilsa Reyes did not believe in silence.

"You knew Mateo," she said, once we'd cleared the coastal ridge and settled into cruise.

"I flew him places."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer you get." I checked the fuel gauge, which didn't need checking. "Eyes on the horizon, Doctor. Reading in a moving plane makes you sick."

"I've done six hundred hours of aerial survey over the Banda Sea. I don't get sick."

"Everyone gets sick eventually. It's just a question of altitude."

She let it go, which surprised me. I'd expected pushing. Ilsa Reyes struck me as a woman who pushed. But she turned to the window instead and watched the islands scroll past beneath us — green teeth in blue water — and when she spoke again her voice had changed register, dropped down into something quieter.

"He wrote to me," she said. "Three weeks before he died. A letter, an actual paper letter, because he said email was 'a rumor spread by people with nothing to hide.'"

I kept my eyes on the horizon. The horizon kept its eyes on me.

"He said the expedition logs were forged," she went on. "He said the recovery records for the *Santa Lucía* material had been altered before they ever reached the survey board. He wouldn't say by whom. He said — " she paused, and I heard her turn the page of memory, find the line again — "he said, '*If anything happens to me, check the compass. The compass will tell you where the truth went.*'"

The engine droned. Somewhere below us a fishing boat dragged its wake across the strait, white thread on blue cloth.

"And then he died," Ilsa said, "and the compass was listed as lost in the accident, and the logs went to the board unaltered, and everyone moved on except me, because I'm apparently constitutionally incapable of moving on." She shifted in the seat. "And then last month a colleague sends me photographs of a lead manifest sheet recovered from a cave on Karakel Island, stamped with the expedition mark, listing artifacts that officially never existed. Including a brass ship's compass."

"You think it's his compass."

"I think it's *the* compass. And I think whoever stamped that manifest wanted certain things found and certain things not found, and I intend to know which is which." She looked at me then, directly, the way she'd looked at me across my own kitchen table the night before. "You testified about that dive. You told the inquiry the compass went down with him."

The horizon wobbled. Just slightly. I corrected for it.

"I told the inquiry what I saw," I said.

"You told the inquiry what you were able to see, from the surface, at dusk, in a swell, while bleeding from a head wound. There's a difference between testimony and certainty, Ms. Crane, and only one of them holds up under water."

"You done?"

"No." She settled back. "But I'll wait. You strike me as a woman who answers better at altitude than at anchor."

I hated her a little for that. Mostly because she was right.

* * *

We made the halfway point — a speck called Pulau Tiga that isn't three islands but one island with delusions — and I brought us down on the grass strip there to stretch legs and top off the auxiliary tank I keep lashed in the back for exactly this kind of trip. Ilsa stood at the edge of the strip looking east while I worked the hand pump, and I watched her watch the water, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't nervous about the island ahead. She was nervous about what she'd do when she got there.

"You loved him," I said. It came out before I could stop it, the way things do at altitude.

She didn't turn around. "He was my research partner for four years. We mapped half the reef systems in this archipelago together. He named a nudibranch after my mother." A pause. "Yes. I loved him. In the way you love someone you assume you'll have time for later. And then there wasn't later." She turned. "Did you?"

The hand pump squeaked. I counted strokes. Forty gallons takes about two hundred strokes, and I needed every one of them to buy time.

"He was my partner too," I said finally. "Different kind. We flew together. Dived together. Ten years of — " I stopped. Started over. "Everyone thinks they knew him. His brother thought he knew him. I thought I knew him. Turns out he was writing letters to you that none of us got to read."

"That's not an answer either."

"No," I agreed. "It's not." I capped the tank. "But here's what I'll give you, Doctor, since you gave me the letter. Whoever forged those logs cost a man four years of his life in a cell, and cost me — " I gestured vaguely at myself, at the plane, at the whole sky I'd been hiding in — "cost me whatever you'd call this. So I'm not flying to Karakel for the manifest. I'm flying there because if that compass exists, then everything I said at that inquiry was wrong, and I need to know it before it kills me slower than it already is."

Ilsa considered this. Then she nodded, once, like a judge accepting a plea.

"Alliance?" she said.

"Don't make it sound official. Official means paperwork."

"Then unofficially: we share what we find. No secrets between us until Karakel. After Karakel — " she shrugged — "after Karakel we'll see who we both are."

"After Karakel," I said, "nobody's anybody. That's generally how it goes."

She smiled for the first time since I'd met her. It rearranged her whole face, and I resented how much I noticed.

* * *

It was forty minutes out from Karakel, coasting down the long green shoulder of the deep-water channel, that I saw the ship.

I saw it because ships in the Coral Triangle are like opinions — everyone has one and most of them are anchored somewhere useless. But this one was moving, and moving *wrong*: no fishing pattern, no ferry lane, cutting a straight military line across the channel toward the northern coast of Karakel. Dark hull. White superstructure gone gray with salt and indifference. Cranes on the aft deck folded like sleeping insects.

I banked slightly to parallel it, keeping distance, and Ilsa leaned across to the window.

"*Sable Fortune*," she said quietly. "Vikram Rao's flagship. I've seen her in Ambon. She's flagged out of Singapore, insured through three shell companies, and every government in the region has tried to catch her stripping wrecks for five years." Her voice tightened. "She shouldn't be here. There's no charted wreck within sixty miles of this channel worth her fuel."

"There is now," I said. "There's a cave full of Spanish silver and somebody's been selling tickets."

I watched the ship through the strut wires. At this range I could make out figures on deck — small, dark, purposeful. A RIB sat davited and ready. Everything about the vessel said the same thing my gut was saying: *they aren't exploring. They're extracting.*

"They have the manifest," Ilsa said. "They must. Same leak that reached my colleague. Which means they know the tide windows, they know the cave coordinates — "

"They know what's in the cave," I said. "What they don't know is what's in the back chamber. Because whoever photographed that manifest never got past the first room." I didn't say how I knew that. I hadn't yet told Ilsa about the slate, or the second chamber, or the thing I'd found written on it in handwriting I'd have known anywhere on earth. Some cards you hold close to your chest until you're sure whose game it is.

"So we beat them there," Ilsa said. "Slack water's at fourteen-forty. If we land now, gear up, and enter on this tide — "

"We're landing on a beach in front of a salvage ship with cranes and cutting torches, Doctor."

"Then we land on the other side of the island and walk."

I looked at her. She looked back at me, calm as ballast, and I realized something that should have frightened me and instead did something worse — it steadied me. Ilsa Reyes was not afraid. Not of Rao, not of the storm coming, not of the dead man whose letters she carried like scripture. She was afraid of exactly one thing, and it was arriving too late, and fear like that doesn't paralyze. It organizes.

"The south beach," I said. "There's a fisherman's track over the ridge. Ninety minutes on foot with gear. We'd hit the cave mouth right at slack."

"Then fly, Ms. Crane."

I put the nose down toward Karakel's southern shore, and as I did, the radio crackled — Channel 16, open frequency, the party line of the sea. A voice came through, accented, unhurried, almost courteous:

"*Unidentified light aircraft over the Karakel channel, this is salvage vessel Sable Fortune. Be advised we are conducting legitimate maritime recovery operations in this area. Suggest you alter course. Over.*"

Ilsa and I looked at each other.

"Legitimate," she mouthed.

I keyed the mic, because some temptations are stronger than self-preservation. "*Sable Fortune*, this is the aircraft you're worried about. We're sightseeing. Enjoy your legitimate operations. Crane out."

Silence on the channel. Then, faintly, I swear I heard laughter — not hostile, almost appreciative — before the transmission died.

Ilsa stared at me. "Tell me you didn't just introduce yourself to Vikram Rao."

"He introduced himself first. Manners matter." I leveled out for the approach, the south beach unrolling below us — white sand, green wall, the fisherman's track a pale scar up through the trees. "Besides, now he knows a plane's watching. Which means he'll post a lookout on the ridge. Which means — "

"Which means we can't use the track," Ilsa finished.

"Which means," I said, wheeling into wind, "we improvise. Welcome to my world, Doctor. Nothing here is charted."

The wheels touched sand at 13:05. Behind us, far up the channel, the *Sable Fortune*'s cranes were beginning to unfold.

And in the forward seat, silent for the first time all flight, Ilsa Reyes took out Mateo's letter, unfolded it, and read it again — as if the answer to everything might be hidden in the creases.

I killed the engine, and the sudden quiet filled up with the sound of the tide, going out, taking our six hours with it.
