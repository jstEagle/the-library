## 8. Tide One: The Descent

**CORAL**

Slack water, Ilsa told me, was a half hour wide and a lie the rest of the time. The sea pauses between tides the way a liar pauses between sentences — just long enough to make you believe it.

We anchored the plane off the cave mouth at first light, in water so still it looked poured. The earthquake had left the reef looking like a table someone had kicked: slabs tilted, coral gardens upended, and where no chart in the archipelago showed anything but blue, a black arch of rock stood above the waterline like a door left open in a house everyone thought was empty.

I'd been flying for ten years on the theory that doors like that were best left shut. I was about to spend a morning proving I'd learned nothing.

"Gear check," Ilsa said, and it wasn't a suggestion. She sat on the float with her wetsuit half-zipped and her hair plastered to her forehead, looking less like a marine archaeologist and more like a woman about to argue with the ocean and win. "Tank pressure. Regulator. Knife. Say them back."

"Tank pressure, regulator, knife," I said. "Also: flashlight, backup flashlight, slate, line reel, and the growing suspicion that I've been talked into something."

"You haven't been talked into anything. You'd have come with or without me. You just like having someone to blame."

I hated how fast she'd learned to read me. It had taken Mateo two years to get that good at it, and I'd loved him for it, and then I'd stood in a courtroom and made sure that skill died with him.

"Reef check," I said, to change the subject. "Current's nothing now. In three hours it'll be a freight train running in reverse. We're back on the plane before the water starts thinking about coming back."

"Before slack ends," Ilsa agreed. "You keep saying 'the water' like it's a person."

"Down here it is. And it's got a temper."

She laughed — short, surprised, like she hadn't budgeted for it — and rolled backward off the float into the sea.

I followed.

* * *

**ILSA**

The cave swallowed light the way a throat swallows a pill: reluctantly, and with a visible effort you could watch all the way down.

I went in second, on Coral's insistence, which told me two things. One: she'd done this before, enough times to have a protocol. Two: she trusted herself more than she trusted me, which was fair, and also the loneliest way I knew to live.

The entrance chamber was cathedral-vast. Our lights swept across walls furred with sponge and black coral, across a floor of pale silt that held our exhalations like fog. The earthquake had cracked the ceiling in long seams, and through them filtered shafts of green daylight, so the whole space was lit like the inside of an aquarium nobody maintained.

And there — everywhere — were the marks.

Chisel cuts. Survey tags, the brass gone green. A grid line scratched into the rock with the obsessive precision of people who believed the work would outlast them. Expedition work. *Our* work, or the shape of it. I put my glove against a tag and read the number and felt the number in my chest before I read it in my head: it was from Mateo's last season. The season that wasn't supposed to have happened. The season the official record said ended with a storm, a lost dive, and a compass that went into the sea with a man's brother's future.

Coral touched my arm. Two taps — *look* — and pointed her light down a side passage where the silt was disturbed. Not by water. Water disturbs silt in fans and ripples. This was drag marks. Boot scuffs. Someone had walked here, in air, recently enough that the marks hadn't finished dying.

We surfaced in an air pocket the size of a chapel, our lights finding a ceiling hung with pale mineral curtains. Coral pulled her reg out and breathed the cave air like she was tasting it for evidence.

"Somebody's been using this as a camp," she said.

"Somebody who knew the tide tables."

"Somebody who *is* the tide tables," she muttered, and I decided not to ask.

We mapped as we went, the way we'd agreed: I measured and called out, she wrote on the slate and paid out line. She was good at it. Better than good — she moved through the water like the cave had been drawn to her measurements, economical, unhurried, checking over her shoulder at intervals so regular they had to be habit rather than thought. I'd spent three years in the field with Mateo and I recognized the style. She'd learned it in the same school. The school where you and one other person keep each other alive by rhythm alone, and where the rhythm, once broken, doesn't grow back.

"Chamber three," I called. "Twenty meters, bearing east. Floor's dropping."

"Floor's always dropping," Coral said. "Caves are just the ocean's way of saying *keep going, see what happens.*"

"You're a very reassuring dive partner."

"I'm a very honest one. There's a difference."

* * *

**CORAL**

Here is what nobody tells you about guilt: it navigates.

I'd spent a decade flying away from a specific patch of ocean, and now here I was swimming through it, and my body knew the way. The turn of the passage. The taste of the water, iron and iodine. The particular hush of a cave that had been visited by people who loved it. Mateo had moved like this. Ilsa moved like this. I moved like this and hated that I still did, that ten years of running hadn't scrubbed it out of my hands.

The second air pocket was smaller, and it had furniture.

A camp stove, rusted to lace. A coil of rope gone to rot. And against the wall, sheltered under a ledge someone had chosen deliberately, a dive slate on a lanyard — the old kind, the white plastic kind, the kind every diver in this part of the world carried ten years ago.

Ilsa saw it at the same time I did. I know because her light stopped moving, and in a cave, a still light is a scream.

Neither of us said anything. We didn't have to; the slate said it. There was writing on it. The plastic had yellowed, the grease pencil had faded to a ghost, but the hand was large and slanting and impatient and I would have known it upside down in the dark, in a hurricane, in my grave.

I'd known it on the manifest sheet too, and I'd let myself pretend. A stamp, a mark, an expedition logo — those could be copied. But handwriting is a confession. Handwriting is a person.

Ilsa reached for the slate and I caught her wrist.

"Wait," I said, and my voice came out through the regulator as a foghorn of bubbles, so I pulled it and said it again in the open air. "Wait. Once we read it, it's real. Right now it's just a slate. I've been living off things that are just things for ten years. It's a good system."

"Coral." Ilsa turned her light so it lit both of us, which felt like a judgment. "You flew me three hundred kilometers on a lie about a charter. You anchored your plane over the reef where your partner died. You are not living off things being just things. You're living off things being unread."

The cave held the silence after that. Somewhere in the rock, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"His handwriting," I said. It wasn't an argument. It was a surrender arriving early, the way weather does.

"I know," Ilsa said, gentler than she'd been a second ago, and I understood that she'd been Mateo's partner in the way I had — the way where you learn to be gentle exactly one second after being right.

She lifted the slate off its lanyard and turned it to the light.

* * *

**ILSA**

The grease pencil had faded, but the letters had been pressed hard — the writing of a man who wrote like he talked, like the words might leave without him if he didn't pin them down.

I read it aloud, because some things need a voice or they don't fully exist:

*Day 9. Logs are wrong. Checked against Dez's numbers — the recovery depths don't match, can't match, he never dove the north wall. F called it "administrative smoothing." It's forgery. If anything happens to me, the manifest sheet is in the chest, chamber with the twin columns. The compass came up with us. Rafe never touched the rigging. I'm going to tell them all of it at the survey board. — M.V., the day after the last dive.*

The day after the last dive.

The last dive was the one that killed him. The official record — the record I'd built half my career on, the record I'd cited in two papers — said the compass was lost, that Rafe Vasquez had rigged the lift bags negligently, that the expedition's logs were recovered and verified. I had *verified* them. I had sat in a university office with photocopies and a red pen and I had verified a forgery, because the numbers were clean, and clean numbers are the most convincing liars in science.

"Coral," I said. "The date."

"I see the date." Her voice was flat in the way the sea is flat before it isn't.

The date was four days after the accident. Four days after the dive that the record said killed him and lost the compass and ended everything. Mateo had been alive. He had sat in this chamber, in this air, with a rusted camp stove, and written down the truth, and dated it, and then —

Then what? Then he'd died anyway? Then he'd walked out and vanished? Then the man who'd gone to prison for it had gone to prison for a crime that was half a lie wrapped around a truth nobody had bothered to find?

"He was here," Coral said. "After. He was *alive* after." She was looking at the slate the way you look at a door you'd walked past your whole life. "I testified — Ilsa, I stood up in a courtroom and I said —"

"I know what you said. It's in the transcript. I've read the transcript forty times."

"Forty?"

"I was his research partner. When your partner dies and his brother goes to prison, you read the transcript. It's the only autopsy you're allowed." I made myself keep going. "The chest. He wrote that the manifest sheet is in a chamber with twin columns. That's the evidence. That's the thing that vacates a conviction — a forged log, a dated slate, a manifest in the defendant's favor."

"Then we find the twin columns."

"We find the twin columns *and we get out before slack ends.* Both. In that order of urgency, actually reversed."

But Coral was already moving, and I saw it happen — saw the current take her that had nothing to do with water. Hope. It runs through a person like a riptide: invisible from above, irresistible from inside, and it doesn't care where it's going, only that it goes. I'd watched it take Mateo that way in our last season, the certainty that the truth was just one more dive down, one more chamber in. It had made him brilliant and it had made him careless and I had loved both versions and failed to save either one.

"Slack ends in under two hours," I said to her back.

"Then we've got under two hours," she said, and kicked deeper into the cave, toward the twin columns, toward the chest, toward the truth she'd flown ten years to get away from at a cruising speed of two hundred kilometers an hour.

I checked my pressure gauge, told myself the number was fine, and followed.

* * *

**CORAL**

The twin-column chamber was exactly where a man who thought in grids would put it: deep, dry at low tide, defensible. Two stalagmites stood like pillars at the entrance, and between them, wedged under a coral shelf, sat a chest.

Rotted. The lid gave like wet cardboard when I lifted it. Inside: silt, a brass fitting gone green, a coil of something that had been rope, and — flat, gray, patient as a headstone — the lead manifest sheet.

Ilsa unrolled it with the tenderness people usually save for infants and explosives. Her light moved across the stamped columns, the ciphered entries, the expedition mark I'd testified about, and I watched her lips move, translating, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"What," I said. "What is it. Say it."

"There are two manifests." She looked up. "The public one — the one the survey board saw, the one the prosecution used. And this one. The real one. And the real one lists the compass, Coral. Recovered. Catalogued. It was never lost. Which means your testimony wasn't just wrong." She stopped, because some sentences you have to walk around before you can walk through. "It means the logs were forged before the accident. Which means the accident wasn't an accident of negligence at all. Which means somebody needed the expedition's real records buried — and Mateo found out — and four days later he was dead, and his brother was in prison, and you were the only witness."

The cave dripped. The tide, far off in its long throat, began — I felt it before I heard it, a change in pressure, a faint pull at my fins, the ocean clearing its throat.

"Slack's ending," I said.

"Coral."

"I heard you. I'm hearing everything today. It's terrible for me." I looked at the manifest, at the slate in Ilsa's mesh bag, at the dark passage continuing deeper into the rock — a passage the survey tags followed, tags numbered past where we'd mapped, tags in Mateo's grid. He'd gone deeper. There was more down there. There was always more down there.

Ilsa put her hand on my arm. "The tide's turning. We come back at next slack. Tide two."

"And if someone else comes back first?"

"Then we come back faster."

I took one last look down that dark throat of rock, at the green daylight seams and the silt holding somebody's boot prints, and I felt the riptide take me whole — hope, hope, the most dangerous current in the Coral Triangle, and I'd been swimming against it so long I'd forgotten it could pull the other way.

We surfaced in the entrance chamber as the water began to move, and far above us, through the crack in the ceiling, I heard an engine.

Not mine.

Ilsa heard it too. Her hand found my wrist in the green dark and gripped, and we hung there in the rising tide, two women holding a dead man's handwriting, listening to a boat we didn't know drop anchor directly over the only door out.
