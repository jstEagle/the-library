## 9. Tide Two: The Fleet Arrives

There is a particular smell to a salvage ship that has been at sea too long — diesel, brine, and the slow souring of men who have been told lies they agreed to believe. The *Sable Fortune* smelled like that on the morning we came off the swell and saw Karakel Island sitting on the horizon like a green fist.

I stood on the bridge wing with my coffee going cold in my hand and did what I always do before a job: I counted the reasons this could go wrong. It's a habit from my father, who ran tugs out of Mumbai for thirty years and used to say that a captain who can't list his own disasters hasn't been paying attention. My list that morning went as follows. One: the monsoon was six days out, maybe five, and the weather buoys were already gossiping about it. Two: the manifest copy in my cabin was authentic enough to hang someone, which meant whoever leaked it wanted deniability more than they wanted me. Three: Karakel had no harbor, no airstrip worth the name, and no reason for anyone official to be looking at it — which was exactly why it bothered me that anyone unofficial might be.

Four, though I didn't write it down: I had a bad feeling, and in my experience bad feelings are just information arriving ahead of its paperwork.

"Captain." Priya, my second, appeared at my elbow with the chart tablet under her arm. She's the only person aboard who doesn't pretend to like me, which is why I keep her. "Reef line runs shallow along the north face. If we want the cave mouth, we anchor east of the spit in eighteen meters and run the divers in by tender."

"And if we want to be honest with ourselves," I said, "we're not here for archaeology."

"We've never once been here for archaeology."

"No," I agreed. "But usually there's a fiction. A permit with a smudge on it. A survey that got lost in the mail. This time we're just thieves with good equipment, and I find I miss the fiction."

Priya looked at me the way she looks at me when she thinks I'm performing conscience instead of having it. She may be right. Conscience is cheap at sea; it weighs nothing and takes up no bunk. But I've learned to let myself feel the discomfort anyway, the way you let yourself feel a rope burn — not because it stops you pulling, but because it reminds you the rope is real.

"Set the anchor," I said. "Two dive rotations today. Full survey of the interior before anything comes out. I want photographs of everything before a single coin changes hands."

"You want documentation."

"I want leverage," I said. "Documentation is what leverage calls itself when it wants to sleep at night."

* * *

The cave did not photograph well, which is how I knew it would be valuable.

The tender crew came back at midday with cards full of images and faces full of something they were trying not to show me. Joss, my lead diver — a Tongan who has pulled cargo out of wrecks from Truk to the Java Sea and claims nothing under water surprises him anymore — sat across from me in my cabin with salt drying white in his beard and told me, in his flat way, what they'd found.

"Chamber system runs back maybe two hundred meters at low slack. Collapsed sections, coral growth everywhere, but somebody's been through it. Recently."

"Somebody being us?"

"Somebody being one person, with a flashlight and no dive gear. There are hand marks in the silt. Boot prints. Size small, maybe a woman's. And Captain—" he paused, and Joss does not pause, so the pause itself was data, "—there's a chest in the third chamber. Rotted to lace. Empty now. But the lid's got an expedition mark burned into it. I took pictures."

He turned the tablet toward me and there it was, stenciled in faded black on rotten wood: the mark of the Meridian Deep Survey. Mateo Vidal's outfit. The expedition whose logs I had read twice in a hotel room in Manado while a lawyer explained to me, in the gentle tone lawyers use for expensive things, that the artifacts listed inside were recoverable, saleable, and legally gray in ways that could be made less gray for the right fee.

And boot prints. Small ones. Fresh enough that the edges hadn't slumped.

I looked at those prints for a long time. There is a version of me — the version my financiers paid for, the version that shows up in other people's stories as the villain with the good watch — who would have felt only irritation. Someone ahead of schedule. Someone eating into margin. In that version, the correct response is to double the dive rotations, post a watch on the cave mouth, and treat the interloper as weather.

But I have never entirely been that version, whatever my invoices say. What I felt, standing over those boot prints, was something worse for business. I felt intrigued.

Because here is the thing about the Meridian mark on that chest: the manifest lists artifacts recovered by that expedition years ago. Recovered, catalogued, and then — according to every record I could buy — lost. Lost in a dive accident. Lost with a dead man. The whole legal architecture of my job rests on that loss. If the chest is here, empty or not, then either the loss was a lie, or the recovery was, and both possibilities change what I'm holding.

"You recognize the mark," Joss said. Not a question.

"I recognize the paperwork attached to it."

"The prints go in, come back out. Single visitor. They didn't take the chest — couldn't have, alone, in that current. But the smaller artifacts from the side alcove—" he swiped the screen, showed me a shelf of coral where the silt was disturbed in neat, deliberate lines, "—those are gone. Or moved."

Moved. Not stolen. Moved, the way you move things when you want them somewhere safer than where you found them.

I dismissed him and sat with my coffee, which had achieved room temperature and a flavor I'd stopped tasting days ago, and I thought about the arithmetic of the situation. Six tidal cycles until the monsoon seals this cave until doomsday. A fleet of eleven souls and two decompression chambers. A financier in Singapore who expects a hold full of catalogued treasure and does not expect questions. And somewhere on or near this island, a person with small boots who knows things about the Meridian Survey that the manifest doesn't say.

The professional move was to report it. Call the office, describe the interference, request authorization to escalate. That's what the manual says. I wrote the message three times in the satellite terminal and deleted it three times, and each deletion told me something about myself that I filed away for later inspection.

Here's what stopped my thumb on the fourth attempt: if I report an unknown party at the site, the office reports to the financier, the financier reports to his lawyers, and within forty-eight hours there will be people on this island whose entire function is to make problems disappear, and I don't mean the kind you dredge up. I've worked for men like that. I watched one of them solve a labor dispute off Balikpapan in a way that still visits me at night, wearing the drowned man's face. And whoever my small-booted visitor is, they walked into that cave alone, without tanks, at slack water, knowing the tide tables better than my own dive planner did. That's not a rival salvager. That's someone with a history in these waters.

You don't feed people with histories to men who make problems disappear. Not yet. Not until you know whether the history is worth more than the silence.

I deleted the draft and told Priya the site was clean.

* * *

That evening I did something I almost never do, which is walk my own deck slowly, without purpose, letting the crew see me and pretending not to notice them seeing me. It's an old trick. A captain who appears at random seems omniscient; a captain who appears predictably seems blind. But tonight I wasn't performing. Tonight I wanted the ship's noise around me — the generator hum, the winch grease, Deckhand Okonkwo singing badly to himself in the head — because my cabin had started to feel like the inside of a question.

The diver, Ansel, found me at the rail aft. He's young, Dutch, clever with a rebreather and careless with his mouth. He'd been in the second rotation, deepest chamber.

"Heard we had company," he said.

"Heard that from where?"

"Silt tells stories, Captain. Somebody was in there inside the last week. Maybe days." He leaned on the rail beside me, uninvited, which is either disrespect or trust depending on how the next sentence goes. "You're not reporting it."

It wasn't a question either, and I let the silence answer for me, and he nodded like a man confirming a rumor he'd started himself.

"For what it's worth," he said, "whoever it was didn't touch the good stuff. The coins in the fifth chamber, the silver bar — all undisturbed. They went for the documents. The manifest sheet, the slate thing wedged behind the coral. Paper and lead, not gold." He shrugged. "A thief takes value. That person took evidence."

I turned and looked at him properly. "Evidence of what?"

"That's your job to wonder, isn't it?" And he pushed off the rail and left me there, which was either insolence or the most useful thing anyone had said to me all week.

Paper and lead. I went back to my cabin and pulled the manifest copy from the lockbox and read it again, slower, the way you reread a letter when you suspect the postscript matters more than the body. Artifacts recovered, itemized, stamped. And near the bottom, in the inventory of items *not* recovered — the losses, the casualties of the final dive — a brass navigation compass, serial-numbered, listed as destroyed with its owner.

Destroyed. With its owner. That was the phrase. I remembered it because the lawyer had tapped it twice with his pen and said, *this line is our friend.* Destroyed means gone means unclaimed means ours.

But chests rot and paper survives, and someone with small boots had crawled through a flooding cave to move paper, and I found myself doing a thing I despise in other men: hoping. Hoping that the compass line was a lie. Hoping that the accident was a story, that the dead man's logs were forged, that the whole tidy edifice of my employment was built on a grave that wasn't a grave. Because if it was a lie, then the treasure was dirty in a way that even my financiers couldn't launder, and a man can refuse a dirty job with his honor intact. Whereas if it was all true — if the manifest was honest and the loss was real — then I was simply a thief, and thieves don't get to hesitate.

Hesitation, I understand now, was the tell. A hunter doesn't hesitate. A hunter tracks, closes, kills. What I was doing on that bridge at midnight, watching the phosphorescence swirl in our wake like spilled stars, was not hunting. It was investigating. And investigation, unlike hunting, requires you to consider the possibility that you're on the wrong side of the story.

* * *

At 0400 the watch called me up because of a light.

Not on the water — on the island. High on the ridge above the cave, a single point of white, steady, then gone. Ten minutes later, again. Deliberate intervals. Not a fisherman; fishermen flash in panic patterns or don't flash at all. This was a signal, or a mistake by someone who thought they were invisible, or a lure.

I stood on the darkened bridge with the binoculars and watched the ridge breathe light at the sea, and I thought about boot prints in silt, and a rotted chest with a dead expedition's mark, and a young diver's parting shot — *they took evidence* — and I made the decision that will follow me, whichever way this ends.

I picked up the radio and did not call Singapore.

Instead I woke Priya. She came to the bridge with her hair unbound and her patience already spent, and I handed her the binoculars and pointed at the ridge, and she watched two more flashes before she lowered them.

"Someone's signaling," she said.

"Or signaling that they know we're watching."

"Which is worse."

"Considerably." I poured us both coffee from the bridge thermos, which is against regulations and therefore the only honest ritual left aboard. "Here's where we are. In four days the monsoon lands, the cave floods, and everything in it belongs to the sea again unless we strip it first. Behind door number one, I report the interference, bring down the financier's cleaners, and we finish this job in an atmosphere I won't describe before coffee. Behind door number two, we proceed quietly, I find out who our visitor is and what they know, and I decide — *I*, not the office — whether the manifest we're working from is a map or a forgery."

Priya drank her coffee and looked at me over the rim. "And if it's a forgery?"

"Then the treasure is evidence, the job is obstruction, and everyone holding a piece of it is holding a bomb."

"Everyone," she said, "including us."

"Including us. Which is why I want to know before the storm, not after."

She set down her cup. She's known me nine years and she has never once told me I'm a good man, which I respect, because we'd both know she was lying. What she said instead was: "Door two. But Vikram — if your visitor turns out to be dangerous, or if they cost us the window, I'm reporting it over your head. Don't make me choose between you and the crew."

"Fair terms."

"And one more thing." She nodded at the ridge, where the light had gone dark again, keeping its own counsel. "Whoever's flashing up there flashed *after* we anchored. They know we're here. They've known since we dropped the hook. So ask yourself what they're waiting for."

I asked myself exactly that, all through the grey hour before dawn, while the *Sable Fortune* swung gently on her chain above a reef she'd come to plunder, and the answer kept arriving in the same shape: they're not running. Whoever is on that island — small boots, steady hands, a history with a dead man's expedition — they aren't hiding from us.

They're watching us back. And that, more than the monsoon, more than the financier, more than the whole crooked ledger of this voyage, is what convinced me the manifest in my lockbox is lying to me.

By sunrise I'd doubled the cave watch, triple-checked the dive rotations, and written — and this time kept — a single encrypted note to myself in the ship's log, dated, timed, witnessed by no one:

*Site compromised prior to arrival. Visitor unidentified. Manifest authenticity in doubt. Proceeding under own authority.*

If this goes wrong, that line is the only honest thing I'll have brought home.
