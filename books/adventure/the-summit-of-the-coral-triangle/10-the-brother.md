## 10. The Brother

The radio at Karakel was a rusted Marine HF set bolted to the wall of the old copra shed, the kind of machine that crackled like it was chewing gravel before it deigned to carry your voice anywhere. I'd used it twice in my life before this trip — once to file a weather report nobody read, and once, four years ago, to hear a voice I'd sworn I'd never hear again.

I told myself the third time didn't count as a pattern.

It was dusk on the second day, tide one behind us and tide two breathing down our necks, and Ilsa was in the cave with a headlamp and a notebook, transcribing cipher columns by hand because she didn't trust her own camera not to die. Which left me alone in the shed with the manifest sheet folded in my breast pocket like a letter from someone dead, and a decision I'd been circling since dawn: call Rafe, or don't.

Here's the thing about decisions you circle for twelve hours. You've already made them. The circling is just the part where you pretend you still have a choice, because pretending is cheaper than doing.

I keyed the mic. "Karakel station calling Port Vanda Corrections relay, Karakel station, over."

Static. Then the relay's bored operator, a woman who sounded like she was reading my call sign off a menu. Patch requests took twenty minutes to route inland to the prison, and another ten for them to drag whatever inmate you wanted to a phone that hung on a wall between the laundry and the chapel. Twenty minutes is a long time to stand in a shed holding a handset that smells like salt and other people's emergencies.

I spent them doing what I do best, which is inventory. Fuel: enough to reach Vanda with a margin I'd have called comfortable last week and called thin now, because thin had started meaning something different lately. Water: two days. Air tanks: three, after Ilsa and I burned through the cave's first chamber. Patience: unmeasured, possibly zero.

And guilt: one unit, large, previously buried at depth, now surfaced and taking up most of the available space in the hold.

* * *

They say grief gets smaller with time. That's a lie told by people who've never carried any. Grief doesn't shrink. You just grow around it, the way a tree grows around a fence wire, until the wire's invisible and the tree is crooked and everyone admires how interesting the shape is.

Mateo had been dead ten years this monsoon. His brother had been inside for four of them. And in all that time I had spoken to Rafael Halloran-Mateo exactly once — through glass, in a visitation room that smelled of disinfectant and vending-machine coffee, where he'd looked at me with his brother's eyes and said, "You did what you could," which was either forgiveness or a sentence he'd decided I'd have to live inside. I still wasn't sure which. Four years later I was still not sure, and I had built an entire flying career on top of the not-sure, the way you build a house on a fault line and then act surprised when the ground starts talking.

The relay clicked. "Port Vanda Corrections. State the inmate name and number."

"Halloran, Rafael. Number—" I gave it. I'd never written it down. It lived in the same drawer of my skull as Mateo's birthday and the registration number of the plane I'd been in when everything ended, filed under things I couldn't lose even if I sold everything else.

Another wait. The shed darkened around me. Out past the reef line, the sea was doing its strange post-quake thing, lying flatter than flat, like a held breath. Somewhere under all that calm water was a cave full of Spanish silver and one item that wasn't silver at all: a brass compass stamped with an expedition mark, catalogued, dated, recovered — recovered, the manifest said, *recovered*, present tense, ledger-perfect — six weeks after I stood in front of a marine board and swore on my license that it went to the bottom with Mateo and stayed there.

If the compass was in that chest, then either the manifest lied, or I did.

I knew which one the marine board would believe. I knew which one I'd spent ten years making sure everyone believed.

"Go ahead, caller." A different voice now, younger, wary. The prison phone voice. "You're connected."

Then, quieter, closer to the receiver, almost gentle: "Coral?"

Four years, and he knew my breathing before I said a word. Brothers. They calibrate to the people their family loves. Even the ones who stopped loving you, probably. Especially those.

"Hey, Rafe," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Ten years of bush flying teaches you to keep your hands level in turbulence; apparently it works on vocal cords too. "You sound good."

"I sound like a man talking on a prison phone." A pause, and something that might have been a laugh if laughter were allowed in that building. "But they moved me to the good block. Morning sun. I get to see weather now. You know how long it's been since I cared what the weather was?"

"How is it?"

"Beautiful," he said, and meant it, and that undid me more than anything else he could have said. A man finding beauty in a strip of sky over a exercise yard, four years into a sentence for a crime that — maybe, possibly, according to a rotting lead sheet in my pocket — wasn't his at all.

"Rafe." I looked at the door. Old habit: check who's listening before you say the dangerous thing. The shed was empty except for moths and me. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish before you—"

"You found something."

Not a question. The static hummed. Somewhere in the walls a gecko ticked like a slow clock.

"Why would you say that?" I asked, too fast, which answered him better than any confession.

"Because you called me." He said it simply, without cruelty. "Ten years, Coral. You flew supplies past this island forty times and never once patched through. People only find me when they've found something else first. So. What is it? A lawyer? A pardon petition? Or is it the wreck?"

"The wreck," I said, because lying to Rafe felt suddenly like kicking a dog that had never bitten anyone. "There's a galleon site off Karakel. The quake opened a cave. There's a manifest, and it lists cargo that was supposedly recovered years ago by Mateo's expedition. Including—" My throat closed around it. I made myself say it anyway. Words are just words until you say them out loud; then they're true. "Including the compass."

Silence. Long enough that I checked whether the line had dropped.

"Rafe?"

"I'm here." His voice had changed. Flattened out, the way the sea goes flat before it does something. "Say the rest."

"There's more. Ilsa Reyes is here — Dr. Reyes, Mateo's research partner. She says Mateo wrote to her before the accident. Said the expedition logs were forged. Rafe, if the logs were forged, then the negligence findings were based on forged records, and if the records were forged, then—" I stopped. Because the next word was *you*, and saying it out loud in a copra shed on an island neither of us could see the other end of felt like pulling a splinter that went all the way to the heart.

"Then it wasn't my fault," Rafe finished for me, level as a spirit gauge. "Is that what you're telling me? Four years in, and you're calling to tell me the paperwork might have been wrong?"

"It's not paperwork. It's a primary source. It's—"

"Coral." And there it was, the thing I'd flown ten thousand hours to avoid hearing: not anger. Pleading. "Stop. Please. Listen to me, because I've had four years to think about this and you've had four days, and I promise you I've done the math more carefully than you have."

I gripped the handset. Outside, the flat sea had begun, very faintly, to talk — that low shushing of swell arriving from somewhere far away, the ocean clearing its throat.

"Some truths cost more than the lie did," Rafe said. "That's the whole lesson of this place. Everyone in here has a truth they couldn't leave buried. You want to know what I learned in year one? The lie kept people safe. The lie kept the expedition's families from losing everything — the financier would have gutted them, sued them into the water, you know he would have. The lie gave Mateo's mother a story she could live with instead of one that would have killed her. And yes, the lie put me in here. But Coral — I'm *fine*. I teach the literacy class. I've got morning sun. In eighteen months I walk out and start over somewhere no one knows my name. You dig this up, you don't free me. You detonate ten years of everybody's lives, including yours, and for what? A compass? A piece of brass?"

"For the truth," I said, and heard how small it sounded, how Sunday-school.

"The truth is a place," Rafe said. "Some places you can live in. Some places you can only drown in. I've been drowning in mine for four years so that other people didn't have to. Don't you dare make it mean nothing."

I pressed my forehead against the cool tin of the shed wall. Moths bumped the lamp. Somewhere across the island, faintly, I could hear Ilsa's generator, the little two-stroke heartbeat of the camp, proof that someone else was awake and working and believed in what we were doing.

"Mateo found out," I said quietly. "About the forgery. Before the dive. Didn't he."

A silence with a whole ocean in it.

"That's not a question I can answer on this line," Rafe said at last, and his voice had gone careful, official, the voice of a man who had learned in prison that some sentences are doors and you don't know which ones open onto freedom and which open onto more cell. "And that's not a question you should be asking. That's my point. Every answer past the one you already have makes it worse. For you. For me. For her memory—" He caught himself. Too late.

"Whose memory, Rafe?" I asked. "Mateo's?"

Nothing. Static, and the sea, and my own pulse ticking in my ears like the gecko in the wall.

"You were there," he said finally, and it wasn't an accusation, which was somehow worse. "You were the last one up. You saw what the water did. Whatever's in that cave, whatever's on that sheet — you already told the world your version, Coral. The board accepted it. I accepted it. Let it be. Some caves are supposed to close back up."

"Tide six closes it," I heard myself say. "Permanently. After that, nobody ever gets in again. Nobody ever proves anything, one way or the other, ever."

"Then let the sea keep it," he said. "The sea's been keeping worse secrets longer."

* * *

We talked another six minutes. About nothing. The literacy class. A guard who smuggled in mangoes. Whether I still drank my coffee black — he remembered, four years and concrete walls later, that I drank my coffee black, and I had to press my hand flat against the wall to keep standing while he asked.

At the end he said, "Promise me you'll think about it. Not that you'll stop — I know you, you won't stop, stopping isn't in your kit. Just think. Really think. About who pays, and whether they agreed to."

"I'll think about it," I said.

"And Coral?" A breath. "Whatever you decide. I'm not angry at you. I want you to know that, in case the line cuts. I was never angry at you. I was angry at the water, and the water doesn't care, so eventually I ran out of places to put it. But not at you. Never at you."

The line clicked dead before I could answer, which was probably mercy, because I don't know what my answer would have been, and Rafe Halloran deserved better than a woman's uncertainty delivered collect.

I stood in the dark shed for a long time after. The lamp drew its moth congregation. The sea kept up its new whisper against the reef, patient as a debt collector.

He was right. That was the hell of it. He was right about the cost, right about the families, right about the arithmetic of eighteen months versus a lifetime. If I flew away tomorrow and let tide six seal the cave, Rafe walked free on schedule, the mothers kept their survivable stories, and I kept my license and my altitude and my beautiful crooked tree.

And Mateo stayed dead twice — once in the water, once in the record — and the man who forged the logs stayed vanished, and the financier stayed rich, and I stayed a liar with a clean flight log.

I thought about it. Really thought, the way he'd asked. I gave it the full weight of a woman who had once sworn an oath on a document and broken it, and knew exactly what oaths cost.

Then I folded the handset back on its hook, picked up my torch, and walked down toward the cave, where Ilsa's light was bobbing in the dark like a small stubborn star, and where the tide was already turning.

Which is why, when I got to the plane at first light to run the preflight, I stopped with my hand still on the strut — because the tires were flat. Both of them. Slashed, clean and deliberate, in the wet grass, while we'd slept.

And the spare air tanks were gone.
