## 11. Tide Three: Sabotage

I woke to the sound of Coral swearing in three languages, one of which I was fairly sure she'd invented on the spot.

The third morning on Karakel had begun the way the others had — grey light through the tent fly, the smell of instant coffee and salt, the tide chart taped to a rock like scripture. But this morning the litany coming from the beach had a different music to it. Not the ordinary profanity of a woman whose coffee had boiled over. This was the profanity of a woman standing over a wound.

I crawled out of the tent and saw it immediately. The Cessna sat on the sand shelf above the high-tide line where we'd lashed it down, and it looked wrong. It sat nose-heavy, listing slightly to port, like a bird with a broken wing pretending it could still fly. Both tires on the left side were flat. Not soft. Flat — slashed, the rubber peeled open in clean crescents, deliberate as signatures.

"No," I said, which was not useful, but it was honest.

"Oh, there's more," Coral said, her voice pitched in that terrifyingly calm register I was learning meant the opposite of calm. She jerked her chin toward the tarp at the base of the wing. "Look under there."

I looked. The spare air tanks were gone. Both of them, plus the regulator kit, plus the dive slate bag that held our spare lines and lights. Everything we needed to go back into the cave. Everything except the gear we'd carried into camp two nights ago, which now represented our entire working inventory: two tanks, half-charged, and my personal slate.

"Who walks onto an island in the middle of nowhere," I said, "and steals air tanks?"

"Someone who needs them," Coral said. "Or someone who wants us to stop diving." She crouched by the tire and ran her thumb along the cut without touching the edge. "That's a filleting knife. Or a dive knife. Clean stroke, both tires, same depth. That's not vandalism. That's craft."

I stood there with my coffee going cold in my hand and felt something cold slide down the back of my neck, because I had a thought I didn't want to have, and the thought had a name.

* * *

We spent the morning doing what scientists and pilots do when they've been violated: inventory, argument, and theory.

Coral's theory was Rao. It was tidy. The Sable Fortune had been anchored off the north point for two days; we'd seen her running lights through the cave mouth at night, and yesterday one of her tenders had cut too close to the reef, close enough that Coral had watched it through binoculars and muttered things about insurance premiums. A shore party wasn't impossible. A shore party with a knife and a grudge against competition was practically standard operating procedure for a man like Vikram Rao, who struck me as the kind of person who kept his menace laminated.

"It's him," Coral said, pacing the sand in front of the plane. "It's textbook. Don't sink the plane — too loud, too traceable. Just make sure the archaeologist and the pilot can't get back underground. He doesn't even have to hurt us. He just has to make us wait."

"Maybe," I said.

"You don't think so."

"I don't know what I think." I did, actually. I knew exactly what I thought, and I hated it. "But look at the cuts again. Whoever did this walked up a beach in the dark, past our tent, past you — you sleep like a heron, you told me, one eye open — and knelt down and cut two tires quietly enough that neither of us heard a thing. Then they went into the plane's cargo pod, found the tanks under the tarp and the tie-downs, unlashed them, and carried them off. Two steel tanks are eighty pounds together. They didn't drag them. There are no drag marks."

Coral stopped pacing. "So?"

"So that's someone who knows planes. Who knows how your tie-downs work, where you stow gear, how close they can get before you wake up." I made myself say it. "Rao runs salvage barges, not bush strips. Dez Okafor flew with Mateo for six years. Dez Okafor knows aircraft the way you know weather."

The name landed between us like something dropped from a height.

Coral went very still. I'd learned to read her stillness by then — there was the stillness of attention, and there was the stillness of a woman holding a door shut from the inside. This was the second kind.

"Dez is dead," she said.

"He's missing," I said. "There's a difference, and we're both standing on an island full of evidence that the official record of Mateo's last expedition is worth approximately nothing. His body was never recovered, Coral. The inquiry couldn't even establish where he was the night of the accident. Everyone assumed he drowned or ran. Nobody checked whether he did both."

"And you've been sitting on this theory since when?" Her voice had an edge I hadn't heard before, and I realized, with the particular sting of being right at the worst moment, that I'd been sitting on it since the manifest. Since I read the cipher key and recognized the notation style — the little hooked cross-references, the way depths were recorded to the tenth of a meter instead of feet. Mateo's habit. But the *hand* behind some of those entries, the corrections, the marginal arithmetic — I'd spent three years sharing a lab bench with expedition data. I knew the difference between Mateo's numbers and the numbers of the man who processed them.

"Since the cave," I admitted. "I should have said."

"Yes," Coral said. "You should have." And then, because she was Coral, and because fairness lived in her somewhere beneath all that armor, she exhaled and added, "Although in my defense, I was busy suspecting everyone equally, which is its own kind of rigor."

It was almost funny. Neither of us laughed.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it keeps a guest list. Ten years after a death, you think you know who belongs to it — the partner, the brother, the widow if there is one, the parents. You build your mourning around those names like a house around its load-bearing walls. And then some afternoon a name you'd written off years ago comes walking back through the middle of it, tracking wet footprints across everything, and you realize the house was always bigger than you'd furnished.

Dez Okafor belonged to my grief. I'd just never let him have a room in it.

He'd been the third member of the Karakel survey, the junior researcher, the one who handled the equipment logs and the sample chain-of-custody and, apparently, according to whatever Mateo had scrawled on that slate before he died, a great deal more than that. I'd met him twice. Once at a conference in Manila where he gave a talk about photogrammetry of submerged sites that was, honestly, better than Mateo's, and once for a week on the old research station, where he'd fixed my spectrophotometer with a paperclip and taught me a card game I still didn't understand. He'd been funny. He'd been meticulous. He'd been twenty-six years old and so obviously in awe of Mateo that it embarrassed all of us a little.

And then the accident, and the inquiry, and Rafe Halloran going to prison, and Dez Okafor evaporating — no forwarding address, no publication, no conference appearance, no social media ghost, nothing. In my field, people don't vanish. People give keynote speeches about their trauma within eighteen months. A total disappearance isn't grief. It's either a corpse or a fugitive, and the sea never sent back a corpse.

I had told myself for a decade that the answer was corpse. It was easier. A dead man can't have forged anything. A dead man can't be kneeling beside a Cessna in the dark with a knife in his fist.

A live man, though. A live man has reasons.

* * *

By noon we'd rigged the flat tires as best we could — Coral pumped one enough to confirm the sidewalls were done for, then swore in her invented language again and started inventorying the repair kit with the grim focus of a woman doing arithmetic she already dreaded. Two patches. We needed four. The nearest strip with tires was a day's flight, assuming the monsoon cooperated, which the monsoon had shown no interest in doing.

"We're committed," she said finally. "Whatever happens next, we're here until the weather window or the tide says otherwise."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. Comfort is for people with options." She looked out at the water, at the flat grey swell marching in from the northeast, and I watched her do the thing she did — convert fear into logistics, logistics into motion. "New rules. One of us sleeps while the other watches. Gear stays inside the tent. And tomorrow at slack water we go down anyway, on the half-charged tanks, because whoever took ours wanted us grounded, and I have never once in my life done what a saboteur wanted."

"There's a version of that sentence that gets us killed."

"There's a version of every sentence that gets us killed. Flying is mostly choosing which ones." She glanced at me, and for a second the wry mask slipped and I saw underneath it, the tiredness and the terror and something else, something that had been growing between us with the patience of coral. "You think it's him. You really think it's him."

"I think the evidence thinks it's him," I said. "I think a man who forged expedition logs ten years ago and disappeared would want that cave sealed and flooded more than Vikram Rao ever could. Rao wants treasure. Treasure can be negotiated with. Dez — if it's Dez — wants the past to stay drowned. You can't split that with him. There's no deal that leaves him whole."

Coral was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed, gone somewhere private.

"When I testified," she said, "there were three of us who knew what happened that night. Me, Rafe, and Dez. I told the truth as I understood it. Rafe told the truth as it convicted him. And Dez told nothing at all, because Dez wasn't there — or so the record says." She turned a shell over in her fingers, white and worn, and didn't look at me. "If Dez is alive, Ilsa, then the record has been lying longer than I have. And I need you to understand what that does to a person. Every year I flew away from that night believing I was the only liar in the story. If he's alive, I was just the only liar who got caught in her own head."

"You weren't lying," I started, and she shook her head, and I stopped, because some arguments you lose on purpose.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it does before weather, the sky bruising green-black in the northeast. We ate rice and tinned fish and didn't talk much. Coral took first watch, wrapped in a blanket at the tent mouth with the tide chart on her knees, and I lay down and discovered that paranoia is a terrible mattress.

Every sound became a footstep. The reef dropping its rubble as the tide fell. A noddy tern resettling. The wind worrying the guy-lines. Somewhere past midnight I drifted into a shallow, glittering sleep full of fragments — Mateo's handwriting, a paperclip bent into a repair, a knife parting rubber with a sound like a kiss — and woke with a start to Coral's hand on my shoulder.

"Your watch," she whispered. "Nothing moved. But take these." She pressed the flare gun into my hands with the seriousness of a sacrament.

I sat up in the dark and watched the island breathe. The cave mouth was a black seam below the tideline, exhaling its slow rhythm of surge and suck. The Sable Fortune's lights burned steady off the north point, indifferent as stars. And beyond them, inland, the karst ridge stood up against the storm-light like the spine of something sleeping.

Around two, I heard it.

Not a footstep. Smaller than that. A scrape — metal on stone, brief, from the direction of the ridge trail, the narrow limestone path that climbed from the beach toward the island's interior. The direction nobody had any reason to go. The direction, I realized with my heart slamming, that led away from the sea and toward the old collapsed shelter we'd found on day one, the one with the cold fire ring and the empty ration tins we'd both pretended not to notice because noticing would have meant questions.

I raised the flare gun and didn't fire it. Because firing it meant committing, and because some animal part of me understood that whoever was out there had been careful for ten years, and carelessness now — mine or theirs — would cost more than silence.

The scrape came again, closer to the ridgeline, and then stopped altogether, and the island resumed its breathing, and I sat there in the dark with the flare gun shaking faintly in my grip, knowing two things with absolute certainty.

Someone was living on this island.

And in the morning, I was going to walk up that trail and knock.

* * *

Dawn brought the proof, though not the way I expected. I'd barely gotten the coffee water on when Coral, checking the tide line, went silent in a way that pulled me to her side like a hook.

Footprints. Bare feet, long-toed, pressed deep in the wet sand above the last night's high-water mark — a line of them running from the ridge trail down to within thirty meters of our camp and back again. Someone had come down in the dark and stood there. Watching us sleep. Close enough to hear us breathing.

And wedged into the fork of a driftwood stump at the turnaround point, placed where the dawn light would find it, was a single object.

An expedition slate. Old style, the magnesium-alloy frame Mateo's team had used, the kind that hadn't been manufactured in fifteen years. Tied to it with waxed cord was a scrap of waterproof paper, and on the paper, in small, precise, unhurried handwriting, eight words:

*I'm sorry about the tires. Ask her about the logs.*

Coral read it twice. Her face did something complicated — fury and relief fighting to a draw, and underneath both, dread, surfacing slow as a body rising in dark water.

"'Ask her,'" she said quietly. "Not ask us. Ask *her*." She looked up at me, and the wet-rope tension between us pulled one notch tighter. "Ilsa. What logs is he talking about? What do you know?"
