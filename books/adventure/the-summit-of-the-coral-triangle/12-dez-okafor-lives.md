## 12. Dez Okafor Lives

There are easier ways to introduce yourself to two women than a note left in a flooded sea cave, but I've had ten years to get good at cowardice and I wasn't about to break the streak on a Tuesday.

Let me back up.

My name is Dez Okafor, and according to the expedition insurance filings, the maritime board, and one very thorough obituary my sister wrote before I could stop her, I am dead. Drowned, technically — lost with all hands in the Karakel incident, body unrecovered, which was the one part of the plan that worked exactly as designed. The rest of it has been a decade of eating fish I caught myself and talking to a hermit crab I named Gerald, which tells you everything you need to know about how well the last ten years have gone.

I watched Coral Crane's plane come down on the far beach three days ago, and I have not slept since.

You have to understand what that plane means. That plane is a ghost with an engine. For ten years I've told myself the story that keeps me sane: Mateo died because the sea is the sea. Rafe went to prison because negligence is a lottery and somebody's number came up. Coral flew away because grief makes people stupid with distance. And I vanished because — well. Because of the part of the story I don't tell Gerald, and Gerald has heard everything.

The part where I forged the logs.

* * *

Here's how it actually happened, since apparently I'm doing this now, since apparently the universe has decided that after ten years of successful hiding I will be flushed out by a bush pilot with engine trouble and a marine archaeologist with a flashlight, both of whom are currently poking around my cave like they own the place.

The Aurelia Expedition had a financier. His name was Marcus Vane, and he wore linen suits in the tropics the way other men wear armor, and he had sunk two hundred thousand dollars into our survey of the Spanish wreck sites off Karakel, and by month four we had found nothing worth two hundred thousand dollars. We'd found coral. Beautiful coral. Coral does not repay investors.

Vane flew out on the supply boat, stood on the deck of our little research vessel in his immaculate shoes, and said the sentence I've been hearing in my sleep ever since: "Gentlemen, I need the preliminary reports to reflect recoveries. Artifacts located and catalogued. Locations pending. You're explorers — explore something."

Mateo laughed at him. That was Mateo. He laughed the way other men throw punches, quick and delighted, and he said, "Marcus, we'll find what's there or we'll find nothing, and either way you'll get the truth, because that's the whole point of the work."

Rafe backed him up, quieter, the way he did everything. Coral was flying supply runs and didn't even hear the conversation. Which left me.

Which always left me. I was the one who wrote things down. I was the expedition's pen, and Vane understood something about pens that I didn't yet understand myself: a pen doesn't argue. A pen gets tired. A pen has debts — mine were real, mortgage-shaped, sitting on a house in Cebu that my mother lived in — and a pen can be made to understand that a "preliminary" report is just a draft, just optimism, just a placeholder until the real finds come in.

Except the real finds never came in. And the placeholder became the record. And the record became the manifest. And somewhere between the second forged catalogue entry and the ninth, I stopped feeling like a man writing fiction and started feeling like a man committing fraud, which is what I was, and the only thing worse than knowing it was knowing that the compass entry — item fourteen, a silver ship's compass recovered from the outer chamber, location pending — was going to hang there forever like a hook waiting for a coat.

Because we never found a compass. Not then.

* * *

Mateo found out six weeks before he died.

He didn't confront me dramatically. He was diving with me, just the two of us, hanging at fifteen meters over a sand flat doing nothing in particular, and he turned his mask toward me and held up his slate, and written on it in his terrible handwriting were the words: *Item 14. Where?*

That was it. Two words. He'd been through my files. Of course he had — he was the lead researcher, it was his expedition, and I had committed the additional sin of being sloppy, of letting the forged entries drift out of my voice and into someone else's grammar. Item fourteen read like a man inventing a compass rather than a man describing one. Because it was.

I wrote back: *Later. Surface.*

He shook his head. Wrote: *Now. Or I write Vane tonight.*

And I looked at this man — my friend, my dive buddy, the best swimmer I ever knew, a man who had once towed me four hundred meters against a current when my fin strap snapped — and I felt something I have never confessed to anyone, not even Gerald, and certainly not to the priest on the mainland whose confessional I visited exactly once, drunk, three years ago, and fled from halfway through.

I hated him. For about nine seconds. Nine seconds of pure, white-hot, drowning hatred, because he was going to end my life with two words on a slate, and I had a mother, and I had a mortgage, and he had integrity the way other people have lungs, effortlessly, without thinking about it.

Then the current shifted, and the hatred passed through me like cold water, and I wrote: *Okay. Tonight. I'll tell him myself.*

And I meant it. That's the thing I need whoever finds this to understand — I meant it. I swam up planning my confession, rehearsing it, already tasting the ruin of my career and finding, to my surprise, that it tasted almost like relief. Ten years later I can tell you that relief was real. Confession always tastes like relief right up until the moment you have to open your mouth.

I never opened my mouth. Because that night, the night before I was going to tell Vane and Mateo both, in front of witnesses, everything — the storm came early, and the dive went wrong, and Mateo went into the water one last time, and the sea took the decision out of all our hands.

Or most of our hands. That's the other part.

* * *

I need to say the other part now, because if I'm writing a confession I might as well commit to the form, and because Coral Crane deserves to know it even if she hates me for it, which she will, and rightly.

After the accident — after the search, after the days of dragging the water and finding nothing — there was an inquiry. And the inquiry needed a story, and Vane needed a story, and the story Vane wanted was one where the negligence belonged to Rafe Halloran, the brother, the dive master, the man who had signed off on the conditions. Because if the negligence belonged to the expedition's methods — to Vane's schedule pressure, to the budget cuts that had us diving on aging equipment with a skeleton crew — then Vane was liable, and Vane's lawyers descended on that inquiry like gulls on a fish carcass.

And I was asked, gently, by men in linen, what I remembered about the equipment checks. And I said what I was coached to say. I said Rafe had cleared the rig. I said the checks were his responsibility. I said it in a flat voice with my hands folded, and I watched Rafe Halloran look at me across that room, and I watched him understand, and I watched him say nothing.

He said nothing. Do you understand? He knew I was lying — he must have known, some part of him knew — and he said nothing, because saying something would have meant telling the inquiry about the forged logs, would have meant digging into every document I'd touched, would have meant Mateo's death becoming a scandal instead of a tragedy, and Rafe loved his brother more than he loved himself. So he took it. He took ten years for a lie I told, and I sat in the back of that courtroom with my heart hammering like a fist on a door, and I did not stand up, and I did not speak, and when it was over I walked down to the water and I kept walking.

Not literally. Literally I took a boat, then another boat, then a long time in places where nobody asks questions. But that's what it was. I walked into the sea and let it close over the name Dez Okafor, and I have been underwater ever since, in every way that matters.

Coral testified too. I've read the transcripts — I got copies years ago, through channels I won't describe, because guilt is a meticulous archivist. Her testimony was the strange one. She put the compass in Mateo's kit. She swore she saw it, the silver compass, item fourteen, the one that never existed outside my imagination and my fraudulent handwriting. Everyone assumed she was mistaken, traumatized, grasping. I assumed it too.

But here's what kept me awake last night, watching her fire die down across the bay: what if she wasn't mistaken? What if Mateo found a compass — a real one, a genuine recovery, maybe the one true artifact of the whole cursed expedition — and logged it himself, quietly, without telling me, without telling Vane, a little gift of honesty tucked inside my dishonesty? What if he carried it on that final dive because it was his proof, his one clean find, the thing he was going to show Vane alongside my confession as evidence that truth pays better than lies?

What if the compass exists? Then the manifest isn't entirely forged. Then the record has one true line in it, and that line leads somewhere, and the women in my cave are following it, and everything I buried is coming up with the tide.

* * *

So. The note.

I've written eleven drafts. This is not an exaggeration; I keep them in a waterproof bag under a rock shaped like a sleeping dog, and Gerald lives nearby and judges me. Drafts one through four were apologies. Five through seven were explanations. Eight was mostly self-pity and has been burned. Nine attempted to shift blame to Vane, which is accurate and also cowardly, so it's been burned too. Ten was just the words *I'm sorry* repeated until the page filled, which felt honest and useless in equal measure.

Eleven is the one going in the cave. It says:

*I know why you're here. I was on the Aurelia expedition. I forged the recovery logs — items 3 through 19 — under pressure from the financier, Marcus Vane. Mateo discovered it six weeks before he died and gave me until that night to confess. I never got the chance. The compass may be real. He may have found it himself. I have been on this island since the inquiry. My name is Dez Okafor. I am the reason Rafe Halloran is in prison. Tell Coral I watched her plane land and couldn't run anymore. Tell her I'm sorry doesn't cover it. Tell her I'll stand up in any court she puts me in front of. I'm done hiding. The tide took ten years to bring you here. I won't waste the delivery.*

It's too long. Confessions should be short; that's the whole trick of them, the trapdoor quality. But I've spent ten years being brief with the truth, so perhaps length is my penance.

* * *

Getting the note into the cave is its own problem. The cave mouth opens at low slack and closes like a mouth, and I've been inside exactly twice in ten years — once in the first year, when I couldn't stay away, when I went in with a torch and found the carved coral and sat in the dark shaking, and once last year, when I dreamed Mateo was waiting and woke up already paddling and had to turn back at the threshold because the tide was turning and I am many things but I am not suicidal, whatever my sister's obituary says.

Tonight the slack window is narrow. Storm season is stacking up behind the horizon like furniture movers deciding whether to knock. I take the outrigger I've built and rebuilt three times, and I paddle across the bay with the moon behind cloud, and I think, absurdly, of Coral Crane's rule — never land twice in the same place — and how I have violated its spiritual equivalent every single day for a decade. Same island. Same rock. Same face in the still water of the same pool. Some people run. I hid, which is running with extra steps and worse scenery.

The cave takes me in. Inside, the air is stone-cold and mineral, and my torch beam finds the marks the two women have left — chalk arrows, a neat grid of measurements, a strip of survey tape fluttering like a nerve ending. They've been systematic. They've been careful. One of them has good handwriting, and I feel a stab of something adjacent to hope, because careful people check their facts, and checking facts is how dead men get exhumed and living ones get arrested, and God help me, I want to be arrested. I want it so badly my teeth ache. There is no version of freedom I've enjoyed in ten years. Prison would at least have walls I deserve.

I wedge the note into the split of a coral formation near their last chalk mark, weighted with a piece of ballast stone that I swear on everything holy is just ballast stone. Then I sit in the dark for a while, because the tide hasn't turned, because the cave has me for another forty minutes whether I like it or not, and because some part of me wants to leave something else here — wants to carve my name into the wall next to the old Spanish marks, *Dez was here, Dez is sorry,* graffiti as sacrament.

I don't. The note is enough. The note is already more courage than I've managed in a decade, and courage, I've learned, is a currency you spend in small denominations or not at all.

On the paddle back, the wind picks up out of the northeast, and the water goes choppy and opinionated, and I think about storms. I think about the barometer falling the way it falls before weather that rearranges coastlines. I think about the fact that in a few days none of this will matter — the cave will flood, the sea will keep its secrets, and the only evidence of anything will be whatever those two women carry out in their hands and their hearts.

Unless they carry me out too. Unless I make them.

* * *

I'm on the ridge above the beach at dawn, which is where I do my best brooding, when I see the ship.

She comes around the southern headland slow and heavy, gray hull, working decks, crane arm folded like a mantis forelimb. I know salvage vessels the way farmers know weather. She's not a survey ship and she's not a fishing boat and she's not lost. She drops anchor off the reef with the unhurried confidence of something that knows exactly what it came for.

And below me, at the tree line, Coral Crane and the archaeologist are standing very still, looking at the same ship, and even at this distance I can see the moment the morning changes temperature for both of them.

I should go back to my hut. I should bank my fire, eat my fish, and wait for the storm to settle everything the way storms do. That is the Dez Okafor move. That is the play I've run ten thousand times: hide, wait, survive, repeat.

Instead I find myself walking down toward them, hands empty, heart going like a bilge pump, rehearsing the only opening line I've got after ten years of practice:

"You're going to want to hear this from me before you hear it from anyone else."

They turn. Coral Crane looks at me the way you look at a ghost, which is fair, because that's what I am — and then her eyes change, and I realize with a lurch that she recognizes me, that the dead man is standing in front of her with seawater in his beard and a confession in his pocket, and that the worst possible moment for the truth has finally arrived, right on schedule, riding in ahead of the monsoon like it booked passage.
