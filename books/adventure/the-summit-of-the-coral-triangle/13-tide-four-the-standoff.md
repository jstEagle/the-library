## 13. Tide Four: The Standoff

The tide had turned an hour before Vikram Rao found us, which meant the water in the cave mouth was doing that slow, greedy climb it does when nobody's watching — a finger's width every few minutes, patient as a debt collector. Ilsa and I were sitting on the coral shelf just inside the entrance, eating crackers out of a foil sleeve because we'd forgotten plates, going over Mateo's slate for the hundredth time like two women trying to read tea leaves in handwriting.

I'd been dreading this moment since I first saw the Sable Fortune's masthead lights on the horizon two nights ago. Not because of what Rao might do to us. Because of what he might do to *me* — which was worse, because it wouldn't involve a weapon at all.

He came around the headland at slack water in his tender, alone, which was either a courtesy or a message. With Vikram Rao I'd learned by then that those were usually the same thing. He stepped onto our shelf without asking permission, which told me everything about how he'd been raised and nothing about how he'd been ruined, and stood there for a moment surveying the cave like a man inspecting property he already considered half his.

"You've been busy," he said.

"We've been thorough," Ilsa corrected, and I loved her a little for it, even as my stomach dropped through the shelf into the black water below.

Rao held up a sheet of lead, flat as a serving tray, stamped with the expedition mark I'd known for a decade — the one with the frigate bird and the three stars, the one Mateo had designed himself over cheap rum in a Puerto Princesa bar while I sketched the border because he couldn't draw a straight line sober or otherwise. The manifest. Or rather, *a* manifest, because there were apparently more copies than anyone had bothered to count, and every one of them was now a live grenade rolling around the Coral Triangle looking for someone to detonate under.

"Yours is the third I know about," he said. "There's a fourth in a bank vault in Singapore. Possibly a fifth. My buyer has been generous with leaks."

"That's not how manifests work," Ilsa said. "That's how evidence works."

"Evidence," Rao repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. Well."

I want to tell you I sized him up coolly, all bush-pilot nerve and poker face. What actually happened is that my hands started sweating so badly I had to wipe them on my shirt twice in the first minute, and Ilsa noticed both times, and said nothing, which was its own kind of kindness.

Because here was the thing: Vikram Rao wasn't holding a gun. He was holding something I wanted. And men who hold what you want don't need guns. They need only patience and arithmetic, and Rao struck me as a man who did his sums twice.

* * *

Let me back up four hours, because the shape of this day matters.

Tide three had gone long. We'd spent it in the second chamber, the one past the squeeze where you have to exhale fully and turn your shoulders sideways, mapping by feel and by the weak glow of Ilsa's dive light bouncing off flowstone. We'd found the slate chamber empty except for what mattered: Mateo's writing, dated eleven days after the dive everyone called final. Eleven days. He'd been alive, and writing, and someone had needed him very much to be dead.

We'd also found Dez Okafor's note — tucked under a coral ledge, weighted with a shell, written in the careful block letters of a man who knows his cursive would identify him faster than a photograph. *I forged the logs. They made me. Mateo knew. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.* Three times, the apology, each one smaller than the last, like a man apologizing himself down to nothing.

Ilsa read it aloud once, quietly, and then we sat in the dark and didn't speak for a long time. There are silences that are empty and silences that are full, and that one was so full it sloshed.

Then the tide began to come in, and we swam out, and I climbed into the plane and checked the fuel gauge the way you check a wound — often, and wincing — and we ate crackers, and I pretended the next thing coming wasn't already visible from the beach.

It was. The Sable Fortune had moved overnight. She'd anchored off the north point, close enough that her generator noise carried across the water like a neighbor's television through a thin wall. Her tenders ran back and forth all morning, ferrying gear, and I counted divers on the deck through binoculars and got to seven before I lost track, and seven is a lot of divers for a site that officially doesn't exist.

"They're not waiting for the monsoon," Ilsa said. "They're racing it."

"Everyone's racing something," I said. "That's practically the regional motto."

She laughed, and then stopped laughing, because we both understood I hadn't entirely been joking.

* * *

So: the standoff. Except it wasn't a standoff, not really, because standoffs require two people pointing things at each other, and all any of us were pointing was paper.

Rao set the manifest sheet down on a flat coral table between us, deliberately, the way a dealer lays down cards. Sunlight caught the stamping — the frigate bird, the stars, the columns of entries in Spanish and English both, item numbers running down the margin like a hymn to somebody else's greed.

"I'll be direct," he said, "because I find indirection wastes daylight, and daylight is the one resource here none of us controls." He looked at me. "You flew medicine into river villages for ten years. You have a reputation. Reputation is collateral. I'm offering you a loan against it."

"Charming," Ilsa muttered.

"The site floods in—" he checked his watch, an affectation, since his phone was more accurate, but men like Rao trust the object they can hold, "—forty-one hours. After that, Karakel keeps its secret forever, and everyone's expenses become sunk costs. Before that, there is approximately nine million US dollars in recoverable material in these chambers, conservatively estimated, and I am prepared to split it."

"Split it," I said.

"Fifty-fifty after costs. Your plane, your airstrip access, your local credibility, your silence. My equipment, my divers, my buyer, my discretion." He spread his hands. "You walk away funded for life. I walk away funded for life. The forgery story stays buried, which — forgive me — serves you better than it serves me. You testified against a man based on logs you now know were false. That's not a problem money solves, but money makes it survivable."

And there it was. Not a threat. An invoice.

I stood very still and let the offer land on me the way a wave lands on a swimmer who's decided not to fight it — feeling its whole weight pass through, taking inventory of what it moved.

Nine million dollars. My fuel bills alone ran five figures some months, and I paid them late, and I paid them with money I earned flying cargo that occasionally included things I chose not to ask about, because the alternative was grounding myself, and grounding, I had learned, was the one condition I could not survive. Nine million dollars was never having to look at a fuel gauge with dread again. It was a new engine, a new airframe, maybe a hangar with a roof that didn't leak, maybe — and here the thought arrived uninvited and stayed — maybe enough to make amends in ways that cost something. Rafe's legal appeals. Compensation for a decade of a man's life. Money couldn't buy back time, Rao was right about that, but it could hire the lawyers who fought for it.

And all it would cost was everything I'd flown out here to do.

"You know what's in the manifest," I said. "You've read it."

"Item forty-one," Rao said. "A brass ship's compass, provenance logged as recovered, condition noted as water-damaged but intact. Recovered — per the log — during the final survey week." He watched my face while he said it, and I hated him a little for making me watch him watch. "The compass that figured in your testimony. The one you swore was lost with your partner."

"It was lost," I said. "As far as I knew. As far as anyone—"

"As far as anyone was permitted to know," Rao said gently, and gentleness from a man like that is more frightening than anger, because anger spends itself and gentleness is budgeted. "Dr. Halloran. Ms. Reyes. Let me tell you what I actually am, since we're being direct. I'm not a pirate. Pirates are romantic and poor. I'm a contractor. Governments hire people like me when they want things done before paperwork catches up; collectors hire people like me when they want things done before ethics catches up. I have never in twenty years hurt anyone who didn't first try to hurt my balance sheet." He paused. "I'm telling you this so you understand the offer is real, and so you understand what happens if you decline it. If you decline, I proceed without you, recover everything, and the forgery story surfaces anyway — because my buyer loves leverage more than he loves artifacts — and you get nothing. No money, no credit, no protection. Just the truth, arriving at the worst possible speed."

"He's good," Ilsa said to me, low. "I'll give him that. He's rehearsed."

"I haven't rehearsed a day in my life," Rao said, sounding almost wounded. "Rehearsal implies doubt."

* * *

Here is what I did not say, standing on that coral shelf with the tide climbing behind us and nine million dollars laid out between us like a bride price:

I thought about saying yes.

Not for long. Maybe eight seconds. But I thought it honestly, all the way through, the way you have to think a bad idea or it comes back later wearing a better costume. Eight seconds of imagining the rest of my life with the weight lifted — no testimony, no inquiry, no license board, no Rafe looking at me across a visiting-room table with that terrible forgiveness of his. Eight seconds of freedom, priced and quoted, cash on delivery.

And then I thought about Mateo's slate, and the date on it. Eleven days. He'd spent eleven days knowing, and writing, and presumably deciding what to do about it, and whatever he'd decided had gotten him killed — or hadn't, and the drowning was genuinely an accident, and even that possibility was its own kind of horror, because it meant he'd died still carrying the knowledge, still planning, still trusting the world to give him time.

Mateo had trusted the world to give him time. The world had declined. I was not going to make the same request and expect a different answer.

"No," I said.

Rao didn't move. I'd expected a flicker of something — disappointment, recalculation, the small mechanical adjustments of a negotiator shifting targets. Instead he just looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, and finally said, "May I ask why? Genuinely. Not as tactics."

"Because I've spent ten years burying one lie," I said, "and I've gotten very good at it, and I've noticed that getting good at something is the surest sign you'll keep doing it."

He absorbed that. Out past the reef, the Sable Fortune's generator changed pitch, someone adjusting a load somewhere, the sound of other people's decisions arriving across open water.

"And the money?" he asked.

"The money's real," I admitted. "God help me, the money is *real*. That's what makes it a temptation instead of an insult."

For the first time, Vikram Rao smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man who has just confirmed a hypothesis.

"Ms. Halloran," he said, picking up the manifest sheet and sliding it into a waterproof tube with the unhurried precision of a surgeon closing, "you are the first person in six years to decline me for a reason I believe." He turned to go, then paused, one foot on the wet rock, and delivered the sentence I'd been waiting for since he stepped ashore — the sentence underneath all the other sentences, the load-bearing one:

"The tide turns in nineteen minutes. My divers enter the cave at the next slack. I'd suggest you be elsewhere, or be very good at holding your breath." A beat. "I'd prefer the former. For what it's worth — and I recognize it isn't worth much — I'd prefer it quite strongly."

Then he was in the tender and the tender was a wake and the wake was a long white scar unzipping toward his ship, and Ilsa and I stood on the shelf watching it go.

"Nineteen minutes," she said.

"Forty-one hours until the site floods," I said. "Seven divers. One buyer in Singapore who loves leverage. And a man who just told us, in his way, that he doesn't want us dead." I wiped my hands on my shirt a third time. "Ilsa, I need to tell you something, and I need you to not interrupt, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve."

She turned to me. Behind her, the cave mouth breathed — a slow inhale of green water, the sea taking back what it had briefly lent.

"When I testify next month," I said, "if I testify — when I stand up in that room and say the logs were forged and my statement was false — Vikram Rao's buyer loses his leverage. Which means Rao loses his contract. Which means the only thing standing between that fleet and a clean getaway is a government survey that won't arrive before the monsoon." I looked at the water rising over the shelf where the manifest had lain. "Unless someone gets the evidence out first. All of it. Tonight, at the next slack, deeper than Rao's divers will bother to go, into the chamber past the squeeze."

Ilsa was quiet for exactly as long as it took me to regret starting the sentence.

"You're proposing we beat them to their own crime scene," she said, "and steal the proof of your own perjury."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"How else would you like me to say it?"

"Sympathetically."

"Not a service I offer." But she was already pulling her fins out of the mesh bag, and checking her gauge, and doing the small competent things that were, I was learning, how she said yes.

The tide turned. The water at the cave mouth went glass-still, held between breaths, and somewhere off the north point the Sable Fortune launched her first tender, and I heard the outboard before I saw it — a thin, insectile whine crossing the water straight at us, carrying men whose orders we could only guess at.

I stepped to the edge of the shelf, spat into my mask, and pulled it down over my face.

"Stay close," I said. "And whatever you see down there — however deep it goes — don't let me stop swimming."
