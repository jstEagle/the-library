## 14. What Coral Buried

Ilsa Reyes sat across from me in the cockpit of the Cessna, knees drawn up under her chin, rain hammering the fabric overhead like a fist on a door that wouldn't open. We were waiting out a squall line at the edge of Karakel's lagoon, engines cold, no reason to be anywhere, which is the most dangerous condition two people can share.

She'd asked. That was all. She'd asked me to tell her what happened the night Mateo died, and I'd said *later*, and she'd said *there is no later anymore*, and she was right. The barometer was falling like a man off a cliff. The tide was eating hours. And somewhere out there was a confession I'd been carrying for ten years, packed so tight it had become part of my body — a second skeleton, heavier than the first.

So I told her.

* * *

It started with weather, because everything with me starts with weather.

The night of the dive, the sky over the atoll had that bruised, low-slung look I've learned to read from ten thousand feet and still ignore at sea level. The expedition boat — the *Meridian Star*, forty feet of borrowed money and borrowed time — rode at anchor in the channel, and the whole crew was giddy, because we'd found it. The galleon's scatter field. Coral heads, silver bars, a compass rose carved into living rock that Mateo swore matched the manifest cipher he'd been chasing for three years.

Mateo Vidal could make you believe anything. That was his gift and it was also, in the end, the mechanism of my ruin. He stood on the deck with wet hair plastered to his forehead and said, "One more dive. Slack water tonight, before the front comes through. If we don't log the site properly now, the survey window closes and some government pencil-pusher claims it for a museum basement."

Rafe didn't want to go. I remember that with a clarity that feels less like memory than like punishment. Rafe Vidal was the careful one, the checklist one, the one who tested every regulator twice and made Dez recite decompression tables like catechism. He looked at the sky, then at the swell coming through the channel, and said, "Tomorrow. It'll keep."

And Mateo laughed — that laugh, bright and reckless as a flare — and said, "It'll keep if the storm does, and the storm won't."

Dez Okafor was already kitting up. Dez, who never contradicted anyone who signed his paychecks. The expedition's financier had been leaning on all of us by then; I knew that even if I didn't know the half of it. Money was thin, tempers thinner, and there was a hunger in the way Dez checked his gauges that I mistook for enthusiasm and should have recognized as fear.

I went because Mateo asked. That's the sentence I've carried. Not *because I wanted to*. Not *because I was reckless too*. Because Mateo asked, and loving him meant that his wanting was a current I didn't know how to swim against.

Four of us went down: Mateo, Rafe, Dez, me. Forty meters, dusk light going blue-black, the cave mouth below us like a held breath. The plan was simple — twenty minutes inside, tie-off lines, photograph the chamber, surface before the front hit. Textbook. Except nothing about that night was textbook, starting with the fact that Mateo had already been down twice that day and everyone knew the tables said he shouldn't, and nobody said so, because saying so would have meant being the one who took the discovery away from him.

I want you to understand something about panic underwater. It doesn't feel like panic. It feels like the water getting personal.

We were maybe twelve minutes into the chamber when the surge came through. Not the storm yet — just the ocean clearing its throat ahead of it — but inside a cave, a surge isn't weather, it's a hand. It shoved the silt off the floor in a wall of brown, killed our visibility to arm's length, and snapped the tie-off line Dez was managing. In the black, I heard Mateo's regulator free-flow — that shriek of escaping air that sounds exactly like screaming — and then I heard him moving, fast, wrong direction, deeper instead of out.

Rafe went after him. Of course Rafe went after him. And I did the thing I have spent ten years not saying out loud:

I froze.

Not long. Maybe thirty seconds. Maybe forty. Long enough that when my own brain came back online, my gauge showed I'd burned air I needed for ascent, my light was pointing at nothing, and the choice in front of me wasn't *save Mateo* anymore. It was *save myself or die trying and save no one*.

I swam for the entrance. I swam for my life while my partner drowned thirty meters behind me, and I surfaced alone into the teeth of the rising wind, and I hauled myself onto the deck of the *Meridian Star* and vomited seawater and screamed his name until my voice tore.

They found Mateo two days later, after the storm, in an outer chamber. His slate was in his hand. Remember that — the slate. It matters more than I understood then.

Rafe they pulled out alive, half-drowned, bent, wrecked. He'd found Mateo's body and tried to bring it up and the surge had taken them both apart. He nearly died in the recompression chamber. And in the fog of hyperbaric oxygen and grief, what he remembered most clearly was that he'd been the one who insisted on checking the anchor line, the one whose job it was to call the dive if conditions turned — and he hadn't called it. He'd gone down anyway, because his brother asked.

Here is what I did, and I need you to hear it the way I've never let myself hear it.

The investigators came. Port authority, maritime board, men with clipboards and kind, terrible voices. And they asked me what happened, and I told them the true things — the surge, the silt, the broken line, the free-flow — and then they asked me one question too many. They asked whether anyone had raised concerns about the dive before descent. Whether the conditions had been marginal. Whether the decision to dive had been sound.

And I thought about Rafe in the chamber, fighting for his life because he'd gone back for his brother. I thought about what would happen to him if I answered honestly — that yes, the dive was unsound, that yes, we all knew it, that yes, we went anyway because Mateo's charisma had overridden everyone's judgment including mine. Negligence. Reckless operation. On a commercial-adjacent expedition, with a financier's insurance riding on it, those words meant prison. They meant Rafe — careful, decent Rafe, who had tried to stop it — going to a cell while I flew free because I'd frozen and run and survived.

So I lied.

I told them the conditions had deteriorated without warning. A rogue surge, unforeseeable, an act of the sea. I told them no one had voiced concern. I told them the dive plan had been sound. I signed my name under it. And when the board wanted corroboration, the expedition logs — Dez's logs, neat and typed and wrong — said exactly what I'd said, because Dez had already forged them under pressure from the man who owned our debt, and I didn't know that then, but my lie fit his forgery like a key fits a lock, and together they closed around Rafe's throat.

Two years. That was the sentence. Negligent operation causing death. He got two years because I couldn't stand to watch a drowning man drown twice.

People ask — Ilsa asked, just now, in this cockpit, in this rain — why I didn't recant later. Why I let it stand. And the answer is ugly and simple and mine: because recanting meant admitting I'd lied under oath, which meant perjury, which meant my own license, my own freedom, and worse — it meant telling the world that Coral Crane, the woman who tells stories about other people's courage, had frozen in the dark and left her partner to die and then built a lie on top of his body to protect herself from the consequences of her own cowardice.

Every year it got heavier. Every year the cost of truth went up, like interest compounding on a loan I never meant to take. And so I did what I do. I flew. I made a rule — never land twice in the same place — because a woman who never stays anywhere can't be cornered anywhere, can't be asked anything twice, can't be known well enough for anyone to notice she's running.

Ten years of runway. Ten years of quinine and gossip and cargo, of being competent and charming and gone. All of it — every mile — was me outrunning a thirty-second freeze in the dark at forty meters.

* * *

I stopped talking. The rain kept on. Ilsa was quiet for a long moment, and then she reached over and put her hand flat on the panel between us, not touching me, just near me, the way you'd steady yourself on a bulkhead in rough air.

"The slate," she said. "You said Mateo died holding his slate."

"He always wrote underwater. Depths, times, observations. Why?"

"Because the slate we found in the cave — the one with his handwriting, dated *after* the dive —" She paused, and her voice did something careful. "Coral, if he was writing after the accident, then either the timeline everyone believes is wrong, or Mateo didn't die where they say he died. Either way, your testimony describes a night that didn't happen the way you swore it did."

I stared at the rain streaming down the windshield and felt the full weight of what she was saying settle onto my chest like a hand. For ten years I'd believed my lie had buried the truth cleanly. But the truth hadn't stayed buried. It had written itself down, in Mateo's own hand, and waited in the dark for someone to come read it.

"And Rafe," I said. My voice came out small. "He begged me not to dig. On the radio. He said some truths cost more than the lie did." I laughed, and it was a bad sound. "I thought he was protecting himself. Some old shame. But he wasn't protecting himself at all, was he. He was protecting *me*. He knew. Somehow he knew I'd lied, and he took the two years anyway, and he's spent four more years making sure I never had to look at it."

Ilsa didn't offer absolution. I loved her a little for that, even then. What she said was: "Then the only thing left to decide is whether you spend the rest of your life paying interest on a debt that forgiveness already cleared — or whether you walk into that port office and pay it in full."

Outside, the squall line lifted off the lagoon like a curtain being drawn, and through the gap I could see the channel, and beyond it the headland, and beyond that the low dark shape of the Sable Fortune's mast lights, closer than they'd been at dawn.

Vikram Rao was done waiting.
