## 15. Love Against Duty

I have spent my entire career in the company of things that are older and more patient than I am. Coral heads that grow a centimeter in a decade. Sediment cores that read like ledgers written by someone with all the time in the world. The ocean itself, which forgives nothing and forgets less, and which has taught me — or so I told myself for years — to be equally unhurried about truth.

It was a comfortable lie, the way good lies always are. Because here is what I never put in any paper, never said aloud at any conference, never even wrote in the margins of my own field notes where only God and future archivists could find it: three years ago, I sat on radiocarbon dates from the Sulu shelf that contradicted the accepted chronology of the trade routes, and I let them sit in a drawer because publishing them would have burned down the career of the man who funded my lab. I told myself the dates were probably contaminated. I told myself the field needed stability. I told myself a great many things in the tone of voice scientists use when they mean *I was afraid*.

So when I say I understood Coral Halloran — understood her the way you understand a wreck site, by lying against her in the dark with a light — understand that I wasn't being generous. I was being precise.

We were still in the cave shelter's antechamber, the two of us, with Mateo's slate wrapped in my shirt between us like a third person who had opinions. Outside, the barometer I'd carried since graduate school — an aneroid instrument older than either of us, brass-cased, temperamental as a cat — had been falling all afternoon. Not falling. *Plummeting*. It was making sounds. Little ticking complaints, like something inside it counting down.

Coral noticed me looking at it.

"How bad?"

"Landfall's moved up." I turned the dial toward her. "The monsoon trough absorbed the squall line. We're not looking at six tides anymore. I'd say four. Maybe three and a half if the pressure keeps doing this."

"Three and a half tides," she repeated. She said numbers the way other people said prayers — carefully, with weight, as though mispronouncing one might change its value. "That changes the math."

"It changes everything."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her do the thing she did — the pilot's arithmetic, fuel and distance and wind and margin of error, running behind her eyes like ticker tape. I envied it. My own calculations were never so clean. Mine involved people.

"Coral," I said. "Before we plan anything. There's something you need to know about what happens after."

"After what?"

"After we turn it in. If we turn it in." I picked up the slate, set it down again. My hands wanted occupation; I denied them. "I've been thinking about the sequence. Your testimony gets corrected, Rafe's conviction gets vacated pending review, Dez's confession goes into evidence, the manifest goes to the survey authority. That part's clean. That part I'd do in a heartbeat."

"But," she said.

"But the survey authority will want to know how we found the cave. And the answer is: a bush pilot with a suspended-adjacent license flew an unregistered archaeologist into a protected zone during quake season, based on information from a salvager's leaked document chain." I made myself look at her while I said it. "They'll pull your flight records, Coral. All of them. Every unsanctioned landing, every cargo run that skirted the customs codes, every favor you've flown for ten years. You told me yourself your license is held together with tape and goodwill. This ends it. Not suspends it. *Ends* it."

She didn't flinch. That was the thing about her that kept catching me off guard — everyone else I'd ever met flinched at consequences the way they flinched at cold water, full-body, involuntary. Coral just went very still, the way she did in turbulence, hands loose on controls that weren't there.

"And you?" she said. "What does it cost you?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for two days, hoping she wouldn't ask, knowing she would, because Coral Halloran missed nothing that happened inside thirty feet of her — it was why she was still alive, probably, and certainly why she was so tired.

"You remember I told you Mateo wrote to me before he died," I said. "Claiming the expedition logs were forged."

"I remember."

"I didn't tell you what I did with that letter." I reached into the inner pocket of my field jacket, where it had lived for eleven years, through four postings and two evacuations and one marriage that failed partly, I suspect, because my wife could feel the shape of it against my chest when I hugged her. A letter, soft as cloth now from handling. "I did nothing. I wrote back asking him for documentation. He sent it — photographs, page references, the financier's initials in the margins. And I sat on it, because the financier was on my doctoral committee, because he controlled the grant that paid for my lab, because I told myself I needed more corroboration before I could responsibly—"

"Stop," Coral said quietly.

I stopped.

"You're doing the thing," she said. "The academic thing. Where you bury the plain sentence under nine layers of hedging. Say the plain sentence."

I almost laughed. It came out as something else, something rougher. "The plain sentence is that Mateo Reyes-Villar handed me the truth eleven years ago and I filed it in a drawer because I was a coward, and if I had acted then, Rafe Halloran would have gone to prison for eight months instead of eight years, and Mateo might be alive, and you—" I gestured at her, at all of her, the restless competent architecture of her, "—might be someone who sleeps."

The rain chose that moment to arrive properly — not the squall's theatrical burst but the real monsoon overture, a long steady drumming on the cave mouth, the sea beyond it going the color of gunmetal. The barometer ticked down another notch. Somewhere out in the dark, the Sable Fortune's anchor lights were presumably swinging at their moorings, and Vikram Rao was presumably watching the same sky we were, doing his own arithmetic.

Coral took the letter from my hand. Unfolded it. Read it by headlamp, slowly, twice.

"This is his handwriting," she said at last. Her thumb hovered over the signature without touching it, the way you hover over a wound you're afraid of reopening. "He dotted his i's like little anchors. He thought it was funny. He was going to get a tattoo of—"

She stopped. Closed the letter along its original creases, precisely, like closing a casket.

"He asked you for help," she said, "and you gave him a drawer."

"Yes."

"And now you're telling me that turning this in — all of it, the whole rotten archive — burns you too."

"It exposes the suppression finding, yes. It won't end my career the way it ends yours. I'll lose funding, allies, probably the survey chair I've been promised. I'll spend five years being the woman who accused a patron. But I'll survive it. Academia eats its own and digests slowly; I'll live." I heard my own voice go dry and clinical and hated it, and made myself stop. "That's not the point, Coral."

"What is the point?"

"The point is that I've been calculating whether I can afford to do the right thing, and I realized tonight that I've been running that calculation for eleven years, and every version of it comes back the same: the right thing is the only entry in the ledger that doesn't depreciate." I looked at her. "You taught me that, actually. Or Mateo did. Through you."

She was quiet for a long time. The rain filled the silence the way water fills a hold — evenly, patiently, from the bottom up.

"There's a version of tomorrow," she finally said, "where we fly out at first light, straight to the port authority, and I never fly again. And there's a version where we take the manifest and the slate and Dez's confession and we disappear, and Rafe rots, and you keep your chair, and I keep my sky. And there's a version where Rao wins, and none of it matters anyway because he'll strip the site and melt the lead sheet down for ballast." She laughed, short and without humor. "Three futures. I've been flying between them all day like they're islands."

"Which one are you circling?"

"The first one." No hesitation. That was the thing I'd come to trust fastest about her: once Coral decided, the decision arrived complete, no scaffolding left standing. "But I keep noticing that I'm not the only one on this manifest, so to speak. So I'm asking. Not ordering. Asking." She turned to face me fully, headlamp to headlamp, and the light caught her face in a way that stripped out all the competence and left only the person underneath, the one who had been nineteen once, and in love, and afraid. "If we do this, I need to know you're choosing it. Not paying penance. Choosing."

And that — that was the moment. Not some thunderclap, not the storm arriving early to underline the scene. Just a woman asking another woman a direct question in a wet cave while the world's weather closed around them like a fist.

Because here is what I had been refusing to name for days, cataloguing it instead the way I catalogue everything, shelly fragments, context unknown: the way I watched her hands on the yoke. The way her laugh — rare, rusty, deployed like emergency equipment — rearranged something in my chest. The way I had caught myself, twice, memorizing her profile against the cockpit glass, and had told myself both times that it was scientific interest in a subject under stress.

Ilsa, I said to myself, in the tone I used on grad students who were lying in their data. Be honest for once in your career.

"I'm choosing it," I said. "Not the sacrifice. Not the redemption arc. You." I swallowed. "I'm choosing you, and the right thing happens to be attached, the way the dinghy is attached to the plane. I can't seem to want one without the other anymore."

Something moved through her face — I want to say relief, but it was bigger than relief, it was the expression of a woman setting down luggage she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," she said, "and it contained the word *ledger*."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them bureaucratic."

Her mouth twitched. Then, before either of us could retreat into wit, which was our shared native language and our most reliable hiding place, she crossed the space between us — two steps, maybe three, the floor gritty with coral sand — and kissed me.

It was not a movie kiss. It tasted of salt and old coffee and the metallic edge of fear that had been in the air for days. Her hands were shaking. So were mine. We were two women in our thirties and forties respectively, damp, exhausted, professionally doomed, and it was — I say this as someone whose professional objectivity is legendary — the single most honest physical event of my adult life. Nothing performed. Nothing hedged. The plain sentence, spoken in the only language left to us.

When we broke apart, the barometer ticked, and we both laughed — shaky, real laughter, the kind that lives next door to crying.

"Well," she said. "That complicates the crew dynamics."

"Immeasurably."

"We should probably establish rules. No kissing during instrument approaches. No saying 'copy that' in—"

"Coral."

"Yeah."

"Shut up and hold my hand for a minute. The storm's coming and I'd like to not be brave for sixty seconds."

So she did. We stood in the cave mouth with the rain coming down like the end of an era, her hand around mine, calluses against ink stains, and I felt the strange double weight of it: grief for Mateo, arriving on schedule, and something new and green growing straight up through the grief like coral through a hull. They weren't opposed. Nobody had ever told me that. Nobody tells you that love doesn't wait for a clear season; it grows in monsoons or it doesn't grow at all.

After a while she spoke, her chin resting on top of my head because I am shorter and she had discovered she liked it there.

"First light," she said. "We load the slate, the manifest, your letter, Dez's note. We fly to Puerto Sampaguita. Port authority, sworn statements, the whole ugly filing cabinet. Then whatever they do to me, they do."

"They'll impound the plane."

"I know."

"You'll be grounded. Possibly for years. Possibly forever, if they decide the customs violations were criminal rather than sloppy."

"I know, Ilsa." She squeezed my hand. "Ten years ago I would have run. Five years ago I would have negotiated. Tonight I just want to land somewhere and stay landed." A pause, and then, drier: "Also, if we're doing this, we're doing it before Rao does something stupid, and Rao does something stupid approximately every ninety minutes."

As if summoned by the mention — and I have since learned that Coral's universe operates on exactly this principle, dramatic irony as a physical law — the radio in her pack crackled to life.

Not the port authority band. Not weather. The marine VHF, channel 16, the hailing frequency, carrying across black water from a ship that had no business having our frequency.

A man's voice, accented, unhurried, terribly polite:

*"Unidentified aircraft at Karakel, this is salvage vessel Sable Fortune. We know you're listening. We have your friend Okafor aboard — he walked into our camp an hour ago, offering to trade. And Dr. Reyes? Captain Rao says to tell you the drawer is open."*

The rain drummed. The barometer fell. Coral's hand tightened around mine, and I felt the tenderness of a moment ago transmute, instantly and completely, into something with edges.

"Copy that, Sable Fortune," Coral said into the radio, and her voice was steady as a horizon. "Tell the captain we're coming."
