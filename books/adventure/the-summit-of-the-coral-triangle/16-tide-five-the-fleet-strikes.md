## 16. Tide Five: The Fleet Strikes

Vikram Rao had run salvage operations on four oceans, and he had learned long ago that the sea did not negotiate. It offered terms, and you either met them or it collected. The monsoon was now forty hours out by his best estimate — thirty-six if the barometer kept falling the way it had been falling since dawn — and the Sable Fortune still sat off Karakel with less than half the manifest recovered.

"Mr. Rao." Damar, his dive master, stood dripping in the wheelhouse doorway, and there was something in his face that Vikram didn't like. Not fear exactly. The men who worked for him were past fear; they were paid too well for it. It was more like the look of a man carrying news he'd rather set down somewhere else. "The cave's the problem. The entrance channel's too narrow to run gear through fast. We're losing two hours every tide just moving tanks and lights."

"I know the arithmetic, Damar."

"The crew knows it too. They're asking about the charges."

Vikram turned from the window. Below, the lagoon lay green and flat as bottle glass, deceptively calm, the kind of water that made men forget what was coming. On the far side, the black notch of the cave mouth showed above the tide line like a keyhole into the island's throat.

He should have said no. He would tell himself later, in the years after, that he had known — that some part of him standing at that wheelhouse window had already seen the rock come down, had already heard the sound Dez Okafor's world would make when it ended. But knowing a thing and refusing it are different animals, and Vikram Rao had built a career on not confusing them.

"Controlled charges," he said. "Low yield. Widen the mouth, nothing more. You put anyone near the overhang—"

"We know the protocol."

"Then follow it." He looked back at the lagoon. "And get me a head count before and after. I want names, not numbers."

Damar went out. Vikram stayed at the window a while longer, watching the water, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for three days. Six tides. That was what the buoys said, what the weather routers confirmed, what the falling glass repeated like a patient teacher. Five of those tides were gone. This one was running out beneath his keel even as he stood there, and somewhere behind him in the dark of the island, a woman named Coral Crane was holding documents that could end him — or save him, depending entirely on which way the next forty hours broke.

He thought about the deal he'd offered her at the cave mouth. Split the value, bury the forgery. She'd looked at him then the way people look at a menu when they've already decided they're not hungry. He'd respected it more than he'd admitted.

His radio crackled. "Charges are set, Mr. Rao. Clearing the area now."

Vikram picked up the handset. In another life — a life where he'd taken the survey contract instead of the private one, where he'd stayed honest at wages that wouldn't have fed his daughter's school fees — he might have been the man counting artifacts instead of stealing them. He pressed transmit. "Stand clear. Fire on my mark."

He marked. 

The blast came as a white plume off the cliff face, then the sound, rolling across the lagoon a full second late, deep enough to feel in the deck plates. Gulls lifted off the rocks in a shrieking sheet. The cave mouth gaped wider now, ragged-edged, exhaling a breath of disturbed water.

For eleven seconds, everything held.

Then the island answered.

It came as a groan first — a structural complaint from deep inside the rock, the sound a house makes settling, except houses don't settle sideways. The plume of spray hadn't finished falling when the eastern lip of the new entrance sheared along an old fault line nobody had sounded, and a chamber roof somewhere inside let go all at once, a collapse that sent a column of dust and dead-calm water surging out of the mouth like the island clearing its lungs.

On the Fortune's deck, men stopped moving. Even the wind seemed to pause to listen.

"Damar," Vikram said into the radio. "Head count."

Static. Then: "Counting now." A pause that stretched. "We had three inside the second chamber, Mr. Rao. Two are coming out. They're saying—" Another pause. "They're saying there was someone else in there. Not ours. A man. They saw him before the roof came down. He was deeper in, near the old gallery. He may have been living in there, Mr. Rao. There's a camp."

Vikram closed his eyes. *A camp.* Of course. The ghost they'd been chasing for days — the slashed tires, the missing tanks, the notes in careful handwriting. Dez Okafor, alive, hiding in the one place on earth he thought no one would ever blast open.

"Get our two out and stabilized," Vikram said. "Nobody goes back in until we know the ceiling's done moving."

"And the trapped man?"

Vikram looked at the cave mouth, at the water still shivering around the rubble fan spreading from its base. The tide was turning. In ninety minutes it would be flooding hard, and the chamber beyond the collapse would be filling with water that had nowhere to go but up — which meant the air pocket, if there was one, would be shrinking by the minute.

"He'll drown," Vikram said quietly, "unless someone swims faster than the sea."

* * *

Coral heard the blast from the airstrip, felt it in her teeth before her ears caught up.

She was elbow-deep in the Cessna's cowling, chasing a fuel line that had developed opinions about the humidity, when the ground shuddered and a boom rolled across the island like a door slammed by a giant. Her hands went still. Every animal instinct she owned stood up at once and pointed toward the water.

"No," she said aloud, to no one. "No, no, no."

She ran. Past the hangar, down the coral track, boots skidding on wet limestone, Ilsa shouting something behind her that she didn't stop to hear. By the time she reached the overlook above the lagoon, she could see it all laid out below: the Sable Fortune at anchor, the workboats pulled back from the cliff, the ragged new wound in the rock face, and the water around the cave mouth still trembling with aftershock ripples.

"They collapsed it," Ilsa said, arriving beside her, breathing hard. "God, they actually—" She stopped. Squinted. "Coral. Look at the boats. Nobody's celebrating. Nobody's *moving*."

Because celebration required a reason, and the fleet had none. Coral watched the divers being helped aboard, watched the way the crew clustered and gestured, and read it the way she read weather — fast, instinctive, without wanting to be right.

"Somebody's trapped," she said.

"You can't know that."

"I can know that." Her heart was going hard, not from the run. From the shape of the thing. Because there was only one person on Karakel Island who wasn't on that boat, wasn't on her airstrip, wasn't anywhere — and he'd been leaving notes in the dark like a man writing letters to a grave. "Dez."

Ilsa grabbed her arm. "You don't know he was inside."

"I know he lived in there, Ilsa. I know he's been hiding in that cave for ten years like a hermit crab, and I know Vikram Rao just dropped a mountain on top of him." She was already pulling away, already moving down the trail toward the waterline where the skiff was tied. "And I know the tide's turning, which means whatever air pocket is left is getting smaller while we stand here having this conversation."

"Coral—"

"What?" She spun on her. "Say it. Say whatever you're about to say, because I promise you I've already said it to myself. *It's not your problem. He forged the logs. He's why Mateo's dead, maybe. Let the sea do what the sea does.*" Her voice cracked on the last of it, and she hated that, hated the honesty of it. "I've said all of it. And none of it matters, because he's under that rock drowning in the dark, and I'm the only person on this island who can dive a flooded collapse and live."

Ilsa stared at her. Then, quietly: "What do you need?"

"Boat. My spare tank from the plane — the ones they didn't steal. Lights. And twenty minutes of your life praying to whatever god handles idiots."

* * *

The cave mouth looked different widened. Where it had been a keyhole it was now a wound, and the water at its threshold moved wrong — sluggish, confused, sucking gently inward as the flood tide began its work. Coral kitted up in the skiff with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect. Ten years since her last cave dive. Ten years since the last time she'd breathed compressed air in an overhead environment, and the last time had killed Mateo.

Funny how the body remembers. Funnier how it doesn't care.

"Rule of thirds," Ilsa said, clipping her light to Coral's harness. Her voice was level, but her knuckles weren't. "One third in, one third out, one third reserve. If you so much as *brush* against that number, you turn around. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Liar."

"Probably." Coral put the regulator in her mouth, then took it out. "If I'm not out in fifty minutes—"

"I know."

"—don't come in after me. Get the manifest, get off the island, get Rafe's file to the authorities. That's the mission. I'm just the errand."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. For a moment Coral thought she'd argue, and some traitorous part of her wanted her to — wanted someone to tell her not to go, so she'd have something to push against. Instead Ilsa leaned in and kissed her, quick and fierce, salt-stung, and said against her mouth: "Fifty minutes. Then I break my promise and come in after you, and you can be furious about it underwater."

Coral laughed — a short, cracked sound — and fell backward off the gunwale into the green.

* * *

The flooded passage swallowed her light whole.

She'd forgotten — or spent ten years pretending she'd forgotten — what cave diving was. Not open water, where the world is wide and forgiving and always offers a horizon. This was the opposite of horizon. This was a corridor of rock with a ceiling of stone and a floor of stone and her own exhalations boiling silver above her head, the only proof she existed. Her light carved a cone out of the black and the black took everything else.

The collapse zone announced itself as a field of rubble, slabs the size of truck doors stacked at drunken angles, silt hanging in the water like smoke after a battle. She threaded it slow, checking her gauge more often than she needed to, telling herself the checking was discipline and not terror wearing discipline's clothes.

The air pocket found her before she found it.

Her light touched a ceiling that shimmered — that impossible, beautiful shimmer — and she rose into it, spitting out her reg, gasping air that tasted of stone and stagnation and, faintly, of a human being who'd been breathing it too long.

"Hello the pocket," she called, voice echoing small in the dark.

Silence. Then, from somewhere ahead, weak and disbelieving: "...Crane?"

"Who else comes swimming into a collapsing cave for a man who stole her tires?"

A laugh — wet, ragged, half sob. She followed the sound, finning low, and her light found him: Dez Okafor, ten years grayer than his file photo, wedged in a gap between two fallen slabs, one leg pinned to the knee, a dive slate floating beside him with half a sentence written on it and abandoned. His lips were gray-blue. His eyes were the worst part — not afraid, just stunned, the eyes of a man who had already written his own ending and was being interrupted mid-sentence.

"You shouldn't be here," he rasped.

"Funny. I say that to myself every morning." She swam close, got her shoulder under the slab's edge, and pushed. Nothing. Pushed again, teeth gritted, air screaming through her demand valve. The rock did not care. "Talk to me. How bad's the leg?"

"Pinned at the knee. Crushed, probably. I can't feel my foot." He coughed. "Crane. Listen. The logs. I forged them. I wrote it in the note, but you have to hear it from my mouth, because if I die in here—"

"You're not dying in here."

"—the financier, Aldous Wren, he pressured me, he *paid* me, and Mateo found out, and I swear to God I never thought—" His voice broke apart. "I never thought the dive would happen that day. I never thought anyone would die."

Coral stopped pushing. In the beam of her light, in a shrinking pocket of bad air, with the tide rising somewhere behind her, Dez Okafor finally said the words she'd flown ten years to outrun.

"I know," she said. Her voice came out strange and steady. "I know, Dez. I testified against his brother on a lie I told to protect myself. We're both liars in a hole. Difference is, mine's about to fill with water, so hold this light and push when I push."

She repositioned. Found the slab's balance point — not lifting it, levering it, using the rubble beneath as a fulcrum the way she'd use a pry bar on a stuck cowling. Physics. Fuel lines and stuck bolts and ten thousand roadside repairs. Everything she knew about freeing things that didn't want to move.

"On three. One. Two—"

The slab shifted an inch. Dez screamed through his teeth and shoved, and the gap widened enough that his leg slid free with a sound she'd spend years hearing in her sleep. Then they were moving, him on her back, his good arm locked around her neck, her fins churning through water that was visibly higher than it had been five minutes ago.

The return through the collapse zone was the longest swim of her life. Rule of thirds, abandoned. Reserve, burning. At the rubble field her gauge needle crossed the line Ilsa had made her promise on, and she crossed it going the wrong direction, and she kept going, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant freezing and freezing meant two corpses instead of one.

The shimmer of the exit appeared like mercy. She rose into open water, into light, into Ilsa's arms on the skiff, and the last thing she registered before the world went soft at the edges was the sky to the northwest — bruised purple, banded, organized.

The monsoon, arriving early.

Behind them, from the deck of the Sable Fortune, came the sound of a ship's bell ringing fast — the alarm for dragging anchor.
