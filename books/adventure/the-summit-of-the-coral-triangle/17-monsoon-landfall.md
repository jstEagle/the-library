## 17. Monsoon Landfall

### Vikram

The barometer had been lying to us all day, and I'd known it the way you know a crew member is about to quit — not from any one sign, but from the accumulation of small dishonesties. The wind had backed two points in an hour. The swell had come around to the northeast when every chart said it shouldn't. And the birds had gone. That was the thing that settled it for me, actually. Ten thousand birds don't agree on anything except weather.

I stood on the bridge of the *Sable Fortune* and watched Karakel Island turn the color of a bruise and thought: we are forty hours early and six hours late.

"Captain." Osei, my dive master, at the door of the wheelhouse, dripping, holding his radio like it had personally insulted him. "Buoy update. It's not a Category Two anymore. They've bumped it. Landfall's been moved up — they're saying tonight."

Tonight. The word sat in the cabin like a third man.

"Recall the divers," I said.

"We've got two teams down in the second chamber. Halloran's woman is still inside with them — the archaeologist."

Of course she was. Dr. Reyes had spent three days treating my operation like a museum tour she hadn't paid for, photographing everything, cataloguing everything, standing between my men and the good pieces with the serene immovability of a customs officer. I could have had her removed. I had considered it. I had also, in the small hours, considered that everything she was doing was more correct than anything I was doing, and I had hated her a little for it, the way you hate a mirror.

"Then recall everyone," I said. "And get the anchor watch doubled. If that bottom starts to move—"

The bottom moved.

I felt it before I heard it — a shudder through the hull, deep and wrong, the ship's whole skeleton flinching. Then the sound arrived: a long rolling groan out of the island, out of the reef shelf itself, as if Karakel had decided, after ten thousand years of holding its mouth shut, to say something.

The quake tide. Again. The same tectonic temper that had opened the cave in the first place, closing its fist around it.

On the water surface off our port beam, the sea began to behave like something draining out of a bathtub. The reef shelf that had sheltered our anchorage all week started showing coral heads we'd been floating over without a thought, brown and sharp as teeth.

"Anchor's dragging," the helmsman said, very calmly, which told me he was terrified. Good. Calm helmsmen in weather like this are the ones who haven't understood the problem yet.

"Engine room — full ahead. Osei, get those divers up *now*."

He was already running.

Here is what nobody tells you about salvage work: half of it is theft with paperwork, and the other half is waiting for the sea to decide whether today is the day it collects on you. I had spent twenty years in the first half telling myself the second half would never collect. Standing there watching my anchor chain saw backward across coral while the sky went the color of wet slate, I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Manila. The manifest pages in my safe were worth perhaps two million dollars. My ship was worth four. My crew — eleven men, most of whom I'd poached, lied to, or underpaid into loyalty — were worth considerably more than either, though no ledger anywhere recorded that.

The storm made the decision beautifully simple. You cannot spend money at the bottom of the Coral Triangle.

### Ilsa

The cave tried to keep us.

That's the only honest way to put it. We were in the third chamber — Mateo's chamber, I was already thinking of it that way, because his slate had been found there and because grief assigns addresses — when the water began to rise under us. Not gradually. Not the polite tidal breathing we'd mapped over five days. This was a fist closing. Dez had gone back in for the last of the documentation an hour before, against everyone's advice including his own common sense, and when the collapse came it took the passage behind him with it.

We heard it through thirty meters of rock: a boom, then silence, then the particular silence of a lot of water moving where water has never moved before.

Coral got to the collapsed passage first. Of course she did. I have watched this woman walk away from every invitation, every kindness, every fixed address for a decade — Rafe told me that much in the letters I wasn't supposed to have — and I have now watched her run toward a rockfall without a single heartbeat of hesitation. There is a version of courage that looks like recklessness from the outside, and then there is whatever Coral Crane has, which looks like recklessness from the inside too, and works anyway.

"Dez!" Her voice bounced off the wet limestone. "DEZ!"

A cough. Then, muffled, absurdly apologetic: "Still here. Mostly. There's a gap — air pocket — I can see light through a crack but I can't—"

"Don't talk. Breathe. We're coming."

We were not coming, not through there. The passage was sealed with rubble and rising water. But Coral was already doing the map in her head — I watched her do it, eyes half-closed, hands sketching on her thigh, the bush pilot's trick of holding terrain in the body instead of on paper. "There's a chimney," she said. "Second chamber, the one with the blowhole. It surfaces above the high-tide line. If the quake didn't plug it—"

"If the quake didn't plug it," I said, "it's also how the storm surge comes *in*."

"Then we go fast or we go never. Pick one."

I picked fast. I am a marine archaeologist; I have spent my life choosing slow, choosing careful, choosing the trench over the treasure. But I had spent the past week reading a dead man's handwriting on a slate underwater, and slow had stopped being available to me somewhere around Tide Four.

We went up the chimney with the surge already hissing below us like something impatient. Coral went first and hauled me through the top by the wrist, and we came out onto Karakel's shoulder into a world that had ended while we were underground.

The sky was black-green. The palms were bent flat and staying there, committed. Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the sea — the sea had left. It had withdrawn off the reef shelf like a held breath, exposing a hundred meters of dead coral and stranded fish, and every instinct I owned screamed that this was the tsunami drawdown you read about in disaster reports, the ocean inhaling before it exhales.

"The boat," Coral shouted over the wind. "Vikram's people — the divers —"

I looked at her. She was soaked to the skin, bleeding from one forearm where the limestone had taken its toll, and she was looking at the *Sable Fortune*'s anchorage with an expression I can only describe as furious compassion. These were the people who had sabotaged her plane, stolen her tanks, cornered her at the cave mouth with an offer designed to buy her silence. And she was counting heads.

"How many were down?" she asked.

"Two dive teams. Six, plus Osei."

"And the ship?"

I squinted through the rain. The *Sable Fortune* was underway — barely — engines at what sounded like full emergency power, dragging her anchor in a wide, ungraceful arc across the exposed reef shelf, her bow swinging toward the coral heads with the dreamlike inevitability of a car sliding on ice.

"She's going to ground," I said.

"No," Coral said. "She's going to ground *on purpose*. Look — he's steering for the sand spit behind the island. He's beaching her."

Even now, part of me filed it away: Rao knew his ship was lost and chose the softest wreck available. Whatever else the man was, he was not careless with other people's lives. It was the one thing about him that had kept me from truly fearing him, and in the next hour it would be the thing that saved eight of us.

The exhale came.

We felt it as a sound first — a roar building out of the northeast like a freight train with no tracks — and then the sea came back over the reef shelf in a brown wall, and we ran for high ground, and behind us the *Sable Fortune* rode the surge in onto the sand spit with a grinding shriek that I will hear for the rest of my life, and lay over fifteen degrees, and did not move again.

### Coral

Here's the thing about a beached ship in a monsoon: it doesn't sink, so nobody drowns — until the storm surge comes back for a second act, or the hull opens, or the fuel tanks let go. A grounded ship is a clock. Everything aboard is fine right up until it very suddenly isn't.

I counted survivors on the sand spit through the rain-glasses: eight figures hauling a ninth on a improvised stretcher, one diver limping, somebody waving both arms over their head in the universal language of *please*. The injured man was one of Vikram's divers — caught in the second chamber when the collapse came, leg broken by falling rock, hauled out through the blowhole by his teammates minutes after Ilsa and I came up it. Small mercies: the sea had nearly killed all of us in the same doorway.

My plane was on the strip on the island's lee side, tires slashed and repaired with Dez's patch kit, fueled for exactly one evacuation load, because I had planned for one evacuation load because I had planned, as always, to leave. The universe has a sense of humor and it is not subtle.

Vikram Rao waded ashore to meet me, which took a certain kind of man. His ship was dying fifty meters behind him and he walked up out of the surf holding his radio above the water like a waiter carrying soup, and when he reached me he didn't negotiate, didn't charm, didn't do any of the things I'd braced for.

"Halloran. How many can you carry?"

"Four, plus pilot, in this weather. Maybe three if the crosswind's bad, and the crosswind will be bad."

"I have nine who need out and one who needs a hospital tonight or he loses the leg." He said it flatly, no performance, and I realized with a strange lurch that this was probably the first honest conversation we'd ever had. "Your aircraft is the only way off this island. My radios are drowned, my tender is matchwood, and the nearest help is a fishing village that thinks my ship is a ghost story."

"You'll stay with your ship."

"I'll stay with my ship." No hesitation. "But my crew goes. All of them. Starting with Osei and the injured man."

"And the manifest pages in your safe?" Ilsa asked, appearing at my elbow with rain streaming off her jaw and murder in her voice, and God, I loved her, I loved her in that exact moment with a clarity that would have embarrassed us both in calmer weather.

Rao looked at her for a long moment. Wind flattened the rain against all three of us.

"In my safe," he said, "along with my ship, my career, and quite possibly my liberty, depending on what your authorities decide I am. Which means they're going nowhere, and neither am I." He turned back to me. "Fly my people out, Ms. Crane. That's the debt. I know what I owe you and I know what I owe them, and the second one is due first. Go."

I went. Because of course I went. The woman who runs runs toward — Ilsa said that to me once, teasing, and it had stung, and it was true, and standing on that airstrip with the hangar doors banging and the rain coming down like the sky had a grudge, I finally understood that running toward and running away had always been the same motion for me. I just needed better destinations.

Three loads. That was the arithmetic: nine souls, four seats, three trips, and a window between storm cells that the barometer promised and the sky had not yet agreed to. I flew the first load out through rain so hard the wipers were decorative, dropped them at the mission strip on Panu, and came back with the engine singing a note I didn't love. Second load. The injured diver strapped across the rear bench, Osei holding his hand and reciting dive tables under his breath like a rosary. Back again.

On the final approach for load three, lightning showed me the *Sable Fortune* on the sand spit, heeled over like a beached whale, and a single figure standing on her tilted deck with a lantern, keeping watch over a ship that was already gone. Keeping watch over the safe.

I set the plane down on a runway that was half river by then, and Ilsa and I loaded the last four, and as I ran up the engine I looked back once at the island — at the black shape of the hill where a cave was filling with the sea forever, taking Mateo's slate and Dez's confession-note and ten years of my lies down into the dark where they'd started.

"Last call," I said, mostly to myself.

"Coral." Ilsa's hand on my shoulder, shouting over the engine. "The tide charts. The final window closes at dawn. After that nothing lands on this coast for a week."

I looked at the lantern on the wrecked ship. I looked at the flooded dark of the cave mouth, barely visible even to me, who knew exactly where to look.

One trip left in the airplane. One window left in the tide. And two things on this island that couldn't both come with me.

I pushed the throttle forward and lifted off into the rain, and started doing the arithmetic that was going to define the rest of my life.
