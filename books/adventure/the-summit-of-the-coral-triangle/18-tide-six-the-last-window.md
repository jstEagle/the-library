## 18. Tide Six: The Last Window

The barometer had been falling all night, and by dawn it read like a confession: twenty-nine inches and dropping, the needle trembling the way a compass needle trembles near iron. I'd learned to read weather the way other people read faces, and this face was saying something ugly.

Between storm cells there was a window. Not a big one — maybe ninety minutes of gray, gusty, flyable air before the next squall line rolled off the sea like a dropped ceiling. Ninety minutes. Enough to make one run to Karakel and back with a load of injured divers, if the wind behaved, which wind never does.

Ilsa stood at my elbow on the beach strip, holding the door frame of the Cub while I ran the preflight with rain hammering the fabric wings. Her hair was plastered to her skull and she didn't seem to notice or care.

"You don't have to keep going," she said. "The navy cutter's coming. They said—"

"The navy cutter is four hours out and those men are bleeding now." I said it flatly, because anything else would have cracked. "Two of them have decompression hits. Every hour counts."

"And after this load?"

"After this load I go back for the manifest." I checked the fuel strainer, wiped it, checked it again. My hands were steady. That surprised me. "And Mateo's slate."

Ilsa went quiet in that way she had — not silence as absence but silence as weight. When she finally spoke, her voice was low. "The tide chart says the cave seals for good on tide six. After tonight, Karakel keeps its dead."

"Then I'd better be quick."

"Coral." She grabbed my sleeve. Rain ran off her fingers onto my wrist. "If you're on that beach when the cell comes through—"

"I won't be."

"You can't promise that."

No. I couldn't. We both knew it. Ten years ago I'd made a different kind of promise, standing over a body on a dock with a police officer's notebook open, and I'd broken it before the ink dried. I wasn't going to stand in the rain and swear another oath I might have to eat.

So I just looked at her, and she looked at me, and whatever passed between us didn't need words. It was the same thing that had been passing between us since the night we admitted what was growing — tenderness with the storm already audible, love chosen with full knowledge of the invoice.

She kissed me once, hard, salt and rain, and then shoved me toward the cockpit like she was angry about it. "Go," she said. "Fly ugly, land soft."

* * *

The first three runs blurred together into a single act of repetition, which is what flying in weather really is: doing the same correct things over and over until the sky gets bored and lets you live.

Load one: two divers from the Sable Fortune, both bent, both moaning in the way men moan when they're scared and trying not to show it. One of them clutched my headrest the whole flight and told me his name was Ferro and that he had a daughter in Surabaya and that Captain Rao had ordered them into a cave that any sane man could see was a coffin. I flew him to the clinic strip at Selatan Point and handed him to the nurses and did not tell him he'd be telling that story to an inquiry board someday. He had enough problems.

Load two: Dez Okafor.

They'd pulled him out of the collapsed chamber an hour before dawn — Coral Crane, who runs, diving into flooding black water on reflex, hauling a man who'd spent ten years hiding on an island because of a lie he helped write. He was wrapped in a thermal blanket on my rear bench, gray-faced, one arm splinted, and when he recognized me he flinched like I'd hit him.

"Coral," he said. "I need to say—"

"Not now." I banked around a rain shaft, watching the horizon where the next cell was building itself out of steam and spite. "You'll say it to the authorities, into a recorder, with your name attached. That's the only way it counts. That's the only way it ever counted."

He was quiet a long time. Then: "Mateo trusted you. Did you know that? Even at the end. His last letter to Ilsa — he asked if you were okay."

That one went in under the ribs and stayed there. I flew the rest of that leg with my jaw set so tight my teeth ached, and when I landed I sat on the edge of the cargo pod for thirty seconds and let myself shake, because shaking on the ground is free and shaking in the air costs altitude.

Load three was Vikram Rao himself and his last two crewmen, hauled off the reef where the Sable Fortune was grinding her hull into coral she had no business touching. Vikram climbed into my plane soaked, bleeding from a gash above his ear, and looked around the cabin with the expression of a man taking inventory of everything he'd lost.

"You know," he said as we lifted off, "in another life, you and I would have been competitors. Good ones."

"In this life you blasted open a protected site and got your ship wrecked."

"In this life," he agreed, "I did." He was silent for a while, watching the sea scroll past below us, the whitecaps already standing up in ranks. Then, quietly: "The pages. The ones I took from the cave. They're in the map case behind your seat. I put them there before we loaded Ferro."

I glanced back. There it was — the oilskin packet, the stolen manifest pages, sitting in my plane like they'd always belonged there.

"Why?"

"Because a man should leave a wreck with clean hands if he can manage it." He almost smiled. "And because I've read them now, all of them, and I know what they mean for your brother-in-law. Rafe, isn't it? My diver mentioned him. Small industry, this triangle." He closed his eyes against the turbulence. "Take them in yourself. It'll carry more weight than if they surfaced from my salvage hold."

I wanted to hate him. It would have been tidier. But the truth was that somewhere between the mutiny and the reef, Vikram Rao had done the arithmetic most men never do — what a thing costs versus what it's worth — and he'd paid up without being asked twice.

I set him down at Selatan Point, watched the medics take him, and turned the Cub back east while the sky behind me went the color of a bruise.

* * *

One more window. The radio was screaming advisories — monsoon landfall moved up, landfall moved up, all vessels seek shelter — and the tide tables on my knee board said what they'd said for two days: tide six, slack water at 16:40, and after that the cave mouth goes under nine meters of surge and stays there until the sea decides otherwise, which will be never in our lifetimes.

I came in low over Karakel at 15:50 with the wind quartering across the beach at thirty knots and gusting forty-five. Landing on that strip was like setting a teacup down on a galloping horse. I did it anyway, because the runway was the beach, and the beach was going away, and so was everything else.

Ilsa was waiting at the tree line with two dry bags and a face like a held breath.

"You're insane," she said, helping me tie down the tail.

"Consistently. It's one of my virtues." I took the bags. "How long to pack the chamber?"

"Twenty minutes if we take only what matters. Manifest sheet, slate, Dez's note, the carved coral fragments, my field notes." She'd already organized it; of course she had. Ilsa Reyes organizes grief into labeled boxes. "The rest stays. The site documents itself better drowned than looted."

We worked fast and mostly silently, two people who'd learned each other's rhythms in five days of shared danger. She wrapped the lead manifest sheet in foam and canvas. I carried Mateo's slate myself, wrapped in my own shirt, because some things you don't delegate.

His handwriting was still on it, faint under the mineral bloom. *Dive log corrected. Originals forged. Tell Ilsa.* Dated eleven days after the dive that officially killed him — no, wait. Dated eleven days after the dive that officially killed *Rafe's* future, the dive where I lied, the dive where Mateo somehow survived long enough to know the truth and die anyway, alone, with nobody believing him.

I'd had five days to get used to that fact and it still knocked the wind out of me every time I picked the slate up.

At 16:20 we heard the sea change.

It's a sound you don't forget once you've heard it — a long withdrawal, a sucking exhale, the water pulling back off the reef flat like a lung emptying. Ilsa grabbed my arm. "That's not slack water," she said. "That's drawdown. The surge is loading."

The earthquake tide had opened the cave; the monsoon tide was going to close it with prejudice. We had minutes, not the tidy half hour the charts promised. Charts assume polite weather. This was not polite weather.

"We go now," Ilsa said. "Both of us, or neither."

"It's my—"

"Don't you dare say 'my responsibility.' You said that about flying loads out and I let you have it because you were right. This time we're both going, because I'm the one who can read the cipher, and if the sheet shifts in the surge and we lose a page, everything we've bled for washes into the dark." She was already wading toward the cave mouth, water thigh-high and rising. "Move, Crane."

* * *

Inside, the cave was already talking. Water sloshed in the lower chambers with a rhythm too fast for tide — surge pulse, arriving early, the ocean clearing its throat. Our headlamps swung across walls I'd memorized over five days: the carved coral, the rotted chest, the niche where Mateo's slate had waited ten years for someone to read it.

Ilsa packed the final artifacts with hands that shook only slightly, which for her was heroic composure. I stood at the inner chamber's edge and let myself look at it one last time.

Ten years ago I'd testified that the compass went down with Mateo. That it was lost. That Rafe's negligence killed him. I'd said it in a room with bad lighting and worse coffee, and I'd believed, in the way you believe things you need to believe, that I was protecting everyone including myself.

The compass was here. It had been here the whole time, recovered years earlier, logged on a manifest stamped with the expedition mark, sealed in a cave that no honest chart ever showed. Which meant the logs were forged, which meant Mateo knew, which meant my testimony — sworn, sincere, and wrong — had helped bury an innocent man for a decade.

"Grief resurfaces like a body buried too shallow" — I'd written that in a notebook once, back when I thought I was writing fiction. Turns out I was writing memoir.

"Coral." Ilsa's voice echoed off wet stone. "Water's at the ledge. We have maybe four minutes to the mouth."

I took one last look at the dark water pooling in the lowest chamber, where the rest of the galleon's cargo slept on, unlooted, undocumented, safe in the way only drowned things are safe. Let the archaeologists come someday with robots and patience. Let the site give up its secrets slowly, honestly, to people who hadn't lied their way into it.

We waded out against the push, the surge grabbing at our knees like hands, and came out into light that was failing fast — not sunset light but storm light, green-black, the color the sky turns when it means business.

The beach was half gone. Waves were running up to the tree line where the Cub sat tied down, rocking on her gear like a nervous animal.

* * *

Getting her into the air off that beach in that wind was the worst takeoff of my life, and I include the one I made out of a river gravel bar in flood season with a crocodile sunning itself at mid-field.

Wind ninety degrees across the strip, gusting to fifty, rain so horizontal it looked like the sea trying to climb aboard. Ilsa in the right seat — no argument, no discussion; if the plane went in, we went in together, and she'd made that clear by simply climbing in and buckling before I could object.

I held her on the brakes until the gusts eased in a lull — there's always a lull, the sky inhales before it coughs — and then I pushed the throttle forward and we went, bouncing, wallowing, the left wing lifting in a gust that nearly put us over, wheels leaving the sand in a shower of spray with maybe forty feet of beach left under us and black water beyond.

Then we were up. Climbing out over the reef where the Sable Fortune lay broken and groaning, her back visibly bent now, waves breaking clean over her midsection. I tipped a wing to her, briefly, the pilot's salute. Whatever else she'd been, she'd carried men home from my beach today.

Below us, the cave mouth vanished. I felt it rather than saw it — a shift in the water's color over the point, the pale smear of the entrance disappearing under the first real surge lines. Tide six, sealing. Karakel keeping its dead, keeping its treasure, keeping the version of history that belonged to the sea.

In my lap, in a canvas bag between the seats, rode the manifest sheet, Mateo's slate, Dez's confession note, and Vikram Rao's returned pages. Everything. The whole rotten, redeemed truth of the last decade, weighing maybe eight pounds.

Eight pounds. Ten years. One innocent man in a prison cell who'd begged me not to dig because some truths cost more than the lie did.

He'd been wrong, Rafe. Or he'd been right, and the cost was simply one I'd finally decided to pay. Both things could be true. I was learning that the hard way, at two hundred feet, in a monsoon, with the woman I loved reading the fuel gauge and the storm cell painted across the windshield ahead of us like a wall built by God specifically to inconvenience me.

"There," Ilsa said, pointing. "Cell's moving faster than forecast. We can't go around south — that adds forty minutes. North adds thirty."

"So we go through."

"Through the gap?" She leaned forward, studying the ragged seam between the two storm masses, a corridor of dirty gray maybe three miles wide. "Coral, that gap is closing."

"Everything closes." I lined up on it. "Tides close. Caves close. Windows close. The trick is to be through before they do."

She laughed — actually laughed, high and slightly hysterical, the laugh of a woman who has spent five days underwater and in gunfire-adjacent company and has decided that terror and joy are the same sensation wearing different clothes. "You're going to get us killed."

"Not today." I fed in power. "Today I'm landing twice in the same place, remember? Can't do that from the bottom of the sea."

The gap swallowed us. Rain hit the windshield like thrown gravel, then slacked, then hammered again, and the turbulence was the kind that makes the airplane feel less like a machine than like a leaf arguing with a river. Ilsa read the instruments aloud in a voice gone calm and clinical — airspeed, heading, the flickering needle — and I flew by feel and faith and the memory of ten thousand hours of weather, and for eleven minutes the world was nothing but noise and water and the two of us breathing in the small warm dark of a fabric-winged box defying the entire Pacific.

Then we broke out the far side into a light so sudden and golden it felt like forgiveness.

The sea below was steaming. A rainbow stood on the horizon, absurdly complete, arch to arch. Somewhere ahead was Selatan Point, the clinic, the port authority office where tomorrow I would sit in a humid room and say true things into a recorder with my hands shaking and my voice steady.

I reached over without looking and found Ilsa's hand. She gripped back hard enough to hurt.

"Tide six," she said softly. "Sealed."

"Sealed," I agreed. "But not silenced."

Behind us, Karakel disappeared into the storm's skirts, keeping its drowned galleon and its flooded chambers and its ten-year-old secret, which was no longer a secret at all — because it was riding east in a battered bush plane, in a shirt that smelled of salt and fear, in the handwriting of a dead man who had waited a decade for someone to fly it home.

The radio crackled. Selatan Point tower, such as it was — a shack and a windsock — asking my intentions.

I keyed the mic. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"Selatan, Cub Seven-Three-Sierra, one soul plus one, request straight-in. And tell the port authority to have paper ready." I glanced at Ilsa, at the bag in her lap, at the rainbow standing over the water like a signature on a document. "Tell them I've got testimony coming in — and a name they'll want to clear."

Ahead, the runway appeared out of the gold, wet and short and exactly where I'd left it.

For the first time in ten years, I was landing twice in the same place.

On purpose.
