## 19. The Wreck of the Sable Fortune

There is a particular sound a ship makes when she decides she is done with you.

It is not the alarm. It is not the grinding of steel on coral, though that comes too — a long, obscene shriek like a dentist's drill applied to a cathedral. No, it's what comes after: a settling, a shudder running keel to mast, the whole vessel exhaling through her rivets as if to say, *Well, captain, this was your idea.*

I heard it at 0340 on the morning of landfall, and I will hear it for the rest of my life.

* * *

Let me be honest about how we got here, because honesty has been in short supply aboard the Sable Fortune since I signed her papers. The barometer had been falling all evening — not falling so much as collapsing, the needle sliding down its arc like a man giving up on a rope. My weather man, a Filipino named Bautista who reads satellite imagery the way monks read scripture, told me at 2200 that the monsoon had jogged twelve degrees south of every forecast track.

"Twelve degrees," he said, "is the difference between weather and God."

I told him to log it and keep the pumps manned. I did not tell him to weigh anchor. That decision was mine, and I made it because of arithmetic: three more dives would clear the forward hold of everything worth carrying, and the manifest pages in my cabin safe were worth more than the ship, more than my commission, possibly more than my life once certain parties in Singapore learned I'd read them. Three dives. Six hours. What could a storm do in six hours?

The answer, as it turns out, is anything it wants.

* * *

The anchor dragged at 0300. I felt it in my feet before the watchman called it — that faint tremor through the deck plates, the Sable Fortune walking herself backward across the reef shelf like a sleepwalker. By 0315 we had dragged four hundred meters and the depth under the keel had gone from eighteen fathoms to nine. By 0330 I was on the bridge in a rain jacket over my pajamas, which is the uniform of every captain who ever lied to himself about a storm.

"Port engine," I said. "Give me steerage."

Chief Engineer Osei came up the ladder already shaking his head. Water in the fuel line. Not much — enough. In nine fathoms with a lee shore of coral and an island whose name I still can't pronounce properly, "not much" is a synonym for "all of it."

At 0340, the Sable Fortune found the reef she had come to plunder, and she took it personally.

The impact threw me against the chart table. Somewhere below, a tank let go; somewhere above, the radar mast went over the side with a sound like a whale dying. And then the settling. That terrible, dignified settling, as if the old girl had finally decided to lie down in the one grave she'd been circling for a week.

"All hands to muster stations," I said into the PA, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "This is not a drill. This is not a salvage. This is a wreck, and we are its cargo."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about command: it is mostly logistics performed while terrified. We got twenty-two souls off the ship and onto Karakel's beach by dawn, using the fast boat in relays through seas that stood up like walls and lay down like liars. Two men went into the water during the third run. One of them, Aris — my best diver, the one who'd spotted Coral Crane's plane on the reef flat days ago — didn't come back up where he was supposed to. He washed ashore an hour later half-drowned, his left arm bent wrong above the elbow, and spent the morning going quietly, terribly gray on a tarp under an overturned dinghy.

By noon the Sable Fortune was down by the bow, her stern a black iron island fifty meters off the reef crest, and the sea between us and her was full of our own cargo — crates of artifacts bobbing like drowned toys, centuries of somebody else's history scattered across a lagoon by my greed. I stood on the beach and watched it and felt something in my chest I hadn't felt since my father's funeral: the specific weight of having done a thing that cannot be undone.

Bautista found me there. "Landfall in six hours," he said. "Maybe less. The eye is coming here, Captain. Directly here."

"And the plane?"

"Gone since before dawn. Crane flew out two loads of Dez Okafor's people yesterday." He hesitated. "She'll come back. She always comes back. That woman has made a religion of returning."

* * *

I want to explain something about Coral Crane, because I have watched her now for eleven days and I have not yet understood her, and a man in my line learns to distrust what he cannot price.

She flies like the plane is part of her skeleton. She lies like breathing — small lies, navigational lies, the lies of a woman who learned young that the truth is a door people kick in. And yet she has spent this week doing the single most expensive thing I have ever seen anyone do: she keeps coming back. Back to the cave, back to the beach, back to the drowning place, each time with less fuel and worse odds, hauling strangers out of the water while the sky came apart overhead.

I run a salvage fleet. I know exactly what everything costs. I could not put a number on her.

* * *

She came in at 1400, low over the water with the wind quartering from the northeast at speeds I'd have called unflyable if I hadn't been watching them be flown. The Cub — and yes, I know aircraft now, I've had eleven days of forced education — bucked down the strip of wet sand that served Karakel for a runway, bounced twice, and stopped with her tail swinging like a metronome counting down something.

Coral climbed out into the rain, and the first thing she said to me was not a greeting.

"How many hurt?"

"Two serious. Aris worst — compound fracture, he's shocky, and I've got no morphine left worth the name. The rest are cuts and terror." I paused. "Terror I can treat locally."

She looked past me at the wreck, at the stern rising out of the spray with its name peeling, and something crossed her face that wasn't satisfaction. I'd expected satisfaction. I'd have paid for satisfaction. Instead she looked almost grieved, the way you might look at a dead animal you'd been hunting — the kill never being quite the thing you wanted.

"I can take two," she said. "Aris and one other. The strip's marginal and getting worse. After this load, I'm done until the front passes."

"You could leave us all here," I said. "It would solve several of your problems. It would certainly solve mine, eventually, in the sense that dead men don't testify."

"I don't solve problems that way." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Get your diver."

* * *

Carrying Aris to the plane was a comedy of six men and a stretcher and wind that kept trying to vote. His arm was splinted with a piece of the Sable Fortune's own rail, which struck me as either poetry or blasphemy. As we loaded him, he grabbed my sleeve with his good hand.

"Captain. The forward hold. When the survey teams come — there's nothing in it worth what it cost. Tell them that. Tell them I said the cave gave it up easy but the sea wants it back."

"Aris—"

"Tell them." His eyes were fever-bright and absolutely certain, and I nodded, because there was nothing else to do with a man like that except agree.

Coral got both patients aboard, got the engine running, and then stood in the rain beside the open door, looking at me across the wing. Rain ran off her cap brim in ropes. Behind her the sea was the color of dishwater and rising.

"Well?" she said. "You getting in or writing a will?"

"The ship—"

"Will float or won't without your opinion. Get in."

And that is how Captain Vikram Rao, master of the Sable Fortune, salvor of the Coral Triangle, scourge of government surveys on three coasts, came to be jammed into the rear seat of a bush plane between a crate of quinine and a coil of anchor line, wearing one shoe, because I had lost the other somewhere between the bridge and the beach and no one had seen fit to inform me.

* * *

The flight lasted nineteen minutes and rearranged my theology.

We went up through rain that hit the windshield like thrown gravel. The Cub climbed the way old dogs climb stairs — stubbornly, with complaint, but climbing. Below us the lagoon appeared and vanished in the murk, and once, just once, the cloud tore open and I saw the Sable Fortune from above: small, dark, broken-backed on the white seam of the reef, already becoming geography. Already becoming the thing they'd name the reef after, in forty years, on charts drawn by people who'd never know there'd been a man in her wheelhouse who thought he was smarter than a monsoon.

Coral flew through a gap in the squalls I would not have driven a car through, set down on the mainland strip in a crosswind that should have folded us, and taxied to the shelter shed as if parking outside a bakery.

The medics took Aris. I sat in the shed on an oil drum, dripping, one-shoed, and listened to the storm arrive behind us like an audience taking its seats.

Coral came in, wrung out her cap, and looked at me.

"You're a long way from your ship," she said.

"My ship is a long way from being a ship." I rubbed my face. "Ms. Crane. Before the authorities arrive, before the questions start — and they will start, and some of them will be mine to answer — there is a thing I need to give you."

Her eyes narrowed. Everything about her narrowed, like a cat deciding whether the movement in the grass is food or threat. "If this is a deal—"

"It is the opposite of a deal. It is the first thing in eleven days I've done without calculating the return." I reached inside my jacket, where I'd kept them dry against all logic, and brought out the manifest pages. The stolen ones. The ones from my safe, the ones worth more than my commission, the ones certain parties in Singapore believed were secured. Rotted at the edges, the lead sheet's cipher columns ghosting through the paper copies, Mateo Reyes's expedition mark stamped in faded ink near the header.

I held them out.

She didn't take them. "Those are evidence."

"They are. They're also leverage, and I am standing in a shed with one shoe telling you I no longer want any." I heard my own voice do something unfamiliar, which was shake slightly. "Do you know what I was going to do with these? Trade them. Piece by piece. To you, to the fleet's owners, to whichever ministry bid highest. I had a whole architecture planned, Ms. Crane. Very elegant. Very profitable. And then my ship put herself on the reef she came to rob, and I stood on a beach and watched ten centuries of somebody's history floating in the surf, and I understood that I had become the kind of man who looks at a drowned church and sees inventory."

The rain hammered the tin roof. Somewhere outside, the medics' radio crackled with Aris's vitals, holding, holding.

"There's a man in prison," Coral said quietly. "Because of what's in those pages. Or because of what isn't."

"I know. Your Dr. Reyes told me things I pretended not to hear. And I have a diver who swears the logs were forged, and I have a stowaway named Okafor hiding on your island who apparently agrees, and I have spent a week surrounded by people telling the truth at me from every direction while I held the one document that could prove it and priced it hourly." I pushed the pages toward her again. "Take them. Give them to whoever locks people up and lets them out. I'll confirm where I got them, and I'll swear to the rest, and I'll spend two years in depositions, and it will serve me right."

"Why?" It wasn't hostile. It was genuinely curious, the way she'd looked at the wreck — grief and arithmetic in the same glance. "You don't strike me as a convert, Rao. You strike me as a man having a very bad week."

I laughed. It surprised us both.

"I am having a very bad decade," I said, "and only noticed today. Look — you want the honest answer? You pulled me out of the water I made. Both of us. Me and my diver and my crew, out of a mess I sailed into with my eyes open and my ledger closed. There is no version of the mathematics where I get to keep these pages after that. They'd burn my hands. Take them before I come to my senses, Ms. Crane. I've been told I do that around money."

* * *

She took them.

She held them the way you hold a bird — careful, a little afraid of it, aware it might mean more than it weighs. Then she looked at me for a long moment, and whatever audit she was running behind those eyes, I passed it, which may be the first inspection of my life I'm proud of.

"The authorities will be here within the day," she said. "When they ask you why you handed these over, what are you going to tell them?"

I considered the question seriously, because it deserved it. Outside, the monsoon arrived in earnest, and the shed roof began to sing.

"I'm going to tell them the truth," I said. "That I came here to strip a grave, and the sea stripped me instead, and a woman who had every reason to let me drown came back for me anyway. And that I looked at her and realized I'd been reading the wrong charts my whole career." I stood, joints announcing themselves. "There's a survey team coming for that site, I'm told. Official. Thorough. If they need a salvor who knows where all the bodies are buried — literally, in my case — I find myself unexpectedly available. At honest wages."

Coral almost smiled. Almost. On her, it was a decoration of rank.

"Don't make me regret the fuel," she said, and walked out into the rain toward the radio shed, the manifest pages sealed inside her jacket, over her heart, where secrets apparently live.

I stayed on my oil drum and listened to the storm work the coast over, and for the first time since I signed for the Sable Fortune, I wasn't calculating anything at all.

Then the radio shed door banged open, and I heard Coral's voice cut through the rain — sharp, cracked, alive:

"She's awake. Ilsa's awake — and the first thing she asked for was the pages."
