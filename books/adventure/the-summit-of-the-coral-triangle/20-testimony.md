## 20. Testimony

The Port Authority office on San Isidro smelled like wet paper and forty years of bureaucratic despair, which is to say it smelled like every government building in the Triangle, and I loved it a little for that. There is a particular comfort in a place where nothing has ever happened quickly enough for anyone to get hurt.

The ceiling fan turned with the enthusiasm of a man being paid by the hour. Rain hammered the louvers sideways — the monsoon had settled in like a relative with no return ticket — and somewhere down the hall a radio crackled through a weather advisory nobody needed. We could all see the weather. It was trying to come inside.

The clerk who'd summoned me was named Officer Bautista, a small round man with reading glasses on a cord and the unhurried manner of someone who had processed a thousand crises and would process a thousand more after lunch. He set a legal pad, two pens, and a glass of water in front of me as if arranging an altar.

"Ms. Halloran." He said my name carefully, the way you handle something you suspect might be sharp. "You understand what you're here to do."

"I'm going to perjure myself retroactively," I said. "In reverse. Un-perjure. Whatever the word is when you take back a lie you swore to under oath ten years ago."

"*Recant,*" he said, with the satisfaction of a man who owned the word.

"Recant," I agreed. My hands were shaking. I put them flat on the table so they'd have something honest to do.

He studied me over his glasses. "You could still walk out. The storm gives everyone an excuse this week. Nobody would think less of you."

"Officer Bautista," I said, "I have spent ten years making sure people thought less of me. It's been very restful. I'm retiring from it."

Something moved behind his face — not quite a smile, but the place where one kept its boat moored. He clicked on a recorder the size of a cigarette pack, checked the little red light twice, and said, "Then let's begin. State your full name for the record."

"Coral Anahera Halloran. Commercial pilot, license number—" I gave it, and heard how strange it sounded, saying it out loud when I wasn't sure anymore whether I'd ever hold it again. "And former witness in the maritime incident inquiry of eleven years ago. The Mateo Halloran dive fatality. The conviction of Rafael Halloran for gross negligence resulting in death."

"You are aware your statement today may result in the reopening of that case."

"I'm counting on it."

"And you're aware that under the revised testimony protocol, statements made in mitigation of a prior false sworn account—"

"Bautista." I looked at him. "I've flown medicine into river villages during civil disturbances. I've landed on sandbars at night by moonlight and memory. I once talked a customs officer out of impounding my plane using nothing but a thermos of coffee and flattery about his mother's cooking. You don't need to protect me from the paperwork. Just point it at me."

He took off his glasses, polished them on his shirt hem, put them back on. "Fair enough," he said. "Begin whenever you're ready."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about telling the truth: it doesn't feel like relief. Relief is a landing after a long flight — wheels down, weight settling into your spine, the world suddenly simple. Truth isn't like that. Truth is more like opening a door you've kept locked for a decade and finding the room exactly as you left it, dust and all, everything waiting for you, patient as only guilt can be patient.

So I told it. All of it, in order, because Bautista asked for order and order was the only thing keeping me upright.

I told him about the expedition — the survey contract, the financier whose name I made myself say clearly, Aldous Wren, because saying it softly would have been another kind of lie. I told him about the artifacts recovered from the Karakel shelf: the astrolabe, the coin lots, the compass. *The compass.* Silver-cased, engraved, logged into the manifest with a serial number I could still recite in my sleep, because I'd recited it wrong for eleven years. I told him I testified it went down with Mateo. That I saw it lost. That I built my whole account around that single anchor point, and that the anchor was forged.

"The logs were falsified before the dive," I said. "Dez Okafor has confirmed it — I have his written confession, dated three days before the cave collapse, and I'll surrender it to you with this statement. Wren pressured him. The recovery numbers were inflated to justify a second season of funding. Mateo found out. He wrote to Dr. Reyes about it weeks before he died — she has the letter. And then Mateo died, and the inquiry needed a cause, and Rafe was the dive coordinator, and I—" 

My voice did something I didn't authorize. I stopped. Drank the water. The glass rattled against the table and Bautista pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me all week.

"I gave them the cause they needed," I finished. "I said the compass was lost in the dive. I said I saw the equipment failure myself. Neither of those things was true. I didn't see any failure. What I saw was Mateo's slate, later, in the cave — his handwriting, dated *after* the dive we were told was final — and I knew the timeline was manufactured, and I said nothing, because saying something meant admitting I'd already lied, and I couldn't—" I breathed. "I couldn't afford it. So Rafe paid instead. Four years. Four years of a man's life, purchased with my signature."

Bautista wrote for a long time. The fan creaked overhead. Outside, the rain kept up its argument with the roof.

"Why?" he asked finally, not unkindly. "The first lie. Before the cover-up, before any of it. Why did you swear the compass was lost?"

And there it was. The room behind the door, the part I hadn't even shown Ilsa completely, the part Dez's note had cracked open like a seam in a hull.

"Because I panicked," I said. "Because we were twenty meters down and the vis went to zero and Mateo didn't come back on the line, and in the black water I convinced myself the gear was cursed, that the expedition was rotten from the keel up, that the compass — which I had last seen in Mateo's kit — had to be gone, had to be *gone*, because if it came back, if it surfaced somewhere, then the story would unravel and I'd have to know what really happened to him. I lied to make the world simpler. Then the inquiry asked me to swear to my lie, and swearing felt safer than thinking. That's the whole anatomy of it, Officer. No conspiracy on my side. Just cowardice with a pilot's license."

He looked at me over the glasses for a long moment. "You understand," he said, "that this doesn't just reopen the case against Rafael Halloran. It opens one against you. False sworn statement is itself an offense. There will be consequences."

"I've read the menu," I said. "I'm ordering anyway."

* * *

They took a break at some point — Bautista's supervisor came in, a tall woman with gray at her temples named Director Ocampo, who listened to thirty seconds of my recitation and then pulled up a chair herself, which I gathered was either very good or very bad news. She asked precise questions. When had Dez contacted me. Had anyone else seen the confession. Did I understand the manifest sheet in evidence bore the expedition mark matching the serial numbers now corroborated by Dr. Reyes's letter. I answered everything. Somewhere in the second hour my hands stopped shaking, and I noticed their stillness the way you notice a engine running smooth after months of sputtering — with suspicion first, and then something like gratitude.

It was Ocampo who finally leaned back and said, "Dr. Reyes is downstairs. She's given her supplementary statement. She's asking to be present when we contact the correctional facility regarding Mr. Halloran."

"Put her on speaker," I said. "Rafe should hear it from all of us or none of us."

Ocampo raised an eyebrow. "You've spoken to him?"

"Once. From Karakel. He told me not to dig." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a borrowed coat. "I've never been good at taking directions."

* * *

The call went through at 4:40 in the afternoon, local time, though time had gone soft and unreliable hours ago. The correctional facility's line was bad — a delay, a hiss, the institutional voice of a duty officer — and then Rafe's voice, and the whole room got quieter than a room containing rain and a ceiling fan had any right to be.

"Rafael Halloran? This is Director Ocampo, Port Authority of San Isidro. I'm calling regarding your case file. Are you able to speak freely?"

A pause long enough to fall through. "Ma'am, I can speak."

"There has been a development. A new sworn statement has been filed today concerning the incident inquiry of eleven years ago. The statement concerns falsified expedition records and materially contradicts prior testimony. Your conviction is vacated pending review, effective immediately. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Another pause. On the table in front of me sat Dez's confession in its plastic sleeve, and beside it the manifest pages, and beside those the transcript of my own voice, four hours of it, every cowardly syllable accounted for. I watched the red light on the recorder and thought about how many red lights I'd flown past in my life.

"I understand," Rafe said, and his voice was careful, level, the voice of a man handling something explosive. "Vacated pending review. That's not exonerated."

"No," Ocampo said honestly. "Not yet. But the review board convenes within the week, and the supporting evidence is substantial."

"Can I ask—" and there the level broke, just slightly, a hairline crack in poured concrete. "Who filed the statement?"

The room looked at me. Bautista looked at me. Ocampo looked at me, and tilted the phone an inch in my direction, and I understood that this, too, was part of it — that truth delivered at last means delivering it yourself, no proxies, no intermediaries, no altitude between you and the ground.

"It was me, Rafe," I said. "It's Coral."

The silence on the line went on so long I checked whether we'd lost the connection. Rain filled it. The fan creaked. Somewhere below, Ilsa was waiting in a corridor, holding a letter from a dead man, and I wished with a sudden ferocity that she were beside me, and understood that wishing it was its own kind of answer.

"You didn't have to do that," Rafe said finally. "I told you not to dig."

"I know."

"Some truths cost more than the lie did. I told you that too."

"I know," I said. "You were right about the price. You were wrong that I couldn't pay it." I pressed my palms flat against the table again, steady, steady. "Four years, Rafe. I'm sorry. I won't insult you by explaining why. The statement's all there — every word of it, mine, sworn, on record. Whatever the review decides, you'll never again be sitting on a charge that was built on my lie. That much I could do today, and I did it."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh, in the way that things can be almost other things. "Ten years flying away from it," he said, "and you land in a port authority office to fix it. Mateo would've found that funny."

"Mateo found everything funny."

"Yeah," he said, and his voice went rough and young. "Yeah, he did." A breath. "Coral. Thank you isn't—"

"Don't," I said. "Don't spend it on me. Spend it on getting out. There's a woman downstairs who's spent eleven years keeping your brother's letters, and when you're free, you're going to want to read them. Both of you together. That's the debt you can pay."

Another silence, softer this time. "Deal," he said. And then, quieter, almost lost in the line static: "Fly safe, Coral."

"I'm grounded," I said. "License suspended, pending review. Plane impounded as evidence."

A real laugh this time, short and disbelieving. "You? Grounded?"

"Temporarily," I said. "Everything's temporary. That's sort of the whole lesson."

* * *

Afterward, Ocampo dismissed Bautista and stood at the rain-streaked window with her back to me for a while, watching the monsoon do its work on the harbor. The fishing boats were double-moored and riding it out; beyond them, grey water, grey sky, no border between.

"You understand what happens next for you," she said, without turning.

"Suspension stands until the review concludes. Possibly revocation. Possibly charges of my own."

"Possibly." She turned around. She had the look of a woman deciding how much of the truth to spend, and I recognized the expression intimately, having worn it for a decade. "For what it's worth — and it's worth little, coming from this office — the review board sees very few people who walk in and unmake their own testimony voluntarily. Most fight to the last procedural ditch. The ones who don't tend to fare better than they expect." She picked up her folder. "Get some sleep, Ms. Halloran. You look like you flew through a typhoon."

"I did, mostly."

"Mm." At the door she paused. "Dr. Reyes is waiting for you in the corridor. She refused to leave until you were done. She's been there six hours."

I stood alone in the humid office for a moment after she left, listening to the rain and the dying fan, looking at the legal pad where my confession lay in Bautista's neat hand — page after page of the worst year of my life, transcribed, witnessed, sealed. Ten years of running, and it had all come down to a folding table, a glass of water, and a small man with a recorder.

My hands were steady now. Completely steady. I flexed them, testing, half-expecting the tremor to come back like weather. It didn't.

I walked out into the corridor, and there was Ilsa, sitting on a bench under a flickering fluorescent tube, mud still dried on her boots from Karakel, Mateo's letter folded in her lap like something alive she was keeping warm. She looked up, and read my face the way she read tide tables — instantly, completely.

"Well?" she said.

"He's vacated," I said. "Pending review. Pending everything. But vacated." My voice held. "Ilsa — it's done. The lie's on the record as a lie. All of it. Mine included."

She stood up slowly, and crossed the corridor, and stopped close enough that I could see she'd been crying sometime in the last six hours and had decided, in typical Ilsa fashion, to treat it as private data. "And you?"

"Grounded. Probably charged. Possibly ruined." I shrugged, and found the shrug weighed nothing at all. "Turns out the truth costs exactly what Rafe said it would. I just had ten years of interest saved up."

Ilsa looked at me for a long moment — grief and suspicion long since burned down to something steadier underneath — and then she did the last thing I expected. She laughed. Quiet, cracked, real.

"Come on, pilot," she said, taking my arm as the lights flickered and the rain redoubled against the windows, as somewhere across the drowned Triangle a cell door was, at that very moment, being unlocked for a man who had stopped believing in doors. "Let's go find out what stillness feels like."

I let her lead me toward it — toward the waiting, the reckoning, whatever the review board and the world decided I deserved — and discovered, walking out of that office with empty hands and a full record, that I wasn't afraid of the landing at all.

I'd finally, after ten years, stopped flying away from it.
