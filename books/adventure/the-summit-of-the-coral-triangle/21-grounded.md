## 21. Grounded

The port authority office in Sorong kept my plane on a tie-down by the fuel farm for eleven days, and I walked past it every morning like a man visiting a grave he wasn't sure was occupied.

They'd wrapped the tail in orange evidence tape. Somebody had zip-tied a laminated tag to the strut with my registration number and the word *HOLD* in letters so official they looked like they'd been printed by God's own bureaucracy. The left tire still wore the scar from where Dez — or whoever it had been, though Ilsa was right, it had been Dez, cowardice has a signature — had slashed it before the monsoon made the point moot.

I'd stopped at that fence every day for the first week. By day eight I made myself walk past without stopping. By day ten I didn't take the road past the airfield at all.

That's what suspension does to you. It doesn't ground your body. Your body was never the problem. It grounds the part of you that measures time in tides and fuel gauges and the distance between thunderheads. It takes away the instrument panel you've been reading your own life on for ten years, and hands you instead a room with a fan, a bed you don't need because you're not tired, and a town full of people who now know your name for reasons you can't fly away from.

My license was suspended pending review of my "aeronautical conduct during emergency operations." That was the phrase. *Aeronautical conduct.* As if flying three loads of drowning men out of a collapsing storm were a conduct problem, like I'd parked in someone else's spot. The investigating officer, a careful, exhausted man named Wurianto who smelled of clove cigarettes and fairness in roughly equal measure, explained it to me twice with genuine apology: the same flights that saved lives also flew without clearance, over restricted airspace, carrying salvaged cultural property, in weather no authority had sanctioned. The heroism and the violations were the same flights. There was no way to award one without examining the other.

"I could tell you it'll likely be resolved favorably," he said, on the last day, tapping ash into a tin lid. "But I won't, because I don't know. What I can tell you is that the wreck site survey is proceeding, Dr. Reyes has filed her reports, and the Okafor matter is—" He paused, choosing. "—being handled as compassionately as a court can handle anything."

Dez had survived the collapse. Broken femur, punctured lung, four days on a ventilator in Sorong while the monsoon threw rain at the hospital windows like it wanted them back. He'd woken up, heard that his confession recording existed, heard that Rafe Mateo's conviction was under formal review, and asked the nurse whether the ceiling fan could be slowed down. That detail reached me through Ilsa, who visited him more than I did. She said forgiveness is a thing you do in person or not at all, and she was doing hers in person, in a plastic chair, hour after hour. I told her I'd get there. She said she knew. She didn't say when, which was its own kind of mercy.

I got there on day nine. Dez was propped up with his leg in traction, looking like a man who'd been through a wringer and come out the far side thinner but recognizably himself. He cried before I said anything. I sat down and let him. When he finally spoke, his voice was gravel and shame: "You came."

"You're the only other person alive who was there," I said. "That makes us family. The worst kind."

He laughed, then winced, then laughed again anyway. We talked for two hours about everything except the forgery and the lie, and when I stood to leave he caught my wrist and said, "Coral. I'm sorry I let him die thinking I betrayed him." And I said, "Mateo didn't die thinking anything. He died knowing. There's a difference, and you should carry the better one." Then I walked out into the heat and sat on the curb outside the hospital for twenty minutes, shaking, before I could stand up.

* * *

Stillness, it turns out, is not death. This surprised me more than anything else that season.

The first three days I paced my rented room like a caged thing, checking the barometer app on my phone out of habit, flinching at every aircraft that passed overhead — and there are always aircraft passing overhead in Sorong, the whole town is a throat that swallows and releases planes all day long. Every Twin Otter drone was a reminder. Every Cessna's shadow crossing my window was a hand pressing down on the back of my neck.

Then, around day five, something shifted. I woke up and the fan was turning and the light through the shutters was the color of weak tea and I lay there and realized I had nowhere to be. Not a schedule. Not a charter. Not a patient waiting on quinine, not a rumor to outrun, not a tide to beat. Nowhere.

A lesser version of me — the version from chapter one of this disaster, the woman who landed twice in ten years and both times it cost somebody everything — would have called that peace. I knew better. I knew it was just the absence of motion, and absence isn't the same as presence. But I lay there anyway, and I didn't reach for the phone, and I didn't check the weather, and I let the morning happen to me at its own speed.

By day seven I'd learned the names of the woman who sold rambutans near the jetty (Bu Yanti, three grandchildren, opinions about durian she would share whether asked or not) and the boy who swept the fish market steps (Andi, sixteen, saving for a welding course in Manado). I'd eaten breakfast sitting down, at an actual table, twice. Ilsa found me on day six at Bu Yanti's stall, holding a bag of rambutan and looking faintly bewildered, like a bird that had voluntarily landed.

"You're buying fruit," she said.

"I'm buying fruit."

"Voluntarily."

"Don't make it weird, Ilsa."

She grinned and stole one out of the bag, and we ate them standing in the shade while the harbor did its slow glittering business below us, and neither of us mentioned planes or manifests or the word *suspended*, and it was the best hour I'd spent in a decade.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about running: you get good at it. You get so good that running becomes the only skill you trust. You start believing that if you just keep moving — new strip, new cargo, new dawn — the thing behind you will eventually lose the scent. It took a grounded license and an impounded airplane and a monsoon and a cave and a dead man's handwriting on a slate for me to understand that the thing behind me wasn't chasing me at all. It was waiting. Patiently. In every quiet room I refused to enter. All those years of fuel stops and dawn departures, and I'd never once outrun it; I'd just declined every invitation it offered to sit down and talk.

So I sat down. Metaphorically. Mostly literally, on Bu Yanti's plastic stool, eating fruit, learning the names of things that weren't going anywhere.

It didn't kill me. That was the revelation, and it arrived without trumpets: stillness doesn't kill you. It just makes you look at what you've been refusing to look at, and it turns out you can survive that too. You can survive your own face in an unmoving mirror. You can survive the memory of a night ten years ago, replayed not as the nightmare version where you drown, but as the true version where you surface alone and lie to a rescue coordinator with a steady voice and a heartbeat like a drum corps. You can survive knowing that your testimony sent an innocent man to prison for four years, and that the truth cost you nothing but the lie had cost everyone else everything.

Rafe's release came through on a Tuesday.

* * *

Ilsa told me first, because she'd been monitoring the review board's communications with the obsessive attention of a scientist who has waited years for a result. She knocked on my door at an hour I was beginning to recognize as morning rather than pre-dawn, and she was holding her phone against her chest like it contained a live coal.

"It's done," she said. "Vacated. Full exoneration recommendation. They're releasing him this week."

I sat down on the edge of the bed. The fan turned. Somewhere below, Bu Yanti was arguing cheerfully with a customer about the price of mangoes.

"Say something," Ilsa said gently.

"I don't know what to say. I've been rehearsing this conversation for ten years and none of the drafts fit."

"What did the drafts say?"

"That I'd beg his forgiveness. That I'd offer him money. That I'd explain why I did it until he understood." I looked at my hands. "None of that's real. The only real thing is that I lied and he paid for it, and no speech changes the exchange rate."

Ilsa sat beside me. She didn't touch me yet; she'd learned my weather patterns by then, knew when contact would steady me and when it would capsize me. "What are you going to do?"

"Nothing," I said, and heard how it sounded, and corrected: "Not nothing. Whatever he wants. Which might be nothing. Which might be never seeing me again. Both of those are answers I'll accept."

"You love him," she said. Not a question — she meant Mateo, meant the ghost, meant the whole drowned architecture of my old life.

"I loved Mateo. Rafe is — Rafe is the brother of the man I loved and the man I wronged, and I owe him a debt I can't calculate, and I have spent ten years being afraid of him, which is its own kind of devotion." I exhaled. "I don't know what I feel. I know what I owe. Maybe those turn out to be the same thing."

"They rarely are," Ilsa said, "but they can travel together."

* * *

They released him on a Thursday, from the district facility outside Sorong, and I found out afterward that he'd declined the press conference, declined the interview requests, declined everything except a ride into town and a room. Ilsa offered to come with me when I went to see him. I told her this was a door I had to knock on alone. She kissed me — we were doing that now, easily, like people who had always done it — and said, "Whatever happens in there, come home after."

Home. She'd used the word casually, the way you use a word you've decided means something, and I carried it with me like ballast all the way across town.

Rafe was staying at a losmen near the church, a place with a porch and a view of exactly nothing, which I suspected was the entire appeal. He answered the door before I knocked, which meant he'd seen me coming, which meant some part of him had been waiting too.

Ten years since I'd stood in front of him. He'd gone grey at the temples. Prison had carved him leaner and quieter, and there was a stillness in him that I recognized immediately, horribly, as kin to mine — the stillness of a person who has also spent years learning what cannot be changed. We looked at each other across the width of the doorway, two survivors of the same crash, and I understood that whatever happened next, we had already survived the worst of it separately.

"Coral," he said.

"Rafe."

He stepped back and opened the door wider. "I made coffee. It's terrible. Come in."

We sat at a table under the ceiling fan — everyone in this story ends up under a ceiling fan eventually, it's the region's universal confessional — and he poured two cups of coffee that was indeed terrible, burnt and over-sweetened in the way of institutional catering, and we drank it because it was there.

"I'm not going to ask you why," he said.

I blinked. "What?"

"The night you testified. I've had four years to wonder, and I'm done wondering. You already told the truth, Coral. To the board, to the investigators, to the recording. You handed over the manifest, you gave up your license, you dove into a flooding cave to save a man who forged the logs that put me away. I read the reports." He set down his cup. "There is nothing you could say in this room that would add to that. So I'm not asking why. I'm asking nothing of you at all."

"That can't be right," I said. My voice came out strange. "I put you in prison."

"You did. And then you took it apart brick by brick, at enormous personal cost, because it was the right thing to do. Most people who knock over a house never come back with tools." He smiled, and it was Mateo's smile, and it went through me like cold water. "I'm not saying it's forgiven. Forgiveness is a process, not a verdict. But I'm saying the account is settled enough that we can be in a room together. That's more than I expected to ever want, and it turns out it's enough."

I cried. I hadn't planned to; I'd planned to be dignified and restrained and to leave before it got messy. Instead I sat in a losmen under a bad fan drinking terrible coffee and wept like the decade was leaving my body through my eyes, and Rafe sat across from me and let me, occasionally sliding the sugar tin closer, which was such a Mateo gesture that it made me cry harder.

When it was over he walked me to the door, and on the porch, in the gold light of late afternoon, he said the thing I'll keep:

"I used to think I needed an apology from you. Then I thought I needed an explanation. Turns out what I needed was for someone to tell the truth, and you did, and it cost you everything, and that's worth more than any words you could say now." He paused. "Fly safe, Coral. Whenever they give you back the sky."

* * *

Wurianto called on day fourteen with the news I'd been half-dreading, half-hoping for: the review board had concluded its assessment. My license would be reinstated — not suspended indefinitely, not revoked, reinstated — effective the following Monday, contingent on completion of a recurrent training checkride and a formal debrief on the Karakel operations. The violations would remain on my record. The rescues would remain on my record. Both, permanently, side by side, the way lives actually work.

"There will be conditions," Wurianto warned. "No unsanctioned cargo. No unlogged flights. And Ms. Halloran — the board noted, specifically, that you surrendered the manifest and slate rather than attempting to retain or sell them. That weighed heavily."

"I didn't do it to weigh heavily," I said. "I did it because it was theirs. The sea's, the record's, Mateo's."

"I understand. But it weighed anyway." A pause, smoke drawn and released somewhere on his end of the line. "Congratulations, Captain. Welcome back to the sky. Try to stay out of caves."

I hung up and sat very still for a long moment, listening to the fan, feeling the strange weight of a future that had edges again.

Then I walked down to the airfield, past the fuel farm, to the tie-down where my plane had spent two weeks wearing evidence tape like a sash. The tape was gone. The tag was gone. Someone — Wurianto, I suspected, though he'd deny it — had even washed the salt spray off the cowling. She sat there in the late light, dented and salt-bitten and utterly herself, and I put my hand flat on her propeller hub the way you'd touch a horse's neck, and I felt the tremor go through me that I always felt, the one that used to mean *go*, and I stood there and let it mean simply *here*.

Monday, I'd fly the checkride. Some examiner would sit beside me and watch me do stalls and steep turns and short-field landings, and I'd perform competence like a pianist playing scales, and it would be fine, because the competence was never the problem. The problem had been what I pointed the competence at. Ten years of pointing it away.

I walked back through town as the light went amber and rose-colored, past Bu Yanti closing up her stall, past Andi sweeping the market steps, past the losmen where Rafe sat on his porch reading something, who raised one hand as I passed and I raised mine back, and it was nothing, and it was everything.

At my door, Ilsa was waiting with two cups of the good coffee from the place by the jetty, and she handed me mine and said, "Well?"

"Monday," I said. "I fly Monday."

"And after Monday?"

I looked at her — at this woman who had walked into my airstrip asking dangerous questions and stayed to help me answer them, who had chosen tenderness with the storm already audible, who was already drafting the survey plan for a site that would be underwater forever, whose report would be the last human document ever to describe that cave — and I found that the answer was easy, which frightened me more than any storm cell ever had.

"After Monday," I said, "I'm going to land twice in the same place. On purpose. As many times as I want."

She smiled, and the fan turned, and somewhere out past the harbor the evening tide was coming in, unhurried, keeping its ancient schedule, indifferent to whether any of us were watching — and I realized I was watching. For the first time in ten years, I was watching.

The phone buzzed in my pocket. I almost didn't check it. I did.

Unknown number, local prefix, one line:

*Ms. Halloran — Vikram Rao. The Sable Fortune is being scrapped where she lies. Before I sign her away, there's something on board you should see. It belonged to Mateo.*

I looked at the message for a long time. Then I looked at Ilsa, who was watching my face with the particular attention of a woman who has learned to read weather in it.

"What?" she said.

I turned the screen toward her, and we read it together, standing in the doorway, while the tide came in.
