## 22. The Next Chart

The license came back in a plain envelope, no ceremony, the way the government does everything it's ashamed of. I stood in the port authority office doorway where I'd once given my second testimony and read my own name printed on a card that said I was allowed to fly again, and I felt exactly nothing for about four seconds.

Then I felt everything, all at once, like a tide coming in over a sandbar.

"You'll want to frame that," said the clerk, a woman named Bernadette who had witnessed both of my interrogations and had never once offered me coffee until the third visit.

"I'm going to laminate it," I said. "Frame it, laminate it, and bury it in the backyard."

"That's very you."

I'd been grounded eleven weeks. Eleven weeks of watching other people's planes climb out of Sorong in the early light while I learned to drink coffee sitting down instead of standing at a fuel pump. Eleven weeks of discovering that stillness did not, in fact, kill me — which had been my working theory since I was twenty-six years old and it had never once been tested, because I never let it get tested. Running was the one experiment I refused to run out of.

Turns out you can sit in a chair. Turns out the chair holds.

* * *

The survey was Ilsa's from the day the review board signed off on Rafe's vacated conviction. She'd written the proposal before the monsoon even cleared — I know because she wrote half of it in my kitchen, barefoot, arguing with a laptop about bathymetric resolution while I cooked rice and pretended not to be memorizing the shape of her concentration.

"Karakelel site is sealed now," she'd said, not looking up. "Sixty meters to the cave roof. That makes it a time capsule. Nobody loots it, nobody disturbs it, and everything Mateo touched down there stays exactly where he left it."

"Is that what you want?" I asked. "Or is that what he'd want?"

She looked up then. "Both. That's the trick nobody tells you about grief — sometimes the answers overlap."

So the proposal went in: full archaeological survey of the Karakel deep site, remotely operated vehicle first, divers only if conditions allowed, funded by the ministry, blessed by three committees, and staffed — this was the part she fought hardest for — with a pilot cleared to operate in marginal weather, because the site's only practical access was a strip of beach on an island with no name on most charts.

There was exactly one pilot with that clearance who also knew the approach by heart.

"They're going to make me interview for it," I said.

"They're going to make *me* justify recommending you," she said. "Given your history with the site."

"My history with the site is why I'm qualified."

"That's what I told them." She closed the laptop. "They didn't love it."

"And then?"

"And then I showed them the transcript of what you did during the landfall. Three loads out, in cells, off a beach, with a busted radio." She shrugged. "Committees love transcripts."

* * *

We flew out on a Tuesday in the dry season, which in the Coral Triangle means it only rained twice a day instead of continuously. The survey team was five people and four hundred kilos of equipment, and my old Cessna — released from impoundment with a new engine and a smell of fresh upholstery that would take a year to wear into something honest — carried all of it plus me plus Ilsa riding shotgun with a chart case on her knees like it was a sleeping child.

"Say it," she said as we leveled off at nine thousand feet, Karakel a green smudge ahead.

"Say what?"

"The thing you always say. The rule."

I checked the manifold pressure, which didn't need checking. "Never land twice in the same place."

"And today?"

"Today we land twice in the same place." I let that sit in the cockpit between us for a while. "On purpose."

"How's it feel?"

"Like wearing someone else's shoes." I glanced at her. "Good shoes, though. Broken in by somebody sensible."

Below us the sea did its ordinary miracle, blue shading to teal shading to the pale glass of the reef flats. Somewhere under that glass was a cave that had opened for one season and closed forever, taking with it a rotted chest, a lead manifest, a slate with Mateo's handwriting on it, and the last hiding place of Dez Okafor's confession. The ministry divers had placed a bronze plaque on the reef above it two months ago — I'd read the photos. *Here lie the truths of the expedition of 20—. Recovered at cost.* They hadn't put names on it. Ilsa had fought about that too, and lost, and made her peace with it in the way she made peace with everything: thoroughly, and out loud, and then never again.

"Mateo would have hated the plaque," I said.

"Mateo would have hated the plaque, the ROV, the committee, and the fact that his girlfriend is dating the woman who testified against his brother." Ilsa adjusted the chart case. "He'd have gotten over it. He got over everything. It was his best quality and his worst."

* * *

First landing: the beach strip, wheels down on wet sand at 0740, tide low enough that the firm pack gave us six hundred usable meters. I'd flown this approach in a storm with survivors strapped in behind me and my hands shaking so hard I'd had to grip the yoke with both fists. Today the air was clean, the wind was eight knots on the nose, and the touchdown was so smooth that Dr. Ferreira, the sedimentologist, applauded from the back seat, which I chose to take as professional respect rather than condescension.

Second landing: we offloaded, ferried gear to the survey camp — three tents, a generator, a dive compressor, and a satellite dish pointed at the sky like a metal flower seeking a very specific sun — and then, at 1100, with the tide turned and the light flat and perfect, I took off again, flew a lazy circuit around the island, and landed again on the same beach.

Bernadette would have appreciated it. There was no one to appreciate it but me, and Ilsa, who stood at the edge of the strip with her arms crossed and her hair whipping and watched me roll out to a stop ten meters from where I'd started.

"Twice," she said when I shut the engine down.

"Twice."

"Same place."

"Same place." I climbed out onto the sand. My legs felt strange, like they'd been expecting to carry me somewhere else and had been told, firmly, no. "It's a terrible rule anyway. Never land twice. You know what it actually means? Never stay. Never be known. Never give any place enough time to hand you a bill for what you've done there." I looked up the beach toward the black notch in the cliff where the cave mouth had been, above the waterline now, just a shadow among shadows. "This place handed me every bill I owed. Figured the least I could do was park here twice."

Ilsa came and stood next to me. She didn't take my hand, exactly; she hooked her little finger through mine, which was our thing by then, small enough to be private, strong enough to mean it.

"For the record," she said, "the rule served you. It kept you alive through some years that would have killed a person who stopped moving."

"I know."

"But?"

"But it also kept me from being at Rafe's mother's funeral. And from answering about forty letters. And from noticing that the woman who ran the fuel dock in Ambon had been leaving a light on for me every night for six years in case I needed it." I exhaled. "You stop moving long enough, you see all the lights that were on the whole time."

* * *

The survey itself was Ilsa's element, and I was content to be furniture in it. For six days the ROV went down through water so clear it felt like cheating, and the screens in the camp tent filled with images none of us were quite ready for: the collapsed chamber where Dez had died, now a cathedral of settled silt; the manifest sheet, scanned in situ, its lead surface furred with ten thousand years' worth of patience; and there, wedged in a crevice the ROV's arm could reach but barely, Mateo's slate.

Ilsa worked the controls herself for that retrieval. Nobody suggested otherwise. When the slate broke the surface, dripping, its wax face miraculously legible under a century of protective muck, she held it in both hands the way you hold something that is simultaneously priceless and worthless — priceless because it was his, worthless because everything it could tell us, it had already told.

"He wrote to me," she said quietly, to the slate, to the tent, maybe to him. "Three weeks before. I thought he was being paranoid. I told him to document everything and send it to the university legal office." Her jaw tightened. "He documented everything. He sent it to me. I sat on it for a decade because acting on it meant admitting I'd ignored it."

"I know," I said. "I read the inquiry file. Page forty."

"Page forty." She laughed, wet-eyed. "My entire cowardice, summarized on page forty."

"Your entire cowardice and mine are probably cross-referenced somewhere in appendix B."

That got the laugh out of her properly, and the team pretended very hard to be busy with their instruments, and the generator hummed, and outside the tent the sea lay over the cave like a hand laid gently over a mouth — not silencing it anymore, just keeping it warm.

* * *

Rafe came out on the supply boat on the last day.

I heard the diesel before I saw him, and by the time I'd walked down to the waterline my heart was doing something unsporting against my ribs. Four years inside, then eighteen months of review hearings, and here he was: thinner, grayer at the temples, squinting in the daylight like a man relearning it. He stepped off the boat into ankle-deep water and stood there looking at me, and I braced for it — the anger, the question, the bill.

"Coral."

"Rafe."

He looked past me at the camp, at the tents, at Ilsa coming down the beach with the slate wrapped in padding in her arms. Then back at me.

"You look different," he said.

"I've been sitting down a lot."

A ghost of a smile. His brother's smile, and not his brother's smile, and I had finally lived long enough in one place, long enough in my own skin, to hold both of those facts at once without dropping either.

"I didn't come to ask you anything," he said. "I want you to know that. Whatever you're bracing for — don't. The truth you gave them was worth more than the lie ever cost me. That's the whole speech. That's it."

"It's a good speech."

"I practiced." He nodded at the slate in Ilsa's arms. "Is that his?"

"Yeah."

"Can I—" He stopped. Started again. "I never got to say goodbye to him. Everybody got a version of goodbye except me. Prison doesn't do funerals for brothers."

Ilsa unwrapped the slate right there in the shallows, salt wind lifting the padding away, and held it out to him. Rafe took it like a man taking a sleeping child from someone he trusted absolutely. He read the wax lines — Mateo's cramped, urgent handwriting, the last things he ever wrote — and his shoulders shook once, and then he straightened, and he read it again slower, and when he finally looked up his eyes were red and clear.

"He says here he forgives whoever needs it," Rafe said. "'Whoever needs it.' That's so like him. Generous to a fault, literally to a fault."

"He'd have included you first," I said.

"He'd have included *you* first." Rafe handed the slate back to Ilsa with exaggerated care. Then he looked at me, and whatever passed between us wasn't forgiveness exactly — forgiveness implies a debt ledger, and we'd burned ours — it was simpler than that. Recognition. Two people who had survived the same wreck from opposite ends of it.

"Dez's family sent word," he said, turning to go. "They're putting a marker for him in Dili. His sister asked if I'd speak. Me. Can you imagine."

"Actually," I said, "yes."

He waded back to the boat. Before he climbed in he paused, waist-deep, and called back across the water:

"Hey, Crane. Word of advice from a free man. Land twice."

* * *

We stayed a seventh day. There was no operational reason to. The survey data was packed, the ROV crated, the permits countersigned. But Ilsa wanted one more dive on the outer reef — recreational, she insisted, though she brought a slate of her own, because archaeologists cannot help themselves — and I wanted nothing, which was its own kind of luxury. Wanting nothing. Lying in the shade of the wing while she swam and letting the island hold me without checking, every ninety seconds, whether it intended to.

At sunset we walked the strip together, end to end, six hundred meters each way. Her shoulder against mine. The tide coming in soft over the sand where my tires had pressed two sets of tracks that morning, already filling, already erasing, the sea keeping its own records the way it always had — patiently, completely, and without judgment.

"Next chart," she said, when we reached the windsock pole at the south end. "What's on it?"

"Ferry runs. Med supplies. The usual gossip-and-quinine circuit." I leaned against the pole. "And a new rule."

"Oh?"

"Land twice anywhere it matters." I turned to face her. The light was going gold and then rose behind her, and I thought about the woman who'd stood on a dock ten years ago refusing an invitation to stay the night because staying meant being seen, and being seen meant being billed, and I looked at this woman now — this woman who had read my confession, survived my confession, chosen me after my confession — and found that the dread was gone. Not suppressed. Gone. Evicted. The rooms it used to occupy had new tenants: her, the work, the mornings.

"So," I said. "Stay the night."

Ilsa smiled. "I thought you'd never ask."

"I asked ten years ago and got refused."

"Different woman," she said. "Different pilot. Same island." She hooked her little finger through mine and pulled me toward the camp, toward the tents, toward the small lit window of the life we'd built out of wreckage. "Come on, Crane. Wheels are stopped. Let's keep them that way."

And for the first time in my flying life, they were — and I found that I loved the sound of nothing at all.
