## 1. Dead Water Season

The river was dying the way old men die — slowly, and in places no one thought to look.

Kestrel Kavanagh stood at the wheel of the *Marrow*, one hand resting on the brass rail worn smooth by thirty years of other hands before hers, and read the water the way a priest reads scripture: for what it said, and for what it refused to say. The channel ahead should have run six feet deep this time of year. She could tell from the color — that bruised olive where the silt hung thick — that it ran three at best, maybe less around the bend where the current slackened and dropped its burden early.

She eased the throttle back until the engine's mutter dropped to something like a purr, though nothing about the *Marrow* purred anymore. The boat was forty-one years old, patched with welded plate and stubbornness, and she loved it with the fierce, unsentimental love you give a thing that has never once lied to you.

"Dead water season," she murmured to herself, because out here there was no one else to talk to, and a voice unused too long goes strange.

The Empty Quarter spread on either side of her like a held breath. To the south, the salt flats glared white under a sun that had already burned through the morning haze; to the north, the reed marshes went on forever, a green-gray ocean of them, whispering when the wind moved and silent when it didn't. Somewhere beyond all that — past the drowned timber, past the channels that appeared on no chart worth trusting — lay the river towns. Callow's Bend. Harrow Light. The Ferry at Two Sisters. Places she had not set foot in for six years, and did not intend to think about today, and thought about anyway, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

The *Marrow* drew four feet loaded. Three and a half if she stripped her. Kestrel knew every inch of her draft the way she knew the lines of her own palm, and she threaded the dying channel accordingly, cutting wide where the water lied shallow, hugging the cutbank where the current had scoured deep. A heron lifted off the mudflat ahead, unhurried, insulted. Good sign. Herons didn't waste their dignity on water that couldn't feed them.

An hour later she came up on Perdition Narrows — named, according to local legend, for a barge crew that had starved there waiting for rain, and according to Kestrel's own experience, for the simple reason that everything about it was a lie. The channel looked wide open. It wasn't. She killed the engine and let the boat drift, leaning over the rail to study the surface.

There. The faint seam in the water, a line as fine as a scar, where two currents met over the submerged wing dam some optimistic government crew had sunk back before anyone admitted the rivers were failing. She spun the wheel, feathered the throttle, and slid the *Marrow* through the gap with six inches to spare on either side, feeling the hull shiver once as it kissed the sand below — a touch, not a strike — and then they were through, into deeper water, and she allowed herself the small luxury of exhaling.

This was the part of the work she still loved without complication. Not the hauling, not the haggling, not the long nights anchored alone in the middle of nowhere with the mosquitoes singing hymns against the screens. This. Reading water. Passing through. Being, for a few hours at a time, exactly as competent as she pretended to be.

* * *

The settlement at Miller's Reach consisted of eleven buildings, a fuel tank, and a dog with strong opinions, and it materialized out of the reeds the way all these places did — suddenly, and as if it had been waiting to be caught. Kestrel backed the *Marrow* against the rickety dock while old Tomas Ferro limped down to meet her, already talking.

"—told the ministry we needed diesel and they sent us textbooks. Textbooks, Kestrel. What am I supposed to do, educate the generator?"

"You could try," she said, tossing him the mooring line. "It's got less sense than most of my students."

He grinned, gap-toothed, and got to work. Unloading took two hours: diesel, flour, batteries, antibiotics, a crate of roofing tin, and — buried under canvas so Tomas wouldn't see it and lecture her — a case of the good coffee she'd traded half her winter fuel margin for in Harrow Light. Some mercies you delivered quietly.

While Tomas signed the manifest, Kestrel sat on the dock rail and drank watered-down tea and listened to him talk, because listening was free and he needed it more than the diesel. The Reach was down to nineteen people. The fishery quota had been cut again. The ministry's new concession maps — the ones plastered on every bulletin board from here to the coast — showed this whole stretch of river zoned for "industrial extraction," which everyone understood to mean that soon the water itself would belong to somebody, and the somebodies were not the people who'd lived beside it for a hundred years.

"They say the aquifer's fine," Tomas said, spitting into the brown current. "Ministry survey says there's plenty. Plenty for the mines, anyway." He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only the tired arithmetic of a man counting what remained. "You believe those surveys, Kestrel?"

"I believe the river," she said. "The river says it's dying."

Tomas looked at her a moment too long, and she realized she'd said more than she meant to. That was the trouble with coming back to the towns, even this far to their edge. People remembered you. People expected things. Six years ago she had made herself a stranger on purpose, and strangers were safe — but every dock she tied up to chipped away at it, and someday there wouldn't be enough stranger left to hide behind.

She cast off before the feeling could settle.

* * *

Two days later, running light toward the supply depot at Gannet Junction, she found the ministry launch waiting for her at the confluence — a sleek gray patrol hull with an official pennant snapping in the hot wind, and standing on its deck a man in a linen suit that had no business within fifty miles of open water.

Kestrel considered, briefly, simply not stopping. But the launch carried a radio array worth more than her boat, and men in suits who waited two days at a confluence wanted something badly enough to make trouble, and trouble was bad for business. She brought the *Marrow* alongside.

"Captain Kavanagh." He said it like he'd practiced. He was young for the job — mid-thirties, sunburn just beginning to defeat his city pallor, eyes quick and pale as a pike's. "Deputy Undersecretary Corven. Ministry of Resources and Concession."

"That's a lot of words for one boat ride."

"It's a lot of ministry." He smiled without warmth and gestured at her deck. "May I?"

"You may stay right there. My deck's carrying cargo."

"What cargo?"

"The kind that doesn't answer questions from strange boats."

Something flickered across his face — irritation, quickly filed away. He produced a leather folder instead and held it up like a preacher with a psalm. "Then I'll be brief. Eleven days ago, a contracted survey team operating under Meridian Geohydrological failed to make a scheduled check-in. Seven personnel. Last known position is here—" he tapped the map inside the folder, "—the upper backcountry, past the salt marsh line. Their concession permits expire in twenty-three days. If the team can't be located and their final surveys recovered before expiration, the ministry forfeits the concession window entirely. Do you understand what that concession is worth?"

"I understand I don't care."

"Four hundred million, Captain." He said it flatly, letting the number do its work. "And the ministry is prepared to pay a finder's fee of sixty thousand credits for recovery of the team or their survey materials. Plus expenses. Plus a hazard bonus if conditions are — " he glanced at the reeds, the glare, the drowned country stretching north, " — difficult."

Sixty thousand. Kestrel kept her face still, but the number landed somewhere below her ribs and stayed there. Sixty thousand was a new engine. Sixty thousand was the *Marrow*'s hull replated stem to stern, the rust cut out of her bones. Sixty thousand was never having to take another cargo she didn't want, another season of watching the channels die and saying nothing.

"Why me?" she asked. "You've got patrol boats. You've got contractors."

"My patrol boats draw five feet." His pale eyes moved over the *Marrow* — the patched plates, the scarred pilothouse, the whole stubborn animal of her. "Your boat draws less. And your reputation suggests you know the backcountry channels better than any living pilot. Including, I'm told, the ones that aren't on our charts."

"And who told you that?"

"A ferryman at Callow's Bend. Odile Marchand. She said — and I quote — 'If Kestrel Kavanagh can't find them, they don't want to be found.'"

The name went through her like cold water through a cracked hull. Odile. Talking about her to ministry men. Recommending her, after six years of silence, after the way she'd left — no goodbye, no explanation, just an empty berth and a debt unpaid in every way that mattered. Kestrel stared at the man in the linen suit and felt the loneliness she'd spent six years building into something load-bearing shift, groan, and begin, very quietly, to fail.

"Twenty-three days," she said.

"Twenty-two, now. Time runs whether you're deciding or not."

She looked north, past the confluence, to where the mapped world gave out and the real one began — the dead channels, the salt marsh, the drowned timber standing in the shallows like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down and never gotten up. Nobody came back from up there in dead water season. Everybody knew that. It was practically a law.

But nobody had ever offered her sixty thousand to go, either. And the *Marrow* needed things Kestrel couldn't keep pretending she didn't need.

"Half up front," she said. "Deposited before I cast off. And I run my own boat, my own route, my own hours. You don't call me. I call you."

Corven smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "The ministry finds those terms acceptable."

Of course it does, she thought, watching him pass the folder across the gap between boats — the map inside marked with a red circle around coordinates in country so empty the cartographers hadn't bothered naming it. Nobody offers sixty thousand for a rescue. Rescues are hopes, and hopes are cheap.

Whatever the ministry had lost up there in the dying water, it wanted back badly enough to pay like it was afraid.

Kestrel turned the *Marrow*'s bow north, into the country that didn't want her, and opened the throttle until the reeds began to run.
