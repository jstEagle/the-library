## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The coordinates put her at the mouth of a channel that had no business holding water, and yet it did — a brown ribbon threading through reed beds gone silver with seed, the kind of water that looked solid until you tested it and found it was mostly memory. Kestrel brought the *Marrow* in slow, engine at half-voice, because channels like this one changed their minds overnight. What had been deep enough for the survey team's flat-bottomed skiffs three weeks ago might now be shin-deep over a sandbar that hadn't existed when the maps were drawn.

The river was doing its dying-season work. Every year the same ritual: the flood came down from the high country in spring, fat and arrogant, and by late summer the Empty Quarter's veins thinned to threads. The old pilots called it dead water season, and they said it the way farmers spoke of drought — not as an event but as a condition, something you endured rather than fixed. Kestrel had run these channels through eleven dead water seasons now. She knew their moods the way other people knew the faces of their children.

She did not know this channel's mood, though. That was the first wrongness.

* * *

She anchored the *Marrow* two hundred meters short of where the GPS insisted the camp should be, in a wide pool shaded by a leaning stand of paperbarks whose roots drank straight from the channel. The morning was already hot enough to make the air above the reeds shimmer like hammered tin. Cicadas sawed away in the paperbarks, a sound so constant it stopped being sound and became pressure.

Kestrel stood at the rail and read the bank the way she'd been taught to read it — not by her father, who had wanted her in a classroom, but by the river itself, which was a harsher instructor and never lied.

The bank told her things. It told her the water had dropped forty centimeters since the survey team arrived; there was a pale band of dried silt above the current line, crisp-edged, unweathered. It told her that cattle — or something cattle-sized — had come down to drink on the far side within the last day, hoofprints sharp as coin-stamps in the mud. It told her that nothing heavier than a wading bird had disturbed the near bank in at least four days, because the crust of mud there had curled and cracked into small plates, the way mud does when it dries undisturbed under sun.

Four days. The Meridian Survey Team had gone silent nineteen days ago. The ministry's official timeline said equipment failure, then weather, then — when neither explanation survived contact with anyone who knew the Quarter — nothing at all. Silence. The ministry was very good at silence.

Kestrel took the dinghy in alone, leaving the *Marrow* riding at anchor with her shotgun broken open on the chart table out of habit rather than expectation. The last hundred meters she poled, because the bottom here was soft as bread dough and an outboard would have stirred up a plume of silt visible from orbit. She wanted to arrive the way weather arrives: without announcement.

The camp announced itself anyway. Canvas caught the light through the trees — a pale rectangle of it, absurdly clean against the green-brown riot of the reed beds.

* * *

She beached the dinghy and stood for a long moment at the tree line, letting the place come to her before she went to it.

Six dome tents, guyed and staked, flysheets still rigged against rain that hadn't fallen in weeks. A mess fly strung between two paperbarks, folding tables beneath it, a gas stove sitting square and patient on its aluminum case. Solar panels angled on stands toward the northern sky. A generator under a tarp, fuel cans ranked beside it like soldiers at inspection. An antenna mast lashed to a sapling, wire still humming faintly in the breeze — or maybe that was her imagination, the way a dead man's phone seems to ring.

Everything intact. Everything orderly. And everything wrong.

Kestrel walked the perimeter first, boots quiet on the dried mud, because the perimeter always spoke first if you gave it time.

It spoke of haste. Not panic — haste. The kind of departure where people take what matters and leave what doesn't, moving fast but moving together, someone counting heads. Two tents had been abandoned mid-collapse, poles half-slid from their sleeves, as if struck in a hurry and not restaked. A mess kit lay on the ground beside the stove, cup still nested in bowl, spoon resting in the cup. A pair of reading glasses sat folded on top of a data logbook at the end of the nearest table, left there the way a person leaves glasses when they expect to come back in five minutes.

Nobody comes back in five minutes. Not from here.

She crouched at the fire pit. Cold ash, raked flat — deliberately, professionally, the way field teams kill their fires before moving camp. But the ash held no bone fragments, no foil scraps, no food waste. Field teams eat. Field teams burn their packaging. This fire had been used for heat and nothing else, and recently — the ash beneath the surface layer was still grey-black, not yet bleached by dew.

She moved to the food stores next, and this was where the dread began, the slow cold seep of it, starting somewhere behind her sternum.

Crates stacked under the mess fly, lids corded down. She cut one cord with her knife and lifted the lid. Tinned meat, sealed. Rice in rodent-proof drums, sealed. Water purification tablets, full bottles. A crate of fresh produce gone to rot — tomatoes collapsed into black lace, citrus shriveled to fists of leather — but the preserved stores untouched. Nineteen days of silence, and nobody had eaten the food.

People don't walk away from food. Not in the Empty Quarter. Not anywhere. Hunger is older than loyalty, older than fear, older than every order ever given at gunpoint. If six trained field personnel had abandoned this camp voluntarily, they'd have taken the rice at minimum. If they'd left in a hurry expecting rescue, they'd have eaten while they waited.

They hadn't eaten. Which meant they hadn't waited. Which meant they hadn't chosen.

Kestrel straightened slowly, and the cicada pressure seemed to press closer, and she became aware — fully, unpleasantly aware — of how loud her own breathing was in all that green silence.

* * *

She found the tracks an hour later, and they were the worst thing of all, because they made sense, and sense was the one thing the official story couldn't afford.

The camp sat on a low terrace above the channel. Behind it, the ground rose gently to a scrub ridge; ahead, it fell away through reeds to the water. The team's comings and goings were written in the mud of the terrace like a ledger: boot prints overlapping boot prints, weeks of them, going to the water, coming back, going to the instrument stations she could see staked out along the far bank — survey markers, little orange flags gone pink with sun.

And then, on the landward side, a single corridor of prints leading away toward the ridge. Six sets. All six walking abreast, evenly spaced, no staggering, no drag-marks, no scuffs of resistance. Walking, not running. And interspersed among them — this was the detail that made her crouch down and stay down for a long time — a seventh set of prints, heavier, deeper at the toe, in a pattern that paced alongside rather than led. Boots with a different tread. Contractor tread, the kind issued by the security firms that rode along on government surveys, the kind she'd seen on men in Callow's Bend who never took off their sunglasses.

One set of those heavy prints would mean an escort. But there were more than one set. She counted four distinct tread patterns, pacing the six, flanking them, and at one point — she measured it twice with her walking stick — the six civilian lines converged tightly together, shoulder to shoulder, boxed, while the contractor prints spread wide around them like a hand closing.

They had walked out of their own camp in formation, guarded by their own security detail, and they had not come back.

No tracks led toward the marshes proper — the deep country to the south where the ministry's search planes had spent two futile days looking. No tracks led to the water except the ordinary daily traffic of a working camp. Whatever direction those twelve feet had gone, it was inland, toward the ridge, toward the salt marsh beyond it, and the ground past the first hundred meters was baked hard as fired clay and kept its secrets.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the hardpan and looked south, at the haze-shimmered line where scrub gave way to marsh, and felt the dread finish its work and settle into something colder and more useful: certainty.

Equipment failure didn't march six people inland at gunpoint. Weather didn't post guards around a mess tent. Nothing in the ministry's story explained a spoon left in a cup, a full rice drum, and seven sets of footprints where six should have been.

The wind moved through the paperbarks, and somewhere down the channel the *Marrow* tugged at her anchor, impatient, faithful, the only thing in the world that had never lied to her. Kestrel thought about turning back. She thought about the contract money, about what a ministry inquiry would say if she reported exactly what she'd seen — *the camp was tidy, Minister, the food was untouched, they left in step* — and about how quickly such a report would disappear into a drawer, and how quickly the woman who filed it might follow.

Then she saw it. At the edge of the hardpan, half-scuffed, nearly erased by wind: a single print she hadn't counted. Smaller than the others. Shallow, dragged at the heel, the mark of someone exhausted being moved along by someone else's schedule. And pressed into the mud beside it, deliberate, unmistakable — a cartographer's habit, drilled into every survey hand — a small cross scratched with a compass point, the field symbol for a reference datum.

Someone had marked this spot. Someone had wanted whoever came after to know: *I was here. I walked. Count us.*

Kestrel looked from the cross to the southern haze, where the marshes waited with whatever the ridge was hiding, and understood that the missing team had not vanished.

They had been taken. And at least one of them was still alive to leave messages.

She turned back toward the channel, already calculating tide, fuel, daylight — and froze.

Downstream, faint but closing, came the sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: an outboard running fast and shallow, running her channel, running it with the particular arrogance of someone who believed no one else was on the water.

She killed her lantern, faded back into the paperbark shade, and watched the bend where the stranger would appear.
