## 3. The Man in the Cutbank

The channel narrowed to a throat of clay and dead reeds, and Kestrel took it at half-throttle, the *Marrow's Grace* shouldering through water the color of old tea. Three days past the abandoned camp, following a thread of logic no map supported — that whoever had walked out of that camp would have walked toward water, because everything in the Empty Quarter eventually walked toward water — she had been reading the banks like a woman reading handwriting. Game trails. Broken sedge. The particular way a frightened man leaves sign: not scattered, but *aimed*.

The cutbank appeared on her port side an hour before noon, a twelve-foot wall of stratified earth where the river had gnawed into the high ground and left its work exposed like a cross-section of the world. She almost missed him. Almost.

There was a wrongness to the shadow at the bank's base. Shadows didn't have boots.

Kestrel killed the engine and let the boat drift, hand drifting to the rifle racked beside the wheel. The current carried her in slow, silent rotation, and with each degree of turn the shadow resolved into a man — or what was left of one. He was half-buried, deliberately so, mud packed over his legs and hips up to the waist, his back against the cutbank as if the earth itself had been asked to hold him. His face was a mask of cracked clay except for two eyes, fever-bright, tracking her boat with the fixed intensity of a man who had decided that everything moving was either salvation or death and could no longer tell the difference.

She brought the *Grace* alongside with the tenderness of long habit, dropped anchor, and stood at the rail with the rifle held low but visible.

"Don't come up here," the man said. His voice was ruined, sanded down to a rasp. "Don't— I'll break it. I'll break it before you take it."

"Take what?"

His arm moved, stiff as a rusted hinge, and Kestrel saw the case: a hard-shell waterproof survey case, orange once, now the color of the mud he'd packed himself into, strapped across his chest by its carry harness. He held it the way a drowning man holds a spar. The way a mother holds a child.

"I'm not here for your case," she said. "I'm here because six people walked out of a camp three days from here and nobody's found them."

Something flickered behind the fever in his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or the last flicker of a mind still doing arithmetic.

"You're the pilot," he rasped. "Kavanagh. They said — they said if anyone came, it would be you. Because you're the only one stupid enough to run the dying channels." A laugh came out of him then, dry as seed husks, terrible to hear. "Stupid enough. God. *Blessed*, rather."

"What's your name?"

"Voss. Elias Voss. Cartographer, Meridian Survey Team." He recited it like a man giving testimony, which, in a way, he was. "Number seven of seven. The only number left."

Kestrel looked at him a long moment. Half-buried men did not survive three days in the Empty Quarter unless the land had helped them, and the land did not help people by accident. He had chosen this bank, chosen this mud, chosen to bury himself to the waist where the river damp would keep his core temperature down and the bank would hide him from anything passing on the water. That was not delirium. That was discipline wearing delirium's face.

"Can you walk?" she asked.

"I can be *carried*," he said, with a ghost of dignity. "There's a difference. I've earned the difference."

* * *

He weighed nothing. That was the first thing she noticed as she hauled him up the bank and over the gunwale — the lightness of a man who had eaten his rations in reverse order, saving the best for a tomorrow that kept refusing to arrive. She laid him on the deck plates in the shade of the pilothouse, cut the harness free of the case only after he'd made her promise — twice, three times, his cracked hands gripping her wrist with surprising strength — that the case would stay within his sight at all times.

Then she fed him. Water first, in small measures, then broth from the thermos, then nothing more for an hour, because she'd seen starving men kill themselves with kindness before. He drank like a man remembering how. And somewhere in the middle of the second cup, with the fever still burning in his cheeks but his voice steadier, Elias Voss began to talk.

Not all at once. In pieces. The way survivors always talked, as if the story were cargo too heavy to lift in one pass.

"We finished the aquifer transects on the fourteenth day," he said, staring at the canopy overhead as though the data were written there. "Deep soundings. The ministry contract called for verification of existing surveys — confirmation work, they told us. Routine." His mouth twisted. "I ran the numbers myself. Three times. Four. Because the numbers were wrong. Because they couldn't be right."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong in the direction of *money*." He turned his head and looked at her, and his eyes were clear now, horribly clear. "The concession permits were sold on the promise of proven water. Deep, renewable aquifer under the eastern quarter — enough to justify every permit, every lease, every settlement displacement. But there is no aquifer. Not there. Not at depth. What the original surveys showed was a perched water table — seasonal, shallow, already committed to the river system. The ministry knew. The falsification wasn't an error. It was *architecture*. The permits are built on water that doesn't exist, and in nineteen days they vest, and everyone who signed them becomes rich on a lie, and every town downstream of the draw becomes a casualty on paper that hasn't been printed yet."

Kestrel kept her hands on the wheel, though they weren't moving. The river murmured against the hull. Somewhere a heron complained.

"That's a large claim to make from a hole in a riverbank," she said.

"It's a large claim to make from four hundred pages of instrument records." He tapped the case between them without looking at it, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "I'm the cartographer, Captain. I don't interpret. I *draw what is*. And what is, is a fraud worth more than every boat on this river put together."

"And the others? Your team? Where did they walk?"

The clarity went out of him then, like a lamp turned down. He was quiet for a long time, and when he spoke again his voice had gone flat, rehearsed, the tone of a man who had given this testimony to the dark every night since it happened.

"On the fifteenth day, Rennick — our security lead, Meridian's own contractor man — told us we were being extracted early. Route change. Through the salt marsh instead of back down the channels. He said there was a threat assessment. He said it with a list of names in his hand and four other men with rifles standing behind him, and he smiled while he said it, which is how I knew." Voss swallowed. "They marched us in single file. Marsh country, hip-deep, no landmarks. And at a certain point — I know the point, I've mapped it in my head a thousand times — they separated us. Took the others ahead. Told me to stay with the instruments, that I'd follow with the second group."

"But there was no second group."

"There was no second group." He laughed again, that awful dry laugh. "I waited six hours in the marsh. Then I went looking. I found—" His hands closed on empty air. "I found signs. Signs I won't describe to you yet, because you'll want to turn this boat around, and I need you to keep going forward. I buried myself in that cutbank because Rennick's men came back for me. Twice. I heard the airboats. I heard them calling my name in a friendly voice, Captain. *Friendly.* Like a lure."

Kestrel looked at the man on her deck — the cracked mud, the strapped case, the eyes that had seen whatever lived in the salt marsh — and felt the cold arithmetic of it settle into her bones. Six people had walked into the marshes escorted by their own protection. One had crawled out. And someone was paying airboat crews to call his name in a friendly voice.

She thought about the ministry official in his pressed shirt, offering her triple rate to find the vanished team, his smile exactly as warm as Rennick's must have been.

"They sent me to find you," she said quietly. "Did you know that?"

Voss's head came up. For a moment he looked at her the way a man looks at a door he'd believed was a wall.

"Then you have a choice," he said. "Same choice I made in the marsh, the night I stopped waiting for the second group. You can deliver me to the people who hired you, collect your pay, and never think about what's in this case. Or you can be the second person alive who knows what those pages say, and spend the rest of your life looking over your shoulder." His voice cracked, steadied. "I won't pretend it's an easy arithmetic. I did mine in the dark, in the mud, with the airboats circling. Mine came out to this." He touched the case again. "It comes out to this or it comes out to nothing."

Kestrel stood at the wheel a long moment, the engine idling beneath her feet like a held breath. Ahead, the channel bent toward open water, toward the ministry, toward the easy money and the pressed-shirt smile. Behind her lay the salt marsh, and whatever Voss refused to describe.

She swung the wheel hard over, away from both.

"Not to the ministry," she said. "Not yet. First you're going to eat something that isn't broth, sleep twelve hours, and then you're going to show me every page in that case. Because if I'm going to be the second person who knows, I'm going to *understand* it. All of it."

Voss stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to laugh — really laugh this time, ragged and disbelieving, the laugh of a man whose arithmetic had just changed.

"Captain," he said, "you should know something. Rennick's boats aren't searching the marsh anymore."

"How do you know?"

"Because yesterday morning," Elias Voss said, "they started searching *downriver*."
