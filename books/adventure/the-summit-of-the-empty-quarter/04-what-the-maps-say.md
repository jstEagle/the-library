## 4. What the Maps Say

Elias Voss had not slept in four days, and the not-sleeping had begun to speak to him. It spoke in the voices of his teammates — Priya's dry laugh, Okonkwo humming under his breath while he calibrated the gravimeter, young Ferro asking for the fifth time whether they'd be home by permit season. The voices came out of the reeds and off the water and from inside the aluminum case he held against his chest like a second heart.

Kestrel Kavanagh had given him tea in a tin cup, bitter and over-steeped, and he drank it because his hands shook too badly to refuse it gracefully. She had pulled him half-dead from the cutbank that morning, and now her boat sat anchored in a slough so narrow the willows brushed both gunwales at once, a green throat of water where no patrol boat would think to look. He should have felt safe. Instead he felt exposed, because safety was a thing that had been taken from him along with everything else, and what remained was the case, and the case was heavy in a way that had nothing to do with its weight.

"You going to tell me what's in it," Kestrel said, "or do I have to keep pretending I didn't carry you two miles on my back?"

He looked at her across the small cabin. Her face was weathered into something like driftwood, all planes and patience, and her eyes were the flat gray-green of river water over sand — eyes that gave nothing away until they decided to. He understood, in the way of a man who had spent three years making maps of places that did not want to be known, that she was reading him the way she read current: by what moved beneath the surface.

"The ministry sent us to lie," he said. "And then it sent men to make sure we couldn't stop lying."

* * *

The case opened with a corroded click that sounded, to Voss, like a lock turning on a cell door. Inside, nested in oilcloth, were the survey folios: eleven mylar sheets, hand-annotated, the ink still sharp where he'd pressed hard enough to score the film. Aquifer contours. Piezometric surfaces. Drawdown curves from thirty-one monitoring wells. The real numbers, the ones he had measured himself with instruments the ministry had certified and then, he understood now, never intended to honor.

He laid them out on Kestrel's chart table, weighting the corners with tin cups and a brass compass, and watched her face as she read them. She read slowly, the way careful people do, tracing contour lines with one finger.

"Explain it to me like I'm a ferry pilot," she said, "because that's what I am."

Voss explained. The Empty Quarter's concession blocks — the vast tracts sold for agricultural development, for lithium extraction, for the new irrigation colonies the ministry advertised on every broadcast band — had been priced against water surveys. Those surveys, filed under the Meridian program's official seal, showed sustainable aquifer yields: deep fossil water, replenishing reserves, decades of drawdown capacity. Enough, the ministry's brochures said, to green a desert.

The truth was in the mylar. The aquifer was dying. The piezometric surface had dropped nine meters in six years across the eastern blocks. The recharge estimates in the filed reports assumed an inflow that Voss's own well data proved did not exist — the monitoring stations nearest the recharge zones had been quietly decommissioned two years before the permits were signed, their gauges pulled, their records sealed. What remained were stations placed where the water was deepest and slowest, and their readings had been extrapolated outward like a man measuring a lake from its deepest point and declaring the whole country flooded.

"They sold water that isn't there," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question. She had piloted the shrinking channels for years; she had watched sloughs become mudflats become cracked pans. She had called it drought, called it season, called it the land being the land. Now she was looking at the arithmetic of it, drawn in her own river's handwriting.

"They sold it knowing," Voss said. "That's the part you have to hold. The falsification wasn't error. The decommissioned wells prove intent. You don't remove the gauges that would contradict you unless you already know what they'll say."

His voice cracked on the word *intent*, and he hated himself for it, because he had rehearsed this explanation a hundred times in the marsh dark and it always came out steady there.

* * *

Kestrel asked the question he had been dreading. Not *how* — he could answer how until his voice wore out. She asked *when*.

"Nineteen days," he said. "The concession permits expire on the solstice. There's a renewal tribunal sitting in the capital — administrative, routine, a rubber stamp in any normal year. But the tribunal charter has a clause. Independent evidence of material misrepresentation in the underlying surveys suspends renewal pending inquiry. If the evidence reaches the tribunal before expiration, every permit in the eastern blocks freezes. If it doesn't reach them — if we're late, or dead, or the folios are 'lost in transit' — the permits renew automatically. Another twenty-five years. And the aquifer dies inside ten."

Nineteen days. He said it the way a man says a sentence handed down. Then he did the arithmetic aloud, because saying numbers made them feel less like teeth: three days downriver if the channels held, which they wouldn't; margin for repairs, for evasion, for the contractor's boats; margin for the fact that the tribunal clerk of record was a ministry appointment, and any submission routed through official channels would arrive pre-opened, pre-read, and pre-burned.

"So we don't use official channels," Kestrel said.

"We can't. That's why they let me live." He stopped. He hadn't meant to say that part yet. But it came out, the way the worst things do, all at once. "They kept me alive because a dead cartographer is a martyr and a loose end is a problem, but a broken witness who recants is a victory. They want me found. They want me standing in front of a camera saying I was delirious, that the data was preliminary, that I misread my own instruments. Every day I stay alive out here is a day they spend deciding I'm worth more recanting than buried."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. "And are you?"

"Worth more recanting?" He almost laughed. "I don't know. Ask me again when I've been hungry longer."

But that wasn't what she'd asked, quite, and he knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third passenger.

* * *

Night came down through the willows in long violet stripes. Kestrel banked her stove and the cabin filled with the smell of woodsmoke and river rot, and Voss lay on the bench berth staring at the overhead where condensation gathered and fell in single drops, each one landing with the sound of a clock.

He thought about the team. He thought about it constantly, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Priya had argued with the security detail the last morning — he had heard her through the tent canvas, low and furious, asking why the exfil route had changed without a radio log entry. Okonkwo had been the one who noticed the decommissioned wells first, who'd pulled the archived gauge serials on a hunch and gone quiet for a full day afterward, quiet the way men go quiet when they've seen the shape of a thing they can't yet say. Ferro had just turned twenty-four. He'd kept a photo of a girl in a coastal town taped inside his instrument lid, and he'd asked Voss once, shyly, whether the maps they made would matter.

They had walked into the marshes at gunpoint. Voss had been separated — sent ahead to "secure the instruments," which he now understood meant they wanted the folios' author isolated from the men who might have died defending him. When the shots came, distant, muffled by reed and water, he had told himself they were the contractor's crews blasting stumps. He had told himself that for two days. Two days of telling yourself a lie is enough to build a life on, if the alternative is building nothing.

The guilt was not the sharp kind. It was the settling kind, like silt. He had made the maps. His hands had drawn the false contours onto the official copies — not lies, exactly, but omissions dressed in his own drafting style, his own lettering, so that the forgery wore his signature like a stolen coat. The ministry hadn't needed to fake anything. They had needed only to take his honest work and file it beside his dishonest work and let the honest sheets lend the dishonest ones credibility. He had been the instrument of his own instrument's corruption.

And now the true folios — the ones he'd hidden in the case, the ones he'd crawled into a cutbank to protect, letting the marsh drink half his body weight and most of his mind — those sheets were the only things standing between nineteen days and twenty-five years.

Some nights he thought the case should burn. That the cleanest thing, the kindest thing to the dead, would be silence — that Priya and Okonkwo and Ferro had died for paper, and paper was a poor grave-goods for people like that. Other nights he knew the opposite with equal force: that they had died *because* of paper, because of what the paper proved, and the only monument that would mean anything was the paper arriving.

He did not know which nights were right. He suspected he never would. That was the weight of knowledge — not that it set you free, but that it made you responsible for choosing, every day, what to do with it, and it never chose for you.

* * *

Toward midnight, Kestrel came off watch and found him awake. She poured herself tea and sat across from him without speaking for a while, which he was learning was her way of talking.

"You drew the false maps," she said finally. Not cruelly. Just as fact.

"I drafted them. Under direction. With the understanding that the correction folios would follow." He paused. "The corrections never followed."

"And now you're carrying the corrections."

"I'm carrying the apology."

She nodded slowly, and something in her face shifted — not softening exactly, but a recognition, one tired animal acknowledging another. Later he would learn about her brother, about the reason she ran the empty channels instead of the town docks, but tonight he only saw that she knew what it was to owe the dead a debt denominated in truth.

"Nineteen days," she said. "Then we move at first light, and we move like ghosts. No radio traffic, no registered fuel purchases, no towns bigger than a fuel dock." She looked at the folios weighted on the table, corners fluttering faintly in the stove draft, as if the sheets themselves were restless. "Where does the tribunal sit?"

"Capital. But the clerk of record is theirs." He hesitated, then said the thing that had been forming in him all evening, the plan within the plan. "There's another route. Not official. The tribunal charter requires public notice of any suspension petition — posted, published, entered into the record by any citizen of record. Not a ministry channel. A person."

"A person," Kestrel repeated.

"Someone the record can't lose. Someone whose name carries weight in the river districts." He was thinking, though he didn't say it yet, of the settlements downstream, of the ferry licenses and water rights that hung on the same falsified data, of towns that had been sold the same lie in smaller denominations. "The evidence doesn't have to reach the tribunal. It has to reach *people*. The tribunal follows."

Kestrel was quiet for a long time. Outside, the slough breathed — water moving somewhere in the dark, the willows combing the wind, a fish rising with a sound like a coin dropped in a well.

"That's a harder road than the capital," she said at last. "People are slower than courthouses. And some of them can be bought."

"Some of them already were," Voss said. "That's what the maps say."

She stood, snuffed the lamp to a coal-glow, and went forward to check the anchor line. Voss lay back down and listened to the drops fall from the overhead, each one a tick of the nineteen days, and for the first time since the marsh he did not hear his teammates' voices in them. He heard something worse and better: the sound of the work remaining.

At dawn the wind backed around to the southeast and carried with it, faint but unmistakable, the high whine of an outboard running wide open — far off, maybe ten miles, maybe fifteen, but wrong for the hour and wrong for the channel, and coming from upriver.
