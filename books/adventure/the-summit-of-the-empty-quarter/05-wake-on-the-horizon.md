## 5. Wake on the Horizon

Kestrel heard it before she saw anything, because on the river you always did. Sound traveled strange over water at dawn — it flattened, it carried, it lied about distance — but there was no lying in that whine. A two-stroke running flat-out, no throttle modulation, no fisherman's patience. Somebody was going somewhere fast and didn't care who knew.

She stood in the bow with her coffee cooling in one hand and counted.

Ten seconds of engine, then a gap as some bend or levee swallowed it. Then again, closer. She timed the gaps against the channel she knew lay upriver — the old Meridian cut, the long slough past the drowned orchards — and did the arithmetic twice to be sure she wasn't scaring herself.

She wasn't. It was coming downriver at thirty miles an hour, maybe better, and it would be on them inside the hour.

"Voss." She didn't raise her voice. On a boat you learned that low carried further than loud. "Get up."

He came aft wrapped in the blanket, hair mashed sideways, eyes still swollen from sleep and from everything else. He looked at her face and whatever he found there made him stop asking questions he'd been about to ask. He turned his head toward the sound instead, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she had, slower, worse, and arrive at the same number.

"That's not a supply run," he said.

"No."

"Meridian's security flew contractor colors. Halcyon Group. Grey hulls, blue waterline stripe." He said it flatly, reciting, the way a man recites the names of people who used to be alive. "They had two fast boats based out of the ministry dock at Corvane. If they've put both on the water—"

"Then I only have to outrun one today." She was already moving, kicking the anchor loose, cranking the winch while the coffee went over the side. "Get below. Strap the case to your chest. Not beside you — *on* you. If we take water, you swim with it or you don't swim at all."

He disappeared into the cabin and she took the wheel, thumbing the starter until the old diesel caught with its usual complaint, a sound like a sick animal being asked to work. The *Marrow* — forty years of patched hull and rebuilt engine, named by a man she'd stopped being able to talk to — groaned off the mud shelf and swung her nose downstream.

Downstream. That was the problem. The patrol boat was coming *down*river too, which meant every mile Kestrel ran bought nothing. She could make twelve knots with the current helping and a fair wind; the grey boats could make twenty-five against anything short of a flood. In open channel this wasn't a chase. It was an appointment.

So she wouldn't give them open channel.

The river forked three miles below the anchorage where the survey team's last coordinates had gone dead — the main stem swinging wide and honest to the east, and to the west a mouth so choked with willow and deadfall that no chart printed in the last twenty years bothered marking it. The Sallow Cut. Her father had called it the graveyard of good intentions, and then he'd taken her through it a hundred times until she could run it blindfolded, until she knew which snags were permanent and which moved after every spring flood, which channels had silted shut since last season and which had opened like wounds.

No chart marked it because charts were for people who needed them.

"Kestrel." Voss stood in the cabin door, the black case strapped across his chest like a bandolier, face pale. "What are you doing?"

"What they won't expect." She eased the throttle forward. "Hold onto something that's bolted down. Everything else is negotiable."

* * *

The mouth of the Sallow took the *Marrow*'s paint the way a knife takes skin.

Willow branches raked both sides of the cabin at once, and something under the water — a sunken barge rib, maybe, or just the river's own bone — kissed the hull along the port strake with a long grinding shudder that Kestrel felt come up through her boots and into her teeth. She held the wheel steady through it. Flinching was how boats died here. You committed or you backed out, and backing out meant the open channel and the grey boat, and the grey boat meant men with contracts and quotas and no reason at all to leave witnesses.

"Port," she said, mostly to herself, and hauled the wheel over. The channel bent around a drowned cottonwood whose crown still broke the surface, black and skeletal, hung with drifting mats of weed. Past it the water shallowed to the color of strong tea, and she could read the bottom the way other people read print: the darker veins were the deep runs, the pale fans were sandbars building, the sudden olive smears were weed beds that would wrap a propeller like a fist.

Behind them, faint now, muffled by miles of green, the whine went on. Searching. It would find the fork soon. It would guess wrong first — everyone guessed wrong first, they took the wide water, the water that looked like a road — and that wrong guess would buy her twenty minutes, and twenty minutes in the Sallow was worth two hours anywhere else.

And God help her, some traitorous part of her chest had opened like a window.

That was the thing nobody understood who hadn't done it — the joy of it, the filthy bright joy of running dead water. Every bend was a question only she could answer. The river threw its whole vocabulary at her — snag, shoal, cutbank, blind slough — and she answered in kind without thinking, hands and wheel and throttle moving ahead of thought, the *Marrow* leaning under her like a horse that finally had a rider worth the name. Out here she wasn't a woman who ate alone and slept alone and hadn't spoken to Odile Marchand in six years except across a fuel counter. Out here she was the best thing on the water, and the water knew it, and for those minutes the loneliness that lived in her like ballast went quiet.

"There," Voss said suddenly, pointing starboard. "Was that—"

"Duck."

The branch came through where his head had been and stripped bark down the cabin roof with a shriek. He crouched, white-knuckled on the case strap, and when the noise passed he said, very quietly, "You've done this before."

"I grew up doing this." She threaded the channel between two snags close enough to touch both. "My father ran cargo through here when the ministry still pretended these channels existed. Then they redrew the maps, sold the water rights, and let the willows have it back. Officially, none of this is navigable."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it's mine."

She heard him almost laugh — a single dry note, surprised out of him — and thought: there. That's the first true thing you've given me besides the maps.

* * *

It couldn't last, and it didn't.

An hour in, past the drowned orchard where the trees stood in rows like mourners at their own funeral, the channel narrowed to a throat barely wider than the *Marrow*'s beam, and Kestrel saw the change in the water before she understood it. The current had shifted since the spring flood. Where she remembered the deep run hugging the east bank, the tea-colored surface now slid hard to the west, and sliding with it, just under, was the pale signature of fresh sand pushed across the old channel floor.

New bar. This season's. No chart in existence knew about it, including the one in her head.

She had half a second to choose: swing wide into water she couldn't read, or trust the dark vein still visible along the east bank and thread it.

She trusted it. It was what she did. It was who she was.

The *Marrow* hit the bar anyway.

Not head-on — her angle was good, her luck held just enough for that — but the hull climbed the sand shoulder with a scream of displaced water and ground to a shuddering halt, listing hard to port, engine racing free in the sudden absence of load. Willow leaves rained down through the cabin windows. Somewhere below the deck plates, metal said something final and quiet to itself.

"No," Kestrel said, to the boat, to the river, to herself. "No, no, *no*—"

She threw the wheel hard over, gunned it, reversed, gunned it again, working the helm the way you worried a stuck tooth, and the *Marrow* rocked, slid a foot, two feet, and then came off the bar with a lurch that threw Voss against the bulkhead and sent the case clacking against his ribs. They were moving. Shallow, dragging, wrong — she could feel the propeller kissing sand on every revolution, a stutter in the heartbeat of the boat — but moving.

"How bad?" Voss shouted over the engine.

"We'll find out when we stop!" Which was a lie, because she already knew. She'd felt the hit travel the hull like a word spoken through a wall. Something was sprung. Maybe just a seam weeping, maybe worse, and there was no yard, no welder, no dry dock within eighty miles of water that any sane person sailed.

The joy was gone now, wrung out of her like water from a rag, and what it left behind was colder and more useful: arithmetic. The Sallow had bought her distance but cost her hull. The grey boat was still behind her somewhere, patient, burning contractor money by the hour. And ahead — she pulled the mental chart of the region apart and laid it flat — ahead lay the marshes, the salt flats, the place where the survey team had walked into the reeds and not walked out, and beyond the marsh, the only town left between here and the tribunal route: Callow's Bend.

Callow's Bend. Where Odile Marchand still ran the ferry and the boathouse and, if six years hadn't changed her, the longest memory on the river.

Kestrel had spent years making sure she needed no one. The river had agreed to the arrangement. Now the river was renegotiating.

She throttled back to a crawl where the channel widened, dropped into the cabin, and lifted the deck plate over the bilge. Water stood in the well — not flooding, not yet, but an inch of it that hadn't been there yesterday, seeping slow and steady around a seam aft of the engine bed, glinting like something alive.

Voss crouched beside her. He looked at the water, then at her, and she watched him understand it all at once: that the case on his chest was only as safe as the boat under it, and the boat was bleeding.

"How long does she have?" he asked.

"She has as long as I say she does." Kestrel slammed the plate home and stood, wiping her hands on her thighs. "We pump, we run easy, we make Callow's Bend by tomorrow night. After that—" She stopped. The words she needed next were words she hadn't said aloud in six years, and they tasted like rust. "After that I ask somebody for help. Somebody I swore I'd never ask."

Through the willows, far behind them, the whine of the outboard rose once, fell away, and rose again — closer than before, and no longer guessing wrong.

Voss heard it too. He tightened the case strap across his chest and said, "How fast can this old girl go?"

"Not fast enough," Kestrel said, and gave the *Marrow* everything she had left.
