## 6. The Toll of Salt Marsh

The marsh did not welcome them. It absorbed them.

Kestrel went first, breaking trail through the samphire with a machete she'd taken from the survey camp's abandoned kit, each swing releasing a breath of brine and rot that hung in the windless air like a held note. Behind her, Voss moved in her footprints, the waterproof case strapped to his chest the way a man straps on armor, or a wound. The ground underfoot was a lie in layers — a crust of white salt, then black mud, then water the color of old tea, then nothing at all. Twice she tested a hummock with the machete's flat and watched it sink without a ripple, swallowed whole.

"Stay where I step," she said, for the fourth time. "The marsh keeps what it takes."

"I know," Voss said. "I've seen."

She didn't ask what he meant. She had a suspicion, and the suspicion had been growing since the cutbank, since his delirium, since the words *at gunpoint* — and she wanted one more mile of not knowing.

The Empty Quarter's backcountry had flooded in the wet years and never fully drained, and what remained was a country of drowned trees and salt pans and channels that wandered like drunkards. The sky was a lid of pewter, low enough to touch. Their boat lay beached and holed in a dead channel two miles back, hull stove against a submerged fence post she'd never seen, and the contractor's patrol boat had been behind them when it happened — close enough that Kestrel had heard the engine note change, the hunting note. Overland was the only direction left. Overland, through the one place on her charts marked with a single inked word in her own handwriting, years old: *avoid.*

They walked until the light began to fail, and then they walked into the light's failing, because stopping felt worse than moving. The salt crusted Voss's boots white to the knee. Kestrel's shoulders burned. Somewhere to the north, a long way off, an engine coughed and died, coughed again — the patrol boat working the channels, patient as a heron.

It was Voss who found them. Or it was the crows that found them, and Voss who followed the crows.

They rose off a stand of dead melaleucas in a ragged black sheet, complaining, and Kestrel knew before she knew — the way she knew a sandbar under brown water, the way she knew a channel was dying before the charts admitted it. Birds like that meant meat. Birds like that, in country this empty, meant something that had been meat for a long time.

"No," Voss said. He had stopped walking. "Kestrel. No."

"We have to know."

"I already know."

"Then you have to *see* it," she said, and hated the cruelty of it, and meant it anyway, because she had learned the hard way what not-seeing did to a person. It grew in you like salt in a wall. It cracked you open years later, in some quiet hour, without warning.

They went toward the trees.

* * *

There were four of them.

The marsh had kept them, in its way — the salt and the tannin water had dried them to something like driftwood, and the melaleucas had grown up through and around them as if the trees had decided to hold what the men had dropped. Two were wired to stakes, wrists bound with surveyor's flagging tape, the fluorescent orange gone pale as bone. One lay face-down in a salt pan, arms spread in the shape of the last thing he'd tried to do. The fourth was Meridian's team lead — Kestrel knew him from the ministry dossier, Halvorsen, the photograph of a square-jawed man with a ministry lapel pin — and Halvorsen had a single bullet hole centered in his chest, fired from close enough that there was no argument about it, no accident, no misadventure. Close enough to be a conversation.

Beside him, half-buried in the crust, lay a satellite phone with its casing pried open and its guts stamped into the mud, and a ministry field ledger in a zip-lock bag, and Kestrel knelt and did not let herself think about what she was doing and took the ledger.

The entries stopped eleven days before the team's last logged transmission. The last legible line was in a different hand, hurried, the pen pressed hard enough to tear the page: *Water data falsified at source. R. ordered us to sign the revised surveys. We refused. They took our comms at dusk. If anyone reads this, the numbers Voss kept are the real ones.*

"R," Kestrel said quietly. "Reyes." The security contractor's site commander. The name from the dossier, the man whose company now flew its colors on a patrol boat upriver.

Voss had not come all the way in. He stood at the edge of the trees with his arms hanging and his mouth open, and Kestrel watched him look at the wired men, the two who had been made to stand while — she didn't finish the thought; the marsh finished it for her, in the way the flagging tape was tied, in the way the stakes had been driven. She watched him look, and she watched eleven days of running and hunger and purpose come off him all at once, like a coat dropping.

He went down on his knees in the salt. The sound he made was not a word. It was the sound a hull makes when it opens — not loud, not dramatic, just the sudden structural truth that the thing that held you together has failed.

Kestrel let him. She stood over him with the ledger in her hand and the crows resettling in the dead trees above, and she let him break, because she had seen what happened to men who were not allowed to break. She'd been one.

* * *

They made a cold camp that night on a shell ridge above the high-water line, the only ground in sight that the marsh could not reach. Kestrel built a fire the size of two cupped hands, screened by driftwood, and heated water for the ration packs, and Voss sat with his back against a bleached log and stared at the flames and said nothing for a long time.

"I mapped this place," he said finally. "That was my job. I stood on the survey lines and I put this marsh on paper. Grid squares. Elevation contours." His laugh had no air in it. "I never once thought about what it would be like to be a grid square."

"They were executed," Kestrel said. No point softening it; softening was a currency the marsh didn't accept. "Reyes's people walked them out here and shot them, and the ministry will call it a drowning. A survey team lost in flooded backcountry. Tragic. Unforeseeable." She turned the ledger in her hands. "Same story they always tell. The river did it. The weather did it. Nobody did it."

"You talk like you've heard it before."

The fire cracked. Out on the black water something large moved and subsided, a fish or a memory.

"My brother," Kestrel said.

Voss looked up.

"Teodor. Teddy. He ran supplies out of Callow's Bend — lighters, barges, whatever floated. Fourteen years ago the ministry announced a levee project upriver, big money, and Teddy was one of the pilots who signed on to haul materials." She kept her voice flat, the way you keep a boat flat in a following sea; any tilt and you swamp. "There was a night crossing. His barge went down with two others in a channel the ministry's charts said was dredged and marked. It wasn't. It had silted to eighteen inches. The project engineers knew — there were soundings, there were memos, Teddy's deckhand survived and swore the channel markers had been *moved*, pulled and reset to hide the shoaling, because moving the dredging budget into someone's pocket was cheaper than dredging."

"What happened?"

"The inquiry happened. Six weeks, closed doors, findings sealed. Pilot error, they said. Excessive speed in poor visibility. The deckhand's testimony was ruled unreliable — he'd been drinking, they said, though everyone on the river knew Teddy didn't allow a drop underway." She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "My mother went to the tribunal offices every week for a year. Then she stopped. Not because she believed them. Because believing them was the only way to stop drowning."

"And you?"

"And I stopped going out on the water with anyone from the towns. Then I stopped going into the towns. Then I just — ran the dead channels and the dying ones, ferrying freight to people who didn't ask questions, and I told myself that was independence." She looked at him across the small fire. "It wasn't. It was silence with a salary."

Voss was quiet. The marsh breathed around them, that vast tidal respiration, in and out.

"Your case," Kestrel said. "The aquifer surveys. The falsified data. Nineteen days until the permits expire, and then it goes to a tribunal — and I've seen what a ministry tribunal does with the truth, Voss. It seals it. It buries it deeper than the marsh buried Halvorsen. You could hand those maps to the tribunal personally and in five years some other pilot would run her boat over a channel that killed someone, because the numbers were falsified and the findings were sealed and nobody did it."

"Then what?" His voice was raw. "What's the option? You've seen the patrol boat. We can't outrun it to the capital. We can't fight it."

"No," Kestrel said. "But we don't have to take the truth to the people who buried it. We take it to the people it was buried *from*."

She let that sit. Downriver, a day's hard run if the channels allowed it, lay Callow's Bend — the town she had left, the town that had watched her leave, the town where Odile Marchand still ran the ferry and still, as far as Kestrel knew, remembered her name. Every settlement on the river had a radio. Every settlement had people whose water was promised away on falsified surveys, whose channels were dying while the permits printed money for men in the capital.

"You're talking about going back," Voss said. "To the towns."

"I'm talking about coming home," Kestrel said, and the words came out of her before she could inspect them, and once they were out she found she could not take them back, and did not want to. "There's a ferryman in Callow's Bend who owes me nothing and will help us anyway. There are boatyards there, and my hull needs more than I can give it on a salt flat. And there are a hundred radios between here and the gorge, and not one of them belongs to the ministry."

Voss pulled the case off his chest and set it on his knees, and for the first time since the cutbank his hands were still.

"The contractor will have agents in the towns by now," he said. "Bounties. They'll turn people."

"People turned on Teddy too, at first. Everyone wants to believe the official story — it's cheaper." Kestrel looked out at the dark water, at the drowned country where four men stood wired to stakes in the melaleucas, keeping their terrible appointment with the tide. "But I've been silent for fourteen years, Voss. I know exactly what silence costs. I've been paying it in installments my whole life."

She stood, and stamped the fire dead, and shouldered her pack.

"Sleep," she said. "We move at first light. Callow's Bend by tomorrow dusk, if the marsh lets us out."

Far to the north, faint across the black water, an engine started up — and this time it did not die again. It held its note, steady and patient, and it was pointed downriver.
