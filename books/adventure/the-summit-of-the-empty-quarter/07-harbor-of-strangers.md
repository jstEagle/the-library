## 7. Harbor of Strangers

Callow's Bend announced itself the way it always had: by smell before sight. Woodsmoke and tar and the green rot of the reed beds, and under it all the river's own breath — cold clay and old fish. Kestrel cut the engine a half-mile out and let the current carry them in on slack water, because an idling engine could be heard for three bends in either direction, and she had no way of knowing yet whose ears were listening.

The town sat on its bluff like something that had grown there rather than been built. Stilt houses leaning over the flood line, drying nets strung between pilings like bunting at a funeral, the ferry landing with its boards gone silver-gray from forty years of boots. Beyond it, the church spire — or what passed for one, a repurposed dredge mast with a bell hung in its crown. She had run this approach ten thousand times as a girl, standing at her father's elbow while he worked the wheel, and the sight of it now went through her like a splinter working toward the heart.

"You're slowing down," Voss said from the wheelhouse doorway. He had washed the worst of the marsh off himself at the last creek mouth, but the marsh was still in him — in the gray of his lips, the way his hands wouldn't quite close around the rail. "Why are we slowing down?"

"Because I don't know what's waiting."

* * *

She saw it from two hundred yards: the boat tied up at the public dock, sleek and wrong, all fiberglass and outboard chrome in a harbor of patched timber. A contractor's launch, flying no colors at all, which was its own kind of flag. Two men stood on the dock planking talking to a knot of townsfolk, and even at that distance Kestrel could read the geometry of the conversation — the way the locals stood with their weight back, arms crossed, the way one of the strangers gestured with a paper that caught the light.

A bounty notice. She'd have bet her remaining fuel it had her face on it, and Voss's beside hers, and a number underneath generous enough to make honest people do arithmetic they didn't want to do.

"There," she said, and swung the bow toward the old channel behind Tanner's Island, where the water shallowed over a bar of silt and nobody with a new boat would risk their propeller. The *Maren* drew less than anything afloat on this river; she had been built for exactly these waters, in these waters, by a man who is dead. Kestrel grounded her gently on the soft mud beneath the willows, fifty yards of reed screen between them and the world, and killed the engine for good.

Voss looked at the case strapped across his chest, then at her. "Now what?"

"Now we walk into town like we belong here." She said it lightly, and heard how false it sounded. "One of us does, anyway."

* * *

The back way into Callow's Bend ran along the drainage ditch behind Marchand's boathouse, and Kestrel took it without thinking, her feet remembering the plank bridges and the places where the boards had rotted since — one gave under her heel and she stepped off it without breaking stride, the way you step over your own history.

Odile Marchand was mending a net in the boathouse doorway, and when she looked up, her hands stopped moving.

For a long moment neither woman said anything. The river talked instead, slapping at the pilings, patient as it had always been. Odile had gone gray at the temples since Kestrel had last seen her — six years? seven? — but her eyes were the same, dark and level and very hard to lie to.

"Well," Odile said finally. "The prodigal captain. You look like something the river coughed up."

"That's about what happened."

Odile's gaze moved past her to Voss, hovering in the shadow of the ditch bank, clutching his waterproof case the way drowning men clutch timber. Her eyes narrowed, and Kestrel watched her do the arithmetic — the stranger, the case, the contractor's launch at the public dock, the notice Kestrel would have wagered on. Odile Marchand had ferried this river for thirty years; she missed nothing that floated on it.

"Inside," she said. "Both of you. Before Marta Ellery sees you, because Marta would sell her own teeth if the price was right, and she's been standing at that dock all morning counting strangers' money."

* * *

The boathouse smelled of pitch and diesel and winter-stored rope, and it was warm, and Kestrel hadn't realized until she was inside it how cold she'd been. Odile put tea in front of them without asking whether they wanted it, which was its own kind of welcome, and then sat down across the workbench and looked at Kestrel with an expression that was equal parts affection and reckoning.

"Six years," Odile said. "Not a letter. Not so much as a supply order through my dock. And now you come up my back ditch with a wanted man and a story, I'm guessing, that ends badly."

"It might not end badly."

"Everything ends badly or it doesn't end. That's river wisdom, and you know it better than me." But Odile's voice had softened at the edges, the way it always did when she was losing an argument with herself. She turned to Voss. "You're the cartographer. The one from the Meridian team. Half the dock heard the agents say your name this morning."

Voss's hand tightened on the case. "Then they know I'm alive."

"They know someone's paying five thousand shares for you and the woman who's harboring you." Odile said it flatly, watching Kestrel take the blow. "Five thousand. Kess, that's a new hull for every family on this bend. That's diesel through two winters. That's the school roof fixed and the dredge running again. There are good people here who are going to stand at their windows tonight doing that math, and some of them are going to arrive at an answer they'll hate themselves for. And some won't hate themselves at all."

Kestrel looked down at her tea. The surface trembled faintly — her hands, not the floor. "I shouldn't have come here."

"No," Odile agreed. "You shouldn't have. But you did, and that's the interesting part." She leaned forward. "You could have run for the delta. You could have hidden in the marshes till the permits lapsed and slipped away rich in nothing but your life. Instead you came to the one place on this river where people know your face and know you left them. Why?"

And there it was, the question Kestrel had been rowing away from for six years, sitting in the middle of the workbench between them like a landed fish still gasping.

Because her brother's name was on a ministry memorial plaque in the Callow's Bend chapel, and the plaque was a lie, and everyone in this town had stood at his funeral and believed it, and she had been the only one who knew — knew what really happened on the upper channels that season, knew what the survey reports had buried along with him — and she had said nothing. Had taken her boat and her silence and gone downstream, telling herself the wilderness was cleaner than people, that the river never asked her to choose between truth and survival.

"I came home," she said, and her voice cracked on the word like ice on a thawing channel. "I don't know why else you'd call it."

Odile studied her for a long time. Then she stood, went to the corner cabinet, and brought out a bottle of plum brandy that Kestrel recognized — her father's brand, the label faded, the bottle kept against a day that had apparently arrived.

"We'll need to move you at dark," Odile said, pouring three measures. "My cousin Renny has a skiff and no love for the ministry. There's a slip under the old cannery where the *Maren*'s damage can be patched without being seen — I've got oakum and a man named Fitch who owes me more than five thousand shares, though I'll want to ask him first, because asking is what loyalty looks like when it's still alive." She set the glass in front of Kestrel. "But hear me plain, Kestrel Kavanagh. Loyalty here is a currency running thin. The contractors spent big this morning. Every hour you're under my roof, I'm spending too. So whatever you're carrying in that case, whatever it's worth — decide soon what it's for. Because if it's just for staying alive, you're spending other people's lives to buy it, and I won't help with that."

Kestrel lifted the glass. Through the boathouse wall, muffled by tarred planks, she could hear the town bell ringing the evening hour, the same bell that had rung at her brother's funeral, and beyond it the river going dark.

"It's for the truth," she said. "All of it. To everyone. Even the parts that shame me."

Odile held her eyes a moment longer, then raised her own glass. "Then welcome home," she said, "for whatever that's worth — and it might be worth more than you think."

It was Voss who broke the moment, quietly, from the shadows by the door where he'd been keeping watch on the ditch. "Kestrel," he said. "There's a light moving along the back channel. Two lights. They're not running navigation patterns."

Odile was already blowing out the lamp.
