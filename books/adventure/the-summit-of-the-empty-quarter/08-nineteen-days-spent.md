## 8. Nineteen Days, Spent

The smoke woke him before the shouting did.

Elias Voss came up out of a shallow sleep with his lungs full of it — resin and creosote and something sweeter underneath, the smell of tarred planking surrendering to flame. For a moment, half-dreaming, he was back in the survey camp, back in the last hour of the world as it had been, when Meridian's tents burned and Halloran's men stood in a ring around the fire with their rifles held easy, the way men hold tools they have already decided to use. Then Odile Marchand's hand closed on his shoulder, iron-hard despite her years, and she said, "Not the house. The boathouse. Get up, cartographer. Get up and watch what your maps cost."

He watched.

The boathouse went up like a struck match, all dry timber and forty years of stored pitch, and the heat of it reached them across the channel, pressing against their faces like a palm. Sparks climbed into the dark in slow, deliberate spirals, and the river below caught them — each one a brief red eye on the black water, opening and closing, opening and closing. Odile's ferry, the *Mireille*, was tied at the far dock, and Kestrel had already run down there through the reeds; Voss could hear her voice carrying over the fire's roar, flat and controlled, calling for buckets, calling for the *Mireille* to be cut loose if the wind turned. The wind did not turn. The contractor's people had chosen their night for it, chosen it the way Voss had once chosen survey lines: with care, with patience, with an eye to exactly how much damage was required and not one plank more.

That was the message in it. Not Odile's home, not the *Mireille*, not Kestrel's life — not yet. Only the boathouse, the empty shell of it, the place where a stranger had been hidden. *We know where you sleep. We know whose kindness shelters you. Next time we will not be so precise.*

"They came by water," Odile said beside him. Her voice had no grief in it yet, only a terrible economy, the way a woman sounds when she is counting. "Two boats, no running lights. Old Perrin saw them go back downriver before the fire was even climbing. They wanted to be seen leaving. That was the point."

Voss said nothing. He stood with the waterproof case strapped across his chest the way he had slept with it every night since the cutbank, since Kestrel had fished him out of the mud like a drowned thing, and he felt the maps against his sternum — nineteen days of paper, nineteen days of other people's signatures, the whole weight of the Empty Quarter's stolen water pressed against his heart. The fire painted his face orange and his shadow long behind him, and he thought: *they burned a building to reach me, and I am still standing here, still breathing, still carrying it.* The thought should have been a comfort. It was not. It was an invoice, and he knew whose name was written at the top.

Behind them, in the light of the burning boathouse, the people of Callow's Bend had begun to gather on the bank — a dozen silhouettes, then twenty, silent, watching. Nobody spoke to Odile. Nobody spoke to Voss. They simply stood, and their standing was its own kind of sentence, and Voss could not read whether it was mercy or verdict.

* * *

By dawn the fire was a black ribcage of char against the grey sky, and the permit clock had shrunk to eleven days.

They gathered in Odile's kitchen, which smelled of woodsmoke that would not wash out of the plaster, and Kestrel spread the tribunal route across the table like a patient she already knew was dying. Voss watched her finger trace the river down from Callow's Bend — past the salt marsh, past the drowned channels where they had buried the others, down to the ministry's river station at Verlac, where the concession tribunal would convene when the permits came due.

"Eleven days," Kestrel said. "The *Mireille* makes Verlac in six if the channels hold. We dock, we walk the case into the tribunal clerk's office, we're done." She said it the way she said everything — flat, certain, a woman reading water she had already crossed a hundred times. But her eyes came up and found his, and Voss understood that she had laid the route out not because she believed in it but because she needed him to be the one who killed it.

So he killed it.

"There's a name on the tribunal roster," he said. "Deputy Registrar Anselm Corve. He signed the falsified water surveys — not the forgeries themselves, but the certifications. His initials are on the second page of every document in this case. When the ministry realized the survey data was gone, Corve was promoted. Do you understand? The man who certified the lie was *rewarded*, and now he sits at the tribunal that will rule on the permits those lies sold." He laid the case on the table, opened it, and turned the pages with hands that had learned, over the last weeks, to be steady. "We walk this into Verlac, and Corve receives it. He has eleven days to lose it, burn it, or rule it inadmissible on a procedural technicality no one will ever be able to appeal. We would be handing the evidence to the arsonist and asking him to testify against himself."

The kitchen was quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull argued with the morning. Odile poured coffee none of them asked for, and the sound of it — the thin brown ribbon, the clink of the cup — was the loudest thing in the room.

"Then it's dead," Kestrel said. "Every official road."

"Every road with a door at the end of it," Voss said. And then, hearing himself, hearing the shape of the thought complete itself in his mouth for the first time, he said the rest. "So we don't use a door."

* * *

He had been a cartographer for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned one law above all others: a map is an argument. Every line drawn is a line someone wanted drawn; every blank space is a choice about what deserves to be known. The ministry had understood this before he did. That was the whole crime, in the end — not the falsified surveys themselves, but the argument they made, the beautiful confident maps showing deep, reliable water under the concession tracts, water that did not exist, sold to investors who would drain what little remained and leave the river towns sucking mud. Voss had drawn the base maps those lies were printed over. His linework. His hand.

He stood at the window of Odile's kitchen and looked out at the river, and the audacity came up in him not like courage but like fever — the particular heat of a man who has nothing left to lose and has finally, finally noticed it.

"Every settlement on this river has a radio," he said. "Every one. The ministry gave them out after the floods, eight years back — emergency band, fixed frequency, no encryption because the towns were supposed to be grateful and too poor to matter. I've seen the sets. I repaired two of them myself, in Meridian's camps, from parts." He turned from the window. "There are forty-one settlements between here and the delta. Forty-one radios, all listening to the same band, all in kitchens and boathouses and ferry landings. If the evidence reaches one transmitter with enough power, it reaches all of them at once. Not a tribunal. Not a clerk. The people whose water it is."

Kestrel had gone very still. "One transmitter with enough power," she repeated. "There isn't one. The settlements run five watts. You'd be whispering in a hurricane."

"The delta relay station," Odile said.

Voss turned. The old ferrywoman stood at her stove with her back to them, and when she spoke again she did not turn around. "Ministry relay. Hundred-watt transmitter, maybe more. Sits on the last bluff above the delta, rebroadcasts the shipping forecasts and the ministry's price bulletins to the whole lower river. My husband helped build it, before he understood what it was for." A pause, and the fire ticked in the stove. "It will be guarded."

"It will be guarded," Voss agreed.

"And it sits below the gorge," Kestrel said, and now her voice had changed, and Voss heard in it something he had heard only once before — the day she had run the dead channels with the contractor's boat behind them, that fierce low note of a woman about to ask the river for something unreasonable. "The gorge. At the head of the delta. Which means to reach the relay before the permits expire, we don't go around the gorge."

"We go through it," Voss said.

"Through it," Kestrel said, "at flood."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and the preparation had the quality of a vigil.

Kestrel stripped the *Mireille* to her bones — every crate, every fitting, every ounce the old ferry did not strictly need to float — while Odile moved through the town doing something Voss did not fully understand until he saw its results: by evening, three families had left bread and salt pork on Odile's dock without a word. A boy of perhaps fourteen appeared with a coil of new rope and would not take payment. The blacksmith came at dusk and, without asking what it was for, welded a steel plate over the *Mireille*'s bow where the dead channels had torn her hull. Loyalty here was a currency running thin, Odile had said. But it was not yet spent, and Voss watched it being counted out in bread and rope and steel, quietly, by people who knew what the fire in the night had meant and chose, anyway, to stand on the side of the burned.

He sat on the dock as the light failed and copied the aquifer surveys by hand — two complete sets now, one to transmit, one to hide in a place even Corve's reach could not burn. Kestrel came and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a long time neither spoke. The river moved past them in the dark, patient, indifferent, carrying the last charred flecks of Odile's boathouse out toward the sea.

"My brother's name was Tam," Kestrel said at last, to the water, not to him. "He died in a cover-up smaller than yours, and I spent nine years telling myself that silence was the price of surviving it." She turned her head, and in the last light her eyes were the grey-green of the channels at flood. "I'm done paying it. Eleven days. We make the gorge, we take the relay, and we put your maps into every kitchen on this river. And when the contractor comes — and he will come —"

"He'll come," Voss said.

"— then he can explain to forty-one towns why the water maps were lies," Kestrel said. "Let him chase the truth down the river. The truth has a head start now."

Far upriver, past the black line of the marshes, the first edge of the storm front showed itself — a smothered flicker of heat lightning, silent, repeated, like something enormous counting under its breath. Kestrel watched it the way a gambler watches dice.

"Three days," she said. "The flood peak rides in ahead of the front. If we run the gorge, we run it at the top of the spate, with every channel in the Quarter pouring through that cut at once." She stood, and pulled him up with her, and her grip was iron. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we rig the *Mireille* for a river nobody survives."

Voss lay down that night with the case against his chest and the smell of smoke in the walls, and for the first time since Halloran's men had walked his team into the marshes, he did not dream of the dead. He dreamed of water — a single voice on a single frequency, going out into the dark, and forty-one radios waking, one by one, along the whole length of the river.
