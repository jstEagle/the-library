## 9. The Running of the Flood

The storm came down the valley like a debt being collected.

Kestrel felt it before she saw it — a pressure in her back teeth, a smell of iron and bruised vegetation rolling off the high country — and she stood at the wheel of the *Marrow* with her hands light on the spokes and let the knowledge settle into her the way silt settles into still water. The gorge would be running. Not running. *Raging.* Every tributary from the western escarpment would be dumping its whole season into the narrows at once, and the passage that boatmen called the Throat would be less a channel than a churning mile of white violence between walls too close to swing an oar.

Every pilot on the river had a story about someone who'd tried it at spate. None of the stories ended well enough to be told twice.

Behind her, in the wheelhouse's cramped aft corner, Elias Voss crouched over the radio rig she'd salvaged from Odile's burned boathouse, soldering the last connection by lamplight while rain began to tick against the glass like fingernails. He looked terrible. Three days without real sleep had carved hollows under his eyes, and his hands shook — though whether from exhaustion or fear, even he probably couldn't have said.

"Tell me again," Kestrel said, not turning from the window, "why we're doing this in the middle of a storm."

"Because the tribunal courier route is compromised, and because Halvard's boats are behind us, and because—" Voss exhaled. "Because the storm grounds their aircraft. It doesn't ground us. That's the whole argument, isn't it? It's insane for them *and* for us, but more for them."

"It's insane for us," Kestrel corrected. "For them it's merely stupid."

She checked the engine gauges out of habit rather than need. The *Marrow* was answering as well as she ever had — better, since Kestrel had spent two sleepless nights in Callow's Bend with Odile's wrenches, repacking bearings and patching the hull damage from the dead channels. The old boat was tired but true, the way old things were when you loved them honestly. Which was exactly why it hurt to know what she was about to ask of her.

The radio crackled. Voss leaned in, adjusted a dial, and suddenly the wheelhouse filled with voices — dozens of them, overlapping, the whole braided chorus of the river: the net-menders at Two Herons, the fuel dock at Callow's Bend, the lonely repeater station up at Salt Lick that nobody had maintained in years. All of them carrying the same signal now, because Voss had wired the transmission to rebroadcast through every open frequency, and all of them carrying his voice reading the aquifer numbers into the rain.

"—concession block seven, stated yield four hundred million liters annually, surveyed yield sixty-one million—"

"They're hearing it," Voss said softly. "They're actually hearing it."

"Some are." Kestrel watched the gorge mouth open ahead of them, black rock swallowing grey water. "Some radios are off. Some people won't believe it. And some will believe it and be too scared to say so out loud." She turned the wheel a degree. "That's why the maps go to the tribunal too. Voices fade. Paper burns slower."

"And if we don't make the tribunal?"

"Then we made the river towns listen," she said. "And maybe that's worth the run."

* * *

The Throat announced itself as sound first — a deep, continuous roar, like the earth grinding its teeth — and then as spray, hanging in the air above the gorge mouth in a permanent grey veil. Kestrel brought the *Marrow* down to a crawl at the eddy line just outside it and looked at what she was about to do.

At low water the passage was a winding slot between sandstone walls, tricky but honest, the kind of water a careful pilot could read like handwriting. At spate it was none of those things. The river had climbed twelve feet up the rock and turned the slot into a single boiling chute, waves standing eight feet tall in places, whirlpools opening and closing like mouths, and somewhere in the middle of it all — she could see it even from here — the Old Man, the submerged ledge that had killed three boats in her lifetime, its crest just breaking the surface now in a long line of white foam that looked almost gentle.

Almost gentle. That was the river's oldest lie.

"You can still put me ashore," Voss said behind her. "Run the rest alone. I'm cargo, Kestrel. Cargo shouldn't get a vote, but I'm giving you one anyway."

"If I put you ashore, Halvard's men find you inside a day, and everything dies with you." She rolled her shoulders, felt the old familiar click of tension in her neck, and let it go. "Besides, I need your hands on the throttle. When I say, you give me everything the engine has. No matter what you see. *Especially* no matter what you see."

He swallowed. Nodded. Put both hands on the brass lever like a man gripping a railing over a long drop.

Kestrel took one last look upstream. Far back through the rain, where the channel bent around Harker's Point, she caught it: a white wake, moving fast, and above it the hard geometric silhouette of a contractor patrol boat pushing through weather it should have been sheltering from. Halvard hadn't waited for the storm to pass either. Of course he hadn't. Men who bought rivers didn't respect them.

"They're coming," she said.

"Then they can watch," said Voss.

"No." Kestrel set her jaw, and something in her chest went very quiet and very clear, the way it did only in the worst moments, when there was nothing left to do but the impossible thing. "Then they can follow."

She swung the *Marrow*'s bow into the current and gave her the river.

* * *

The first wave hit like a building falling on them.

Green water came over the bow and smashed across the wheelhouse windows, and for two full seconds Kestrel was blind, steering by feel and memory and the thrum of the hull beneath her boots. Then the spray tore away and the gorge was there, walls streaming, water heaving, and she was reading it — reading it the way she'd read it in dreams for twenty years, the way you could only read water you'd grown up inside of.

Left of the boil line. Ride the seam. The seam was the thread of smoother water that every rapid hid somewhere in its chaos, the river's own intended path, and finding it at spate was part skill and part prayer. She found it. The *Marrow* lurched, surged, found her stride — and then the river took the choice away and simply *carried* them, six knots becoming ten becoming fifteen, the walls blurring past, spray sheeting off the rocks like smoke.

"The Old Man!" Voss shouted. Ahead, the foam line grew, and beneath it Kestrel could feel the ledge in her bones, the way the current stacked and doubled over itself just upstream of it. There was a tongue — there was always a tongue — a narrow lane of green water sliding over the shallow rock where a boat with speed and nerve could plane across instead of tripping into the hole below.

It was thirty feet wide, maybe less, and it was moving sideways in the chop.

"Hold on to something," she said, and drove straight at it.

The world became noise. Water over the deck, water in her eyes, the engine screaming as Voss shoved the throttle to the stop, the hull slapping down off one wave and slamming into the next so hard the china in the galley shattered below them. The foam line rose up — and then the *Marrow* did the thing Kestrel had asked everything of her and no boat should survive: she planed. One long, shrieking, weightless second with the Old Man's drowned granite a hand's breadth under her keel, and then the tail wave caught them, flipped the stern skyward, and threw them down the chute into the gorge's deepest chamber.

Where the whirlpool waited.

Kestrel saw it late — a black rotating pit off the port quarter, twenty feet across, spinning with a slow terrible patience — and the river, having carried them this far, decided to show them what indifference looked like. The stern swung. The whirlpool took hold of the hull the way a hand closes around a wrist, and the *Marrow* began, gently, horribly, to turn.

"Engine!" Kestrel screamed. "Full astern to starboard — *now* —"

Voss slammed the lever. The propeller bit backward against the rotation, and the boat shuddered, fought, held — hung there on the edge of the pit with water pouring over the gunwale in sheets — and then the whirlpool pulsed, breathed, weakened the way they did between pulses, and Kestrel threw her whole weight against the wheel and the *Marrow* slid free, wallowing, half-flooded, alive.

They shot out of the gorge mouth into the wide brown flood of the lower river like a cork from a bottle.

* * *

Behind them, the sound.

Not the river's sound. A different sound — metal on stone, a long tearing crunch that rolled up the gorge walls and came back twice. Kestrel spun the wheel just enough to glance astern, and through the rain she saw it: the patrol boat, halfway through the Throat, riding too high and too fast in water it didn't understand, broaching sideways on the Old Man's tongue. The river folded it once, neatly, the way a man folds a letter. Then the whirlpool took what was left.

Nobody surfaced. Kestrel watched for five seconds, ten, hating herself for watching, unable not to. The gorge gave nothing back. It never had.

"They knew the odds," Voss said quietly. He had come to stand beside her at the wheel, soaked, shaking, his knuckles white on the brass rail. "They chose anyway. Same as us."

"Same as us," Kestrel echoed, and didn't believe it, and let it go.

She was still letting it go when the *Marrow* shuddered — a deep structural groan from below the waterline, the sound of a hull saying its last honest word. The Old Man hadn't missed entirely. Somewhere along her keel, in that one weightless screaming second, the granite had kissed her, and now the river was coming in through the wound.

"How long?" Voss asked.

Kestrel looked at the shoreline ahead — the long gravel bar below Miller's Landing, half a mile, no more — and then at the water rising in the bilge, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nine years old in her father's wheelhouse.

"Half a mile," she said. "We'll make it. We won't make much past it."

* * *

The *Marrow* died the way good boats die: slowly enough to save what mattered, fast enough to break your heart.

Kestrel ran her aground on the gravel bar with the last of her momentum, and the moment the keel touched bottom the river began pulling her apart, peeling planking, prying seams, claiming her plank by plank with the patient thoroughness of a creditor. They waded ashore through waist-deep flood with the waterproof case strapped to Voss's chest and Kestrel's arms locked around him when the current tried to take his legs, and they collapsed on the high shingle just as the storm broke open overhead and the rain came down warm and enormous, drumming on the gravel like applause.

Voss lay on his back, gasping, laughing — or crying; in the rain it was impossible to tell. "You got us through," he said. "Through the *Throat*. At spate. Nobody's going to believe—"

"Nobody needs to believe it," Kestrel said. "They just need to hear the numbers."

But when she turned to look back at the wreck, at the *Marrow* settling into the shallows with her decks awash and her name already going under, something in her chest cracked open that she'd kept boarded shut for twenty years. Twenty years of self-exile, of sleeping alone on the water because the towns had nothing left for her, of telling herself the river was enough company — and here was the proof that it never had been. The river took everything eventually. It had taken her brother. Now it had taken her boat. The only thing it had ever given back was the truth about what mattered.

Voss sat up, wrung out his sleeves, and nodded downstream toward the lights beginning to kindle through the rain — Miller's Landing, then Callow's Bend beyond it, the whole string of river towns waking to the broadcast still crackling from every radio on the water.

"Tribunal office is in Callow's Bend," he said. "Two days' walk, if the ferries aren't running."

Kestrel looked at the lights a long moment. Then she stood, shouldered the case, and started walking.

"Odile will have heard it by now," she said. "Come on. Let's go home."
