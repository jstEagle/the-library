## 10. Where the Water Remembers

The news came upriver the way everything did in the Empty Quarter — slowly, then all at once, carried on the current and the wind and the crackle of a hundred cheap radios tuned to the same frequency.

Kestrel heard it first from Odile's kitchen, three days after the flood, while she sat with her hands wrapped around a mug of chicory coffee that had gone cold without her noticing. The radio on the shelf above the stove hissed through static, and then a voice — a woman's voice, rough-edged, familiar from a hundred dockside greetings — read out the tribunal's interim order: *all concession permits within the Meridian survey corridor suspended pending full inquiry into falsified water data. All permit holders to cease extraction operations immediately.*

The voice paused, and Kestrel could hear it smiling.

*For those just joining us, that means the drilling rigs stop. That means the pumps stop. That means the men who sold our river by the acre are going to have to explain themselves to a room full of people who drink from it.*

Odile turned the dial down a notch, not off, just enough to let the silence breathe. She was a broad woman with forearms like mooring line and gray shot through her black hair like silt in floodwater, and she had not stopped moving since the night she'd dragged Kestrel and Voss out of the shallows below the gorge. Now she stood at her counter with a paring knife and a basket of green tomatoes, and she did not look up when she spoke.

"You haven't been outside yet today."

"I've been outside."

"Past the porch." The knife clicked against the cutting board. "There's people on the dock, Kess. Been there since dawn. They're not leaving."

Kestrel looked into her cup. Her hands were still wrong — the burns from the wreck had scabbed over into ridges of shiny pink, and her left ring finger didn't bend all the way anymore, and probably never would. Small prices. She'd made a list of small prices somewhere in the delirium of the second night, when the fever was on her: one boat, two fingers' worth of grip, a scar across her scalp where the hull beam had caught her. Against nineteen days and a river full of towns that would still be drinking poisoned water if she'd stayed quiet, it was a ledger she could live with.

"What do they want?"

"To see you." Odile set the knife down. "To see that you're real. Half of them think you died in the gorge. Other half thinks you never existed at all, that Voss made you up out of whole cloth." She wiped her hands on her apron and finally turned, and her eyes were the flat brown of the river in low water, hard to read and impossible to fool. "They brought things. Food, mostly. A crate of engine parts somebody's been hoarding since before the war. There's a boy out there who's been asking every hour if the captain of the *Marrow* needs anything."

Kestrel's throat tightened around nothing. "The *Marrow*'s at the bottom of the gorge."

"I know what's at the bottom of the gorge. So does he. That's not the point, and you know it's not the point."

She did know. That was the worst of it. For eleven years she had kept herself apart from these towns — running supplies, taking payment, never staying for the festivals, never learning more names than commerce required. Eleven years since her brother Tomas had drowned in an official lie about a levee inspection, since she'd stood in a meeting hall in Callow's Bend and heard men with ministry letterheads explain that the water level data had been *within acceptable parameters*, and decided that belonging to people was just another way of losing them. She'd told herself the wilderness was honest. The wilderness took everything but it never lied about wanting to.

And now there were strangers on a dock with engine parts and casseroles, and the lie she'd built her whole exile on was dissolving faster than the permits had.

"They'll want to talk," she said.

"They'll want to *listen*. Mostly." Odile picked up her basket. "Voss is already out there. Been talking since sunup. Man found his voice somewhere in that marsh and hasn't put it down since." A pause, gentler than anything Odile usually allowed herself. "Kess. You don't have to go out there today. But you should know they're not going anywhere. Neither are you."

* * *

She went out at dusk, when the heat broke and the mosquitoes rose off the slack water in silver columns. The dock at Callow's Bend was old pilings and newer planks, patched by forty years of hands that never wrote their names on anything, and it was crowded now in a way Kestrel hadn't seen since the fish runs of her childhood. Maybe sixty people. They'd strung lanterns, and the light swung on the gentle chop, and every face that turned toward her held something she had spent a decade avoiding.

Not gratitude. Gratitude she could have handled; gratitude has edges, it ends. This was something wider. This was recognition.

An old man stepped forward first — Halvard Oss, who'd run the chandlery when Kestrel was a girl buying tar and rope with coins earned gutting catfish. His hands shook, but his voice didn't.

"We heard the broadcast," he said. "All of it. Every settlement from the Gorge to the Salt Gate heard it. My granddaughter copied the aquifer numbers onto paper and nailed copies to the church door and the co-op wall and the ferry office, so nobody could say they didn't know." He cleared his throat. "Nineteen days ago I'd have taken contractor money to turn in a stranger. I'll say that plain, because it's true. But we heard what those maps said. We heard what they did to your surveyor's people." Murmurs ran the length of the dock like a current. "You ran the flood for us. Nobody asked you to run the flood."

"The maps did that," Kestrel said. "I just drove the boat."

Halvard almost smiled. "That's what a riverman says. It's never true."

They pressed things into her hands as she moved down the dock — a jar of honey, a coil of new rope, a child's drawing of a boat with a woman at the wheel rendered in furious crayon. The boy Odile had mentioned turned out to be maybe ten, gap-toothed, named Pell, and he had a question he'd clearly rehearsed: whether the *Marrow* could be raised. Kestrel crouched to his level, joints protesting, and told him the truth — that the gorge kept what it took, that boats were wood and steel and always temporary — and watched him absorb it with the terrible resilience of children, and then ask what she'd drive next.

"I don't know," she said, and found she meant it as hope rather than grief.

* * *

Voss found her at the end of the dock, past the lanterns, where the light gave out and the river breathed its slow dark breathing. He'd filled out some since the cutbank; Odile's cooking was doing what Kestrel's emergency rations couldn't. But he still carried himself like a man listening for footsteps, and he still slept with the empty waterproof case near his hand, though the maps inside it had long since become a hundred copies scattered beyond any ministry's reach.

"The tribunal sits in six weeks," he said, settling beside her on the planks. "In the capital. They've subpoenaed me. Subpoenaed *you*, actually — I saw the list. 'Kestrel Kavanagh, master of the vessel *Marrow*, material witness.'" He glanced sideways. "Congratulations. You're officially a person again."

"That sounds like paperwork."

"It's a great deal of paperwork. And a train, or a boat, and a city full of lawyers who've never smelled salt marsh." He was quiet a moment. "The security contractor's dissolved itself overnight. New name, same offices, but the men who walked my team into the marsh — four of them are in custody. The other three are unaccounted for." He said it evenly, but Kestrel heard the wire underneath. "The marsh keeps what it takes too, sometimes. Or they ran. Either way."

"And the ministry?"

"Ministries don't fall, Kestrel. They shed. Deputies will resign, a director will retire to somewhere with good fishing, and the machinery will grind on. But the permits are dead, and the aquifer stays in the ground, and the towns get to decide what happens to their own water." He looked out at the black current. "That's not nothing. I spent nineteen days thinking it might be nothing. It isn't."

Downstream, far off, thunder muttered — the tail of the season's last front, heading out toward the Salt Gate. The river would rise a few inches tonight and fall again by morning, and next week the dead-water season would end and the channels would fill, and boats would move again along routes that had been mud flats a month ago. The water remembered everything and held nothing. She'd learned that twice now, once as a girl and once in the gorge, and both times it had cost her nearly everything she thought she was.

"Odile made me an offer," Kestrel said.

"I heard. Half the dock heard. She announced it over the radio like a proclamation." Voss smiled faintly. "A berth behind the boathouse, and first pick of timber for a new hull. There's a man up at Two Rivers with seasoned oak he's been saving for thirty years, waiting for a project worth it. He says you can have it if you take his grandson on as an apprentice."

"A captain doesn't build her own boat."

"A captain builds her own boat," Voss said, "when the boat is the town's idea and not just hers. That's the difference. That's the whole difference." He stood, brushing splinters from his trousers, and offered her the thing he'd carried since the cutbank — the case, empty now, dented, salt-stained. "I won't need this where I'm going. And it seems wrong for it to end up in a drawer. It ought to hold something going forward. Charts, maybe. Charts of channels that exist because people keep them open."

Kestrel took it. It weighed almost nothing, which was another way of saying it weighed exactly what it had ever weighed.

"When do you leave for the capital?"

"Thursday. Odile's cousin's boat." He hesitated, then: "Come with me. You're subpoenaed anyway. We could—"

"No." She said it gently, and meant it gently. "I'll testify. I'll be there. But I'm done running toward the places that make the lies. Somebody has to stay where the truth has to live." She turned the case over in her burned hands. "Besides. I've got oak coming from Two Rivers, and an apprentice, and a boathouse to help rebuild. Busy season."

Voss laughed — really laughed, the first time she'd heard it sound uncomplicated — and gripped her shoulder once, hard, and went up the dock toward the lights.

* * *

She stayed until the lanterns went out, one by one, and the dock emptied, and the river settled into its night voice. Then she walked back through Callow's Bend, past the co-op with the aquifer numbers still nailed to the wall, laminated now against the rain by someone who understood that paper testimony needed protecting too. Past the church. Past the meeting hall where she'd stood at twenty-two years old and heard the word *parameters* used to bury her brother, and felt the old grief rise and — for the first time in eleven years — pass through her without stopping to stay.

At the boathouse, Odile was waiting with two cups and no pretense of surprise.

"Well?" Odile said.

Kestrel looked at the berth behind the boathouse, at the stack of seasoned oak already delivered under tarpaulin, at the young apprentice-to-be who'd left a note weighted under a stone: *I can start Monday. I already know how to steam-bend. Sort of.*

Power hoarded corrupts; she'd watched it happen in ministry offices and contractor boardrooms and the hearts of men who measured rivers in acre-feet. But this — rope coils left on porches, oak saved thirty years for a stranger, a town nailing evidence to its own doors — this was power the way the river held power: by letting everything through.

"I'll take the berth," Kestrel said, and drank her coffee while it was still hot.

Out on the dark water, something large moved upstream against the current — a barge, maybe, or a boat coming home late — and its wake rolled in slow and patient against the pilings, the way the water had rolled against them for longer than any name on any map, remembering everything, holding nothing, waiting to see what they would build on its banks next year, and the year after, and all the years the permits couldn't touch.
