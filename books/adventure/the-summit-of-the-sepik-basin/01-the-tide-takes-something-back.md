## 1. The Tide Takes Something Back

The tide went out all at once, the way a held breath goes out.

Beatrix Underhill was halfway through entering the morning's manifest figures into the harbor ledger—copra, forty-one tons; timber, twelve; a crate of machine parts she suspected were rifle barrels but had learned not to ask about—when the water in Wewak Bay began to move wrong. Not out and in, the way tides do, patient as bookkeeping, but *out*, steadily, urgently, as if something below had pulled a plug the size of the world.

She set down her pen.

The bay emptied in front of her eyes. Mudflats that hadn't seen sun since the war surfaced glistening, pocked with crab holes and the ribs of some long-drowned boat. Fish flopped in the shrinking channels, silvering the air with their panic. A canoe beached itself with an apologetic crunch, its owner standing hip-deep in what had been three fathoms of water not four minutes ago, staring at his own feet as though they'd betrayed him.

"Missus," said Tomas, the boy who swept the customs house steps, from the doorway behind her. His voice had gone thin. "You see this?"

"I see it."

"The sea is going away."

"Then we'd best let it," Beatrix said, which was the kind of thing a calm person says, and she was proud of how calm it sounded, because inside her chest something old and familiar had begun to knock—the feeling of standing on ground that has decided to stop being ground.

She'd felt it first at five that morning, actually, though she hadn't named it then. She'd been awake since four, as usual, lying on her cot above the customs shed listening to the harbor breathe, when the cot frame had shivered against the floorboards. A long, low tremor, more suggestion than event, rolling up from somewhere under the ranges to the south. She'd catalogued it and dismissed it—the coast shrugged like that sometimes—but her body hadn't dismissed it. Her body had known. Her body had spent six years learning the difference between a building settling and a mountain clearing its throat, and it had woken her fully, heart going, hands flat against the mattress like a woman braced for a fall.

Now the sea was proving her body right.

* * *

By eight o'clock the whole town had come down to look at the naked bay.

They stood along the seawall in loose clusters—fishermen with their nets piled unattended, market women with baskets still on their heads, the Australian district officer in his sweat-stained whites, a pair of mission brothers, children running the mudflat's edge until their mothers shouted them back. Nobody spoke much. There is a particular silence that falls over people watching the ordinary order of things fail, and Beatrix knew it well; she'd heard it once in a tunnel two hundred feet underground, in the half-second between the crack of timber and the roar.

The water kept withdrawing. It wasn't draining so much as *retreating*, pulling back from the land the way a man pulls his hand from a stove, and where it went the light followed, sliding down the exposed slope of the seabed in long amber sheets. And there, revealed by degrees as the shallows emptied—a dark shape at the base of the headland north of the anchorage, where the cliffs ran down into the water in a tumble of black basalt.

A mouth.

That was the word that came to her, unbidden and unwelcome, and she stood at the seawall with her ledger under her arm and looked at it and thought: *a mouth.* An arch of stone perhaps thirty feet across, opening into darkness, its lower lip still sheeted with falling water that came off it in ropes and veils as the last of the shallows drained. Behind it, deeper in, the black did not end. It went *in*. The eye kept trying to find the back wall of the cave and kept failing, kept sliding off into a dark that had weight to it, that seemed to be looking back.

"That's not a cave," said the district officer beside her, a pink-faced man named Calloway whom Beatrix knew only as a signature on freight permits. He said it the way a man says *that's not my wife*, as if the thing before them were an impostor. "I've moored off this headland eleven years. There's no cave there."

"There's no cave there at high tide," Beatrix said.

Calloway glanced at her, mildly surprised to have been answered by a clerk. "Well. Quite."

Around them the locals were not looking at the cave at all. That was the thing Beatrix noticed second, after the cave itself: the Sepik men and women along the seawall had arranged themselves, without any discussion she could see, with their backs mostly to the headland. They watched the fish, the boats, the children, the strange dry smell rising off the mud—and they did not watch the mouth in the cliff. One old man stood facing it deliberately, arms folded, and even he did not seem to be *looking* so much as guarding against it, the way you stand between a fire and a sleeping child.

"Tomas." She beckoned the sweeping boy over. "What do your people call that place? The opening in the rocks."

Tomas came to the wall, looked, and stepped back a half pace, quick, like a man who'd touched something hot. "No name, missus."

"Everything has a name."

"Not that." He said it flatly, without fear exactly—or with a fear so settled it had stopped being fear and become policy. "Old people say the water keeps that door shut. Today the water forgets. Tomorrow it remembers." He shrugged, a small economical shrug that said *I have told you everything I am willing to die telling you*, and went back to his sweeping, which he now performed with unusual devotion, facing away from the bay.

Beatrix turned back to the cave.

The engineer in her—the part of her she kept fed on small rations, ledger arithmetic and the occasional structural opinion about the customs shed's sagging veranda posts—was already working whether she wanted it or not. Basalt headland, wave-cut, but that arch was too regular for wave-cut. Too tall, too round. Water doesn't carve keyholes. Somewhere behind that black mouth there was softer rock, or a fault, or—

Or a tunnel. Driven, not found. The thought arrived whole, the way conclusions do when they've been waiting years in the dark for one fact to walk in and join them. She counted what she could see: the arch's springline sat maybe fifteen feet above the current seabed, and the basalt around it showed tool faces, faint, weathered, but square. Someone had cut that entrance. Someone with drills and dynamite and a reason to hide the cutting below the tide line, where anyone passing in a boat would see nothing but cliff.

Her hand itched. That was the absurd truth of it—six years buried, and her hand *itched*, the way a pianist's fingers itch at the sound of a piano through a wall.

* * *

She lasted until noon.

At noon the water began to come back—not rushing, but returning with a slow, deliberate confidence, filling the bay channel by channel, and the crowd along the seawall dispersed the way crowds do, relieved and vaguely cheated. The cave's lip took the sea first, water climbing the black stone in a bright tongue, and within the hour the mouth was a dark smear beneath a fathom of green, and by mid-afternoon there was nothing to see at all except the headland looking exactly as it had looked every day of the six years Beatrix had lived in sight of it.

Which was, she understood, precisely the point. Six years she had lived in sight of it. Six years she had walked the seawall twice a day and never once wondered about the cliff.

She climbed the stairs to her room above the customs shed, shut the door, and got out the pencil.

Not the pen—the pen was for ledgers, for the careful invisible life, for numbers that added up and asked nothing of her. The pencil was older. It had come over the mountains with her in a kit roll along with a scale rule and a stub of red chinagraph, relics of a profession she no longer admitted to, carried not out of hope but out of the same weakness that makes a man keep a dead wife's photograph in a drawer he never opens. In six years she had drawn nothing but cargo stowage diagrams. But her hands remembered everything. Her hands had been trained by a father who believed a line should be laid down the way a rail is laid—true, or not at all.

She sat at the table by the window, where the afternoon light came in over the bay, and she drew the headland from memory.

It came easily, which frightened her a little. The basalt strata, dipping east. The talus slope. The arch, and she caught herself rendering the tool marks on the stone with a draftsman's hatching, fine parallel strokes that said *cut face* to anyone who could read the language, and she realized she was not drawing a sketch. She was drawing a survey. Elevation, approximate scale, aspect, the visible geometry of an entrance engineered to drown.

She made herself stop. She looked at the page for a long time.

Under the sketch, in the margin, her pencil moved again before she could decide whether to allow it, and wrote two words in her father's cramped notation style, the shorthand they'd shared over dinner tables and depot desks all through her childhood:

*Load-bearing. Why?*

Why would anyone cut a tunnel entrance below the tide line and leave it load-bearing? You don't seal a mine and shore its mouth unless you mean to come back. Or unless you mean for whatever's inside never to come out. Either way, someone had spent money and blasting powder on keeping a door shut, and doors that cost that much to close are doors worth opening.

She heard the sea change outside the window, the evening wind coming around the point, and she became aware of herself sitting in the failing light with a drawing in front of her that could get a person talked about. A harbor clerk who sketches geology. A woman who knows what a cut face looks like. Small things. But she had built her life out of small things, stacked them careful as cordwood, and she knew better than most how a structure fails: not all at once, but at the one member nobody thought to check.

She held the page over the kerosene lamp.

The paper curled, browned, and she watched the arch and the hatched tool marks and the two words in the margin go soft and orange and then to flame, and she dropped it into the tin dish she used for a sink and stood there while it became ash, telling herself this was prudence, this was discipline, this was the whole architecture of her survival doing exactly what it was built to do.

The ash smelled like nothing. Outside, the bay lay smooth and full and innocent, holding its door shut.

* * *

But that night she did not sleep, and near midnight she lit the lamp again, and this time she did not pretend to herself that it was for anything but what it was.

She took from under the floorboard beneath the cot the oilskin packet, and from the packet the map—her father's map, the only thing of his she owned besides the pencil, a surveyor's linen sheet covered in his cramped hand: closed lines, abandoned grades, the skeleton of a mountain railway system that had died unfinished in the high country south of the basin. She had not unrolled it in two years. She unrolled it now, weighted its corners with the inkwell and the tin dish and the scale rule, and smoothed it flat with both palms, and told herself she was only looking. Only remembering. A woman is allowed to remember.

The lines ran everywhere across the linen like veins in a leaf—contours, grades, little annotations in her father's voice: *washout '09, rebuilt '11; grade 4%; tunnel 340 yds, wet; ABANDONED—slip, no funds.* A kingdom of dead infrastructure, a railroad that had been born ambitious and died poor, and somewhere in the middle of it, if the old stories and the old charts agreed on anything, the lines converged toward the ranges above the basin, toward the high cold country where the government had given up and the jungle had taken back every sleeper and rail.

And toward the coast, one line ran down and out and stopped—at a headland marked with a symbol she had never paid attention to in her life, a small circle with a cross through it, her father's mark for a sealed adit.

She put her fingertip on it. The circle was tiny. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea.

"Six years," she said aloud to the empty room, and her voice sounded strange in it, rusty, like a handcar that hasn't run in a season. "Six years, and the sea moves once."

The lamp hissed. Down in the harbor, water slapped the pilings, patient, faithful, keeping its appointments. Keeping its door shut.

She rolled the map up. She put it back under the floorboard. She lay down on the cot and stared at the ceiling beams and rehearsed, as she did every night, the small litany of her invisible life: *I am Beatrice Underhill, harbor clerk, no relations, no history, no debts worth naming.* It had worked six years. It would work tomorrow.

But sleep, when it finally took her near dawn, took her down into a dream of black water draining off stone, and a mouth in a cliff that opened wider the longer she looked at it, and somewhere behind her in the dream, footsteps on gravel, a man's voice saying her real name—the one she'd buried with the license—in a tone that meant papers had been signed, and sums come due, and the tide, all this time, had only been waiting for permission to take something back.
