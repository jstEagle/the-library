## 2. A Debt Written in Ink

The monsoon had come early that year, and Wewak's harbor smelled of diesel and rotting sago. Beatrix Underhill stood at her post in the customs shed, a pencil stub in hand, tallying crates of copra that would never fetch what the shippers hoped. It was dull work, deliberately so. Dullness was the point. For three years she had made herself small—a woman with ink-stained fingers and no history, a clerk who misspelled names and asked no questions, whose greatest ambition was to be forgotten by everyone who might remember her.

She was good at being forgotten. She had practiced.

The rain came down in gray sheets that morning, drumming on the corrugated roof until conversation required shouting, and Beatrix welcomed the noise because it filled the space where thought might otherwise settle. Thought was dangerous. Thought drifted backward, toward a tunnel in the Central Range, toward a man named Vane, toward the particular weight of a debt that was not measured in kina or pounds but in something far less refundable.

She heard the launch before she saw it—the low cough of its engine cutting through the swell—and then, through the rain-veiled window of the customs shed, she made out a figure stepping onto the wet planks of the pier. He carried an umbrella he did not open. He wore a white linen suit that the rain was already ruining, and he walked with the unhurried gait of a man who knew exactly which door he had come to knock upon.

Beatrix knew him. Everyone in the harbor trade knew Ambrose Kell; he was the collector, the closer, the man sent when letters went unanswered. He had come for other people's debts twice before in her time at the shed, and both times he had left within the hour, satisfied or bought off, it hardly mattered which. But this time his launch did not continue up the coast. This time he came ashore and stood in the rain outside the customs shed like a man waiting for someone specific, and Beatrix felt the peculiar cold that enters a body when it recognizes its own name in a stranger's silence.

She told herself it was coincidence. She told herself Kell collected debts all along this coast, from copra buyers and mission treasuries and the occasional failed plantation. She told herself a great many things, the way she always did, arranging her lies in neat rows like the crates on the dock.

Then the shed door opened, and Ambrose Kell walked in out of the rain, and asked the harbormaster—quietly, politely, in the manner of a man reading a menu rather than ordering from one—who kept the ledgers.

* * *

He found her at the second desk, the one nearest the wall, where the damp crept in and the light was worst. He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. Ambrose Kell had the soft menace of a well-made trap: everything about him was courteous, patient, faintly amused, as though the entire exchange were a formality he performed weekly in a dozen ports and could conduct half-asleep.

"Miss Underhill," he said, using the name she had surrendered years ago along with everything else. "Or should I say—no. Forgive me. The papers use the old name. I'll use whatever you prefer."

"That's kind," Beatrix said, and meant nothing by it, and knew he knew she meant nothing by it.

Kell set a leather folio on her desk. It was heavy—not with paper, though there was paper, dozens of pages clipped and tabbed and initialed—but with intention. You could feel it through the cover, the way you can feel a stone through a glove: the dense, settled gravity of a thing that has been decided long before it is presented.

"I represent Marcus Vane," he said. "You'll remember him."

Everyone remembered Marcus Vane. That was rather the trouble with him.

"I'd like to review some documents with you," Kell continued. "Not here, if you don't mind. The walls in this shed are thin, and the harbormaster has opinions. There's a veranda at the Kaiserin Hotel where the rain sounds pleasant and the coffee is tolerable. I've taken the liberty of booking a table."

"You've taken a great many liberties," Beatrix said.

"Only the ones I'm paid for," said Kell, and smiled, and the smile was the worst part of him—wider than courtesy, narrower than cruelty, exactly calibrated to remind you that he found this work interesting, and that a man who finds his work interesting cannot be bored into cooperation or shamed out of it.

She went with him. Of course she went with him. A woman hiding in a port town does not refuse a meeting with the collector of her debts; refusal is a confession, and confession travels faster than any launch.

* * *

The veranda of the Kaiserin faced the bay, and the bay, that morning, still bore the marks of what the earthquake tide had done two days past. The water sat lower than any chart allowed, brown-green and uneasy, and along the northern shore, where the mangroves gave way to black volcanic rock, a dark shape showed above the waterline that had no business showing anywhere. The locals had already begun leaving offerings near it—betel nut, lime, a few wilted hibiscus—and would not go near it themselves, and would not say why, and grew visibly uncomfortable when asked.

Beatrix looked at it the way she tried not to look at anything: briefly, sideways, with the disciplined glance of a woman who had learned that attention is a debt of its own.

Kell watched her look. He was very good at watching people look.

"Before we begin," he said, "I want to be clear about my role. I am not here to threaten you. Threats are inefficient; they create resistance, and resistance creates paperwork, and paperwork is how collectors die of boredom. I am here to explain a situation. Whether it becomes a threat is entirely up to you."

"Go on," Beatrix said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, wrapped around the hotel's thick ceramic cup, were not.

Kell opened the folio. The first document was familiar to her—she had signed it, or something very like it, six years ago, in a lawyer's office in Rabaul, with hands still bandaged from a collapse that had nearly killed her. A promissory note. Principal, interest, compounding quarterly, secured against—she read the clause again now, and it landed differently after six years—"all right, title, and interest in any professional licenses held or hereafter held by the borrower."

Her engineer's license. The last valuable thing she owned, if you could call it owning, since Vane had arranged its suspension himself, quietly, through friends in the colonial administration, the same month he advanced her the loan. She had told herself for years that the license was gone anyway, dead, worthless, a relic. But the paper in front of her said otherwise. The paper said it was collateral. The paper said Marcus Vane owned even the memory of what she had been.

"The principal," Kell said, tapping the page with a fountain pen, "with accrued interest, comes to a sum I won't insult us both by reading aloud. It is large. It is unpayable. We both know this. You earn eleven pounds a month and remit four of them, honorably, to a mission account that holds them against your arrears. At your current rate of repayment, adjusted for interest, the debt matures fully in approximately 2147. I've done the arithmetic. Mr. Vane employs better mathematicians than I do, but I do enjoy showing my work."

"What do you want?" Beatrix asked. Not *what are you offering*. What do you want. Because she had learned, in the school of hard collapses, that men like Kell and men like Vane never offered anything; they only ever wanted, and dressed the wanting as generosity.

"I'm coming to that." He turned to the next document. "Sixty days. That is the term. On the sixty-first day, the note accelerates. Full balance due. Enforcement proceedings follow—civil first, naturally, we are civilized people, and then, if the civil remedies prove insufficient, criminal referral. Falsification of load calculations, misrepresentation of survey data, negligence resulting in loss of life. The charges exist. They have existed for six years, carefully preserved, lovingly maintained, like a wine cellar. Mr. Vane is a great believer in cellars."

Beatrix set down her cup. Her hand shook, slightly, and she hated that it shook, and hated more that Kell noticed it shake, and noticed that she hated it.

"You're describing a prison sentence," she said.

"I'm describing a filing cabinet," said Kell. "But yes. The contents, if ever released, would amount to one."

"And why," she said slowly, "after six years of quiet, after six years of my being nobody, of my paying my four pounds like a widow pays her tithes—why now?"

"Ah." Kell closed the folio partway, and leaned forward, and lowered his voice to the register of a confessor. "Because of the tide, Miss Underhill. Because of what the tide took back."

* * *

Here is what Beatrix knew, and what she had spent two days pretending not to know:

Two nights ago, the earth off the north coast had shrugged. A deep quake, far offshore, felt in Wewak only as a long rolling unease and a smell of sulfur on the wind. And the sea had withdrawn—not the gentle withdrawal of a low tide but a great dragging exhalation, the water pulling back hundreds of meters, exposing reef flats and wreck timbers and, at the base of the black cliffs north of the harbor, a cave mouth that no chart recorded and no living man claimed to have seen.

The old men of the villages would not speak of it. The younger ones spoke of it only in whispers, and what they whispered was a single word, and the word was *gold*, and beneath the word lay another word they would not say at all, and that word was *Halvorsen*.

Beatrix had heard the name once before, years ago, in another life, across another table. Halvorsen. The expedition of 1911. Forty porters. A Norwegian financier with a concession from an administration that no longer existed, a railway scheme into the highlands that died with him, and a legend that the mountain had swallowed his fortune whole—bullion, coin, the payroll of an empire that never got built. Men had looked for it. Men had died looking for it. The administration had eventually closed the whole region, struck it from the maps, and let the jungle have it back.

And now the sea had opened a door on it.

Kell turned the next page of the folio, and Beatrix understood, suddenly and completely, why the coffee was tolerable and the table pre-booked and the rain permitted to fall.

"There is a clause," Kell said, "a rider, appended to your note. It was added eighteen months ago. You were not informed of its addition, because informing you would have constituted notice, and notice would have given you grounds, and grounds are untidy. Mr. Vane prefers tidy. The rider states that the note may be discharged, in full, principal and interest and all accrued penalties, upon the borrower's delivery of a specified consideration in kind."

He read it aloud. He read it slowly, in the voice of a liturgy, letting each phrase fall like a stone into still water.

"*...to wit: accurate intelligence regarding, and practical means of access to, a certain subterranean feature recently exposed by seismic activity on the northern coastline of the Sepik Basin, herein referred to as the Underwater Entrance, such intelligence and access to be delivered to the principal or his designated agents within sixty days of presentment.*"

Beatrix listened to the words land, and felt something in her chest go tight and cold, like a wire drawing taut.

"He wants the cave," she said.

"He wants what's in the cave," Kell corrected, gently. "The cave is merely the door. What's behind it—well. Mr. Vane has his theories. He collects theories about lost expeditions the way other men collect stamps. He has spent, over the years, a genuinely embarrassing sum on salvage charts, decommissioned mine surveys, and the diaries of dead prospectors. He believes—passionately, patiently, with the faith of a man who has never once been wrong about money—that the Halvorsen fortune exists, that it is intact, and that the sea has just handed him the key to it."

"And my debt," Beatrix said, "is the price of the key."

"Your debt is the *handle* of the key," said Kell. "Forgive the metaphor. Mr. Vane does not think of you as a debtor. Debtors are a commodity; he owns thousands. He thinks of you as an instrument. You are a railroad engineer with knowledge of closed lines, suspended credentials, no family, no ties, no reputation left to damage, and—this is the part he values most—no alternative. A man with alternatives negotiates. You will comply. Both of us know you will comply, and Mr. Vane finds a certain economy in that. He has built an empire on knowing precisely what people cannot afford to refuse."

Beatrix stared out at the bay. The cave mouth sat in the cliffs like a black letter in an unread sentence, and the offerings at its base were already washing away with the returning tide, and somewhere beneath the brown-green water was a door that had been shut for thirty years and was now, temporarily, improbably, open.

"And if I simply refuse?" she asked. "If I tell you to take your folio and put it where the sun doesn't shine?"

Kell's smile widened by exactly one millimeter.

"Then on the sixty-first day, I file. And the following day, a warrant issues. And the day after that, two constables arrive at the customs shed during the lunch hour, when the harbormaster is at his meal and the shed is empty, and they escort you to a launch, and you go south, and you stand trial, and you lose. You will lose because the evidence is genuine—you did falsify those calculations, Miss Underhill, we both know it, no point pretending otherwise in front of me—and you will lose because the prosecution will be funded by a man who has never lost a case he chose to prosecute. Six years. Hard labor. And when you come out, if you come out, the debt will still be waiting, grown fat on prison interest, and your license will be ash, and no port on this coast will hire a convicted falsifier of load calculations. Even the ones who don't know why."

He paused, and let the rain speak for a moment, and then added, almost kindly:

"I mention these details not to frighten you. I mention them because Mr. Vane believes that informed consent is more binding than frightened consent. A man who understands the trap and walks into it anyway cannot later claim he was coerced. It is a legal philosophy. It is also, in its way, a religion."

* * *

They sat for a while without speaking. The rain thinned. Through the breaking cloud, afternoon light came down the coast in long slanted bars, and the black cliffs north of the harbor turned the color of wet iron, and the cave mouth drank the light and gave nothing back.

Beatrix found herself thinking about a tunnel.

It was a habit of hers, thinking about tunnels. She had spent the best years of her life inside them—designing them, blasting them, walking their fresh-cut mouths with a lamp and a level and the arrogant confidence of a woman who believed the ground obeyed arithmetic. The Central Range scheme. The Kanduan Pass drive. Eleven kilometers of heading through limestone and water-bearing shale, three shifts a day, seven hundred men, her name on the drawings.

She thought about the night it went wrong.

It was the fourth of March, six years ago. Chain 44, the deep section, where the shale dipped under an ancient riverbed and the water came in sideways, pressurized, tasting of iron. She had signed off on the loading pattern herself. She had cut the powder factor to save cost—Vane's cost, the consortium's cost, the margin that kept the directors happy—and she had known, in the private ledger of her own conscience, that the numbers were wrong. Not catastrophically wrong. Not criminally wrong, she had told herself then. Just... optimistic. Optimistic by a factor she could not quite admit to, because admitting it would mean re-blasting a section at triple expense, and she was thirty-one years old and ambitious and tired, and optimism is the sin that ambition dresses as virtue.

The round fired at 2:14 in the morning. It fired beautifully. Then the ground, which had not agreed with her arithmetic, answered.

The backs came down in chain 43 and 44 together—forty meters of heading, gone, folded like wet cardboard. Seven men were beyond the fall. Three were buried to the waist in muck and rock. And Beatrix Underhill, who had walked the heading twenty minutes before the shot, who had signed the charge sheet with her own hand, was trapped in the refuge bay at chain 45 with a broken left arm and a rising sense—not of fear, not yet, but of *accountability*, arriving ahead of the rescue crews like a smell of smoke before the fire.

She remembered the water. She remembered the sound—the deep groan of settling rock, the hiss of air lines tearing, the way seven hundred men outside became, in the space of four minutes, a single organism hammering on steel. She remembered deciding, with the strange clarity of the buried, that she deserved whatever came.

What came was Marcus Vane.

Not immediately. First came the rescue crews, forty hours of digging by hand through crushed timber and rock, and Beatrix surfaced into daylight with her arm in a splint and her career already dying around her like frost killing a crop. The inquiry began within the week. Load calculations were subpoenaed. Survey records were examined. And every figure, every signature, every annotation in the margins pointed, with terrible precision, to the third engineer of record.

To her.

She had prepared herself for ruin. Honest ruin. A hearing, a stripped license, disgrace, maybe worse—seven men had died in that collapse, and she had drawn the charges that killed them, and she had known it, and part of her had wanted to stand in the dock and say so, wanted it the way the drowning want water.

That was when Vane intervened.

She never learned the full mechanics of it. Lawyers appeared—good ones, expensive ones, retained by a consortium she had never dealt with directly. Witnesses who might have testified against her developed sudden difficulties of memory. A key inspection report vanished from the administration's files and was replaced with a copy whose carbon differed, subtly, helpfully, from the original. The inquiry's scope narrowed. The findings, when they finally came, attributed the collapse to "unforeseeable geological conditions"—which was true in the way that many lies are true—and recommended no individual culpability, and the matter was closed, and Beatrix Underhill walked out of that hearing room with her freedom and her name intact and her soul in a sling.

And two weeks later, Marcus Vane came to see her personally.

She remembered the meeting with a clarity that had never faded, because it was the strangest hour of her life. He came to the boarding house in Lae where she was convalescing, unannounced, without secretaries, without lawyers, carrying nothing but a bottle of whisky and a folder of documents she was not permitted to keep. He sat opposite her on the veranda while the trade winds moved the palms, and he poured two glasses, and he said, in essence, the following:

You are finished. You know it, and I know it, and the inquiry knows it, whatever it says publicly. Your career is over. But you are not guilty of what they would have charged you with—not exactly, not cleanly—and a clean conviction requires clean evidence, and I control the evidence. I have spent a considerable sum making sure the truth stays buried, and I will continue to spend it, indefinitely, for as long as it suits me.

And then he said the rest, and the rest was the debt.

He did not frame it as blackmail. Vane was too intelligent for blackmail; blackmail implies a threat delivered, and threats create martyrs, and martyrs are unpredictable. He framed it as *salvage*. He had salvaged her, he explained, the way one salvages a ship on a reef—because the hull was sound, because the scrap value exceeded the salvage cost, because a competent engineer with no reputation and no options was worth more to him obedient than destroyed. The loan was presented as an act of rescue. The terms were presented as standard. The rider—the original rider, before the cave clause was stitched in—secured her services, her silence, and her signature against a debt designed never to be repaid.

And Beatrix, sitting on that veranda with her broken arm in a splint and her whole future in ruins around her, had looked at the man who had pulled her—there was no other word for it, however much she resented the metaphor—from the wreckage of her own making, and had signed.

She had signed because she was grateful. That was the unforgivable part, the part she could never make peace with: not that Vane had trapped her, but that she had *thanked* him for it. Gratitude is the strongest chain ever forged, because the prisoner forges it herself, link by link, and calls each link kindness. For six years she had paid her four pounds a month and told herself the story of her own rescue, and the story had a villain in it, and the villain was the geology, and the geology was nobody's fault, and she was a victim, and victims owe nothing but their survival.

The folio on the veranda table said otherwise. The folio said she owed everything.

* * *

"So," Kell said, when the light had gone long and gold across the water. "Shall we discuss logistics?"

Beatrix looked at him. "Logistics."

"The sixty days," said Kell. "Mr. Vane is a practical man. He does not expect you to produce the Underwater Entrance from your handbag. He expects you to do what you are uniquely qualified to do: find a route to it, overland, before the season closes. The cave opens to the sea, but the sea approach is suicide—swell, sharks, no landing. The land approach runs through country that was closed thirty years ago and has been eating explorers ever since. There is a rail grade, Miss Underhill. An abandoned one. Cut into the mountains before the war, before the closure, part of the Halvorsen concession itself. It runs from the interior plateau down to a coastal terminus that no longer appears on any chart. If it survives—and Mr. Vane's surveys suggest portions of it do—it is the only way over the range before the snows. And you, uniquely among the persons available to him, possess a map of it."

Beatrix went very still.

"My father's map," she said quietly.

"Your father's map," Kell agreed pleasantly. "Drawn by a surveyor who worked the Halvorsen concession in its final years, inherited by a daughter who keeps it, we are reliably informed, in a waterproof oilskin sleeve inside a false bottom of her trunk at the Kaiserin Hotel, room nine. Do forgive the intrusion into your privacy. Mr. Vane's people are thorough."

Every word of it was true, and every word of it was a knife. They had searched her rooms. They had found the map. They had let her keep it, because a debtor who believes her secrets are still her own is more manageable than one who knows she is naked, and because the map itself was useless to them without her—useless without a railroad engineer to read the grades, assess the trestles, judge which of those forty-year-old cuts would hold a man's weight and which would drop him two hundred feet into a gorge.

The map was the leash. She was the dog.

"And if the map is wrong?" Beatrix asked. "If the grade's washed out, if the trestles are down, if the route doesn't exist anymore? Jungle eats iron, Mr. Kell. I've seen it eat a bridge in five years."

"Then you will have failed," said Kell, "and failure, as established, has consequences. But Mr. Vane is an optimist in the specific way that rich men are optimists: he believes the world can be purchased, and the world usually cooperates. He has engaged guides. He has engaged porters. He has engaged, I'm told, a certain local tracker whose reputation precedes him even into the hotels—though I confess I don't understand the arrangement myself. Mr. Vane handles the expedition. You handle the engineering. The debt handles you."

"When do we leave?"

"Within the fortnight. The snows will not wait, and neither, Mr. Vane feels, should you." Kell gathered his folio, squared its edges, and rose. "One final item, and then I'll release you to your evening. The rider specifies 'access.' It does not specify success. You are not required to bring back gold. You are required to bring back *knowledge*—survey notes, photographs, a reliable assessment of what the entrance connects to and whether it can be worked. Mr. Vane's own parties will handle extraction, once your report tells them it's worth extracting. In the event of your death—and he asked me to convey this with his compliments—in the event of your death, the note passes to your estate, the estate is worthless, and the matter concludes itself with a tidiness he would find aesthetically satisfying. He wishes you luck. Sincerely. He told me to tell you that, and I believe he meant it, in the way that a chess player sincerely wishes his opponent strong health between games."

Kell paused at the veranda rail, straightening a cuff already ruined by rain, and looked out at the black cliffs, and the cave mouth below them filling slowly with the returning sea.

"For what it's worth," he said, "and it isn't worth much—I've collected a great many debts in a great many ports, and I can usually tell which debtors will survive their creditors. Most don't. They fold, or they run, or they drink, or they sign the second paper, the one that sells the next sixty years. But occasionally one looks at the trap, measures it, prices it, and starts planning the exit before the collector has left the veranda. I don't know which you are. Mr. Vane thinks he knows. Mr. Vane is frequently wrong about people. It is, historically, his most expensive hobby."

He tipped an imaginary hat, descended the veranda steps into the last of the rain, and was gone, his launch coughing away up the coast toward the black cliffs, toward the cave, toward whatever waited in the dark behind the door the earthquake had opened.

* * *

Beatrix walked back to the Kaiserin alone, through streets running with water, past the mission church where the evening bell was ringing, past the market stalls shuttered against the wet. Room nine waited for her with its narrow bed and its one window and its trunk with the false bottom, and she sat on the bed in her damp clothes and did not light the lamp, and let the dark come down around her like water filling a hold.

Sixty days.

She turned the number over the way she used to turn over figures in a load calculation, looking for the error, the hidden assumption, the optimistic factor. Sixty days to reach a cave that had killed everyone who sought it. Sixty days over a range that ate railways. Sixty days with an enforcer assigned to watch her, guides she hadn't chosen, and a debt that would not die no matter what she brought back—because men like Vane did not forgive debts, they *rolled* them, they rolled them forward forever, and the cave was not payment but bait, and she was not an instrument but prey being fattened for a longer hunt.

And yet.

And yet there was the other thing, the thing she could not say aloud even to herself, the thing that sat in her chest like a coal that would not cool: that Marcus Vane had pulled her out. That whatever he was—and he was a predator, she knew it now, she had always known it and called it gratitude instead—he had spent his money and his power to keep her from the dock when the dock was what she deserved, and had done it, perhaps, for reasons that began in calculation and ended somewhere she had never dared examine. Six years ago she had signed because she was grateful. Tonight, sitting in the dark in room nine, she understood at last that gratitude had been the trap's first bar, and that the trap had never been the debt at all.

The trap was that she had believed herself saved.

Somewhere out there, the tide was rising again over the cave mouth, sealing it, hiding it, keeping it for whoever could find the landward way—the old rail grade, her father's lines, the closed roads through the green silence of the interior. Somewhere in her trunk, in its oilskin sleeve, the map lay coiled like a sleeping animal, waiting to be read.

She slept badly, and dreamed of tunnels, and woke before dawn with the taste of iron in her mouth.

In the morning, she took the map from the trunk and spread it on the floor of room nine, weighting the corners with her boots, and knelt over it the way she had once knelt over drawings, and began, for the first time in six years, to plan a route of her own.

A stranger watched her do it.
