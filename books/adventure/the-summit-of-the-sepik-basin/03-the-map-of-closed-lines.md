## 3. The Map of Closed Lines

The lamp in Beatrix Underhill's room burned low and amber, the way it always did when she was about to do something she'd promised herself she wouldn't. It was past midnight, the harbor quiet except for the slap of water against the pilings below her window, and the paper she had spread across her desk—weighted at the corners with four brass shell casings she'd carried from another life—was not a shipping manifest.

It was her father's map.

She had kept it folded inside the false bottom of a tin document case for eleven years, through three towns and two names she had briefly borrowed and abandoned like coats that no longer fit. The map was drawn on linen-backed survey paper, soft as cloth now at its creases, inked in a hand so fine and certain it looked less like drafting than calligraphy. *H. UNDERHILL, RESIDENT ENGINEER* ran along the title block in letters that stood up off the page like a challenge. Below it, smaller: *SURVEY OF CLOSED LINES, CENTRAL RANGE DIVISION — 1931–1944.*

Her father had spent thirteen years walking grades that other men had given up on. He had measured them, photographed them, argued for them in reports nobody read, and finally, when the company closed the mountain division for good, he had done the one thing an engineer of his generation was never supposed to do. He had copied the whole network by hand—the tunnels, the trestles, the switchbacks, the water stations, every culvert and cut—and taken it home with him, because he could not bear to watch it be forgotten.

Beatrix had inherited more than his talent. She had inherited his stubbornness, and his weakness for beautiful doomed things, and this map.

She unfolded it now with both hands, smoothing the linen flat against the desk until the brass casings held it taut. The room smelled of kerosene and damp and the faint salt rot of Wewak itself, which got into everything here, into books and boots and the seams of a woman's skin. Outside, the tide was still behaving strangely. Two nights since the earthquake, and the bay had not fully recovered its rhythm; the water came in shallow and left late, as if the sea itself were confused, or grieving, or looking for something it had misplaced.

The map showed the range as a spine of hatched ridgelines running north to south, and across it, like stitches through flesh, the closed lines. The Central Range Division. Four railroads, built over thirty years by men who believed steel could go anywhere, abandoned in a single decade when the copper played out and the government's money dried up and the snows came earlier each year until the passes choked and stayed choked.

Line One ran up the Karawari side, through country so steep the engineers had cut the grade into the cliff face itself, half tunnel and half viaduct, forty-one kilometers of stone arches that local men called *the hanging road*. Closed 1938. Line Two crossed the saddle at Tumam Pass, the highest working railway in the southern hemisphere once, before the snow took it. Closed 1941. Lines Three and Four were feeder roads, short and sickly, dead within two years of their birth.

And between them, connecting everything, running where no line ran anymore—a dotted route. Her father's own hand. He had penciled it in during his last year, after his last survey, a route that used pieces of all four dead lines stitched together with stretches of porter track and river shingle. He had labeled it, in pencil so light you had to tilt the page against the lamplight to see it: *POSSIBLE CROSSING — CONDITION UNKNOWN — FOR BEA, WHEN SHE IS READY.*

He had died before she was ready. Or perhaps she had simply never been ready; perhaps readiness was a thing that happened to other people, people who hadn't falsified a load calculation and buried a friend under nine hundred tons of collapsed shale. Beatrix had spent eleven years telling herself both stories on alternating nights, and neither one had ever quite fit.

But tonight the map lay open, and her finger moved along the dotted line, and she did not stop herself.

She traced it the way her father had taught her to trace a grade—not reading it as geography but hearing it, almost, the way a musician hears a score. There: the river crossing at the confluence, marked with a small careful *ferry gone*, meaning the cable ferry had rotted away and the crossing would have to be made by raft or ford. There: the first tunnel, six hundred meters through a spur of grey limestone, her father's note beside it in tiny characters—*roof sound as of '44; check for rockfall after any tremor.* After any tremor. And there had been a tremor, hadn't there? Two nights ago the whole coast had shuddered, and the tide had run out of the bay like water from a tipped bowl, and a cave mouth had opened in the cliffs below the old mission, and the locals would not speak its name.

Her finger paused there, at the little triangle her father had drawn to mark a spring, and she felt the same unease move through her that had moved through her on the seawall—that sense of something shifting underneath the world, some door that had been shut for a very long time swinging loose on its hinges.

Because the truth, the truth she admitted to no one, not even to the mirror, was that she already knew why Ambrose Kell had come to her counter with those papers. Marcus Vane wanted the cave. Everyone with money and ears in this territory wanted the cave, or wanted whatever the cave was going to be said to contain once the right story got attached to it. And the only way to reach the cave before the rains turned the lowland tracks to soup and the early snows sealed the high passes—the only way anyone had ever reached anything in that country—was up the old grades. Up her father's lines.

Sixty days. Kell had said sixty days, payable in access. Access meant a route. A route meant the map. And the map meant her father, and her father meant the collapse, and the collapse meant Marcus Vane pulling her half-dead out of the dark with his own hands and then holding that rescue over her head for eleven years like a man holds a leash.

She had told herself she would never look at the map again. Looking at it was the first step down a road that ended either in the mountains or in a courtroom, and both of those places contained versions of herself she had worked very hard to kill.

But her finger was moving anyway. It always moved anyway. That was the disease her father had given her along with the gift: the lines pulled at her like gravity. A closed railroad was not dead to an engineer; it was sleeping. Every abandoned grade was a promise someone had made to the future and then walked away from, and somewhere in Beatrix Underhill, beneath the harbor clerk's cheap blouse and the fake ease and the carefully cultivated dullness, that promise still rang like a struck bell.

She traced the route all the way up. Ferry gone. Tunnel, roof sound as of '44. The hanging road, forty-one kilometers, condition unknown since before the war. The saddle at Tumam Pass, where her father had written, with what she recognized now as his version of despair: *snowline dropping year by year. By 1950 this will not be passable after April.* It was 1952. It was nearly March.

Sixty days. The arithmetic sat in her chest like a stone. Sixty days to cross the range, if the grades held, if the trestles held, if the tunnels held, if nothing else fell down anywhere between here and the pass. Sixty days, minus however long it took to buy provisions and hire porters and argue with Kell about terms, which left maybe fifty. Fifty days on grades that had swallowed better men than her, men with full crews and government backing, men who had not been carrying a secret debt shaped exactly like a noose.

She sat back. The lamp hissed softly. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady; that was the other thing people praised about her, back when anyone praised anything—but behind her eyes something was not steady at all.

She thought about Vane pulling her from the tunnel. She tried not to think about it, and failed, the way she always failed. The dark coming down. The sound of the shale letting go, that deep granular roar like the earth clearing its throat. Nine hundred tons. Her calculation had said the slope could take the tonnage. Her calculation had been wrong, or hurried, or flattered—she had wanted the schedule met, wanted the bonus, wanted her name in the division report above her father's, and she had rounded a safety factor down where she should have rounded it up, and Tom Aldery had gone in to set the charges on the face she'd certified, and the face had come down on him while she stood outside with her clipboard and her clean hands.

Vane had gone in after him. Not for Tom—for her. Tom was beyond saving in ninety seconds; everyone knew it; the foremen said so quietly and went back to work. But Vane had heard her screaming, or had seen the way she stood at the lip of the collapse wanting to crawl into it, and he had organized the dig himself, had crawled into the secondary gallery where the survival air pocket might be, and had found her wedged under a bent rail with her leg broken in two places and her hard hat crushed flat, and had dug her out with a hand spade while the gallery overhead groaned like a ship in a storm.

Eleven years ago. And for eleven years, the ledger of that debt had grown interest compounded in silence. Not money. Vane never asked her for money. Money was beneath the transaction. What he asked, every few years, in a letter with no return address, was obedience. Small things, at first. A signature on an affidavit. A phone call she didn't want to make. Then larger things, and then the largest thing of all, which was silence itself—the permanent, purchased, well-paid silence of a woman who knew exactly what her falsified calculation had cost and had agreed, in writing, in her own shaking hand, to spend the rest of her life pretending otherwise.

The papers Kell had brought said sixty days. The papers said *payable in kind*. The papers did not say what kind. But Beatrix had read enough of Vane's instruments to know that the blank spaces in his contracts were never blank; they were just waiting for her to fill them in herself, so that the trap would be, in the legal sense, entirely of her own construction.

Access to the cave. That was the kind. She had known it since the moment Kell laid the folder on her counter and smiled with teeth that were too even, too white, too much like dentures bought wholesale.

And so the map lay open on her desk, and her finger had walked its dotted spine, and she understood—with a clarity that felt less like decision than like diagnosis—that she was going to go. Not because she wanted to. Because the alternative was Vane taking the license she had hidden in the same tin case as the map, the license that was the last true thing left of who she had been, and worse than the license, her freedom, which Vane had already demonstrated he could take whenever he wished and had merely, so far, chosen not to.

She would go up the closed lines. She would find the cave. And she would deliver whatever was inside it to the man who owned her, and then—

And then what?

She didn't know. That was the part that kept the lamp burning past midnight. The part of the plan after the plan had no plan in it. She was fifty days from the pass and eleven years from honest and there was no version of the next two months that ended with her hands clean. She knew that the way she knew the weight of shale on a tunnel roof: by feel, in the bones, without needing to work the numbers.

She folded the map along its old creases, gently, the way you fold a flag. She slid it back into the false bottom of the tin case, under the expired ferry schedules and the bundle of letters from her father she had never answered because answering them would have required telling him the truth, and he had died before she found a lie good enough.

The lamp went out. She lay in the dark listening to the tide come in wrong, shallow and late, and she did not sleep so much as hover, and when morning came it came gray and heavy over the harbor like a lid being lowered.

* * *

The market was loud the way it always was on Saturdays, loud with betel-stained laughter and mackerel sellers shouting prices and children running barefoot between the stalls with more speed than caution. Beatrix had learned to shop early, before the crowds, precisely because she had learned to be invisible, and invisibility in a port town was a discipline like any other: you arrived at odd hours, you bargained badly on purpose, you never let your eyes rest on anything longer than a stranger's eyes would rest.

But today she had come late, on purpose, because she needed something she couldn't ask for directly. Rope. Good rope, climbing rope, the kind sold not at the chandlers but from the back of a stall near the fish section, where a wiry man named Sopu sold coil and chain and secondhand pit gear to the few miners still trickling down from the interior claims. Buying climbing rope was a statement. Buying climbing rope said *I am going somewhere vertical*, and Beatrix had spent eleven years making sure she said nothing at all.

So she was standing at Sopu's stall, testing the flex of a hemp line against her boot heel, when a man stopped beside her.

She noticed him the way you notice weather—not suddenly, but all at once, the way a change in pressure makes itself known in your ears before you've consciously registered the cause. He was tall, broad-shouldered, dressed in a faded army shirt with the sleeves torn off at the seam and trousers rolled to mid-calf, and he carried himself with a stillness that didn't match the market's noise, the way a heron stands in a flowing creek. His skin was the deep brown of the river people from the middle basin, and there was a scar running through his left eyebrow, old and clean, the kind that comes from a branch or a blade and is sewn by someone who knows what they're doing.

He was looking at her hands.

Not at the rope. At her hands, at the way she handled the line—at the way she coiled it over her forearm in flat turns instead of loops, the way riggers and engine crews coil rope, the way her father had taught her before she was ten years old, back when the only ropes in her life belonged to cranes and couplings and the winch house at the Karawari landing.

"You coil like a fitter," the man said. His English was accented but precise, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "Not like a clerk."

"I used to be," Beatrix said, and stopped there, because stopping there was the discipline, and the discipline was the whole of her life.

"Used to be what?"

"A fitter." She let the word stand alone, amputated from everything it implied. *Fitter* was safe. Fitters were tradesmen. Nobody in Wewak connected a woman who coiled rope like a fitter with the woman whose name had been in the division reports eleven years ago, because nobody in Wewak remembered the division reports, and the ones who might had been paid, long ago, to forget.

The man watched her a moment longer. Then he picked up the coil she'd been testing, weighed it in his hand, and set it down again with a small dismissive motion, as if returning something worthless.

"That rope is rotten," he said. "Sopu keeps it for tourists. If you are going up the mountain, you want the line in the oil drum behind the stall. The one soaked in tar. Ask him for the *strong rope*, and pay for the length, not the coil."

"And how would you know I'm going up the mountain?"

The question came out sharper than she intended, edged with the particular paranoia of a woman who has spent a decade assuming every conversation is surveillance. But the man didn't bristle. He tilted his head slightly, the way his people apparently tilted their heads when a question amused rather than offended, and said:

"Because you are buying rope in March. Nobody buys climbing rope in March unless the mountain is calling them. And because yesterday, at the seawall, you drew."

Beatrix went cold.

Yesterday, at the seawall. After the tide ran out. After the cave mouth opened in the black rock below the old mission and the town stood at the rail staring down into the exposed throat of the cliff, everyone staring, everyone speculating, everyone saying nothing real. And Beatrix, out of habit—out of the habit that was older than her disguise, the engineer's reflex of recording what she saw because unrecorded ground was dangerous ground—had taken the stub of pencil from her shirt pocket and sketched the cave mouth in the margin of her freight ledger. Thirty seconds of work. An arch, a rubble cone, a watermark line showing the tide's reach. She had torn the corner off the page and folded it into her pocket, and she had believed, truly believed, that no one had seen.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"You drew the cave," the man said, mildly. "On the back of your paper. You held the paper against your knee and you drew fast, like someone who does it every day. Most people look at a thing like that and see a hole. You looked and saw structure. The arch is cut, not collapsed—I could tell that from where I stood, and you drew it like you already knew. Cut means made. Made means mine. Mine means passage. Passage goes up."

Everything he said was correct. That was the unbearable part. Everything he said was exactly, professionally, devastatingly correct, and it was correct in the vocabulary of her own trade, spoken by a man who had no right to the vocabulary, and it meant that the invisible life she had constructed was transparent to anyone who knew what to look for. She had hidden in plain sight for eleven years by being boring, and this man had undone the hiding in ninety seconds by being observant.

"My name is Ila," he said. "I track for parties going into the basin. River work mostly, sometimes overland. My grandfather walked for the Halvorsen expedition, before the war. Before my time."

Halvorsen. The name landed in Beatrix's memory like a coin dropped down a dry well—she heard it fall and fall and never heard it land. She knew the name distantly, the way everyone in the territory knew it: the expedition that went into the central range in 1911 looking for gold and never came out. Forty porters. Three Europeans. Gone. The company had sent one search party, which had also lost men, and then declared the matter closed and the deaths regrettable, and the file had gathered dust in a government office in Rabaul until the war burned Rabaul down, and after the war nobody had reopened it because reopening files was expensive and the company that would have been implicated had grown large and generous and well-connected in the years between.

Large and generous and well-connected. Large as an empire. Generous the way a spider is generous. Connected all the way, if you followed the thread, to a financier named Marcus Vane.

Beatrix did not say any of this. She said: "That was a long time ago."

"It was," Ila said. "Long enough that the men who did it are dead, or rich, which in this country is often the same thing." He said it evenly, without heat, the way a man states the depth of a river he intends to swim. "My grandfather's name was Yawan. He was twenty-two. He carried a compass and a rifle for the survey party, and he came back as far as the lower gorge, according to the stories, and then the stories stop."

The market noise seemed to recede around them, the way noise does around a conversation that has become load-bearing. Beatrix became aware that she was standing in a fish market holding rotten rope, being interviewed by a stranger who knew what she was, and that the interview had just acquired a subject she could not control.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because you are going up," Ila said. "You will go with whoever Vane sends, or you will go alone, or you will find a reason not to go and they will send someone worse. But you are going. I can see it in how you hold the rope. And a party going into the central range in March without a tracker from the middle basin is a party feeding the river." He paused. "I guide. That is what I am offering. Nothing else."

"Nothing else?"

"Nothing else." He said it twice, which in his manner of speaking was not emphasis but honesty—once for the ear, once for the record. "You will want to know my price. My price is not money. My price is this: if we find them, the dead get named. All of them. Written down, spoken aloud, carried out. Whatever else happens in that cave—whatever gold, whatever papers, whatever your Mr. Vane wants—my grandfather's name comes out. That is the whole bargain."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment, and he looked back, and between them hung the specific tension of two people who have each correctly identified the other as necessary and dangerous. She needed a tracker; the grades were jungle now, the bridges gone, the water stations dry, and a party of strangers walking her father's dotted line without local knowledge would die of a hundred small errors before they ever saw the snowline. And he needed—what? Not money. Not the cave. Something older and heavier, something that had been waiting in his family for forty years the way the gold had waited in the mountain: a name, spoken, written, witnessed.

Two needs, perfectly complementary, both of them pointed at the same black hole in a cliff face. It was the kind of alignment that made alliances, and the kind of alignment that made betrayals, and Beatrix—who had betrayed before, who knew precisely what the machinery of betrayal weighed and cost—felt the shape of both futures at once and did not flinch, because flinching was a luxury her life no longer stocked.

"I can't promise names," she said finally. "I can't promise anything about what's in there. I haven't seen it. Nobody living has seen it."

"I know what I am asking," Ila said. "I am asking you to carry a promise into a place where promises may be impossible to keep. I know that. I make it anyway, because the alternative is to let Vane's men walk in there alone, and I have seen what happens when such men walk into such places alone." Another pause, shorter. "They come back lighter. Things get left behind. Names get left behind."

"You've seen this."

"I have seen men come back from the interior with fewer porters than they took, and paperwork that explains it, and the paperwork is always signed by someone who wasn't there." He picked up the coil of tar-soaked rope from behind Sopu's stall himself, without asking, and handed it to her, and the gesture was somehow both service and warning. "Take this one. Pay for forty meters. Forty meters reaches the bottom of most things."

Beatrix took the rope. Her hands knew its weight immediately, the way her hands knew everything about loads—it was good line, tar-dark, dense, honest. She paid Sopu, overpaid him slightly to preserve her cover as a bad bargainer, and stood a moment in the fish-smelling shade with the coil on her shoulder and the stranger's offer sitting in her mouth like a stone she hadn't decided whether to swallow.

"Ila," she said. "If I go—if I'm made to go—I'll need a tracker. And I'll need one who understands that the man paying for this expedition is not a man who pays for surprises."

"He will get no surprises from me," Ila said. "Only the names. The names are the surprise, and they belong to me."

She almost laughed, and didn't, because laughing was another thing her life no longer stocked. Instead she gave him what she could give safely: a meeting place, a time, a cover story thin enough to survive casual inspection. The chandlery dock, Tuesday, dawn, ostensibly to negotiate porter rates for a legitimate-sounding government resupply run to a patrol post—she had already begun constructing the fiction, because constructing fictions was, at this point, the most practiced skill she owned.

He accepted. He turned and walked into the market crowd with that heron-stillness breaking all at once into motion, and she watched him go, and the watching cost her something, because watching him go meant admitting he existed, and admitting he existed meant the invisible life had just acquired a witness.

That night the lamp burned low and amber again, and the map came out of the tin case again, and her finger walked the dotted line again, slower this time, pausing at each of her father's marginal notes as though the notes were messages left for her specifically, which perhaps they were. *Roof sound as of '44.* *Snowline dropping year by year.* *For Bea, when she is ready.*

Ready. She was not ready. She was fifty days from the pass and eleven years from honest, and tomorrow she would begin lying to Dray, and Dray would begin lying to her, and somewhere above the snowline a cave that the locals refused to name was breathing in and out with the tide, holding whatever it held.

She wrote one word in pencil on the map's margin, in her own hand, beneath her father's lightest note. She wrote it small, so small it could be erased with a thumb.

*Tuesday.*

Then she folded the map away, and put out the lamp, and listened to the tide come in wrong, and did not sleep.
