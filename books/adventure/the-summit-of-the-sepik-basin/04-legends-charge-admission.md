## 4. Legends Charge Admission

Ila had watched the white woman for three days before he spoke to her, and in that time he learned more than most men learn in a year of talking.

He learned that she kept her hands busy even when there was nothing to do—that she would straighten the ledgers on the harbor office counter until their edges aligned like soldiers, then straighten them again. He learned that she ate alone at the end of the fish market, always facing the water, never the street, as if the sea were the only thing she trusted to stay where it was put. He learned that when the tide went out wrong on Tuesday and the bay lay open like a gutted fish, every other clerk came out to stare and gossip, but she stood very still, and her hand moved in her pocket, and he understood without seeing it that she was drawing.

Drawing the cave.

That was what decided him. Not the map—though he had seen the map too, glimpsed through the slats of her shuttered window on the second night, the fine ruled lines of old rail grades climbing into the highlands like veins on the back of an old man's hand. A woman who drew forbidden things from memory, who carried her father's closed lines folded against her body, who did not sleep when the tide breathed wrong—such a woman was going up the mountain whether anyone guided her or not. And a woman who went up the mountain unguided died in the green country between the river and the snow, or worse, reached the cave alive and took what she found back down to men like Vane.

Ila had buried enough of his family's dead already. He did not intend to bury them twice.

So on the third evening, when she came down to the water's edge with her tin of pencils and her habit of standing where the mud still shone wet and exposed, he walked out along the flats to meet her, barefoot, unhurried, the way his grandfather had taught him to approach anything that might bolt.

"You drew it," he said.

She turned. Up close she was younger than her eyes suggested and older than her hands, and she had the particular stillness of people who have learned that movement attracts attention. "I don't know what you mean."

"The cave." He nodded toward the headland, where the mouth of it showed now as a dark seam above the returning water, already half-swallowed by the tide's slow climb. "You drew it Tuesday night. I saw your lamp."

Something flickered across her face—not guilt exactly, and not fear, but the quick animal recalculation of a creature deciding whether the thing before it was predator or weather. "Everyone saw the tide go out," she said. "Half the town sketched it. Sailors sketch coastlines; it's a disease of the profession."

"You are not a sailor."

"I'm a clerk."

"You are a clerk who draws engineering." He let that sit in the air between them, salt-smelling and undeniable. "The way you held your pencil. The way you looked at the cave mouth—you were not looking at a hole. You were looking at a structure. At cuts and grades and load." He crouched suddenly and drew in the wet mud with one finger: three parallel lines, converging toward a point. "A tunnel. Driven, not found. Someone cut that opening with steel and powder, long ago, and the sea has been trying to take it back ever since."

The woman—Underhill, the ledger said, Beatrix Underhill, though the name meant nothing to him and everything, he suspected, to certain men in the lowlands—looked at his three lines in the mud for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed. The clerk was gone from it.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Ila." He wiped his finger on his thigh. "I know the road you're going to need. The one on the paper you keep behind the shutter."

Her stillness became something harder now, something with edges. "You've been watching me."

"I have been watching everyone," Ila said, "the way my people have always watched the lowlands. You watch the crocodiles. It is not rudeness. It is survival." He stood, and nodded again at the dark seam in the headland, where the last light of day was bleeding away into the water. "But we should not talk about this here. There is a man named Kell who asks questions in the trade stores, and a man named Dray who does not ask questions at all, and both of them work for the same fat spider in the hills. Come. If you want the legend, the legend costs tea."

* * *

The place he took her was not a shop but his aunt's kitchen behind the copra shed, a room of smoke-blackened bamboo and one kerosene lamp, where his aunt boiled strong black tea sweetened enough to strip paint and said nothing to either of them beyond setting down two enamel cups. Ila chose the seat with his back to the wall and the door in view, and noted, with a small cold approval, that the white woman chose hers the same way, without appearing to think about it.

"You know the name Halvorsen," he said.

It was not a question, but she answered it anyway, carefully. "There's a Halvorsen Building in the capital. Mining concern. Old money."

"Old blood, you mean." Ila wrapped both hands around his cup, though the tea was hot enough to hurt, because holding something kept his voice level, and his voice needed to be level for what came next. He had told this story perhaps six times in his life, each time to someone who wanted the gold and none of the rest, and he was tired of telling it to such men. But this woman had drawn the cave like an engineer, and engineers, he thought, might be able to hear the whole of it.

"In 1911 a man named Erik Halvorsen came up the Sepik with forty porters and a company survey team. Gold. Everyone knew there was gold in the high country—the traders' stories, the panners' stories, the stories the mountains tell about themselves. Halvorsen believed them all. He was rich and impatient and he did not believe the mountains could refuse him." Ila drank, grimaced at the sweetness, drank again. "My grandfather was one of the forty porters. His name was Tomasu. He was twenty-two years old and he carried sixty pounds of Halvorsen's ambition on his back, and he wrote letters home that my family still keeps, wrapped in oilcloth, in a box under the floor of a house that no longer stands."

Beatrix Underhill set her cup down. She did it quietly, but he saw her fingers were not quite steady. "What happened to them?"

"No one living knows. That is the honest answer, and I will give it to you first because everything after it will be less honest." He looked into the lamp flame rather than at her, because some things were easier said to fire. "They went up in the dry season. The last message came down from a mission station on the middle river—a request for more rice, more cartridges, and a note that they had found 'the door.' Then nothing. No party came down. No porter came down. Forty men and eleven Europeans, gone into the high country like smoke into wind."

"The company searched?"

"The company *claimed* to search. Two relief parties, both in the wet season, both turning back at the first flooded river with great photographs of brave men in canoes. Then the company stopped searching, and started paying. Pensions to the families—small ones, paid irregularly, paid with conditions." His jaw tightened. "My grandmother signed a paper to receive hers. She could not read it. Years later, when my mother learned to read, she asked to see the paper, and the company office had burned, they said. All the records. Every page. In a land where paper does not burn easily, all of it, gone."

"And the legend?" she said softly. "The gold?"

Ila laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, with grief inside it wearing laughter's clothes, and he heard that himself and did not care. "Ah. The legend. Yes. The legend says Halvorsen found a mountain full of gold, richer than any reef ever worked, and that the company men murdered the porters to keep the location secret—or that the porters mutinied over their wages and were slaughtered—or that the mountain itself closed its mouth and swallowed them all, which is what my aunt believes and what she will tell you if you ask her, because it is easier to blame a mountain than a man." He set down the cup. "Every version ends the same way. Forty porters do not come home. Eleven Europeans do not come home. And a company that cannot find its own lost expedition somehow finds the money to become an empire."

He watched her absorb that. Watched her mind move behind her eyes the way a current moves under still water, fast and deep and going somewhere specific. She was doing arithmetic. He could see it: Halvorsen, Vane, the building in the capital, the debt-collector Kell asking questions in the trade stores, the enforcer Dray who did not ask questions. The web assembling itself in her head, thread by thread.

"Your grandfather," she said. "Tomasu. You want to know what happened to him."

"I know what happened to him," Ila said. "He died in the high country in 1911. What I do not know is *where*, or *how*, or whether he died carrying Halvorsen's gold or Halvorsen's secrets or only his own two legs and his own good heart. My family has waited seventy years for a grave. Seventy years for a name spoken aloud by someone who knew him." He leaned forward. "And now the sea pulls back its skirt and shows a door that the old people have always refused to name, and a white woman with an engineer's hands starts drawing it in the night, and men with guns start buying rope and rice in the trade stores. So yes. I know what happened to him. What I want to know is whether you are going to bring me back the truth, or bring me back another silence."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, covering the three lines he had drawn in the mud, erasing them the way the lowlands erased everything eventually.

"What is it you're proposing?" Underhill said at last. Her voice was flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "Because I'll tell you plainly, Ila—I'm not free. I'm going up that mountain because a man owns a piece of my life and wants what's in that cave as payment. Whatever I find, I may not get to choose what happens to it."

"I know," Ila said. "Kell talks loudly in trade stores. Dray buys rope." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, once, twice. "So here is my price, and it is not gold, and it is not negotiable. I will guide you over the closed lines and through the green country and up to the snow before it closes the pass. I will read the rivers for you and the leeches for you and the weather, which this year is reading itself wrong. I will carry your pack if your pack needs carrying. And in exchange—" he held up one finger, "—you promise me this. Whatever is in that cave—bones, papers, names—if the dead of the Halvorsen expedition are there, they come out. Their names come out. Written down, spoken aloud, carried down to the villages where people still remember them. Not sold. Not traded. Not left behind because they are heavy or inconvenient or bad for business. *Named.*"

"That may cost you," she said. "Or cost me. Names are evidence, and evidence makes men like Vane dangerous."

"I am already dangerous," Ila said. "I have been dangerous since 1911. I simply did not know it until now." He extended his hand across the little table, palm open, the way his grandfather's letters described men making bargains in the old days, before the companies taught everyone to shake instead. "Promise me the names, Beatrix Underhill, and I promise you the mountain."

For a long moment she did not take his hand. He watched her weigh it—he actually watched the weighing happen, the way it must have weighed in her when Vane pulled her from a collapsed tunnel, the way it must weigh in her every time Kell's shadow crossed a doorway: survival on one side, truth on the other, and herself in the middle holding the scale. He had seen that look before, on other faces, in other years, and he knew that some who wore it chose survival until the choosing killed them anyway.

Then she took his hand. Her grip was calloused in the places a draftsman's and an engineer's grip is calloused, and it was stronger than he expected, and it did not flinch.

"The names," she said. "I promise."

"My grandfather's first among them."

"Tomasu," she said, pronouncing it carefully, getting it right on the first try, and something in Ila's chest that had been clenched for thirty years loosened by a single degree—not open, not healed, just loosened, the way a rusted gate loosens when you lean on it the first time.

"It is done, then," he said, and released her hand, and finished his tea, which had gone cold and was still too sweet.

* * *

He walked her back through the town as the lamps were being lit, taking the long way, past the trade stores where Dray's purchases sat stacked in the doorway—rope, rice, cartridges, canvas—and past the hotel veranda where Ambrose Kell sat drinking with two men whose boots were too clean for the basin, and Ila felt the woman beside him register all of it without turning her head, cataloguing threats the way he catalogued weather.

At the corner near the harbor office she stopped.

"Why tell me the story?" she asked. "You could have guided me for money like anyone else. Why spend your grandfather on a stranger?"

Ila considered the question honestly, because she had earned honesty and because the answer surprised him a little when he found it.

"Because you drew the cave like a structure," he said. "Not like a purse. The others who have asked me about the mountain—they look at the headland and see gold. You looked at it and saw cuts and grades and load-bearing stone. A person who sees a mountain that way might also see forty men that way. As built things. As things that were made and then destroyed, and can be known." He shrugged. "Also, you promised the names before you asked what you would get. That is rare. Usually the asking comes first."

She almost smiled. It was a small, damaged thing, that smile, like a rail bent by floodwater—still recognizable, still functional, but bearing the mark of what had passed over it.

"Tuesday," she said. "We leave Tuesday, if the boat can be hired. There's a man—"

"I know the man," Ila said. "I will be at the jetty at dawn. Bring the map. Do not show it to Dray."

"How do you know about Dray?"

"I told you," Ila said, already turning away, already moving back into the blue dusk where the watchers lived. "I watch the crocodiles."

He left her there at the corner, a thin silhouette against the lamplight with the sea breathing wrong behind her, and he walked home through the smoking town thinking of Tomasu, twenty-two years old, sixty pounds on his back, walking up into the green silence with forty brothers and never walking down. Thinking of oilcloth and letters and a box under a vanished floor.

Thinking: *Grandfather, I am coming. And I am bringing a stranger who keeps promises badly, maybe—but she keeps them.*

Above the town, unseen behind cloud, the mountain waited with its door half-open and its mouth full of tide, and somewhere in the dark of it, Ila did not doubt, forty names lay waiting to be spoken, patient as bones, older than empires, ready.

Dawn was two days off. The snows would not wait much longer than that.
