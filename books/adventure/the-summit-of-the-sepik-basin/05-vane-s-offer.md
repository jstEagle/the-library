## 5. Vane's Offer

The summons came the way weather comes to Wewak—not announced so much as *felt*, a pressure behind the ears, a stillness in the palms that made the harbor clerks glance at one another and say nothing.

Beatrix was balancing a column of copra weights when the boy arrived. He couldn't have been more than twelve, barefoot on the hot boards of the customs shed, holding out an envelope as though it might bite him. Cream-colored stock. A wax seal pressed with a monogram she recognized from her father's old trade journals: **MV**, entwined like a pair of snakes.

She wiped the coconut oil from her fingers before she touched it. Some habits of respect die hard, even for the things that ruin you.

*Miss Underhill,* the card inside read, in a hand so controlled it looked typeset. *You will dine with me this evening. The Meridian Room, seven o'clock. Wear something that doesn't smell of the harbor.*

No question. No *if it pleases you*. Marcus Vane had never in his life asked for anything; he had only informed the world of what would happen next, and the world—Beatrix included—had generally obliged.

She read the card three times, then folded it along its crease and slid it into the drawer where she kept her father's map, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath a false bottom of ledgers she'd never balance honestly. The two documents lay together now, map and invitation, and she understood with a lurching clarity that they had always been meant for each other. That perhaps they had been traveling toward each other across four years and half an ocean, and tonight they would finally meet.

"You look," said Mrs. Okoro from the next desk, not looking up from her own figures, "like a woman who has been asked to dinner by a shark."

"Sharks are honest," Beatrix said. "They eat you face-first, where you can see it coming."

Mrs. Okoro snorted and went back to her copra. Outside, the bay had refilled days ago, quiet again, the cave mouth drowned once more beneath green water—as if the sea had shown them something by accident and was embarrassed about it now, keeping it hidden the way a miser keeps his strongbox.

Seven o'clock. She had six hours to decide how frightened to be.

She spent all six deciding, and arrived at the Meridian Room frightened exactly as much as she needed to be: enough to keep her careful, not enough to make her stupid.

* * *

The hotel had been built by the Germans before the war and reclaimed by nobody since, and it wore its decay like an old officer's uniform—faded, moth-eaten, but still buttoned correctly. Ceiling fans turned with the lazy authority of things that had never been refused anything. The Meridian Room occupied the seaward corner, all louvred shutters and white linen gone faintly yellow, and Marcus Vane sat at the best table in it as though the room had been constructed around him retroactively.

He was smaller than she remembered. That was the first shock. In her memory—the tunnel, the dust, the enormous hands hauling her up through collapsing shale—he had been a giant, a force of geology. Sitting here in a linen suit the color of bone, stirring a drink he clearly wasn't drinking, he was just a man of sixty-odd with a boxer's flattened nose and eyes the pale gray of river ice. The smallness didn't comfort her. Small men who owned everything were more dangerous than large ones, because they had something to prove.

"Miss Underhill." He rose—a courtesy, nothing warmer—and gestured to the chair opposite. "You found the place. I worried the harbor might have swallowed your sense of direction."

"The harbor and I have an arrangement," Beatrix said, sitting. "It leaves me alone and I leave it alone."

"Until last week." He smiled. It was a good smile, professionally maintained, the kind that reached the eyes without ever being *in* them. "When it gave something back."

A waiter materialized with the silence of long practice. Vane ordered for both of them without consulting her—fish, naturally, the catch of the day—and Beatrix let him. There was no percentage in fighting over a menu when the real negotiation hadn't started yet.

"You've been busy," Vane said, when the waiter had gone. "Four years as a shipping clerk. Copra weights and cargo manifests. You reconcile accounts to the penny—I checked. Do you know what I find remarkable about that?"

"That anyone bothers checking."

"That you're *good* at it." He leaned back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. "You could have been mediocre. Deliberately bad at arithmetic, quietly drunk, forgettable. Most people hiding from their pasts choose one of those three. But you couldn't help yourself, could you? Every column balanced. Every manifest perfect. Because you're an engineer, Miss Underhill, even without the license. It leaks out of you like light under a door."

The fish arrived. Beatrix looked at hers and did not pick up her fork. "You didn't fly me across the territory to compliment my bookkeeping."

"No." The smile remained, but the temperature around the table dropped several degrees, the way it does when a cloud crosses the sun. "I didn't."

He took his time. That was part of it, she realized—the performance of patience, the demonstration that he could afford to wait while she sat there marinating in dread. He cut his fish into small precise portions. He ate one. He dabbed his mouth.

"Sixty days ago," he said at last, "a collector named Ambrose Kell delivered certain papers to you. A debt. My debt—or rather, the debt I hold against you, which amounts to the same thing. You'll recall the terms."

"I recall them."

"Then you recall that they were unusual. Not payable in currency. Payable in—" he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping a gift slowly because the unwrapping is the pleasure— "*access*. To a cave that did not exist until the earth decided to open it. I confess, when I wrote those terms, I thought the tide might never cooperate. That the sea would keep its secret and you would spend your life in honorable servitude, balancing copra columns until your hands fell off. A tidy little punishment. Almost a mercy."

"And then the earthquake came," Beatrix said.

"And then the earthquake came." He set down his knife and fork, aligned them parallel on the plate's rim, and looked at her directly for the first time. Those ice-gray eyes had a quality she remembered from the tunnel—from the moment the dust cleared and she'd seen his face above her, backlit by the lamps of the rescue crew. Not kindness. *Interest.* The interest of a man examining an asset he intends to use until it breaks. "The sea opened its hand, Miss Underhill. And now I want to know what's in it."

"You don't know?"

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, quickly mastered, but she'd seen it, and she filed it away the way she'd once filed stress readings off bridge cables. Marcus Vane did not enjoy being asked questions. More interestingly: he did not entirely know the answer to that one himself.

"I know what the legends say," he said smoothly. "Gold. The Halvorsen expedition, 1911. Forty porters and a fortune in bullion that walked into those mountains and never walked out. I know that my grandfather's company financed Halvorsen, and that when the expedition failed, the company absorbed certain... assets... and certain liabilities, and moved on. I know that for fifty years men have died in those ranges chasing a story." He lifted his glass, considered it, set it down untouched. "And I know that whatever is in that cave belongs to me. By right of inheritance if not by law. The law, as you may have noticed, has never much troubled itself with the interior."

"So you want me to go get it."

"I want you to *lead* an expedition to get it." He produced an envelope from his breast pocket and laid it on the table between them, squaring its corners to the table edge with two fingers, precisely, the way he did everything. "There is a route over the range. The old rail grades—closed lines, abandoned since the war, but graded, cut, engineered. Someone with your particular education could read them. Could follow them. Could get a party through before the snows close the passes, which by my reckoning gives you"—he consulted a pocket watch he did not open—"perhaps five weeks."

"You know about the grades." Her voice came out flat. "You know my father surveyed them."

"I know everything about you, Miss Underhill. That is rather the point of owning a man's debts—you acquire the whole man. Or woman." The smile again, brief as a knife-flash. "Your father's maps. Your apprenticeship on the highland surveys. Your talent, which was considerable, and your crime, which was also considerable, and my discretion regarding both, which has been *very* considerable indeed. Four years of discretion. Do you know what discretion costs, compounded annually?"

Beatrix's hands were steady in her lap. She made them stay that way through pure engineering will, load distributed, tolerances held. "Name your terms."

"Simple. You lead the party through the closed country to the cave. You bring back what's inside—all of it, every gram and every document, sealed and inventoried to my specification. Deliver it to me, and the debt dies. Burned. Forgiven. Forgotten. Your license restored, your history cleaned, your future yours." He spread his hands, magnanimous, a man offering the world on a salver. "Everything you've spent four years hiding from, returned to you intact."

"And if I fail?"

The word *fail* seemed to please him. He turned it over like a sommelier turning a cork.

"If you fail—if the mountains stop you, or the snows, or your own famous conscience—then Kell returns with new papers. And the new papers say that Beatrix Anne Underhill, formerly licensed engineer number 4471, falsified the load calculations on the Karawari trestle, causing a collapse that killed two men, and that Marcus Vane has known this for four years and protected her, and can unprotect her with a telephone call and a stamped envelope." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the flat of his nose, the ice of his eyes. "The license goes first. Then the freedom. There are jurisdictions in this territory where a woman with your debts can be indentured legally, Miss Underhill. I've checked. I check everything."

The fan turned. Somewhere below, the tide moved against the pilings, patient as arithmetic.

"You should know," Beatrix said, and was surprised at how level her voice came out, "that I've considered simply running. Disappearing properly this time. Up the coast, or across it. There are places even your papers don't reach."

"There are," Vane agreed pleasantly. "But you won't. Do you know why?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Because I saved your life. Whatever else you feel about me—and I imagine it's a rich mixture—you cannot quite hate a man who pulled you out of a collapse with his own hands. It's a debt of a different kind, and it binds tighter than the ledger kind. You'll come back to me, Miss Underhill, because some part of you believes you owe me. And that part is correct." He sat back, satisfied, a man closing a ledger with a soft clap. "That is why I chose you. Not your maps. Not your engineering. Your *gratitude*. It's the most reliable chain ever forged."

She wanted to throw the wine in his face. She wanted, more treacherously, to agree—because he was right, and knowing he was right felt like another kind of collapse, the ground going out from under her a second time in one life.

Instead she said: "You're sending someone with me."

It wasn't a question, and they both knew it, and his expression acknowledged the hit with something almost like enjoyment. A dealer's appreciation for a decent opponent.

"Naturally. An expedition requires provisioning, discipline, communication. I'm assigning Mr. Dray." He nodded toward the room's entrance, and Beatrix turned in her chair.

The man stood by the louvred doors where the corridor light put his face in shadow—big, deliberate, dressed in field khaki already broken in, boots already muddy, as if he'd been walking toward this dinner for days. He had a face like a closed crate: nothing written on it, everything packed inside. As she watched, he crossed the room without hurry and took up a position behind her chair, and she felt his presence the way one feels a change in air pressure before a storm.

"Mr. Dray will carry the radio," Vane said. "Daily reports. He'll handle any... logistical disagreements within the party. And he'll ensure that whatever comes out of that cave comes out *complete*. No souvenirs, Miss Underhill. No private arrangements. If a single gram arrives in Wewak outside my inventory, the new papers go in the mail before your boat touches the dock." He raised his glass at last and drank, finally, as though the business were concluded and pleasure could resume. "We understand each other."

"We understand each other," Beatrix repeated.

"Splendid." He signaled the waiter. "Dessert is excellent here. The papaya, particularly. One should always eat well before a journey."

* * *

She walked back along the waterfront because she couldn't bear the idea of walls yet. The night was warm and smelled of diesel and salt and rotting fruit, and the harbor lay black and smooth, holding its cave somewhere beneath it like a coin under a tongue.

Behind her chair, all through dessert, Dray had stood without moving. But once—only once—when Vane had looked away toward the window, Beatrix had caught the big man watching her. Not with menace. With something harder to name. Appraisal, maybe. The look of a man measuring a beam before trusting it with weight.

*Five weeks,* she thought. *The closed grades, the snowline, the cave, and Dray's radio reporting every step of it back to the man who owns my grave.*

At the customs shed she unlocked the drawer and took out the oilcloth bundle and unrolled her father's map by lamplight, tracing the ghost-lines of the abandoned grades north and east, up into country no train had run through in thirty years. The paper was soft as cloth at the folds. His pencil annotations were still sharp: *grade too steep here — retimber trestle — spring flood cuts this crossing.*

And there, in the margin near the highest pass, in handwriting she knew better than her own: *Ask Ila's boy about the upper track. He knows the mountain better than the surveyors did.*

She stared at the name for a long time. She had assumed it was a dead man's note, a name from another generation.

Then she remembered the stranger at the harbor's edge the morning the tide went out—the one who had watched her sketch the cave mouth, who had said nothing, whose stillness had the quality of a man listening to something far away.

She rolled up the map. Five weeks. The trap had closed with a smile, and she had walked into it carrying her father's pencil-marks like a lantern.

Outside, far down the dark waterfront, a figure detached itself from the shadows of the fish racks and began, unhurriedly, to walk toward her.

He moved the way the tide had moved—inevitable, unhurried, arriving exactly where he had always intended to be.

"Ila," she said, before he could speak.

"You have the map," he said. It was not a question either. "Tomorrow we talk. Tonight, sleep. The mountain has already heard you're coming."
