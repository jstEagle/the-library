## 6. Provisions and Lies

Dray had packed supplies for men he did not trust into crates he intended to count twice, and even that felt like generosity.

He stood in the back room of Kessler & Sons Outfitters on Wewak's front street while the proprietor's son dragged the last of the order down from the loft—canvas, rope, tinned meat, quinine—and Dray checked each item against the list in his head because the list on paper was a lie he'd written himself. Vane wanted an expedition provisioned for twelve days. Dray was provisioning it for twenty. The difference would sit in his own pack, distributed through the load like arsenic in a wedding cake, invisible to everyone but him. If Vane meant to strand them all up there—and Dray had begun to suspect Vane meant exactly that—then Dray intended to be the man who could still walk out.

Not out of sentiment. Sentiment was a currency he'd stopped carrying years ago. It was simply arithmetic: a dead enforcer collected no wages, and a stranded one collected nothing at all.

"Rice again?" the proprietor's son asked, breathing hard under a crate stamped with faded Dutch shipping marks.

"Rice keeps," Dray said. "Everything else rots."

The boy grinned as if this were wisdom instead of exhaustion, and Dray let him. There was no percentage in correcting anyone in this town. Wewak ran on small misapprehensions the way the harbor ran on tide, and a man who went around draining them found himself standing in mud with nothing to show for it.

Dray had been in the basin country before—not this far in, but enough to know what the interior did to equipment. Leather bloomed white mold overnight. Matches softened in their boxes until they struck like damp chalk. A rifle bolt could seize in its receiver between breakfast and noon if you didn't wipe it down with oil you'd then need for your own skin, because the mosquitoes here didn't bite so much as mine. He bought accordingly: oilcloth by the roll, not the sheet. Salt pork in sealed tins rather than barrels. A second compass, because the first would inevitably end up in someone else's pocket, and he preferred to know whose.

The list of personnel had come from Vane's office two days ago, typed on the good paper, the paper reserved for things Vane wanted done properly. Five names. Himself. Beatrix Underhill. Ila—the tracker, no surname given, which told Dray something about how Vane filed people who weren't useful to him on paper. Two porters hired out of the coastal villages, brothers named Anis and Tomel, recommended by the mission at Ambunti, which meant they were honest, which meant they were dangerous, in Dray's experience. Honest men asked questions.

And there was the problem of the woman.

* * *

He'd watched her for three days now, ever since Vane's letter had summoned her to the office above the shipping warehouse, and everything he'd seen confirmed what Vane had paid him to confirm: Beatrix Underhill was not what her file said she was.

The file said disgraced railroad engineer, discharged for negligence, debt-ridden, cooperative. Dray had read enough files to know that a file was a story someone had agreed to stop asking questions about. What he saw in the flesh was something else—a woman who moved through the harbor district like water finding its level, unnoticed by everyone, noticing everything. She took her coffee at the same stall every morning at the same hour, which looked like habit and functioned like surveillance. She knew which lighter carried cargo for the interior and which ones were coasters. She knew the name of the pilot who ran the Sepik in flood season and the fact that he'd lost his license for a year in '29. None of this was in her file. All of it was in her face, briefly, when she thought no one was reading it.

Yesterday she'd gone to the map seller's stall near the church, the one run by the half-caste Chinese with the bad eye, and Dray had followed at the distance that made following indistinguishable from walking. She'd bought nothing. She'd stood over the spread of charts—the coastal surveys, the government sheets with their confident blank spaces where the mountains should have been—and she'd read them the way other people read letters from home. Then she'd folded her hands behind her back, which was what careful people did when they didn't want ink on their fingers, and left without touching anything.

A woman planning a trip buys maps. A woman who already has one looks at other people's.

That evening he'd reported to Vane through the usual channel—the coded telegram to the trading post at Bogia, relayed down the coast, arriving on Vane's desk as a shipping inquiry about copra prices. *Prospective passenger conversant with interior routes beyond expectations. Source of knowledge unclear. Recommend continued observation.* Thirty words, billed as forty, the surplus pocketed the way Vane pocketed everything and expected to be pocketed from. It was understood between them, the way certain things are understood between certain men, that the ledger always balanced in Vane's favor and that Dray's compensation lay in the difference between what Vane knew and what Vane suspected, which was where Dray lived.

Vane's reply came back inside a day, which was fast, which meant it mattered. *Determine source. Discreetly.*

Discreetly. The word arrived wrapped in its own irony, because discretion, in Dray's line of work, was mostly a matter of choosing which blunt instrument to apply gently.

* * *

The opportunity came sooner than expected, and it came wearing a smile.

Beatrix Underhill came to the outfitter's herself on the fourth morning, ostensibly to inspect the provisions Vane had ordered for her expedition, actually—Dray assumed—to see what he'd bought and therefore what he expected. He watched her work down the row of crates with a professional's economy, lifting lids, pressing canvas between finger and thumb, weighing a tin of salt pork in her palm and setting it down with the faint nod of a woman who'd provisioned worse expeditions with less money. She checked the rope by uncoiling three fathoms and running it through her hands, and Dray noted that she checked the core, not just the sheath, which was a trick you learned either from a good teacher or a bad accident.

"You've done this before," he said, because saying nothing would have been more suspicious than saying the obvious.

"Railroad supply trains." She set the coil down. "Different problems, same arithmetic."

"What arithmetic is that?"

"That everything weighs something, and everything you carry is something you didn't carry instead." She said it lightly, conversationally, and then her eyes went past his shoulder to the crate he'd been repacking when she came in—the one with the extra rice, the spare boots, the second medical kit—and stayed there perhaps two seconds longer than politeness required.

Dray let the silence do its work. Silence was the only tool he owned that never rusted.

"Mr. Vane wants us comfortable," he said finally.

"Mr. Vane wants us alive long enough to be useful." She smiled when she said it, a real smile, which unsettled him more than a false one would have, because false smiles were transactions and real ones were decisions. "I've met men like Mr. Vane before. They don't spend money on comfort. They spend it on insurance. So the question I keep asking myself is: insured against what?"

"And what answer do you keep arriving at?"

"That I'll know when I get there." She turned back to the crates, ending the conversation the way a surveyor ends a sight-line, cleanly, with the instrument closed. Before she left she paused at the door and said, without looking at him, "You count everything twice. I noticed."

"I count everything three times," Dray said. "But who's counting."

She laughed—one short syllable, genuine—and walked out into the heat, and Dray stood among his crates feeling as though he'd been weighed, measured, and found slightly transparent, by a woman whose file described her as broken.

Broken things didn't look at you like that.

* * *

That night he made his report, and for once the report wrote itself slowly, because the truth had gotten complicated.

The complication had a name, or rather it had a shape: the map. He'd seen it—or seen its edge—two days ago, quite by accident, or by the kind of accident that happens when a man makes a habit of being in rooms before the person who uses them arrives. Beatrix kept her gear at the mission guesthouse, and Dray had cultivated the mission's groundskeeper with two weeks of small kindnesses and one large loan, and the groundskeeper had a master key and no curiosity, which was the ideal combination. Dray had gone through her room in eleven minutes flat, a personal record for the tropics, where humidity turns every search into a wrestling match with your own sweat.

He'd found the map rolled inside a bamboo tube beneath the floorboard under the bed frame, wrapped in oilcloth, and he'd unrolled exactly one corner of it—enough to see the drafting style, the hand-inked contour lines, the notation in a spidery engineer's script along the margin—and then rolled it back exactly as it had been, because a man in his trade learned early that the difference between searching a room and robbing it was whether the owner could tell you'd been there.

The margin note was what stayed with him. It wasn't coordinates. It was a sentence, in faded iron-gall ink: *Grade surveyed 1908, abandoned 1913, passable on foot to km 47, washout beyond.* And beneath it, in different ink, newer, bluer: *Condition of km 47 onward unknown.*

Unknown. Someone had written *unknown* on a map that old, recently, in ink that hadn't faded.

So when Vane's telegram came back—*determine source*—Dray sat on the veranda of the boarding house with the moths committing suicide against the lamp glass and composed his reply with the care of a man defusing something. He couldn't say *she has a private map of the closed lines*, because that raised the question of where she'd gotten it, and the answer, per the file, was her father, and the further answer, which Dray had extracted from a retired harbormaster over three rums and a lie about shared service, was that her father had been a surveyor on those very lines before the company abandoned them, and that the company in question had been absorbed, consolidated, and rebranded into the empire Marcus Vane now ruled.

Which meant the map was a relic of Vane's own company's history. Which meant Vane might already know it existed. Which meant Dray genuinely did not know whether he was reporting a discovery or confirming something his employer had arranged, and a man in Dray's position survived by knowing the difference.

He sent the safe version. *Subject possesses detailed route knowledge, likely familial origin, engineering background. Map condition uncertain. Subject claims deterioration. Assessment: claim inconsistent with observed confidence.*

Then he put down the pencil and poured the last of the rum and sat with the thing he hadn't sent, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it.

Because here was what he'd actually concluded, alone on the veranda with the harbor breathing below him: Beatrix Underhill was lying about the map. She'd lied smoothly, unprompted, when Ila had asked her—in Dray's hearing, at the waterfront, two days ago—whether the old grades were marked on any chart she carried. *It's my father's copy,* she'd said. *Half the ink's gone to mold. The middle section's unreadable.* And she'd said it easily, warmly, with the small self-deprecating shrug of a woman admitting a weakness, and it had been a performance so clean that Dray had almost believed it, and he did not believe things easily.

But he'd held the map. The ink was faded, yes. The middle section was legible. Every contour, every culvert, every gradient marker from the river crossing to the high shoulder of the range. She'd lied about the one thing that made her valuable to Vane and vulnerable to everyone else, and she'd lied to protect it, and the lie itself told him more than the map did.

It told him she expected to be searched. It told him she expected to be robbed. It told him she was playing the same game he was, at the same table, with the same stakes, and had possibly been playing it longer.

* * *

On the sixth day, the expedition assembled on the beach north of the town, where the lighter *Marianne* grounded herself twice daily on the tide to take on cargo for the river stations. Dray supervised the loading with his manifest in a waterproof sleeve, checking each crate aboard against his list, and the porters Anis and Tomel worked with the easy rhythm of brothers who'd been hauling together since childhood, and Ila arrived without luggage worth mentioning—a bedroll, a blowpipe case, a canvas satchel that hung on him like it had grown there—and stood at the tideline watching the water with the patience of a man who had nowhere else to be and no intention of proving otherwise.

Beatrix came last, down the beach path with her pack riding high on her shoulders and the bamboo tube strapped across it, and Dray watched Ila watch her, and watched her watch Ila right back, two people taking each other's measure across thirty feet of wet sand, and neither of them pretending otherwise, which was more honesty than Dray had witnessed in the basin country in six years.

The tide was going out. The *Marianne* settled onto the sand with a groan of timber, and the loading ramp came down, and Dray counted his crates aboard—twice, visibly, letting them see him do it, because a man who counts openly has nothing to hide, and a man with nothing to hide is the most dangerous kind—and then, at the last, he added the crate that wasn't on any list. The one with the extra rice. The spare boots. The second medical kit. His own insurance, stowed in the bow compartment under a tarpaulin, keyed to a lock only he could open.

Beatrix stood at the rail watching him stow it. She didn't ask. That was the thing about her—he was beginning to understand—that she never asked the question that would force a lie. She just noted, and waited, and let time do the interrogating for her.

The lighter backed off the sand on the evening tide and turned her blunt bow toward the river mouth, and the town of Wewak shrank behind them to a smear of corrugated roofs and coconut palms, and the green wall of the interior rose ahead of them out of the haze like something surfacing.

Dray stood at the stern rail with his back to the land and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the veranda. Five people aboard. One employer who wanted something in a cave. One debtor who wanted a debt dead. One tracker who wanted names for his dead. Two porters who wanted wages and had no idea what they were carrying besides rice. And somewhere ahead, up a river and over a range, a cave mouth that the locals wouldn't name, full of whatever it was full of.

Four of those five people trusted him, in the way that passengers trust a crewman—with their lives, without thinking about it. He was being paid to make sure that trust was misplaced. And he was keeping a private reserve against the possibility that his employer planned to dispose of the evidence along with the expedition, which meant he was already disloyal to somebody; the only open question was to whom.

The first stars came out over the range. Below deck, he could hear Anis singing quietly to the river, some endless looping song about a fish and a grandmother, and Tomel laughing at him, and Ila silent as always, and Beatrix's voice, low, asking Ila something about the water level at the second rapids.

Dray lit a cigarette against regulations and smoked it down to the filter, watching the glow mark his position on the dark water like a signal fire.

In the morning they'd reach the first of the closed lines. In the morning, the cat-and-mouse would stop being metaphor. He touched the lock on the bow compartment once, confirming it, the way a gambler touches the card up his sleeve, and went below to sleep with his boots on.
