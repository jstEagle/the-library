## 7. Into the Green Silence

The river took us the way a thief takes a purse—quickly, and without apology.

We had left Wewak before dawn in two long canoes hollowed from ironwood, paddled by men Ila had hired with money that was not mine and would never be. The harbor had been gray silk when we pushed off, and by the time the sun cleared the headland it had turned to hammered brass, and by midmorning the coast was a green smudge behind us and there was nothing anywhere but water and the walls of forest pressing down to meet it.

I sat amidships with my pack between my knees and my father's map rolled inside my shirt, against my skin, where sweat had already begun to soften the paper. Dray sat in the bow of the second canoe like a piece of cargo that had opinions. He had not spoken since the mouth of the Sepik. He did not need to. He watched everything the way a man watches a card game he has already fixed—the other players simply hadn't been told yet.

The heat came down on us like a lid. Not the dry heat of the rail yards I'd known in another life, where the air moved and lied about being merciful. This was wet heat, breathing heat, a heat that got inside your clothes and stayed there, and by noon I had stopped noticing the difference between the river's spray and my own sweat. The porters stripped to their waists and paddled in an unhurried rhythm that I understood, after a while, was not slowness at all but arithmetic. They were spending exactly as much as they needed to spend. Nothing more. A man who wasted motion out here would be dead in a week, and they knew it in their bones the way I knew load tolerances in mine.

Ila knelt in the prow of our canoe and read the water.

That is the only way I can describe it. He did not look at the river; he looked *along* it, reading it the way I once read a gradient profile—a lift of the chin here, a narrowing of attention there, a small frown at a stretch of brown water that looked identical to every other stretch of brown water and was, apparently, not. Twice he raised a hand and the porters swung the canoes toward the far bank for no reason I could see, and both times, within a minute, we passed over water that boiled suddenly around a submerged tree, whole trunks rolling beneath the surface like the backs of sleeping animals.

"What do you see?" I asked him the second time. I meant it as a genuine question. It came out sounding like a challenge, because everything I said these days came out sounding like something.

"Water tells you what's underneath," he said. "If you ask politely."

"And if you don't?"

"Then it tells you anyway." He glanced back at me, and there was something in his face—not quite humor, but adjacent to it. "Later. When you're swimming."

Dray, in the other canoe, asked no questions. That frightened me more than the submerged trees. A man who asks questions wants to learn. A man who asks none has already decided what he knows.

* * *

We made the first crossing on foot in the late afternoon, where the river braided itself into channels wide as streets and shallow as guilt. The porters went first, barefoot, testing each step with a stick before committing their weight. Ila told us to unbuckle our packs' waist straps so we could shed them if we went down. Dray kept his cinched tight and crossed leaning upstream with his boots on, and the current took him to the hip and held him there, quarreling with him, until two porters waded out and hauled him loose by the shoulders.

He came ashore furious. Not scared—furious. It was the fury of a man who believes the world has made an error.

"The bottom drops off," he announced, as though reporting a defect in merchandise.

"It doesn't drop off," Ila said quietly. "It was always there."

I bit the inside of my cheek to keep my face still. But Ila saw it anyway, whatever it was I almost did, and something passed between us—one of those brief, wordless transactions that begin to happen between people thrown together in hard country, whether they want them or not.

The leeches found us that evening. Of course they did. We were five warm animals moving slowly through standing water, and the forest had been waiting for us with the patience of something that has outlasted empires. They came off the grass blades in the dusk, thin as threads, and attached at ankle and calf and, in the porter named Anis's case, one audacious individual at the collarbone. You don't feel them attach. You feel them later, when they're fat and your blood is theirs, and you peel them off and the wound keeps bleeding because their saliva holds anticoagulant, and you stand there in the dark watching your own body leak and thinking: this is what the basin does. It doesn't attack. It attaches. It drinks a little at a time and lets you walk away believing you've lost nothing.

I thought about Vane while I picked leeches off my ankles. I am not proud of how often I think about Vane. Sixty days. Somewhere behind me, in a lowland office with ceiling fans and good whiskey, a man was counting those days down with the serene patience of a creditor who knows exactly what collateral he intends to collect.

Camp that first night was a sandbank shelf above the waterline, under a stand of sago palms. The porters built a fire small enough to hide and cooked rice and tinned fish, and the smoke rose straight up through a gap in the canopy as though the sky had cut a chimney there on purpose. Insects arrived in their millions and formed a halo around the firelight, and beyond the halo the dark was total and full of sound—frogs, insects, something large moving through the undergrowth with no interest whatsoever in concealing itself, because nothing out here needed to conceal itself except us.

Ila ate apart from the rest of us, cross-legged, facing the river. Afterward he walked the perimeter of the camp without a torch, silently, feeling the ground with his feet. I watched him and understood that I was watching a man perform a ritual older than any ledger I'd ever signed. He wasn't checking for danger. He was introducing himself to the place. Asking permission, in the only language the basin respects, which is attention.

"You've done this before," Dray said to him. It was not a compliment. It was reconnaissance dressed as conversation.

"I've walked," Ila said.

"Where?"

"Everywhere the company didn't." He let that sit. Dray flinched internally—I could see it, the small recalibration of a man whose employer's name had just been used as a boundary marker—and Ila turned back to the dark and said nothing more.

I lay on my groundsheet listening to the river and did not sleep. My body ached in places I had forgotten I owned. My mind ran its old circuits: the map against my skin, softening; the grade over the range, forty years closed, washed out in three places according to my father's notes and passable in one, maybe, if the rains had been kind; the snows, which even here, even in this furnace of a country, waited at altitude like a debt of their own. Everything out here was a debt. The forest lent you passage and collected in blood and time. Vane had merely learned the local custom.

Somewhere past midnight I finally drifted, half-asleep, into that state where the river sounds become a single animal breathing—

And then I woke, completely, all at once, the way you wake when a room has changed.

The fire had burned to orange coals. The porters slept in a row under a lean-to of palm fronds. Dray's bedroll was empty.

I did not move. There is a discipline to fear; I learned it in a collapsed tunnel nine years ago, pinned under four hundred tons of rock, waiting eleven hours for a man I now owed everything and hated completely. You do not move until you know what moved. You breathe. You listen.

I listened. Footsteps, very soft, on sand. Behind me. At my pack.

My pack. Where the map was not—where the map had not been since Wewak, since the night I'd sewn it into the lining of my spare shirt after watching Dray inventory our supplies with too much care. My pack held rice, a change of clothes, my drafting tools, a tin of kerosene matches, and a decoy: a folded sheet of paper covered in false contour lines I'd drawn myself, plausible enough to fool a man who read maps the way Dray read them, which was badly.

He went through it the way a professional goes through things—no rustle, no hurry, items lifted and replaced in order. I kept my breathing slow and even and watched the coals through half-closed eyes and felt the leech bites itch and did not scratch. Ten minutes. Fifteen. Then the faint click of my brass compass case being opened and shut, tested, and set back exactly where it had lain, and the weight of him withdrawing across the sand, and the long silence of a man settling back into his bedroll as though he had never left it.

I waited until the frogs resumed. Then I waited longer.

What struck me, lying there in the dark with the river talking to itself, was not anger. It was clarity. This was the transaction laid bare at last: Vane had sent a man to search my belongings on the first night, before anything dangerous had happened, before any trust could form or fail. Which meant Vane had never trusted me at all—which meant, some cold arithmetic part of me noted, that Vane had never intended to forgive the debt regardless of what I brought down from that cave. You do not send a searcher after a partner. You send a searcher after a suspect.

The wilderness strips pretense the way the river strips a bank. One day of walking and everyone in this party had shown their hand: the porters showed competence and patience; Ila showed that he read the land and distrusted the company in equal measure; Dray showed that he obeyed orders I had not heard given; and I showed—what? That I slept lightly, that I noticed, that I had prepared. That I was playing too.

Good, I thought, lying in the dark. Let him report that I'm careful. Let him tell Vane the map in my pack is damaged, faded, incomplete—because tomorrow, when Dray asks me directly about the route, I will lie smoothly, and the lie will match the decoy in my pack, and the trap Vane thinks he's closing will have a second door in it that only I know about.

Toward dawn the rain came, a warm hammering rain that lasted twenty minutes and raised steam off the sand, and when it ended the fire was dead and the sky was the color of pearl and the forest stood dripping and indifferent, having taken our noise and our firelight and our little human arrangements and absorbed them all without comment.

Ila was up first. He crouched at the edge of camp examining the sand, and I watched him find what I already knew was there—the boot prints, the path to my pack and back—and I watched him read them, and I watched him understand.

He looked at me across the cold fire ring. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. His eyes asked the question that mattered, the only question that mattered now:

*Do you know who searched your pack?*

And I met his eyes and answered honestly, which is to say I answered carefully, which out here is the same thing:

"Yes. And so do you. The question is what we do about it."

He straightened, shouldered his own pack, and looked upriver toward the mountains we could not yet see—toward the closed lines, the cave, the snows coming early this year if the cold on last night's wind meant anything.

"We walk today," he said. "Fast. Before he decides what to report."
