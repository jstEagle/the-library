## 8. The Washed-Out Grade

Ila heard the grade before he saw it.

Not the rails—those had been gone for sixty years, sold or stolen or sunk into the mud somewhere between here and the coast. He heard the water. A stream that should have murmured through its bed was falling instead, a long white ribbon down a face of dressed stone, and where it struck the rocks below it made a sound like slow applause, like a crowd that never stopped clapping, patient and enormous.

He smelled it too. Cut stone has a smell when the jungle works at it—wet iron and old mortar and something green and sweet, like the inside of a rotting log. His grandfather's generation would have known that smell from the inside. They had cut these faces with hammers and black powder and their own hands, forty of them, and then they had walked up into the mountains and never come back down.

The party came up out of the ravine in single file, the porters bent under loads that had grown heavier with every day of rain, and Ila stopped them on the last rise before the clearing. He wanted them to see it whole first. Some things you should meet standing up.

It ran along the contour of the mountain like a scar that had learned to live there—a shelf cut into the slope, wide enough for two wagons abreast, walled on the outside by a retaining wall of fitted blocks taller than a man, roofed on the inside by the overhang of the hill itself. The jungle had taken everything soft. Vines hung off the wall in ropes thick as his wrist. A fig tree had rooted in the drainage channel and now stood eight meters tall, its roots prying at the stones with the slow confidence of something that knew it would win eventually. But the line itself—the grade, the shelf, the geometry of it—was intact. It went on around the shoulder of the mountain and out of sight, level as a table laid for guests who never arrived.

"Careful," Beatrix said, and her voice was different than it had been all week. On the river she had been quiet, watchful, a woman keeping her head down the way you keep a flame cupped in wind. Here she stepped forward onto the stone and stood straight, and it was like watching someone put on a coat that fit.

She walked to the edge of the washout.

There was a gap in the wall, maybe thirty meters of it, where the mountainside had let go sometime in the decades since the last train—whether in an earthquake or simply because water is patient and stone is not, no one could say. The grade simply ended. Below the gap the slope fell away in a tumble of blocks and root mats and young trees growing sideways out of nothing, down and down to the river, which showed itself in flashes through the canopy, brown and muscular.

Beatrix crouched at the broken edge and put her hand flat on the last standing block.

"Coursed rubble," she said. "Not random. Look at the pattern—every stone shaped to sit against its neighbors, no mortar doing structural work, just gravity and geometry. Whoever built this understood mass." She moved her hand along the joint. "And see how the face is battered—leaning back into the hill, maybe one-in-six. That's not decoration. A vertical wall fights the earth pushing on it. A leaning wall lets the earth help hold itself up. Someone was thinking about pressure."

Dray stood back with his arms crossed, radiating the boredom of a man who found rocks boring. The three porters waited behind him, shifting their loads. But Ila watched Beatrix, and what he watched was not the wall.

"The culverts are why it's still here," she went on, almost to herself. She pointed upstream along the grade, to where the little ribbon of waterfall crossed the line. "Amateur work buries the water and hopes. This builder gave it doors." There were arched openings in the wall base, half-swallowed by vine, and the stream ran through one of them clean as through a doorway. "Every crossing got a culvert sized for the flood, not the creek. So when the storms come, the water goes through instead of over, instead of taking the whole structure with it. Sixty years without maintenance, and it's still standing, because someone sixty years ago sat down and asked: what will this mountain do to my wall in a hundred years? And then built for the answer."

She said it plainly, without pride, the way a fisherman describes weather. And Ila felt something turn over in his chest—not warmth, not yet, but attention. The sharp kind. The kind you give a track in soft ground.

Because he had spent six days deciding what she was. That was the work of a guide, really: you read people the way you read country, and you plan accordingly. He had read her as a debtor first—that was obvious, written in the way she flinched whenever Dray spoke, in the papers Kell had carried into Wewak, in the careful invisibility of her harbor-clerk life. Then, on the river, he had read her as a liar, a competent one, smooth about the map's condition in a way that told him the map mattered more than she admitted. Liars he could work with; the basin was full of them. But liars were small. Liars wanted things and hid wanting, and that was a shape he knew how to walk around.

This was a different shape. This was a woman who looked at dead men's stonework and saw the living question inside it.

"You know this trade," he said. Not a question. He kept his voice flat, guide's voice, giving nothing.

She glanced back at him, surprised to be addressed, or surprised to be caught. "I was an engineer," she said. "Railroads. Before."

Before. The word had weight on her, like a load shifted wrong. He noticed she did not say *I am*.

"Your father's map," he said. "The closed lines. You didn't inherit an interest. You inherited a language."

Something moved across her face—too fast to name, gone before it finished arriving. Grief, maybe. Or guilt. Both wore the same expression on some people.

"My father surveyed half these grades," she said finally. "He used to take me out on weekends when I was small, walking abandoned spurs, teaching me to read ballast the way other fathers taught daughters to read scripture. He believed a line was a promise. That if you built it honestly, it would carry honest weight forever, and if you cheated it—" she stopped, and her jaw tightened, and she looked away down the long green corridor of the grade, "—if you cheated it, the mountain would find out. Eventually. The mountain always finds out."

Ila turned that over. *The mountain always finds out.* He thought of the bones in the gravel bar two days ago, the ones Dray had wanted to walk past, the ones Ila had refused to leave unmarked. He thought of the cave above, unnamed, waiting. The mountain finding out. Maybe that was what all of this was—not a treasure hunt, not a debt collection, but the mountain conducting its long audit, and all of them, porter and engineer and enforcer alike, merely figures moving through the ledger.

They made camp on the grade itself, in the lee of the wall, because the stone drained better than any ground in the basin and because, Ila suspected, Beatrix had chosen it and he had let himself be persuaded without admitting it. The porters rigged the tarps. Dray built a fire with his usual competence and his usual silence, feeding it like a man feeding something he didn't trust. Rain came in from the east at dusk, the fine warm rain that wasn't rain so much as the cloud descending to sit among the trees, and everything went soft-edged and close.

*

Around the fire, Beatrix changed again.

Ila had been watching for it all evening without knowing what he waited for. She sat apart, sketching—she sketched constantly, he'd noticed, in a battered notebook, quick pencil lines, sections and elevations, the way other people breathed. Tonight she was drawing the washout. The gap in the wall. And as she drew she talked, quietly, mostly to the paper, but Ila was near enough to hear, and the porters drifted near too, because a fire draws everyone and talk draws porters like sugar draws ants.

"The gap can be crossed," she said. "That's the good news. It's ugly, but it's crossable." She held up the page: the gap rendered in hard pencil strokes, arrows showing the fall line, a dotted line where a crossing might go. "The wall failed where the culvert silted—you can see it, the stream above the gap runs shallow and braided, dumping its gravel right there. Water backed up behind the fill, soaked it, and the whole wedge let go. Classic failure. My father would have called it a drowned fill." She tapped the drawing. "But the blocks are all still there. They've fallen forty meters downslope, but they're there. Which means the stone is sound. The wall didn't rot. It was killed."

"Killed," repeated Yawan, the eldest porter, and his eyes went bright with interest.

"Murdered by its own drainage," Beatrix said. "Or by neglect. Same thing, on a long enough timeline." She turned the notebook toward the firelight. "We cross at the downstream end of the gap, where the surviving wall is highest and the drop is shortest. Rig a handline off that fig tree—it's rooted deep in the undisturbed fill behind the wall, its roots are holding more stone than the masonry is at this point. Anchor to the trunk, not the branches. Traverse across the fallen blocks—they're stable, they've had decades to settle—and regain the grade on the far side where the wall steps down. Two hours of work, four hours of crossing with loads. Slower with the rain. Doable."

Dray listened with his jaw working, chewing something, saying nothing. Then: "You've done this before. Crossings."

"I've rebuilt worse," Beatrix said. And then, quieter, in a tone Ila had not heard from her before, a tone with no performance in it at all: "I've also destroyed things I shouldn't have. That's the other thing I know about walls. What kills them isn't usually the mountain. It's the arithmetic done cheap."

The fire cracked. The porters leaned in. And Ila sat very still, because he understood he was hearing something rare: a confession being rehearsed. Not the full one—whatever she had buried, whatever Vane held over her, that was still sealed, still down in the dark chambers of her—but a crack in the seal. *Arithmetic done cheap.* She had done arithmetic cheap once. He was certain of it now, as certain as he was of the lean in that wall. It was in her voice the way a limp is in a gait.

He should have pressed. A guide who wanted leverage would have pressed. Instead he found himself doing something else entirely: he found himself believing her. Believing that she hated it. Believing that the hatred was newer than the crime, and heavier, and that she was carrying it up this mountain on purpose, the way the porters carried their loads—because someone had to, and because setting it down at the bottom would mean it had beaten her.

"Tomorrow we cross," he said, and it came out as agreement rather than command, which surprised him. "First light. Rain or not."

Beatrix nodded, and rolled the notebook closed, and Ila saw on the cover, stamped faintly in old gold, letters worn nearly away: a name. An Underhill name. Her father's notebook, or bought from his estate, or inherited with the debts. The dead man traveled with her. Everything traveled with her—the map, the debt, the shame, the skill. She was a woman hauling an entire wrecked railway up a mountain on her back, and until tonight Ila had only seen the cargo. Now he saw the crane.

He could not sleep, and near midnight he gave up trying, and walked the grade in the rain-dark with his grandfather's compass in his hand.

The needle swung and settled, swung and settled, pointing north-northwest along the line's bearing, and Ila walked it, one hand trailing the top of the old wall, and thought about hands. Forty pairs of hands had dressed these blocks. He tried to feel them in the stone—the chisel marks were still there under his palm, fine fan-shaped scars where each blow had landed, thousands of blows, a conversation conducted in percussion between men and basalt. His grandfather might have been one of those hands. Might have cut the very block Ila was touching. The thought went through him like cold water through a culvert: fast, total, leaving everything rearranged.

The company had named the line after a director, of course. Companies did that. The director had seen it once, from a launch on the river, through opera glasses, and gone home to London. The men who cut it had names in no ledger anyone kept—or ledgers that were kept and then burned, or kept and then buried, which amounted to the same thing. Ila had promised himself, the night Beatrix agreed to carry him along, that whatever was in that cave, the dead would be named. He had thought of it then as bargaining, payment, terms. Walking the grade tonight he understood it was simpler than bargaining. It was bookkeeping. Somebody had to keep the true books, and the company's books were lies, and Vane's books were lies, and even Beatrix's books—he thought of *arithmetic done cheap*, and his hand tightened on the compass—even hers had a poisoned column somewhere. So the mountain kept the real ledger. In bone, in stone, in the memory of streams. And he, Ila, son of a son of a porter, was going to read it aloud.

Ahead of him the grade curved around the shoulder of the mountain and vanished into cloud, and beyond the curve, if the map and the legend both told true, it climbed—switchbacking up through country no one had walked in his lifetime, toward the snowline, toward the cave, toward whatever the earthquake tide had uncovered and whatever it had been covering all along.

Behind him, at the fire, Beatrix slept badly. He could hear her turning, the whisper of the tarp, once a low murmur of words he couldn't catch—apology-shaped, dream-shaped, aimed at someone who wasn't there. Her father, maybe. Or the men in the tunnel. Or the mountain itself.

He came back to camp wet through and stood a moment at the edge of the firelight, looking at her. Debtor. Liar. Builder. All three were true, and none of them was the whole truth, and tomorrow they would step out onto the broken wall together, roped to the same fig tree, trusting the same roots, and the mountain would be watching to see what they were made of.

In the grey before dawn, Ila woke her gently, as agreed. She surfaced fast, sharp, already reaching for the notebook.

"Rain's lighter," he said. "We cross now, while the stream sleeps."

She looked at the gap, then at him, and something passed between them that had not passed between them before—brief, wordless, the look of two people who have independently arrived at the same respect. She slung her pack. He checked the handline anchor twice, because his grandfather had taught him that a knot tied once is a question and a knot tied twice is an answer.

Then Beatrix Underhill stepped onto the fallen blocks of the murdered wall, testing each stone with her boot heel before trusting it, reading the fill like text, and Ila followed her out over the drop with the rope singing softly between them—and halfway across, in the mist, she paused on a block the size of a coffin and said, quietly, almost laughing:

"It's sound. It'll hold. They built it to hold."

And below her feet, forty meters down, the river went on applauding.
