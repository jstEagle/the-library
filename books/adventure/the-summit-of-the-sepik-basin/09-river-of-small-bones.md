## 9. River of Small Bones

The river had been singing to them for two days before it showed them what it kept.

Beatrix heard it first as a change in the air—a thinning of the green silence they had walked through since leaving the last village behind, a faint mineral brightness in the water's voice that meant gravel, shallows, a bend where the current spread itself thin over stone. Ila heard it too. She watched him stop on the trail with his head tilted, the way she herself once stood at the mouth of a tunnel and listened for the sound of rock under strain, and something in her chest tightened at the recognition. Two different trades, the same instinct: read the ground before it reads you.

By midmorning they came down out of the tree ferns onto a wide gravel bar, pale as a scar across the river's brown shoulder. The party strung out along it—Tomas and Ekwai and young Pita with the loads, Dray ranging ahead with the confidence of a man who believed the world was a corridor built to his width, Ila moving along the waterline like a heron, patient and exact.

It was Pita who found them.

Beatrix had stopped to re-tape the blister on her heel, sitting on a drift log silvered by years of flood, when the boy's shout came up the bar—not a cry of alarm, not quite, but that particular rising note of discovery that every expedition knows, the sound that stops all work. She looked up. Pita was crouched twenty yards downstream, hands hovering over something in the gravel, not touching it, and his face had already changed from excitement to something older and more careful, the way children's faces change when they understand, suddenly and completely, what they are looking at.

Bones.

Not one skeleton laid out whole—that would have been almost gentle, almost legible. This was worse. A scatter. A femur half-buried where the flood had rolled it. A rib cage opened like a broken basket, its arcs filled with river sand. A skull resting on its side in the shallows, the water combing through it in small bright threads, and around it—Beatrix counted before she could stop herself, because counting was what she did, counting was how she made the world hold still—around it, more. A jaw here. A hand's worth of finger bones there, still nearly articulated, curled as if around something that had long since washed away or rotted or been taken.

She walked toward them slowly, and the others converged from their separate points along the bar, so that within a minute all five of them stood in a loose ring around the dead, and no one spoke.

The river talked instead, indifferent, running its fingers through the gravel.

"They're old," Beatrix said finally, because someone had to say something, and her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Decades, at least. The bone's gone chalky. Sun-bleached, flood-tumbled." She had seen remains before—in the mountains, in the cuttings, men lost to slides and never recovered because recovery cost more than grief allowed. She knew what time did to a body left outdoors. It didn't hide it. It distributed it.

"Halvorsen," Ila said.

He said it quietly, but the name went through the group like a blade through wet canvas. He had not moved closer. He stood at the edge of the ring with his arms folded, and Beatrix saw that his stillness was not calm—it was the stillness of a man holding a door shut against something enormous.

"The 1911 expedition came up this valley," he said. "Forty porters. My grandfather was one of them. They went into the high country after the gold and they did not come back. The company sent one search party, in the dry season, and they turned back at the snowline and reported the route impassable. Then nothing. For fifty years, nothing." His eyes moved across the scatter of bone without hurrying, without flinching. "No graves were ever dug. No names were ever said."

Dray shifted his weight. Beatrix heard the gravel crunch under his boot and it sounded, absurdly, like a gavel.

"So we know what happened to some of them," Dray said. "Nature happened. River happened. Fifty years ago, before any of us were born." He looked at each of them in turn, and his gaze when it reached Beatrix carried a message underneath the surface message, the way a telegraph wire carries a second conversation in the hum. *Keep moving. Don't make this a thing.* "We're losing light and we're losing altitude days. We bury them or we don't, either way they're dead."

"That is not what burial means," Ila said.

"It's what it means today, on this schedule."

"Then your schedule is wrong."

The words landed between them and neither man picked them up and neither man stepped back, and Beatrix felt the bar tilt beneath all of them, though the ground hadn't moved at all.

* * *

They argued for an hour, standing, while the sun climbed and the loads sat unshouldered in the shade of a pandanus clump and the river ran on with its terrible patience.

Beatrix learned things about people in that hour that no amount of travel together would have taught her. She learned that Tomas, the eldest porter, took Ila's side without saying a word—he simply began, very quietly, to gather flat stones from the margin of the bar, the kind of stones you build a cairn from, and the gathering was his vote and his argument both. She learned that Ekwai was afraid—young men are always afraid of the dead, and she did not blame him, she remembered being twenty-two and walking past a collapsed adit knowing there were men inside—but that his fear bent toward Ila rather than against him, the way green timber bends toward the weight it trusts. She learned that Pita, who had found the bones, had already appointed himself their keeper, and would not eat, and would not sit down, and stood watch over the scatter like a boy guarding a wounded bird.

And she learned about Dray. Not anything new, exactly—she had known since Wewak that he was Vane's instrument, that his politeness was a loaded weapon held muzzle-down—but she saw it now in full daylight, in open country, with witnesses. When reason failed him he reached for authority. When authority failed him he reached for implication. "You want to spend a day stacking rocks," he said to Ila, "fine. But understand what that costs. Every hour here is an hour off the snow window. If winter closes the pass early—and it will, it always does early in a bad year—that's on you. That's on all of you." And then, softer, in the tone of a man sliding a knife between ribs without breaking skin: "Vane doesn't pay for delays. Vane pays for results. Remember whose names are on the payroll."

*Whose names,* Beatrix thought. *That's exactly the question, isn't it.*

Because that was the fracture line, and everyone on the bar could feel it even if only some of them could name it. Dray served a ledger. Ila served the dead. And the dead, unlike the ledger, could not be settled, deferred, renegotiated, or bought out. The dead only asked one thing, and they asked it forever: *say our names.*

Ila had made her promise that, back in Wewak, in the harbor office with the map spread between them like a truce table. *Promise me the dead get named.* She had promised, and she had meant it in the shallow way promises are meant when the cost is theoretical. Now the cost was standing in front of her in pieces on a gravel bar, chalk-white and flood-worn, and the promise had grown teeth.

"I'll say this once," Dray said, in the end, when it was clear the argument was going nowhere he could steer it. "We mark them. Quickly. Stones, whatever, an hour, no more. We do not dig. We do not search. We do not turn this bar into a shrine. And we do not—" and here his voice dropped into something genuinely cold, colder than the threat about schedules, "—we do not write anything down. No notes. No sketches. No record of location. Nothing that goes in a report. Because reports get read, and the wrong people read them, and then this becomes a problem with lawyers attached, and lawyers become problems with police attached, and none of us wants to be attached to that."

*Nothing that goes in a report.*

Beatrix stood very still. There it was—the thing she had been circling for days without quite touching, the shape of the trap she had walked into with her eyes open and her judgment closed. Vane had not sent Dray to protect them. Vane had sent Dray to protect *the route*—to control what was seen, what was recorded, what came back down the mountain in anyone's memory but his own. An expedition with no records was not an expedition. It was a secret wearing boots.

And secrets, she knew better than anyone alive, were how men like Marcus Vane built empires. You didn't need to burn the evidence if you simply arranged never to create it.

She caught Ila's eye across the bar. He gave her nothing—no nod, no appeal, no alliance offered. He was past alliances in this moment; he was inside his grief like a man inside a burning house, and you do not negotiate with someone through a window of fire. But she understood anyway. The promise she'd made in Wewak had just come due, sixty days early, payable in full, right here on this river.

* * *

They marked the dead.

It took most of the day, and Dray spent it the way a foreman spends a shift he resents—counting, timing, muttering about the light. But even he worked. Even Dray carried stones. Beatrix watched his hands, big and blunt and careful despite themselves, setting each rock with a precision that contradicted everything he said, and she understood that somewhere under the enforcer's armor there was a man who had once been somebody's son, and that the bones bothered him more than he would ever admit, and that this knowledge might matter later, at some hour she could not yet foresee.

Ila led the work, and watching him was like watching her father read a bridge. He walked the scatter slowly, reading the river's arrangement of the dead, and where he found bones he gathered them toward the center of the bar, upstream of the flood line, above the reach of the next big water. He wrapped the skull in bark cloth. He set it last, facing upriver, facing the high country the forty had climbed toward and never returned from.

Pita sang. Beatrix hadn't expected it—the boy had barely spoken since the discovery—but as the cairn rose he began a low, wavering chant, old words in his grandmother's language that he clearly only half understood, and the others joined where they knew the lines and hummed where they didn't, and the song went on and on, repetitive as surf, and somewhere in its middle repetitions Beatrix stopped hearing it as noise and started hearing it as architecture: a structure being built out of sound, the way she built structures out of steel, designed to bear weight, designed to hold.

She added her own stones. Eleven of them, she counted—one for each year since the tunnel, since the collapse, since the lie, since the debt. She didn't tell anyone that. Some counts you keep private, the way you keep a wound private, not from shame but because a wound displayed becomes a performance, and hers was not for performing. It was for paying off. Stone by stone. Name by name. Starting, perhaps, here.

Ila spoke the names he knew. There were only four—the ones his family had preserved, passed down through fifty years of telling, the grandfather and three others whose families had lived close enough to keep the memory warm. Four names out of forty. He said each one twice, the way you set a stone twice to be sure it sits true, and when he finished he stood silent for a long moment with his hand flat on the cairn, and Beatrix, watching, felt something crack open in her chest that she could not immediately close.

Four names. Thirty-six missing. Somewhere up-valley, presumably, in the country they were walking into, lay the rest—or lay the place where the rest had died, which was not the same thing and might be worse. And Ila was going to find them. She saw that now with total clarity. He had not joined this expedition for wages. He had joined it because the river had just handed him the first physical proof in fifty years that his grandfather had ever existed, and he would follow that proof into the snow itself.

Which meant, Beatrix realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with altitude, that Ila was now the most dangerous member of the party—not to her, but to the mission as Vane had defined it. A guide who wanted records was a guide who would write things down. A guide who wanted names was a guide who would dig.

Dray saw it too. She watched him see it. Watched the calculation move behind his eyes like weather.

* * *

They camped that night on the terrace above the bar, and the fracture did not heal with darkness; it deepened, the way cracks deepen overnight in freezing rock.

Dray took first watch and spent it, Beatrix was fairly sure, composing his report in his head. Around the fire, conversation died early. Tomas smoked and said nothing. Ekwai slept facing away from the river. Pita lay awake, Beatrix could tell by his breathing, guarding the day's grief the way he'd guarded the bones.

Only Ila stayed by the fire late, feeding it stick by stick, and Beatrix stayed too—not to talk, not at first, but because she could not sleep and the fire was honest company. For a long time neither of them spoke. The river ran below, louder in the dark, carrying its cold down from the icefields they were climbing toward.

"You counted the stones," Ila said at last, without looking at her. "Eleven."

She went still. She had told no one. "You count everything," she said. "It's what trackers do."

"No." He turned his head, and the firelight moved on his face. "Trackers count tracks. What you did was different. Eleven stones is a ledger. You're settling accounts with yourself." He let that sit between them, uncomfortable and precise. "One stone for every year you've been paying for something. Am I close?"

She should have deflected. She was good at deflecting; she'd had eleven years of practice. Instead she heard herself say, "Close enough."

Ila nodded slowly, and did not press, and that restraint told her more than questioning would have. He filed it. Trackers file things—bent grass, broken spiderweb, a stranger's number of stones—and they retrieve the file later, when the trail demands it.

"Tomorrow we climb," he said. "Two days to the old rail grade, if the washout hasn't taken the lower switchbacks. After that, everything your map says and nothing I can vouch for." He poked the fire, and sparks went up and died. "But hear me, Beatrix Underhill. Whatever we find up there—and we will find something, the river has already told us that—I am not leaving those mountains until every name is written down. In ink. On paper. In a copy that leaves this valley in more than one pair of hands." He looked at her directly then, and his voice was quiet and absolutely immovable, bedrock under topsoil. "You promised. I'm telling you now so there's no surprise later: I intend to collect."

She met his eyes. She thought of Vane's hand pulling her out of the dark, and of the paper that hand had held ever since, and of the difference between a debt and a promise—which was, she understood suddenly, the entire moral architecture of her life reduced to a single distinction. A debt you owed to a creditor. A promise you owed to the truth.

"I know," she said. "So do I."

Across the dead fire-pit, in the shadow beyond the coals' last orange reach, she heard a boot shift—soft, deliberate, unmistakable. Dray had been awake. Dray had heard.

And in the morning, when they broke camp and shouldered the loads and turned upriver toward the high country, Beatrix noticed that Dray fell into step beside her, closer than he needed to, and spoke low, under the sound of the water, in the voice of a man making an entry in a ledger that had nothing to do with money:

"Interesting night," he said. "Lots of interesting things said around fires. Vane pays me to remember interesting things." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Walk carefully, engineer. The grade gets steep from here."

Above them, hidden in cloud, the old line waited—and with it, Beatrix understood, whatever it was that Marcus Vane had truly sent them up the mountain to find, or to never find, or to never come back from.
