## 10. Storm on the Shoulder

Ila smelled the storm two hours before the sky did.

It came on the wind out of the northwest, a smell like wet iron and cold stone, and beneath it something older—the mountain itself getting ready to speak. He had grown up reading that language. His grandfather had taught him, back when his grandfather was still only a name in stories, before he became a name among forty. *The mountain warns you first,* the old man had said, or had been said to have said; Ila could no longer be sure which of his memories were inheritance and which were invention. *The mountain always warns you first. It is men who refuse to listen.*

He stopped on the trail and turned his face into the wind and listened.

Behind him the line of porters strung out along the washed-out grade like beads on a broken cord: Tomas with the survey gear, young Beni carrying double rations because Dray had redistributed the loads that morning in a way that put weight on everyone's shoulders except his own, old Manu who had not spoken since the river of small bones, and Beatrix Underhill, last in line as always, her eyes down on the stones as though she were counting them, cataloguing them, filing each one under some private system of engineering grief.

And at the front, where he had installed himself without asking anyone's leave, Ambrose Dray, Vane's man, walking like a man who knew exactly where he was going and wanted no one to notice that he didn't.

Ila tasted the wind again. Then he called the halt.

"We camp early," he said. "There is a shelf above the treeline, an hour's climb. We will not make the pass tonight."

Dray turned. The rain hadn't started yet but the light had gone the color of pewter, and it made his face look like something stamped rather than born. "The schedule says—"

"The schedule," Ila said, "does not say anything about weather. Weather does not read schedules." He pointed west, where the cloud was building over the shoulder of the mountain in a wall that looked less like cloud than like masonry, tier upon grey tier, the kind of sky that had opinions. "That comes tonight. On this side of the pass we would be caught on open grade with a drop on one side and nothing on the other. On the shelf there is rock behind us."

Dray looked at the sky the way a man looks at a contract clause he intends to argue later. "An hour," he said finally. "Then we move at first light regardless."

Ila did not answer that. There was no point answering things Dray said to hear himself say them. He turned and led them up.

* * *

The shelf was older than any of them—a ledge cut by some patient argument between water and stone, wide enough for five men and their packs if they kept their elbows in, walled on the inland side by a face of black basalt that leaned over them like a listening ear. Below, the mountainside fell away through cloud into green dark. Ila had sheltered here twice before in his life, both times alone, both times listening to storms try to peel the mountain's skin off and fail.

They got the tarp rigged against the basalt, got a fire going in a hollow of stone where the wind couldn't find it, and then the storm arrived all at once, the way storms do in the high country—not a beginning but a verdict. Rain came sideways. Thunder walked up and down the range like something looking for a door.

They ate in silence. Or near silence. Dray talked, because Dray always talked, filling the space the way he filled every space, with words about Vane's interests and the schedule and the importance of maintaining discipline on expedition, and Ila ate his rice and watched the fire and thought about names.

Forty names. He carried them now the way he carried water, close to the body, in the vessel of himself. Somewhere above them, past the pass, past the snowline that had come too early this year, there was a cave that held those names written in a dead company's hand, and Ila had made these people climb half a mountain on the strength of a promise: *the dead get named.* He had said it to Beatrix Underhill across a table in Wewak, and she had looked at him with those tired ledger-keeper's eyes and said yes, and he had believed her, or had decided to believe her, which in the mountains amounted to the same thing.

Now he watched her sit apart from the fire, knees drawn up, sketching again—that little book of hers, always the little book—and he wondered what a woman drew when she was drawing her way toward something she feared.

The porter fell at midnight.

Later—Ila would think about this later, in the long accounting the mountain forces on everyone who climbs it—he would understand that it began with the fire. The rain had found a seam in the stone hollow and begun to drown the coals, and someone had to feed the fire wood from the small stack they'd gathered, and the stack sat at the shelf's outer edge where they'd piled it before understanding how hard the wind would come. Someone had to go out along the shelf in the dark with the storm screaming and fetch wood.

Beni went. Because Beni was young, and because Dray had said, in his flat way, "Someone get wood," without moving, and because young men die of sentences like that.

Ila heard it happen more than saw it: a cry torn short, the scrape of boots, the terrible soft sound of a body meeting rock. He was moving before the echo finished, out onto the shelf with the rain hitting him like thrown gravel, and he found Beni by touch—found him wedged against a knob of stone three meters down the shelf's broken lip, one arm bent wrong beneath him, blood thinning in the rain, alive, alive, thank everything alive, his breath coming in whistles through his teeth.

"Rope!" Ila shouted back into the dark. "Rope and the tarp!"

What happened next happened fast and slow at once, the way emergencies do. Hands found his in the dark. A rope came. He tied Beni under the arms with knots his grandfather's ghost guided his fingers through, and then they hauled, four of them hauling against the storm while the mountain tried to keep what it had caught, and Beatrix Underhill was suddenly beside him—not behind, beside—her hands on the rope, her whole slight weight thrown backward, shouting instructions in a voice he had never heard from her, clear and hard and certain: *"On my count—again—together—now—"*

She counted like an engineer. She counted like someone who had spent her life making heavy things move safely, and for the length of that haul Ila forgot to hate her employers and forgot to fear her and simply hauled, and Beni came up over the lip of the shelf and collapsed onto stone, breathing, his arm a ruin but his chest rising and falling.

They got him under the tarp. Ila splinted the arm with a tent pole and strips of shirt while Beni bit down on a leather strap and made sounds he would be ashamed of later and should not have been ashamed of at all. The bone had not come through the skin. That was the whole of their luck, and it was a thin luck, because a man with a shattered arm could not carry a load, and tomorrow they climbed toward snow.

Beatrix sat back on her heels, soaked through, shaking now that it was done, and Ila watched the shaking and understood it. Not fear. Fury. It was in her face, plain as print: fury at the storm, at the shelf, at the sentence *someone get wood*, at whatever arithmetic had put a boy on that edge in that wind.

"Dray gave the order," she said quietly. Only to Ila. The rain covered it.

"Dray gave no order," Ila said. "Dray spoke, and let the mountain do the ordering. It is a skill his kind has."

Her jaw worked. She said nothing else. But she looked at Beni a long time, and Ila filed the look away with the other looks he'd been collecting—the way she read the old rail grades aloud like scripture, the way she lied about her father's map with a liar's smoothness and then sketched cave mouths with a mourner's honesty. She was becoming legible to him, page by page, and he was not yet sure whether what he was reading was a woman climbing toward truth or a woman being marched toward a cliff by men who owned her debts.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it came the fire.

Ila woke to smoke where smoke should not be—at the wrong end of the shelf, at the supply packs, at the stacked crates of rice and tarpaulin and lamp oil that constituted everything between them and starvation on a mountain already wearing its winter. The wind had shifted in the night and driven sparks into the tarpaulin, or so it appeared; the oil had caught, and oil-fed flame in a high wind is not a fire but a decision, immediate and final.

They fought it with wet ground-cloth and rocks and curses. They lost. In twenty minutes the greater part of their provisions became ash and black plastic and a smell like a burned offering, and the wind took the ash eastward over the pass, as though delivering a message ahead of them.

Ila stood in the wreckage with his hands open at his sides and made himself breathe.

Because he had seen it. He had been awake—had slept badly, as he slept badly every night now, dreaming of gravel bars and small bones—and he had seen the moment before the flare-up. He had seen Dray standing at the supply stack in the pre-dawn grey, crouched, doing something with his hands at the base of the oil crate. Not striking a light. Nothing so crude. Adjusting something. Loosening something. And then walking away, unhurried, back to his place by the basalt wall, and lying down, and waiting.

Ten minutes later the mountain had conveniently provided the spark.

"You saw it too."

Beatrix was beside him. She had a way of arriving beside him lately, silent as weather. Her eyes were on the ashes, on the melted tarpaulin fused to stone, on the few salvaged tins lined up like orphans.

"I saw a man arrange a fire," Ila said carefully, "and a mountain light it."

"Vane doesn't want us able to turn back." Her voice was low and level, the voice she used for load calculations, for facts. "No supplies, no return trip. Forward is the only direction that ends anywhere. We're committed now—committed past the point where commitment can be questioned." She looked at him. "You understand what that makes us."

Ila looked down the trail, back the way they had come—down through the treeline, down to the river of small bones, down to the coast, down to Wewak and lawyers and a debt written in ink. Then he looked up, to where the pass waited in cloud, and beyond it the snowline, and beyond that whatever the cave held: gold, or graves, or both.

"A prisoner walks behind a guard," he said. "We walk behind our own need. It is worse. But it is also," he added, and surprised himself with the bitter edge in his own voice, "the same road."

Below them, Beni lay propped against the basalt, grey-faced, his splinted arm bound across his chest. Old Manu knelt beside him feeding him water from the last unbroken flask, and Ila saw the old man's lips moving, and knew without hearing that he was not praying but counting—counting the tins, counting the days, counting the distance to safety and finding the sum short.

Dray emerged from behind the basalt wall with his radio in hand, and Ila watched him key it and speak too quietly to be heard, and watched him lower the radio afterward and stand looking north toward the pass with an expression that for the first time did not look like a mask. It looked like a man reading his own orders and finding, somewhere in the fine print, a clause he had not expected to be inside.

"We move in one hour," Dray announced to the shelf at large. "Light loads. Fast march. The pass by nightfall."

"And Beni?" Beatrix asked.

"Beni goes back down with Manu."

"With what supplies?" Ila asked. "With what food? With what rope?" He let the questions stand in the cold air, each one a small stone. "You would send a wounded boy and an old man down a mountain you have just made hungry."

Something moved behind Dray's eyes—something quick and trapped. "Nobody sent anybody anywhere," he said. "The mountain did."

"Yes," Ila said softly. "The mountain. Which you arranged."

For a long moment nobody breathed. The wind combed the shelf. Dray looked at Ila, and Ila looked back, and between them passed the whole unwitnessed scene of the pre-dawn, acknowledged now, irreversible.

Then Dray did the last thing Ila expected. He shrugged—a small, tired, almost human shrug—and said, "Then we all go forward. All five. Slow, if we must." He turned away, shouldering his pack, and threw one last sentence back over his shoulder like a coin tossed to a beggar:

"Pray the cave's worth it."

Ila stood in the wind and felt the shape of the trap settle around them all—not Dray's trap, or not only his; a larger one, older, with walls of weather and debt and the dead. They were prisoners of the mountain now, every one of them, guards and captives alike, marching together toward a door in the rock that someone intended to close.

Above them, past the pass, the first flakes were already falling—early, wrong, weeks ahead of any winter Ila had ever known—and they came down through the grey light fine and dry and endless, like ash falling upward, like the mountain returning what had been burned to it.

He touched the name of his grandfather where it lived beneath his ribs, and shouldered his pack, and followed them up into the snow.
