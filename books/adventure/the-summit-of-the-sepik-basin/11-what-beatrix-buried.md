## 11. What Beatrix Buried

The fire burned low and orange in the hollow of the shelf where they had made camp, and the mountain stood over them like a question no one wanted to answer.

Beatrix sat with her back against a fallen trunk, her knees drawn up, her hands wrapped around a tin mug of tea that had gone cold an hour ago. She held it anyway. Holding something was easier than holding nothing. Across the flames, Ila sat cross-legged and still, the way he always sat, as though the ground itself had asked his permission before letting him rest on it. The two porters—Ano and the boy called Wem—had rolled into their blankets near the pack line, but she could tell by their breathing that neither of them slept. Dray stood at the edge of the firelight with his rifle slung and his back to them all, watching the dark below the shelf where the storm had come from.

The storm had passed in the night, but it had left its signature: the air scrubbed cold and thin, the stars hard as nailheads, and somewhere far down the valley the sound of water running where water had not run before. The mountain was rearranging itself after the rain. Beatrix knew that sound from a lifetime of listening to it—the groan and settle of earth finding a new argument with gravity.

She had been turning something over in her mind since the gravel bar. Since the bones.

*They were porters,* Ila had said, kneeling in the shallows with a femur cradled in both palms as gently as a sleeping bird. *Forty went up. None came down.* And then he had looked at her—not accusing, not pleading, just looking, the way one man looks at another when he is deciding what they are made of—and he had said, *You knew this route before I offered to walk it. You read those grades like scripture. What did you bury, Beatrix Underhill?*

She had not answered then. She had told herself there would be time, that the truth could wait for a better hour, a warmer fire, a braver version of herself. But the truth did not work that way. She had learned that in a tunnel eleven years ago, under four hundred tons of collapsed rock, listening to the seconds tick past like coins dropping into a deep well. Truth kept. It grew in the dark. It sent out roots through everything you built on top of it until one day the whole structure came down—and you could never again say honestly whether you had built the thing or merely buried it.

She drank the cold tea anyway. It tasted of ash and river water.

"Dray," she said.

He didn't turn. "Sleep, Underhill."

"I need to say something. All of you need to hear it." Her voice came out steadier than she expected, which surprised her; she had imagined this moment so many times in the last three days that apparently some part of her had already rehearsed it to death. "It won't take long. Then you can all go back to pretending I'm just a clerk who got lost."

Dray turned halfway, his profile cut silver by the moon. He said nothing, which from Dray was as good as permission.

Ila opened his eyes.

* * *

"You've all been wondering why Vane wants what's in that cave," she began. "Why a financier sends five people up a closed rail grade into winter for a box of old papers and legend-gold. You've been wondering why he'd send me—a harbor clerk, a disgraced engineer nobody's heard of in ten years—to carry it out." She set the mug down between her boots. "So I'll tell you. But to tell it right, I have to start further back than the cave. I have to start with a tunnel."

The fire popped. A log shifted, shedding a constellation of sparks that rose and died.

"There's a line in the central range—you won't find it on any current map, because it doesn't exist anymore. The Kandrian Cut. Two miles of tunnel through the shoulder of Mount Ambra, part of the highland extension my father helped survey when I was a girl. I grew up on that survey. I learned load calculations at his knee before I learned cursive." She heard her own voice flatten into the clipped cadence of a technical report and forced herself away from it. Facts were her fortress. She had hidden behind them her whole life. "When I was twenty-six, I was the resident engineer on the Cut. Youngest woman ever to hold the post. There were people who said I only had it because of my father's name. I wanted to prove them wrong so badly that I proved them right instead."

Wem had sat up in his blanket. She could see the whites of his eyes across the embers. Ano lay still, but his breathing had changed—she could hear him listening.

"The Cut ran through a fault zone. Everyone knew it. The rock was rotten—squeezing ground, we called it, stone that flows under pressure like slow taffy. The original design called for heavier steel sets and a reduced heading width through the fault section. Slower. More expensive. My crew was behind schedule by four months, and the company was bleeding money, and the regional director had already flown up twice to stand behind me in the tunnel with his arms crossed, saying nothing, which was worse than shouting." She paused. "There's a particular kind of silence that comes off a disappointed superior. It has weight. I've calculated loads my whole life. I know weight."

"What did you do?" Ila asked quietly. Not *what happened*. *What did you do.* He had understood already. Perhaps he had understood since the gravel bar.

"I falsified the load calculations," she said. "I took the design figures for sound ground and applied them to the fault zone. On paper, the steel sets I ordered were rated for twice the expected pressure. In reality, they were rated for half of it. I wrote the numbers myself. I signed them myself. I told myself the geologists were being conservative, that the fault was narrower than mapped, that the ground would hold long enough to get the lining in behind the heading." She laughed, a small terrible sound with nothing in it. "That's what we do, engineers. We don't lie exactly. We negotiate with reality. We tell reality what we can afford to hear, and we hope it agrees."

"And it didn't agree," Dray said. His voice was flat, unreadable.

"It didn't agree." She looked into the fire. "March eleventh. Four-forty in the morning. I was in the heading office, forty meters back from the face, going over the week's advance figures. The collapse started as a sound—I want to say a crack, but it wasn't a crack. It was more like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the lights went brown, and the air pressure changed—that's the thing nobody tells you about a tunnel collapse. Your ears feel it before anything else. The whole world inhales." Her hands had gone white around her knees. She made herself keep talking. "Four hundred tons of rock came down between me and the portal. Eleven men ahead of me in the heading. Three behind. I was in the one pocket of survivable air in two miles of tunnel, and I knew it, and I knew why, and I knew whose numbers had put the steel there that failed."

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"They dug for six days," she said. "They reached me on the seventh. One man came through the last few meters on his hands and knees, through air bad enough to kill a dog, with no lamp because the flame would have eaten the last of the oxygen. He put his hand on my ankle in the dark and said—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, because hiding it now would be one lie too many— "he said, 'Miss Underhill. Marcus Vane. Can you walk?'"

"Vane," Ila repeated. "The financier. He was in the mines?"

"He owned the financing consortium behind the extension. He'd flown up to inspect his investment. When the mountain came down, every other man with authority stood at the portal and argued about liability. Vane stripped off his coat and went into the relief drive with the rescue crews. Day after day. I'm told he was the one who insisted they keep digging past the point where the actuaries said stop." She shook her head slowly. "Eleven men died in that heading, Ila. Three more died of their injuries. Fourteen families. And the inquiry found—"

She stopped. This was the part she had never said aloud, the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"The inquiry found the steel had been specified correctly," she said. "The failure was attributed to unforeseeable geological conditions. Act of God. The report was clean. Immaculate. Because before the inquiry convened, Marcus Vane bought it. He bought the consulting firm, he bought the silence of two senior inspectors, and he bought me." She finally raised her eyes from the fire. "He pulled me out of that tunnel, and then he buried me in a different one. A comfortable one. A well-paid one. For eleven years, wherever I've gone, whatever I've done, there's been a ledger with my name on it and a single entry that never closes. He calls it a debt. Money advanced, favors rendered, discretion provided. Interest compounding quarterly." Her mouth twisted. "But you don't pay blackmail with installments. You pay it forever. That's the design. I should know. I designed things once."

The silence that followed was enormous. Somewhere downslope, water ran in the dark, patient as time.

* * *

It was Ano who spoke first, the older porter, without sitting up, his voice coming out of the blanket like a voice out of the ground. "The fourteen men. Their families. Do they know?"

"No," Beatrix said. "They know the mountain fell on their husbands and fathers. They don't know the mountain was pushed." She swallowed. "Vane's cover held because I let it hold. Every year I didn't speak, the cost of speaking grew. That's how he builds things. Not tunnels—traps. He finds a person's worst moment and makes it bear interest."

"And the cave," Wem said. He was hugging his knees now, the boy's face very young in the ember-light. "The gold of the Halvorsen expedition. The papers. Why does he want them?"

"Because of what Ila found in that gravel bar," Beatrix said. "Because forty porters went up that mountain in 1911 behind a company flag, and none came down, and somebody made sure no one ever asked why. I think the cave holds the answer. Ledgers, records, proof—whoever sealed that entrance sealed it to hide a crime, and I think the company that did it is the same company whose money became Vane's empire. He doesn't want treasure. He wants the evidence. He wants it in his hands so it can disappear the way my inquiry disappeared. Clean. Immaculate." She looked at Ila. "And he chose me to carry it out because I'm already his. Because a blackmailed courier doesn't ask questions and doesn't survive to talk."

Ila had not moved throughout. Now he reached forward and fed a branch into the fire, deliberately, watching it catch, and when he spoke his voice was low and even. "My grandfather's name will be in those papers. You said the dead get named. That was the price of my guiding you."

"Yes."

"Then hear my price again, and understand it has changed." He looked up, and the firelight filled the hollows of his face. "You will not carry those papers to Vane. Whatever happens on this mountain—whatever he holds over you, whatever Dray's orders are—those names go to the light, not to the man who buys darkness. Swear it."

Beatrix felt the words land in her chest like a spike driven into frozen ground. Everything in her that had survived eleven years by keeping small and silent flinched from it. To swear was to choose. To choose was to burn the bridge behind her while she was still standing on it.

And yet—

She thought of the pocket of air in the collapsed tunnel, the seven days of dark, the hand on her ankle. She had spent a decade believing Vane's hand had been salvation. But a man who pulls you out of one collapse only to build you into another is not a rescuer. He is simply a different kind of falling rock.

"If I swear it," she said, "Vane takes my license. My freedom. Maybe my life—he's already sent Dray to make sure of the second part if it comes to that. If I swear it, I lose everything I spent eleven years protecting."

"Yes," Ila said simply. "That is what it costs. The dead paid more."

Dray shifted at the edge of the light. Beatrix turned to look at him fully for the first time since she'd begun, expecting the flat mask, the enforcer's patience. What she saw instead was harder to read: his jaw working slightly, his eyes fixed on the middle distance, as though her confession had struck something in him that he hadn't known was load-bearing.

"Vane told me you'd try a speech like this," Dray said at last. "Somewhere on the mountain. He said you'd cry your sad story and turn the natives soft." He spat into the dark. "He didn't say it would be true."

"Does it matter?" Beatrix asked. "If it's true or not. Your orders don't change."

"My orders don't change," Dray agreed. He turned back to the darkness below the shelf. But he said it a beat too late, and a tone too quiet, and Beatrix had spent her life reading structures for the flaw hidden beneath sound surfaces. Something in Dray had cracked tonight. Whether it would collapse or hold, she couldn't yet calculate.

She turned back to Ila.

"I swear it," she said. "The dead get named. The papers go to the light. Not to Vane. Not ever to Vane." She heard her own voice shake on the last word and did not correct it. "I've been carrying his debt for eleven years. Tonight I'm putting it down. Whatever it crushes when it lands—it lands."

Ila nodded once, slowly, the way a man nods over a contract he intends to honor beyond death. Then he did something he had never done in all the days they had walked together: he reached across the fire and pressed his closed fist to his own chest, then opened it toward her—an offering, a salute, a thing older than either of their languages.

"Wem," he said, without taking his eyes from her. "Bring me the oilskin."

The boy scrambled to the packs and returned with the long wrapped bundle Ila had carried since Wewak, the one Beatrix had assumed held food or rope. Ila unwrapped it. Inside lay a folded sheet of oilcloth, dry and pale, and a stub of pencil bound to it with wire.

"Tonight you write it," Ila said. "Before we sleep. Everything you told us. The date. The tunnel. The fourteen names if you remember them—we will find the rest. And tomorrow, and every day after, you add what we find in the cave. The papers go to the light, you swore it. So we begin the light here, by firelight, with your hand." He laid the oilcloth on the ground between them and weighted its corners with stones. "A debt written in ink can be buried. A debt written by many hands, carried by many backs, cannot."

Beatrix looked down at the blank oilcloth. Her hands, when she took the pencil, were steady—steadier than they had been in eleven years. It occurred to her, with a strange floating clarity, that this was the first honest engineering she had done in a decade: building a record that could bear weight, span by span, name by name, designed to outlast the man who had bought the last one.

She bent her head and began to write.

Behind her, unnoticed by everyone but the dark, Dray unslung his rifle, checked the action with his thumb, and turned his face toward the summit—where, above the snowline that waited for them all, the cave mouth held its forty secrets and whatever Vane had truly sent them up the mountain to bury.
