## 12. The Snowline

Dray had been in cold country before. He'd worked the copper camps in the highlands as a younger man, hauling crates up switchbacks for men who paid him in coin and contempt, and he knew what snow did to a body's arithmetic: how it stole heat faster than fire could return it, how it turned a strong man stupid inside an hour, how it made every decision feel urgent and wrong at once. He had thought himself prepared for this. He had stocked the expedition with that preparation—wool, oilskin, tinned fat, the good storm matches wrapped in waxed paper against his own ribs where no one would think to ask.

What he had not prepared for was the smell.

Snow has a smell, this high and this early. It comes down ahead of itself on the wind, a clean mineral coldness like wet slate and iron, and when Dray woke on the fourth morning above the last village and tasted it in the back of his throat, he lay still in his bag for a long moment and felt something he could not immediately name. Not fear. He had done fear before too; fear was a tool like any other, and he kept his tools sharp. This was older than fear. This was the feeling of a season arriving out of order, of a calendar breaking, of the world quietly announcing that the plan—the plan he had built, the plan Vane had approved, the plan with its tidy margins and its contingency columns—was already dead.

He crawled out into a morning the color of pewter. The others were still in their bags. Beatrix Underhill was not; she stood at the edge of the shelf they'd camped on, her back to him, watching the ridgeline to the north where the air had gone white at its seams. She had her hands jammed in her armpits and her breath came out in slow, measured plumes, and even from behind she looked less like a debtor and more like a woman reading a document only she could see.

"It's early," she said, without turning. She had known he was awake. Of course she had; the man made noise like a landslide whether he meant to or not.

"Snow?"

"The line of it." She lifted her chin toward the peaks. "It should be three weeks higher. Maybe four. The monsoon broke late and the jet stream is dragging polar air down the spine of the range. My father charted this pattern once, in '29. He called it a false spring winter. Everything looks open until it isn't."

Dray grunted. He did not care about jet streams. What he cared about was that Beatrix Underhill had just told him, casually, in the manner of a person discussing weather over breakfast, that she understood this mountain better than the man who owned her debt—and that she had said it aloud, to him, without apparent calculation. Either she was careless, which the evidence so far argued against, or she wanted him to know. He filed it away with everything else he'd filed about her since Wewak: the map she claimed was half-rotted but consulted with the fluency of long marriage; the way she counted porters' loads each evening and adjusted them by weight, not by rank; the way Ila watched her do it with an expression Dray was beginning to recognize as the beginning of respect, which was more dangerous than hostility. Hostility you could manage. Respect you had to kill early or it spread.

"Breakfast," he said. "We move in an hour."

She turned then, and looked at him, and there was something in her face—not challenge exactly, not appeal—that sat in his chest all through the boiling of the water and the doling out of rice, like a stone swallowed whole.

* * *

They crossed the snowline at midday, and it was nothing like the maps.

The old Halvorsen survey notes—Ila carried copies, hand-copied by his grandfather's hand from the originals, which was its own quiet horror if you thought about it too long—placed the permanent ice at four thousand two hundred meters. They hit it at three thousand eight hundred, a dirty white margin creeping down through the scree like frost on a windowpane, and above it the world simply changed registers. Color drained. Sound drained. Their boots stopped crunching and started squeaking, that dry rubber-on-ice complaint, and every man's breath hung around his head like a small private ghost.

The porters took it hardest. These were river people, basin people; snow was a story their grandmothers told, and now it was underfoot and rising. One of them, the young one called Tomas who had found the bones in the gravel bar two weeks ago and hadn't slept right since, kept stopping to look back down the valley they'd climbed out of, as though checking that it was still there. Dray understood the impulse. He felt it himself. Below them the green world went on being green, absurdly, impossibly, a sea of canopy steaming in the sun while above their heads the clouds went grey and granular and began, very softly, to fall.

Not snow yet. Something between snow and sleet, needles of it, stinging. Ila called a halt at a sheltered bend below a rock wall and got the tarps up with a speed that shamed everyone, and Dray stood in the falling white and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since dawn.

Three weeks to the pass, at summer pace. Ten days, if he drove them, if nobody's lungs gave out, if the grade Beatrix kept muttering about—the closed rail line, her father's route, the miracle of cut stone swallowed by jungle—was actually traversable. Against that: a winter arriving three weeks early, five men's worth of fuel, and a radio with a battery he'd been rationing since the second river crossing because Vane's instructions had been explicit about contact schedules. Monthly check-ins. Minimal transmission. *No unnecessary chatter,* the note had said, in Vane's narrow upright hand, and Dray had read that note a hundred times since Wewak and only now, standing in the wrong-colored snow, heard what it actually sounded like.

It sounded like a man who didn't want the conversation recorded.

"We can't make the pass in this," Beatrix said beside him. She'd come up quietly, the way she did; for a big man he was famously easy to sneak up on, a fact his mother had lamented and his employers had exploited. "Not with these loads and not with these men. We need shelter within two days or we need to turn back."

Turn back. The words landed in Dray's stomach like dropped cargo. Turn back meant the expedition failed. Failure meant Vane's contingency column opened, and Dray had read enough of Marcus Vane's contingencies over eleven years to know they were not plans so much as predictions—cold, accurate, and always about people. He turned away from her slightly, angling his body, and pulled the radio from its oilskin wrap.

* * *

The set was a wartime surplus rig, heavy as sin, and it ate batteries like a furnace ate coal. Dray had learned radio in the army and unlearned most of it since; what remained was procedure, the liturgy of it—check frequency, key the handset twice, wait, listen to the hiss the way you'd listen to a door. The hiss tonight had teeth in it. Static crackled with atmospheric charge, the storm building somewhere above them, and when he keyed the mic and spoke the call sign Vane had assigned—a bland string of letters that meant nothing except to two men—he felt the sound go out of him and up into the falling white and vanish.

Then the wait. The wait was always the worst part, though he'd never admitted that to anyone. Eleven years of waits. You keyed the set and then you stood in the silence knowing that somewhere, in a house with electric light and iced drinks, a man was deciding something about your life, and the deciding took exactly as long as it took.

Vane answered on the third try. His voice came through compressed and tinny, stripped of everything that made it human, which somehow made it more recognizably itself, the way a skull is recognizably a face.

"Position?"

Dray gave it. Coordinates, elevation, condition of party, the snowline figure. He reported it clean, the way he reported everything, and he left out the bones and the burned supplies and the porter who'd fallen on the cliff shelf, because those were not items Vane needed yet, and because some animal part of Dray had begun, without consulting him, to hoard information the way a drowning man hoards air.

"The Underhill woman," Vane said. "Progress."

"She knows the route. Better than she let on. The rail grades are real—her father surveyed them. She can get us to the cave."

A pause. In the pause Dray could hear, faintly, the clink of glass on glass. Evening in the lowlands. Ice in a tumbler.

"And after the cave?"

Dray frowned at the handset. "After the cave we descend the western grade. That was the plan. You approved—"

"I know what I approved." The voice was patient, almost gentle, and that gentleness was worse than any anger Dray had ever heard from him. Anger meant surprise, and surprise meant something had escaped the plan. Gentleness meant everything was proceeding exactly as intended, and the thing proceeding was you. "Tell me, Dray. How many people do you suppose have seen the interior of that cave? Living people. Since 1911."

The static hissed. Snow ticked against the tarp behind him. Dray stood very still and did the only arithmetic that mattered.

"None," he said. "That we know of."

"That we know of," Vane repeated, tasting it. "There were six prospectors in '38 who got close. There was a patrol officer in '51 who wrote a report. There were two of my own geologists in '69, young men, ambitious, who went up without telling me because they thought they'd found gold and wanted to present it to me personally. A gift." Another clink of glass. "You've met neither group, Dray. Consider why."

The cold moved through Dray then, and it was not the mountain's cold. The mountain's cold he knew how to fight. This came from underneath, from the floor of him, from a place he'd kept swept and empty for eleven years because keeping it empty was the job. He thought of the geologists. Young men. Ambitious. He thought of the bones in the gravel bar, forty years in the water, and of Ila kneeling over them in the rain while Dray had said *we move on* and hated the sound of his own voice saying it.

"The debt," Dray said. His mouth was dry. "When she delivers, the debt clears. Your word."

"My word." Vane laughed softly, and there was nothing in the laugh, nothing at all; it was a sound shaped like laughter the way a glove is shaped like a hand. "Oh, Dray. You've been useful for a long time, so I'll say this plainly, once, as a courtesy. There is no debt. There never was a debt. There is a woman who falsified load calculations and killed four men in a tunnel collapse, and I have held that paper for nine years because paper like that is worth more than money. When she goes into that cave, she becomes a witness. When she comes out, she becomes a witness with knowledge. And witnesses—" the voice paused, savoring, a man rolling a cigar between his fingers, "—witnesses are a liability class. You understand liability classes. You've managed several for me."

Dray looked at his boots. The snow was gathering on the toes of them, whitening the leather, and he watched it accumulate with the fixed attention of a man trying not to hear something.

"You want them sealed in," he said. "All of them."

"I want the cave to keep its secrets. It's kept them eighty years; it's very good at it. You'll signal me from the lower entrance when the party is committed to the interior galleries. I'll handle the rest. Helicopter, charges, whatever the terrain requires—you won't be involved in the mechanics. You'll be involved in making sure the mechanics aren't interrupted." A beat. "And Dray. The tracker. Ila. He's the one I'm most concerned about. Local boy, family history with the mountain, and now he's spent two weeks learning our routes and our weaknesses. If anything goes wrong on the descent, if anyone reaches the coast and starts talking—"

"I know what happens if anyone talks," Dray said.

"Yes," Vane said pleasantly. "You do. Because you're usually the one who does it."

The transmission ended the way Vane ended everything: cleanly, on his schedule, leaving the silence shaped exactly as he wanted it. Dray keyed off and stood holding the dead handset against his chest like something he'd taken from a corpse.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He sat up under the tarp with the others breathing around him—Tomas whimpering faintly in a dream about bones, the old porter Kofi snoring like a landslide, Ila silent as ever, sleeping the way he did everything, efficiently and facing outward—and he turned the conversation over and over like a stone in the hands, looking for the seam.

Eleven years. He ran the count the way he ran all counts, honestly, because dishonest counting was how men died in the mountains. The strike-breaker at the Madang wharf, 1963. The union organizer whose brakes failed on the Bulolo road. The surveyor in the highlands, the one with the wife and the bad heart, whose medicine bottle Dray had refilled with something else. He had never counted them before. Counting was sentiment and sentiment was rust, and he had kept himself oiled and functional for eleven years by refusing the arithmetic. But Vane's voice had done something to the lock on that room, had rattled it loose the way the earthquake had rattled the bay at Wewak, and now the numbers came through whether he wanted them or not, ticking upward in the dark like a meter running.

Nine. Eleven. He lost track past thirteen and hated himself for losing track, because losing track was a luxury the dead didn't have.

And underneath the count, another thought, colder and more specific: *he told me.* Not confessed—Vane didn't confess, confession implied shame—but disclosed, casually, the way you'd tell a foreman which paddock to burn. He had told Dray about the geologists, about the prospectors, about the patrol officer, because he believed Dray already knew, because in Vane's model of the world Dray was a fixture, a load-bearing wall, a man who could be told anything because nothing touched him.

That was the part that slipped the mask. Not horror at the order—horror he could file, horror he'd filed before. It was the discovery that he was legible. That Vane had read him correctly for eleven years. That the emptiness he'd maintained so carefully, the professional blankness, the swept floor—it wasn't armor. It was just emptiness, and Vane had known, and had valued him precisely for it, the way you value a room with nothing in it: easy to clean, nothing to break.

Except something had broken. Something had broken on a gravel bar full of small bones, when a tracker knelt in the rain and refused to walk past the dead, and Dray had stood there with orders in his pocket and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the floor tilt.

He looked across the dying fire at Beatrix Underhill. She slept on her side with her pack strap still looped around her wrist—she'd done that every night since someone searched her bag on the first night out, and Dray knew it had been one of his hired porters acting on his instruction, and she knew it too, and neither of them had ever said a word about it, and that shared unsaid thing had been building between them like pressure behind a dam. Her face in the firelight was lined and tired and absolutely awake beneath the closed lids; he'd bet his life she was listening to him think.

*She killed four men,* he reminded himself. *Falsified the calculations herself. Four men in the dark.*

*Yes,* said the tilted floor. *And Vane saved her, and kept the papers, and has spent nine years milking her for it, and now means to put her in a hole in a mountain with a tracker and three porters and blow the mountain shut. And he means for you to hold the door.*

Dray looked at his hands in the firelight. Big hands, scarred, competent, hands that had done everything asked of them and never once asked why. He flexed them once, slowly, and watched the tendons move like cables under load.

Outside, the snow thickened. It came down now in earnest, fat flakes spiraling through the fire-smoke, settling on the tarps with a sound like breathing, and the temperature fell the way Beatrix had predicted it would, fast and total, the false spring winter closing its hand. Tomorrow they would need shelter or they would need to turn back, and turning back was death by a slower road, and going forward led to a cave with a grave in it and a plan with his name written in the margins as the man who holds the door.

In the morning, Beatrix stood again at the edge of the shelf, looking north, and this time when Dray came to stand beside her she didn't pretend not to notice.

"The pass closes in four days," she said. "Maybe five. After that we're not climbing out—we're riding the grades down or we're not moving at all. So whatever you're deciding, decide it soon."

He hadn't told her anything. He was certain he hadn't told her anything. But she stood there in the falling snow with her breath hanging white and her eyes forward, waiting, with the patience of a woman who had spent nine years reading dangerous men and had just heard, in the static and the pauses of a radio call she couldn't possibly have intercepted, everything she needed to know.

"I don't know what you mean," Dray said.

"No," Beatrix Underhill said mildly, watching the mountain. "I expect you don't. Yet."
