## 13. Cave of the Forty Names

The upper entrance was not a mouth but a wound.

Ila saw it first from the shoulder of the ridge, where the snowline had come down to meet them like an unwelcome relative—early, insistent, refusing to be ignored. A seam of darker stone in the grey cliff face, half-veiled by ice that had grown over it in layers, season upon season, the way moss grows over a fallen man and the forest pretends he was never there. The earthquake tide that had drained Wewak's bay had done its work here too; the mountain had shrugged, and the old seal had cracked, and now the cave breathed again after fifty years of holding its breath.

He did not say anything at first. He stood with his boots in the new snow and let the others catch up, because some things should not be spoken until they are certain, and certainty about this place had been the one inheritance his grandfather's silence had left him.

"Is that it?" Dray asked. The enforcer's voice carried strangely in the thin air, flat as slate.

"It is the door," Ila said. "What is behind it, we do not know."

Beatrix came up beside him, breathing hard, her engineer's eyes already moving over the rock face the way she moved them over everything—measuring, calculating, reading stress lines in the stone the way Ila read broken grass and bent twigs. She said nothing either. But she looked at the entrance for a long time, longer than calculation required, and Ila understood that she too felt the weight of the place. Some doors you could see through before you opened them.

The two porters, Ano and young Kepas, hung back. They were mountain men from the other side of the range, and their people had their own names for this ridge, none of them good. Kepas made a small gesture with his hand, turning it palm-down twice, the sign for *the dead are beneath us here*. Ano lit no fire that night without asking permission of the ground first, tapping three times with his axe handle.

Ila tapped too. He had learned the gesture from his mother, who had learned it from hers, and somewhere back along that line of women there had been a wife who waited for a man who walked into this mountain and never walked out.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.* Ask. Wait. Listen.

The mountain said nothing. It never had.

* * *

They reached the entrance at midmorning, when the light came down the valley at an angle that cut under the ice shelf and showed them what the earthquake had done: a crack driven deep into the cliff, widened by frost, until a man could pass through sideways with his pack held ahead of him. Inside the crack the air was different. Warmer by a degree, perhaps less, but Ila's skin knew it the way a hand knows the difference between a living thing and a dead one. The cave was breathing. Something inside it was still, impossibly, alive—not living, but *unburied*, and unburied things have a temperature all their own.

Beatrix went first. Of course she did; she had been going first since the wash-out grade, since the day Ila had watched her stand on a wall of cut stone a hundred years old and read it aloud like scripture—the chisel marks, the drainage cuts, the grade calculated to the inch—and he had understood suddenly that he had misjudged her, that she was not a debtor dragging her chain up a mountain but a builder walking among ruins built by people like her father. Builders leave fingerprints. Beatrix Underhill left them everywhere, even in her lies.

Now she went first into the crack, lamp held high, and Ila followed close enough to touch her pack, and behind them came Dray with his rifle slung across his chest like something he was ashamed of, and then the porters, and the darkness closed around them like water.

The passage ran level for thirty paces, then turned, then opened.

Ila heard Beatrix stop before he saw why. Her breath caught—a small sound, quickly swallowed, but in that silence it was as loud as a shot.

"Lamp," she said, and her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Give me the second lamp."

Dray passed it forward. Light bloomed, and the chamber gave itself to them all at once.

It was not treasure.

Ila had prepared himself for gold. He had spent twenty years preparing himself for gold—the way you prepare yourself for grief, rehearsing it in the dark hours so that when it comes it will find you already armored. Gold in crates. Gold in canvas sacks gone rotten. Gold spilling across a stone floor like the sun's own blood, and forty men's bones scattered among it, and his grandfather's skull grinning up at him from the pile with whatever expression bones keep.

There was no gold.

There were shelves.

Wooden shelving, built against the far wall by hands that had known carpentry—sawn planks, notched joints, the work of men with tools and time and no intention of leaving. And on the shelves, row upon row, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed paper and stacked with a clerk's terrible neatness, were ledgers. Hundreds of them. Photographs in tin boxes. Bundles of correspondence tied with wire. A filing cabinet, an actual steel filing cabinet, hauled up a mountain and into a cave by men who needed it hidden more than they needed it used.

And beneath the shelves, along the near wall where the lamplight fell last—

Ila lowered his lamp.

The grave was not hidden. That was the thing that broke something open in his chest and let the cold air of the mountain pour straight into the hot place where his heart had been. The grave was *arranged*. Bodies laid side by side, forty of them—or what remained of forty; the mountain had kept what it wished to keep—shoulder to shoulder in rows, heads to the east, feet to the west, the way his people lay their dead when they can. Someone had buried them. Someone had laid them out with care, and then someone else had sealed the cave, and the two someones had not been the same men, or if they had been, then those men had carried a weight no ledger could record.

"God," Kepas whispered from the rear, and would not come further.

Ano came forward instead, slowly, and knelt at the edge of the rows, and began, very quietly, to sing. Not a song Ila knew—a keening, wordless, the sound mountain men make when words would be an insult to the dead.

Beatrix crouched at the shelves. Her hands hovered over the oilcloth bundles without touching, the way you hover over a sleeping child. "These are company records," she said. "Halvorsen Consolidated. Look at the dates." She peeled back a corner of waxed paper with two fingers, gentle as a surgeon. Beneath it: columns of figures, a rubber stamp faded to a ghost, and a signature Ila did not need her to read aloud, because Dray read it aloud for her, involuntarily, the name dragged out of him like a tooth.

"*Vane*," the enforcer said. "That's—that's Vane's signature. Old Vane. Marcus Vane's father."

"The company that became the empire," Beatrix said quietly. "The company that became *him*."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the storm outside sealed the passes behind them, which Ila would understand later was not coincidence but design—Vane had sent them up in the teeth of early winter, and winter had obligingly locked the door. But later. For now there was only the work, and the work was holy, and Ila moved through it the way a man moves through prayer: hands trembling, mind absolutely clear.

They unwrapped the ledgers one by one and set them on a flat stone Beatrix cleared for the purpose. Payroll records. Supply manifests. A contract for forty-three porters, signed at the coast in 1911, each name entered in a clerk's careful hand—names, villages, next payment owed. Ila read down the column with his finger and his breath stopped at the eleventh line.

*TAMBU, YAWA — guide & headman — village of Ambunti bend.*

His grandfather's name. Written in ink by a stranger's hand sixty years before Ila was born, and waiting here in the dark ever since, patient as bone, for someone to come and read it aloud.

Ila did not know he had fallen to his knees until the stone was cold under them. He did not remember opening the ledger fully, flattening it, pressing both palms to the page as though he could warm the ink back into blood. His grandfather had a name. All his life Ila had carried only the shape of the absence—a man who walked up a mountain, a woman who waited, a silence passed down like an heirloom nobody wanted. And here it was, not a story, not a legend, not the gold the legends promised, but a *name*, typed in triplicate, filed under wages outstanding:

*Final payment due: 2 pounds, 7 shillings.*

They had killed him and still entered him in the books. Still calculated what they owed him. The page existed because somewhere in a lowland office a clerk had needed the number to balance, and the number balanced because the men themselves had been written off, written *out*, erased from the world above and entombed in the world below with their debt unpaid and their graves unmarked and their families told nothing, *nothing*, for sixty years.

Photographs next. Tin boxes, and inside them prints gone sepia and soft: the expedition at the coast, grinning men with rifles and pith helmets; the line of porters strung out along a river bar, small as ants under the green immensity; a camp at dusk, fires lit, and Ila found his grandfather in the third photograph without looking for him—he simply *knew*, the way you know your own reflection—second from the left, a lean man with a walking stick and a cloth bound at his brow, looking directly into the camera with an expression that was not fear. It was assessment. He was measuring the men who would kill him, and Ila could see that he knew it, and had chosen to walk with them anyway, because forty mouths in the villages were hungry and the pay was real.

The last photographs were not photographs anyone had meant to keep. They were evidence, taken by whoever took them and hidden by whoever hid them: the campsite burned. Fresh earth in long mounds. Men with shovels standing in a row, faces turned from the camera—all but one, a tall man in a white shirt who looked straight into the lens with the flat confidence of a man who knows no court will ever see this picture.

On the back, in pencil: *H. site, Oct. '11. H. says burn these. Did not.*

Someone had disobeyed. Someone had carried the truth up the mountain and shelved it in the dark and sealed the door, and had died, probably, for the mercy of it, and Ila knelt in the cold and put his forehead against the tin box and wept—for the disobedient clerk whose name he would never know, and for the headman with the measuring eyes, and for his mother who had died still waiting, and for himself, kneeling in a cave full of paper while outside the snow came down like a white silence swallowing the world.

"Sacred ground," Ano murmured. "This is sacred ground now. Whatever it was before."

"No," Ila said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him, because nothing in him was steady. "It was always sacred. They just buried the proof."

* * *

Dray stood apart the whole time, at the mouth of the inner passage, rifle in his hands, saying nothing. Ila watched him read the ledgers by lamplight—watched the big man's face do things faces are not built to do, the mask of the professional cracking along seams Ila had been watching widen since the river of small bones, since the storm shelf, since the night the supplies burned and Dray's eyes had gone strange and distant, a man beginning to suspect what he was carrying and for whom.

"They knew," Dray said finally. His voice was rough, like a blade drawn across stone. "The company knew. Halvorsen knew. They hired forty men, worked them up the mountain, and when the gold didn't—" He stopped. Started again. "When the *expedition* failed, they killed every soul who could testify, filed the deaths as lost to weather, and buried the paperwork where no auditor would ever climb." He looked down at the ledger in his hands, at the eleventh line, at the name. "My employer's father signed the payroll. My employer's father signed the *order*." He said it as though tasting it. "Marcus Vane built his empire on this floor."

"And sent us to bury it deeper," Beatrix said. She was standing very still, and Ila saw in her face the same recognition he had seen on the washed-out grade—the look of a woman doing arithmetic she does not want to finish. "He didn't send us to retrieve it. Think about the timing. The radio check-ins. The route he approved. Dray—" she turned to the enforcer, and her voice was quiet and terrible, "—what exactly did Vane tell you to do if we found the cave?"

Dray did not answer. He did not have to. In the lamplight his face answered for him, and the answer was the reason the storm had come early, the reason the supplies had burned, the reason five had become six and six had become prisoners on a mountaintop with one road down and that road watched.

But that reckoning belonged to the next hour, and the hour after that, and Ila did not yet know it. What he knew, kneeling on the stone with his grandfather's name flattened beneath his palms, was simpler and older than any plan:

Forty men had walked into this mountain carrying another man's dream, and the mountain had given them back to no one, and the world above had agreed to forget them, and the forgetting had been *profitable*, and the profit had built towers in the lowlands and bought judges and bought silence and bought, sixty years on, a disgraced engineer's freedom and a tracker's loyalty and an enforcer's rifle, all of it purchased with the same coin, minted on this floor, stamped with the same fading ghost of a signature.

Ila rose. His knees cracked. He gathered the ledgers—the payroll first, then the photographs, then the correspondence—and wrapped them in the oilskin he had carried from the coast without ever letting himself ask why, because some part of him had known since Wewak that he would find paper here, not gold, and that paper burns faster than memory unless someone carries it out bodily, at cost, at any cost.

"We take them down," he said. "All of them. Every name gets read aloud in my village. Every name gets read in a courtroom if there's a courtroom left standing after."

"You understand what that means," Beatrix said. Not a question. A warning offered like a gift.

"I have understood it since I was a boy," Ila said. "I was only waiting for the names."

Behind them, deeper in the mountain, past the shelves and the grave and the steel cabinet, the passage continued downward into blackness—and from that blackness, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that stopped every man in the chamber at once.

Metal, shifting against metal. Heavy. Deliberate.

Something below had heard them coming.
