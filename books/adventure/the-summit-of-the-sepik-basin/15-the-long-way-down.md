## 15. The Long Way Down

Ila had grown up with rivers, and rivers had taught him the first law of water: it does not fight you, it waits for you to make a mistake.

The water in the lower gallery was not a river. It was something older and more patient than a river—water that had found a hollow in the mountain and filled it, decade after decade, rising by finger-widths in the dark, learning the shape of every stone. It lay black as poured tar, and it breathed. That was the worst of it. Not the cold that came off it, not the smell of iron and old timber, but the breathing—a slow swell and fall against the gallery walls, in, out, in, out, as if the mountain itself had lungs and they were drowning.

He stood at its edge with his lamp held high and made himself look at it the way he had taught himself to look at all frightening things: without blinking.

Behind him, Beatrix crouched over her father's map by lamplight, and beside her the old company drawing she had unrolled from the foreman's office above—the drainage plan, brittle as a dead moth's wing, its ink faded to the color of weak tea. Her fingers moved across it without hesitation. That was the thing about Beatrix Underhill that Ila had stopped trying to deny somewhere on the washed-out grade two days ago: when she looked at lines on paper, she did not see paper. She saw the mountain.

"Here." She tapped a junction of faded lines. "The pump gallery runs level for maybe two hundred meters. It was cut below the adit level on purpose—everything drains down to it, they pumped it back up and out. When the company abandoned the workings they'd have shut the pumps off, and the water's been climbing ever since." Her lamp swept the black surface. "It's stopped climbing. See the tidemark on the wall? It's been at this level for years. Which means somewhere below us, the water found its own way out. There's a natural fissure, or an old borehole, something. The mountain made its own drain."

"And we follow the drain," Ila said.

"We follow the drain." She looked up at him, and in the lamplight her face was pale and set, the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "It'll be chest-deep in places. Maybe deeper. The air's moving, though—" she lifted the lamp flame, and it leaned, faintly, toward the dark end of the gallery, "—which means the way out is that way, and it's open."

Behind them, from the upper chambers, came a sound that made them all go still: the distant, rhythmic knock of Dray's signal lamp against the shaft mouth, three flashes and a pause, three flashes and a pause. Talking to the sky. Talking to the helicopter that would come in the morning with Vane's men, and no room in it for witnesses.

Tamau shifted the oilskin bundle higher on his shoulder. It was heavy—ledgers and photograph albums and the company's own confession, wrapped in waxed canvas and bound with cord—and he carried it the way a man carries a sleeping child, carefully, with his whole body.

"The records come with us," Tamau said. It was not a question and not a request. It was a stone laid down.

"Yes," Ila said, in the language of his village, and then again in the trade tongue, so there could be no mistake. "Yes."

* * *

They went into the water at the gallery's lowest corner, one by one, lamps held above their heads.

The cold was total. It climbed Ila's legs like a living thing, closed over his waist, his chest, and he heard his own breath go thin and high and forced it down, forced it slow, the way his grandfather had taught him to breathe when crossing the flooded karst caves of the upper Sepik as a boy. *The water is not your enemy. The panic is your enemy. The water only wants you to hurry, and hurrying is how the water wins.*

Beatrix went first, testing every step with a pole cut from a broken stull, reading the floor beneath the black water by touch and by the geometry in her head. "Sump here—waist deep, floor's level for ten meters—watch the left side, there's rail iron, don't catch your feet." Her voice echoed strange and flattened in the flooded stone. She had stopped being afraid of the mountain, Ila realized. Or rather, she had made a bargain with her fear long ago and knew its terms.

The second porter, Yawan, came behind her. Then Tamau with the records, held high above the water in both arms, his face a mask of concentration. Then the last porter, old Mek, who had come up the mountain complaining about his knees and had not complained once since they found the grave. Ila went last, walking backward for the first hundred meters, his lamp trained on the gallery behind them, watching the black water and the dark beyond it for anything that moved.

Nothing moved. The mountain did not need to move. The mountain only needed to wait.

They waded. The gallery ran straight and level, cut by men with drills and dynamite sixty years ago, and the water rose and fell against them with that slow tidal breathing, and their lamps made a small trembling circle of amber in an ocean of dark. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred meters of stone, the storm that had driven them here still worked at the mountain's shoulders. Ila heard it as a deep, irregular groaning, like a giant turning in its sleep.

Twice the water deepened to their chins, and they crossed those places by swimming, lamps held overhead, packs and the oilskin bundle passed hand to hand above the surface. Once Beatrix stopped so suddenly that Yawan nearly ran into her back, and her lamp found a shape standing in the water ahead of them—a pump, vast and furred with white mineral growth, its iron body furred over, its great wheel still, the machine that had once held this water down now standing drowned and blind in the dark like a dead god.

"The pump gallery," Beatrix said quietly. "We're in it. Which means the shaft is—" she turned, counting her paces, reading the mountain's bones, "—there. Forty meters. The shaft head was above the waterline when the pumps ran. If the water found its own way out below us, the shaft might be dry. Might be."

*Might be.* Ila had learned on this journey that those two words were the whole of engineering, and the whole of courage, and possibly the whole of life.

They reached the shaft. It rose above them out of the black water, a square of deeper darkness, and when Beatrix threw a stone up into it they heard it clatter against timber and then, after a long falling, land on something dry with a knock like a knuckle on a door.

Dry. Above the water, dry.

There was a ladder in the shaft wall, iron rungs set into the rock, and the first twenty rungs were under the water and furred with slime. Beatrix went up first, her lamp clenched in her teeth, and the sight of her climbing—small and deliberate and utterly without hurry—was the bravest thing Ila saw in all the long dark of that day.

* * *

It was on the last crossing that the water won its mistake.

They were nearly through. The final flooded stretch ran forty meters to the foot of the shaft, and beyond the shaft, Beatrix promised, the old drainage adit broke out onto the mountainside—a borehole the water had found, or the water had found the borehole, and either way it meant sky. Tamau had passed the oilskin bundle forward to Yawan for the crossing, because Yawan was the strongest swimmer, because the water here came to their necks, because it was the sensible thing, the careful thing, and Ila approved it at the time and would approve it every day of his life afterward and hate himself every day of his life afterward, both at once, which was, he was beginning to understand, what grief was.

Yawan was ten meters from the shaft when the floor went out from under him.

It was not dramatic. There was no roar, no collapse, no warning. One of the old stulls—a rotted timber prop holding up a section of false floor over some deeper working—simply gave, the way dead things give, quietly and all at once, and the water swallowed him. His lamp went under and came up and went under again, and his hand came up out of the black holding the oilskin bundle high, higher, straining it toward the light even as his head went down, and Ila understood, in one white-hot instant, what Yawan was doing: he was not swimming for himself. He was swimming for the records. His body had become a raft for the dead men's names.

Ila went in after him.

The water under the broken floor was moving—a slow, sucking pull, the mountain's drain drawing breath—and it took Ila's legs and turned him and he fought it the way his grandfather had taught him, not against it but across it, angling, angling, his lungs full of fire, his hand closing on cloth, on an arm, on Yawan's wrist, and for one moment he had him, he had him, Yawan's face surfaced beside his own, eyes wide and calm in a way that Ila would see every night for the rest of his life.

"The bundle," Yawan said. Not *help me*. Not *pull*. The bundle.

Ila's hand found the oilskin and tore it free of the drowning man's grip, and the current took Yawan the way the tide takes a leaf, without malice, without haste, and the black water closed over his head and did not open again.

They found the bundle later, floating. They found Yawan never. Ila called his name into the flooded dark until Beatrix's hand on his shoulder told him what his own body already knew, that the mountain had made its choice, and the mountain's choices were not appealed.

* * *

They came out of the drainage adit into evening.

It was a hole no wider than a grave, cut into the mountainside's knee, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging moss, and the air that came through it was cold and clean and smelled of snow and stone and living things, and Ila knelt in it and put his face into the wind and shook.

Behind him, in the green twilight, Tamau sat with the oilskin bundle in his lap, unwrapped it, and checked every ledger, every photograph, every page, by lamplight, with the thoroughness of a man performing a rite. When he finished he rewrapped it, bound the cord, and held the bundle against his chest.

"He held it up," Tamau said. "The whole time. He held it up so it wouldn't get wet." His voice did not break. It went somewhere else instead, somewhere flat and vast, like ice under load. "Forty names in there. Now forty-one."

"No," Ila said. He heard his own voice come out of him, low and certain, and did not know where it came from either. "Forty names in there, and one more now that we carry in us. We will read them all. We will read all of them, and we will say his name last, and no one—" he looked up at Beatrix, at Mek, at Tamau, at the small dark hole in the mountain behind them, "—no one will ever make us stop."

Beatrix said nothing. She was looking at the bundle, and then at the mountainside below them, where far down through the thinning cloud the lights of a helicopter moved against the dark like a slow green star, circling, searching, patient as water.

"They'll have heard the blast route's blocked," she said at last, quietly. "Vane will assume we're sealed in. Which means the only people in the world who know we're out are standing right here." She shouldered her pack. "The rail grade starts below the tree line. If we move tonight, we're at the coast before his men think to look down."

Ila took one last look at the adit mouth, at the black breathing dark behind the moss, at the place where Yawan had gone down into the mountain carrying nothing but his own name.

Then he turned his back on it, and began to climb down.
