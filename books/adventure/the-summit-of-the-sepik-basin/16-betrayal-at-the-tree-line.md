## 16. Betrayal at the Tree Line

The light hit them first.

After the flooded galleries—after hours of black water to the chest and the weight of the oilskin packet strapped to Ila's back, after the porter Tamu's body going down into the drainage sump with the records held high above the water until the water took him anyway—the grey daylight at the cave's lower mouth felt less like salvation than like an accusation. It fell through the trees in long, cold shafts, catching the mist that rose off the river below, and Beatrix stood in it dripping, shaking, unable to decide whether her hands trembled from cold or from the arithmetic she could not stop performing.

Four had gone into the cave. Three came out. The packet weighed perhaps four pounds dry, and it had cost a man who had a wife in the lowlands and a laugh like a door swinging open.

They had climbed for two hours through the forest above the river, Ila setting a pace that asked no questions and permitted none, until the trees began to thin and change. The broadleaf canopy opened. Moss gave way to tussock grass, bleached gold and silver by the altitude, and the air took on the thin, clean edge of snow country. They had crossed the snowline days ago on the way up; now they were descending through it again, and the storm front that had chased them down the mountain hung behind them like a grey curtain, close enough that Beatrix could feel the pressure of it against her back.

The tree line was a ragged border ahead—stunted pandanus and gnarled beech leaning away from the wind, and beyond them, far below through a break in the cloud, the glint of the river valley and, somewhere in it, the rusted skeleton of the old Halvorsen tramway that would carry them down to the coast.

Ila stopped.

Beatrix, three steps behind, walked into the stillness of his back and understood before she saw. There was a wrongness in the clearing ahead, the particular wrongness of a place where a man is standing who should not be standing there.

Dray stepped out from behind a wind-twisted beech with his rifle held low but not lowered, and Beatrix felt the last three days of terror compress into a single cold point somewhere behind her sternum.

* * *

She had imagined this moment, in the flooded dark, in the way a drowning woman imagines air. Dray, waiting above. Dray, with the radio and the rifle and Vane's voice in his ear. She had rehearsed what she would say—*the records are copies now, killing us buys him nothing*—and she had rehearsed what she would do, which was put her body between Dray and Ila and the packet, because if the truth was going to die on this mountain it was not going to die without her fingerprints on the attempt to save it.

But seeing him now, she found her rehearsals useless. Dray looked wrong. Not the wrongness of a man about to commit murder—she had seen that wrongness before, in Vane's offices, in the faces of men who had learned to think of people as line items. This was a different wrongness. Dray looked like a man whose skeleton had been removed and replaced with something that barely held him upright.

His lips were cracked and grey. There was a burn scar fresh along his left forearm where the supply fire had caught him, wrapped in a strip of filthy bandage. And his eyes—she had thought on the first day, back in Wewak, that Dray's eyes were the flattest she had ever seen, a man's eyes looking out of a locked room. They were not flat now. They were the eyes of a man in a locked room with the door open, who has just discovered he is afraid of what's outside.

"Stop there," he said. His voice came out sanded down to the grain.

Ila stopped. Beatrix stopped. The wind moved through the tussock grass in long silver waves, and somewhere above them the storm made a sound like a train crossing a trestle.

"You're a long way from your helicopter, Dray," Beatrix said. She was surprised by how steady her voice came out. Somewhere in the last three days she had used up all her fear on things worse than this.

"Helicopter can't land in this weather." He shifted the rifle. "Couldn't land yesterday either. That's why he sent me ahead on foot. That's why I'm here and it isn't."

"You could have waited at the cave mouth," Ila said quietly. "You knew the lower entrance. You let us walk out."

"I let you walk out," Dray repeated, as if the words were in a language he was still translating.

Beatrix took one step forward. Ila's hand touched her arm—not restraining, just reminding—and she stopped, but she kept her eyes on Dray.

"The radio," she said. "He's told you something on that radio. Something that changed your face. What was it?"

Dray laughed. It was a terrible sound, short and airless, like a man coughing up a stone. "Changed my face." He looked at her for a long moment, and then, moving with the deliberate slowness of a man handling something that might detonate, he slung the rifle over his shoulder—away from them, she noted, away—and reached into his jacket.

He threw the radio at her feet.

It landed in the tussock grass, a black box the size of a brick, its antenna snapped off clean. Broken. Deliberately, thoroughly broken.

"I'm supposed to confirm you're dead," Dray said. "Both of you. The porter too, if he made it. I'm supposed to report a cave collapse. Rockfall, secondary to the flooding. Tragic. Unavoidable. Vane's lawyers have been drafting the language since Tuesday." He swallowed. "There were four of them, you know. The last ones who found this place. Nineteen sixty-one. A survey team. Two company men and two locals."

The wind gusted. Beatrix did not move. She was afraid that if she moved, if she breathed wrong, whatever had opened in this man would close again, and she would spend the rest of her life not knowing what he had been about to say.

"Vane's father covered up the massacre in 1911," Dray went on, and his voice had gone flat again, but it was the flatness of a man reading from a document he had memorized against his will. "The gold, the ledgers, the forty porters. That was the founding sin. The company that became Vane Consolidated was built on top of it like a road built over a grave. And in sixty-one, when the survey team found the upper gallery—the records, the grave—Marcus Vane was thirty-one years old and he made a decision." Dray's jaw worked. "My father made it for him, actually. My father was Vane's father's enforcer. Same job. It runs in the family like a blood type."

"What happened to the survey team?" Ila asked. His voice was very quiet, and very steady, and Beatrix heard in it the same tone he had used over the bones in the gravel bar, the tone of a man who had made a promise to the dead and intended to keep it.

"They never came down the mountain," Dray said. "Officially. Unofficially—" He stopped. He looked at his own hands as though they belonged to someone else. "My father took three men up and came back with three men and said the weather had them. And my father told me this story when I was nineteen, the way you'd tell a boy a story about a lion hunt. Proud of it. And I laughed, God help me. I laughed, because I was nineteen and it was a story." He raised his eyes to Beatrix. "And then three weeks ago Vane sat me down in his office and gave me the same story with the same ending, about you, and he smiled while he said it, and I heard my own father's voice coming out of my mouth before I'd said a word."

* * *

The storm was closer now. Beatrix could see it eating the ridge behind them, a grey tide of cloud and rain swallowing the slopes they had crossed that morning. In an hour, perhaps two, it would be on them, and after that would come the snow—the early, vicious winter that had already arrived a month ahead of schedule and turned their descent into a race with a closing door.

She looked at the broken radio in the grass. She looked at Dray.

"Why?" she asked. It was the only word she had. "You've been his for years. You burned the supplies. You were going to seal us in that cave—I heard you tell the porters to stack the crates across the gallery mouth. Why are you standing here now telling me this?"

"Because you came out," Dray said simply. "Vane's arithmetic only works if people don't come out. You came out. Tamu came out carrying the truth and it killed him, and he came out anyway." His voice caught on the porter's name and he pushed through it. "I've spent my whole life being the man who makes sure people don't come out. And you—" he gestured at her, at all of her, the mud and the leech scars and the shaking hands and the oilskin packet's absence on her back because Ila carried it—"you're a liar and a forger and a coward. I read your file. I know what you did to that tunnel, what your calculations buried. You're not better than me, Mrs. Underhill. You're the same animal that got caught."

"Yes," Beatrix said. She did not flinch from it. She had confessed it around a fire five nights ago, and there was no taking it back and no reason to. "I am."

"But you came out anyway," Dray said. "Knowing what was waiting. That's the part my arithmetic can't do." He unslung the rifle, and Beatrix's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs—but he only worked the bolt, ejected the single chambered round into the grass, and then, methodically, field-stripped the weapon with the efficiency of long practice, scattering its parts across twenty feet of tussock. The bolt he hurled off the slope, into the cloud below. "I'm not going to kill you. I'm not going to help you, either, because if I help you, Vane will know what I am before you ever reach a courtroom, and he'll bury this deeper than the cave, and he'll bury me with it, and none of it will matter. So I'm going to be the one thing I've never been."

"Which is?" Ila asked.

"Useless." Dray almost smiled. It was a ruined thing. "I'm going to sit here at the tree line until the storm closes the pass, and when Vane's men come up looking for me I'm going to tell them I lost you on the glacier in the whiteout. And they'll believe it, because it's the kind of failure I would report. And by the time the weather clears enough for them to search properly, you'll be down the tramway and gone." He looked at the sky, at the storm, and his ruined almost-smile faded. "And I'm going to stay on this mountain. After. I'm not going back down."

Beatrix understood then, and the understanding went through her like cold water. "Penance," she said.

"My father's bones are in the lowlands cemetery with a company headstone and a lie on it," Dray said. "Mine can stay up here. Near the cave. Near the forty." He shrugged, a small, terrible gesture. "It's not much. I know it's not much. It's the only currency I have."

* * *

The wind was rising. Beatrix could feel the storm's leading edge now, a fine cold rain beginning to sift through the broken light, and she knew that every minute they stood in this clearing was a minute stolen from the descent. The tramway grade below them was rotten and washed out in four places she knew of; in a whiteout it would be suicide. They had to move.

And yet she did not move. Because there was something she had to say, and she suspected that if she did not say it now she would never have the courage again.

"Dray." He looked at her. "What you're doing—staying here, taking his blame on yourself so we can carry the truth down—it's not nothing. You're right that it isn't much. But it isn't nothing." She crouched and picked up the broken radio, turned it over in her hands. "But I won't carry your penance for you. I've seen what it does to a person, carrying something that isn't theirs. I carried Vane's secret for six years and it nearly killed me. So I'm going to tell you what I wish someone had told me." She stood. "Come down with us. Walk into that courthouse and say all of this out loud, in the light, where it counts. Testimony is the only penance that ever freed anyone. I know, because I'm about to find out."

Dray stared at her. For a moment—just a moment—the locked room behind his eyes opened all the way, and she saw what was in it: a boy of nineteen laughing at his father's story, and the forty years since, and the door he had never known was there.

Then he shook his head, slowly, and the door closed.

"Not yet," he said. "I'm not ready to be that man in the light. Maybe I'll follow you down. Maybe I'll be a few days behind you, and maybe I'll walk into that courthouse, and maybe I won't. But if I do—" he looked at Ila, and something passed between them that Beatrix could not read, some current older than either of them—"it will be for the forty names. Not for you. Not for me. For them."

Ila nodded once. Then he reached into his jacket and took out the oilskin packet—not the original; the original, Tamu's original, had gone into the sump with him, and what Ila carried was the copy he had made in the cave by candlelight, page by page, while Beatrix held the light and her hands shook—and he held it out.

Dray looked at it and did not take it. "No," he said. "That's not mine to hold. That's yours. That was always going to be yours." He stepped back. "Go. The storm won't wait and neither should you. The tramway's third trestle is gone—I scouted it on the way up. There's a bypass on the upstream side, old maintenance stairs cut into the rock. Look for the iron ring bolted into the cliff. My father showed me a photograph of it once, from the company files." His mouth twisted. "The company keeps records of everything except its sins."

They turned to go. Three steps into the tussock, Beatrix stopped, because there was one more thing, and it was not courage this time but simple arithmetic—the engineer's habit of accounting for every load on the line.

"Dray. When they come for you. When Vane's men ask where you lost us." She looked back at him, standing alone in the silver grass with the storm rolling down on him like a tide. "Tell them we went down the glacier on the east side. It's a lie that costs you nothing and buys us a day."

Dray nodded. And then, as they turned again toward the tree line and the broken tramway and the long descent into the valley where the courthouse and the lowlands and the end of everything waited, she heard him call out—one last thing, thrown after them into the rising wind, and she carried it down the mountain with her the way Ila carried the names:

"Mrs. Underhill! The gold is still in there! He'll never stop—not for the records, for the gold! Whatever you do in that courtroom—" the wind took the rest, and then the wind took him, the grey curtain of the storm closing over the tree line like a book shutting, "—make it count!"

Beatrix ran. Ila ran beside her, the packet strapped tight against his heart, and behind them the storm swallowed the mountain, and ahead of them, far below through a tearing gap in the cloud, the rusted bones of the Halvorsen tramway caught the last of the light—three days of rotten trestles and dead rails between them and the truth, and Vane already waiting at the bottom of it.
