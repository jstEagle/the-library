## 17. Race Before the White Silence

The snow came down the mountain like a verdict.

Beatrix felt it before she saw it—a pressure change in her ears, a hush that swallowed even the wind, and then the first flakes, fat and wrong-looking against the green of the tree line they'd just left behind. Above them, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and somewhere in that gray ceiling was a front Ila had been watching for two days with the expression of a man counting down to something.

"It's early," he said now, standing at the edge of the washout where the old Halvorsen grade began its long descent toward the coast. "A month early. Maybe more."

"Then we don't stop." Beatrix shrugged the oilskin pack higher on her shoulders—the one that held the records wrapped in canvas and waxed cloth, forty names and a massacre's worth of ledgers pressing against her spine like a second skeleton. "How far to the first trestle?"

"Four kilometers. If it's still standing."

She looked at him. He didn't look away.

"If it's still standing," she repeated, "we cross it. And if it isn't, we find another way down, because there is no other way down. This grade was cut through sixty kilometers of country that has no paths, no villages, and no mercy. The company built it because nothing else could get men and machinery over this range. That means nothing else can get us over it either—except going back, and going back is Dray's radio and Vane's helicopter."

Ila said nothing for a moment. Snow gathered on his shoulders, on the rifle slung across his back, on the compass hanging open in his hand. Then he closed it with a snap.

"Then show me how a railroad woman reads a mountain."

* * *

The grade itself was a ghost.

It ran along the mountainside in long, patient shelves of cut stone, benched into the slope by men with drills and dynamite seventy years ago, and the jungle had been trying to swallow it ever since. Ferns grew waist-high across the ballast. A tree had fallen across the right-of-way a decade or more ago and rotted where it lay, its trunk furred with moss like something buried alive. In places the retaining wall below the grade had slumped outward, and the edge simply wasn't there anymore—just air, and beneath the air, six hundred meters of cloud-forest canopy going down into shadow.

Beatrix walked it the way she'd once walked track inspections, and the old knowledge rose in her like water finding its level.

"See the drainage cuts?" she said, pointing with her ice axe at the shallow channels scored into the uphill side every twenty meters. "Whoever built this understood water. Water is what kills a grade—not the trains, not the rock. The water gets under your ballast, freezes, thaws, freezes again, and one morning your roadbed is soup. These men cut drains so the mountain would bleed safely past them instead of through them."

"And the mountain won?"

"The mountain always wins eventually. But—" She crouched, brushed snow from a culvert headwall of fitted stone, still true after seven decades. "Not today. Today the mountain is on our side. It built us a road."

They made three kilometers in an hour and a half, which was fast, which was too fast, and Beatrix knew it and did it anyway. The snow thickened as they dropped—no, she corrected herself, as they descended out of the front's leading edge and into its body; altitude wasn't the whole story here, the front was riding down the valley behind them like a tide. Behind them the world was already white. Ahead, the grade vanished into flurries that came sideways now, stinging, erasing the cut stone ten meters at a time so that walking it became an act of memory: here the wall should be on my left, here the curve should bear right, here—

Here the ground ended.

She stopped so suddenly that Ila walked into her shoulder. Where the grade should have continued, curving along the spur toward the river bend below, there was a gap. Forty meters of it, maybe fifty. The retaining wall had let go sometime in the last seventy years and taken the roadbed with it, and the fill material—rock, spoil, whatever the builders had used—had slid down the mountainside in a long brown scar that disappeared into the white.

"Trestle," Ila said quietly.

And there it was. Below the gap, half-buried in the slide debris, the skeletal legs of the old timber trestle still stood—six bents of blackened hardwood rising out of the rubble, their caps furred with moss, their bracing hung with vines. The deck was gone. The stringers were mostly gone. What remained was a ladder of dead timber bolted to the face of the mountain, descending steeply to a shelf of intact grade maybe thirty meters below.

"That's not a crossing," Ila said. "That's a grave with stairs."

"That's a crossing," Beatrix said, "if we're careful, and if the timber's sound, and if the bolts are sound, and if we go one at a time." She was already moving down the slide scar, testing each step, feeling for the shift of loose rock under her boots. At the first bent she put her hand on the timber and pressed. Pressed harder. Put her whole weight against it, then kicked it, then climbed it two rungs and jumped.

The bent groaned. Held.

"Seventy years," she called up, "and the heartwood's still dense as iron. They used kwila for these—there's wood rotting all around it and this hasn't moved." She looked up through the falling snow at Ila's silhouette. "One at a time. Pack between us on the rope. Three points of contact, always. If a bolt lets go, you don't grab the timber—you ride it down to the shelf and pray the shelf is real."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then the records go over with you, and I go over after them, and Vane wins." She said it flatly, without drama, because drama was a luxury and the cold was already taxing everything else. "Move."

He came down the way she told him, slow and deliberate, and when his boots hit the shelf beside hers he exhaled a breath that shook, just slightly, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

The second trestle was worse, and the third was worse than that.

By midday the whiteout had closed around them completely. There was no horizon. There was no up and down except what her feet told her and what the grade's geometry insisted on, and Beatrix found herself navigating by engineering—by the remembered cadence of curves and grades, by the feel of superelevation underfoot where the rails had once banked into turns, by the drainage cuts she counted like rosary beads. Once, the grade passed through a short tunnel blasted through a rib of bedrock, and inside it the snow stopped and the silence was so total she could hear her own heartbeat, and for three seconds she stood in the dark and let herself remember another tunnel, another collapse, the sound of her own falsified numbers coming down on top of her.

Then Ila's hand closed on her arm, gentle, and they walked out the other side into the white.

At the third trestle the deck survived—rotted, holed, sagging, but present—and they crossed it on hands and knees, distributing weight across stringers that cracked like pistol shots but did not give way. On the far side, huddled in the lee of a cut, they ate cold rice and dried fish and did not speak much. Beatrix caught herself doing calculations without meaning to: distance covered, distance remaining, rate of descent, rate of the storm's advance. The numbers kept coming out ugly.

"We're losing the race," Ila said, reading her face the way he read everything else.

"We're losing the race on foot." She stared out into the white, and her mind, treacherous, useful mind, was already somewhere else. "But this is a railroad, Ila. It was built to move things faster than legs can carry them."

"There are no trains."

"No." She turned to look back up the grade, into the storm, and smiled in a way that she knew from experience frightened people. "There aren't."

* * *

They found it four kilometers further down, in a maintenance siding carved into a bench above the river gorge: a handcar, or what remained of one.

The shed had collapsed on it. Years of rain had gotten into the timber. But the frame was steel—lighter than the American cars she'd known, British-built, the company's fingerprints all over it—and the mechanism was simple the way all good machines are simple: a lever-and-cam drive, two pump handles, four small wheels on a chassis. She cleared the debris off it with hands that were bleeding somewhere under her gloves and hadn't noticed, and checked the axles, and spun each wheel and listened.

"They're round," she reported. "Mostly. The bearings are dry but they're not seized. The track—" She stood and looked down the grade, where the rails emerged from the snow like the spines of drowned animals. Rusted, yes. Spiked into ties that were more termite dust than timber, yes. But the rails themselves, steel laid heavy for ore traffic, might outlast civilization.

"You want to ride that," Ila said. Not quite a question.

"I want to *coast* that. We don't pump—we pump and the wheels find every rotted tie and throw us into the gorge. We push it to the top of the next grade, get on, and let gravity do the work while we steer with our feet and our prayers. The grade profile—" she tapped her temple, where her father's map lived alongside everything else she'd memorized and couldn't forget— "drops nearly nine hundred meters over the next twelve kilometers, most of it in the first five. One good run could put us below the storm."

"One bad run puts us at the bottom of the gorge."

"Yes." She met his eyes. Snow had gotten inside her collar and melted down her back and she was shivering and she didn't care. "Ila. I spent eleven years building things that killed people because I lied about the numbers. Every bridge I signed, every grade I certified—I was gambling with strangers' lives to make a financier rich, and I told myself the margins were fine, the margins were always fine, until the day they weren't and a tunnel fell on me and Marcus Vane pulled me out and owned me forever after." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "This is the first time in my life the engineering is pointed at something true. Let me have it."

For a long moment the only sound was the storm moving through the trees above the cut.

"Push," Ila said.

* * *

The first kilometer nearly killed them, and the second was worse, and by the third Beatrix had stopped being afraid and entered the strange clear country beyond fear where there was only the machine and the line and the falling snow.

They pumped anyway—gently, in short strokes, keeping the car rolling through the flats and the reverse grades, easing off hard whenever the rails sang a note she didn't like. Twice the track ahead vanished into a washout and they saw it barely in time, bailed off, dragged the car around the gap on shoulders screaming with lactic acid, and pushed on. Once a trestle appeared out of the white so suddenly that Ila made a sound low in his throat, and Beatrix made the call in half a second—deck intact, stringers sound, speed too high to stop—and they went across it at speed with the timbers hammering underneath them like a drum corps, and came out the other side laughing, both of them, wild and shocked at the sound of their own laughter.

The gorge deepened on their left. The snow turned to sleet, then to freezing rain, then, as they dropped, to a cold grey drizzle that was almost warm. The jungle came back—first as stunted moss-bearded scrub, then as real forest, ferns and pandanus and the sudden riotous green of lower altitudes—and with it the light changed, the whiteout thinning into mere storm.

At kilometer eleven the grade ended.

Not faded, not washed away—ended, at a great stone abutment above the river, where the rails had once continued onto a bridge across the gorge and the bridge was gone. All of it. Every span, every pier, nothing left but the stubs of girders and the river running brown and swollen far below.

Beatrix brought the handcar to a stop with her boot heel, and the silence afterward rang in her ears.

"Down there." Ila pointed with his chin. On the far bank, faint through the rain, a track switchbacked down toward the floodplain—the old portage trail, the one the map showed, the one that reached the coast road in a day's hard walking. "We swim the river at the gravel bar below the falls. The water's high but the bar breaks the current."

Behind them, up the mountain, the storm went on burying the grade, the trestles, the handcar, the whole impossible apparatus of a dead railway carrying three living people's testimony down out of the clouds. Somewhere up there, too, was Dray—left at the cave mouth with his radio and his penance—and Beatrix found she could not decide whether the man was guarding their retreat or reporting it, and whether it mattered.

It mattered. Everything mattered now. The records against her spine were the only copies that mattered, and Vane knew they existed, and Vane had lawyers and boats and forty years of practice at making problems disappear.

"He'll know we're coming," she said. "Dray's radio, or the helicopter pilot, or just his own instincts. He'll be waiting at the coast."

Ila shouldered his pack and looked down the river toward the sea he could not yet see.

"Good," he said. "Let him watch us arrive."

They started down the portage trail together, three sets of footprints in the mud where the snow could not follow, and ahead of them, past the last bends of the river, the lowlands waited with their lights and their lawyers and their lies—and Beatrix Underhill, engineer, liar, witness, walked toward all of it with her chin up and the storm dying at her back.
