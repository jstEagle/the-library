## 18. Vane at the Mouth

The sea came back to them the way a debt comes back—suddenly, and with interest.

Ila smelled it before he saw it: salt and rot and diesel, threaded through the cold pine air of the last descent. Three days they had come down out of the white silence, three days of Beatrix reading the dead rail grade like scripture, calling out rotten trestles and washed-out culverts while he carried the oilskin bundle against his chest like a second heartbeat. The records inside it were damp at the corners now, soft as old leaves, but whole. Forty names. Ledgers in a dead man's hand. Photographs of men standing over trenches that had never been dug for any mine.

And beneath all of it, wrapped separately because Beatrix had insisted, the bullion's weight was gone from them entirely—they had left it in the dark, sealed behind Dray's hesitation and their own haste—but its ghost rode along anyway. Ila could feel it in the way Beatrix walked, quick and guilty, like a woman carrying something stolen even though she had taken nothing.

They came out of the tree line at dusk, onto the shoulder of land above Wewak's harbor, and Ila stopped so suddenly that Tamis, the last porter still with them, walked into his back.

Below, the bay lay flat and pewter-colored under the first stars. The earthquake tide had long since returned what it had borrowed; the cave mouth on the far headland was drowned again, invisible, as if the sea had agreed to keep the secret. Fishing boats rocked at their moorings. Smoke rose from cookfires in the town, thin and domestic and ordinary.

And on the shingle beach below them, where no boat had any business landing at this hour, stood a launch with twin engines and a man beside it wearing a white suit.

"Vane," Tamis breathed.

"No," Ila said quietly. "Vane sends other people to do his walking. That is Vane himself."

He had never seen the financier in person. He had seen his name stamped on company ledgers, on the deed to half the port town, on the tin signs nailed to trees along the government road—VANE CONSOLIDATED MINING, PROGRESS THROUGH ORDER. He had expected a fat man, or a hard one, someone whose cruelty would show in the shoulders. Marcus Vane was neither. He was silver-haired and straight-backed, and he stood on the wet stones of the beach with his hands clasped behind him like a churchwarden waiting for the congregation, patient, unhurried, absolutely certain.

With him were six men. Two held lanterns. One held a rifle with the casualness of a man holding an umbrella. And two more—thin, pale, in city clothes ruined by the boat trip—stood slightly apart, and Ila knew them for what they were before either spoke: lawyers. He could smell paper on them from two hundred yards.

Beside him, Beatrix made a sound low in her throat. Not fear. Something older than fear.

"He came himself," she said. "After everything, he actually came."

"That should tell you something," Ila said.

"It tells me everything." She shifted the oilskin higher under her coat. "It tells me what's in here is worth more than he ever told us."

* * *

They went down because there was nowhere else to go. The grade behind them was buried under the storm front; the ridge line was a day's climb through snow they had no strength left to fight; and Tamis had a fever burning in him that made each step a negotiation. Ila took the lead anyway, putting his body between the rifle and Beatrix without deciding to do it, the way you put your hand between a child and a snake.

The sand crunched under their boots. Up close, Vane's suit was immaculate in a way that seemed almost supernatural—as if the swamp mud of the basin had simply declined to touch him. His face was weathered but kind-looking, the face of a grandfather in a photograph, and when his eyes found Beatrix they warmed visibly.

"Miss Underhill." His voice was soft, pitched to carry only across the space between them. "You look terrible. Which means you succeeded."

"You know what we found," Beatrix said. Not a question.

"I know what my cave contains, yes. I've known since I was nine years old, when my father got drunk for the only time in his life and told me why we were rich." Vane smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Forty men went into those mountains in 1911 to haul out gold for the Halvorsen syndicate. My father's syndicate. Forty men came out of the story, but not out of the mountain. The company needed the expedition to fail quietly—the war was coming, the claims were contested, and dead men make excellent witnesses. So the mountain was made to eat them. It has been eating them ever since. Every few years, someone finds a bone or a ledger page, and every few years, someone pays to have it forgotten." He spread his hands, a gesture of almost pastoral resignation. "You would not believe how expensive forgetting is, Miss Underhill. It is the single largest line item in my family's history."

"And now?" Ila said.

Vane turned to him, and for one unguarded instant the kindness fell away and Ila saw the thing underneath: not rage, nothing so human as rage. Arithmetic. He was being weighed, priced, entered into a column.

"Now," Vane said, "the tide has changed. An earthquake opens my cave to every fisherman on the coast. A disgraced engineer with a grudge and a guide with a grievance walks into it. And somewhere in that oilskin—" his eyes flicked to Beatrix's coat, precise as a surveyor's transit "—are documents that could put my entire enterprise into receivership, and me, possibly, into a courtroom. So." He gestured, and one of the lawyers stepped forward with a leather case, opening it on a portable desk he produced from nowhere, lamplight falling on clean white pages. "Let us discuss terms."

* * *

What followed was the most obscene thing Ila had ever witnessed, and he had watched a river take his cousin's body and give it back three days later, swollen and wrong.

Vane offered everything.

Full pardon—not just forgiveness of the debt, which he waved away as though it had been a misunderstanding between friends, but the falsified load calculations too, the collapsed tunnel, the men hurt in it, the whole buried architecture of Beatrix's disgrace. Burned, shredded, forgotten. Her engineer's license restored by morning, with letters of recommendation from men who owed him. A house in the port town. A partnership. A number spoken aloud that made Tamis, feverish and swaying, whisper a word in his own language that meant *a thing beyond counting.*

"All of it," Vane said gently, "for one small act of tidiness. The papers burn tonight, on this beach, in front of me. You keep the gold's location—you've earned that much—and in the spring my crews open the cave properly, legally, and no one alive will ever connect Miss Underhill to anything but a successful survey. The dead stay dead. The past stays past. Everyone walks home rich." He paused, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "Or refuse, and I promise you—I am a man of my word, ask anyone in this town—that you will lose the license anyway, the freedom anyway, and the guide and the porter will lose considerably more. There is no third door, Miss Underhill. There never was. That is rather the point of doors."

The lanterns hissed. Somewhere out on the water, a fishing boat's engine coughed and died, and the silence after it was enormous.

Ila did not look at the money. He looked at Beatrix.

He had known her for less than a month, and in that month she had lied to him twice that he knew of and probably a dozen times that he didn't. She had used his grief as a lever to get him onto the mountain. She had confessed her crime around a fire with her eyes fixed on the flames because she couldn't fix them on him. He had spent most of the journey telling himself she was exactly what she appeared to be: a debtor, a survivor, a woman who would sell whatever was necessary to buy back her own skin, because everyone he had ever met from the world of ledgers and licenses was exactly that.

But he had also watched her kneel in a gravel bar full of small bones and count them, one by one, aloud, in a voice that shook. He had watched her read a drowned railway like a love letter. He had watched her, in the dark of the cave, hold his grandfather's ledger page up to her lamp and sound out the faded mission-school handwriting syllable by syllable until she found the name—*Anaru Ila, porter, aged 19*—and then look up at him with tears running freely down her filthy face and say, "Nineteen. He was nineteen years old," as if the boy had been hers.

So he watched her now, and he tried to see what she saw.

She was looking at the fire pit the lawyers' assistants were already building—dry driftwood, lamp oil, a neat pyre prepared in advance, because Vane had never doubted, not for one second, that it would come to this. She was looking at Tamis, who could barely stand, who would die on the mountain if they turned back. She was looking at her own hands, and Ila understood with a lurch that she was looking at them the way she must have looked at them after the tunnel came down: measuring what they had destroyed, weighing what they might destroy next.

"Beatrix," Ila said.

She didn't turn.

"They gave my grandfather a number," he said. "In the company ledger. Entry forty-one. No name—just a wage owed, marked *unclaimed.* For fifty years, that page sat in a drawer in that cave, and the only person who ever said his name out loud was my grandmother, and then my mother, and then me. Saying it was our job. Ours alone." He stepped closer, close enough that the nearest guard shifted his weight. "If those papers burn, the ledger burns. And then nobody says it ever again. Not even us. Because we'll be the only proof left, and you know what happens to the only proof left."

"I know," Beatrix said. Her voice was very quiet. "Ila, I know."

"He was nineteen."

"I know."

Vane cleared his throat softly, the way a man does when the orchestra has played long enough. "Miss Underhill. The offer is generous because the situation is urgent. But it is not infinite."

And Beatrix Underhill turned to face him, and Ila saw her arrive at herself.

It happened in her shoulders first. All the way down the mountain she had carried the oilskin hunched against her spine, protective, guilty, a smuggler's posture. Now she straightened, and the bundle shifted to rest against her chest, both arms wrapped around it, the way you carry a child, the way you carry a body home.

"Mr. Vane," she said. "Do you know what I was doing the night your men pulled me out of that tunnel?"

"Beatrix—" Vane began, in a voice of warm warning.

"I was lying under four hundred tons of rock that I had personally certified as safe, listening to a man named Okoro bleed to death six feet away from me, because I falsified the load figures and you paid to make it disappear. You saved my life so that I would owe it to you. That was the design. That was always the design." She lifted the oilskin free of her coat, and every rifle on the beach came up, and she ignored them completely. "For eleven years I thought the debt was the price of surviving. It isn't. The debt is the cage. And you—" she looked at him with something that was not hatred, which frightened Ila more than hatred would have "—you are not my creditor. You're just the lock."

She held the bundle out over the fire pit.

Vane's composure cracked—one hairline fracture, quickly plastered, but Ila saw it. "Think carefully," he said, and the warmth was gone from his voice entirely, and what remained was the arithmetic again, cold and total. "That paper is the only leverage you will ever have against me. Burn it, and you burn your own protection. Burn it, and I have no further reason to be reasonable about anything."

"You're right," Beatrix said. "That's exactly true. It's the only leverage I have." She glanced sideways at Ila, and for the first time since the beach, she smiled—a thin, exhausted, utterly steady smile. "Good thing I'm not the only one holding it."

Ila's heart stopped, started again.

Inside his shirt, against his skin, the second copy lay where it had lain since the flooded galleries: forty pages in oilskin, copied by lamplight through two nights while the others slept, in Beatrix's precise engineer's hand and Ila's careful mission-school script, page for page, name for name. *Anaru Ila, porter, aged 19.* They had never spoken of it. They had never needed to.

Vane saw the understanding move across her face, and then across his own lawyers' faces, and then—slowly, terribly—across his. His eyes went to Ila, and Ila met them, and let him see everything: the grief, the fury, and underneath both, the calm of a man who has already decided.

"You can shoot us," Ila said. "Six rifles, two of us, one sick boy. Very good odds. But the copies are already off this beach—in three places, Mr. Vane, and one of them is a place you'll never find. Kill us and the names get read in a courthouse without anyone alive to contradict them. Let us walk, and maybe, maybe, we negotiate what the courts hear." He shrugged. "You taught the whole coast how this works. Debts get paid. Yours just came due."

The lanterns hissed. The tide breathed against the shingle, in and out, patient as everything else in this country.

Marcus Vane looked at Beatrix Underhill for a long moment—at the woman he had built, brick by brick, out of guilt and gratitude, expecting a servant and finding instead an engineer, which is to say a person who understands exactly where the weaknesses in a structure lie.

Then he smiled, and it was the worst thing Ila had seen all year, worse than the bones, worse than the flood, because it was patient.

"Well," Vane said pleasantly, and nodded to his men, and the rifles lowered by degrees. "Then I suppose we'd better talk about the spring surveys. Miss Underhill—my launch is at your disposal. We have a great deal to discuss, you and I. Starting with the fact that the government magistrate arrives in Wewak on Thursday's steamer."

He turned and walked toward the boat, certain as sunrise, and Beatrix looked at Ila over the smoking, unlit pyre, and whispered:

"Three days."
