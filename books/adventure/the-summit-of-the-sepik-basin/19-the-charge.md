## 19. The Charge

The wind off the bay carried the smell of kerosene and old salt, and Beatrix Underhill stood at the mouth of the cave she had come three hundred miles to find, and said the words that would damn her or free her.

"Burn it," she said. "Tonight. All of it."

Marcus Vane smiled.

She had imagined that smile a hundred times on the long descent—through the whiteout on the shoulder of the range, across the trestles with their rot-eaten decking, down the closed grade her father had surveyed before she was born. She had imagined it in the flooded galleries while the water climbed her ribs, in the dark while Dray's rifle muzzle traced a slow line across their chests, in the moment Ila's hand closed around her wrist beneath the surface and hauled her toward the light. In every imagining, the smile was the same: patient, proprietary, the smile of a man who had already counted what was in your pockets.

Now it was here, on Vane's actual face, creased and sun-weathered and utterly confident, and it was worse than she had imagined, because it was aimed at her like a tool.

"I knew you were sensible," Vane said. He was sixty now, heavier than the man who had hauled her out of Tunnel Nine on the Kandrian line, but the voice hadn't changed—that low, unhurried baritone that had talked a coroner's jury out of a manslaughter verdict and a government out of a hundred miles of mountain rail. "Sensible people survive, Miss Underhill. Sentimental people end up in gravel bars, waiting for someone to mark their bones."

Behind him, on the shingle where the boats had grounded, four of his men stood with the loose-limbed ease of professionals. Two lawyers hovered at the edge of the tide line in city shoes, their document cases clutched like Bibles. A brazier crackled. Someone had thoughtfully brought a kettle.

Ila stood at her left shoulder. He had not spoken since they came down off the last spur and saw the masts of Vane's launches against the dawn. He stood the way he had stood on the cliff shelf in the storm—weight forward, still, reading. She could feel him reading her now, and she did not flinch from it, because there was nothing left in her to hide. He had seen her confess her falsified load tables to a fire full of strangers. He had seen her weep over a drowned porter. He had seen her, in the cave of the forty names, kneel in the dust beside a mass grave and press her palm flat against the stone as if she could take the dead men's pulse.

Whatever she did in the next hour, he would understand it. That was the only mercy left in the world, and it was enough.

"The records first," she said. Her voice came out level. Good. Ten years of hiding had taught her that much: level was everything. "You want them burned, you burn them here, in front of me. And the bullion stays where it is. You get your salvage crews in after we're gone."

Vane laughed softly. "The bullion is a rounding error, Beatrix. I told you that on the mountain. Forty years of interest on a company's founding sin—that's what's in those ledgers. Halvorsen didn't lose his expedition to the weather. My grandfather's company shot forty men in the dark and wrote them down as drowned, and the ledgers prove it, and the ledgers are worth more than the gold, which is why they've been sitting in a hole for sixty years instead of in a courtroom." He spread his hands, a man unveiling a gift. "Burn them, and you walk away rich. Refuse, and I'll have you for the Kandrian collapse—Dray's already signed a statement, haven't you, Dray?"

Dray stood apart from the other men, near the waterline. He had not looked at Vane once since they landed. He was looking at the cave mouth—at the low black arch where the tide sucked and breathed, where the flooded lower galleries ran, where Tomasi had gone down with the records strapped to his chest and come up without them, and without his breath.

"I signed it," Dray said, to the sea.

"Then it's settled." Vane turned back to her, and his voice dropped into the register he must have used on foremen and magistrates for forty years, the register that made obedience feel like reason. "The lawyers have the fire permit, such as it is. The kerosene's already inside—I had men up there an hour before you came down. You'll want to see it done yourself, I expect. Closure. Sentimental people like closure." He smiled again. "Then we'll go to Wewak, and you'll sign the release, and the debt dies, and you can be nobody again. I know how much you love being nobody."

* * *

They let her walk up to the cave mouth alone.

That was the mistake—or it would have been, if she had been the kind of person Vane believed she was. But he had never actually looked at her, not in ten years. He had looked at the thing he owned: the falsified calculations, the buried report, the woman who owed him her liberty the way other people owed money. He had pulled her out of Tunnel Nine because a dead engineer would have brought the inquiry he couldn't afford, and he had kept her ever since the way a man keeps a lever—because it moves weight.

He had never once asked himself what else she knew.

The kerosene had been sloshed through the outer chamber in generous, unthinking quantities. She could smell it from twenty yards, sharp over the cave's mineral breath. Two of Vane's men had already stacked the records in the center of the chamber, on a tarpaulin, neat as a funeral pyre—ledgers, photograph albums, the company's founding correspondence, sixty years of a massacre filed in a dead man's handwriting. Beside the stack, a five-gallon tin stood open, a rag trailing from its mouth.

And beyond the stack, deeper in, past the fallen slab where the upper gallery narrowed, the original company's stores still sat where the Halvorsen party's killers had left them: cases of dynamite, sweating in their waxed paper, sixty years old and, if her father's textbooks were to be believed, more stable for the aging. Nitroglycerin sweats out and crystallizes and grows murderous with time; gelignite merely grows quiet. These were gelignite. She had counted the cases by lamplight two nights ago, in the dark, while Dray slept with his rifle across his knees. Eleven cases, fifty sticks to the case, and a crate of deto—no. The detonators had been ruined by water in the lower galleries. She had checked. She had checked with her hands in the freezing dark, and come up with nothing, and spent the rest of that night staring at the ceiling of the world thinking she had found the one door her own craft could not open.

But that was before the boats. That was before Vane's men came up the mountain with their kerosene and their tarpaulins and their city shoes.

She stopped at the chamber's mouth and looked back down the shingle. Vane stood where she could see him, arms folded, savoring it. Ila stood beside him—no. Not beside him. Three steps away, and angled, and his eyes were on her, and in his hands, wrapped in oilskin and bound with the same cord that had lashed the records to Tomasi's chest, he carried a bundle the size of a hymnal.

They had copied everything. Two nights in the cave, by the light of a single lamp, while Dray stood guard at the entrance and pretended not to see: Ila writing names in a schoolteacher's careful hand, Beatrix pressing the fragile pages flat and reading aloud the columns of figures while Ila's pencil moved. The ledgers stayed in the chamber. The truth came out in oilskin.

Vane thought he was burning the evidence. Vane had never understood that evidence is not paper. Evidence is what a man carries in his chest up a flooded gallery, and what his friend carries the rest of his life.

"Go on," Vane called up. "It's yours to light, Beatrix. I want you to have that."

She turned and stepped into the chamber.

* * *

The inside of the cave was brown twilight and the smell of the sea and, underneath it, the ghost of the dead. She had felt it the first time, and she felt it now: the chamber was not empty. Forty men had been marched in here—or driven, or herded—and the walls remembered. She had learned to read structures the way Ila read country, and this one read like a slaughterhouse: the scorch of torches, the drag marks, the place where the floor dipped toward the lower galleries like a throat.

She knelt at the record stack. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she would have expected them to shake. But there is a kind of calm that comes at the end of a long calculation, when every variable has been weighed and the answer is what it is, and she had been calculating since the snowline, since Dray's radio crackled with Vane's voice and the word *silence* passed through the static like a fish under ice.

The kerosene tin sat open beside the pyre. She lifted it, and instead of pouring it over the ledgers, she walked with it—deeper, past the fallen slab, into the narrowing throat of the upper gallery, where the company's stores lay under a tarp of their own. She set the tin down among the cases. She tipped it. Fifty liters of kerosene went into the cracks between the waxed-paper bricks, soaking into the paper, pooling in the low places, finding the fuse ends of the old cord the original company had left coiled in its crate like something sleeping.

Then she went back to the pyre, and she took the rag, and she stood a moment with her hand in the open air of the chamber where forty men had died unnamed.

"I'm sorry," she said, in English, and then, badly, in the few words of trade language Ila had taught her on the river: *your names are carried. Your names go down the mountain.* It was not a promise she could keep for the dead of this chamber—the ledgers would burn, and the gold would be sealed under a thousand tons of the mountain, and no one would ever dig them out. But Ila's oilskin would carry the names to his village, and his village would carry them to the memorial ground, and they would be read aloud into the air where names belong.

That was the testimony. Not a courthouse. Not her voice, cracked and disgraced, before a magistrate who owed his appointment to men like Vane. This. The mountain itself, closing its hand.

She struck the match on the second try—her hands had finally begun to shake—and touched it to the tarpaulin.

Flame went up the records with a soft rushing sound, and the chamber filled with light for the first time in sixty years, and Beatrix Underhill turned and walked out of the cave at the pace of a woman who has done exactly what she was told.

* * *

"Done," she said, when her boots reached the shingle.

Vane exhaled, long and satisfied, like a man setting down a weight. "Good girl." He gestured, and the lawyers began packing their cases, and one of the hired men started forward to watch the fire do its work. "Now. The release, and then—"

"The fire needs watching," Beatrix said. "Kerosene burns uneven. If it goes out halfway, you've got half-burned ledgers and a very bad morning."

Vane's eyes narrowed, just slightly. "There's a man going up to—"

"It'll be twenty minutes before it catches through the whole stack, and the draft in that chamber is wrong, it pulls back toward the galleries. I'm an engineer, Marcus. Let me watch it. It's the last thing you'll ever need from me."

Something in the phrase—*the last thing*—must have pleased him, because he waved her back up. She went. Behind her she heard him saying something to Ila, low and amused, and she did not turn around, because turning around was the one thing she could not afford. She climbed to the chamber mouth and stood in the smoke-thick light with her back to the sea, and she watched the fire, and she timed it.

That was the whole of her art, in the end. Not the bridges, not the tunnels, not the elegant grades her father had penciled across the highlands. Timing. A charge is nothing but a promise kept at the right instant: the moment when the load is committed, the moment when the rock has nowhere left to be but down. She had falsified load tables once because she had miscalculated, because she had been young and proud and behind schedule, and a tunnel had come down on men who trusted her, and a man named Vane had pulled her out and bought her silence with her own guilt. Every hour since had been a fuse burning toward this chamber.

The fire reached the stack's heart. She saw the flame change color, go from orange to a whiter, hungrier blue at its base, and she felt the draft shift as the heat began to pull air through the cave's throat—and she turned and walked out, and this time she did not walk.

She ran.

She was three strides down the shingle when Vane said, "Beatrix—"

She was ten strides when the first of the kerosene found the fuse cord, and she was at the waterline, seizing Ila's arm, when the upper gallery of the Halvorsen cave took eleven cases of sixty-year-old gelignite and a hundred and fifty liters of kerosene and gave them back to the world.

The sound was not a sound. It was a decision the mountain made. The sea went flat and then leapt. The cliff face above the cave mouth bulged, hung, and came down in a single silent white mass, and then the noise arrived—a crack like the sky splitting along a seam—and the shockwave picked up the shingle and threw it, and threw the boats, and threw four hired men off their feet, and took Marcus Vane's smile and every other expression off his face and left him staring, open-mouthed, at the place where his family's secret had been entombed for sixty years and would now be entombed forever.

The dust came down the beach like a living thing. Beatrix pulled Ila behind the hull of the nearest launch and held on to him and felt the mountain settle above them, groan by groan, ton by ton, the way she had felt a hundred cuts settle in her working years—except that every one of those had been a failure, and this one was not. This one was the best-engineered collapse of her life. She had given the fire twenty minutes to reach the stores, and she had given the mountain no choice at all.

When the dust thinned, the cave mouth was gone. Where the black arch had breathed with the tide there was a raw scar of fractured rock, and below it a fan of rubble fifty yards wide, and the tide was already at work on the edges, patient, indifferent, taking the loose stone back into the bay.

The gold was under that. The ledgers were under that. The grave of forty men was under that, sealed now under a thousand tons of the mountain, and no salvage crew on earth would dig through it, and Marcus Vane's leverage had just ceased to exist.

* * *

The silence afterward was enormous.

Vane stood where the shockwave had staggered him, dust in his hair, staring. One of the lawyers was saying something about—about *costs*, of all things—and then stopped, because Vane raised one hand, and the hand was shaking.

"You," Vane said. He turned, and Beatrix had never seen his face do what it did then: it came apart, the way a face does when forty years of certainty meet one second of arithmetic gone wrong. "You stupid, ungrateful—do you know what you've—"

"Yes," Beatrix said.

She stepped out from behind the boat hull, and Ila stepped with her, and from inside his shirt, dry and safe against his chest, came the small crackle of oilskin pages.

"I know exactly what I've done," she said. "I've destroyed your evidence. All of it. The ledgers, the photographs, the grave—everything you spent sixty years and forty lives keeping buried. You can't prove what was in that cave. And neither can I." She let that land; she watched him reach for it, the first thread of relief. "But Ila can *read* what was in it. From memory. Forty names, Marcus, copied out in his own hand, and the dates, and the company marks, and the payments your grandfather signed. It's not evidence. It's better. It's testimony."

"You'll never be believed," Vane said. "A disgraced engineer and—" he gestured at Ila, and Beatrix saw Ila look at the gesture, and file it away, the way he filed everything. "A tracker with a grudge. Against my word, my lawyers, my—"

"Your word." Beatrix almost laughed. "Marcus, you ordered the last people who found this cave killed. Dray heard you say it over the radio at the snowline. Dray, who signed a statement against me, who will now be delighted to sign a different one, because you just tried to have him buried in a mountain too." She saw Dray, down the shingle, straighten slowly, and say nothing, and the nothing was louder than any agreement. "And there's a magistrate in Wewak who's been waiting ten years for someone to open the Kandrian file, and you're about to hand him the reason it was closed."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. The tide came in around the rubble of the cave mouth, and somewhere in the settling dust, the mountain gave one last small sigh of stone finding stone.

"You'd burn your own life down," he said at last, and it was not a question, and it was almost—almost—admiration. "Just to be free of me."

"No," Beatrix said. "I'd burn it down to be free, full stop. You were just what it cost."

She turned her back on him—turned her back on Marcus Vane, on the launches, on the lawyers and the hired men and the ten years of fear—and she walked up the beach with Ila beside her, and no one moved to stop them, because there was nothing left to stop them for.

Behind them, Vane's voice came once, cracking across the shingle: "You'll lose everything! Your license, your name, everything you have left!"

Beatrix did not turn around.

"I know," she said. "I've seen the load tables. It holds."

* * *

They climbed the coast track as the sun came up properly, gilding the smoke still rising thin from the buried cave, and at the first bend Ila stopped and unwrapped the oilskin and counted the pages with his fingers, the way a man counts the living after a flood. Forty names. Sixty years of a company's arithmetic. His grandfather, third from the top.

"You knew," he said. "In the chamber. When you told Vane you'd burn it."

"I knew the stores were there. I didn't know if the old cord would take the fire." She looked back down at the bay, at the scar on the cliff, at the small figures of Vane's men already arguing on the shingle. "I'm an engineer, Ila. We don't promise outcomes. We promise the math was done honestly."

Ila looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something he had never done: he laughed, once, quietly, and wrapped the oilskin back around its cargo of names.

"Down the mountain, then," he said. "The magistrate in Wewak keeps morning hours, and I want to be in the front row when you give him the Kandrian file—and everything else."

Beatrix Underhill looked down at her own hands—the hands that had falsified the tables, that had lit the match, that had held a drowned man's records—and found they were steady at last.

"Yes," she said. "Let's go tell the truth. All of it. Even my part."

They started down toward the lowlands, and behind them the sea worked patiently at the rubble, and the mountain kept its secret, and the names rode in oilskin toward the light.
