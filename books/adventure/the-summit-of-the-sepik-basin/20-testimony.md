## 20. Testimony

The courthouse in Wewak had been built by the same colonial hands that had built the Halvorsen company—white coral stone sweating in the coastal heat, louvred windows that let in the smell of the harbor along with the flies, a ceiling fan turning with the lazy authority of something that had never once been hurried. Beatrix Underhill sat on the hard bench outside the hearing room and watched the fan turn and thought: *I have waited three years to be this afraid.*

She had dressed in the only clean clothes she owned—the gray blouse she'd worn as a harbor clerk, the skirt that had come through the basin stained at the hem with mud no amount of scrubbing would fully lift. She had considered borrowing better. Ila had offered; the mission sisters at the convent had offered. She had refused them all. If she was going to stand up in front of strangers and confess what she had done, she would do it looking like exactly what she was: a disgraced engineer with nothing left to lose and nothing left to hide.

Her license sat in her breast pocket like a splinter of bone.

*Beatrix M. Underhill, Civil Engineer, Grade One.* The cardstock had gone soft at the corners from years of handling. Her father had framed the original certificate; she'd carried the wallet copy since she was twenty-three years old, through two continents, one collapse, one disgrace, and one long self-imposed exile at the end of the world. It was the last thing she owned that proved she had ever been good at anything.

In sixty hours it would be gone. She found, testing the thought the way you test a loose tooth, that she wasn't afraid of losing it. She was afraid of the moment just before—of having to hold it out in her own hand.

The doors opened. A clerk with a damp forehead beckoned. "Underhill. They're ready for you."

Ila rose from the bench beside her. He had washed and put on a white shirt buttoned to the throat, and without the river mud and the green silence around him he looked younger and older at once—a man standing in two worlds and refusing to pretend he belonged to either. The oilskin packet rode under his arm. Forty names inside, copied out in his careful hand while the mountain burned behind them.

"You don't have to go first," he said quietly. "They'll call me after. You could wait."

"No." She stood. Her knees held. "I've been waiting my whole life to stop waiting."

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than she expected, and hotter. A magistrate named Okoro—newly arrived from the territorial administration, his face still carrying the sea voyage's grayness—sat behind a desk too large for the room. To his right, a table for Vane's people: two lawyers in tropical wool that must have cost more than Beatrix earned in a year, and beside them, empty until now, a chair reserved for Marcus Vane himself.

He came in when she did.

That was deliberate, she understood at once. He wanted her to see him before she saw anyone else—to feel the old gravity, the debt-shaped weight that had lived between them since the tunnel. He wore white linen and an expression of mild, paternal concern, the face he had shown her across a hospital bed six years ago when he'd told her the collapse hadn't been her fault, that the load tables were fine, that accidents happened, that he would take care of everything.

He had taken care of everything. That was the whole horror of it. Every kindness had been a lien.

"Miss Underhill." He inclined his head, courteous as a host. "You look well. The mountains agreed with you."

"Mr. Vane." Her voice came out level. Somewhere below her fear, she registered surprise at that. "You look like a man about to hear some arithmetic."

A flicker crossed his eyes—the first hairline crack in the porcelain—and then the lawyers murmured and everyone sat, and the machinery of the law began to turn with all the speed of the ceiling fan.

Okoro read the charges in a dry voice: unlawful entry into protected lands, destruction of property, assault upon a licensed security contractor, and—the words that made the lawyers sit forward—"possession and dissemination of documents pertaining to the Halvorsen Expedition of 1911, being materials alleged by the petitioner"—a nod toward Vane—"to be fraudulent and defamatory fabrications."

"Fabrications," Ila repeated softly, from his chair. Not a question. Just placing the word on the table where everyone could look at it.

"Mr. Vane contends," said the senior lawyer, a woman named Castellanos with a voice like oiled hinges, "that these so-called ledgers are forgeries produced by persons seeking to extort compensation from the Kandrian Mining and Timber Company. We have retained experts who will demonstrate—"

"Your experts can demonstrate whatever they're paid to demonstrate," Okoro said mildly, not looking up from his papers. "We'll hear the witnesses first. Miss Underhill. You were present at the cave. You fired the charge that sealed it. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And you destroyed property belonging to whom?"

"To Mr. Vane," Beatrix said. "Or to whoever inherits what he stole. The dynamite stores were Halvorsen company stock, cached in 1911. The bullion in the lower chambers likewise. As far as I know, every gram of it was mined by men whose families were never paid, and guarded by men who murdered forty porters to keep the secret." She paused. "So I suppose the honest answer is: it belonged to the dead. And I gave it back."

Castellanos smiled the smile of a woman watching a moth fly toward a lamp. "Miss Underhill, you'll forgive me, but you are hardly a disinterested party. You owed Mr. Vane a substantial debt. You stood to gain its forgiveness by manufacturing evidence against him. Isn't that so?"

"It is so," Beatrix said, "except for the part you got wrong."

"Which part?"

"The evidence wasn't manufactured. And the debt wasn't money." She reached into her breast pocket. "But we'll get there. I'd like to start six years ago, if the magistrate permits. Because if we're going to talk about debts and fabrication, we should begin with mine."

Okoro folded his hands. "Proceed."

* * *

She told it plainly, because she had learned in the basin that plainness was the only voice that carried over distance.

She told them about the Kessel Line extension, the mountain crossing she had engineered at twenty-eight, the youngest grade-one engineer on the project. About the winter schedule and the investors breathing down the company's neck. About the night she had recalculated the load tables for the Number Three tunnel—not because the figures were wrong, but because they were slow, and slowness threatened the opening date, and she had been young enough to believe that confidence was a form of competence.

"I shaved the safety factors," she said. "Fourteen percent. I told myself the rock was sounder than the surveys suggested. I told myself I knew that mountain better than the instruments did." Her hands were flat on the table, steady, and she made herself look at each face in turn. "On the fourth of March, the Number Three tunnel collapsed during a shift change. Eleven men were inside. Six died."

The fan turned. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed over the harbor.

"Marcus Vane was the financier. He arrived at the site within a day. And instead of ordering an inquiry—which would have found my tables, which would have ended my career and very likely seen me charged—he did something worse. He buried it. He declared the collapse an act of geology. He paid the widows generously enough to buy their silence. And he kept my falsified tables in his safe, and he told me, in those words, in the hospital: *I'll take care of everything.*"

She drew a breath.

"For six years, 'everything' has meant him owning me. The debt he called due this season wasn't denominated in currency. It was denominated in obedience. When he sent me into the mountains, it wasn't to retrieve gold. It was to retrieve the last proof of how his empire began—with a massacre in 1911 and a falsified report in my handwriting. He wanted the records burned and the witnesses quiet. That is why there were five of us going in and three coming out, and why the third was his own enforcer."

Vane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, rueful, pitched perfectly for the room. "Miss Underhill is a gifted storyteller. Ask her sometime about the maps she carries—her father's maps of closed lines, drawn by a man who spent his career falsifying—"

"My father never falsified anything in his life," Beatrix said, and the steel in her voice surprised even her. "And you know it, because you tried to hire him in 1909 and he refused you. He said you smelled like a man who'd sell the rivets out of a bridge with passengers on it. He was right. He usually was."

Okoro raised a hand before Vane could answer. "Mr. Vane will have his opportunity under oath. Continue, Miss Underhill."

* * *

She continued. She gave them the basin in words: the green silence, the rail grades swallowed by jungle, the gravel bar scattered with small bones that Dray had wanted to walk past and Ila had refused to leave unmarked. She gave them the storm on the shoulder, the supplies burning in the night, the moment she had understood they were prisoners. She gave them the confession at the fire—hers, the second one, the one that had cost her more than any courtroom ever could—and she watched the lawyers' faces as she described how Dray had radioed down from the snowline and learned there was no forgiveness waiting, only a helicopter and a list of people who weren't meant to survive it.

At that point Castellanos interrupted, smooth as poured cream: "Magistrate, this is hearsay piled on invention. We have no corroboration for any of—"

"You have this," Beatrix said, and nodded to Ila.

He rose and brought the oilskin packet to the magistrate's desk, and unwrapped it the way a priest unwraps a relic, and began, without hurry, to read.

"*Halvorsen Expedition, payroll ledger, final entry, October 1911.*" His voice filled the hot little room the way it had filled the caves—low, unhurried, absolutely certain. "*Forty porters engaged at Wewak, wages advanced against return. Signed: E. Halvorsen.*" He turned a page. "*Company memorandum, marked confidential. Re: disposal of native personnel. The gold does not move until the story cannot.*"

He read the photographs' descriptions—the mass gallery, the rows of men laid down with their belongings arranged above them like offerings. He read the ammunition requisitions. He read, in a voice that did not break though the room had gone very still, the name at the bottom of the porter roll, entry thirty-one:

"*Tambe, son of Anu, of the middle river villages.*"

He closed the ledger and set his palm flat upon it, and looked at Marcus Vane across the width of the room.

"My grandfather," Ila said. "My mother's father. My mother never knew what became of him. She died not knowing. I walked into a mountain so that my daughter will not die not knowing. These are not fabrications, magistrate. Fabrications do not smell of the grave."

No one spoke. The fan turned. Vane's face had not moved, but Beatrix—who had studied that face across six years of small cruelties—saw the thing underneath it now, the way she had once seen the flaw beneath sound-looking rock: a fine tremor at the corner of his jaw, the micro-settlement of a structure beginning to find its failure plane.

It was Castellanos who recovered first, reaching for the ledger. "The court should note these documents are unprovenanced—"

"They are provenanced," Okoro said quietly. "By forty families in the middle river villages who have been missing grandfathers for fifty years. I have already sent word." He looked at her over his spectacles. "Madam lawyer, your client may wish to consider his position before this hearing becomes something larger than either of us."

* * *

They broke at noon. Beatrix stood in the corridor with her back against the coral stone, letting the heat press on her like a hand, and Vane came to her alone.

The lawyers had been dismissed to draft responses. The corridor was empty except for the two of them and the distant sound of the harbor. Up close, the porcelain was visibly cracked now; she could see the effort it took him to hold the paternal warmth in place.

"Beatrix." Just her name, soft, intimate, the voice from the hospital bed. "Think about what you're doing. You testify fully, you surrender that license, and what are you? Nothing. A clerk again. Less than a clerk. I built half this coast—you know that. By next month this hearing will be a footnote and you'll be a madwoman who blew up a cave. Whereas if the record were to become... simpler. If certain pages were to go missing. I can make you whole. I can make you *more* than whole. A line of your own. The highlands survey. Everything your father wanted for you."

She looked at him for a long moment. What she felt was not hatred, and that surprised her most of all. It was pity—the clean, cold pity of an engineer reading a fracture map.

"Do you know what I did last night?" she asked.

His eyes narrowed a fraction.

"I took out my father's map. The closed lines. I spread it on the convent table and I read every grade, every trestle, every tunnel he surveyed and never got to build. And I realized that in six years, you never once asked me about the work itself. Not one question about loading, about drainage, about the rock. You bought my skill the way you bought the widows' silence—as inventory." She pushed off the wall. "You're offering me the highlands survey as if it were a gift. Marcus, it's a cage with a view. I've lived in it for six years. I'm not moving back in."

"Then you destroy yourself," he said, and the warmth finally fell away, and what was underneath was small and cold and old. "You understand that. Everything you are goes into that room this afternoon and doesn't come out."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "That's rather the point."

She walked away down the corridor, and did not look back, and heard nothing behind her—which was, she understood, the sound of an empire discovering that its foundation had been inspected.

* * *

The afternoon session lasted four hours.

She surrendered the license herself. That had been the hardest hour, harder than the confession of the falsified tables, harder than describing the bones in the river gravel. Okoro asked her formally whether she understood that admission of the 1919 falsification constituted grounds for permanent revocation of her certification, and she said yes, and then she took the soft-cornered card out of her breast pocket and laid it on the desk in front of the whole room, and slid it across.

"Grade One," she said. "Earned honestly, kept dishonestly. Take it."

The scratch of the clerk's pen recording the surrender was the loudest sound in the world.

Then the afternoon turned, and turned against Vane. The magistrate had not been idle over lunch. A telegram lay on his desk from the territorial capital; another from the mission at the middle river; a third, Beatrix noticed with a strange floating sensation, bearing the letterhead of the Kandrian company's own board of directors, requesting copies of *all* testimony regarding "certain historical irregularities." Castellanos read it twice and went gray. Empires, Beatrix thought, watching the lawyer whisper urgently into Vane's ear, do not crack all at once. They crack the way tunnels do—one creak, one dusting of grit, and then the whole mountain deciding.

By the time the session adjourned, Vane's chair stood empty. He had left during the reading of the board's telegram, unhurried, escorted by neither lawyer nor enforcer, a man departing a building he no longer controlled. Dray, she learned later, had given his own statement that morning in a separate room—flat, complete, merciless in its precision—and had then walked to the police post and asked to be locked up, and been obliged.

Outside, the light was going amber over the harbor. Ila waited on the courthouse steps, the oilskin packet gone from under his arm—it lived now in the magistrate's safe, under seal, awaiting the investigators from the capital—and without it his arms hung strangely, like a man who has set down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten his own shape without it.

"Well," he said.

"Well," she said.

They stood together on the steps while the town flowed past them—clerks, fishermen, children, the ordinary traffic of a world that did not yet know what had shifted beneath it today and would learn soon enough, when the telegrams finished arriving and the newspapers finished chewing.

"You lost your paper," Ila said, meaning the license. He said it gently, the way he said everything that mattered.

"I did."

"What will you do now? A woman with no trade, no money, no name worth anything in this territory." He was almost smiling. "You could always be a guide. I know someone who could teach you to read rivers."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, the first in longer than she could measure. Then it faded into something quieter, and she looked out at the water, at the bay where three years ago a tide had drained away and shown her a cave mouth and cracked open the careful invisible life she had built over her own grave.

"I don't know what I'll do," she said. "For the first time since I was twenty-eight years old, I genuinely don't know. And it feels—" She searched for the word and found it, bitter and cleansing both. "*Clean.* Like air after a blast clears. Everything's rubble, and I'm standing in it, and it's mine."

Ila nodded slowly. Then he touched her elbow, light as a falling leaf, and turned her toward the street, where a boy was running up through the amber light with a telegram flapping in his hand, calling her name—her real name, spoken aloud in public for the first time in three years, and not in accusation.

"Miss Underhill! Miss Underhill—from the capital! They say come quick—they say it's about the highlands!"

Beatrix Underhill stood in the ruins of everything she had spent six years protecting, and felt the strangest thing stir in her chest, and recognized it, at last, as the future.
