## 21. Forty Names Read Aloud

The village of Ambunti had no word for *funeral* that fit what they were doing. The old men had argued about it for three nights, Ila heard, sitting in the smoke of the men's house, and in the end they had chosen a word older than any of them: they called it a *calling home*. Not a burial—the dead had been buried badly, or not at all, forty years and more along the gravel bars and the flooded galleries of the mountain—and not a mourning either, because you could not mourn properly men whose names had never been spoken aloud since the day they died. What you could do, the elders decided, was call them. Call them by name, one by one, until each one knew the way back.

So on the morning of the ceremony the whole village turned out, and half the river besides. Canoes came up from Pagwi and down from Timbunke, loaded with relatives who had spent their lives believing their grandfathers and great-uncles had simply walked into the mountains one year and never come back—lost to sickness, lost to spirits, lost to shame, because everyone knew a man who abandoned his family was better off forgotten. Forty families' worth of forgetting, Ila thought, standing at the edge of the cleared ground where the posts had been raised. Forty families who would learn before sundown that their men had not abandoned anyone. That they had been taken. That they had called for help from inside a mountain while men with ledgers wrote down the dates of their deaths as line items.

The oilskin packet lay in his hands. It was lighter than it should have been. He had carried it down through the whiteout and across the drowned galleries and over the rotted trestles of his grandfather's last road, and all that weight had somehow left it unchanged—a kilo of paper, water-stained at the corners, smelling faintly of kerosene and cave air. Inside were the ledgers. The photographs. And folded separately, wrapped twice in barkcloth, the list.

Ila had copied the list three times since coming down from the snowline. Once in pencil, in the mission schoolhouse at Ambunti, his hand shaking so badly the teacher's widow had put her own hand over his until it steadied. Once in ink, carefully, checking each spelling against the ledger entries. And once more, two nights ago, when he had dreamed of his grandfather standing at the mouth of a tunnel with his back to him, waiting, and woken knowing he had gotten one name wrong—*Yawan*, not *Yawen*—and could not sleep until he corrected it.

Forty names. He had read them so many times now that they lived behind his eyes like the river lived there, always moving, always present. But reading them alone in a schoolhouse was nothing. Today he would say them into open air, in front of the whole basin, and the air would carry them up the river and over the ranges to wherever the dead waited to hear themselves spoken.

The morning was bright after the storm-weeks, the sky scoured clean, the Sepik running high and brown and fast below the village green. Someone had built a platform of split sago palm. Someone else had hung the photographs—the few that survived—from a line strung between two posts, weighted with stones so the wind wouldn't take them. Faces in faded sepia: young men in company shirts, some grinning, some solemn, some looking past the camera toward something the photographer couldn't see. Ila's grandfather was among them. Third from the left in the group photograph taken at Angoram before departure, holding a coil of rope over one shoulder like it weighed nothing, wearing an expression Ila recognized from his own face in still water: the look of a man who believes the world is about to make sense.

He had been twenty-three. Younger than Ila was now.

Old women moved through the crowd laying out food—not for the living, though the living would eat it later, but set out first on banana leaves for the returning ones. Taro. Fish smoked over coconut husk. Betel nut wrapped in pepper leaf. A full meal, laid for forty men, most of whom had been boys when they ate their last one.

Ila stood apart, as he had stood apart for weeks, holding the packet against his chest. He had thought he would feel ready today. He did not feel ready. He felt the way he had felt at the cave mouth the first time, watching the tide drain out of Wewak harbor and expose that black opening in the cliff—the feeling that something enormous was about to be asked of him, and that refusing was not among the choices.

Then he saw her.

She stood at the very edge of the gathering, where the cleared ground gave way to kunai grass and the path down to the canoes. Beatrix Underhill. She had come upriver two days ago on a government launch, riding deck with the cargo, and had slept in the mission rest-house without telling anyone she'd arrived. Now she stood in a dress too plain for the occasion and too formal for the heat, her hands clasped in front of her like a woman at a graveside—which, Ila supposed, was exactly what she was. No one had invited her. No one had told her not to come. She had simply appeared at the edge of things, the way she had appeared at the edge of his life on the dock at Wewak, catching him tracing a map of closed lines, and offered exactly what was needed and nothing more.

Her face was thinner than it had been in the mountains. The trial had done that—he had sat in the back of the lowlands courthouse for three days of her testimony and watched her dismantle herself piece by piece in front of strangers: the falsified load calculations, the collapse, the men hurt in the tunnel she had signed off on, the years of Vane's quiet blackmail dressed up as debt. She had surrendered her engineer's license with her own hands, laid it on the clerk's table like a woman setting down something heavy she had carried too far. Vane's empire was cracking around her testimony; the newspapers had begun using words like *massacre* and *cover-up* in the same sentence as the company's name. None of it had made her face easier to look at. There was a kind of ruin that looked like relief, and she wore it.

Their eyes met across the crowd. She started to turn away—to give him the privacy of his own grief, which was the kind of courtesy she understood best—but Ila lifted his chin slightly, the smallest gesture, and she stopped. She did not come closer. She did not leave. She stayed at the edge, uninvited but not turned away, and that was right. That was exactly right. Some debts you pay by staying where you are.

An elder touched his elbow. It was time.

* * *

They had given him the platform because he was the one who went up the mountain, but also, he suspected, because he was the one who came back changed in a way the elders could read. Old Man Kombra said it plainly the night before: *You carried their names in your body. Your voice knows the road now.* So Ila climbed the steps with the oilskin packet and unwrapped the barkcloth, and the crowd went quiet in a way he had never heard a crowd go quiet—not silence as absence of sound, but silence as attention, four hundred people breathing together, waiting.

He began with the ledger numbers, because the truth required them. "In the year 1911," he said, in the trade language first, then again in his mother tongue, "the Halvorsen expedition engaged forty carriers to move stores from the coast into the highlands. The company recorded wages paid. The company recorded rations issued. In the month of the big rains, the company recorded—" and here his voice caught, and he let it catch, because hiding it would have been its own kind of lie— "the company recorded that forty carriers had been *written off*. Written off, the way you write off a canoe broken on a bar. The way you write off a tool worn past use."

He held up the ledger page so the front rows could see it. The photographs of the pages had already been in the newspapers, but seeing the actual paper, the actual ink, the actual handwriting of some clerk who had entered forty human beings under *losses, miscellaneous*—that landed differently. He heard an old woman begin to keen, low and rhythmic, and others took it up, and for a moment the sound filled the whole green like wind filling a sail.

Then the keening settled, and Ila unfolded the list.

"Kombra, son of Waima." His own clan name, shared with the elder beside him, and Kombra bowed his head as if struck. "Of Timbunke. Carrier. Twenty-six years old."

A man near the front cried out once, sharply, and was held by his brothers.

"Wanpis, son of Yatmel. Of Pagwi. Carrier. Thirty-one years old."

Name by name. Village by village. Age by age. Ila read slowly, giving each name time to travel, and as he read, people rose from the crowd—one here, one there, sometimes three or four from the same cluster of relatives—and walked forward to touch the photographs hanging on the line, or to lay a leaf of betel on the food set out for the dead, or simply to stand facing the mountain, visible far to the south this clear morning, blue and white-capped and indifferent. For every name, someone stood. By the twentieth name, the edge of the green was ringed with standing figures. By the thirtieth, the standing ones outnumbered the seated.

"Yawan, son of Sumbukapi." His voice cracked on the corrected spelling, the name he had rewritten at two in the morning by lamplight. "Of Ambunti. Head carrier. Twenty-three years old."

That was his grandfather. That was the man third from the left with the coil of rope. Ila had promised himself he would not weep until the list was finished, and he kept the promise barely, his vision swimming, the sepia faces blurring into the living ones until he could not have said which was which—and then he understood that this was the point, that this was the whole design of the morning: the dead and the living standing together at the edge of the same green, indistinguishable, one community again.

"Marum, son of Kaupa. Of Timbunke. Carrier. Nineteen years old."

Nineteen. Younger than Ila. A boy sent up a mountain to carry another man's fortune and written off in a ledger column for his trouble.

"And the fortieth name," Ila said, and paused, because the fortieth name was the one the ledgers had almost erased entirely—entered late, in different ink, squeezed beneath the line as if the clerk had been ashamed even of his own record-keeping. "Tomas Halvorsen. Expedition leader. Who refused to leave his carriers when the company ordered it, and who died with them, and whose family in the south has spent forty years believing he deserted them." He looked out at the crowd. "We will send word to the Halvorsens. Their man did not desert anyone. He stayed."

The keening rose again, and this time Ila let himself join it, one voice among hundreds, and the sound went up off the green and over the brown flood of the river and south toward the mountain where the cave mouth now lay buried under ten thousand tons of collapsed limestone—buried by his own hand and Beatrix's, by her timing and his fuse, sealing the gold and the grave together forever. People had asked him whether he regretted it, the destruction. Whether the gold should have come down to buy boats and roofs and school fees. The elders had answered for him, once, in the men's house: *Some things are paid for with blood, and if you spend them, the blood comes due again.* The mountain had held the dead long enough. Let it hold them now with their names known, their story told, their murderers ruined in the courts. That was wealth enough. That was the only inheritance worth carrying down.

When the last echo faded, the elders came forward one by one and broke the ground at the foot of the posts, planting forty stakes of black palm, one for each name, each stake carved overnight with its name in the new mission orthography and the old signs both. By midday the green looked like a forest beginning—forty young trees waiting to be planted where the stakes stood, one for each man, so that in fifty years there would be a grove where children played without knowing why the trees were sacred, only that they were.

* * *

The crowd thinned toward evening as the feasting began, and Ila found her where he knew he would find her: at the edge again, at the boundary between the firelight and the dark, eating nothing, watching the dancing with the expression of a woman auditing her own right to be present.

"You didn't eat," Ila said, coming to stand beside her.

"I ate on the launch."

"That's not what I asked."

Beatrix almost smiled. In the firelight her face looked older and younger at once, the way stone looks after rain. "It didn't seem right. Taking food laid out for—"

"The food for the dead was eaten by the dead. This is the food for the living. Different leaves." He handed her a skewer of smoked fish without asking, and after a moment she took it, because Beatrix Underhill had learned in the mountains that some refusals cost more than acceptances. They stood together in silence while the drums worked through their patterns and young men stamped the old steps their grandfathers had died too soon to teach them.

"I keep thinking about the nineteen-year-old," she said finally. "Marum. I did the arithmetic during your reading. When my tunnel collapsed, the men I hurt—they all lived. I've been carrying that like a shield. *No one died.* And then I stand here and listen to forty names, and I think: the only difference between me and the men who wrote that ledger is that I got caught early, and they got caught late." She turned the fish skewer in her fingers without eating it. "Your grandfather's company and my railroad. Same arithmetic. Men on one side of the equals sign, money on the other."

"No," Ila said.

She looked at him.

"You falsified a calculation and men were hurt, and you lied about it for years, and you testified anyway, knowing it would cost you everything. They falsified a ledger and killed forty men and hid it for forty years, and would have hidden it forever." He shook his head. "You spent the whole climb telling me you were not a good person. You were right. Good people don't do what you did. But good people also don't do what you did on the mountain. Both things are true. My grandfather taught my mother that a man is not one thing. Neither is a woman."

Beatrix looked out at the dancers for a long moment. "Vane's lawyers served papers on me at the courthouse steps. Last day of testimony. Wrongful destruction of property—the bullion. They're claiming the gold was lawfully held assets." Her laugh had no humor in it. "Lawfully held. He's going to fight for years. He may even win the claim. And I have nothing left to defend myself with except—" she gestured vaguely at the oilskin packet under Ila's arm— "except that."

"The copies are safe. Copied again, three times, in three places. One set goes to the Halvorsen family. One stays in Ambunti. One goes to the archives in Port Moresby." He studied her profile, the firelight moving on it. "You're afraid."

"I'm always afraid. It's the only skill I've ever fully mastered."

"Not that kind." Ila turned to face her fully. "You're afraid there's nothing left of you after the losing. License gone. Money gone. Name ruined in the lowlands papers. You're standing there wondering what a person is when you take away all the things she used to be." He nodded slowly, as if confirming something to himself. "I wondered the same thing on the mountain. After the porter fell. After Dray burned the supplies. I thought: whatever I was when I started this—tracker, guide, grandson—that man is used up. And then I found out the thing underneath doesn't get used up. It just gets quieter. And it's the truest part."

From inside his shirt he drew something he had been carrying since the courthouse, wrapped in the same barkcloth that had wrapped the names. He unwrapped it and held it out on his open palm.

The compass. Brass, green with age, the glass cracked in one corner, the needle swinging lazily and settling north with the stubborn patience of a thing built to outlast its owners. His grandfather's compass—the one the family said had gone up the mountain in 1911 and never come down. Except it had come down. Found in the lower gallery, wedged in the mud beside the records chest, its needle still faithful after forty years in the dark. Ila had cleaned it, oiled the pivot with a drop of cooking fat, and carried it against his chest through the trial days without quite knowing why.

"It pointed the way up for him," Ila said. "And it failed him, or the world failed him—I don't know which. But it came back. Things that go into the dark and come back are worth keeping." He pressed it into her hand and closed her fingers over it before she could refuse. "You're going to need a direction. People who lose everything always do. Take the one that came back from worse than you have."

"Ila, I can't—"

"You can. It's not a gift for you. It's a gift for him." He nodded toward the forty stakes standing black against the firelight, the grove-to-be. "He wanted to build a road into the mountains that would last. Instead he got a grave. But roads aren't the only thing that lasts. Names last. Truth lasts. A compass that comes back out of the dark lasts." He stepped back. "Carry it. That's all. Carry it north, south, wherever you go next. Every place it points will be a place he never got to see. That's how he travels now."

Beatrix stood holding the compass, and Ila watched something move through her face that he had seen once before—at the tree line, when Dray handed over his radio and asked to be left behind as penance, and mercy arrived from the place least expected and turned out to be the heaviest thing a person could be handed. She looked down at the cracked glass, at the needle finding its bearing, and her jaw tightened the way it did when she was determined not to be grateful, and then loosened, and she said, quietly, in the trade language, pronouncing the words carefully like a woman handling unfamiliar tools:

"Thank you" — and then, after a pause, getting it almost right — "*tenkyu tru*."

Behind them the drums changed pattern, and the elders began calling the young men to plant the first of the forty trees by torchlight, and somewhere down at the landing a boat engine coughed and started—the government launch, preparing to run the night current back downriver. Ila heard footsteps on the path, and turned to see the mission teacher's widow hurrying toward them with a lantern, a letter in her hand, her face lit with the particular excitement of a bearer of news.

"There's a launch letter come for the gavia woman," she said, breathless, holding it out. "From the government. Marked urgent. The survey office." She glanced between them, curiosity winning over manners. "They're asking after engineers. Someone down there says the mountains want new lines."

Beatrix went very still. The compass needle trembled in her grip, swung, and settled—north, steady, faithful, pointing toward the high country where the old grades slept under the jungle and the new ones waited to be dreamed.

She looked at the letter. She looked at the mountain.
