## 22. Open Lines

The frost came early to the high grades that year, and Beatrix Underhill learned its schedule the way she had once learned the schedules of tides and timetables—by standing in it before dawn with her hands bare, letting it tell her what it knew.

She was on the Kandari spur, six hundred meters above the last road, driving spikes into rail that would carry ore down from a copper seam the government had only just agreed to open. The work was honest in a way that still surprised her. Nobody here knew her name. The foreman called her *Underhill* because that was what was written on her contract, and the other laborers called her nothing at all, which suited her fine. She was simply the woman who could read a grade at a glance, who could look at a cut slope and say where the water would go in the wet season, who never once rounded a number to make a ledger look kinder than it was.

Her hands had healed crooked. She noticed it most mornings when she wrapped them—the left index finger that no longer fully straightened, a souvenir of the cave, of the drainage gallery where she had felt the water rising around her ankles and understood, with a clarity she had never managed in any courtroom, exactly what she had been built for. Not ledgers. Not lies. Lines. Water lines, grade lines, the long patient arithmetic of getting from one place to another without killing anyone.

She drove the spike home. The ring of iron went out over the valley and came back changed.

* * *

The camp sat on a shelf of ground the old colonial engineers had leveled eighty years ago and then abandoned, as they had abandoned everything in these mountains that asked for more than a season's patience. Beatrix had chosen it herself, walking the old survey pegs with a crew of twelve, finding the one place where the shelf was wide enough for tents and the drainage fell away clean on both sides. It had felt like a conversation with dead men whose work she respected even as she despised what they had done with it. They had known how to build. They had not cared whom they buried under the building.

That distinction, she had come to understand, was the whole of everything.

At midday the mail runner came up from the valley—a lean boy of maybe seventeen who made the climb twice a month with a canvas satchel and an expression of profound indifference to whatever news he carried. He handed Beatrix two items: a newspaper folded into quarters, three weeks old, and a letter with a government crest pressed into the wax.

She set the letter on her knee and read the paper first. Old habit. Bad news first, so the good news has somewhere solid to land.

The front page carried Vane's face, or what remained of it in the cheap halftone—still handsome, still silver-templed, but smaller now, cornered. *VANE CONSORTIUM SHARES SUSPENDED PENDING INQUIRY*, said the headline. Below it, a second story: the mining leases in the Sepik headwaters revoked. A third column mentioned, almost as an afterthought, that testimony in the lowlands proceedings had entered its fourth week. Her own name appeared once, in the seventh paragraph, spelled correctly, which surprised her. *Beatrix Underhill, formerly chief engineer of the Kavieng–Torricelli line, testified that falsified load calculations prepared under her supervision in 1919 contributed to the collapse that killed three men.*

Three men. Tomas Reyes. Anselm Kwei. And the third, the young one whose name she still could not say aloud without her throat closing—Peter Halloran, nineteen years old, three weeks into his first job underground. She had spent eleven years keeping their names off her tongue. Now they were in a public record, in print, permanent, and the strangest part was how light she felt carrying them there. Grief did not get lighter when you finally set it down. But it stopped being something you hid, and hidden things are always heavier than held things.

Vane would stand trial in the spring. Dray's confession—written out in a careful clerk's hand from the radio transcript Ila had carried down the mountain—had done what forty years of rumor never could. The Halvorsen massacre had a date now, a place, a body count, and a family name attached to the order that caused it. The gold itself was gone, sealed under four hundred tons of rock that Beatrix herself had brought down with a fuse timed to the second. Some nights she dreamed of the blast—not the fire but the sound, the deep throat-clearing of the mountain swallowing its own secret—and woke with her heart hammering and her hands steady, which told her something about what kind of woman the years had made her.

She folded the paper away and opened the letter.

* * *

It was from the Department of Works, signed by a director whose name she did not recognize, written in the stiff officialese of a government still learning what it owed its own interior. The gist took three paragraphs to arrive:

The highlands surveys were proceeding. Three candidate corridors had been identified for new rail construction into the basin country. Two of the survey teams had already lost men to the rivers. The third team's senior engineer had resigned by telegram, citing weather. The Department required someone who could read closed lines—old grades, washed-out trestles, the forgotten engineering of the colonial era—and judge honestly whether they could be rebuilt or must be abandoned. Someone, the letter said, *with demonstrated experience in difficult terrain and, we will be candid, a tolerance for controversy.*

They knew who she was. Of course they knew. The trial had seen to that. This was not an offer extended in ignorance; it was an offer extended *because*—because the woman who had blown up a fortune to publish a massacre was also, whatever else might be said of her, the finest route engineer in the territory, and governments in a hurry forgive much in exchange for competence.

The salary was modest. The term was eighteen months. The signature block included a line that made her laugh aloud, alone in her tent at four thousand feet: *Applicants should note that this appointment carries no license privileges and no immunity from prior findings.* They wanted her honesty, and they intended to keep her paying for her dishonesty, both at once, forever. It was the most reasonable document she had ever received from an institution.

She read it twice. Then she walked out of the tent into the blue afternoon and stood looking west, where the range climbed toward the passes she had crossed in whiteout with two companions and a satchel of oilskin-wrapped truth, and she let herself feel the thing she had been refusing to feel since the courthouse: that she wanted this. Not redemption—she had stopped believing in that word somewhere around the fourth week of testimony, when a widow in the gallery had turned away from her and she had understood that some accounts are never settled, only acknowledged. What she wanted was simpler and harder. She wanted to build something that would still be standing, and standing honestly, after everyone who remembered her name was gone.

* * *

The letter stayed in her breast pocket for nine days while she worked, which was its own kind of answer, though she made herself wait anyway. Waiting was a discipline she had learned late and paid for dearly. In the evenings the crew gathered around the cook fire and argued about football and the price of betel nut, and Beatrix sat among them mending gloves, anonymous, debt-free, and discovered that anonymity chosen is a very different garment from anonymity worn as camouflage. The first had fit like a shroud. This one fit like a coat.

On the ninth evening, a figure came up the spur trail in the last light, and Beatrix knew the walk before she could make out the face—that unhurried rolling gait, weight kept low, the walk of a man who had spent his life reading ground and trusting his feet to negotiate with it.

"Ila," she said.

"Underhill." He grinned, and the grin was the same as ever, quick and crooked, though there was gray in his beard now that had not been there a year ago. He dropped his pack by the fire without asking permission, because he never had and never would. "Your line is crooked."

"It's not crooked. It's following the contour."

"It's crooked." He crouched, studied the rails disappearing into the dusk, and shrugged. "But it will hold. That's the important thing." He looked at her then, and his eyes went briefly to her breast pocket, where the letter's corner showed above her flannel shirt, and she realized he had known about it before he came. News traveled the highlands faster than mail. "You haven't answered it."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're still here, and you'd be gone if you'd said yes, and you'd be drunk if you'd said no." He fed a stick into the fire. "So you're deciding. Good. Deciding slowly is the first honest thing engineers learn."

She laughed despite herself. Around them the camp noises softened—men turning in, the cook banking his coals—and the mountain sounds rose to take their place: wind in the casuarinas, water running somewhere below in the dark, the vast indifferent breathing of a landscape that had swallowed better expeditions than hers and would swallow worse ones yet.

"They want me to survey the highland corridors," she said. "New lines. Into the basin country."

"I know. My village is in the basin country." He said it without accusation, which was its own message. The memorial had been eight months ago now—forty names read aloud at dawn while the river ran brown with the season's first rains, and Beatrix standing at the edge of the gathering, uninvited, unturned-away, holding the weight of every eye that chose to look at her. Ila's grandfather's compass hung at her neck these days instead of in her pocket. She had stopped being able to tell whether she carried it as penance or inheritance, and had decided the question didn't matter. "The elders have talked about it. A rail line means the company men come back. It also means medicine comes up the valley in hours instead of days. We are not fools. We can hold two truths."

"That's more than I can do sometimes."

"You did it in the cave." He poked the fire until it flared. "You held the truth and the dynamite in the same two hands. Nobody else there could have done both."

"The porter drowned doing it."

"Tomaso chose. That's different." Tomaso—the young porter who had gone under in the flooded gallery with the oilskin satchel strapped to his chest, who had surfaced once, twice, and then not, and whose grip on the records had not loosened even in the water. His name was on the memorial wall now too, added in fresh paint beneath the forty, the first name of a new list that Beatrix prayed would stay short. "His mother sends greetings, by the way. She says you owe her a visit, and she is right, and you have been a coward about it for two seasons."

"I've been busy."

"You've been hiding. It's different, and you know it's different, because hiding is the thing you swore off in a courtroom in front of God and the lowlands press." He said it gently, the way he said everything that cut. Then he reached into his shirt and drew out a folded paper of his own, worn soft at the creases, and passed it across the fire. "And since we are speaking of letters—this came to my village addressed to *the tracker who guided the Underhill expedition*. The government is thorough, if slow. They ask if I would serve as guide and liaison for the highland surveys. They ask if I know anyone qualified to lead them."

Beatrix looked at the paper without taking it. "Ila—"

"They ask," he continued, merciless, "if I know a former railroad engineer, disgraced, difficult, honest since approximately one year ago, who might be persuaded to trade her shovel for a theodolite." He smiled. "I have not answered either. I thought we might decide together. It worked well enough the last time, considering."

"Considering we got five people out of seven alive and blew up a mountain."

"Considering," he agreed, "that the dead have their names back, and a thief is in prison, and you are driving spikes for wages instead of drowning in a debt, and I am sitting at your fire eating your rice, which is terrible, by the way, you have not improved." He leaned forward, firelight moving on his face. "Beatrix. The snows will come early again this year. They always do now, or so the old people say. There is a corridor through the range that my grandfather's generation used before the companies closed it—a good line, stone-cut, sound, needing perhaps two years' work to open. I have walked it. You have read it on your father's map. If the government opens those mountains, I would rather it be done by people who remember what the last ones buried there."

She was silent a long moment. Above them the stars came out in the hard cold way they did at altitude, whole constellations wheeling up from behind the western peaks, and somewhere far below a night bird called across the valley and got no answer.

"My father drew that map," she said at last. "He surveyed half those grades before the war. He used to say the mountains weren't against us—they were just waiting to see if we meant it." She touched the compass at her neck, felt the brass cold through her shirt. "I spent eleven years proving I didn't mean anything. I'd like to spend the next eighteen months meaning it."

"So you'll write to them?"

"I'll write tonight." She pulled the letter from her pocket, unfolded it flat on her knee, and found the acceptance line near the bottom—*I, the undersigned, accept appointment upon the terms stated*—and beneath it the blank for her signature. Her pen was in her kit. Her hand, when she took up the pen, was perfectly steady, and she noted that too, the way she noted grades and waterlines and the load-bearing capacity of old trestles: as fact, as data, as the plain truth of a structure tested past its failure point and found, against all expectation, sound.

She signed. The scratch of the nib was very loud in the quiet camp.

Ila watched her fold the letter, seal it, and set it atop her pack for the morning runner. Then he lay back against his pack with his hands behind his head, entirely at home, and looked up at the wheeling sky.

"There's one condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When the line reaches my valley—and it will, within five years, the copper will see to that—I want the first train to stop. One minute. Engine whistle, all hands standing, facing east toward the cave country." He turned his head to look at her. "Not for the dead. For the living. So nobody who rides that line ever forgets what it cost to open it honestly."

Beatrix looked at him—at the gray in his beard, the firelight, the patient certainty of a man who had bargained with grief and won terms he could live with—and she found that her eyes were wet, and let them be, because there was no one here to hide from and no reason left to try.

"One minute," she said. "I'll put it in the survey specifications myself. Clause one."

"Clause one," he agreed. "Everything else is clause two."

The fire burned down to coals. Somewhere below, the river they were bridging ran on through the dark toward the coast, carrying meltwater and starlight and, in time, if they did their work honestly, the weight of trains. Beatrix sat a while longer after Ila slept, the signed letter safe against her knee, and watched the eastern peaks where the passes waited—closed lines, open country, and ahead of her, for the first time in her life, a road she had chosen with her eyes wide and her debts paid and nothing in her pockets worth hiding.

Then she banked the fire, wrapped herself in her blanket, and slept without dreaming of tunnels, and woke before dawn to frost on the rails, and went out bare-handed to meet it, listening to what it knew.
