## 1. The Town That Forgets on Purpose

Kessik sat in the fold of two valleys like a coin dropped between couch cushions—lost by everyone who mattered, and therefore safe.

August Iversen had learned the town's grammar over three years: which mornings the bakery's chimney smoked before dawn, which afternoons the river ran green with glacier flour and which ones it ran gray and mean, which of the regulars at the Ferryman's Rest would talk to him and which would only nod, their nods carrying the particular weight of men who knew a story about you but had agreed, collectively, to let it lie. That was Kessik's whole genius. It forgot on purpose. Not out of kindness—Kessik was not a kind town—but out of a kind of civic laziness that amounted to the same thing. Nobody here wanted to know your past because knowing it might obligate them to do something about it, and nobody in Kessik had ever done anything about anything except the weather, and even then only grudgingly.

He kept his name off the permit ledgers the way other men kept their hands clean. The district office sat four hours downriver at Vellmark, and its clerks cared about commercial outfitters running paying clients into the high country—guides with certifications, insurance, liability. But a man taking two fishermen up the lower Fly for cash, no paperwork, no receipt, no trailhead register entry beyond a scrawled initial? That fell below the line where the ledgers stopped looking. August lived below that line. He had built his life there, plank by plank, the way you built a shelter below tree line when you'd learned what the wind could do above it.

The fishermen were usually German or Dutch, occasionally English, always convinced they'd discovered something. *A guide, way up here, this cheap, and he can actually read water.* They didn't ask why a man with his hands—broad, scarred across both knuckles, the left index finger shortened at the second joint by something cold and fast—was charging half what a certified guide in Vellmark charged. Or if they asked, he gave them the answer he'd rehearsed until it wore smooth as river stone: *I like it quiet.* Everyone accepted that. Quiet was a currency in Kessik. People paid in it constantly.

On the morning the letter came, the river ran green.

August noticed it from the porch of the cabin he rented from old Mrs. Halvorsen, whose eyesight was failing and whose rent demands had not risen in three years because she believed he was a carpenter named Anders Vik, a fiction he maintained with occasional repairs to her fence and a willingness to listen to stories about her late husband's dogs. Green water meant clear skies upstream, high pressure parked over the icefields, a stable melt. A good day for the run he had booked—a pair of brothers from Rotterdam who wanted grayling and did not want adventure. His favorite kind of client. Adventure was a thing he sold the memory of, not the experience.

He was loading the drift boat onto the trailer when Tomas Kettunen's truck rattled up the track and stopped with the engine still running, which was how August knew something was wrong. Tomas never left the engine running. Fuel cost money, and Tomas counted everything twice.

"You've got mail," Tomas said through the window.

"I've got clients at nine."

"This isn't the kind of mail you take fishing." He held it up. A single envelope, heavy stock, cream-colored, addressed in a hand August recognized the way you recognize a voice from a nightmare—before you understand what you're recognizing, while the body is already reacting. His own name written in ink so confident it looked printed. No return address. Postmarked Vellmark, which meant someone had carried it there by hand rather than trusting it to the mountain post.

August set down the fuel can. "Who brought it?"

"Mail run yesterday. I signed for it." Tomas hesitated, and that hesitation told August more than the envelope did. Tomas had read enough of the situation to be afraid of it, and Tomas was not a man who scared easily; he'd once talked a drunk Russian poacher off a bridge with nothing but patience and a thermos of coffee. "There's a signature on the back of the letter inside. I looked. I'm sorry, but I looked."

"Tomas."

"Soren Vask." He said the name flatly, the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd been tested for. "It's Soren Vask's signature, August. On a letter to you. In this year of God's patience."

The river made its green noise against the pilings. Somewhere up the valley a woodpecker worked at a dead spruce, patient as a clock. August found that he had walked to the porch rail without deciding to, and that his hand, when he took the envelope, was steady—which surprised him, because underneath the steadiness everything had gone loud, the way a slope goes loud an instant before it moves.

* * *

He didn't open it right away. He made himself do the morning first—the Rotterdam brothers, their cheerful terrible casting, the grayling rising to nymphs in the tailout below the old railway bridge. He made himself read the water, make the calls, net the fish, tell the joke about the Dutchman and the bear that they laughed at because guides' jokes are always funnier than they are. He performed competence and calm for six hours while the envelope rode in his chest pocket like a coal wrapped in cloth.

But all day, beneath the performance, the letter worked on him. Because a signature was not just a signature. A signature was a door, and he had nailed that door shut from his side, and now someone on the other side was knocking politely, on cream-colored stationery, and politeness from that man had always been the most frightening thing about him.

Three years ago—no. He corrected himself, as he always did, with the precision of a man who checked knots twice. Three years and seven months. The Karst-Arne expedition, six clients, three guides, the North Couloir route on Mount Hessel above the Upper Fly headwaters. The storm that came twelve hours early. The summit push that should have been called at the shoulder camp and wasn't. The fixed lines that iced. The traverse where the rope team went, and the cornice, and the sound—he still heard it sometimes, in the space between waking and sleep, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat—and then the white silence after, and the long descent carrying what was left of the day's arithmetic: three climbers gone. Two of them clients. One of them a guide, Petra Lund, twenty-nine years old, who had argued with him at the shoulder camp and lost the argument and died winning it.

The inquiry board had been gentle with him, in the way that boards are gentle when they've already decided. *Guides operate under commercial pressure. Weather forecasting in the region remains imprecise. Mr. Iversen's decisions, while ultimately consequential, were consistent with industry practice given available information.* Consistent with industry practice. He had read that phrase perhaps ten thousand times since, and each time it sounded less like absolution and more like an indictment of the entire profession. But the certification board hadn't been gentle. His lead certification, suspended. His insurance eligibility, revoked. His name entered into the regional blacklist registry that every outfitter in three districts consulted before hiring, a list that functioned less as a record than as a curse. *Iversen, A. — fatality incident, multiple. Do not engage.*

Soren Vask had testified at that hearing. August remembered it with total clarity, the way you remember the one moment of a crash you're conscious for. Vask had stood in his good coat and spoken in measured tones about operational realities, about the pressures of client expectations, about how the decision structure on Karst-Arne had been diffuse—*shared,* he'd said, *the decisions were shared*—and August, sitting three meters away with his hands flat on the table to keep them from doing anything, had understood that he was watching a man bury a knife in him with the handle turned toward the audience. Vask had organized the expedition. Vask had taken the deposit from the client who demanded the summit. Vask had radioed the shoulder camp and said, in words August could still recite: *You have a window. Use it or lose the season.* And then Vask had stood before the board and described a tragedy that had, apparently, happened to no one in particular, caused by no one in particular, weather being weather and mountains being mountains.

The board had needed someone to blame who wasn't rich. August had been available.

Afterward: the blacklist, the divorce papers signed by mail, the slow southward slide through towns that got smaller and more forgetful until he reached Kessik, which forgot better than any of them. He had told himself it was temporary. For the first year he'd believed it. By the second year he'd stopped believing it and started building instead—Tomas, the boat, the clients below the ledger line, Mrs. Halvorsen's fence. A life assembled from parts that couldn't be traced back to the man on the list. Anders Vik, carpenter. Quiet, he liked it quiet.

And now cream-colored stationery, and that handwriting, and a signature like a knife laid on a table.

* * *

The brothers from Rotterdam drank beer at the Ferryman's Rest and told him they'd come back next season, and August smiled and shook hands and meant none of it and all of it, and walked home along the river in the blue hour with the envelope finally in his hand.

He opened it on the porch, standing, because sitting down felt like surrender. Inside: one page, same heavy stock, same confident hand. No salutation. Vask had never wasted words on warmth; warmth was overhead expense.

*Iversen.*

*I'll keep this short because I know you don't read my letters and I know why.*

*A job. One crossing, Upper Fly range, west to east, before equinox snows. Client is legitimate, cargo is simple, pay is double your best year and triple what you're making gutting trout for tourists under another man's name—yes, I know about that too, I know everything about where you ended up, it's my business to know things.*

*You'll say no. You've had three years of practice saying no. So I won't argue. I'll just leave this with you: the passes close in five weeks. After that, the offer closes too, and it doesn't reopen. There are things moving in the high country this season that concern you whether you move with them or stand still. Standing still has never once saved you, August. Ask Petra.*

August read it twice. Then he read the last line again, and something behind his sternum went cold and bright, the way a headlamp beam looks across a glacier at night.

Ask Petra. As if she were a mutual acquaintance. As if she'd left them both equally. As if the man who ordered the push and the man who led it shared a grief, when in truth they shared only a date on a calendar and a list of names, and only one of them had carried the bodies down.

He burned the letter in the wood stove, holding the corner until the flame reached his fingers, watching the confident ink curl and go brown and vanish. Burning felt correct. Burning was what you did with evidence, and the letter was evidence—not of a crime, but of a weakness. Evidence that three years of planks and pseudonyms and green-water mornings could be undone by thirty grams of cream-colored paper.

He slept badly, which was to say he slept the way he always slept, which was barely, in a bed he'd built himself with joints tight enough to hold but a frame that creaked at every turn like a boat working in a swell. Around two in the morning he got up and stood at the window. The sky over the western ranges was clear, stars hard and close, and above the black sawtooth of the divide he could pick out, without trying, without wanting to, the massif where Hessel stood. Invisible at night, of course. But he saw it anyway. He had spent three years teaching his eyes not to look, and they had never once obeyed.

* * *

In the morning he rowed Tomas across the narrows to check the fish racks, mostly as an excuse, and told him about the letter, or the shape of it, leaving out the last line because some wounds you don't show even to the man who shares your boat.

"Double pay," Tomas said. He was a broad, slow-moving Finn with a beard going iron-gray and hands that had mended nets for forty years. "One crossing. No questions." He repeated it the way he'd repeat a tide table, testing the numbers. "And you burned it."

"Burned it."

"Good. Because the answer's no." Tomas shipped the oars and let the boat swing in the current, which he only did when he wanted August to look at him. "August. Listen to me as a friend, because I'm about to talk like a businessman. This outfitting thing—we grossed eleven thousand last season. Eleven. After the trailer axle, after Mrs. Halvorsen's roof, after Renny's medical bills when he took the hook through the hand—you know the number. We're fine. We're fine the way a man standing in water up to his chin is fine. And this—" he flicked two fingers toward the west, toward the divide, toward the invisible mountain— "this is not a job. Men like Vask don't offer jobs. They offer doors, and then they stand behind you while you walk through, and whatever's on the other side, you're the one it lands on. You told me yourself. Karst-Arne. He said *you have a window.*"

"He said that."

"And you used it."

"I used it." The current pushed the boat gently against the cable crossing, and the cable hummed. "Three people are dead because I used it."

"Or because he said it," Tomas said. "Which is the thought you've been not-thinking for three years, and which is exactly why he's writing to you now. A man doesn't come back to the witness he buried unless he needs the grave dug deeper or filled in. Either way, August, you're the shovel." He picked the oars up again, unhurried. "Say no. Keep the name off the ledgers. Keep the water green."

They crossed the narrows in silence after that, the good silence of men who work together, and August pretended it had settled the matter.

But walking home in the evening, past the bakery and the shuttered assay office and the church whose congregation had shrunk to a dozen stubborn souls, he caught himself doing the thing he'd sworn off: reading the mountains. The light on the divide was doing that late-summer thing, going bronze and brittle, the snowline already creeping down a hundred meters from its July height. Five weeks, Vask had written. The passes close. And August, who had spent three years practicing smallness, felt the old hunger stir in him like a muscle remembering itself—the hunger for altitude, for thin air and honest consequences, for a country where a man's mistakes killed him quickly instead of slowly, in installments, the way the lowlands did.

He hated Vask for that. Hated him most precisely for this: that even the letter, even the manipulation, even the mention of Petra—it had worked. Somewhere under the dread and the anger, the door he'd nailed shut had come loose on its hinges, and through the crack he could smell the high country, cold and mineral and merciless, and some part of him that he'd starved carefully for three years pressed its face to the gap and breathed in.

That night he dreamed of the shoulder camp. In the dream the storm had not come yet; the sky was that impossible pre-storm blue, and Petra was arguing with him, and somewhere below, faint as a radio from another world, a voice was saying *you have a window, use it or lose the season*—and August woke with his heart slamming and lay in the dark listening to the frame of his bed creak like a boat, and understood that the quiet was over.

Not because he'd said yes. He hadn't said yes. He'd burned the letter.

But burning, he knew now, had only ever delayed the reading.
