## 2. A Signature Like a Knife

The letter burned badly.

August Iversen had expected it to go up all at once, a bright and merciful flash in the woodstove of the Kessik outfitting office, but the heavy paper resisted. It curled at the corners first, blackening like frost-bitten skin, and for a long moment the signature itself survived—the looping, self-satisfied *S.V.* that he had last seen eleven years ago on an expedition manifest that should have been torn up instead of filed. He watched it with the detached attention of a man watching someone else's hand bleed, and then the ink finally gave, and the name went to ash along with everything else.

He stood there longer than he needed to. The stove ticked. Outside, the Kessik River ran its October voice under thinning ice, and somewhere up the street a dog was complaining about the cold in the particular way dogs did here, as if the whole town owed them better weather. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a life built deliberately small.

But the terms were still in his head. That was the trouble with burning things—you could destroy the paper, not what it said.

*Double pay. One crossing. No questions.*

He'd read it three times before the stove, standing in the doorway with his boots still on, because some part of him had known even then that he wouldn't sit down with it. You didn't sit down with a knife. You read it fast and you decided where to put your hands.

*Double pay* meant Vask knew about the ledgers. Not the permit ledgers August kept scrupulously clean—the ones that showed only river runs, day trips, nothing above the tree line—but the other ledger, the one every guide in a border town kept in his head: what he actually earned, what he actually owed, what winter would cost if the salmon runs stayed thin and the tourists stayed home. Double pay wasn't generosity. Double pay was a man who had done arithmetic on someone else's desperation and liked the answer.

*One crossing* meant the Upper Fly range, before equinox snows. Which meant the passes. Which meant—

August crouched and fed the stove another split of birch, mostly so his hands would have something to do. The fire took it greedily. He let himself look at the flames and not think about the col, and failed completely, the way he always failed, the way he had failed for eleven years every time the word *crossing* arrived in any sentence addressed to him.

Three climbers. That was the number the newspapers had used, because numbers were cleaner than names. Halvardsen. The Brandt cousins—no relation to anyone who mattered now, or maybe relation to everyone who mattered now, the world being small and cruel in its bookkeeping. And Petra Lindqvist, who had been twenty-four and who had trusted him absolutely, right up until the cornice didn't.

His fault. That was the verdict, delivered by an inquiry board that never left base camp, ratified by an industry that needed someone to blacklist, and accepted—this was the part no one understood, the part that kept him in Kessik when Kessik offered him nothing—by August himself. He had signed the summit push order. His signature, like Vask's, except his had been written by a younger man trying to please an older one, and the mountain had read both signatures and taken its payment.

He locked up the office at six. The lock was temperamental and he jiggled it the way you had to, and the streetlight came on over the boardwalk with its usual stutter, and August Iversen walked home through a town that forgot on purpose, carrying a letter's ghost in his coat pocket like a stone.

* * *

Tomas Rennick lived above the gear shop they co-owned, in rooms that smelled of boot wax and pipe smoke and, tonight, of the lamb stew he made whenever he wanted to talk someone out of something. August recognized the strategy from across the room. Eleven years of partnership taught you a man's cooking the way it taught you his silences.

"You got a letter," Tomas said. Not a question. "I saw the courier come by. Nobody sends you letters."

"Nobody does."

"So?"

"So it's handled."

Tomas ladled stew into two bowls without looking at him, which was its own kind of accusation. "Handled," he repeated. "You've got the face you get when something's handled. Like a man who's buried a cat and is deciding whether to tell his daughter."

"I don't have a daughter."

"No," Tomas agreed. "That's the tragedy of you." He set the bowls down, sat, and fixed August with the flat gray stare that had ended more arguments between them than shouting ever had. "Soren Vask."

The name landed in the warm room like a dropped ice axe. August picked up his spoon. "It's handled."

"You burned it."

"Tomas."

"You burn things when you're scared of them. You've been doing it since I've known you. Receipts, letters, that photograph of the '09 survey team—I'm not stupid, August, I just mind my own business, which is a full-time occupation around you." Tomas ate a mouthful of stew, chewed with terrible patience. "Double pay. One crossing. No questions. Am I close?"

August put his spoon down. The stew smelled good and he hated it. "How could you possibly know the terms?"

"Because there's only one letter a man like Vask writes, and it has one shape. Everyone in this valley knows the shape. Half of them have gotten one." Tomas leaned back. "Rolf Aune got one four years ago. Took it. Came back with money nobody asked questions about and a way of not sleeping. You remember how he looked by spring? Like a man listening to a radio nobody else could hear. He's in Whitehorse now. Won't come back over the pass even in summer."

"Rolf Aune was never a strong guide."

"Rolf Aune was a fine guide," Tomas said, "until the high country got its hooks back into him. Same as it will with you. Same as it's doing right now, this minute, while you sit there pretending my stew interests you." He tapped the table once, twice, a drummer counting himself down. "Refuse it. Whatever it is. Refuse it tomorrow, refuse it loudly, and if he shows up here—and he will show up here, men like Vask don't trust the mail with anything that matters—we'll both be standing in the doorway when he does."

"He won't come."

"He'll come."

August looked at his partner—at the broad, careful face, the reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, the hands of a man who had spent thirty years coiling rope and pricing carabiners and never once asking the mountains for more than they freely gave—and felt the strange, shameful thing move in his chest again, the thing that had woken when he first broke the envelope's seal.

Not dread. Or not only dread. Dread was the floor of it. Above the floor, rising like heat off rock, was something older and less forgivable.

Hunger.

* * *

He didn't sleep.

He lay in his narrow bed above the shop—he kept the small room though he owned half the building, because a man hiding from his own reputation shouldn't sleep in anything generous—and listened to the wind come down off the divide and test the shutters, one by one, like a burglar with patience. North wind. It carried a mineral smell, faint, almost imaginary: snow that hadn't fallen yet, waiting somewhere above the tree line in the high cirques, patient as debt.

*Double pay.*

He turned the terms over the way you'd turn a core sample, looking for flaws. Double pay for a single crossing meant either a client with more money than sense or cargo worth more than clients. Vask didn't spend money; Vask positioned it. Every kroner, every dollar, every favor Vask had ever deployed had come back to him multiplied, and August had watched it happen for years from the cheap seats, had watched men sign on to Vask's expeditions the way sailors signed onto certain ships, knowing the odds and taking them anyway because the alternative was staying ashore, and staying ashore, God help him, was the one thing August Iversen had tried to be for eleven years and had never once managed to be honestly.

*One crossing.*

The Upper Fly range in late season. He lay in the dark and his traitor memory laid out the map without being asked: the lower trail green-black with spruce, the river braiding silver at Kettle Ford, the long moraine valleys where the light came down like something poured, and then the tree line, and then the ice. The Col of Hanging Ice, which he had crossed nine times and buried three men beside. The shoulder beyond it that he had reconnoitered alone in '11 and sworn, aloud, to no one, never to attempt—a line so clean and so vicious that he had dreamed about it for a year afterward and then trained himself to stop dreaming, the way you train yourself to stop checking a wound.

No one climbed the shoulder. That was the point of swearing about it.

*No questions.*

And there it was, the term that should have ended everything, the one Tomas would seize on, the one any sane man would seize on. No questions meant the client didn't want scrutiny. No questions meant whatever crossed the range with him would cross unexamined, and August had spent eleven years learning exactly what flourished in unexamined places. He had been the unexamined place himself. He knew the ecology.

So refuse it. Say no. Tell Vask, when he came—and Tomas was right, he would come—to take his double pay and his silence and his client and go to hell by whichever route he preferred, there were several, all of them scenic.

August got up at two in the morning and lit the lamp and got out the maps.

He told himself it was professional due diligence. If Vask pressed the matter in person, a man ought to know what he was refusing. This was a lie of such long standing that it had practically become furniture, and he stepped around it and unrolled the range chart on the kitchen table and weighted the corners with a tin cup, a whetstone, a box of matches, and the brass compass he had not opened in three seasons.

The Upper Fly spread out under the lamplight, blue contour lines stacked like the rings of some drowned tree. His finger moved without his permission. Lower trail. Kettle Ford. Tree line. The col. And then west, off the marked routes entirely, to the shoulder—the blank shoulder, the unclimbed one, where the cartographers had run out of confidence and simply stopped drawing lines.

He stood over it for a long time. The wind worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, the river kept saying the only thing rivers ever say, which is *downward, downward*, and August thought: that's the difference between us, old friend. You've never once wanted to go up.

The hunger was fully awake now. He could feel it the way you feel a storm front—not the weather itself but the pressure before it, the ache behind the ears, the animal certainty that something large was coming and that you would rather stand in it than hide from it. Eleven years of low-stakes river runs, of permit ledgers kept clean, of a name kept out of print and out of conversation, and underneath all of it this: that he had loved the high country with the whole of himself, and love like that doesn't die when it kills. It just goes underground. It waits. It learns the shape of a man's ordinary days and bides.

And now a letter with a knife for a signature had cut the ground open, and the thing was stirring.

He rolled the map away before dawn. He cleaned the lamp glass. He put the compass back in its drawer and did not look at it, which took more discipline than the whole rest of the night combined.

At seven, Tomas came down the inside stairs with the coffee pot and found August already dressed, already shaved, sitting at the table with his hands flat on the wood like a man pinning something down.

"You didn't sleep," Tomas said.

"No."

"And?"

"And nothing. It's refused. It stays refused." August met his partner's eyes and held them, and it cost him more than the whole night had. "If he comes, we turn him away together. That's the end of it."

Tomas studied him for a moment—long enough that August felt seen straight through, straight down to the rolled-up map and the drawer with the compass in it—and then nodded slowly and poured the coffee.

"All right," Tomas said. "Together, then."

Which was when the bell over the shop door rang, early, too early for customers, with the particular confidence of a man who has never once in his life waited to be admitted anywhere.

Through the frosted glass of the inner door, a shadow crossed the floor of the shop, unhurried, well-shod, and stopped.

"August," said a voice he had spent eleven years hoping never to hear again, rich and easy and cold at the center, like a lake over deep water. "Still keeping the temperamental lock, I see. Some things are a comfort."
