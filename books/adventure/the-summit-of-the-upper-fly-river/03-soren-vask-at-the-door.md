## 3. Soren Vask at the Door

The knock came at dusk, three measured raps, the kind of knock that belongs to a man who has never once waited to be let in.

August was in the back room of the outfitting shed, re-waxing a rope that didn't need it, when the sound reached him. He knew every knock in Kessik by now—the double-tap of the postmistress delivering bills, Tomas's flat palm against the doorframe, the drunken percussion of river men at closing time. This one was none of those. This one had patience in it, and money, and something underneath both that made the hair on his forearms lift before his mind caught up to why.

He set down the wax. He did not move toward the door.

Through the gap beneath it, lamplight from the street threw a shadow: broad shoulders, a hat with a stiff brim, boots too good for this street. The shadow did not shift. It did not check its watch or shuffle its weight the way most waiting men do. It simply stood, the way stone stands, and August understood that whoever was on the other side would stand there until morning if he made them.

"August." The voice came through the wood, low and even, worn smooth like a river cobble. "I can hear you breathing."

There was only one voice in the world that could put his whole name in his mouth like that—no *Iversen*, no *mister*, just *August*, as though the syllables were property the speaker had never surrendered. Eleven years since he'd heard it. Eleven years, and still it landed in his chest like a piton driven into rotten rock.

He crossed the room and opened the door.

* * *

Soren Vask had grown old the way glaciers grow old—by losing mass and gaining authority. The man who stood under the lamp was thicker through the middle than the climber August had once followed up three continents, silver at the temples where there had been black, a new heaviness in the jaw. But the eyes were unchanged. Pale grey, nearly colorless, set deep under a shelf of brow, and they moved over August the way they used to move over a route: reading it, pricing it, deciding what it would cost and whether the price was worth paying.

"You look terrible," Vask said.

"You look rich."

"I am." Vask smiled without warmth. "You're not. May I come in, or shall we conduct business on your doorstep like a pair of fishmongers?"

August stepped aside. It was not a decision so much as a surrender of ground he'd already lost; he'd known, from the first rap of knuckles, how this evening would go, the way you know a slope is loaded before you ever cut the first step.

Vask entered the shed the way he entered everything—taking inventory. His gaze went over the coiled ropes, the dented stoves, the racks of secondhand crampons Tomas had bought off a bankrupt trekking outfit two seasons back. August watched him read the room and knew exactly what the reading said: *small*. *Shabby*. *A man reduced*. Vask's face performed nothing about it, which was worse than a sneer. A sneer would have been honest.

"So this is what became of you," Vask said mildly.

"This is what I built." August closed the door. "It's not much, but nobody's ever died in it."

The words were out before he could weigh them, and he watched them land—not as a wound but as an inconvenience, a pebble in Vask's boot to be shaken out. That was the thing about Soren Vask that eleven years had not softened in August's memory: guilt slid off him. Other men carried their dead like ballast. Vask jettisoned them at altitude.

"The accident," Vask said, "was seven years ago, not eleven. You've been hiding for four years longer than you needed to."

"Don't call it that."

"What should I call it? The incident? The event?" Vask removed his hat—a good hat, beaver felt—and set it on the workbench with the care of a man who respected fine things and expected them to respect him back. "Three climbers died above the Serac Line. An inquiry was held. Conclusions were drawn. Some of them were even fair. And then the industry did what industries do—it needed someone to blame, and it chose the guide, because guides are replaceable and reputations are not. I told you then it was nonsense. I'm telling you now."

"You told me to keep climbing," August said. "You stood at base camp with a radio in your hand and told me the window was closing and the clients wanted the summit, and you told me to keep climbing. Those were your words. I've had seven years to forget them and I haven't managed a single day."

Something flickered behind the grey eyes—there and gone, quick as a ptarmigan flushing from scree. Not remorse. August had learned long ago not to expect remorse from this man. It was closer to irritation, the irritation of a chess player reminded of a game he'd rather not have lost.

"Weather forecasting in those days was witchcraft," Vask said. "Every guide on the mountain took the same window. Half of them turned around. You didn't. Is that my fault?"

*Yes*, said everything in August that had spent seven years screaming into pillow stuffing and cold rivers and the bottomless indifferent dark of three o'clock in the morning. *Yes, you bastard, it is entirely your fault, and we both know it, and you know I know it, and you came all this way anyway because you believe—because you have always believed—that enough money makes a debt disappear.*

What he said was: "Why are you here, Soren?"

Vask studied him for a long moment. Outside, the lamplight in the street guttered in a wind coming down off the divide, carrying the first mineral breath of autumn. Somewhere down the block a dog started up and thought better of it.

"Tea," Vask said. "If you have any. We're going to be a while."

* * *

They sat across the scarred worktable from each other, two tin mugs between them, and August found himself cataloguing the changes in his visitor the way he'd once catalogued changes in a glacier before trusting it with his weight. The hands were the same—broad, capable, a climber's hands despite everything, despite the years of ledgers and boardrooms that had clearly intervened. There was a ring now, heavy gold, and a watch that probably cost more than the shed they were sitting in. But the calluses were still there at the base of the fingers. Whatever else Soren Vask had become, he had not stopped going into the mountains himself. That was somehow the most unsettling discovery of the night.

"Henrik Brandt," Vask began.

"The mining man."

"The mining magnate, if we're being precise, which I know you enjoy not being. Copper, gold, a rare-earth play in the north basin that's going to make him one of the five richest men in the country inside a decade. He has a daughter. Ilse. Twenty-six years old, educated in the capital, headstrong in the way that only daughters of self-made men are headstrong—which is to say, entirely convinced of her own competence and armed with enough money to test the theory."

"She wants a trek."

"She wants a crossing." Vask leaned forward, elbows on the table, and August recognized the posture from a hundred expedition briefings, the lean that meant the salesmanship was over and the truth—or Vask's version of it—was beginning. "The Upper Fly range. West to east, over the Col of Hanging Ice and down the Kessik side. Before the equinox snows seal the passes. Three weeks, perhaps less if the weather turns early, and the weather will turn early because it always does now, and everyone pretending otherwise can go argue with the satellites."

"That's a serious route."

"It's a serious client. She'll pay accordingly. Double your standard rate, plus a completion bonus that would buy this shed, the lot it stands on, and Tomas's silence for a year."

"Tomas doesn't need buying."

"Everyone needs buying, August. Some people are just bad at naming their price." Vask waved this aside before August could answer. "You'd take her over with a small crew—your choice, within reason. No media. No fanfare. Her father wants it quiet, which suits you, I imagine. Quiet is your trade now, isn't it? The river runs, the unlisted permits, the name kept out of the ledgers. Don't look surprised—I make it my business to know where useful men are hiding."

"I'm not hiding."

"No?" Vask glanced around the shed again, slow this time, letting the shabbiness speak. "Then what would you call it?"

August said nothing. The wind worried at the eaves. In the mug between his hands, the tea had gone cold and bitter, and he drank it anyway.

"Why me?" he asked finally. "There are a dozen guides on this side of the range with clean records and current certifications. Half of them would do it for single pay and a photograph with the daughter."

"There are no dozen guides," Vask said, "who know the west approaches to the Col of Hanging Ice. There are no dozen guides who've stood on the shoulder above it and lived. There is exactly one guide who reconnoitered that line eight years ago, drew the map that exists in exactly one copy, and then buried the whole business under an avalanche of scandal." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing cold in it at all, which made it colder. "I need the one guide, August. The rest are scenery."

"The shoulder's unclimbed."

"It's unrepeated. By you. Which means you're the only man alive who knows whether it goes."

"It doesn't go. That's why I never went back."

"Everything goes," Vask said, "for the right price, in the right weather, with the right man leading." He said it without bravado, without challenge—flatly, the way a man states the load rating of a rope. And that flatness frightened August more than any threat could have, because it wasn't rhetoric. It was creed. It had always been Vask's creed. It was the creed that had put three men into a crevasse field in falling weather because the window was closing and the clients wanted the summit and the mountain, in Soren Vask's cosmology, was a vendor that honored its contracts.

August looked at his own hands on the table. Guide's hands, still, after everything—scarred, steady, belonging to a man who had sworn a vow in a hospital corridor seven years ago with a dead client's brother watching from the far end of it. *Never again the high country. Never again other people's summits.* He had kept that vow the way you keep a wound clean: daily, painfully, with the constant knowledge that infection was one careless moment away.

And here was the infection, sitting across the table, drinking his tea.

"No," August said.

Vask did not react. August had expected—had braced for—surprise, or anger, or the smooth escalation of an offer doubling itself. None of it came. Vask simply set down his mug, aligned it with the table edge with two fingers, and looked at August with an expression of patient, almost gentle certainty, the look of a man watching water find its level.

"Yes," Vask said quietly. "But not tonight. Tonight you say no, and you mean it, and I respect it. I'll leave you the terms in writing—you'll burn them, I know, but read them first. And I'll tell you one more thing, free of charge, because unlike some people I keep my accounts balanced." He rose, retrieved his hat, settled it on his head with a small precise motion. "Henrik Brandt is sending his daughter over that range whether you guide her or someone else does. And the someone else, if it isn't you, will be one of the scenery guides. One of the dozen. And she will be above the tree line, on a route she paid to survive, when the equinox snow comes down on schedule." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "You've spent seven years deciding the mountain owes you. Fine. But think about what you owe the mountain. It's the only creditor whose books you can't burn."

The door closed behind him with the softness of a man who had gotten everything he came for.

* * *

August did not sleep.

He sat in the dark of the shed long after Vask's footsteps faded toward whatever expensive lodging the town offered, and then longer after that, while the wind walked the ridgelines and the stars wheeled behind cloud and the river below town kept up its one endless argument with the stones. Twice he got up and lit the lamp, and twice he put it out again, because in lamplight the shed filled with ghosts—the Serac Line, the radio crackling, Vask's voice saying *keep climbing* in a tone that had left no room on earth for any other sentence.

Tomas found him there at dawn, standing at the open door with his arms folded, watching the light come up the valley the way he watched it every morning of his life—as a forecast, a promise, a threat.

"Well?" Tomas said. He was a compact man, sixty-two, with a white beard and the permanent squint of someone who'd spent forty years reading water. He'd heard enough through the thin walls, or read enough in August's face, to skip the questions.

"He offered me the Brandt girl. Over the Upper Fly. Before the snows."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

Tomas grunted. He came to stand beside August in the doorway, and for a while they watched the mist burning off the river together, and neither of them said anything, because they had been partners long enough to know that the important conversations happen in the silences between sentences.

"He'll be back," Tomas said at last.

"He's already gone."

"Men like that don't go. They circle." Tomas spat thoughtfully into the yard. "You want to know what I saw last night, through that window, while you two played cards with each other's souls? I saw you listening. Not to him—to the mountains. Seven years, August. Seven years you've kept your eyes on the river and your back to the divide, and last night some part of you turned around and looked up." He clapped August once on the shoulder, hard, the way he did when he meant something. "That letter you burned. That man at your door. They're the same thing, and they're not done with you, and God help me, I don't think you're done with them either."

August said nothing. Above the eastern ridge, the sun cleared the rock and struck the high snowfields, and for one blinding moment the whole upper range burned white and gold against the blue—beautiful, indifferent, patient as debt.

He turned his back on it and went inside to make coffee.

But that afternoon, when the mail came, there was a second envelope in the box. No signature this time. Only a single sheet, a single line, in a woman's precise hand:

*I know who you are. I know what my father is using me to carry. Meet me before you decide anything.*

And beneath it, an address in Kessik, and a time: tomorrow, noon.
