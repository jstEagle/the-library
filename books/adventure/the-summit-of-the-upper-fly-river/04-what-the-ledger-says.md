## 4. What the Ledger Says

The offices of Brandt Mining occupied the top four floors of the Meridian Building, and from her father's corner suite Ilse could see the whole gray sprawl of Kessik laid out below like a circuit board someone had abandoned mid-design. The river cut through it in a braided silver seam. Beyond the town the foothills began their long climb toward the Upper Fly range, white-capped even in late summer, and she had spent most of her twenty-six years looking at those mountains through glass—first the nursery window, then the boarding school chapel, then this office, where she was permitted to sit at a desk that wasn't hers and do work that didn't matter.

Today the desk held the environmental compliance filings for the Corriedale Creek operation, because her father had asked her to "familiarize herself with the regulatory portfolio" before her trek, and because Ilse had learned long ago that when Henrik Brandt told you to familiarize yourself with something, he usually meant he wanted you to admire it.

She had been admiring it for three hours. And she had stopped admiring it somewhere around page forty of the third quarter's water-quality appendix.

The problem was small. That was what made her sit up straighter in the leather chair, what made her pull the lamp closer despite the wide gray daylight. The problem was *small*—a discrepancy of eleven parts per million in the dissolved-solids readings for monitoring station CC-4, repeated across three consecutive quarterly reports but not in the raw lab annexes bound at the back. The summary tables said one thing. The annexes, the actual laboratory certificates with their barcodes and analyst signatures, said something quietly, consistently worse.

Eleven ppm was nothing. She knew that much. It wouldn't kill fish, wouldn't color the creek, wouldn't matter to anyone except a regulator comparing tables against annexes—which no regulator did, because the tables were supposed to be *derived from* the annexes. They were supposed to match. They were supposed to be the same numbers wearing different clothes.

Hers did not match. In forty-one places across two fiscal years, they did not match, and every mismatch ran in the same direction: the summaries were cleaner than the truth.

Ilse sat very still and listened to the building breathe around her—the hum of the ventilation, the distant elevator chime, a phone ringing twice before someone on the executive floor swallowed it. Her father's suite smelled of cedar paneling and the coffee he drank black from a cup with the company crest on it, and it smelled, faintly and always, of money being counted somewhere out of sight.

She turned back to the first discrepancy and checked it by hand. Then the second. By the fifth her pen was moving down the margin in small, precise ticks, and by the fifteenth her hand had begun to shake, not from fear exactly, but from the peculiar vertigo of watching a familiar landscape reveal itself as a map of somewhere else entirely.

*You're imagining it,* said the reasonable voice, the voice that had kept her compliant through twenty years of her father's arrangements. *Transcription errors happen. Rounding happens.*

Rounding did not happen in the same direction forty-one times. Rounding did not happen only in quarters that preceded permit renewals. She checked that too—pulled the renewal dates from the operations binder on the shelf behind her, cross-referenced them against the filing calendar—and found that the cleaned-up summaries clustered like birds around a feeder: always just ahead of a renewal application, always just ahead of an inspection window, always just clean enough.

The last time she had felt this particular cold spreading through her chest, she was nine years old, and she had found her mother's letters hidden in a hatbox—letters that made it clear her mother had known, before the illness took her, exactly what kind of man she had married, and had stayed anyway. Ilse had put the letters back and said nothing for seventeen years. She was tired of saying nothing. But she was also, she admitted to the empty office, afraid in a way that had teeth.

Because here was the other thing. Here was the thing she had been turning over since breakfast, since before the ledger, since her father stood in his doorway that morning with his hands in his pockets and said, too casually, the way he said everything that mattered:

*"You'll travel light. Vask's people will handle the logistics. There'll be a courier satchel in your kit—sealed, waterproofed. You don't open it, you don't let it out of your sight, and you deliver it to a man named Halloran at the ranger station past the divide. That's all I need from you."*

A holiday trek across the Upper Fly, her father had called it, when he'd first proposed it six weeks ago. A chance to see the mountains properly instead of through glass. He'd even seemed wistful about it, and she'd been touched—genuinely touched—in the way of a woman who has been starved for tenderness and will accept it in any currency offered.

Now she looked at the satchel instructions written in her own notebook, in her own handwriting, and heard them again with new ears. *Sealed. Waterproofed. You don't open it.* Her father moved ore samples by freight. He moved contracts by courier services with armored cars and insurance riders. He did not move documents across a mountain range on his daughter's back unless the documents could not be allowed to travel any road where someone might inspect them.

Unless they were running *from* something.

Her pen stopped ticking. She looked at the forty-one marks marching down the margin of the compliance report, neat as fence posts, and understood that she was holding one end of a thread whose other end was sewn into the satchel she would carry over the divide.

* * *

She should have gone home. It was past seven; the executive floor had emptied; the cleaning crew's vacuum droned somewhere near the elevators. Instead Ilse found herself in the records room on the thirty-ninth floor, using the after-hours access code her father had given her without ever suspecting she'd memorize it, standing before the archive cabinets that held the mine's institutional memory in gray boxes and hanging folders.

The air conditioning kept the room at a museum chill. Fluorescent tubes buzzed overhead, one of them dying in slow strobes. She pulled the files for Corriedale Creek going back eight years, carried them to the reading table, and began the unglamorous archaeology of her father's business.

It took her until nearly ten o'clock to find the pattern's spine, and when she found it, she wished she hadn't.

The discrepancies didn't start as fraud. That was the detail that lodged under her skin like a splinter. Eight years ago the mismatches were sporadic—a bad quarter corrected the next, an honest mistake apologized for in a cover memo signed by a site manager named T. Okafor. Then, five years back, the apologies stopped. The corrections stopped. The summaries went uniformly, serenely clean while the annexes went quietly dirty, and the transition happened across exactly two quarters—the same two quarters in which the consulting firm of **Vask & Associates** appeared in the correspondence file as "environmental reporting liaison."

Vask. Soren Vask. The name meant nothing to her except that it was on the trek itinerary—*S. Vask, route consultant and expedition sponsor*, listed right beneath her father's name on the letterhead he'd shown her. She had assumed he was a family friend. She had assumed, in her hunger to be trusted with something real, that the whole arrangement was a gift.

She sat in the cold records room with the correspondence file open in front of her and read five years of her father's environmental conscience being outsourced, quarter by quarter, to a man whose firm billed for "analytical reconciliation services" at rates that made no sense unless reconciliation meant something other than arithmetic.

There was a photograph paper-clipped to the earliest Vask & Associates proposal. Two men at a camp table in high country, squinting into sun, mugs raised. On the left, younger and broader than she'd ever seen him, her father. On the right, lean and dark-eyed, a man with a climber's weathered face and a smile that didn't reach his eyes even in a photograph. Someone had penciled a name on the back: *S. Vask, Upper Fly survey, with H. Brandt — season 9.*

Season nine. Eleven years ago. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eleven years ago was the year of the disaster she'd grown up hearing about in fragments—the guided expedition on the Upper Fly that lost three climbers, the guide who'd been blacklisted, the scandal her father had followed with an interest she'd always found oddly intense. She remembered him reading about it at dinner, folding the newspaper with finality, saying to no one: *"That's what happens when a man confuses ambition with judgment."*

She had thought he meant the guide. Sitting in the strobing fluorescent light with the photograph in her hand, Ilse wondered, for the first time, whether he'd been talking about himself—or about the man beside him at the camp table, raising his mug to a future they were building together out of other people's mountains.

* * *

At half past ten she locked the records room, walked the darkened executive floor past her father's office door—light off, which meant he was either home or lying—he descended in the elevator with a maintenance worker who nodded politely and got off two floors early, and stepped out into the Kessik night, where the wind came down off the foothills smelling of dust and glacier melt and distance.

She didn't go home. She walked. Past the shuttered outfitters' shops with their racks of rope and stoves visible through the glass, past the riverfront where the braided channels muttered to themselves in the dark, up the hill to the little café on Alder Street that stayed open late for the guides and prospectors, where she bought tea she didn't drink and sat by the window with her notebook and tried to think like a person instead of a daughter.

Here is what she knew, she wrote. Item by item, because lists were how she kept panic at arm's length.

1. The compliance filings are falsified. Systematically, directionally, and in coordination with permit cycles.
2. The falsification begins when Vask & Associates enters the record.
3. Father has hired me a mountain crossing. I am to carry a sealed satchel I may not open, to a contact I have never met, past every checkpoint and customs post and investigating eye between here and the far side of the divide.
4. There are investigators. This she had inferred rather than confirmed—but she had inferred it from the fact that her father had suddenly become attentive to her, warm even, in the weeks since the regional environmental authority announced its "comprehensive review of historical discharge data" in the upper watershed. He had taken her to dinner. He had asked about her life with a gentleness she hadn't seen since before her mother died. Men like Henrik Brandt did not become tender without reason. They became tender the way a river became smooth—over something submerged.

5. Therefore: the trek is not a gift. The trek is an extraction. She was not being sent to see the mountains. She was being sent to disappear—conveniently, deniably, beyond the reach of anyone who might want to ask her or the satchel questions, at least until the review concluded and whatever verdict fell had fallen on heads other than Brandts.

She read the list twice. Then she wrote a sixth item, and her pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next page:

6. I can refuse to carry it.

And beneath that, smaller:

7. Or I can open it.

The tea had gone cold. Outside, a pair of guides crossed the street under the lamp, arguing cheerfully about rope diameters, their voices carrying in the thin mountain air, and Ilse watched them with the acute homesickness of a woman looking at a country she'd never been allowed to enter. They belonged to the world on the far side of the glass—the world of trails and weather and competence, the world her father bought views of but never entered except by helicopter, the world she had been promised as a child and deflected into boarding schools and charity galas and now this desk-that-wasn't-hers.

If she refused the satchel, her father would simply find another courier, and she would go back to being furniture. If she opened it, she would know—and knowing, she could not unknow, and her father would know that she knew, and then she would truly be dangerous to him, and Henrik Brandt had never in his life left anything dangerous to him within arm's reach.

But there was a third option, and it had been forming in her all evening like ice forming on still water, crystal by crystal.

If the satchel contained evidence—if it contained the originals, the true annexes, the unreconciled numbers—then it was not contraband. It was testimony. And testimony did not need to be buried past the divide to protect her father. Testimony needed to be *delivered*—to the reviewers, the regulators, the regional authority whose comprehensive review notice she had folded into her notebook three weeks ago and told herself she'd clipped out of idle curiosity.

The same documents could bury him or save him, depending solely on whose hands received them. Her father planned to use the mountains to make the evidence vanish. The mountains could just as easily carry it the other way.

Ilse closed the notebook and pressed her palm flat against its cover, feeling her own heartbeat come back to her through the cardboard, steady, insistent, hers.

She would go on the trek. She would pack well and smile at the right moments and let her father believe his arrangement was sealed. She would watch the crew he and Vask assembled, and she would learn which of them were men who followed orders and which might be men who could be told the truth. Somewhere on that route, in that country of ice and silence where her father's lawyers and helicopters and money thinned out to nothing, she would find her moment.

The lamp above the café counter flickered. The guides paid their tab and pushed out into the dark, and Ilse Brandt, daughter of the most powerful man in the valley, sat alone by the window with a forged holiday itinerary in her bag and a decision cooling in her chest like a struck blade.

Three days later, a letter would arrive at an outfitter's shop down by the river, bearing terms that would set the whole machine in motion—double pay, one crossing, no questions—and the guide who received it would burn it and lie awake and tell himself he had refused.

Ilse knew none of this yet. She only knew that when she finally walked home through the sleeping streets of Kessik, she caught herself glancing up—not at her father's lit windows in the Meridian Building, but past them, to where the white shoulder of the Upper Fly range floated above the town in the starlight, patient and indifferent, waiting to learn what she would bring it.
