## 5. The Crew Assembles

The letter from the bank came on a Tuesday, folded into the same canvas mail pouch that carried the fishing licenses and the seed catalogs, and August read it standing at the counter of the Kessik outfitting post while Tomas pretended to inventory carabiners three feet away.

He didn't need to read past the second line. The second line had a number in it.

"Bad?" Tomas asked, not looking up.

"The loan's called. Ninety days."

Tomas set down the carabiner he'd been holding for ten minutes and finally looked at him. Tomas was sixty-one and built like a fence post that had decided to stay standing out of spite, and his face did most of its talking around the eyes. Right now the eyes said *I told you so*, which they had been saying, in one dialect or another, since the day August had first mentioned Soren Vask's name.

"You could sell the boats," Tomas said.

"Selling the boats is closing the business."

"Selling the boats is surviving."

August folded the letter along its crease and put it in his shirt pocket, where it sat against his chest like something with a pulse. Outside the shop window, the Kessik River ran brown and quick with early melt, and two of his hired hands were hosing down the rafts, and everything looked ordinary, and nothing was.

Three seasons of low-stakes river runs. That was what his life had become: drift boats and day-trippers, families from the lowlands who wanted photographs of themselves holding trout, corporate groups who paid extra for the good cooler. Work that never rose above eight hundred meters. Work that kept his name off the permit ledgers for anything with snow on it, because August Iversen's name on a high-country permit was a thing that made clerks make phone calls, and phone calls made newspapermen, and newspapermen made the old story new again. *Three dead on the Upper Fly. Guide blamed. Where is he now?*

He had gotten very good at being small. He'd thought he was good at it permanently.

But ninety days was ninety days, and the bank in Kessik was not a bank so much as a man named Aldous Petrie who played golf with men who owned things, and Petrie had already called twice this spring about the late interest payment, and the third call would be the last one.

*Double pay,* the burned letter had promised. *One crossing. No questions.* He had fed it to the woodstove eleven days ago and watched the signature curl black—*S.V.*, that knife-stroke of a final letter—and he had told himself the smoke smell would leave the room by morning.

It hadn't left the room. It hadn't left him.

"I'm going to do it," he said.

Tomas closed the inventory ledger with a soft, deliberate sound, the way a man closes a door on a sickroom. "Then we're done talking about it."

"We're not done talking about it."

"No," Tomas agreed. "We're not. But you've stopped listening, so there's no point." He took off his reading glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Conditions, then. If you're going to hang us both, hang us on your terms."

"My route," August said. It came out of him fast, the way the true things always did. "My route or nothing. Nobody tells me which line we take over that range. Not Brandt, not Vask, not the daughter, nobody. And my rules on the mountain are law. Anyone breaks them, they walk back down alone, client or crew, I don't care whose money is behind them."

"And?"

"And I pick the crew."

Tomas grunted. It was as close to a blessing as August was going to get. "You'll want Mara Quist."

"I know."

"She'll say no."

"I know that too."

* * *

Mara Quist lived nine kilometers up-valley in a cabin she'd built herself the summer after her certification, with a woodshed full of split spruce stacked to the eaves and a dog named Bristle who announced the truck a full minute before it arrived. August parked at the gate because Mara did not let vehicles past the gate, and walked up through the yard while Bristle circled him stiff-legged, making a show of suspicion that fooled neither of them.

She came out wiping her hands on her thighs. She was thirty-four, broad-shouldered, with a sun-creased squint and hair she cut herself with sewing scissors, and when she saw who it was her expression went through several distinct weather systems before settling on flat.

"August."

"Mara."

"Tomas called ahead. I told him no."

"He told me you'd say no."

"Then why are you standing in my yard?"

"Because you said it to him, not to me."

That earned him a long look. She leaned against the rail of her porch, arms crossed, and Bristle sat down heavily between them like a chaperone. Behind her, through the open door, August could see a wall of route maps pinned edge to edge—the whole eastern divide, annotated in her cramped handwriting, every pass and chute and hanging valley catalogued. She knew the range better than any guide working it. Better than he did now, probably; she'd spent the six years since his disgrace building the knowledge he'd been too busy hiding to build.

"Vask's money," she said. "That's the rumor. Brandt Mining's daughter, over the Fly before equinox. Half the guides in the valley have heard the number they're paying."

"It's real."

"And you're taking it."

"I'm taking it because the bank is calling the loan and because—" He stopped. The rest of the sentence was dangerous, and she watched him decide whether to say it, and he said it anyway. "Because part of me wants to see that country again. And I hate that part of myself, but it's there, and it's not going away, and if I have to cross that range I'd rather do it with someone who'll tell me when I'm lying to myself."

Something moved behind the squint. Not softness exactly. Recognition.

"You know what your name does," she said quietly. "You know what happened to mine the day yours broke. I lost two contracts that season. Clients who'd booked me a year out called up and asked, politely, whether I'd been on that expedition. Whether I'd trained under you. Whether I was the kind of guide who—" She shook her head. "Six years, August. Six years I climbed out of that hole by myself."

"Yes."

"And now you're asking me to tie into the same rope again."

"I'm asking you to get paid double to do the best route work of your career, on terrain nobody's guided, with a crew you help choose, under rules I'll put in writing." He held her eyes. "And yes. Under my name. Which means if it goes wrong, it goes wrong on me twice. I know what I'm asking."

Bristle sighed and flopped over sideways, and Mara looked down at the dog as if betrayed by the timing.

"Who else?" she said.

"A cook. Renny Oduya, if he'll come—he's been wanting a big trip since his knee healed. And porters. Two, maybe three."

"Anyone lined up?"

"One application came through the post office box last week. Dev Okafor. References from three expeditions I've never heard of, all glowing, all—"

"All what?"

"Clean," August said. "Too clean. Every reference answers the same way. Every employer remembers him fondly. Nobody has a single reservation, not even the small honest ones people always have—you know, *strong but talks too much*, *great worker, bad with money*. Nothing. It reads like it was written by one person."

Mara was quiet a moment. "Or he's just genuinely good."

"Nobody's genuinely good."

"You were, once. Before." She pushed off the rail and stood straight, and he understood that the conversation had turned without her needing to announce it. "Fine. Conditions of my own. I weigh everything that goes on a pack animal, and I mean everything—if a client wants to bring a case I don't like, I open it or it stays in Kessik. I carry the med kit and nobody touches it but me. And when we're above tree line, my word counts same as yours. You override me, you do it out loud, in front of everyone, so there's a record."

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair. It's the only reason I'm saying yes." She stuck out her hand and he took it, and her grip was dry and hard and brief. "God help us both," she added, not joking at all.

* * *

Renny Oduya said yes before August finished the sentence.

They sat in the kitchen behind the Kessik diner where Renny worked the lunch shift, and the offer lasted maybe forty seconds—double pay, three weeks, high country—before Renny pulled off his apron and hung it on the chair like a man setting down a load.

"You had me at *three weeks*," he said. "Lidia's been after me to take her to the coast, and coast costs money, and here you are dropping money out of the sky." He was fifty-two and thick through the middle and his left knee clicked audibly when he stood, an inheritance from a crevasse fall in '09 that he discussed the way other men discussed weather. "High country, though. Real high country?"

"Passes at four thousand meters. Maybe higher, depending on my line."

Renny whistled through his teeth. "Last time I cooked above three thousand was the Kavir survey, and that was flat walking. This'll be different."

"Different how?"

"Different means I plan food like a miser and cook like a magician, and you don't ask what's in the stew." He was already pulling a notepad from the drawer, already writing, his hand moving in the shorthand of a man who had provisioned forty expeditions and buried none of them. "How many mouths?"

"Six. Maybe seven if we take a third porter."

"Appetites?"

"One client. Rich family. Assume she eats like a bird and pack like she eats like a horse."

Renny snorted. "Rich girls either don't eat or eat everything, no in-between. I'll plan for both." His pencil paused. He looked up, and for the first time his round, cheerful face went serious. "August. This is the Fly. The upper Fly."

"I know."

"This is the one that—"

"I know, Renny."

Renny held his gaze for a moment, then nodded slowly and went back to his list. But at the door, apron still over his arm, he said, "For what it's worth—I never believed the papers. A man who cooks for a crew sees what a crew looks like after a bad day, and I heard what your crew looked like after that one. Men don't look at a man they blame like that. They look *through* him." He shrugged his big shoulders. "Anyway. Double pay. Three weeks. Don't get me killed."

* * *

Dev Okafor presented himself at the outfitting post on a Thursday morning, ten minutes early, wearing boots too new to have ever carried a heavy load and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his expressions, as though it had been sent ahead to scout.

"Mr. Iversen. Dev Okafor. We spoke by letter."

"We spoke by letter," August confirmed, and shook his hand, and noted the handshake: firm, practiced, released exactly on time. Everything about the man was exactly on time.

He was perhaps thirty, tall, lean in the way of someone who worked at being lean rather than having it happen to him, dark-skinned, with careful diction and a small scar through one eyebrow that he did not explain and August did not ask about. He had brought a résumé, printed, in a plastic sleeve, which was itself a small oddity—guides did not carry résumés in plastic sleeves, and porters certainly did not.

"You've done the Karakoram approaches," August said, scanning the page again. "Two seasons. And the Cordillera Blanca. And a winter traverse in the Sawtooths."

"Yes, sir."

"Your references speak very highly of you."

"I work hard."

"They don't just say you work hard. They say you're indispensable. All three of them. Word for word, nearly." August set the page down and looked at him. "Indispensable is a strange word for a porter. Porters carry. Guides are indispensable."

Something flickered behind Dev's eyes—fast, controlled, gone. "I've been lucky with employers."

"Lucky," August repeated. He let the silence sit. Most men filled silences. Dev Okafor did not fill them; he stood inside them comfortably, hands loose, smile maintained at low power, and that too was a kind of answer. "Pay is double standard rate. Three weeks, possibly more. High altitude, storm risk, and I'll be honest with you because I'm honest with all my crew: the client's family has enemies, and there may be complications. If that's a problem, say so now."

"Complications," Dev said, tasting the word. "What kind?"

"The kind I don't fully know yet. If I did, I'd tell you."

Another pause, measured to the milligram. Then the smile widened by a degree. "I don't scare easy, Mr. Iversen. And I need the work."

"Why?"

"Why does anyone need work?"

"Most people say *rent*. Or *family*. Something specific. You gave me a category."

For the first time, Dev's composure cost him visible effort. "There was an incident on the winter traverse," he said. "Not my fault, but my name was attached. References were... arranged, afterward, by people who owed me. I've been rebuilding since. This job pays enough to finish rebuilding." He met August's eyes steadily. "That's the specific answer. Is it good enough?"

It was a good answer. It was almost too good—an answer shaped precisely to fit the hole August had poked, delivered without hesitation. And yet it had the ring of truth in it somewhere, or at least the ring of a truth adjacent to whatever the actual truth was. August had spent six years learning to hear the difference between a lie and a managed truth, and Dev Okafor sounded managed, not false.

Or he sounded like a liar who had studied men who manage truths.

"Report Monday, six a.m., for packing," August said. "Mara Quist weighs every load. If she says it doesn't go, it doesn't go. You answer to her below tree line and to me above it. Any questions?"

"Just one." Dev gathered his plastic-sleeved résumé and tucked it away. "The client. What's she like?"

August frowned. "I haven't met her."

"You signed a contract without meeting the client?"

"The contract's with Vask. The client arrives when she arrives." He watched Dev absorb that, watch the small recalibration move through the man's face like weather across a valley. "Why do you ask?"

"Professional curiosity," Dev said smoothly. "It helps to know who I'm carrying for."

He left. August stood at the counter a long time after the bell over the door stopped swinging, looking at the résumé copy he'd kept, three references in three ranges, all glowing, all clean, all singing the same note like glasses rimmed by the same wet finger.

*Tomas will say turn him away,* he thought. *Mara will say keep him close where you can watch him.*

Both of them would be right, and neither of them would be able to prove anything, and that—that uncertainty sitting in his gut like a swallowed stone—was the price of doing business with Soren Vask's money. Everything Vask touched came pre-corroded. Even the help.

* * *

That night he laid it all out on the big table in the back of the post: the route maps with his proposed line inked in red, the manifest half-built in Mara's handwriting, Renny's food lists, the bank letter, the contract with its terrible generous numbers. Tomas sat across from him drinking tea he wasn't drinking and studying the red line the way a man studies an X-ray.

"Through the Col of Hanging Ice," Tomas said finally. "You drew us through the col."

"It's the safest line. Serac exposure, but short. Everything else funnels into the drainages Vask's people can reach by vehicle."

"You've thought about Vask's people."

"I've thought about everything." August tapped the map. "His letter said no questions. My conditions say my route. Those two things are going to collide, Tomas. I can feel it coming like weather over the ridge. He wants a crossing. I don't yet know what *he* wants crossed."

Tomas set down the untouched tea. For a while he said nothing, and the stove ticked, and outside the river kept up its brown argument with the rocks.

"When your father died," Tomas said at last, "you were nineteen, and you took the guiding work because it was the only thing that paid, and I told your mother you'd be fine because you had the two things a mountain man needs: judgment and luck. Judgment you were born with. Luck you spent." He stood, joints cracking, and put his hand flat on the red line, covering it. "This job, you spend the last of it. So listen to me. Watch the porter. Watch the client. Watch Vask. And when the moment comes—and it will come, August, it always comes—when you have to choose what you are, choose faster than you did last time."

He went home. August sat alone with the maps until the lamp burned low, running the line in his mind, pitch by pitch, ford by ford, the fragile machine of it—Mara's iron competence, Renny's stove-side genius, Dev's beautiful suspicious perfection, and somewhere ahead, unseen, a rich man's daughter arriving like a letter he hadn't opened yet.

Six people, three weeks, one range, and a hundred ways for it to come apart.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the river went on talking, and he fell asleep listening for footsteps that hadn't yet started up his stairs—but would, soon, because the message had come that afternoon from Vask's office, four words on a card:

*She comes Friday. Early.*
