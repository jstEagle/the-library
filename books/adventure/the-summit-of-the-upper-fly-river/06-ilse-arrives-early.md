## 6. Ilse Arrives Early

The plane came down through cloud the way her father's decisions always did—suddenly, without consultation, and with a great deal of noise.

Ilse Brandt watched the Kessik airstrip resolve out of the murk: a gravel scar, a tin-roofed hangar, a scatter of buildings pressed against the treeline as if the forest had been negotiating with them for a century and winning. Beyond the town the river ran wide and gray-green, braided with gravel bars, and beyond the river—when the cloud tore for a moment—the mountains stood up out of the earth like something that had been waiting for her specifically.

She had been told Friday. She had arrived Wednesday, on the chartered twin otter her father's office booked without asking whether she wanted the extra days, because Henrik Brandt did not believe in asking and Ilse had stopped believing in answering. The pilot said something about weather windows over his shoulder; she nodded at the back of his head and let him think she'd heard.

Her luggage was modest by her standards and obscene by anyone else's in this town: two expedition duffels her father's outfitter had packed in the capital, a leather case of documents she was not supposed to have, and the small hard-sided case she carried herself, always, never checked, never explained. The satchel was in there too, wrapped in a wool shirt. She had not opened it since the night she found it in the safe with her name written on the tag in her father's handwriting, which was somehow worse than if it had been anonymous. *For I., via V.* As if she were a routing instruction. As if she were freight.

The hangar smelled of diesel and cold canvas. A man from Vask's office met her—a smooth, forgettable man named Pell who shook her hand like he was checking it for rings—and told her the guide would collect her tomorrow morning, six o'clock, and that until then she should rest at the hotel and stay off the river road after dark.

"Is there something wrong with the river road?" she asked.

Pell smiled the way men smile when they've been paid to have no opinions. "It's just advice, Miss Brandt."

Everything in this country, she was beginning to understand, was just advice. Advice about routes. Advice about timing. Advice about what to pack and what to say and whom to trust, all of it arriving pre-loaded, none of it requiring her consent. Her father had built an empire on the principle that other people's judgment was a rounding error, and now she was standing in a diesel-smelling hangar at the edge of the map, being rounded.

She thanked him anyway. It cost nothing, and it kept her face doing what it was supposed to do.

* * *

The hotel was called the Landing, which was optimistic, since nothing landed there but floatplanes and rumors. Her room had a window over the river and a radiator that knocked like someone politely imprisoned inside it. She unpacked slowly, deliberately, making a ritual of it—boots by the door, layers folded by weight, the document case locked in the wardrobe under her spare sweater. Old habit. In her father's house you learned early that rooms were read like ledgers, and you arranged your things so they said what you wanted them to say.

What hers said now was: *a client. A rich man's daughter on a holiday trek. Harmless.*

It was the best cover story she had ever written, and she hated how well she knew the craft of it.

That evening she ate alone in the hotel dining room, under a mounted fish the size of a canoe, and listened. This was the other old habit, the useful one: sit where you can hear, say less than you know, let the room tell you what the paperwork won't. Two rangers drank coffee at the corner table and talked about an early snow line on the western passes. A freight agent argued on the phone about a delayed resupply for "the Vask party." And near the bar, a woman with sun-cracked hands and a guide's bearing mentioned—Ilse caught only the tail of it—a name.

"—Iversen's running it, apparently. Tomas signed the contract, but it's August up front."

"Iversen." The other voice dropped. "After what happened on the—"

"After what happened," the woman said, flat as a closing door, "he's still the best rope on this side of the range. You want sentimental or you want alive?"

Ilse turned her wineglass slowly by the stem and looked at nothing, and behind her eyes a filing cabinet opened.

She had found the scandal files the way she found most things—by accident, in a drawer that was supposed to be locked, three years ago, on a rainy Sunday when her father was in Zurich and the house was hers in the way a museum is yours. A folder labeled only *F.R. EXPEDITION — LEGAL*, thick with clippings and correspondence and a coroner's interim report that even her father's lawyers had flagged as too raw to keep copies of. Three climbers dead on an unnamed face above the Upper Fly. A guide who had turned back—or been ordered forward, depending on which deposition you believed—and a second guide, Soren Vask, whose testimony was immaculate, polished, and, to Ilse's careful eye, rehearsed.

And August Iversen, in the photographs: younger, leaner, a face like a closed fist, standing at a press conference he clearly wanted to die rather than attend. The caption in one clipping called him *the guide who quit*. Another called him *the guide who ran*. The legal summary, typed by someone in her father's own office, called him *discredited; no further engagement advisable*.

No further engagement advisable. Her father had put that phrase in a file and then, years later, hired the man anyway—to carry his daughter across the same range, carrying the same kind of secrets that had rotted the first expedition from the inside. There was a symmetry there that made her skin prickle. Either her father had forgotten, which was unlike him, or he remembered perfectly and considered Iversen's disgrace a feature: a blacklisted guide owed money, hungry, discreet by necessity, unlikely to ask questions because questions were what had destroyed him.

Or—and this was the thought she kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it—her father had chosen him precisely because Iversen had once refused to lie for Soren Vask, and might, given cause, refuse again.

She didn't know yet. That was the honest accounting, and she forced herself to make it every night before sleep: *you don't know. You have a folder full of other people's conclusions and a gut full of suspicion, and neither is evidence.* But she would watch. She had come early for exactly this—one unscripted day, one look at the man before the performance of the trek began, before everyone settled into their assigned roles.

At breakfast the next morning she asked the hotel keeper, casually, where a person might see the river at dawn. He pointed her toward the boatyard below the Landing, where the outfitting sheds stood on stilts over the water, and told her the guides were usually rigging by six.

* * *

She saw him before he saw her.

He came down the ramp from the shed with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a crate in his arms, moving with the unhurried economy of a body that had learned exactly what everything costs. He was taller than the photographs suggested, or perhaps the photographs had flattened him; there was a way he held his head slightly forward, reading the water, that no camera would bother with. He set the crate down, looked upstream at the sky, then downstream at the sky, then went back for another load, and in between these errands he spoke four words to a heavyset man with a ledger—Tomas, presumably—and those four words seemed to settle some question the ledger had raised.

This was the man from the files. Older. The fist of his face had been weathered open somewhat, but the knuckles of it were still there, around the mouth, between the brows. He wore his competence the way other men wore uniforms—not displayed, just on.

Ilse stood at the rail of the boatyard walkway with her coffee going cold and did the thing she had come to do: she watched him work.

It was not impressive in any way that could be photographed. He checked knots that were already sound. He re-seated a fuel can that wasn't leaking. He paused at the edge of the dock and studied the current for a long moment with an expression she recognized from her father's boardroom—the look of a man pricing risk—but when he turned away from the water his face emptied of it entirely, and that was the part that snagged her. Her father priced risk and then performed confidence. This man priced risk and then simply proceeded, as though the number had been paid somewhere private and needed no receipt.

"Miss Brandt."

Pell's voice, behind her, too loud for the hour. On the dock, Iversen's head came up.

She had planned this moment, and like most plans it survived contact for approximately one second. Because when he looked at her—when his gaze crossed the water and found her standing there in her town clothes among the stilted sheds—he did not look at her the way people looked at Henrik Brandt's daughter. No quick recalibration of posture, no arithmetic of wealth behind the eyes. He looked at her the way he'd looked at the sky: assessing conditions, expecting nothing.

Then recognition moved through his face like a shadow of a bird, and was gone before it landed.

He knew her. Not as Brandt's daughter—from the files. From the other side of the folder. She saw it happen, that flicker, and she understood that he had recognized *her* too, and that both of them were now pretending otherwise, and that this pretense was the first thing they would ever build together.

"Miss Brandt." He came up the ramp and offered a hand callused into topography. His grip was brief and dry. "August Iversen. We weren't expecting you until Friday."

"I like to arrive early," she said. "It lets me see what people are like before they're performing for me."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile; more like the memory of one, decommissioned. "And what am I like?"

"Efficient," she said. "You've loaded that dock twice as fast as your partner thinks is necessary, and you haven't once looked at my luggage, which tells me either you're uncurious or you already know what's in it."

"Both," he said, pleasantly, and she felt the small shock of a parry landing exactly where she'd aimed her thrust. "Your gear was manifested weeks ago. I don't need to look at bags. I need to look at weather, water, and people. Bags are the least of it."

"And what do I look like? As weather?"

He considered her honestly, which disarmed her more than flattery would have. "Fair this morning," he said. "Front expected later in the week. Could go either way."

Tomas appeared at his shoulder then, wiping his hands, wearing the expression of a man who has spent twenty years translating his partner's bluntness into hospitality. "Miss Brandt! Welcome to Kessik. You're ahead of schedule—we'll get you settled, there's a café two doors up that does real eggs, and—"

"I'd rather see the operation," Ilse said. "If Mr. Iversen can spare ten minutes."

"He's got a river run at seven," Tomas began.

"It's five past six," Iversen said. "Ten minutes."

They walked the length of the boatyard while Tomas hovered and then, wisely, withdrew. Iversen showed her the raft frames, the repair bench, the drying loft where ropes hung like kelp. He talked the way he worked—economically, without ornament—and she matched him, asking short questions and listening to whether the answers contained anything besides competence. Most men's answers did not. Hers, she knew, did not either, when she was performing. The trick was finding the seam where the performance ended.

"You've done the Upper Fly crossing before," she said, at the rail, looking at the water. Not a question. A placement of a piece.

"Once," he said. "Years ago."

"The route you're taking us—is it the same one?"

A pause. Small, but she had been trained in pauses by masters; she could weigh them to the gram. "No," he said. "Different line. Lower approach, higher exit. Why?"

"Curiosity." She turned to face him directly, and let a little of the mask slip—not much, a centimeter, enough to change the light. "My father chose you, Mr. Iversen. I'm told that's unusual. I like to understand unusual things."

"Do you." He looked at her, and for a moment the assessment ran both directions openly, two instruments pointed at each other, each reading the other's needle. What he saw, she suspected, was exactly what he'd decided to see: a rich man's daughter playing at adventure, asking clever questions she'd learned in boardrooms, dangerous only in the way luggage is dangerous—heavy, and someone else's problem. She could practically hear the category he was filing her under. It was almost comforting, how wrong he was. Almost insulting. She kept her face pleasant and let him keep it.

What she saw in him was harder to dismiss. She saw a man who had been ruined for refusing—or for obeying; the file genuinely did not make clear which, and that ambiguity was the most interesting document she'd ever read—and who had rebuilt himself into something with no soft surfaces left. She saw that he would take her across the range competently and ask nothing, and that if the mountain demanded a choice between her and the cargo, he had not yet decided which one she was.

"There's your run," she said, nodding at the clients gathering at the far dock, six tourists in rented waders looking like a shipment of something fragile.

"There's my run." He stepped back, and the transactional warmth returned to his voice like a lamp coming on. "Friday we start. Mara Quist will fit your pack tomorrow—she'll tell you what stays and what goes, and I'd take her word over mine. Eat everything Renny puts in front of you. Sleep as much as the nights allow."

"And after that?"

"After that," he said, "we walk."

He went down the ramp to his clients, and she stayed at the rail and watched him load them with the same unimpressive excellence, and felt the strange double vision of the week settle fully into place: she was traveling with a man her father's own files had buried, carrying documents her father needed hidden, toward a pass that the snow intended to close early, and every single person in this arrangement was lying to every other one—including, possibly, herself.

One thing she was sure of, watching him push off from the dock and take the raft out into the gray-green current with those four tourists trusting him absolutely: whatever August Iversen had done on that unnamed face years ago, he had not stopped paying for it, and he had not stopped being good. Both facts could be true. Both facts, she suspected, were the whole point of him.

She finished her cold coffee and went to write a letter she would not send, to a regulator she had not yet dared contact, beginning with the words *I may have found the man who can help us*—and stopping, pen hovering, because she did not yet know if that was hope or just another kind of risk she was pricing without a license.

Below her, the raft dwindled downriver. Above the eastern ridgeline, thin and high and wrong for the season, the first veil of cloud was already forming over the peaks.

Friday, he had said. They would walk on Friday.

The snow, she thought, watching that cloud, might have other plans.
