## 7. Gear, Lies, and Weight Limits

Mara Quist had weighed gear for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to read a manifest the way other people read faces. A manifest told you who a client really was. The honeymooners always over-packed on comfort items and under-packed on socks. The corporate retreat groups ordered satellite phones they didn't know how to operate and never touched once. The serious trekkers—rare, treasured—sent lists so lean they were almost ascetic, and Mara loved them for it.

The Brandt manifest was none of these things.

She stood in the back room of the Iversen outfitting shed at six in the morning, three days before departure, with the packing list spread on the scarred plywood table and the first grey light leaking through the window above the solvent shelf. She had read it four times. Each time, it got worse.

The list came on Brandt Mining letterhead, which was itself a small offense. Clients didn't send manifests on letterhead; they sent them scrawled on the backs of receipts or typed in emails with too many exclamation points. This one had arrived by courier, in a stiff envelope, signed at the bottom by a logistics officer whose name—P. Hessler—meant nothing to her and was clearly meant to mean nothing to anyone.

Mara ran her finger down the columns again, mouthing the items.

*Client equipment, personal:* standard. Boots, layers, sleeping bag rated to minus twenty, which was generous for the season but not alarming. *Crew equipment:* standard. Tents, rope, stove fuel, med kit—the med kit list was longer than usual, which she approved of, because Renny's cooking was a known cardiac variable.

Then the third column. *Expedition support, per contract addendum.*

Two satellite uplink cases, hard-shelled, twenty-two kilograms each. A field server. Four sealed steel drums, contents unspecified, forty kilograms each. A document case, locked, weight listed as "approx. 8 kg." A second document case, locked, "approx. 12 kg." And at the bottom, in smaller type, as though whoever had typed it hoped the font size would do the work the words couldn't: *Drums and cases to be handled by designated personnel only. No inspection required or authorized.*

No inspection required or authorized.

Mara set the list down and looked out the window at the yard, where the pack train stood hipshot in the corral, breathing steam into the cold. August had taken the contract against every instinct he had, and she had watched him do it—watched him sit across from Soren Vask in the Kessik hotel's dining room with the expression of a man signing his own subpoena, and then watched him come back to the shed and say, in the flat voice he used when he'd already decided and wanted to skip the argument, "We're doing it. My route, my rules, double pay."

She had taken the job for her own reasons, which she had not said aloud and did not intend to. Eleven years ago she had been the promising one, the young guide with the clean record and the sponsorships, until the Iversen disaster happened and every outfitter in the valley quietly stopped returning her calls. Guilt by rope team. Her name had been on the same permit as his. That was all it took. So when August had asked her—awkwardly, not meeting her eyes, as if he were doing her a favor by hiring her—she had said yes before he finished the sentence, and she still didn't know whether it was loyalty or the hope of clawing back something the scandal had taken. Both, probably. She'd stopped trying to separate them.

But she had not signed on to haul forty-kilogram drums she wasn't allowed to look inside.

She pulled on her jacket and went out into the yard.

* * *

The shed smelled of canvas, rope dressing, and the paraffin Renny insisted on for the tent seams. August was already there, which didn't surprise her; he slept the way cats did, in short suspicious intervals, and was always the first body moving in the morning. He stood at the gear racks with a grease pencil behind his ear, ticking items off a board.

"Manifest's a problem," Mara said, by way of good morning.

"Manifest's a manifest."

"August." She laid the paper on the trestle table and weighted its corners with four carabiners, a habit from windier offices. "Two uplink cases. A field server. Four sealed drums at forty kilos apiece. Two locked document cases. And a clause saying we can't open any of it." She watched his face while she said it, the way she'd learned to watch clients' faces at the moment they realized what they'd actually booked. "This isn't a sightseeing trek for a magnate's daughter. This is a freight run wearing a trek's clothes."

He didn't look at the list. That was the thing about August Iversen—when a fact was dangerous, he looked away from it, not toward it, as if attention were weight and he was rationing what he let the scales hold.

"Vask's terms said no questions," he said.

"Vask's terms said *you* don't ask *him* questions. There's a difference between not asking and being told you're not allowed to know what's on your own pack train." She tapped the drum line. "Forty kilos each. Four of them. That's a hundred and sixty kilos before we count the cases. Add the client, the crew kit, the food, the rope, the tents—we're over the horse limit for the lower trail by a good margin, and we haven't even talked about the porters."

"We'll take more porters."

"On a route where every extra body is another mouth and another liability above the tree line? You taught me that arithmetic, or someone with your face did."

That landed. She saw it land, a small flinch around his eyes, and she didn't enjoy it, but she didn't take it back either. There had been a time when she'd have softened it. That time had ended somewhere on a glacier eleven years ago, in a radio call she still heard some nights.

August finally came to the table. He read the manifest the way he read terrain—fast, then again slowly, then a third time with his finger on the numbers. She watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, watched him arrive at the same impossible sum.

"The drums are survey equipment," he said.

"Then why are they sealed?"

"Survey cores get sealed. Moisture."

"August." She kept her voice level. "I packed survey cores for the Kesselmann exploration in '09. They came in open crates with packing straw, and the geologists complained the whole trip because the straw got in their samples. Nobody seals steel drums for moisture. You seal drums for things you don't want smelled, or spilled, or seen."

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a horse shifted in the corral, and the sound of it seemed very loud.

"Re-weigh everything," he said at last. "Everything. I want real numbers, not manifest numbers. If we're over limit, we cut from the client comfort list and I'll take the complaint. But the drums and cases go."

"They go *unweighed*, you mean. The clause—"

"I know what the clause says." He said it too fast, and then, catching himself, slower: "Weigh them. Just—weigh them. Don't open them. Not yet."

*Not yet.* Mara picked up on it the way she picked up on weather. Not *never*. Not *that's an order, Mara*. *Not yet*, which meant August already suspected what she suspected, and was choosing, for now, not to know it in any way that could be repeated back to him.

"All right," she said. "But I'm logging the weights. Everything goes in the trip book. If a horse goes down in a river because we're over limit, I want it on record that I flagged it."

"Log it," he said. "You always do."

* * *

She spent the day with the spring scale and the trip book.

It was slow, unglamorous work, and it was the work she was best at. Mara had built her reputation—not the ruined one, the older, private one—on the belief that mountains didn't kill people; margins did. Every expedition that ever went wrong had, somewhere back in a warm room, a packing list that was quietly lying. So she weighed the rope, coil by coil. She weighed the tents, the stoves, the fuel cans, the food sacks Renny had labeled in his looping hand—*BEANS, DO NOT QUESTION*. She weighed the client gear that had arrived the previous evening in two enormous branded duffels that weighed less than they looked, which told her something about Ilse Brandt that the manifest never would: the daughter of a mining magnate packed like someone who had read a book about trekking and believed it.

The uplink cases she weighed last, or told herself she'd saved them for last because of logistics. Twenty-two kilograms each, exactly as listed, which bothered her more than if they'd been off. Exact weights meant someone had packed them on a scale, which meant someone had cared about the numbers, which meant the numbers mattered to someone. Sightseeing clients didn't care about exact weights. Couriers did.

She set one case on the trestle, turned it over, and looked at the underside. There was a plate there, riveted, with a serial number and a manufacturer's stamp she half recognized—communications hardware, the kind the mining companies used for remote-site data links, the kind that cost more than her truck. A field uplink wasn't for calling home. It was for pushing data—large amounts of it, fast, encrypted if you paid for the option. Nobody carried a field server and two uplink cases over a mountain range to send postcards.

She wrote the serial number in the trip book. Then, after a moment, she wrote it again on a separate page, the page she kept for things she wasn't ready to say out loud. She'd started that page years ago and had never shown it to anyone. It had exactly three entries on it before today. Now it had four.

The drums she weighed without touching the seals. Forty kilograms each, as listed, and when she rocked one gently on its base she heard the sound she had been dreading: not the slosh of liquid, not the rattle of empty space, but a dense, settled, solid weight, shifting with a slow dry grind. Cores. Sample cores in sealed tubes, or something packed to sit like them. Whatever was in the drums had been packed by professionals to survive rough handling and inspection, and had been sealed against exactly the kind of person Mara Quist was: the kind who noticed.

The document cases were the strangest. Eight kilograms and twelve. She hefted the twelve and felt the weight distributed wrong for paper—too dense, too even, the way a case felt when it was lined, or shielded, or filled with something that didn't want to be photographed through a bag. She set it down and found herself standing with her hands on her hips in the empty shed, listening to the wind work at the roof tin, feeling the particular loneliness of being the only person in a crew who had read the whole list.

Renny came in at midday, smelled the mood on her, and made her soup without being asked. He was a small round man who had cooked for expeditions on three ranges and had survived all of them by the simple method of never having an opinion until someone asked, and then having a very good one.

"Over limit?" he asked.

"By sixty kilos, minimum, if we take everything on that list. More if the client's duffels are anything like they look."

"Mm." He stirred the pot. "Vask's money's good, though."

"Vask's money's *heavy*, Renny. That's not the same as good."

He nodded as if she'd said something wise, which was his way of agreeing with things he'd already decided, and ladled her a second helping she hadn't asked for either. Before he left he paused at the door, and without turning around said, "In '09, on the Kesselmann run, the client who lied about his gear list was the one who drowned at the ford." Then he was gone, and Mara sat with her soup and understood that the cook had read the manifest too, and had drawn the same line she had, from the sealed drums to the water.

* * *

By late afternoon the yard was in order and the numbers were in the book, and Mara went looking for August and found him not in the shed but out behind the corral, standing at the fence with his back to her, looking at the Upper Fly country the way a man looks at a woman he wronged. The peaks were out for once—hard white against a hard blue sky, the range holding its breath before the equinox snows. Somewhere up there was the col they'd cross, and the shoulder beyond it, and the valleys where three climbers had died with August Iversen's name on the permit.

"Real numbers," she said, and handed him the trip book.

He read standing up, which she knew meant he was taking it seriously; August read bad news sitting down so he could put his head in his hands, and he didn't want to put his head in his hands in front of her.

"Sixty kilos," he said.

"Sixty-one, if we take the client's second duffel, which I recommend we don't."

"We cut the client's duffel. What else?"

"We don't cut anything else and stay legal. Which means the drums ride double-lashed on the strongest horses, and if we lose a horse at Kettle Ford—and August, at Kettle Ford we can lose a horse—we lose forty kilos of whatever it is, in the river, in front of the client."

He closed the book. For a while he didn't say anything, and the wind came down off the range and moved through the corral and the horses turned their rumps to it, all together, like a congregation.

"You weighed the drums," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I weighed everything. That's the job."

"Did you open one?"

"No." She let the pause stretch just long enough. "The clause says I can't. It doesn't say anything about what happens when the client asks what's in them. Because she will, August. They always ask. And when the daughter of Henrik Brandt asks me what we're hauling over a mountain in sealed drums that her father's logistics officer forbade us to inspect, I'd like to know what I'm supposed to say. Because right now the honest answer is *I don't know*, and the true answer is *something your father doesn't want seen*, and I can't say either one to a client on day one."

August looked at the mountains. When he spoke, his voice was low, almost to himself.

"Vask said no questions."

"Vask," Mara said, "is not on this trek. Vask is a signature on a check. We're the ones who carry it, and we're the ones who drown with it."

He turned then and looked at her, and for a second she saw something move behind his eyes that she recognized from eleven years ago—the look of a man doing arithmetic with lives, the arithmetic he swore he'd never do again. Then it closed over, the way water closes over a stone.

"Pack it as listed," he said. "Log the weights. And Mara—" he handed the book back, and his hand was steady, and his voice was not quite, "—keep that second page."

She stood at the fence after he'd gone, the trip book under her arm, the last light going orange on the icefields. In the shed the four sealed drums sat in a row in the corner like passengers waiting for a boat, patient, certain, heavier than any manifest admitted.

Three days to departure. She went back inside, opened the trip book to the page no one was supposed to see, and wrote, beneath the serial number, in her small even hand: *Whatever this is, we are carrying it over the Upper Fly. And someone went to a great deal of trouble to make sure we never ask what.*

Outside, the first wind of the coming front came down the valley, early, and cold, and wrong for the season.
