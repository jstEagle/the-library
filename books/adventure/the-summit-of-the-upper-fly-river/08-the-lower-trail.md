## 8. The Lower Trail

Dev Okafor had been on the trail three days before he understood that the hardest part of his job was not the lying.

The lying was easy. He'd learned that in Lagos, then in Fort McMurray, then in a windowless office above a dental clinic in Kessik where a man named Pell—soft-spoken, gray-cardiganed, with the patient hands of someone who had never lifted anything heavier than a pen—had explained the arrangement over lukewarm coffee. *You're not betraying anyone,* Pell had said, folding his glasses closed with one finger. *You're providing a service. Iversen's crew is carrying something my client needs to know about. That's all this is. Eyes and ears.* And Dev, who owed four months of rent and whose references—the beautiful, immaculate, too-clean references Pell had manufactured for him—were the only reason he was standing on a glacier trail at all, had nodded and said yes.

No. The hard part was Renny.

Renny sang while he cooked. Badly, cheerfully, songs from three countries and no discernible tradition, banging the dented pots of the mess tarp like a man conducting an orchestra that had wronged him personally. He fed Dev extra without being asked, sliding the heel of the flatbread onto his plate with a conspirator's wink, as if they were already friends, as if they had been friends for years, as if friendship were simply what happened when two men ate beans at altitude. On the second night he'd noticed Dev favoring his left shoulder under the porter frame—heavier load than anyone else's, because Mara Quist distributed weight by some private mathematics Dev hadn't yet cracked—and had wordlessly re-packed half of it into his own duffel while Dev was fetching water.

"You don't have to do that," Dev said.

"Course I don't." Renny grinned, gap-toothed, steam rising off him in the cold. "But you'll carry it anyway, so we may as well pretend it was your idea."

That was the hard part. Not the lying. The being liked.

* * *

He photographed the gear on the first morning, before the light was fully up, crouched behind the cook tent with his phone held low against his thigh like a man checking the time. It was a skill he'd gotten good at: the casual angle, the shutter click buried under some other noise. He shot the four sealed drums stacked by the horse line, their stenciled serial numbers half-obscured by frost. He shot the two aluminum cases Mara had locked to the pack saddle with her own carabiners, the ones labeled SATELLITE UPLINK in letters too official for a sightseeing trek. He shot August Iversen himself, standing apart from the others at the tree line, looking up at the mountain the way a man looks at a house he grew up in and burned down.

Then he walked forty minutes ahead of the column, alone, the way a good scout does, until the trail bent around a granite shoulder and the party vanished behind him. There, wedged in a crevice behind a lichen-crusted boulder exactly where the relay instructions said it would be, he found the beacon.

It was smaller than he expected—a matte-gray box the size of a paperback, solar strip along one edge, antenna flush to the casing. Someone had placed it well. Someone who knew this valley. Dev crouched, keyed the nine-digit code into its membrane keypad, and waited for the green blink that meant the buffer was open. Then he typed, in the compressed shorthand Pell had drilled into him over two evenings:

*DAY1. 6 PAX + 4 HORSE. DRUMS X4 SEALED, SERIALS ATTACHED. CASES X2 LOCKED BY GUIDE Q. IVersen LEADS PERSONAL. CLIENT B (F) FIT, QUIET, WATCHES EVERYTHING. ROUTE LOWER FLY N BANK, KETTLE FORD DAY3.*

He attached the photographs. The beacon's little transmitter drank them in with a soft pulse he felt more than heard, a tickle in his back teeth. Then he wiped the keypad with his sleeve, replaced the box in its crevice, stacked a few loose stones against the base the way they'd been stacked, and stood up with his heart going harder than the walk justified.

Behind him, far below, the column moved through the trees like a slow bright thread. Mara Quist's orange jacket. Renny's ridiculous red hat. Ilse Brandt walking with the careful economy of someone who had trained in secret and didn't want it noticed. August Iversen at the rear, always at the rear, watching the backtrail the way other men watched the road ahead.

Dev turned north and scouted on, and told himself what Pell had told him to tell himself: *You're not betraying anyone.*

* * *

The thing about the Lower Fly, Dev discovered, was that it did not care what anyone was carrying.

The trail climbed out of the river spruce in long switchbacks, gaining the benchlands where the glacial silt gave way to gravel and wind-stunted pine, and every hour the air got thinner and more honest. By the second afternoon they were walking through a country of pale stone and loud water, the river braided into a dozen silver channels below them, the icefields hanging above like weather that had decided to stay. Dev had carried loads in the oil sands, in the Chugach, on contract jobs in the Karakoram foothills where nobody asked names. He knew how to move under weight. But this place had a way of making weight feel like a moral condition. Everything up here was exactly what it appeared to be. Rock was rock. Ice was ice. Water was water, and cold, and fast.

Only the cargo lied.

He caught himself thinking about the drums at odd moments—at rest stops, when Mara checked the lashings; at night, when the horses shifted and one of them clinked. Four sealed drums, manifest listed as *fuel, aviation, 20L each*, except that aviation fuel didn't need drum liners, and fuel didn't get packed with desiccant bags, and fuel certainly didn't warrant the aluminum cases with the tamper seals that Mara Quist had weighed twice on packing day with an expression Dev had photographed too, from across the yard, because Pell paid bonuses for expressions.

*What's in them?* he had asked Pell, once, in the office above the dental clinic.

Pell had smiled the smile of a man declining a question he'd anticipated. "My client doesn't pay you to know things, Mr. Okafor. He pays you to see them."

And now Dev saw them, and the seeing sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't stop running: six people, four horses, one narrow season, and somewhere above them a pass that would close like a door. Whatever was in those drums, someone wanted it over the Upper Fly badly enough to buy a disgraced guide, fake a porter's résumé, and seed the valley with relay beacons. People did not spend that kind of money and care on things that were innocent. Dev had worked for enough careful men to know that the price of a secret was roughly proportional to the damage it could do.

He just hadn't expected to like the people carrying it.

* * *

On the third morning they came down to Kettle Ford, and Dev stopped thinking about anything but staying alive.

The river came out of the glacier in a single braided fury, gray-white with rock flour, loud enough that August had to shout the briefing twice. They crossed at the widest braid, where the current spread itself thin over a gravel fan, horses spaced ten meters apart, packs high-lashed, each person anchored to a rope trailed from the lead animal. Dev went third, behind Mara, and the water hit him at mid-thigh with a force that made the whole world feel like it was leaning. Cold wasn't the word. It was a hand closing around his legs, a pressure that wanted him folded and gone downstream. Halfway across, Renny's lead horse stumbled on a rolling cobble, the pack frame swung, and for one lurching second the whole crossing tilted toward disaster—

—and then August was there.

Dev would replay it later, frame by frame, because part of his job was noticing how people performed under stress, and what he saw was not performance at all. August moved upstream of the stumbling horse without appearing to hurry, took the trailing rope in a single wrap, set his boots wide, and simply became a fixed point. His voice carried over the water, low and unhurried, a string of small instructions—*ease her left, let the rope run, Renny, off-side, now*—and the crisis unbraided itself around him the way water parts at a stone. Thirty seconds later the horse stood on the far gravel, blowing, the pack intact, Renny laughing shakily and swearing with affection.

Ilse Brandt stood hip-deep at the exit channel, soaked to the waist, watching August with an expression Dev filed away carefully. It wasn't gratitude exactly. It was recalibration. A woman revising a number she'd assigned to a man.

Dev keyed nothing into the beacon about that. Some observations felt too much like theft.

* * *

That evening, camped on the bench above the ford with the fire low and the mountains going blue-black around them, Dev took his turn at the dish water and listened to the crew talk, which was another part of the job. Listening was the real work. Photographs were evidence; talk was architecture.

Mara was re-checking the lashings again, though the horses weren't going anywhere until dawn. She did that—touched everything twice, counted everything twice, a woman building certainty by hand. Renny was telling a story about a client on the Coppermine who had insisted on bringing a suit of armor, and whether or not it was true, it made Ilse laugh, an unguarded sound that surprised even her. August sat slightly outside the firelight, mending a strap, contributing nothing, missing nothing.

"Okafor," Mara said suddenly, across the fire. "Your references. Who was the outfit in the Karakoram?"

The question landed lightly and was not light at all. Dev kept scrubbing the pot. "Meridian High Latitudes. Out of Skardu originally, then a Canadian partner."

"Mm," said Mara. "I wrote them. Last week."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-valley, ice calved off a serac with a sound like a distant door.

"And?" Dev said.

"No reply yet." Mara looked at him across the flames, level, unhostile, the look of a woman who had decided to give a problem time to solve itself. "Mail's slow out there. I'm sure it'll come."

"I'm sure," Dev said, and smiled, and hated himself with a completeness that startled him.

Later, in his bag, staring at the roof of the tent where the wind made the fabric breathe, he ran the numbers again, the other arithmetic, the one Pell had built for him. Eighteen days to the far side. Payments staged at five checkpoints, the last and largest on delivery. Enough to clear the debts, enough to disappear properly afterward, enough to become someone new—which was, when he was honest, all Dev Okafor had ever wanted. To be someone new. The references were fake but the ambition wasn't.

Except that Renny had given him half a flatbread. Except that Mara had written to Skardu. Except that Ilse laughed like a woman hearing her own voice for the first time, and August Iversen stood in freezing rivers holding ropes for people who had hired him to fail.

Dev rolled onto his side. In the dark, very quietly, he rehearsed the coded message he would leave at tomorrow's beacon, the one Pell expected, the one that would keep the payments coming:

*DAY4. ALL NORMAL. CREW COHESIVE. NO SUSPICION.*

And then, because the wind was rising and the mountains were honest and he was tired of being the only liar among the rocks, he lay awake a long time composing the sentence he would probably never send, the true one, the one that began: *I think you should know what you're actually asking me to do to these people,* and ended nowhere at all.

Above the tents, past the black teeth of the ridge, the Upper Fly range stood under the early stars, white and enormous and waiting. Tomorrow they would turn up-valley for good, out of the gentle benchlands and into the country where the beacons grew scarce and the passes narrowed and whatever was in the drums would start to matter.

Dev slept at last, badly, and dreamed of water closing over his head, and woke once to hear, faint and impossible, the tick of a transmitter in his back teeth—though when he sat up and listened, there was only the river, and the wind, and far off on the southern bench, a light moving where no light should be.
