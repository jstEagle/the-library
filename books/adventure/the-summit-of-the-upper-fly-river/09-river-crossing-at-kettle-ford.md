## 9. River Crossing at Kettle Ford

The Kettle Ford announced itself an hour before they reached it—first as a change in the sound of the valley, a low grinding undertone beneath the birdsong, like millstones turning somewhere underground; then as a smell, cold and mineral, the breath of ice that had been dead for ten thousand years; and finally as a gray-white shimmer through the trees that made Mara Quist rein up her horse and say, quietly, "That's higher than it should be."

August had already seen it. He'd been reading the water since the second bend of the trail, watching the silt load in the side streams thicken from tea-colored to the color of wet cement, which meant the glacier above was melting hard, which meant the sun on the icefield was doing what late-summer sun did to the Upper Fly's feeder glaciers no matter what the forecasts promised. Every crossing on this route was a negotiation with a river that changed its terms hourly.

"Higher than what?" Ilse Brandt asked. She'd ridden up alongside Mara without being invited, which August had noticed she did often—not out of friendliness, he suspected, but because Mara answered questions with actual information instead of courtesies.

"Than the survey," Mara said. "Than my memory of it. Both."

"How bad?"

Mara glanced back toward August, at the rear of the column, and did not answer. That in itself was an answer, and August watched Ilse understand it—the small straightening of her spine, the way her chin lifted half an inch. She didn't like being managed. He'd catalogued that about her by the third day: the daughter of Henrik Brandt had been managed her whole life and had developed an allergy to it so acute that even kindness, if it came wrapped in condescension, made her flinty.

Good, August thought. Flint was useful. Spoiled would have been fatal up here.

He brought his horse forward along the line of pack animals, past Renny's mule train—six animals, loaded heavy because Renny never met a ration he didn't trust more than a forecast—and stopped where the trail broke from the trees onto the gravel flat.

The Kettle Ford spread before them, three hundred meters of braided channels separated by gravel bars, all of it moving fast and opaque with rock flour, the color of skim milk left too long in the sun. In low water you could wade it knee-deep on a clear line of cobbles the rangers marked with poles. The poles were gone. All of them. Which told August the water had been over the bars recently, and recently was the operative word, because the river was still rising—he could see it, actually see it, the leading edge of the day's melt pushing down from the icefield, wetting the gravel another hand-span up the bank while they stood there.

"We camp," he said. "Cross at first light."

Renny pulled up beside him, squinting at the water with the expression of a man appraising a rival cook. "It'll drop overnight."

"It'll drop some. Not enough. But we cross cold and rested or we don't cross." He turned in the saddle. "Dev—take the high line upstream, find me the last place the channels braid together. Count the channels. I want to know if it's four or six before dark."

Dev Okafor nodded and moved off, easy in the saddle, his camera swinging on its strap. August watched him go the way he watched everything Dev did now—with a mild, unplaceable itch he hadn't yet bothered to scratch. The man's references had been immaculate. Too immaculate. Nobody who'd portered the Selwyn Range for six seasons needed references at all; their hands showed it, their saddles showed it, the way they scanned terrain showed it. Dev's hands were soft as a clerk's. But he worked hard, asked good questions, and hadn't complained once about the weight distribution, which put him ahead of most hired muscle August had known.

The itch could wait. The river couldn't.

* * *

They camped on the bench above the flat, and August spent the evening doing what he'd spent twenty years doing: walking water. He went down alone after dinner, headlamp off, letting his eyes adjust to the last gray light, and read the ford the way Renny read a recipe—line by line, looking for what wasn't written down.

The main channel had cut hard against the far bank. That was new; the old guidebooks had it mid-channel. A fresh logjam had formed two hundred meters downstream, and when a jam formed below a ford, the water backed up and reorganized every channel above it. The safe line from five years ago was now a lie. Somewhere in that braided mess was a channel running thigh-deep and fast enough to take a loaded mule off its feet, and somewhere else was the line that would hold, and the difference between them was maybe forty meters of gravel bar.

He found it—or thought he found it—a diagonal seam where two currents met and cancelled each other, leaving a tongue of slower water between whitecapes. He paced it against landmarks: the split granite boulder on the near bank, the leaning spruce on the far side, a notch in the ridgeline beyond. Then he walked back to camp and drew it in his notebook by firelight, and made Mara look at it, and made her draw it herself from memory, because a plan one person understood was not a plan.

"You're worried," Mara said. Not a question.

"I'm respectful," August said. "There's a difference. Worried means you think something might go wrong. Respectful means you know the river doesn't care whether it does."

Behind them, at the edge of the firelight, Ilse Brandt sat on a rock with a map across her knees, and August realized she'd been listening. She didn't pretend otherwise. She just looked at him across the flames with that level gray gaze of hers, the one that took measurements, and said nothing, and went back to tracing the contour lines with one finger.

* * *

First light came up the color of pewter, cold enough to fog breath, and the river had dropped perhaps eight centimeters. Enough. Barely.

August ran the briefing the way he always did, short and flat, no drama. "One animal at a time on the line. Riders walk—no one crosses mounted. Renny leads his train; I lead the first mule and set the pace. If an animal goes down, you do not go in after it. You do not be a hero. Gear is replaceable." He looked at each of them in turn. "People are harder to replace. Questions?"

"What happens if someone does go in?" Ilse asked.

"Then everyone turns downstream and watches the water, and whoever sees the swimmer throws the throw-rope, and nobody enters the current. Glacial water takes the fight out of a strong man in ninety seconds. You can't fight it. You can only get out of it." He said it without emphasis, the way you'd recite a phone number, and saw Ilse register that—the flatness itself was information. This was a man who had learned these sentences the expensive way.

They rigged up. Renny's train went first, because August wanted the heaviest, most experienced stock setting the line and compacting the gravel for the lighter animals behind. Renny himself led the bell mule, a big dun named Cassius who had crossed more rivers than any three horses in the district, and the string followed nose-to-tail into the gray water.

For the first hundred meters it went like a lesson. The diagonal seam held exactly as drawn; the water ran mid-thigh on the mules, fast but honest, pushing at their flanks without cheating at their footing. August waded behind the third animal with the rope in his fists, reading each step, calling the line—"Left! Left of the white rock!"—and Renny echoed it forward, and the train threaded the seam like beads on a wire.

Ilse stood on the near bank with Dev and Mara, watching. August was aware of her the way you're aware of weather—not constantly, but continuously.

The trouble started at the last channel.

It was supposed to be the shallow one, the ankle-biter, the formality before the far bank. But the river had moved a gravel bar in the night—August saw it a half-second before Renny felt it, a smooth tongue of new deposit right where the line crossed, and the main current, cheated of its old bed, had doubled itself through the gap beside it. The water in the final channel wasn't thigh-deep anymore. It was waist-deep and angry, and Cassius hit it at the exact moment his hind feet came off the new bar.

The dun went down like a dropped sack.

Not dramatically—that was the horror of it. One moment the mule was walking; the next the river simply removed his legs from under him and he was swimming, rolling, the pack frame catching water and levering him sideways, and behind him the rest of the string piled into the same hole because that was what strings did, they followed the bell mule into hell if the bell mule led there. Renny went down too, tangled in the lead rope, and August heard him make a sound he'd only heard once before, on a different river, in a different life.

"Drop the ropes!" August roared, and was already moving.

He didn't think about it. Thinking was for afterward. His body did what twenty years had trained it to do: he angled upstream of the wreck, not down, because you never fought a drowning animal from below—you got above it and gave it something to lean on. He got a wrap around a boulder with the rope end, bit off the slack, and let the current pay the line out until it lay across Cassius's shoulder exactly where a swimmer's shoulder meets the neck, the one place pressure makes an animal turn its head instead of panic.

"Head up, Cassius. Head UP." He hauled. The dun's head came around, nostrils wide, eyes showing white, and for three endless seconds nothing happened except that August's boots began to slide on the cobble underfoot and the cold closed around his own waist like a fist.

Then the mule's feet found gravel. It was that simple and that violent—one instant of chaos, one instant of purchase—and the animal surged upright with the rope still across its shoulder, and August rode the surge backward, paying line, keeping the tension alive so the mule knew the anchor was real. "Renny. Renny, stand up. Stand UP."

Renny came up coughing, hatless, spectacles gone, clinging to the tail of the fourth mule, which had somehow kept its feet. The rest of the string, freed of the tangle, had found the shallows on their own and stood blowing and shivering in the eddy below the bar, packs awash but intact.

"On your feet. Walk them out. Downstream, angle with the current, let the eddy carry you." August's voice came out level, and he was distantly proud of that, because inside he was nineteen years old again and watching a rope come tight around a man who was already gone.

They got them out. All of it—six mules, Renny, every pack but one pannier of flour that the river accepted as toll. It took eleven minutes. It felt like a season.

When the last animal stood dripping on the far gravel, August became aware that his hands were shaking, fine and fast, adrenaline draining out through his fingertips. He knelt and checked Cassius's legs with his palms, feeling for heat and swelling, and found nothing worse than a scraped hock, and stayed kneeling a moment longer than the job required.

"Ilse." Mara's voice, sharp, from the near bank. "Ilse—"

He looked up. Ilse Brandt was in the water.

She'd entered at the seam without waiting for the ferry line to be re-rigged—wading, staff-less, dressed in her riding gear with the hem knotted up, following the exact line he'd called, left of the white rock, and she was halfway across before anyone could shout sense at her. The water hit her at mid-thigh and then at her hips and she leaned into it the way he'd told Renny's string to lean, angled upstream, weight low, taking each step only when the last one had proven solid. She was not graceful. She was not fast. Twice the current shoved her and twice she stopped entirely, both hands on her stick, and waited the current out like a tree waits out wind, and then she moved again.

She came out of the river on the far gravel thirty seconds later, soaked to the ribs, breathing hard, hair plastered to her face, and looked directly at August.

"The seam holds," she said. "In case you were going to test it yourself next."

Mara crossed with Dev on the re-rigged line, cursing under her breath the whole way, and the camp went up on the far bank in a cloud of steam and drying wool. Renny sat wrapped in blankets drinking the brandy he pretended he didn't carry, mourning his spectacles, and refused all sympathy with a wave of his burned-red hand. "River owes me a pair of glasses," he announced to no one. "I'm putting it on the invoice."

* * *

Evening found August repairing the wetted panniers by the fire, stitching a torn seam with waxed thread, when Ilse came and sat across from him. Not beside—across, with the fire between them, which he understood as a kind of courtesy. Or a kind of tactics.

"That was well done today," she said.

"It was badly done today. It nearly went wrong. Well done would have been boring."

"You said gear is replaceable. You went in anyway."

"I went in with a rope and an anchor and a plan. That's not courage, that's arithmetic." He pulled the stitch tight. "Courage is what people call it when the arithmetic goes bad."

She was quiet a moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope a ptarmigan muttered its idiot song into the dusk.

"My father has a file on you," she said.

August's needle paused, then resumed. "I imagine he has files on most people."

"He kept it in his study. Not the office—the study. Personal things live in the study." She watched him across the flames. "Three climbers. The Vask expedition, nine years ago. They blamed you in the inquiry. The permits board blacklisted you. And yet here you are, and the way you handled that river today—" she stopped herself, and he watched her decide to finish honestly, "—was not the work of a man who kills clients."

"No," August agreed. "It isn't."

"But it happened."

"It happened." He tied off the stitch and bit the thread and looked at her, really looked, for the first time since she'd arrived in Kessik with her father's money and her father's schedule and her father's reasons she clearly didn't share. Up close, tired, river-soaked, she looked less like cargo than she had on day one. She looked like someone carrying something heavy and pretending it was luggage.

"Ask me what you want to ask me," he said.

She blinked. Of all the responses available to her, he'd clearly chosen the one she hadn't rehearsed.

"Why do you do this work," she said finally. "If you're good enough to do it the way you did it today. Why hide in a border town running fishing parties for tourists who can't cast."

Because the mountains are the only place the ledger balances, he almost said. Because every crossing is a debt paid slowly. Because when the water takes something from you and gives it back, you learn what you're worth, and out there is the only place the number comes out honest.

"Because it's simple," he said instead. "Out here, the mountain tells you the truth about yourself whether you want it or not. In town, you can go years without hearing it."

"And what does the mountain tell you?"

He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "That I'm still afraid," he said. "Every time. And that I go anyway. That's the whole gospel. Fear, and going anyway, and counting everyone back at the end of the day." He glanced up. "Today I counted everyone. That's the only report card I care about."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—not softened, exactly. Recalibrated. The contempt she'd carried since Kessik, the polite armor of a woman who assumed every man she hired was for sale, developed a crack in it, and through the crack he could see her making a decision she hadn't planned to make yet.

"There are things in those packs," she said quietly, "that aren't camping equipment."

August's hands went still.

"I've watched the manifest. I've watched Dev photograph the loads when he thinks no one's looking—I don't know why he does it, but he does it every morning. And I know my father, Mr. Iversen. My father does not spend this much money to move his daughter across a mountain range. He moves documents across mountain ranges. Daughters are what you tell the guides."

The fire popped. Across the camp, Dev Okafor stood up from his bedroll, stretched elaborately, and wandered toward the horse line with his camera in his hand.

August looked at Ilse Brandt, and she looked back at him, and between them, unspoken, the whole shape of the job rearranged itself like channels in a flooding river.

"All right," he said. "Tell me what you know. Start at the beginning."

But even as she opened her mouth, he was watching Dev's silhouette against the last light, and the itch he'd been carrying since Kessik finally found its name.
