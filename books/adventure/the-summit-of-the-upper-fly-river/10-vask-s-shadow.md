## 10. Vask's Shadow

The valley on the far side of the divide was called Sorel by the old trappers, and Sorel Vask had always felt a proprietary affection for the coincidence. It was a narrow, gray-green corridor of spruce and slide alder running parallel to the Upper Fly, separated from the main route by a wall of ridgelines that no client would ever cross and no ranger bothered to patrol. From its floor you could not see the Fly. But you could hear it, on still evenings—a long white noise of water working through rock, like a room where someone was always whispering.

Vask stood at the mouth of a tributary draw with his binoculars raised and let the whisper fill his ears. Six days behind them now, maybe five if the weather held. Close enough.

"Radio," he said, and Pell, his radio man, handed over the set without looking up from the map he was annotating. Pell was a careful man. Vask had chosen him for that the way he'd once chosen rope for a fixed line—not for strength, but for the certainty that it would not stretch.

He keyed the frequency Dev Okafor had been given at the trailhead, coded twice over, buried in a band the rangers' scanners ignored. "Shadow to Lantern. Position."

Static, then the flat, unhurried voice he was paying four months of a porter's wages for. *Lantern. Day six. We made the ford at Kettle this morning. Iversen took the pack train across on the north line—high water, but he read it right. No losses. Camp is on the gravel bar below the moraine shoulder. Ilse Brandt is well. Renny's cooking something with onions, God help us all.*

"Morale?"

A pause, the length of a man deciding how honest to be. *Good. Better than good. Iversen's good. I'll say that straight, since you're paying for straight.*

Vask smiled at the gray wall of the valley. "You are indeed. And the satchel?"

*Still with her. She sleeps with it under her head like a dog with a bone. Mara Quist weighed the drums at the trailhead—I saw her do it, wrote the numbers on her wrist. She hasn't said anything to Iversen yet. She will.*

"She will," Vask agreed. "Keep your eyes. Next check at the tree line. Shadow out."

He handed the set back and stood a moment longer with the binoculars, though there was nothing in the draw but rock and the first gold of larch turning early. Behind him, his team of four was breaking camp with the economy of men who had done ugly work in beautiful places and no longer noticed the difference. Pell. The brothers Ansel and Rurik, ex-military, quiet, reliable up to a point that Vask had mapped precisely. And a young tracker named Foss who laughed too easily and would be the first to run if things went wrong. Vask kept him anyway. Every team needed a weathervane.

He turned back up the valley and began to walk.

* * *

People who had never met Soren Vask imagined, from his reputation, a certain kind of man—loud, grasping, a bully with money. The people who had met him usually revised the picture within the hour. He was not loud. He had never needed to be. Loudness was what men used when they had run out of patience, and Vask had made a career of never running out of patience. He was sixty-one years old, silver at the temples, straight-backed from thirty years of carrying his own pack when lesser expedition leaders rode horses, and he possessed the single most dangerous quality a man in the mountains could have: he remembered everything, and he forgave nothing, including himself.

Especially himself. Though he'd learned, long ago, that forgiveness and accountability were two different currencies, and only one of them bought anything.

The falsification had begun, as these things always do, with a small and reasonable lie.

It was eleven years ago. He'd been forty-nine, at the height of his reputation—the man who put up first ascents in the Sorels, who ran the Brandt account, who could get a drill rig's worth of equipment over passes that the government maps said didn't exist. Henrik Brandt had come to him with a survey problem. The Upper Fly drainage, where Brandt Mining wanted its next open pit, sat on top of an aquifer. The environmental filings required core samples and water-table readings from a dozen stations through the valley, and the preliminary numbers were bad. Not catastrophic-bad. Inconvenient-bad. The kind of bad that would add four years and forty million to the permitting timeline.

"We can resample in the spring," Brandt had said, in the boardroom with the river-stone fireplace, "when the water's lower. Vask, you know that drainage better than any man alive. You can tell me—is it really as bad as these kids in rubber boots say?"

And Vask, who had spent three weeks in that valley the previous autumn, who had seen the seeps running orange with iron leachate in August when they should have run clear, had weighed the question the way he weighed everything: against the mountain, against the money, against his own reflection.

"It's marginal," he'd said. "The readings are conservative. I'd want a second season of data before anyone panicked."

It was not even a lie, strictly. It was an opinion. It was the kind of thing a man could say and still look at himself in the mirror, and he had said it, and Brandt's people had heard what they wanted to hear, and the second season of data had been collected by a subcontractor whose lead hydrologist was on Brandt's payroll, and the numbers had come back clean, and the pit had been permitted, and Vask had taken his consulting fee—large, legal, and documented—and told himself the story that men in his position always tell: that the aquifer would probably hold, that the mitigation plan was probably adequate, that he was a mountain man, not a regulator, and it had not been his job to save the valley.

The aquifer had not held.

Three years ago the seeps below the pit had turned the color of rust and stayed that way. A herder's goats had died along the lower Fly. A junior researcher at the provincial institute had pulled the original preliminary cores—stored, as it happened, in a facility Vask himself had once helped fund—and found that they did not match the filed results. Not by a little. By the whole width of a valley.

And here was the thing that Vask understood and Henrik Brandt, in his fortress of lawyers, still did not fully grasp: the originals existed. Physical cores, in sealed tubes, with chain-of-custody tags. A field ledger in a dead hydrologist's handwriting, recovered from his widow's attic by someone—Vask had spent two years and a great deal of money trying to learn who. The documents had moved like a live coal from hand to hand, and eighteen months ago they had come to rest, according to his best intelligence, in the one place he could not buy, bully, or burgle: in the possession of Ilse Brandt, who had taken them from her father's own archive and told no one.

Not even her father. Especially not her father. That was the elegance of it. Brandt believed his daughter was being sent over the range for her safety, out of reach of investigators and journalists while he fought the inquiry in the courts. He had hired Vask to move her, and had entrusted to the expedition—through the back door, through a courier arrangement Vask had engineered himself—the very satchel of duplicates and supporting papers that Brandt wanted destroyed in the high country, where a crevasse or an avalanche would make a tidy, unprovable grave for them.

But the girl had the originals. Vask was nearly certain of it. And if the originals reached a regulator's hands, they would not merely embarrass Brandt. They would reach backward, through the subcontractor, through the second-season sampling, through the boardroom with the river-stone fireplace, to the man who had said *it's marginal*—and the man who had said it had signed the second-season report as consulting reviewer. His name was in the ledger margins. His initials were on the chain-of-custody transfer. Eleven years of reputation, of first ascents, of the Vask name on the good maps, hanging from a thread of ink in a dead man's handwriting.

So he was not, whatever his team might assume, chasing a rich man's daughter across a mountain range for money. The money was real—Brandt paid magnificently, and Vask had never been a man to refuse magnificence—but money was the least of it. He was chasing his own name. He was chasing the version of himself that existed in the public record, the one carved into the climbing world the way his routes were carved into the Sorels, and he intended to keep it there, intact, for as long as he drew breath.

A man could lose money and recover. A man could lose a client, a wife, a finger to frostbite, and recover. But a name, once it went into the water, did not come back out clean. Vask knew that better than anyone alive, because he had watched it happen to August Iversen.

* * *

That evening, camped in a stand of black spruce with the fire small and smokeless, Vask took out the thing he permitted himself to carry and no one else to see: a folded newspaper page, eleven years old, kept in a waxed pouch against the damp. He did not read it. He knew it by heart. He simply looked at it, the way other men looked at a photograph of the dead.

THE UPPER FLY DISASTER, the headline said. THREE LOST AS EXPEDITION OVERRULES GUIDE'S TURNBACK.

He had been there. That was the part the papers never got, the part the inquiry had skirted with the delicacy of men who did not want to look too closely at how the climbing world actually worked. He had been there, at the high camp, with the storm building in the west like a wall coming down a hallway, and August Iversen—thirty-four years old, the best mountain judgment Vask had ever seen in a younger man, and he had hated him for it, had hated him with a clean and private passion—had said the words: *We turn back. Now. This is not a discussion.*

And Vask had said, in front of the clients, in front of the sponsors, in front of the young climbers whose careers he controlled: "The window holds. Iversen's being conservative. We go for the summit at first light."

He had not ordered them to their deaths. That was the sentence he had rehearsed for eleven years, in the inquiry room and in the dark at three in the morning, and it was true in the way that all the most damaging sentences are true. He had not ordered. He had merely overruled. He had merely made it clear, with a smile and a shrug, what would happen to the career of a guide who turned a Vask expedition around on a maybe-storm. And the clients, hungry for the summit, hungry to be led, had voted with their boots, and three of them had gone into the white and not come back, and August Iversen had carried them down the mountain one at a time over two days of weather that would have killed a lesser man, and had come down into the valley with their names written on his face forever.

The inquiry had needed someone to blame. Vask had been careful, had been charming, had had lawyers, and had had, above all, the great good fortune that August Iversen stood in front of him like a wall—because Iversen had stood in that inquiry room and said, *I was the guide. It was my call. I let them vote, and I should not have. It's on me.*

He had lied to save Vask. Vask had understood it at the time and had never understood it since. Perhaps the man had been too broken to fight. Perhaps he'd believed, in some ruined and honorable corner of himself, that a guide's job was to absorb the fall. Whatever the reason, Iversen had taken the blacklist, the disgrace, the exile to river towns like Kessik, and Vask had walked out of the inquiry room into the sunlight with his name clean and his account book full, and had spent eleven years discovering that a clean name carried on a rotten foundation weighs more every year, like snow loading a slope that has not yet decided to slide.

He folded the newspaper away.

"Pell," he said. "Tomorrow's route."

Pell crawled over with the map. The Sorel valley climbed steadily for two more days to a high bench below the divide, where a pass—the Sorel Notch, on no map that mattered—let a fit party cross into the Fly drainage a day's march above the Col of Hanging Ice. If Iversen held his current pace, Vask's team could be waiting at the notch, or could drop down onto the Fly trail ahead of them, wearing whatever faces the situation required. Rangers. Rescue. Old friends of Iversen's, even. There were a dozen masks in Vask's kit and he had worn them all.

"Rurik says the girl's father is getting nervous," Pell offered. "Wants updates daily now."

"Brandt is always nervous. It's what made him rich." Vask traced the bench with a gloved finger. "He wants the documents and he wants his daughter, in that order, and he wants both without fingerprints. We will give him the first and decide about the second."

"And Iversen?"

Vask looked up. Pell was watching him with the careful blankness of a man who had heard stories. Everyone on the team had heard stories. The disaster was eleven years old and still traveled with both of them like a shared shadow.

"Iversen is a guide," Vask said. "He'll take the safe line, the standard line, the line that gets the client over and home. He's spent eleven years learning to be careful." He said it with something that was almost fondness, and was not. "Careful men are predictable. Predictable men are interceptable. We'll be at the notch in two days. Have Foss scout the far side tomorrow—alone. If there's any ranger presence, I want to know before we're committed."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then we cross, we set the cordon, and when the time comes, we offer August Iversen the same bargain we'd offer any careful man: walk away, and be paid, and be unnamed." Vask smiled, and it was the smile that clients had trusted on four continents, the smile that said the leader had the situation in hand. "He's had eleven years of being no one, Pell. I'm betting he's tired of it. Tired men take the money."

Pell nodded and rolled the map. Around them the spruce stood black against the last light, and somewhere over the divide, faint as memory, the Upper Fly went on whispering through its rocks—carrying, if you believed the old trappers, everything that ever fell into it down to the sea, and giving nothing back.

* * *

The radio crackled once more before he slept. Dev Okafor's voice, lower now, pitched for a different kind of message.

*Lantern to Shadow. One more thing. Mara Quist opened a drum tonight. Iversen was there. They were at it a long time. I couldn't get close. This morning Iversen and the Brandt woman talked alone for an hour. Something's changed in how they move—closer together, checking each other's gear. I don't think they trust the crew anymore.*

Vask lay back against his pack and looked up through the black lattice of branches at the first hard stars.

So. The drums were open, the girl and the guide were aligned, and the careful man had begun, at last, to be careless in exactly the way Vask needed him to be—because a man with a secret stops walking the safe line and starts walking the fast one.

"Two days," Vask said to the stars, softly, so that Pell, dozing by the fire, would not hear and wonder what the leader found so amusing. "Two days, August. And this time, when I overrule you, no one will be left to carry the blame down but you."

Over the divide, the mountain said nothing. It was very good at that.
