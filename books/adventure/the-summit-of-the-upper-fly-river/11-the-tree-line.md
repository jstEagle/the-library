## 11. The Tree Line

The forest changed before the map said it would.

Ilse noticed it first in the sound—the river's constant argument fading beneath a hush of needles, the way the horses' hooves stopped ringing on stone and began to sink into something soft and black and centuries deep. Then it was the light itself, going green-gold and vertical, falling in long slanted columns through spruce so tall she had to lean back in the saddle to find their crowns. Moss climbed the trunks to impossible heights. Somewhere above, wind moved through branches none of them could see, and the whole forest breathed with it, one vast slow lung.

*Hemlock and spruce to two thousand meters,* her father's field guides had said, back when she'd read them for pleasure instead of evidence. *Above that, the world simplifies.*

She had been reading everything lately as evidence. It was an occupational hazard of what she'd found in the filing room, and she was beginning to suspect it was also an occupational hazard of traveling with August Iversen—watching him, cataloguing him, testing each small observation against the scandal files she'd memorized in her father's study at three in the morning, four years ago, when she'd first understood that her father kept dossiers on men the way other men kept trophies.

"Ground's getting soft," Mara Quist called from the point. "Keep the string off the seeps. Water under snow-bridge crust even down here."

"I see it," August said.

He always said it that way—two words, no more, as if language were a rationed commodity he'd learned to stretch across bad years. Ilse had been counting his sentences the way she counted everything else. In six days on the trail he had spoken to her perhaps forty times, and half of those had been instructions: *Drink now. Check your girth. Step where I step.* He was not rude. That was the strange part. There was a courtesy in his economy, a kind of respect, as though he believed she could be trusted with silence—that she didn't need the constant patter of reassurance lesser guides used to paper over their own nerves.

Or he simply couldn't stand the sound of his own voice around a client. Around *her*. Around the daughter of Henrik Brandt, whose money had built the road they'd left behind at Kessik and half the mine tailings staining the rivers below.

The column wound upward through the trees. Renny's mule carried the kitchen panniers and complained about it musically, a low braying commentary that Dev Okafor answered with jokes in three languages. Dev was good that way—good at filling space, good at making Renny laugh, good at being exactly where work needed doing without being asked. Almost too good. Ilse had watched him once, early on the second day, standing at the edge of camp after everyone slept, holding his satellite phone up toward a notch in the ridge like a man offering incense to an indifferent god. She'd told herself it was nothing. A man checking weather. Everyone checked weather.

But she had also told herself, for twenty-six years, that her father's late nights were just business.

*You're becoming paranoid,* she admonished herself, and then: *No. You're becoming careful. There's a difference, and the difference is documents.*

The trail steepened. The horses blew and steamed. Above them, through a break in the canopy, she saw it—the line itself, abrupt as a drawn border. Below: forest, dense and dark and sheltering. Above: rock, scree, a paleness of lichen and snow-patch climbing toward peaks that still held last winter in their couloirs. No gentle transition, no struggling krummholz. The Upper Fly country did not negotiate its boundaries. It simply ended one world and began another.

They made camp at midafternoon in a hollow where a stream cut through the last of the timber, and Ilse stood at the tree line while the others worked, looking up at the bare country with an emotion she couldn't immediately name. It wasn't fear. It was closer to recognition—as if some part of her had always known the world got honest this high, stripped of cover, and had been waiting all her life to reach it.

"You'll want your shell layer before sundown."

August had come up beside her without a sound. For a big man he moved quietly; she'd noticed that too, added it to the file. Up close, in the flat green light, the lines around his eyes looked less like age than like topography—a face mapped by weather.

"Thank you," she said. And then, because she had promised herself she would not spend nine days in the mountains with this man and leave knowing nothing but his gear preferences, she said: "Can I ask you something?"

He didn't answer, which she took as permission.

"The Kesselhorn expedition." She watched his profile rather than his eyes; people gave more away in profile. "Four years ago. Three climbers died. You were the lead guide."

The change in him was not dramatic. That was what struck her most, afterward—how little happened. His breathing didn't alter. His hands, resting easy on the strap of his pack, didn't close. Only his gaze moved, from the high country to a middle distance somewhere between them, and the temperature of his attention dropped by degrees she could feel on her own skin.

"You've done your reading," he said.

"My father keeps files."

"I know. I've met your father."

Something passed behind his eyes then—not anger, something older and wearier, a door opening onto a dark room and closing again before she could see inside.

"They say you pushed for the summit too late in the day," she went on, because stopping now would have been worse than any answer. "That you ignored a forecast. That three people followed you into a storm because they trusted your judgment, and your judgment was—" She caught herself. Rephrased. "That's what the reports say."

"And what do you think?"

It was the first question he'd ever asked her. She felt it land like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to reach bottom.

"I think," she said slowly, "that reports are written by people who weren't there. I think my father taught me that without ever meaning to. I think a man who wanted only money wouldn't have set conditions the way you did—I heard you tell Vask your route or no route. And I think..." She hesitated on the last part, then committed, because hedging was her father's disease and she'd rather die of honesty than live with his. "I think you take us the long way around things. Every day. Extra distance, extra hours, terrain nobody would pick for speed. You're not avoiding the mountain, Mr. Iversen. You're avoiding something specific. And I'd guess it has names."

For a moment the forest held its breath with her. Wind came through the unseen crown of the hemlocks, and the great trunks groaned faintly, like ships at anchor.

"Three dead," August said at last. His voice was level, scraped clean of anything. "Aldous Wren. Petra Sandoval. Tomas Lindqvist's brother, Joren. Good climbers, all three. Better than me." He turned his head and looked at her directly, and she understood that the directness was the cruelty he was doing to himself, deliberately, the way a man presses on a bruise to keep himself honest. "My call. My route. My rope team. They died on my mountain, in my weather, following my judgment. That's the whole story. Case closed."

"That's not a story," Ilse said quietly. "That's a verdict. Someone handed you a verdict and you signed it."

Something flickered—there and gone, fast as a fish turning in dark water. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"No," she agreed. "I don't. Because you won't tell me. But I know verdicts, Mr. Iversen. I grew up in a house full of them. Verdicts are what powerful people write about inconvenient ones, and the convenient thing about a verdict is that it ends the questions." She held his gaze, her heart going hard against her ribs. "You signed yours very neatly. I just wonder who wrote it."

He looked at her for a long moment. The green light shifted; a cloud had crossed the sun somewhere above the canopy, and the whole forest dimmed as if a hand had passed over a lamp. When he spoke again, his voice had retreated to the guide's register, professional and closed.

"Your shell layer. Before sundown. Storm's coming out of the northwest—you can smell it if you try."

And he turned and walked back down toward camp, leaving her alone at the tree line with the honest country rising bare above her and her question hanging in the air like breath in cold.

* * *

The storm announced itself all evening before it arrived.

Mara smelled it first, naturally—or claimed to—and by the time the light failed there was a bruised look to the western sky, a colorless pressure building behind the peaks like water behind a dam. August doubled the guy-lines himself, checked every knot in camp, and stood for a long time facing the northwest with his collar up, reading the air the way Ilse read a balance sheet: line by line, hunting the discrepancy.

Renny fed them barley and dried elk and a sauce involving pine tips that should not have worked and did. Dev told a story about a porter strike in a range whose name Ilse didn't catch, doing all the voices, and even August's mouth moved at the corners, which Ilse suspected was the equivalent of another man's laughter. The fire burned small and disciplined. Everything in this crew was small and disciplined, she thought—rations, words, warmth—all of it measured out against a scarcity nobody named aloud.

She washed the pots with Renny because it was something to do with her hands, and because Renny talked, easily and endlessly, and talking to Renny was like warming yourself at someone else's fire.

"He doesn't hate you, you know," Renny said, apropos of nothing, scrubbing a pan with sand. "The boss."

"I hadn't assumed he did."

"You assumed something. Everybody assumes something. They assume he's cold, or broken, or guilty as sin—guiltiest man in the range, that's the popular one." Renny's hands kept working, cheerful and relentless. "Four years I've known him. He pulled me out of a permit office in Kessik when I was drinking myself into the river. Gave me a job nobody else would give me. Never once mentioned it after. That's the thing about August—he does the large kindnesses and then locks the door on them so nobody can thank him. Drives a person mad."

"What happened on the Kesselhorn?" Ilse asked, watching steam rise off the pot. "Really."

Renny's hands slowed. Just slightly. "You'd have to ask him."

"I did ask him."

"And?" Renny glanced up, and there was something shrewd in the round cook's face that the clowning usually hid. "What did he give you?"

"Three dead. His fault. Case closed."

Renny nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something. "That's the version he gives himself, love. Hasn't updated it since. Man's still running the same software he loaded the night they zipped those bags." She handed over the last pot. "Verdicts are comfortable. Terrible, but comfortable. You always know where you stand in one."

Ilse carried that back to her tent through the first spits of rain, and lay awake listening to the storm assemble itself in the dark—wind arriving in long surges through the dying forest, the canvas cracking like sails, the horses shifting and stamping. Once, between gusts, she heard footsteps pass her tent, unhurried, and knew without looking that August was walking the perimeter again, checking knots in the black, keeping his vigil over people he refused to let close enough to check on him.

*Who wrote your verdict?* she thought at the canvas ceiling. *Because I've seen my father write them. And they never read like the truth. They read like tidiness.*

Sleep took her finally in the smallest hours, and the storm came fully with the dawn.

* * *

It was Mara who woke them, hammering a fist on the tent pole, shouting over a wind that had torn the night apart. "Up! Everyone up! Snow line's dropping—we move in twenty minutes or we don't move at all!"

Ilse crawled out into a white roar. The temperature had fallen fifteen degrees in the night; her breath froze on her scarf. Snow was coming down thick and dry out of a sky the color of dishwater, already dusting the tents, already filling the tracks of yesterday. Above camp, the tree line—the clean, honest border she'd admired—had vanished entirely, erased behind a curtain of gray-white, and the high country beyond it had become a rumor.

August stood at the center of camp being lashed by it, motionless, studying the ridgeline with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Mara," he called. "How far to the hut?"

"Upper Fork shelter—two days, maybe three in this. After that, nothing till the Col."

"Weather window?"

"Closed." Mara's jaw was set. "And reopening sometime next week, if the front behaves. Which fronts don't."

Ilse watched August absorb this. Watched him turn, briefly, and find her in the chaos of breaking camp—not checking on her, exactly. Measuring. Weighing her the way he weighed weather, the way he weighed everything: against the cost of being wrong.

Then he raised his voice over the wind, and every word came out clipped and load-bearing, a sentence built to hold weight.

"We push for the hut today. Long day, hard trail, weather on our backs the whole way. Nobody straggles, nobody heroics. We make the shelter or we dig in—and I'd rather not dig in." His eyes swept the crew, and rested on her a half-second longer than the rest. "Miss Brandt. Can you walk twelve hours?"

Ilse looked past him, up to where the tree line had been, at the wall of white swallowing the world she'd come here to cross.

"Yes," she said.

He nodded once, already turning away—and it seemed to her, in that half-second before he did, that something in his face had shifted. Not softened. Recalibrated. As if her answer had been entered into a ledger he kept, and the entry had changed a number somewhere deep in the columns.

Behind them, the first true flakes of the storm came heavy and silent through the tops of the hemlocks, and the forest that had sheltered them began, branch by branch, to let the sky through.
