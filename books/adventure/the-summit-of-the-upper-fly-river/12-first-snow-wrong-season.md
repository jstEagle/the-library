## 12. First Snow, Wrong Season

Mara Quist woke to a sound she had not expected to hear for another eleven days: the soft, granular whisper of snow sliding off nylon.

She lay still in her bag for a moment, listening, the way an old sailor listens to rigging. Above her head the tent fly sagged under a weight that had not been there when she'd closed her eyes, and when she unzipped the door a hand's width, cold air poured in carrying that particular mineral smell—stone and ozone and something almost sweet, the smell of new snow on old rock. The world outside had been redrawn in white. Not much of it. A dusting, maybe two centimeters, enough to soften the scree and silver the dwarf pines along the creek, enough to make the whole basin look like a photograph of itself.

Enough to be wrong.

She sat up and checked her watch out of habit, though she already knew what it would say. September ninth. The forecast models Henrik Brandt's people had supplied—and August had grudgingly accepted, because there was no budget for a proper meteorological consult—put the first serious snowfall at the equinox, three weeks out, with the high passes holding clear until at least the twenty-fifth. Mara had looked at those forecasts the way she looked at any document produced by a mining company: with respect for the paper and none for the hands that held it. Even so, this was early even by her pessimism. This was the mountain clearing its throat.

She dressed quickly, pulled on her boots, and stepped out into the blue half-light before dawn.

The camp was quiet. Renny's cook tarp was still furled; Dev's tent was dark; Ilse Brandt's little dome stood neat as a folded letter. At the far end, August Iversen's shelter was empty, which meant he was already up, which meant he'd seen it too. She found him thirty meters below camp, standing at the edge of the moraine where the trail dropped toward the river flats, his back to her, watching the sky the way a man watches a door he expects trouble to come through.

The clouds were wrong. That was the thing she wanted to say and didn't yet know how to frame. They weren't the fat white cumulus of a fair-weather system burning off by noon. They were layered, gray-on-gray, moving in from the northwest with the flat, patient authority of weather that intended to stay. Behind them, higher up, the peaks of the divide wore fresh caps, and the light coming off them had that hard, metallic quality that made everything look like it had been machined rather than grown.

"Two centimeters," Mara said. "Maybe three on the north faces."

August didn't turn around. "I measured against my pole. Two and a half at camp."

"Forecast said nothing for nine days."

"Forecasts are opinions with graphs." He said it without humor. He turned then, and in the pre-dawn gray his face looked older than it had in Kessik, the lines around his eyes cut deeper, as if the altitude were carving him down to some more essential version of himself. "Snow line's dropped six hundred meters overnight. That's not a dusting passing through. That's a season arriving ahead of schedule."

Mara came to stand beside him. Below them the creek ran loud and white over its stones, the only color in a world gone monochrome. "Window's shrinking."

"Window's shrinking." He rubbed his jaw, and she heard the rasp of stubble against glove leather. "We're four days behind where I wanted to be. Kettle Ford cost us a day and a half. The landslide detour above the ford cost another. I'd built in slack for exactly this kind of thing, but the slack is spent."

"How much do we have left?"

He was quiet long enough that she counted his breaths—five, six—before he answered. "If the weather holds, we make the Col of Hanging Ice in five days, cross, and drop into the western valley with two days' margin. If we get one storm day, margin's gone. If we get two—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Every guide in the range carried the same arithmetic in their heads, the same ledger of dates and elevations and the bodies that had been found in spring when the melt gave back what winter had taken.

"We should push a longer day today," Mara said. "And tomorrow. Get below tree line on the far side before anything serious sets in."

"That's the plan." He said it flatly, and she heard what was underneath the flatness: the knowledge that plans were things mountains amused themselves with.

They stood together in silence while the light came up, slow and colorless, the way it does under cloud. Mara watched him from the corner of her eye. Eight years she'd known August Iversen, first as the name on other guides' lips—the man who walked three clients off the Upper Fly and walked himself home—then, after Kessik, as the quiet partner who took the low runs nobody wanted and never once asked for the high country back. She'd signed onto this expedition for reasons she hadn't said aloud to anyone, reasons that had less to do with double pay than with settling a debt she felt she owed a man who didn't know he was owed anything. And in eight years she had learned to read him the way she read weather: by pressure, by the small shifts others missed.

Something was pressing on him now. It had been since packing day in Kessik, and it wasn't the weather.

* * *

The chance came mid-morning, after they'd broken camp and gained the first bench above the creek, a long traverse through larches gone gold early, their needles drifting down through the new snow like small fires falling. August called a water halt at a seep where the ground stayed soft, and while Renny filled bottles and Dev checked the pack train's cinches and Ilse stood apart, as she always did, studying the route ahead with an attention Mara found increasingly interesting—August retreated behind a boulder with the satellite phone.

Mara gave him two minutes. Then she followed.

He heard her coming—of course he did; he always did—but he didn't stop what he was doing, which was staring at the phone's blank screen as if it might confess something.

"No signal?" she asked.

"Full bars." He pocketed the phone. "I'm deciding whether to use them."

"To call Vask?"

"To ask Vask why his client's cargo weighs four hundred kilos more than a sightseeing trek requires."

There it was. Mara leaned against the boulder, arms crossed, and let the silence work for her the way she let silence work on nervous clients. Around them the larches ticked and dripped. Somewhere above, a load of new snow released from a rock band and slid down with a long exhalation, and both of them tracked the sound automatically, professionally, before returning to each other.

"I've been meaning to have this conversation," Mara said.

"I know."

"You've been avoiding it."

"I've been hoping you'd be wrong." He turned to face her fully. Up close, in the flat light, she could see how tired he was—not trail-tired, which was clean, but something older, a fatigue he'd carried out of the high country eight years ago and never set down. "Tell me what you've got."

So she laid it out, the way she'd rehearsed it in her head through two sleepless nights and one long climb. "The manifest Vask's office sent lists standard expedition equipment. Tents, rope, stove fuel, food for twelve days, medical kit, repair kit. What actually got loaded at Kessik included three hard-sided cases with satellite uplink hardware—cases rated for electronics transport, padded, lockable, the kind of thing a survey crew uses to move data out of the field. Two sealed steel drums, fifty liters each, stenciled with a shipping code I traced to a Brandt subsidiary. And a courier satchel chained to the inside of the lead pack frame, which nobody has opened because the key went straight into Ilse Brandt's pocket and she hasn't taken it off her neck since."

"You went through the gear."

"I weighed the gear," Mara corrected. "In front of witnesses, during packing, because that's what a responsible guide does when a client's manifest doesn't match the load. I didn't open anything. But I did the math on the drums' weight distribution, and I talked to the freight handler who loaded them, and he told me they arrived at the Kessik depot sealed with mine-site security tape. Not office tape. Mine-site." She paused. "August. Sightseeing clients don't carry mine-site cargo over the divide. Nobody carries mine-site cargo over the divide unless they don't want it going through a checkpoint, a customs house, or a courtroom."

August looked past her, up the valley, at the white-dusted heights. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice he used on technical ground. "I told Vask at his hotel: my route, my rules, and nothing on my mountain that puts my crew at risk beyond what the mountain already does. He agreed. He shook my hand."

"Do you believe him?"

"I believe he wanted me to believe him." A muscle worked in his jaw. "That's different."

"So do we turn around?"

"Turn around on what grounds? A heavy manifest? I refuse a contract mid-range on suspicion, and every permit office in the province hears about it inside a week, and the last honest work I can get dries up too." He laughed once, short and without any pleasure in it. "Besides. You saw the same thing I saw at Kettle Ford. You saw what happened when that horse went down. If there's something in those drums that matters—if someone wants it badly enough to follow us over the divide—then turning around doesn't end the danger. It just concentrates it somewhere with fewer witnesses."

Mara studied him. "You've thought about this a lot."

"I've thought about nothing else since packing day." He pushed off the boulder and stood straight, and for a moment she saw the guide he must have been before—the one clients trusted with their lives, the one whose confidence had once looked like arrogance and now, stripped of everything, just looked like competence. "Open one."

She blinked. "What?"

"The drums. You've wanted to since Kessik. I'm giving you permission." He was already moving toward the pack line, unclipping the lead animal's panniers with quick, sure hands. "Not here. Up there—that overhang, fifty meters. Sheltered, sightlines both directions. If we're going to do this, we do it properly."

* * *

The overhang was a slab of gneiss tilted away from the valley, its underside dry despite the snowmelt seeping everywhere else, wide enough for the two of them and the drum and room to work. August hauled the drum up from the pannier himself—it weighed perhaps forty kilos, and he moved it with the controlled economy of a man who had learned never to waste strength at altitude—and set it on its rim in the gray light.

The security tape was exactly as the freight handler had described: black, embossed, BM-SECURITY repeating around the seal like a warning written in a language only lawyers spoke. August took out his knife, considered the tape for a moment, and then instead of cutting it, peeled it—slowly, carefully, keeping it whole in one long strip, which he rolled and tucked into his chest pocket.

"Evidence," he said, seeing her look.

"You think we'll need evidence."

"I think," August said quietly, "that a man who keeps the tape is a man who hasn't decided what kind of story he's in yet."

Then he broke the seal.

The lid came off with a sigh of cold air, air that smelled of machine oil and stone dust and something faintly chemical, and Mara leaned in beside him, and together they looked down into the drum.

The top layer was straw packing, the cheap kind, already flattened. Beneath it, nested in cardboard sleeves, lay cylinders of grey stone—rock cores, drilled and extruded, each one labeled in neat engineer's handwriting with depths and grid coordinates. Mara lifted one out. The core was maybe a meter long, split lengthwise so both faces showed, and she didn't need to be a geologist to read what it said. Dark bands of ore-grade mineralization ran through it like veins through marble. Rich. Very rich. Far richer, her instinct told her, than anything a company would want public knowledge of.

Under the cores, wrapped in oilcloth, was a second layer: aluminum sample tubes, dozens of them, each tagged and dated. And beneath those, at the bottom of the drum, a metal document case, the kind with a gasket seal, the kind built to survive immersion.

August lifted it out and set it on his knee. For a moment he didn't open it. His thumb rested on the latch, and Mara watched his face go through something complicated—not surprise, because she suspected he had stopped being capable of real surprise about Henrik Brandt's operations somewhere around the third discrepancy, but a grim, weary confirmation, the feeling of a diagnosis finally delivered.

"Open it," she said.

He opened it.

Inside, in waterproof sleeves, lay papers. Survey reports, field data sheets, laboratory analyses—a complete original record of an environmental assessment, the kind of document that by law existed in duplicate, one copy filed with the provincial regulator, one retained by the company. Except these were annotated. Corrections in red ink. Figures crossed out and replaced. Whole pages where a baseline reading had been struck through and a lower number substituted in a different hand, initialed at the bottom with initials Mara didn't recognize and one set she did, because Soren Vask's signature had been burned into her memory the day August had shown her the letter in Kessik, back when they'd both still believed this was a simple escort job.

A ledger, bound in gray board, sat at the bottom of the case. August opened it to the first page and went very still.

"What is it?" Mara asked.

He didn't answer immediately. He turned pages—slowly, deliberately, the way a man reads a verdict. Then he handed it to her without a word.

It was a reconciliation ledger. Downstream water quality readings, month by month, year by year, going back six years. In one column, the readings as filed with the government. In the facing column, the readings as recorded in the field. The filed numbers were beautiful—clean, compliant, comfortably within every limit. The field numbers were another story entirely. Arsenic. Heavy metals. Acidity spikes that coincided, month after month, with the dates of tailings pond releases. Someone had falsified six years of environmental monitoring, systematically, and someone had kept the proof, and that proof was sitting in a steel drum on the shoulder of the Upper Fly range, being carried over the divide by a disgraced guide and his crew under cover of a rich man's daughter's holiday trek.

Mara closed the ledger. Her hands were steady—she was proud of that—but her mouth had gone dry.

"They're not smuggling treasure," she said. "Or weapons. They're smuggling the evidence against themselves."

"Or evidence against someone else." August's voice was very quiet. "Think it through, Mara. Why would Brandt ship the originals out of reach? Because copies exist somewhere—in a regulator's file, in a whistleblower's hands, in a journalist's safe—and originals matter. Originals establish custody, chain of evidence, authenticity. Whoever holds these holds the truth in its only provable form." He looked up at her, and in the shadow of the overhang his eyes were the color of the slate around them. "Vask isn't paying us double to protect a girl from the weather. He's paying us to move the truth across a border before anyone can seize it. And I don't think he intends to give it to the regulators."

"You think he means to destroy it."

"I think men like Vask don't hire mules for documents they plan to surrender." August began repacking the case with the same unhurried precision, fitting the ledger back beneath the sample tubes, folding the oilcloth. "I think he needs it out of Kessik, out of reach of whatever investigation is closing in, and once it's over the divide and in his hands, it stops existing. And everyone who knows it existed—" He paused. He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Three climbers on a mountain eight years ago had finished that sentence for him once already.

Mara crouched beside him, helping seat the lid, and for a moment they worked in silence, two professionals sealing a secret back into its steel shell. Then she said, "Ilse."

"What about her?"

"She's not stupid. She grew up in that house. She took the key to the satchel and she hasn't let it leave her neck, and she watches the pack train like she's counting everything twice." Mara met his eyes. "Either she's guarding her father's cargo, or she's afraid of it. And I've watched her face when the drums get loaded and unloaded. It's not pride, August. It's fear."

August sat back on his heels. Outside the overhang, the snow had thickened without either of them noticing—fat flakes descending through windless air, silent, accumulating on the larch branches, on the pack tarps, on the world. The window was shrinking. The job had changed shape. Everything he'd agreed to in Kessik, all his conditions, his route, his rules, now rested on top of a lie he'd been paid well to carry.

"When do you tell her?" Mara asked.

"I don't know if I tell her anything." He rose, brushing snow from his knees. "She may already know. She may be the reason the cargo exists. Or she may be a hostage to it, the way her father uses her—as cover, as leverage, as a face too innocent to search. Until I know which, every word I give her is a word I might be giving to Brandt Mining."

"And until you decide, we keep carrying it."

"Until I decide, we keep carrying it." He said it heavily, like a man accepting a weight that had no business being on his back at this altitude. Then he paused, halfway out from under the overhang, and looked back at her, and something in his expression shifted—the weariness parting briefly, the way cloud parts, to show the man underneath. "Thank you. For weighing the gear. For making me look."

"Somebody had to. You were busy hoping."

That earned the ghost of a smile. "Go get Renny moving. We're making a long day of it. I want the pass approach by tomorrow night, and I want—" He stopped. His eyes had gone past her shoulder, up-valley, and fixed on something with a sudden, total attention that made the hair rise on Mara's neck.

She turned.

Halfway up the slope above camp, where the trail switchbacked through the gold larches, Ilse Brandt stood in the falling snow, motionless, watching them. How long she had been there was impossible to say. The snow had erased her footprints as fast as she'd made them. Her hands were buried deep in her coat pockets, her breath steaming, and across forty meters of white silence her eyes held August's with an expression Mara had never seen on a client's face—steady, searching, and unmistakably afraid.

She knew, Mara thought. Or she suspected. Or she had come looking for exactly this.

August straightened slowly, the document case's weight seeming to settle onto him even though it sat sealed in its drum ten feet away. He did not call out to her. She did not come down. For three long seconds the only sound was the snow falling on the larches, and then Ilse Brandt turned and walked up the trail toward camp, leaving prints that the weather was already forgiving, and August Iversen watched her go with the look of a man who has just realized that the hardest crossing of his expedition will not be made of ice.
