## 13. Terms Renegotiated at Altitude

The drums sat where Mara had left them, wedged against the rock wall of the overhang like something the mountain itself had coughed up and refused to swallow. August crouched beside them for a long time before he touched them again, the way a man might circle a wound before deciding whether to probe it.

He had opened one at first light, when the others were still zipped into their bags and the glacier below the moraine was doing its slow, groaning work of settling. Survey cores, wax-sealed and numbered in a hand he didn't recognize. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger—cheap paper, cheap binding, the kind of book that wasn't meant to survive. Readings in columns. Dates going back six years. He'd read enough of it to understand what it was: the true record, kept by someone inside Brandt Mining who had measured what came out of the tailings ponds and written down the numbers that never made it into the filings.

Someone had died keeping this book, or been paid enough not to care. Either way, it was sitting under a tarp on his mountain, in his care, and every hour it traveled higher was an hour it became more dangerous.

He heard Ilse's boots on the scree before he saw her. She moved better than she had two weeks ago—she'd learned to place her feet, to read the ground—but she still came around the corner of the overhang with her hood back and her breath steaming, and she still looked at him the way she did, direct as a level bubble, as if she were measuring him for something.

"You're up early," she said.

"So are you."

"I slept badly." She glanced at the drums, then back at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic—the tarp pulled back, the open drum, the ledger lying in the dirt where he'd set it. He watched her decide not to pretend. "That's why you've been watching me all morning," she said. "Since Kettle Ford, maybe. Since the tree line. You found something."

August stood. His knees cracked in the cold. Below them the valley fell away in long gray steps, and above, the col they had to cross tomorrow was hidden behind a shoulder of cloud that hadn't moved all night.

"Sit down," he said.

* * *

They sat on opposite sides of a flat boulder with the stove between them, and he poured her tea because his hands needed something to do while he decided how much truth a man could hand across a rock in the high country without it becoming a weapon.

"Your father's filings," he said. "For the Upper Fly operation. Water quality, discharge volumes, reclamation schedules. I've seen copies—Tomas keeps friends in the permit office, and after what happened to me I learned to read other people's paperwork the way most men read weather." He turned the tin cup slowly on the stone. "The numbers in those filings don't match the numbers in this book. Not by a little. By orders of magnitude. Six years of discharge readings falsified, and the survey cores prove the ground contamination goes deeper than anything your father's engineers ever reported."

Ilse didn't flinch. That was the first thing he noticed. The second thing was that she wasn't surprised.

"I know," she said.

August set the cup down. "You know."

"I suspected. There's a difference, and I've had three weeks on this trail to let suspicion harden into knowledge, so let's call it knowledge now." She pulled her gloves off finger by finger, deliberately, the way her father probably did in boardrooms. "I work in the environmental compliance office. Did work. My job was to sign off on quarterly reports, and for two years I signed whatever was put in front of me because the alternative was asking questions, and the last person who asked questions was reassigned to a coastal office so remote it might as well have been underwater. Then I started pulling the raw sensor data myself. Comparing it to what went into the reports." She looked out at the cloud-wrapped col. "The gap wasn't subtle, Mr. Iversen. It was architectural. Someone built the falsification the way you'd build a bridge—to carry weight, to last."

"And your father."

"My father commissioned the bridge." She said it flatly, without heat, and somehow that was worse than anger. "I confronted him in March. Politely. Family language—we speak in implications, he and I. He told me the regulators were hostile and the numbers were defensible and that I should take a holiday. A trek, he said. You used to love the mountains. Get away from the office, clear your head." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He booked it himself. Chose the route. Chose the timing—before equinox snows, when the passes close and nothing moves in or out for months. Chose the crew." Her eyes came back to him. "Chose you, specifically, through Soren Vask."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool.

"Vask came to me with double pay and no questions," August said. "I should have known no questions was the whole contract."

"He needs you because you're invisible. A blacklisted guide running an unregistered crossing—that's a route with no paper trail. Whatever crosses the range with us doesn't exist until it comes down the far side, and by then—" She stopped herself. "What's in the drums, exactly?"

"Cores. And a ledger of real readings. Originals, from the look of the handwriting—field notes, not copies." He watched her face as he said it. "You didn't know they were in your gear."

"No." Something moved behind her eyes then, the first honest crack in the composure. "But I knew *something* was. Dev checks our cargo every night. I've watched him do it—he thinks nobody notices, but he photographs the sealed cases with his phone when he thinks we're asleep, and yesterday he spent twenty minutes weighing the drums and muttering. And Vask has a second team somewhere behind us—I've seen their smoke twice, in the parallel valley, and Dev always seems to know exactly where we are when he 'checks' the beacon." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "I'm not stupid, Mr. Iversen. I've just been alone. There's a difference too."

* * *

The wind picked up through the middle of the day, funneling down from the col with that thin whine that meant weather was stacking up behind the peaks. They struck camp and gained the shelf above the moraine, and August called an early halt—not because the light demanded it, but because some conversations shouldn't happen within earshot of five other people, one of whom photographed cargo in the dark.

He and Ilse walked ahead along the shelf, far enough that the crew became small figures behind them, Renny limping with his splinted wrist, Mara bringing up the rear with her eyes working the ridgelines the way she always did now, since the shots fired into the scree.

"Here's what I can't reconcile," August said, when they'd stopped in the lee of a granite fin. "If your father wanted the evidence destroyed, he could burn it in an office basement. If he wanted it moved, there are roads, aircraft, couriers who don't ask questions and don't charge double. Instead he puts it on the backs of pack horses, over a pass that kills people, guarded by a man he knows is compromised and a crew with a traitor in it. That's not transport. That's a deniable loss. The mountain eats the cargo, the mountain eats the witnesses, and Henrik Brandt grieves publicly for his daughter lost on an adventure holiday."

"Yes," Ilse said. "I worked all of that out around the second river crossing. Which is why I haven't slept properly since Kettle Ford either." She turned to face him fully, and the wind pushed her hair sideways and she didn't bother with it. "So here are my terms, guide. Not my father's terms. Mine."

"Go on."

"The originals go to the regional regulators' office in Calloway. On the far side. Their jurisdiction ends at the divide—my father's lawyers own every court on this side of the range, every filing clerk, half the press. But Calloway is federal. An injunction filed there freezes the mine's expansion permits within the week. I have contacts—a professor, two former colleagues, one very stubborn journalist. What I don't have is a way across the range that isn't controlled by my father or by Vask." She held his gaze. "Until I hired one. Or rather, until my father hired one for me, which may be the only generous thing he's ever done."

"You're asking me to finish the job," August said slowly, "but deliver it backwards."

"I'm asking you to do what you already agreed to do—get us over the Upper Fly before the snows. The only thing that changes is whose side you're standing on when we come down the other side." She paused. "And I know what I'm asking. I read the file, Mr. Iversen. My father keeps files on everyone useful, and yours was thick. Three climbers dead on the Serac Route. Your signature on the summit decision. A blacklist that followed you into towns so small they don't appear on maps. You have every reason to want this cargo to fall into a crevasse and every reason to walk off this mountain tomorrow and let the Brandts destroy each other."

The wind screamed over the fin above them. August looked past her, down the valley, to where a thread of smoke rose in the parallel drainage—Vask's team, patient as a spider.

"Vask ordered that summit push," he said. The words came out of him like something extracted. "The weather window was closing. I wanted to turn back. He told the clients the route was good, told me the sponsors were watching, and three men who trusted both of us walked up into a storm because we were too proud and too paid to say stop." He looked at her. "I've spent eight years being invisible because being visible got people killed. And now you're asking me to carry a fight up a mountain, on purpose, with my name attached."

"I'm asking you to be seen," Ilse said quietly. "Once. For something that matters."

* * *

They walked back to camp as the light went copper and then gray, and August did not give her an answer, because he was still building it. At the fire he checked the rope coils, checked Renny's wrist, checked the sky, and did all the small honest work of a guide while the large dishonest work of the world rearranged itself in his chest.

It was Mara who forced the issue, in her way. She came and sat beside him at the edge of the firelight with her mug and said, without preamble: "Whatever's in those drums, it's heavier than the manifest says. And whatever you two negotiated on the shelf, it changes the weight distribution of this entire expedition. So either tell me, or I start making decisions based on what I can guess, and my guesses are getting ugly."

August looked at the fire. Looked at Ilse, sitting across the flames, watching him with that level, unflinching gaze. Looked at Dev, at the edge of the group, too clean, too quiet, his phone presumably sleeping in his pocket full of everything he'd photographed.

"Tomorrow we cross the col," August said. "After that, four days of high country with no descent options and Vask somewhere behind us. Nobody's making any decisions tonight except about sleep." He stood, and found Ilse's eyes once more across the fire. "But for the record—since everyone's guessing anyway—the cargo goes to Calloway. All of it. We finish the crossing, and we finish it as the crew we are, and anyone who has a problem with that destination has until first light to say so out loud."

The silence that followed was long enough to hear the glacier groan. Dev looked at the fire. Renny looked at his bandaged wrist. Mara smiled, slow and wolfish, and raised her mug a centimeter.

Ilse held August's gaze, and something passed between them that was not trust yet—it was older than trust, harder, the thing trust gets built out of when two people discover they're standing on the same narrow ground with the same drop on either side.

Then, from the darkness beyond the firelight, where the horses were picketed, came the sound of a hoof striking stone—and Dev was on his feet, moving toward it faster than curiosity required, his hand already reaching for his pocket.
