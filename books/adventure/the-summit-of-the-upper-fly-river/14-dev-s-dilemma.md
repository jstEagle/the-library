## 14. Dev's Dilemma

The hoof-strike was nothing. A horse shifting its weight against the picket line, annoyed by a stone under its foreleg or a moth at its ear. Dev knew that before he reached them—knew it in the way the animals stood hipshot and drowsy in the cold, steam rising off their backs like breath from sleeping men—but he went through the motions anyway, running his hand along a bay's neck, checking the hobble knots, making himself look like exactly what he was supposed to be: a careful porter doing careful work.

Only when he had his back to the fire did he take out the beacon.

It sat in his palm no bigger than a cigarette case, matte black, warm from his body heat. He'd kept it against his skin since Kessik because cold killed batteries and because a thing this small could be lost so easily—a pocket seam giving way on a scree slope, a river crossing, one bad stumble—and then what would he tell them? What story would cover the absence of a device that had never appeared on any manifest?

He thumbed the receive key. The screen glowed green in the dark of his cupped hands, and there it was, the message that had been waiting since noon:

*POSITION CONFIRMED. HOLD PARTY AT CURRENT CAMP 36 HRS MINIMUM. DISABLE COMMS. V.*

Thirty-six hours. He did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you can't stop your tongue finding a broken tooth. Thirty-six hours meant the storm front building over the western peaks would arrive while they sat here. It meant Renny's wrist—one more day of swelling, one more night without proper rest—would go from bad to worse. It meant Vask's second team, moving light and fast down the parallel valley with radios and rope and whatever else money bought, would close the gap that August Iversen's route discipline had spent nine days opening.

And *disable comms*. Which meant the satphone. The one lifeline to weather forecasts, to rescue coordination, to the ranger station at the valley mouth that existed for exactly the kind of situation Vask was engineering.

Dev stood in the horse-smell and the cold and looked at the little black case in his hand, and felt the specific loneliness of a man standing between two fires, both of them burning him.

He'd taken the job in a hotel bar in Kessik, eleven weeks ago, from a man named Corrigan who wore good boots and paid cash and never once said Brandt or Vask aloud. The terms had been simple: join the crew as a porter, photograph anything unusual in the gear, report positions daily through the relay beacon, and collect the balance of a sum that would have bought Dev's mother a house with a roof that didn't leak and a road that didn't turn to soup every rainy season. Simple. Clean. The kind of work that happened everywhere, all the time, in every industry, and that nobody ever called betrayal because nobody ever said the word where the money could hear it.

He'd told himself it was surveillance, not sabotage. Eyes, not hands. That if it ever came to something real—someone in danger, someone bleeding—he'd be a porter first and a spy never.

That had lasted until Kettle Ford, when the river took Renny's lead packhorse sideways and August went into the water without hesitating, without even taking off his jacket, and came up hauling the animal's cheek strap in one fist and the pack frame in the other, and the whole crossing reorganized itself around him the way water reorganizes around a stone. Dev had watched from the bank with a rope coil in his hands he hadn't needed to use, and understood that he was not watching a disgraced guide scraping out a living. He was watching the best mountain man he had ever seen do the most ordinary thing in the world as if it were sacred.

After that it got harder. After that, Renny started saving him the burnt-edge pieces of flatbread because Dev had mentioned once, drunk on altitude and tiredness, that his mother used to cut the burnt edges off and eat them herself so the kids wouldn't have to. After that, Mara started teaching him knots—not condescendingly, not as a test, but because she'd noticed his anchor hitches were sloppy and sloppy anchors kill people, and Mara Quist did not let people she liked carry sloppy anchors. After that, Ilse Brandt, who was supposed to be a spoiled heiress being escorted across a range like luggage, had knelt in the mud at the tree-line camp and helped him re-seat a pack frame rail for forty minutes without complaining once, her hands blistered, asking real questions about how the load should sit.

And after that—the worst after that—August himself, two nights ago, sitting beside him at the fire while the others slept, saying quietly: *You check those pickets better than anyone I've hired. Where'd you learn stock?*

Nowhere, Dev had said. Just picked it up.

August had nodded, accepting the lie the way you accept weather, and Dev had lain awake half the night feeling the beacon against his ribs like a second heart, a black-hearted twin beating out of time with his own.

* * *

He put the beacon away and went back toward the fire, and found Ilse awake, wrapped in her bag with just her head showing, watching him come.

"Everything all right?" she asked.

"Horse was fussing." He held up his hands, empty, palms out—an instinctive gesture he hated himself for. "They spook easy above the trees."

"Mm," she said, and closed her eyes, and he walked past her to his own bedroll knowing—knowing—that she hadn't believed him either. She'd been watching him too. Everyone watched everyone now; the shots fired into the scree three days ago had seen to that. Trust on this mountain had become a currency in hyperinflation: everyone holding it, nobody sure what it bought.

He lay down and stared at the underside of the fly sheet, where the firelight made slow orange shadows, and ran the options the way Mara had taught him to run a rope—hand over hand, feel every inch, find the weak spot before it finds you.

Option one: obey. Cut the satphone's antenna wire tonight—it was a two-minute job, the phone lived in the equipment barrel ten feet from his head—or simpler still, drop it in the river tomorrow at the ford they'd cross by midmorning. Accidents happen. Phones die. Then the party sits out the storm front, thirty-six hours becomes fifty, Vask's team arrives wearing friendly faces and official-sounding radio calls, and Dev collects his money in some border town while these five people learn what it costs to be delivered into Henrik Brandt's version of a rescue.

Option two: refuse. Tell August everything, tonight, right now, wake him up and put the beacon on the ground between them and say *this is who I am, this is what I've done.* And then? August was not a forgiving man—everyone in the trade knew the stories, and Dev had read enough between the lines in nine days to know the stories were true. August would strip his gear, take back every gram of shared weight, and put him off the team wherever they stood, which was a day below the Col of Hanging Ice, which might as well be the moon. Or worse—August would keep him on, watchful, and the mission would bend around his presence like a stream bending around a deadfall, and when Vask's interception came anyway, Dev would be the reason it worked.

Option three—

Option three was the one his hands had already thought of, somewhere below the level of his conscience, the way hands know things before heads do. He'd felt it forming all evening, during dinner, during the silence after Ilse and August's quiet war of eyes across the fire. During the hoof-strike he'd invented an excuse to answer.

Loosen, don't cut. Slow, don't stop. Buy hours, not days—and give himself one more night, maybe two, to decide whose side the hours belonged to.

It wasn't courage. He knew that. It was the coward's compromise, the bought man's little tax paid to his own self-respect, a coin dropped in the tray so the account didn't show entirely red. But it was also the only move that kept every door open, and Dev Okafor had grown up in a house where keeping doors open was survival, where his mother locked nothing and burned nothing and threw nothing away because the world could change overnight and the thing you discarded on Tuesday was the thing you needed on Thursday.

He waited until the fire burned down to coals. He waited until Mara's breathing went long and even, until Renny stopped shifting, until even the horses stood asleep. Then he rose, silent in his felt-soled camp boots, and crossed to the equipment barrel.

The satphone lay in its padded sleeve under a coil of spare cord, exactly where the manifest said. Dev lifted it out. It weighed almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it—everything it carried, every voice it could summon, every helicopter it could call down out of the sky, and it weighed less than a mug.

He turned it over. The charging port, the antenna nub, the battery door with its tiny screw. There were a hundred ways to kill a phone and ninety of them left evidence. He'd thought about this for days, in the boring hours on trail, the way you worry a loose tooth.

What he finally did was almost nothing. He opened the battery door, worked the contact clip a quarter-millimeter off its seat with his thumbnail—so slightly that a casual inspection would see nothing, so slightly that the phone would power on, would even appear to charge, and then die within minutes of actual use. Intermittent failure. The worst kind, the kind that reads as bad luck, bad batch, bad cold. And then, because a dead phone alone wouldn't hold them—he went to the fuel cache, found the white-gas bottle rigged to the stove line, and backed off the hose clamp at the fitting two turns. Not off. Two turns. Enough that vibration on tomorrow's climb would weep fuel slowly into the fitting, enough that by afternoon the stove would sputter and starve and need cleaning, need rebuilding, need the kind of fiddling that eats an hour, then another. A stove problem on a cold morning above the tree line is not a delay you argue with. It's a delay you endure.

Two failures, unrelated, unremarkable. Weather closing in besides. Any competent leader—any leader except the one they actually had—would read the signs and hold camp.

Which was the trap inside his trap, and Dev saw it only as he was tightening the last thing, crouched in the dark with his fingers smelling of gasoline: August Iversen did not read signs the way other leaders did. August read signs the way predators read sign. Nine days of watching this man find routes in fog and footing in scree and lies in a client's packing list—what were the odds he wouldn't smell two coincidences arriving together?

Close to zero. Dev had known it going in. He did it anyway.

He stood, wiped his hands on his trousers, and looked back at the sleepers. Ilse's face was turned toward the fire, younger in sleep, less armored. Renny's bandaged wrist rested outside his bag where the swelling could breathe. Mara slept like a knife in a drawer. And August—August lay on his side facing outward, toward the darkness and the horses and the glacier beyond, the way he always did, the sentry's posture worn so long it had become the shape of his bones.

*I'm sorry,* Dev thought, and didn't know which of them he was saying it to. Maybe all of them. Maybe himself, most of all, which would have surprised the man in the hotel bar in Kessik, who had not considered himself part of the cargo.

He got back into his bag. The beacon pressed cold against his ribs now, and he did not take it out, and he did not send the confirmation the message demanded—*COMMS DISABLED, HOLDING*—because sending it would make it true, would make him the author of it instead of merely the instrument. He'd send it in the morning. He'd have until morning.

Sleep came slowly and left early. He dreamed of his mother's roof leaking, and woke gray-handed with dawn to the sound of August already up, already moving through the camp with that economy that never wasted a motion, kneeling at the stove, priming it, striking the lighter—

The stove caught, coughed, caught again, then began its thin dying stutter, flame shrinking, starving, guttering blue-white and low.

Dev watched August's face across the fire pit. Watched him shut off the fuel, wait, try again. Watched him frown—not confused, not yet, but listening to the machine the way he listened to everything, with his whole attention, the way you listen to a person you suspect is lying.

Then August looked up, and his eyes went around the circle of waking faces—Mara stretching, Renny groaning upright, Ilse emerging pale from her bag—and settled, for one moment too long, on Dev.

"Cold night," August said mildly, to no one in particular. "Everything holds?"

"Everything holds," Dev said.

August nodded slowly, and turned back to the stove, and began—to Dev's stomach's slow drop—to take it apart.
