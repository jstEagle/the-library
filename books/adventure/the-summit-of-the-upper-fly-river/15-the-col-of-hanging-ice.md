## 15. The Col of Hanging Ice

The mountain announced itself before they saw it.

August heard it first—a deep, tectonic groan rolling down the valley at dawn, the sound of the Hanging Ice settling its weight against the col like an old man shifting in a chair that might not hold him. He stood outside his tent with a tin cup cooling in his hands and listened to it the way a sailor listens to rigging in a rising wind: not for what it said, but for what it was about to say.

By full light the seracs were visible above them, a thousand feet of fractured glacier clinging to the shoulder of the col, blue-green ice stacked in leaning towers and overhanging blades, all of it poised above the route like a verdict waiting to be delivered. The morning sun struck the upper faces and set them burning white, and where the light touched, meltwater ran—thin silver threads down the flanks of towers the size of church steeples.

Mara came to stand beside him, breath steaming. "That's the line?"

"That's the line."

"Through all that."

"Under it. There's a ramp on the left side, see—the shadowed gully. It threads between the two big blocks. If we move fast and move quiet, we're through the fall zone in forty minutes."

"And if we don't?"

August drank the cold tea. "Then we find out why they call it the Hanging Ice."

Behind them the camp was already stirring. Renny had the stove going, and the smell of oatmeal and melted snow drifted across the moraine. Dev was checking loads with the methodical care he brought to everything, cinching straps, testing buckles. Ilse stood apart from the others, facing the col, her hood back despite the cold, studying the route with an expression August had learned to read over the past days—not fear exactly, but the focused attention of someone measuring herself against something larger than she was.

She'd been doing that since Kettle Ford. Measuring. And being measured.

He'd stopped thinking of her as the magnate's daughter somewhere on the high trail. She carried her pack without complaint, learned knots after a single demonstration, and asked questions that were never stupid. She was still a client—soft hands under the gloves, a stride that would tire by afternoon—but she was a client who watched, who remembered, who did not ask twice for anything she could work out once.

It unsettled him more than he admitted. Clients were supposed to be cargo. Cargo didn't look at you like that.

* * *

They roped up at the base of the ramp at seven, while the shadows still held the ice hard and blue. August led. That was never a question; he'd told Vask's people when he signed on that he would lead every technical pitch personally or not at all, and whatever else Soren Vask wanted from this crossing, he wanted it done, so August got his way.

The rope order mattered too. Himself, then Ilse, then Dev, then Mara, then Renny with the spare load, anchored by Mara's steady hands. He'd thought about it half the night. Putting Ilse second meant she climbed in his footsteps, on his protection, close enough that if something went wrong above he could reach her. It also meant she'd be close enough to watch how he worked, and some part of him—some part he distrusted—wanted her to watch.

"You'll want your tools short," he told the group, driving a pick into the verglas at the ramp's mouth to demonstrate. "Swing for placement, not for sound. The ice here is old—it rings hollow in places. Trust your feet more than your axes. Front points first, small steps. And nobody stops in the open. We move between the towers, rest in the shadows behind them. Understood?"

Five heads nodded. Five faces looked up at the leaning blue world above them.

"One more thing." He let his voice flatten, the way he did when the words mattered. "If I shout *ice*, you don't look up. You get under the lip of the nearest block, you press in, and you count to sixty. Looking up is how people die out here. The ice doesn't care about being seen."

Renny made a joke then—something about preferring not to be seen by anything that size—and the nervous laughter it raised was useful, the way a valve is useful. August let it run three seconds and killed it.

"Rope's live. Let's climb."

* * *

The ramp was steeper than it had looked from below, as ramps always are. Forty-five degrees of water-ice veined with older, darker layers, the whole surface scoured by wind into ripples like frozen sand. August climbed it in a rhythm he'd built over twenty years: pick, pick, step-step-step, pick, pick, step. His world narrowed to the next fifteen feet—the texture of the ice, the angle of the sun on the upper towers, the faint ticking sounds the glacier made as the warmth crept into it.

He placed protection where he could find it—two screws in a thick vein, a piton hammered into a crack where the ice thinned over rock. Not enough anchors for comfort. Enough for conscience.

At the halfway stance he set a belay and brought the others up one at a time, watching each of them work. Mara came first, economical and sure, a woman who'd clearly done this kind of ground before and lied about her résumé less than most. Dev climbed well—strong, deliberate, maybe a little too deliberate, as though he were memorizing the route rather than simply climbing it. August filed that away with the other small oddities about Dev Okafor and said nothing.

Renny came up swearing cheerfully, which was Renny's way of managing fear, and took the stance with relief written all over him.

Ilse came last.

She climbed the way she seemed to do everything now—with a concentration that had burned off the softness of the first days. Her swings were shorter than ideal but accurate. Her front points found the ripples in the ice and held. Twice she paused to place her feet better rather than rushing, and both times August felt something in his chest loosen slightly. She reached the stance breathing hard, cheeks flushed dark with cold and effort, eyes bright.

"Well?" she said.

"You're slow," August said.

"I'm alive."

"That's the faster way. Up here, alive is fast." He checked her anchor, found it good, and moved on before she could see what his face was doing.

* * *

Above the ramp the route threaded between the two great blocks—the Twin Sisters, some forgotten guidebook had called them, though no book August trusted had ever come this far north. The gap between them was fifty yards of broken ground, and above it hung the main body of the serac field: acres of ice canted at angles ice has no business holding, split by crevasses wide enough to swallow a truck, dripping in the strengthening sun.

August moved through the gap in bursts. Thirty seconds of exposed travel, then flat against the lee of the western block, counting. Then thirty seconds more. He kept the others tight on the rope, no slack, so that any movement he made translated immediately into movement for them—no hesitation, no decisions left to make in the open.

Halfway through, the mountain spoke again.

Not a groan this time. A crack—a single sharp report from high in the eastern towers, like a rifle shot, followed by a low grinding rumble that August felt through his boots before he heard it.

"*Ice!*" he shouted, and threw himself under the lip of the western block, hauling the rope taut as he went.

The others reacted well. Better than well—Ilse was already moving before the word finished leaving his mouth, folding herself into the shadow of the block with a speed that told him she'd been listening, really listening, all morning. Dev pulled Renny in. Mara pressed flat, axe held across her body.

Ice came down.

Not much of it—a serac the size of a garden shed calving off the lower flank of the eastern tower, shattering as it fell, spraying the route with fragments from fist-sized to boulder-sized. The big pieces missed them. The spray did not. A chunk the size of a loaf of bread struck the ice two feet from Ilse's head and exploded into shards that rang off her helmet, and another caught Renny across the forearm as he shielded his face.

Then it was over, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"Count," August said quietly. "Sixty seconds. Nobody moves."

They counted. Above them the sun kept working on the towers, patient as erosion. Somewhere high up, water ticked onto ice.

At sixty, August rose, scanned the route, and waved them forward. They crossed the remaining exposure in two quick bounds and came together in the sheltered basin beyond, where the col's crest finally showed itself—a narrow saddle of wind-scoured snow between the serac field and the rock buttress to the east.

"Everyone report," August said. "Injuries, damage, now."

Mara: clean. Dev: clean. Ilse: shaken, helmet scored, otherwise fine. Renny—

Renny was holding his right arm against his chest, and even through his glove August could see the swelling starting at the wrist.

"Sprained," Renny said quickly. "It's a sprain. Piece caught me on the way through. I'm fine."

"Let me see it."

"It's fine, August."

"*Let me see it.*"

Renny extended the arm with the reluctance of a man surrendering a weapon. August peeled back the glove with care. The wrist was already purpling at the edges, puffy along the thumb side. He pressed gently and Renny's breath hissed through his teeth.

"Not broken," August said. "Or if it is, it's a crack, not a break. Either way, you're not loading it. No poles. No carrying. Mara takes your share of the load."

"The load's fine—"

"Mara takes the load." He said it without heat, which somehow made it heavier. "You can cook one-handed. You can walk. You can't haul. That's the deal."

Renny looked at his own wrist as if it had betrayed him personally. "How far to the far side of the col?"

"Two hours to the descent gully. Then a long afternoon."

"Then I'll walk on my own two legs and keep my opinions to myself."

"That," August said, "would be a first."

The laugh that went around the group was thin but real, and he was glad of it. Fear needed somewhere to go. But as he turned to study the crest, the humor drained out of him, because the crest told him something he hadn't been able to see from below.

The descent gully—the only sane way off the col's northern side—was choked.

A recent avalanche, days old at most, had filled the upper third of it with debris: car-sized blocks of snow and ice jumbled together like a dropped masonry wall. Below the debris the gully narrowed into a chute that funneled directly into the path of everything the Hanging Ice shed. It was, in short, exactly the kind of terrain August had sworn off, the kind of ground where the mountain holds all the cards and deals them slowly, watching you sweat.

"We can't go down there," Mara said, arriving beside him. She'd seen it too. Mara always saw it too.

"We can't stay here either. This basin catches everything that comes off the eastern towers. By afternoon it'll be a shooting gallery."

"So we go over the buttress? The rock?"

"The rock's fourth-class with packs, doable. But it puts us on the east flank, and the east flank drains into—" He stopped. He didn't need to finish. They both knew what the east flank drained into. Every route off this col except one drained into the same place eventually: the great avalanche chutes below the shoulder, the terrain he'd reconnoitered years ago and promised himself, in the particular solemnity of the young and grieving, never to touch again.

"There's always the other thing," Mara said quietly.

"There's always the other thing."

She didn't push. That was one of the ten thousand reasons he trusted her. She looked at the debris-choked gully a moment longer, spat over the edge in the mountaineer's benediction, and went back to redistribute Renny's load.

August stayed at the crest alone for a minute, feeling the wind come up the northern side, cold and clean and smelling of distances he hadn't stood downwind of in six years.

* * *

They ate in the basin's shelter, huddled against the blocks, and August laid out the choice the way he'd promised himself he would—honestly, completely, without steering.

"Three options," he said, drawing lines in the snow with a tent stake. "One: we descend the gully. Fast, if we're lucky. The debris is a day old, maybe two—it's mostly settled. But the chute below it is active terrain, and if the Hanging Ice sheds anything bigger than what already came down, we're in the runout. Two: we traverse the rock buttress east. Slower, more technical, and it ends us above the east-flank chutes, which are worse than the gully. Three—"

He stopped. Ilse was watching him with that level gray attention, and Dev was watching him too, and he understood suddenly that option three was the reason he'd gathered them, that some part of him had already decided and was merely dressing the decision as a consultation.

"Three?" Ilse prompted.

"Three is the shoulder." He pointed east and up, to where the rock buttress merged into a long snow-and-ice ridge that climbed away toward the high shoulder of the range—the unclimbed line, the one he'd stared at through binoculars from a distance camp six years ago, the one that had featured in his nightmares ever since wearing a different face each time. "It's steep. It's exposed. It's never been done, as far as anyone knows, which means nobody knows what's wrong with it. But it stays high. It stays out of the runout zones. And it puts us on the far side above everything Vask's people can reach easily."

Silence. The wind worried the ropes where they lay coiled.

"How steep?" Dev asked.

"Fifty degrees in places. Fixed lines most of the way. Eight hours if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?" Ilse asked.

August met her eyes. "Then it goes wrong at altitude, on an unclimbed ridge, four days from help. I won't lie to you. Option three is the one where we bet everything on our own hands."

Renny cradled his wrist and said nothing, which from Renny was a sermon.

"I didn't come this far," Ilse said at last, "to be buried in a gully because it was on the map." She looked around at the others. "I vote for the shoulder."

"I vote we don't decide anything with a broken wrist and an empty stomach," Mara said. "Camp here tonight, behind these blocks. Move at first light, whichever way we choose. Cold ice climbs better than warm ice anyway."

August nodded. It was the right call, and he was grateful to her for making it, because his own judgment had gone strange somewhere on the ramp—too eager, too hungry for the high line, and he knew that hunger. It had been the last thing he'd felt before the worst day of his life.

They made camp in the lee of the western block, tents pitched low and pegged with ice screws. Renny cooked one-handed and refused help twice. As the sun dropped behind the western peaks the temperature fell like a stone, and the Hanging Ice above them went silent and gold, beautiful the way predators are beautiful, and August sat outside his tent long after the others slept, watching the last light die on the towers.

Footsteps in the snow. Ilse, wrapped in every layer she owned, lowering herself onto the ice beside him without asking permission.

"You climbed well today," he said.

"You said I was slow."

"You were slow. Slow and correct beats fast and lucky. Up there, correct is the only style there is." He paused. "Most clients freeze, the first time ice comes down. You got under the rock before I finished shouting. Where did that come from?"

She was quiet for a while. High above, a serac caught the last red light and held it like a coal.

"My father's house taught me to read rooms," she said finally. "Which conversations are about to go wrong. Which silences mean something's already broken. I suppose a mountain is just a room that's stopped pretending." She glanced at him. "You knew that gully was bad before we topped out. You knew on the ramp. Why didn't you say something?"

"Because saying it wouldn't have changed the ground. And because I wanted to see the options with my own eyes before I put my fear on all of you."

"And now you've seen them."

"Now I've seen them."

"And?"

August looked east, to where the shoulder climbed away into the darkening sky, invisible now, entirely present.

"Now I think we're going to climb a mountain that doesn't want us," he said. "First light. Get some sleep."

She rose, hesitated, and squeezed his shoulder once—gloved hand, brief pressure, gone—before retreating to her tent. August stayed where he was. The stars came out over the col, hard and indifferent, and beneath them the Hanging Ice ticked and settled, keeping its own counsel, holding its weight the way it had held it for ten thousand years.

Waiting, as it had always waited, to see what the small warm creatures below would dare.
