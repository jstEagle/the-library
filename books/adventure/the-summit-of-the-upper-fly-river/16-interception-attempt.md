## 16. Interception Attempt

Soren Vask stood at the window of the forward camp's main tent and watched the weather come down the valley like something being poured. The clouds had been building all morning over the western divide—gray at first, then bruised, then the color of old iron—and now they were spilling eastward in long ragged tongues that ate the light off the glacier below. He had seen this sky before. He had seen it on the day three men died under his orders, though he had never once said so aloud, and he was not a man who confused silence with innocence.

The camp sat on a shelf of moraine above the parallel valley he'd chosen months ago, when this operation had still been, in his own private accounting, a simple matter of logistics. A second team. Six men, well paid, lightly questioned. Radios, ropes, rifles wrapped in oiled canvas against the damp. A route that shadowed Iversen's line through the next drainage over, close enough to intercept, far enough to stay invisible until invisibility stopped being useful.

He turned from the window and looked at his men spread around the wood stove: Halvard, his foreman, a heavy quiet Norwegian who'd worked Vask's mine claims for eleven years and had never once asked what a job was actually for; the Kessler brothers, twins, ex-military, interchangeable except that one of them smiled too easily; Petrov, the radio man, hunched over the set with headphones clamped to his skull like a man listening for voices under water; and young Anselm, twenty-three, hired in Kessik two weeks ago because he could read terrain maps faster than anyone Vask had ever tested.

They were playing cards by lamplight, and none of them were looking at him, which suited him fine. Vask had built a career on the principle that men work best when they believe the work is smaller than it is. Halvard thought they were escorting a survey crew. The Kesslers thought they were providing security for a corporate asset. Only Petrov knew the full shape of it, and Petrov had been paid enough to keep the shape inside his own head.

Vask poured himself coffee from the dented pot on the stove rail and drank it black, scalding, the way he'd drunk it since the accident—the word *accident* still doing its quiet work in his vocabulary, still standing guard over everything that had happened on the Upper Fly seven years ago. He did not think of himself as a liar. He thought of himself as a man who understood that truth, like load-bearing snow, had to be distributed carefully or the whole slope came down.

Petrov lifted one earphone off his ear and swiveled toward him. "Beacon check," he said. "Okafor transmitted at fourteen hundred. Standard position code."

"And?"

"Grid nine-fourteen. They're a day behind your projection. River crossing cost them time." Petrov hesitated, which was unlike him. "There's a second string in the transmission. Unscheduled."

Vask set the cup down. "Read it."

"Four characters. Then a repeat request." Petrov pulled a pencil stub from his jacket and wrote the characters on the margin of his log sheet, small and careful, the way a man writes something he doesn't want to be true. "He's asking whether to proceed to the rendezvous point as planned, or hold short. Says—" Petrov squinted at his own shorthand— "says the client is watching him now. Says she has eyes."

*She.* Vask felt the letters land in his chest like stones dropped into a well. Ilse Brandt. The daughter. The cargo that was not the cargo, the girl whose presence on this expedition had always struck him as slightly wrong—a magnate did not send his only child over a glaciated range as a holiday unless the holiday was cover for something, and Vask had assumed the something was simply distance, a daughter parked beyond the reach of subpoena while her father's lawyers managed the fire at home.

But if she was watching Dev Okafor—if she had noticed the porter photographing gear, noticed the relay schedule, noticed anything—then the arithmetic changed. Vask had planned for a traitor who was discreet. He had not planned for a traitor who was observed.

"He wants an answer by tomorrow's window," Petrov said. "Sixteen hundred."

"Tell him hold short of the rendezvous by half a day," Vask said. "Tell him the patrol will come to him." He paused, and the pause was longer than any decision deserved, and he knew why it was longer, and he let it be longer anyway, because there was a name moving through his mind that he could not make lie flat. *Iversen.* August Iversen, on the mountain again, leading strangers across country that had killed three of Vask's own climbers while Iversen stood at the anchor and watched the rope go slack and lived.

Seven years. Seven years since the inquiry, since the permit board struck his name from the rolls, since Henrik Brandt—Brandt, who had been on that expedition's financing papers, Brandt whose money had bought the summit push, Brandt whose filings Vask had signed his own name against—had quietly absorbed the scandal and moved on to richer ground while Vask took the fall beside Iversen and then, unlike Iversen, had climbed back up the ladder by making himself useful to the man who'd bought the rope that broke.

Useful. That was the word Vask had lived inside for seven years. Useful men got work. Useful men got forgiven, in the way that mattered, which was financially. And useful men, when their patron's empire began to smell of rot, were the ones sent up into the cold to retrieve the evidence before the auditors found it.

That was what the drums were. That was what the satchel was. Survey cores drilled from the upper valley in the first year of the mine's operation—cores that told the true story of the tailings seepage, the water table, the readings that had been inverted and refiled until the numbers sang the song Brandt's regulators wanted to hear. Vask had helped write the false version. His signature was on the certification page, third from the top, between a hydrologist who'd since emigrated and a junior engineer who'd since died, both conveniently beyond testimony. Which left Vask. And left the originals, locked in a storage room in Kessik until Brandt decided the safest place for them was the bottom of a crevasse on the far side of the range, carried there under the flag of a daughter's holiday.

Except the daughter had other plans. Vask had begun to suspect it weeks ago, from the pattern of her father's phone calls—Henrik asking not *is she safe* but *where exactly is she*, asking it with the flat urgency of a man tracking something that might escape. And now the porter's coded message confirmed it in four characters: the girl had eyes.

Vask looked out the window again. The cloud had closed the valley completely. Snow was starting—not the equinox snows, not yet, those were ten days out by every forecast he'd bought, but a front system, wet and low, the kind that made tracks vanish and ridgelines go soft and turned a planned interception into a gamble.

Halvard set down his cards and looked up at him without being asked. It was the thing Vask valued most about the man: Halvard read a room the way a good guide reads a slope, by weight and drift, and he always knew when the boss wanted something before the boss said it.

"Well?" Halvard said.

"We move tomorrow," Vask said. "Before the front settles in. We go up-valley to the saddle where the two drainages nearly touch—the low col, you've seen it on the map. From the crest we can drop into Iversen's valley above their last camp and meet them on the trail as a rescue patrol."

"A rescue patrol," Halvard repeated, flatly, the way he repeated things he intended to remember later.

"There's weather coming. Any party on the high trail in weather looks like it needs help. We'll carry a stretcher, red blankets, medical kit—the whole costume. We hail them from two hundred meters, friendly, official, ranger colors. Iversen can't refuse a rescue approach without looking like a madman, and the girl—" Vask turned his cup slowly on the stove rail, thinking— "the girl will see six men with a stretcher and believe it, because people believe what they're primed to believe, and everyone on that trail has been primed by the sky itself."

"And once we're close?" Halvard asked. Not *what do we do*. Never that. Halvard had learned, over eleven years, never to ask the question that required an answer in words.

"Once we're close, we take stock. The documents travel in a sealed case with the girl's gear. We identify it, we lift it, and we walk away. Nobody dies. Nobody even gets hurt, if everyone keeps their head." Vask heard his own voice say *nobody dies* and registered, somewhere beneath the sentence, the small cold fact that he had said those exact words once before, on another mountain, to another team, standing under another bad sky. He had believed them then, too.

Anselm spoke up from the card table, young and unguarded. "And if they don't cooperate?"

Vask looked at him the way a teacher looks at a student who has accidentally asked the correct question. "Then we escalate," he said. "A warning shot into scree does wonders for cooperation. Above the tree line, sound carries like a sermon. One round over their heads and every argument they have gets very short."

The Kessler brothers exchanged a glance that contained an entire conversation. Petrov wrote the transmission for Dev Okafor in cipher, sealed it, and keyed it into the evening window, and Vask listened to the encoder's soft mechanical chatter and thought about how much of his life now traveled through machines that could betray him, and how little of it would survive daylight.

He slept badly, in the cot at the rear of the command tent, with the rifle case unlocked within arm's reach and the wind testing the guy-lines all night like a burglar working a door. In the gray hours he dreamed—not of the mountain, not of the dead, he had trained himself out of those dreams years ago with a discipline he mistook for health—but of a ledger. A green ledger, cloth-bound, its pages filled with numbers in his own handwriting, and in the dream he was trying to burn it and it would not burn, the pages curling and going dark at the edges and then flattening out again, whole, legible, patient. He woke at first light with his heart going hard and the taste of smoke in his mouth and no fire anywhere in the camp.

That, he understood, was what the documents were. Not paper. A fire that wouldn't take.

* * *

They moved at dawn under a sky that had stopped threatening and started delivering. Wet snow fell in fat slow flakes, the kind that stuck to everything and made the world feel upholstered, muffled, complicit. Six men in white over-suits, packs stripped to essentials, the stretcher broken down and lashed across two frames, the red blankets rolled tight and waterproofed, the medical kit bulging with props Vask had assembled himself in Kessik—splints, ampoules, a blood-pressure cuff, a clipboard with authentic-looking ranger forms he'd had printed at the same shop that did the district's actual stationery. The costume was good. Vask prided himself on costumes. He had worn one for seven years.

The climb to the low col took most of the day. The drainage was steep and brushy below, then open scree, then the long gray ramp of the saddle itself, wind-scoured, holding last winter's snow in its shaded folds. Vask led the final pitch himself, because he always led the final pitch, because there was a part of him—smaller now than it used to be, but alive, insistent—that needed to feel the mountain under his boots and know he still belonged on it. The air was thin and clean and cruel. Above the col the clouds tore open for twenty minutes and showed him the far valley: the great white shoulder of the range, the hanging glaciers, and, tiny and absurd, a thread of human passage on the lower trail—a pack train, a smear of color, movement.

Iversen's party. A day ahead of schedule now, making up lost time, driving hard for the tree line before the real snows.

Vask stood on the crest with his binoculars and counted them. Six figures. He picked out the shapes: a large man at the front breaking trail—Iversen, or close enough; a woman with a guide's stride; a slighter figure in bright shell fabric who kept stopping to look around, stopping the way clients stop, hungry-eyed, out of depth. The girl. He watched her for a long time. She did not look like cargo. She looked like someone reading a map of her own life and finding errors in it.

"They'll camp at the basin below the moraine band," Halvard said, coming up beside him, reading the terrain with the flat competence Vask paid him for. "Water, shelter from the north wind. If we descend the east chute tonight we can be on the trail above their camp by midmorning. Come down on them from uphill, dressed as the patrol, flags up, hands visible."

"Uphill," Vask said, and nodded. "Good. Uphill is authority. Nobody argues with a rescue party that appears above them in weather." He lowered the binoculars and looked at his own reflection of doubt in the glass lens cap. "One thing. Before we commit. Does anyone on this team have a problem with what happens if it goes sideways?"

Halvard answered for all of them, which was his function: "Nobody's here to die, Soren. Same as you."

It was the most honest sentence spoken in the camp in three weeks, and Vask turned away from it quickly, the way a man turns from a mirror he didn't mean to catch.

They descended the east chute in failing light, roped in pairs, moving carefully through the wet snow that was already turning to grain at altitude—the first spit of the season's real winter, arriving early, arriving rude, uninvited to anyone's plan. By midnight they were bivouacked in a rock hollow two hours above Iversen's likely camp, stove lit inside a windbreak, sentries staggered, and Vask lay in his bag listening to the mountain tick and settle around them and tried to sleep and could not, because tomorrow had a shape he recognized and the shape frightened him more than he allowed himself to say.

In the morning the front had thinned to high haze. Perfect interception weather: visibility good enough to be seen, broken enough to explain a patrol's sudden appearance. The team dressed into the costume—ranger-orange panels sewn onto the chest, the stretcher assembled, the red blankets draped, the clipboard prominent, the rifles left wrapped and slung low where a casual eye would read them as tripods. Vask checked each man personally. He adjusted Anselm's blanket roll. He put the clipboard into Halvard's hands because Halvard's face was the least criminal face he owned.

"One hundred fifty meters," Vask said. "We hail. Friendly. Names withheld—we're 'the district patrol,' nothing more. We ask after injuries, offer assistance, mention the weather window closing. We get close enough to see the gear lash-ups. We find the case. And then—" he paused, choosing the sentence like a foothold— "and then we improvise."

They descended through the last of the scree in loose formation, flags tied to ski poles held high, and at nine-forty by Vask's watch they rounded the shoulder of the moraine band and saw the trail below them, and the party on it, strung out along the lakeshore flats in the thin gold morning light.

What happened next happened fast, and afterward Vask would replay it many times and understand it differently each time, the way you reread a letter that ruined you.

They had closed to perhaps three hundred meters, flags visible, when a figure detached from the party's flank and began climbing. Not toward them—obliquely, along the ridge spur to their right, quick and certain, a woman with a guide's economy of motion, gaining height with the specific purpose of gaining sight. Mara Quist. Vask didn't know the name yet, but he knew the type instantly: the professional who doesn't wait to be told what a thing is, who climbs to find out.

Through the binoculars he watched her reach the spur's shoulder, plant herself against the wind, raise her own glasses, and begin to read them. Read the flags. Read the stretcher. Read the orange panels. And then—this was the moment, this was the hinge the whole day swung on—read the rifle slings, and stop moving entirely, and stand very still in the way that trained people stand still when they have just understood something.

"She's made us," Petrov said at his elbow. "She's signaling downhill. Hand movements. Flag code."

Below, the party was reacting. The big man at the front—Iversen, unmistakably Iversen now, the build, the way he turned, the economy of a body that had spent decades deciding where to put its weight—was pulling his people off the open flats and into the boulder field at the base of the moraine, herding them with gestures, unhurried, absolute. The pack train went to ground. The girl crouched behind a boulder with her hood up and her eyes, Vask was certain, already counting his men.

"Hail them," Vask ordered. "Now. While we still have the story."

Halvard raised the megaphone and called out the patrol script—rescue party, weather advisory, do you require assistance—and the words went down the valley and came back off the rock walls thinner and hollower than they'd left, and nobody below answered, and the silence that answered instead was the loudest thing Vask had heard in seven years.

"They're not buying it," Anselm said. There was something almost relieved in the boy's voice, and Vask noted it, filed it, worried at it.

"Close the distance," Vask said. "Slow. Flags high. Hands empty."

They came down another fifty meters, sixty, and the party below melted deeper into the boulders, and Quist on her spur shifted position to keep her sightline, and the geometry of the encounter tightened like a knot being drawn closed by both ends at once. Vask stood in the open with his binoculars and understood, with the clarity that arrives only when a plan has already failed and the failure hasn't fully landed yet, that he was no longer running an interception. He was running a standoff, on a mountain, in front of witnesses, against a man whose entire life had been rebuilt around the lesson that Vask's orders kill people.

He raised the megaphone himself. "August Iversen!" he called, and the name went down the valley like a dropped stone. "Soren Vask! We should talk!"

Nothing. No answer. Just the wind, and the flicker of movement among the boulders, and Quist's orange signal panel flashing on the spur—three short, three long, the alpine distress inversion, or a warning, or a taunt; Vask couldn't parse it and didn't need to. The message was legible without the code: *we see you, we know what you are.*

For a long minute neither side moved. Two groups of armed men on a mountainside, pretending at civility through a megaphone, each waiting for the other to make the mistake that would license the next escalation. Vask felt the old hunger rise in him—the summit-push hunger, the *we've committed, push on* hunger, the hunger that had once sounded like leadership and had actually been vanity wearing a harness—and he felt, simultaneously, the counterweight, fainter, older, the memory of standing at the anchor with a severed rope in his hands and three names he would never say aloud.

"We're coming down to talk," he broadcast. "Unarmed. Five minutes. You have my word."

"You have nothing I want," came the reply, finally, in Iversen's voice—flat, cold, carrying up the valley with terrible clarity, each word placed like a piton. "Take your costume and go home, Vask. Whatever you're carrying under those blankets, the mountain already knows it's not medicine."

And then the shot.

It came from the boulder field, single round, high, deliberate—it cracked overhead and went into the scree slope above Vask's team, and the slope answered. Half an acre of loose rock let go with a sound like a hillside clearing its throat, and the debris came down in a slow brown cascade two hundred meters to the team's left, close enough to feel, far enough to miss, precisely calibrated, Vask realized, to deliver a message rather than a casualty. A warning shot fired not at men but at the mountain itself, using the mountain's own voice: *one step closer and the next release won't miss.*

The Kessler brothers wanted to return fire. Vask watched them bring their rifles up out of the blanket wraps, watched the safeties click, watched the twin grins surface—the smiles of men who had finally been given permission to enjoy themselves—and he said, in a voice that surprised him by how level it was:

"Hold. All of you. Hold."

Because he understood, in that moment, the thing the mountain had been trying to teach him since dawn: that the first shot fired in anger on this terrain would not end the encounter, it would only convert it—convert a standoff into a siege, a siege into a hunt, and a hunt, at altitude, in early snow, into a death lottery where the odds respected no one's plans. He had buried three men who died of exactly this arithmetic. He would not add to the sum. Not today. Not for paper.

"Withdraw," he ordered. "Up the chute. Now."

They withdrew. It was not a rout—it was disciplined, covered, unhurried in appearance—but Vask felt it in his chest as a rout nonetheless, because withdrawal was surrender wearing boots, and Iversen's party watched them go from the rocks without firing another shot, without gloating, without even showing themselves, and that restraint was worse than mockery. It was the restraint of a man conserving everything—ammunition, energy, anger—for what came next.

They regained the col by midafternoon, wet, cold, silent. At the bivouac hollow Vask sat alone outside the windbreak with the radio log in his lap and wrote the transmission to Dev Okafor himself, in his own cipher hand, because some messages you do not delegate:

*Rendezvous compromised. Party alerted. New directive: delay them. Cost them a day, two days. Sabotage what you must. Hold position at the high camp. Vask comes to you.*

He keyed it into the evening window and listened to it go, and then he sat in the falling dark and looked up at the range, where the hanging glaciers glowed faintly under the first stars like something embalmed, and he understood the new shape of his life with a clarity that felt less like planning than like sentencing.

Iversen knew. The girl knew. The guide knew. The whole fragile machinery of disguise—costumes, clipboards, coded porters, deniable cargoes—was burned, and what remained was the oldest transaction the mountains offered: pursuit, endurance, and whichever party wanted it more. Vask had wanted things more than other men his entire life, and it had made him rich, and it had made him alone, and it had put three bodies under a glacier, and it had brought him here, to a cold hollow above a lake, writing orders by lamplight for a bought man he no longer trusted, preparing to chase a rival up a face that had already beaten them both once.

Behind him, at the stove, Anselm was cleaning his rifle with a care that looked, to Vask's experienced eye, less like devotion than like hesitation. The boy had asked, yesterday, what happened if they didn't cooperate. Today he had watched the answer. Tonight his hands moved slowly.

Vask told himself it meant nothing. He had gotten very good, over seven years, at telling himself what things meant.

Above the col, the wind turned and began to come down out of the north, and it carried with it the first dry sting of true winter—the equinox snows arriving early, ahead of every forecast, indifferent to schedules, to ciphers, to the difference between the guilty and the innocent. Vask stood at the edge of the firelight and watched the clouds massing over the high shoulder, the same shoulder, he realized with a chill that had nothing to do with temperature, that Iversen's party would have to cross, and that he would have to follow them across, and that the mountain had just quietly informed them both that from this night forward, it would be choosing the terms.

Somewhere down-valley, in a bivouac he could not see, August Iversen was making his own calculations by his own fire. Vask could almost hear them. And the thought that settled over him as he went to his cot—settled and stayed, heavy as the coming snow—was that for the first time in seven years, he was no longer certain whose arithmetic was better.
