## 17. Renny's Fever

Ilse heard it before she understood it: a sound in the night, low and rhythmic, coming from the cook's end of the camp. Not snoring. Snoring had a shape to it, an architecture of breath. This was thinner, wetter, the sound of a man trying to breathe around something that was not air.

She lay in her tent and listened for a long time, counting the intervals, the way her father counted invoices—without mercy, without sleep. The wind moved through the pines above the tree line's last ragged fringe, and beneath it Renny's breathing rose and fell like something drowning slowly in shallow water.

By morning he could not hold a spoon.

* * *

It began, as these things always did, small. A redness at the edge of the splint Mara had fashioned after the fall on the Col of Hanging Ice—a bruise-colored bloom that might have been nothing, might have been the strap cutting into swollen flesh on the long descent. Renny himself had laughed it off, the way he laughed off everything, his humor a kind of pack he carried along with the pots and the flour sacks. "Wrist's just sulking," he'd said. "Give it a day."

But wrists did not sulk in colors like that. Ilse had seen that color before—in a clinic in Zürich, in the third year she'd spent there under a name that wasn't quite hers, studying emergency medicine before her father's lawyers found her and folded her back into the family like a letter someone else had opened. She had seen a man lose an arm to a color like that. He had waited four days to mention it because he didn't want to be a bother.

Renny was not going to wait four days. There wouldn't be four days.

She went to him at first light, when the frost still stood on the tent fabric like a crop of white hair, and asked—casually, she thought, though nothing about her voice was casual—to see the wrist. He grumbled, good-natured, and unwound the splint, and Ilse felt the bottom drop out of her stomach in the way it only ever dropped quietly, without drama, the way ice lets go of a rock face at three in the morning.

The skin around the laceration site was hot to the touch—not warm, hot, a fever living in the flesh itself—and streaked. Red lines climbing from the wound toward the elbow, thin and definite as pen strokes. His forearm was swollen so tight the skin shone. And when she pressed one finger gently beside the wound, the tissue gave and then refused to rise again, like dough pressed by a tired hand.

Cellulitis, at minimum. Lymphangitis, certainly. Sepsis, if they were unlucky—and up here, at this altitude, with this cold, with antibiotics fifty miles behind them in Kessik, luck was not a currency anyone carried.

"August," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her. She had spent years practicing steadiness. It was the one inheritance from her father she had never managed to refuse.

* * *

August came fast. That was the thing about him that Ilse had been learning, day by day, the way you learn the grain of wood by sanding it: he came fast to problems and slow to people, and he never confused the two. He knelt beside Renny's sleeping bag without a word, looked at the arm the way he looked at a route up a face—reading it, weighing it, finding the line or deciding there wasn't one—and then he said, "How bad?"

And Ilse told him the truth, all of it, in the flat clinical language she had once sworn never to use again. Streaking. Pitting edema. Fever of—I put my hand on his forehead, I don't have a thermometer, but I'd guess thirty-nine and climbing. If it reaches the blood we have hours, not days. He needs IV antibiotics and debridement, and he needs them yesterday.

August looked at her then. Not at the arm. At her.

"You talk like a doctor," he said.

The camp had gone very quiet. Mara had stopped mid-task with a fuel canister in her hands. Dev stood at the edge of the cook tarp, and even the horses seemed to have stilled, heads down, grazing with the deliberate slowness of animals who know the weather has changed.

"I talk like someone who used to want to be a doctor," Ilse said. "There's a difference. I quit." And then, because August's eyes did not release her, because lying to this man had become a thing she no longer knew how to do: "I didn't quit. My father ended it. Three years of medical school in Zürich, and then Henrik Brandt decided his daughter would not be spending her life on night shifts in an emergency ward when she could be spending it being photographed at mine openings. He bought the dean's silence and pulled me out between semesters. I finished my clinical rotations anyway—illegally, informally, in a charity clinic where nobody checked credentials because nobody had any. Two years. Deliveries, trauma, infections. A lot of infections. People who came too late because coming early cost money."

She heard herself saying it and realized she had never said it aloud to anyone. Not to Dev, who had traveled beside her for two weeks. Not to Mara, who shared her tent. The words came out whole, pre-formed, as though they had been waiting in her chest for exactly this pressure to squeeze them out.

"So," August said, and there was something in his voice she couldn't name yet—something that wasn't suspicion and wasn't quite gratitude either, something more like recalibration—"when your father sent you over my mountain with a satchel you weren't supposed to open, he also sent the only person in this crew who can keep his cook alive."

"Yes," Ilse said. "I suppose he did. He's thorough that way. He just never asks what else is in the package."

* * *

They worked on Renny through the middle of the day, in the biggest tent, with every flap tied back so the light could get in. Ilse directed; August assisted; Mara boiled water and instruments until the whole camp smelled of iron and steam.

There was no operating theater here. There was a folding table, a headlamp rigged to a guyline, a first-aid kit designed for broken bones and blisters, and the contents of Ilse's own kit—the kit she had packed secretly, against her own cover story, because some part of her had known from the beginning that she could not cross a range like this pretending to be only a client. A part of her had always known she could not pretend to be anything. The pretending was the thing that had been killing her for six years, longer than any infection could take a man.

Lance and drain. Irrigate with the strongest antiseptic they had, diluted to the strength she remembered from the clinic. Pack the wound. Splint elevated. Fluids forced by mouth, liters of it, sweetened because Renny would drink sweet when he would drink nothing else. And then the hard conversation, the one Ilse had rehearsed in her head while her hands worked: the antibiotics in the kit were oral, broad-spectrum, and almost certainly insufficient. She needed August to understand that they might buy time rather than cure, and time, up here, was a slope—they were either gaining it or losing it, never standing still.

"He keeps this down, the fever may break in forty-eight hours," she told August, outside the tent, in the blue shadow of the afternoon. "If it doesn't—if the streaking climbs past his shoulder—we turn back. No route matters. No documents matter. We carry him down to the ranger station however we have to carry him."

August nodded, and Ilse watched something move behind his face—something old and heavy, the ghost of another expedition, other men who had needed carrying. She had read enough of his history in her father's files to know what lived in that look. Three climbers. One decision. A reputation buried under the ice with them.

"You've done this before," she said quietly. "Carried someone."

"Once," August said. "Not far enough." And he turned away and went to check the horses, because that was how he answered questions—he answered them with work.

* * *

Renny's fever broke on the second night, or broke enough—dropped from a furnace to a stove, from delirium to exhaustion. Ilse sat with him through the worst hours because she could not sleep anyway, and because she remembered, from the charity clinic, that the sick do better when someone stays. Not doing anything. Just staying. It was the cheapest medicine in the world and the hardest to prescribe.

He surfaced out of the delirium near dawn, and the first thing he said, hoarse and half-laughing, was: "Didn't figure you for the type."

"The type?"

"Doctor type. You've got the hands of a rich girl. Soft. I noticed the first week." He grinned weakly at her. "No offense meant."

"None taken," Ilse said, and found herself smiling, actually smiling, for the first time in days. "You're right. They were soft. They're less soft now."

"Where'd a rich girl learn to lance a wound like a butcher?" Renny asked, and then, catching himself, amended: "Like a surgeon. Butcher's what my mother called the vet in our village, and what you did was better than what he did."

Ilse told him the truth, because Renny was the kind of person who collected truths the way other men collected bottle caps, trading them back in stories at every fire. She told him about Zürich. About the dean her father bought. About the charity ward with its leaking roof and its forty beds and its patients who arrived by tram with appendices already burst, because a taxi cost money and the tram was free. About the night she had held a woman's hand through a septic shock she could not reverse, and how the woman's daughter, nine years old, had asked her afterward if her mother had died because God wanted it, and how Ilse had said no—no, sweetheart, she died because she got sick too late, and getting sick late is a kind of poverty—and how she had walked out of that ward and known that she could not go back to her father's world, not ever, not for anything, because she had now seen what that world did at a distance, with its ledgers and its lawyers and its photographs at mine openings.

"That's why you're really here," Renny said. It wasn't a question.

"That's why I'm really here," Ilse agreed. "I'm not on holiday. I ran. My father thinks this trek is a gift he's giving me. It's the door I'm leaving through."

Renny was quiet a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere out in the dark a horse shifted its weight, and the picket line creaked.

"My old man drove a delivery truck," Renny said finally. "Forty years. Died with the steering wheel worn smooth under his hands and nothing in the bank but the funeral money, which the union paid. I cook for outfits like this one because it's honest work and somebody's got to feed the people who do the hard parts." He looked at her with his fever-bright, clear-eyed honesty. "You're not what your old man built. I figured that out about you somewhere around Kettle Ford, watching you watch August save my gear train. Nobody looks at a person like that unless they're learning something."

"What was I learning?"

"How to be saved," Renny said, "by somebody who isn't for sale." And then he slept, properly slept, the deep untroubled sleep of a man whose body has decided to fight another day.

* * *

Word travels through a small camp faster than weather. By evening everyone knew—or thought they knew—that the client's daughter was a doctor in hiding, that she had fled her father rather than vacationed from him, that the polite fencing of the first weeks had been exactly what it sounded like: two people measuring each other across a table neither could see.

Mara came to her at dusk, while Ilse was boiling new dressings, and sat down on a stone nearby without invitation, which was how Mara did everything—arriving as though she had always been there.

"I owe you an apology," Mara said.

"For what?"

"For reading you wrong since the trailhead. Spoiled. Ornamental. Cargo." Mara's voice was dry as the scree slopes she loved. "I've guided enough clients' daughters to fill a valley, and ninety percent of them are exactly what their fathers send them here to prove they're not. I pattern-matched you inside an hour. It's a lazy habit and it nearly cost us. If you hadn't spoken up about that wrist—"

"I nearly didn't," Ilse admitted. "I sat with it all night before I said anything. Telling felt dangerous. Like the moment I used the training, the cover story was over."

"It was over the minute you signed on with a hidden medical kit," Mara said. "Cover stories don't survive contact with altitude. Nothing false does. The mountain strips it off you like bark off a deadfall." She paused, and then said, more carefully: "Dev took it hard, by the way. Your telling the truth. He kept looking at you like—like he'd been caught holding something. I don't know. Watch him."

Ilse watched Dev that evening, and saw what Mara meant, though she could not yet name it: a restlessness in him, a man checking a compass he hadn't shown anyone. She filed it away, the way she had learned to file things in her father's offices—neatly, without expression, against a future need.

* * *

Later, when the others had turned in, August appeared at the edge of the firelight with two tin cups and the last of the tea, and handed her one without asking, which was also how he did things.

"You hid it well," he said.

"I had practice. Hiding things runs in my family. It's practically the business model."

"And the medicine? Why hide that? Of everything you could have concealed, why hide the one thing that saves lives?"

Ilse wrapped her hands around the cup and looked into the coals. "Because attention is the thing I can't afford," she said. "Do you understand what it means to be Henrik Brandt's daughter? Every room I enter, people reorganize themselves around me before I've said a word. Guides become polite. Doctors become deferential. Nobody tells me the truth about anything, because everyone assumes I already know it, or that it doesn't matter what I think, or that my father will hear about it either way. In the clinic in Zürich, for two years, I was nobody. I was the volunteer with the steady hands. Patients told me things they'd never told anyone. Nurses corrected my technique without flinching. I was—useful. Actually useful. And then I came home, and within a month I was furniture again." Her voice tightened. "I hid the training because I wanted, just once, to be judged on what I do instead of what I cost. Even from you. Especially from you, maybe. You looked at me at Kettle Ford like I was luggage that talked."

August was quiet for a long moment. The fire cracked. Above them the sky had cleared hard and cold, stars sharp as nailheads, and somewhere in that darkness the high peaks stood unseen, holding their snow.

"I did look at you like that," he said finally. "I was wrong. It's not the first time I've been wrong about a person on this mountain, and it's cost me more than misjudging you." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. But Ilse had read the files; she knew what the elaboration would be, and she let him keep it, the way you leave a wound undisturbed because the packing is doing its work.

"Your father," August said instead. "When this crossing is done—whatever happens with whatever's in those drums—what happens to you?"

"I don't go back," Ilse said. "That's decided. It's been decided for six years; I just haven't had the courage to act on it. This trek is me acting on it." She met his eyes across the fire. "Whatever you're carrying in those sealed cases, August—whatever Vask wants recovered, whatever my father wants destroyed—I want the opposite. I want it delivered. To the regulators, to the press, to whoever on the far side will take it. I'll testify. I'll sign my name. I'm done being a package."

August looked at her for a long time, and Ilse watched something settle in his face—not agreement exactly, not yet, but the ground shifting beneath a decision, the way snow settles before it either holds or slides.

"Get some sleep," he said at last, standing. "Tomorrow we make distance while Renny can travel. The window's closing faster than the forecast says."

He gathered the cups and walked toward his tent, and Ilse stayed by the fire a few minutes longer, alone with the stars and the smell of pine smoke and the strange lightness in her chest, the feeling of a story set down after being carried too long.

In the dark, she heard Dev's tent flap open and close, twice, and the soft double-click of something electronic. She filed it away.

The fever had taught her something she hadn't expected: that the truth, once spoken, was like the antibiotics—too late to prevent the infection, but just in time to save the patient. Tomorrow the mountain would test whether the same was true of all of them.
