## 19. White Silence

The storm did not howl so much as it erased. For three days the wind had come off the col in long, flat pulses, packing the cave mouth with spindrift until the world outside shrank to a white seam the width of a man's hand, and then, by the third morning, to nothing at all. Mara Quist woke to a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving, and for a disorienting moment she did not know where she was—only that she was cold in the particular, patient way of stone that has been cold for ten thousand years and intends to remain so.

Then Renny coughed, twice, wet and shallow, and she remembered everything.

The bivouac cave sat at the base of a granite rib below the col, a hollow the glacier had ground out of the mountain's flank sometime in the last ice age and never bothered to finish. It was deep enough for six bodies and their gear if nobody minded intimacy, high enough to sit in if you chose your spot, and dry only in the sense that the water came down the back wall in a fine cold mist rather than a stream. August had found it on the second day of the storm, when the whiteout swallowed the col route and the temperature dropped through the floor of what the forecast had promised, and he had gotten them into it with the brisk economy of a man who had spent years learning exactly how mountains killed people and had no intention of providing fresh material.

Now he sat near the entrance with his back against the rock, working a whetstone along the edge of his ice axe in long, slow strokes, not because the edge needed it but because a man needed to do something with his hands or the silence would get into him. The blue light of the sealed entrance fell across his face and made him look older than he was, carved rather than lined.

Mara counted heads the way she did every time she woke, a habit from her rescue days: Renny curled in the deepest hollow with his bandaged wrist cradled against his chest, fever-bright and sleeping in the thin, restless way of the infected. Ilse beside him, awake, holding a thermometer she'd produced from the medical kit she wasn't supposed to have. Dev at the far wall, silent as always lately, his eyes on the middle distance where his conscience apparently lived. And August at the mouth of the cave, whetting steel against a storm that had sealed them in like a jar.

Six of them, and outside, somewhere in the white, Soren Vask and his men, and a mountain that had stopped pretending to be indifferent and started actively trying to keep them.

Mara pulled her sleeping bag around her shoulders and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn. Fuel for five more days at strict rationing, six if they burned the reserve. Food for four, at half-meals, and Renny needed more than half, not less, because fever ate a body from the inside. The storm had already cost them two days beyond the worst forecast, and the col above them was now, in all likelihood, a wind-slab trap that would need a day of careful assessment before anyone put a boot on it. Every hour they sat in this cave was an hour the margin thinned. She had learned margins on the Waddington rescue, on the Columbia icefields, on every trip where the weather turned and the plan turned with it, and she knew with a professional's certainty that theirs was nearly gone.

She also knew that none of that was what was keeping her awake.

What was keeping her awake was a conversation she'd been rehearsing for eleven days, across the lower trail and the ford and the tree line and the first technical pitch, and had not yet managed to begin.

* * *

The storm's third day passed the way all storm days pass: in small ceremonies. Renny, propped against his pack, insisted on cooking lunch himself one-handed and produced a pot of barley and dried mushrooms that tasted, in that altitude-hungry way, like the finest meal any of them had ever eaten. Ilse changed his dressing with steady hands, and Mara watched August watch her do it, and saw the last of his suspicion of the Brandt woman go out of him like a coal settling. Dev volunteered for the worst chores—melting snow, hauling the waste bucket to the cave's far throat—and did them without being asked, which in a bought man was either guilt or rehearsal, and Mara hadn't decided which.

In the afternoon the wind dropped for forty minutes, a false lull, and August was up and out of the cave like a man pulled on a wire, and they all heard him moving just beyond the entrance, probing, and then they heard the wind come back and heard him come back with it, shaking the spindrift off, saying nothing, and the nothing said everything. No window. Not today. Maybe not tomorrow.

It was after the evening ration, in the blue dark of the sealed entrance, that Mara finally stopped rehearsing and started talking.

"August."

He looked up from the whetstone.

"Walk with me." There was nowhere to walk. The cave was eleven paces long. "Sit with me, then. At the back. I want to say something and I'd rather the others didn't hear it."

Something moved behind his eyes—wariness, she thought, the reflex of a man who had spent five years learning what it cost when people said things about him. But he set down the axe and followed her to the rear of the cave, into the cold mist of the back wall, where the sound of the wind was a distant pressure and their voices would be swallowed whole.

She sat on her folded foam pad. He sat across from her, knees nearly touching hers, and waited. That was the thing about August Iversen that the scandal files never captured: he waited. He let silence do work. She had hated that about him for six years, and she understood now that she had hated it because it was the same patience she'd needed from him and never gotten.

"I signed on to this trip because of you," she said. "Not with you. Because of you."

He didn't move. The mist beaded on his hair.

"Six years ago, when the Kessik thing happened—the Fly expedition, the three dead—I was two years out of my rescue certification. I'd just been made lead on the regional team. Youngest lead they'd ever had." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice of a report rather than a confession, and made herself push through it. "You and I had done two courses together at the guide school. Shared a rope once on the Columbia. You probably don't remember."

"I remember," August said quietly. "You rapped past me on the ice because you thought my anchor was slow."

"It was slow." And then, because the point was not to spar: "After the expedition, the inquiry. They interviewed everyone you'd ever worked with. Everyone you'd ever trained with. I sat in a room in Kessik for four hours while a man from the guides' association asked me whether I'd ever seen you cut corners. Whether I thought you were the kind of guide who'd push a summit past the turnaround. And I said—I said I'd only shared a rope with you once, but yes, I thought you were aggressive. I said you were the kind of climber who took the direct line when the traverse was safer." Her hands had closed into fists inside her bag. She made them open. "It wasn't even a lie. It was worse than a lie. It was a truth I had no business offering, dressed up as professional judgment, because the man asking me questions had the power to end my career and I wanted him to leave my name out of it. And he did. He left my name out of it. And then six months later the association quietly declined to renew my lead certification, and when I asked why, the answer was that my judgment in the matter of Iversen had been called into question. By me. I'd done their work for them. I'd sharpened the knife and handed it over and then wondered why it found my throat."

The wind pressed against the mountain. Somewhere above them, tons of snow were moving in the dark, sorting themselves, deciding.

August said nothing for a long time. Long enough that she began to fill the silence—she caught herself starting to apologize, starting to explain, starting to do the thing she'd watched clients do for fifteen years, and she stopped herself, and made herself sit in it. He had waited for her for six years, in a way. He could have ninety seconds of it now.

"You were right," he said at last.

She blinked. "What?"

"On the Columbia. I was aggressive. I did take the direct line." He turned his hands over, looked at the palms as if reading something there. "The inquiry got that part correct, whatever else it got wrong. I took the direct line because I was racing Soren Vask's time up the same face, and because I had three clients who'd paid for a summit and a reputation I thought was worth more than the weather report. I pushed past the turnaround by ninety minutes. The serac came off at the ninety-first." His voice didn't break. It went somewhere else instead, down into a register she'd never heard him use on the trail, a voice with no performance in it at all. "I've done the arithmetic of those ninety minutes every day for six years. Some days it comes out that I killed them. Some days it comes out that the mountain did and I just held the door. I've never once gotten it to come out that it wasn't my fault, and I've stopped expecting it to. That's not the same as forgiving myself. I want to be clear about that. But it's the ground I stand on."

"I'm not asking you to absolve me," Mara said. "I'm telling you what I did. I helped bury you. Not with a shovel—with a sentence, in a room, and then I went home and let them take my certification anyway and told myself it was your fault, that your disgrace had contaminated everything it touched, and I signed on to this expedition to prove—to prove something, to you or to the association or to God, that I was better than the ruin you supposedly dragged me into. I took this job out of resentment, August. I weighed your cargo and checked your knots and watched your route decisions like a prosecutor, waiting for you to fail so I could be right about you."

"And?" he said.

And. She almost laughed. Six years of rehearsing and his whole response was *and*.

"And you haven't failed," she said. "You've been the best guide I've ever shared a mountain with. At Kettle Ford I watched you read that crossing the way a doctor reads a chart, and when Renny's train went sideways you didn't hesitate, you didn't perform, you just *solved it*, and I stood there on the bank thinking: this is the man they buried. This is what they put under the ground. And I've been carrying that since the ford, and I'm carrying it now, and I can't carry it into the col tomorrow. So I'm asking you for something, and you can refuse, and I'll deserve the refusal, but I'm asking."

"Ask."

"Forgive me. Not for the certification—they'd have taken it regardless, probably, once the association needed someone to blame for the climate around you. For the sentence. For the rope we shared and what I did with it afterward. I want to walk up that shoulder tomorrow without it."

August looked at her for a long time. The mist moved in the blue dark between them. She watched him decide something, watched it happen the way she'd watched him decide at the ford—no theater, just the internal machinery running and then stopping, resolved.

"I can't give you all of it," he said finally. "Not tonight. Maybe not ever, and I won't pretend to, because I've spent six years around people who pretended things at me and I'd rather cut off my own hand." He held up that hand, as if to make the point literal. "But I can give you this: I believe you. I believe you came because of resentment and I believe you're here now because of something else. I watched you re-weigh that manifest in Kessik when you thought no one was looking, and I watched you spot Vask's fake patrol from a ridge at four thousand meters, and I know what you are, Mara. What you did to me was one sentence in a room. What you've done on this mountain is three weeks of the best judgment I've ever worked beside. I can hold both of those. I don't have to choose tonight which one you are. Neither do you."

It wasn't absolution. She felt it land like a half-payment, a debt marked *partially settled, balance outstanding*, and she understood that the balance might never be settled, and that he was right not to pretend, and that this—this unfinished, unlovely, honest half-thing—was worth more than the clean forgiveness she'd imagined receiving. Forgiveness performed was just another kind of lie. He was offering her the true version: *I see what you did, I see what you are, and I will keep walking beside you while those two facts learn to live in the same rope team.*

"Half-accepted, then," she said.

"Half-accepted," he agreed. "The other half we'll negotiate at the summit. Assuming there's a summit."

"Assuming there's a summit," she said, and heard, in the echo of her own words, how much ground that assumption had lost since Kessik.

* * *

She slept, after that—really slept, for the first time in three days, the deep drugged sleep of a weight set down, even a weight only half set down. She dreamed of the Columbia, of blue ice and a young man rapping slowly on a bad anchor while she sailed past him full of certainty, and in the dream she stopped beside him and checked his anchor and it was fine, it had always been fine, and they had climbed the whole face together in weather that never came, and she woke with wet eyes in the cold dark and lay still and let the storm have its sound.

When she surfaced fully, the cave had shifted into its deepest hour. The blue at the entrance had gone black. Renny's breathing rasped at the far end, faster than it had been at dinner, and Ilse's silhouette was bent over him again, the thermometer's faint chemical glow in her hand. Dev was awake too, she realized—awake and sitting up, and there was something wrong with the geometry of him, something coiled and unresolved, his hand resting on the top of his pack where the relay beacon was stowed.

She watched him for a long moment across the dark. Watched his thumb move over the casing, once, twice, like a man touching a wound to see if it still hurt. Watched him look at Renny, at Ilse, at August sleeping by the entrance with the whetstone still loose in his hand.

Watched him close his eyes and open them again, and make no move at all.

Mara lay in the dark with her heart going slow and heavy, and told herself she had imagined it. Told herself it was the storm, the confinement, the way three days of white silence made a man's small gestures look like portents.

But she did not sleep again. And when the wind finally began to die in the hour before dawn—falling away in long diminishing sighs, like a beast lying down at last—she was already awake, already dressed, already watching Dev Okafor watch the entrance of the cave, waiting for the light that would let them move, and wondering what he was waiting for too.
