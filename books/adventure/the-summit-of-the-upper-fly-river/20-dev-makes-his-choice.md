## 20. Dev Makes His Choice

The beacon woke him an hour before dawn, buzzing against his sternum like a trapped wasp.

Dev had learned to sleep with it there, strapped inside the inner pocket of his down jacket, because sleeping with it anywhere else meant getting up in the cold to answer it, and getting up in the cold meant explanations. In the cave it was different. The whole crew slept in a heap of shared warmth, Ilse's head against her pack, Mara curled with her back to the wall, Renny breathing in the shallow, feverish rhythm that still scared Dev more than the storm did. August lay nearest the entrance, as he always did, his sleeping bag unzipped to the waist, one hand resting on the ice axe he never fully surrendered even to sleep.

Dev eased out of the pile and took the beacon to the far wall, where the blue light of the storm-dimmed ice glowed through the cave mouth like the inside of a lung. He thumbed the receive key and held the unit close to his ear so the voice wouldn't carry.

*Position check,* said the voice, flat and clipped through the compression. *Confirm party strength and condition.*

Dev looked at his thumb on the key. He had been answering these checks for nineteen days. *Party of six, one injured, pinned by storm, bivouacked above the Kettle drainage.* He had given them everything—elevations, route intentions, the state of Renny's wrist, the fact that August Iversen still led from the front and still, after everything, read the snow better than any man Dev had ever walked behind. He had given them all of it in coded bursts, three words at a time, and every transmission had felt at first like nothing, like paperwork, like a job. Somewhere on the parallel valley route, Soren Vask had built a picture of them out of Dev's reports, the way a man builds a fire one twig at a time, and Dev had told himself the fire was for warmth.

He no longer believed that.

*Confirm,* the voice repeated.

He didn't press the key.

The message queue light blinked amber. There was more. He waited, watching his own breath feather against the ice wall, and after a long moment the beacon shivered again and delivered the rest.

*Vask to Okafor. Storm clears tomorrow, 0900. Party will descend via the east flank. There is a chute at grid four-seven-one, north-facing, loads above the treeline. You will lead them into it. Iversen will defer to you if you frame it as a shortcut—tell him the lower trail is drifted in, tell him anything. When the party is committed, you step aside. What comes down the chute is not your concern. Your extraction is arranged. Your payment is doubled. Confirm.*

Dev read it three times. The words didn't change.

North-facing chute, loads above the treeline. He had seen it two days ago from the col, before the storm closed in—a steep gully running down from the hanging glaciers, choked with fresh snow, the kind of terrain that looked like a road and behaved like a gun. A party of six, roped together, carrying a man with a fever, descending into that at mid-morning when the sun had been working on the slopes for two hours—Dev was a porter, not a guide, but he had grown up below mountains, in a country where the mountains took people every year, and he knew what a loaded slope sounded like when it decided. He had heard it once, from a distance. He had heard the silence afterward, too, which was worse.

*Confirm,* the beacon prompted, patient as a debt collector.

He thought about the money. He made himself think about it properly, the way he had made himself think about it when he signed on, because he had never been a man who lied to himself about his own motives and he wasn't going to start now. Eight months of pay. More than his father had made in a decade in the pits. Enough to buy his mother the house back. Enough to never again stand in a hiring line in Kessik while men looked through him. He had told himself the job was surveillance, that was all—watch, report, protect the client's interests. Everyone watched everyone in this business. Everyone reported. He had told himself that the information was harmless because information was always harmless until it wasn't, and he had kept telling himself that right up until the shots came down the scree and he had stood on the ridge with the others and watched Mara Quist read the deception in the pattern of the fire and understood, with a clarity that had not left him since, that he was the reason they were hunted.

And now they wanted the chute.

He looked across the cave at the sleeping pile. Renny had cooked for him every night since the ford, had saved him the larger share of the rice twice without saying so, had taught him the trick to keeping the stove lit at altitude. Mara had re-rigged his pack straps on the second day without being asked, the way you fix things for someone you expect to keep. Ilse had sat with him through the worst of the storm watch and asked him about his mother's house with a genuine question, not a polite one. And August—August had pulled him out of a crevasse two nights ago on the glacier edge, had held the rope with his whole life in his hands and never once, in the dark, in the wind, asked Dev whether he deserved saving.

*Confirm,* said the beacon.

"No," Dev said, out loud, to a machine, in a whisper, and the word felt like the first honest thing he had said in nineteen days.

He didn't confirm. He did something better and worse. He took the beacon in both hands and carried it to the cave entrance, where the wind came through in long slow pulses, and he held it out over the drop and thought about the extraction, the doubled payment, his mother's house, and then he thought about Renny's breath going shallow and stopping somewhere under a hundred tons of snow because Dev Okafor had framed a lie well enough that a good man deferred to him.

He threw it. Not far—his arm wasn't what it was at twenty—but far enough that it cleared the lip of the drop and dropped into the blue dark below the cave mouth, and he heard it hit, one small plastic crack of a sound, and then the wind took over and there was nothing.

The silence afterward was enormous. He stood there shaking, not from cold, or not only from cold, and understood that he had just done the one thing that could not be undone. Vask would expect a confirmation by 0900. When it didn't come, Vask would know. There was no version of this where Dev Okafor stayed a ghost in the machine. He had been a bought man for nineteen days and now he was a marked one, and the strange thing, the thing he would remember for the rest of his life, was how light he felt—how the fear was real and total and yet sat on top of something that felt almost like standing up straight after years of stooping.

He turned back into the cave.

August was awake. Of course he was awake. He was sitting up against the wall with the axe across his knees, watching Dev with the flat patient attention of a man who had spent nineteen days watching everyone and had seen a porter stand at the cave mouth for a long time doing nothing, and had drawn his own conclusions, or at least his questions.

"Couldn't sleep," Dev said.

"No," August said. "Neither could I." He didn't move. The storm hissed beyond the entrance. "You were talking to someone."

It wasn't a question, and Dev understood that the hour for versions was over. He could give August a version—the relay beacon was standard safety equipment, he was checking the forecast, he'd dropped it by accident—and August would nod and file it away and the machine of the crew would grind on with the lie still in its gears, and tomorrow, if Dev led them anywhere, August would be watching his every step, and the trust would be a surface over a void. Or he could do the other thing. The thing his father had done once, badly, in front of the whole pit, when the foreman falsified the safety report and his father stood up in the mess and said the numbers were wrong. It had cost them everything. Dev had spent his whole life being careful never to be his father.

He sat down across from August, close enough that they wouldn't need to raise their voices over the storm.

"I have to tell you something," he said, "and when I'm done, you're going to want to put me off this mountain. I'd want to put me off this mountain. But I'm asking you to hear all of it first."

August's eyes didn't change. "Go on."

"My name isn't just Dev Okafor, porter, references on file. My references are clean because they were bought. I was hired nineteen days ago by a man inside Brandt Mining—I never met him, I know his voice, I know the account number my money comes from—and my job was to report your position, your route, your strength, your condition. Every day. To Soren Vask."

He watched the name land. August didn't flinch, but something behind his face went very still, the way a slope goes still before it moves.

"The beacon I carried," Dev went on, because now that the dam was broken the words came fast, nineteen days of them, "the relay I told you was for weather. It was for him. I told him about the ford. I told him about Renny's wrist. I told him you were pinned here. And this morning he ordered me to lead you into the east-flank chute when the storm clears—tell you the lower trail is drifted in, get you committed, and step aside." His voice cracked and he let it. "He's not trying to intercept you anymore, August. He's trying to bury you. All of you. And I was the shovel."

The cave was silent except for the wind. Mara had gone still in her sleeping bag—awake, Dev realized, had probably been awake for all of it—and her eyes were open and fixed on him with an expression he couldn't read. Ilse was sitting up too, one hand at her throat.

August looked at him for a long time. Long enough that Dev felt the cold coming up through the ice floor and into his bones, felt the rope of his whole future pay out into the dark.

"You threw the beacon," August said at last.

Dev blinked. "Yes."

"Off the entrance side. I heard it hit." August turned the ice axe over in his hands, once, slowly. "You could have kept it. Played it out. Told him you lost it in the storm. Bought yourself another week of being two people." He looked up. "Why didn't you?"

And Dev, who had prepared arguments and confessions and pleas, found that the true answer was the only one left. "Because you pulled me out of the crevasse," he said. "And you never asked if I was worth it. I've been asking myself ever since. This morning the answer came, and it wasn't the one he paid for."

August nodded, once, the way he nodded at a snow bridge that held. Then he said, in the voice he used on the rope, the voice that left no room: "Everything he knows, we have to assume he knows. Every route we've discussed, every camp, every weakness. Which means every route we've discussed is dead to us." He was already looking past Dev, at the cave mouth, at the storm, at the shape of the country beyond it. "Except the one we haven't discussed."

Mara was up now, silent, moving to sit at August's shoulder. "The east flank is out," she said. "The lower trail he'll be watching. The col route is compromised."

"The shoulder," Ilse said quietly.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Dev didn't know what the shoulder was, but he knew what it meant by the way Mara looked at August, and by the way August didn't answer right away, and by the way the wind chose that exact moment to rise to a scream outside the entrance and fall away again, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"You said you'd never take anyone up there," Mara said.

"I said a lot of things," August said. He looked around the cave—at Mara, at Ilse, at Renny still sleeping his shallow sleep, at Dev, last of all, and longest. "First we deal with this. Dev stays or goes, and that's not my decision alone."

"It's mine to ask for," Dev said. "And I'm asking to stay. Not because I've earned it. Because there's a chute on the east flank with your name on it, and I'm the only one who knows exactly where Vask expects you to be, and when. A man can be useful after he's been a traitor. He just can't pretend he wasn't one."

August studied him for a moment more, and then something in his face shifted—not forgiveness, Dev would not presume to that, not yet, maybe not ever—but recognition, the look of a man who had also once stood at the bottom of what he'd done and decided to climb out of it rather than live in it.

"You'll carry double," August said. "Renny's share of the rope work. Every hard pitch. And if we live through this, you'll testify. Same as the rest of us. Your name goes in the record next to everything you did."

"Yes," Dev said. It was the easiest word he had ever said.

"Then sleep," August said. "All of you. The storm breaks in the morning, and when it does, we're not going where he expects us." He looked at the ice wall, at the blue dark beyond it, and his voice dropped low, almost to himself. "We're going up the shoulder."

* * *

Dev didn't sleep. He sat against the wall with his pack hugged to his chest and watched the cave mouth go from black to grey to a pale, blown-silver light, and he thought about his mother's house, and about how he would never send her the money now, and about how she would have preferred it that way, if he had ever had the courage to ask her.

Near dawn, Renny woke feverish and asked for water, and Dev was the one who brought it, melting snow over the stove with steady hands, and Renny looked up at him and said, "You look terrible, Okafor," and Dev said, "You should see the other guy," and Renny laughed, a small cracked sound, and drank.

Outside, the storm was dying. The wind fell away in stages, the way a fever breaks, and by first light the silence on the mountain was so complete that Dev could hear his own heart. He stepped to the cave mouth and looked out at a world scoured and rebuilt—new snow to the depth of a man's chest on every lee slope, the east flank gleaming white and innocent and loaded, the chute invisible under its blanket, waiting.

And beyond it, high and to the north, he could see it: the shoulder. A dark fin of rock rising out of the white, unclimbed, unroped, impossible, catching the first sun like a blade.

Behind him, he heard August come up beside him, and heard the long breath the man took, like a swimmer before deep water.

"He'll know by now," Dev said. "The beacon's silence. He'll know I turned."

"I know," August said. His eyes were on the shoulder, and there was something in his face Dev had never seen there before—not fear exactly, and not peace either, but the place where the two of them met and shook hands. "Which means we stop being invisible today." He shouldered his pack. "All of us."

They struck camp in the blue shadow of the fin, six small figures on a white mountain, and began to climb.
