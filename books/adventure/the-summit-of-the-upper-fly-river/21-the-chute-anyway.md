## 21. The Chute Anyway

The storm came back on the second morning after Dev's confession, and it came the way bad news comes—not all at once, but in a pressure you feel before you understand it.

August stood at the mouth of the bivouac ledge and watched the sky over the Col of Hanging Ice turn the color of old pewter. The wind had swung north in the night, and with it had come a smell he knew too well, a smell like iron and cold stone, the smell of a front that intended to stay. Below them, the valley they had to cross lay in a deceptive calm, the fresh snow from three days ago lying smooth as a bedsheet over the slopes of the chute.

The chute. Vask's chute. The one Dev had been ordered to steer them into.

He'd spent the night with the map weighted under stones, tracing and retracing the alternatives, and the alternatives had all died the same death: the eastern traverse was corniced to hell after the storm, the high shoulder was a two-day climb they didn't have food for, and the low forest route was now behind them, sealed by the rockfall that had forced them up in the first place. The map offered him a menu of ways to die, and the chute was simply the fastest one.

"You're going to look at it all morning," Mara said, coming to stand beside him, "or you're going to look at it once and decide."

"I'm deciding."

"You're performing deciding. There's a difference. I've watched you do both."

He almost smiled. Almost. Behind them, Renny sat against the rock with his splinted wrist cradled in his lap, and Ilse was repacking the rope in long, careful coils, and Dev stood apart from all of them the way a man stands when he no longer trusts himself to be counted among the crew. Dev hadn't slept either. August had heard him turning in the dark, heard the small sounds a man makes when he's rehearsing a confession he's already made and can't stop making.

"The line through the chute," August said. "You saw it yesterday from the ridge."

"I saw a line." Mara pulled her balaclava down and spoke through wool. "I saw a lot of white with a wish in the middle of it. That's not the same thing."

"It's a marginal line. It's the difference between a slope that loads and a slope that slides. There's a rib of bedrock running down the left side—look, where the wind's stripped it bare. Rock releases heat. Snow doesn't bond to it. If we stay on the rib, we're on the one part of that face that can't avalanche."

"And if we drift off the rib?"

"Then we're in the chute."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Wind worried at the edge of the ledge, plucking loose crystals off the snow and carrying them away in little smoking streams. "How wide is the rib?"

"Two meters. Maybe three."

"And how long?"

"Four hundred meters of descent, then the crossing, then the trees."

"Two meters wide for four hundred meters," Mara said, "in fresh snow, carrying a litter's worth of gear, with six people who haven't eaten properly in two days." She turned to look at him, and her eyes above the balaclava were flat and clear and without any comfort in them at all. "You're describing a tightrope, August. You know that, yes? You're describing a tightrope over a drop."

"I'm describing the only way through before this front buries the whole valley."

"And if the front buries it while we're in it?"

He didn't answer that, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie, and he had spent six years building a life out of refusing to lie on mountains.

* * *

He gathered them at the ledge's mouth and laid it out the way he'd learned to lay things out after the disaster—plainly, completely, with nothing held back. That was the lesson the mountain had taught him at the price of three lives: people die when guides protect them from the truth.

"The front arrives tonight, maybe tomorrow morning. After that, this valley closes for the season and we're on the wrong side of it with food for three days and a man who needs a doctor." He nodded at Dev's leg, strapped and splinted, the bone set as well as Renny's fevered hands could set it. "There is one route. The chute Vask wanted us in. We go through it on the bedrock rib, single file, roped in pairs, and nobody—nobody—moves until the person ahead is clear. If the rib fails, we lose the rib. If the rib holds, we're through by midday and under the trees before dark."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Ilse asked.

She asked it the way she asked everything now—not to challenge him, but because she had earned the right to the whole arithmetic. Six days ago he'd have read the question as an heiress's impatience. Now he heard what it was: a woman who had decided to stop being protected.

"Then some of us are through and some of us aren't," he said. "I won't dress it up. This is the worst line I've ever chosen, and I'm choosing it because every other line is worse. You can vote against it. I'll honor that. But the vote has to be unanimous either way, because a crew that goes down a slope half-believing in it is already dead."

They looked at each other. Renny, fever-bright and stubborn, said, "I didn't come this far to be left on a ledge." Dev said nothing at all, but he moved to the front of the group and stood there, and August understood that the man was offering himself as the first body down the slope—the place where the mistake, if there was one, would land first. Atonement has a grammar, and this was Dev's: the forward position.

"No," August said. "I lead. That's not a vote, that's a fact of the trade. Dev, you're second. Your leg's bad but your eyes are good, and I need someone behind me who'll shout the second the snow sounds wrong."

They voted. Six for. Nobody looked surprised, and nobody looked happy, and both of those things were right.

* * *

They reached the top of the chute an hour after first light, and August made them stop and read the slope the way you read a hostile face before you speak to it.

The chute itself was a broad gouge in the mountainside, two hundred meters wide at the top, funneling down to a throat between rock towers, filled with the storm's fresh load lying over the old wind-slab. Even from above he could see the layering—the new snow sitting proud and unbonded on the crust beneath, a book with a loose page in it, waiting for a hand to turn it. Somewhere on that slope was the weight that would turn it. A single climber. A rope team. A horse.

The rib ran down the left flank like a spine. Bare gray rock showed through in patches where the wind had scoured it, and the snow on the rib was thin, crusted, welded to the stone by three nights of refreeze. It was exactly what he'd told them. It was also, he could now see at close range, narrower than he'd told them. In places the rib pinched to barely the width of a man's shoulders, with the scoured hard-snow of the chute wall on one side and open air over the valley on the other.

"Two meters," Mara said quietly, beside him. "You said."

"I measured generously."

"You measured like a man who'd already decided."

"I did decide," August said. "Yesterday. I just wanted the mountain to talk me out of it." He looked at the sky. The pewter had thickened; the first spits of snow were already coming down, dry and granular, the kind that loads a slope fast. "It didn't. Rope up. Pairs. Ilse with me on the lead rope—no, wait." He stopped himself. He'd been about to put her where his instinct wanted her, close, where he could reach her. He made himself undo it. "Renny with me—he's the one who can't catch himself if he slips. Ilse with Mara. Dev anchors the rear with the spare line."

They rigged the horses' loads into two carry-packs and split the feed, and then came the question he'd been dreading, because he'd known the answer before he asked it. The chute was no place for animals. The rib was less than no place. But the horses were how they'd carried the tents, the stove, the rope, the food, and without them the crossing to the far valley became a starvation march.

"The gray can do the rib," Mara said. She'd raised the mare from a colt; her voice was level in the way that costs something. "She's sure-footed and she's light. The bay's too heavy, and he knows it—look at him. He's already saying no."

The bay was indeed saying no, ears back, testing the wind, a big honest animal telling them plainly what he thought of the slope below. August walked him to the edge of the rib and let him look, and the horse looked, and planted his feet.

"He'll go if I lead him," Renny said. "He trusts me."

"He'll go if you lead him," August said, "and he'll put one hoof six inches off the rib, because that's what a thousand pounds does when it's frightened, and six inches off the rib is the chute." He put his hand on the bay's neck. The animal's hide shivered under his palm like water. "We walk him down the chute itself, on the low angle at the mouth, before the loading gets bad, and we take the feed off him and carry it. If the slope holds for twenty minutes, it holds for him. If it doesn't—" He stopped.

"If it doesn't, we lose him and the feed," Mara finished. "Say it. This crew runs on people saying it."

"If it doesn't, we lose him and the feed," August said.

* * *

The plan was simple, and simple plans on mountains are the ones that kill you, because the mountain doesn't fight the complicated parts.

August went first down the rib with Renny on the rope behind him, and for the first hundred meters it was almost—almost—what he'd promised them. The rock held under his boots. The crusted snow rang faintly, like porcelain, and held. Below and to his right the chute lay open and white and silent, and the silence of a loaded slope is not peace; it is a held breath, and every step he took he listened to that breath for the smallest stutter.

At the pinch—the place where the rib narrowed to a shoulders' width with air on both sides—he stopped and set an anchor and talked Renny across, hand over hand, the cook's face gray above his beard, his splinted wrist held tight against his chest. Renny crossed. August breathed. Behind them, small and far above, he could see Mara bring Ilse to the pinch, and the two of them folded against the rock like letters being posted through a slot, and then they were across, and then Dev came down with the deliberate, ugly care of a man on a bad leg who has decided that ugly care is the only style available to him.

Then the horses.

They'd sent the bay down the low mouth of the chute first, Renny's idea in the end—Renny knew animals better than any of them, fever or no—and from the rib August watched the big horse go down the gentler tongue of snow at the chute's throat, picking his way, blowing plumes of breath, and for a few minutes it looked like the one thing that day that would go right.

The front arrived early.

It didn't build. It simply arrived, the way a door slams—the wind swinging hard into the valley and the snow coming down in a white hiss that erased the far side of the chute in seconds. August heard Mara curse above him. He felt the temperature fall through his gloves like a change in the blood.

And the bay, half-way across the mouth, spooked.

Later, August would replay it a thousand times and find the exact moment—the gust hitting the horse's flank, the whiteout swallowing the far tower, some memory of fear older than training—and there would be nothing in that moment to have done differently, which was its own particular cruelty. The bay wheeled. Two steps took him off the low tongue and onto the steeper load above it, and the slope spoke.

It didn't roar. That was the terrible thing, how quietly it began—a sound like a long exhale, a soft slumping whisper, the whole face of the chute above the horse shifting a hand's width, and then the hand's width became a meter and the whisper became a hiss and the hiss became a sound like a freight train falling downstairs, and the bay was running, and the slab was running, and the horse was fast but the mountain was the mountain.

"Rope team, DOWN—anchor—DOWN!" August had them on the rib with anchors set before the slide reached the throat, all of them flattened against the bedrock while the world below went white and deafening. The slab took the low tongue and the throat and the runout in eleven seconds—August counted, because counting was the only thing his hands could do—and when it was over and the powder fog began to settle, the chute's mouth was a smooth new surface, unbroken, featureless, and the bay was not in it.

They found the packs forty meters down in the debris field, torn free, one of them split open along its whole length. Feed grain scattered and buried. Half their food, gone into the mountain in the time it takes to say a sentence.

Renny stood at the edge of the debris and called the horse's name three times, his voice cracking on the third, and the mountain said nothing back, and after the third call he stopped, because he was a practical man even with a fever, and practical men know the sound a silence makes when it's final.

* * *

They came off the rib into the trees at the last of the light, six people and one gray mare, moving through the timber in a silence that had nothing to do with stealth. The snow fell steadily now, the front settled in for the night, and every step of the descent August did the arithmetic he didn't want to do and couldn't stop doing.

Half the food gone. Four days to the far valley at their best pace, and Dev's leg would not permit their best pace. Five days, then. Six. Rations for three, cut thin. Cut thinner. The numbers didn't care about what anyone deserved. That was the lesson of the chute, the lesson it taught in eleven seconds: the margin for error was not merely gone. It had never been as large as he'd believed. He had spent six years thinking that caution was a wall he'd built around himself, and today the mountain had shown him the wall was made of snow.

They made camp under a spruce stand, walls of snow blocks cut in the failing light, the stove hissing over a measured handful of fuel. Mara divided the evening ration into six portions with a precision that was its own kind of grief. Nobody spoke about the bay. Nobody had to. The gray mare stood hipshot at the edge of the camp with her head low, and Renny went to her and stood with his forehead against her neck for a long time, and that was the eulogy.

Ilse sat down next to August at the snow wall, close enough that their shoulders touched through the layers, and looked out into the falling dark.

"You chose the line that held," she said. "Everyone's alive."

"The line that held," August said. "And the horse." He turned his hands over and looked at them, at the rope burns and the cold-swollen knuckles, at the instruments that had done exactly what he asked of them and saved exactly what they could. "I keep thinking about what Tomas would say. He'd say I traded a horse for six people and got the better of the bargain. He'd be right. It doesn't feel like being right."

"It isn't supposed to feel like being right," Ilse said. "It's supposed to feel like this. That's how you know you haven't stopped being someone who counts the cost." She was quiet a moment, and then she said, "Tomorrow, when we move—I heard you doing the numbers at the stove. Three days of food, five days of walking. I can do that arithmetic too, August. I grew up in a house built on arithmetic." Her breath steamed and drifted. "So say it to all of us in the morning. Whatever the plan is. Say it the way you said the chute."

"I will."

"Because there's something else, isn't there." It wasn't a question. She'd learned to read him the way Mara had, the way a crew learns, and he realized with a small cold shock that he'd given himself away—some set of his jaw, some pause too long at the stove. "There's something you haven't said yet."

August looked out at the snow falling through the black trees, at the white silence where a horse had been, and thought about the thing he'd seen from the rib at midday, in the one minute the fog had opened—the far valley below them, and on the trail at its mouth, small with distance but unmistakable, a line of men moving. Not toward the pass. Toward the lower hut. The hut that stood on the only safe descent.

Vask hadn't been trying to catch them in the chute. The chute had only ever been the cheap option. Vask had gone ahead and closed the door.

"Tomorrow," August said, and put his hand briefly over hers, and the snow fell on the camp, and the mare breathed, and the mountain kept what it had taken.
