## 22. Hunger Arithmetic

Renny counted the food the way he used to count cards, back in another life, in another town, before kitchens and trail cookery had saved him from himself. He laid everything out on the spread tarp inside the shelter hut's lee wall—every tin, every waxed block of pemmican, every sack of flour gone half to damp—and he made the numbers tell him the truth.

The truth was this: four days. Four days at full rations for five people, if you didn't count the mare, who would graze what there was to graze and grow thinner doing it. Six days at three-quarter rations. Eight days at half, if nobody's body rebelled against the arithmetic, if nobody burned more than they took in, if the weather held and the route held and Dev's leg held.

Dev's leg was not going to hold. Renny knew that the way a cook knows a sauce is about to break—not from any single sign but from the whole gestalt of it, the smell and the set and the silence underneath. The wound had been cleaned and splinted and bound with Ilse's careful hands, and still it had that heat to it when Renny carried the man his portion, that sweetish wrongness under the antiseptic smell that meant infection working its way deeper while everyone above it talked about routes and rations and the col.

So: eight days at half rations, or four days at full, and either way the last day would be spent descending into whatever Vask had waiting below, on legs fed at half strength, carrying a man whose leg was cooking itself from the inside out.

Renny did the thing he always did with bad numbers. He cooked anyway.

He melted snow in the big pot, stirred in the flour with the practiced wrist-flick that kept lumps from forming, added the pemmican fat and a pinch of the salt that was running low, and produced from almost nothing a porridge that smelled like a reason to keep walking. That was the job, as he understood it. Not just calories. Reason. A man could march on hunger if the hunger came with a smell worth marching toward.

Mara came in first, stamping snow off her boots, then August, then Ilse with her arm through Dev's, taking more of his weight than the boy wanted to admit. They arranged themselves around the pot with the wordless economy of people who had been eating together too long to need words for it. Renny ladled out five portions, and then stood a moment with the pot in his hands, weighing it, because he had already weighed it twice in his head and the number hadn't changed.

He gave Dev his full portion. He gave Ilse hers, because she'd been burning fuel on the carries. He gave Mara hers, because Mara never complained and therefore never got watched closely enough. And then he divided what remained between August's bowl and his own, and the division was not equal, and he made sure August saw him make it unequal.

August looked at the bowl. Looked at Renny. Said nothing, which was its own kind of conversation between them, and ate.

* * *

It was later, in the blue hour when the storm-light failed and the hut's single candle made more shadow than light, that Renny said it.

They were all there. That was the thing—he'd meant to say it privately, to August alone, man to man over the dishes the way these things were supposed to be done. But the hut was small and the cold drove everyone inside and there was no private anywhere, so he said it with all of them listening, which maybe was better, because a thing said in front of witnesses is harder to argue down.

"I've been counting," Renny said. He was cleaning the pot with snow, working it in slow circles, not looking up. "We've got four days at full feed or eight at half, and I've done the sums both ways, and here's what the sums say. They say we're short. Not by much. But short."

"We knew that coming into the chute," Mara said.

"We knew it *hoping*," Renny said. "Hoping's not a ration." He set the pot down. His wrist—the good one; the other was strapped and useless since the col—ached with the cold, and he was grateful for the ache, because it gave his voice something to lean on. "There's a hut. Old survey hut, maybe two hours on, the one marked on Vask's own map back at Kettle Ford. It'll have a stove, maybe an old cache, walls at least. I stay there. You take my share and split it four ways and the arithmetic works. Full rations, four days, everybody strong enough to move fast and fight if it comes to fighting."

Nobody spoke. The candle flame leaned in some draft Renny couldn't feel.

"You're slowing us," Renny went on, because now that he'd started he might as well finish, and finishing things was the one discipline he'd never lost. "I'm not ashamed of it. A man with one hand and a fever coming on slows a rope team, slows a carry, doubles the watch. I've known it since the chute. I've been waiting for the numbers to prove it so I could say it without it being self-pity." He finally looked up, found August's face in the dark. "It's not self-pity, August. It's bookkeeping."

"Renny," Ilse started.

"It's bookkeeping," he said again, gentler. "And I'm asking you to let me do it."

* * *

August was quiet long enough that Renny thought, absurdly, of bread proving—of dough sitting under cloth, of the patience required, of how the worst thing you could do was keep punching it down to check on it. So he waited. Around them the others breathed. Dev shifted against the wall and winced and pretended he hadn't. Outside, the wind worked at the hut's old boards like a patient carpenter looking for the loose ones.

"No," August said.

"August—"

"No." Not loud. Not angry. Just final, the way a door closes. "You want to talk bookkeeping? Fine. Here's mine." He held up one finger. "One: a man alone in a locked valley with Soren Vask's men moving in it isn't safe, he's bait. Two: that hut hasn't been stocked since before you were cooking professionally, and if its stove draws badly—and old huts' stoves draw badly—you'll be burning body heat you don't have to stay warm. Three." The third finger came up and stayed. "Three: I have left a man behind before. On the mountain, following orders, doing the sensible arithmetic. Three men. I know exactly what it costs, and it cost me eleven years, and I am not paying it again. Not for rations. Not for anything."

Renny felt something in his chest turn over. "That's sentiment, not sums."

"It's both," August said. "The best decisions usually are. You taught me that—with the food. Every meal on this trip has been a sum and a sentiment at once, and we're all still walking because of it." He leaned forward into the candlelight, and Renny saw the tiredness in him, real and unhidden for once. "We don't leave people. That's the whole ledger. That's the entry that matters. Everything else is columns."

"The mare eats too," Mara said quietly. "Her feed factors in. If we split Renny's share four ways instead of five—"

"Then we go hungry together," August said, "and we arrive hungry together, and hungry men who arrived together beat fed men who arrived apart. Ask anyone who's survived anything."

Silence again. Then Dev, from the shadows, his voice thinned by fever but steady: "He's right. And Renny—I'd be carrying your guilt along with my leg, and I can't afford the weight."

A sound came out of Renny that was half laugh, half surrender. He looked around the hut—at Ilse watching him with those level eyes that missed nothing, at Mara already refiguring something in a battered notebook, at Dev pale and grinning, at August with his stubborn closed face that wasn't closed at all, really, hadn't been for weeks.

"Well," Renny said. "Then somebody help me recalculate, because if I'm staying, I'm cooking double-strength, and I'll need every scrap of flour we've got."

Ilse laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first laugh in days—and reached for the flour sack, and the candle flame steadied, and outside the wind went on working at the boards, patient as ever, finding nothing loose.

But late that night, lying awake between the sleepers, Renny heard it: far off down the valley, thin on the wind, a sound that was not wind and not rockfall and not any animal he knew.

A whistle. Two notes, repeated. The kind a man makes to call a dog—or to signal across a distance to other men.

Renny lay still in the dark and listened, and did not wake anyone, and began, silently, to count again.
