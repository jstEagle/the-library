## 23. Vask Closes the Valley

The hut sat where the valley pinched shut, a squat timber box with a rusted stovepipe and a view of everything that mattered. Soren Vask had known about it for eleven years—had slept in it once, in another life, when the Upper Fly was still a place he loved and not a ledger entry. Now he stood on its porch with his hands in the pockets of a down jacket that cost more than August Iversen's entire outfitting operation, and he watched his men turn the last safe door out of the mountains into a tollgate.

Kovac was inside already, sweeping the floor with the barrel of his rifle like he was tidying up for guests. The younger one, Pell, was stacking firewood against the north wall in a neat, compulsive row, as though order could be imposed on a situation that had long since stopped obeying anyone. The third man, Halloran—the deserter from the shoulder climb had been replaced by him two days ago, airlifted into the lower valley by a helicopter whose registration number Vask had paid to have scrubbed from three databases—was checking the radio mast with a signal meter and an expression of professional boredom.

Three men. One hut. One trail, and it ran straight past their window.

Vask had occupied worse positions with fewer men. He'd held a col in Karakoram weather for six days with two frostbitten Uzbeks and a case of expired rations, waiting to intercept a rival expedition's resupply. That had been business too. Everything, in the end, was business. That was the lesson the mountains had taught him, or perhaps the lesson he'd brought to them and they had merely confirmed: sentiment was ballast, and ballast got people killed.

He went inside. The hut smelled of old smoke and mouse droppings and the ghost of someone's instant coffee. Kovac had already claimed the bunk nearest the stove, which was correct; rank had its small privileges, and Vask had never been a man to waste energy on false modesty. He set the satellite phone on the table between a tin plate and a deck of cards someone had left face-down years ago, and he looked at it for a long time.

One call. That was all this required. One call, and either the thing ended here, in this valley, with everyone alive and paid—or it ended higher up, in the cold, in ways he did not particularly want to imagine but had prepared for anyway.

He thought about which version he preferred.

* * *

The truth—which Vask admitted to himself only in the privacy of his own skull, where no auditor could reach it—was that the plan had been unraveling since the cornice.

He had committed six men to the shoulder. He had told himself it was a calculated risk, the kind of gamble that separated operators from tourists. Pride, yes. But also arithmetic: if Iversen reached the far side with those documents, Vask's name went into a regulatory file beside Henrik Brandt's, and no amount of money laundered a signature off a falsified survey. He had helped build the lie fifteen years ago, back when he was a rising guide with a talent for making inconvenient readings disappear and a mentor named Brandt who rewarded discretion with contracts. The original cores, the original ledger—they existed somewhere in that party's baggage, and they were the only physical proof that the numbers Brandt Mining had filed for a decade and a half were fiction.

So he climbed. His men followed because he paid them and because, for a while, his certainty was contagious.

Then the mountain started collecting. A porter turned an ankle at the icefall and had to be walked down alone. Another man's crampon snapped at the traverse and Vask made the call to push on without him, which the man had taken personally. And then, two nights ago, the youngest of them—a quiet Swede named Lindqvist who had signed on for hazard pay and nothing deeper—took his share of the advance, left his tent standing, and walked out during the white hours. Left a note under a stone: *Not my fight. Not my mountain.*

Vask had read the note twice and felt something move in his chest that he refused to name. Contempt, probably. Contempt was safe. It was the emotion he kept polished for exactly these occasions, the way other men kept a lucky coin.

But contempt didn't refill a rope team. Contempt didn't change the fact that he now had four men fit to move, that two of them were grumbling openly about the terms, and that Iversen's crew—crippled porter, half-rations, injured cook and all—had crossed the crest ahead of him and were descending toward the very valley Vask now sealed.

There was a version of Soren Vask, younger, who would have read that as humiliation. The older version read it as information. Iversen had done what Vask had not: he had spent his pride instead of hoarding it. He'd gone over the top of the range through weather that sane men hid from, carrying a broken man on a litter, and he'd made it. Vask's team, better equipped, better fed, unburdened by conscience or cargo, had lost the race over the same ground.

Which meant the race was no longer over the ground. It was over the valley. And valleys could be owned.

* * *

He stepped back onto the porch as the light went amber behind the western ridgeline. The trail below the hut was visible for nearly four hundred meters before it bent around a moraine and vanished into the forest belt. Anyone coming down would be exposed across that stretch—visible, slow, tired. Tired men don't run. Tired men with a littered companion on their backs don't even try.

Kovac came out and stood beside him, close enough that Vask could smell the woodsmoke on his jacket.

"Radio check," Kovac said. "Mast's good. We've got the relay at Kessik, clean signal."

"Any traffic?"

"One burst from the ranger station this morning. Routine weather. Nothing addressed to anyone."

Vask nodded slowly. Nothing addressed to anyone. He filed that away. If Iversen had contacted the station, there would have been something—a request, a position report, a name. There wasn't. Which meant either the party was still above the forest, moving slower than Vask had estimated, or Iversen was staying dark out of the same instinct that had kept him invisible for eleven years.

Both possibilities pleased him. A man hiding is a man who can be threatened with exposure. A man moving blind is a man who can be herded.

"You want us to take them at the moraine bend?" Pell asked from the woodpile. "Four hundred meters of open ground. We'd own it."

"No," Vask said, and heard his own voice come out flat and certain, the voice of a man who had thought this through in the shower of some hotel in a town whose name he hadn't bothered to learn. "No shooting unless they force it. Dead guides make inquiries. Dead clients make investigations. I need this finished quietly, and quiet means the documents change hands and everyone walks down under their own power."

"And if they don't hand anything over?"

Vask looked at the young man for a moment. Pell had a good jaw and bad patience, and Vask had hired him mostly for the first quality, hoping to train out the second.

"Then we wait," Vask said. "They're coming down off a winter ridge with half their food gone and a man who can't walk. They will come down the only trail there is, and it runs through us. Hunger negotiates better than I ever could."

He let that sit in the cold air between them. Then he went back inside, sat at the table, and picked up the satellite phone.

* * *

There was a ritual to it, Vask found. Thumbing through the contact list until he reached the number he'd assigned to Iversen's borrowed handset—a cheap thing bought in Kessik under a false name, because of course August Iversen traveled dark, of course he did, that was the whole architecture of the man—and pressing the green key, and hearing the connection handshake whine up through the satellites like something climbing.

It rang eight times. Vask counted. On the ninth, it would have gone to nothing, and he would have tried again at dawn, and again after that, patient as erosion. But on the eighth ring a voice answered—not Iversen's voice, he knew that instantly, but a woman's, clipped and guarded, the voice of someone who had been coached never to say more than necessary on an open channel.

"This line is monitored," she said. "State your traffic."

Ilse Brandt. Of course. The daughter had taken the radio watch, which told Vask several things at once: that the party was functional enough to maintain watches, that Iversen trusted her with communications, and that the two of them had closed ranks in a way that should have worried him more than it did.

"Soren Vask," he said. "I'd like to speak with August."

A pause, long enough to measure. Then, muffled, distant, the sound of a handset changing hands, wind breathing across a microphone, and finally—

"Vask."

August's voice. Eleven years since Vask had heard it in person, and it was lower than he remembered, rougher, stripped of whatever ambition had once lived in it. It sounded like a man talking from inside a storm.

"I'll be brief," Vask said. "You're above the forest line, descending east. You have maybe two days of food, a porter with a shattered leg, and one route out. My team holds the hut at the valley mouth. The safe descent is closed."

Silence on the line. Not the dead silence of a dropped call—the live silence of a man choosing his words with a knife at his throat. Vask had heard that silence before. He'd heard it on the old expedition, the night he ordered the summit push, when Iversen had argued and then, in the end, obeyed. Three men had gone up into that weather because August Iversen had followed the chain of command all the way to the point where it broke.

That was the lever. Vask had chosen it deliberately, the way he chose everything.

"You've always been good at following orders, August," Vask said. "It's what ruined you. Someone gave you a bad order and you carried it, and you've spent eleven years paying for a decision that was mine." He let that land. He meant it to wound, and he knew—Iversen knew—that it was true, and that was what made it a weapon instead of an insult. "So here's your order. The last one anyone will give you on this mountain. Hand over the girl and the satchel. You walk down rich. Your name stays out of every file, every courtroom, every newspaper. Nobody learns you were ever here. Isn't that what you wanted? To be unseen? I'm offering you invisibility with interest."

The words came out smooth, rehearsed in the way Vask rehearsed everything—not word for word, but shape for shape, argument for argument. He had built this offer the way he built routes: from the anchor backward. Money first, because money was universal. Anonymity second, because for August Iversen anonymity was not a preference but a scar tissue, eleven years thick. And underneath both, threaded through them like wire through cable, the accusation: *you obeyed once, and it destroyed you; obey me now, and it will save you.*

It was, Vask reflected, almost elegant. He was genuinely proud of it.

On the far end, wind. Static. Then Iversen's voice, and it had changed register—gone quieter, which Vask recognized with a small cold shock as the dangerous register, the one from the old mountain, the night things went wrong.

"You kept the originals," Iversen said. "Fifteen years. You kept the proof that you falsified the surveys, sitting in a drawer, because you're the kind of man who keeps leverage even against himself. And now you want them burned."

"I want this finished."

"So do I." A breath. "You're right about one thing, Vask. I followed orders once. Three men are dead because I treated the chain of command like it was load-bearing. I've had eleven years to do that arithmetic, and I'm done with it. So no. The answer's no."

And then the line went dead—not cut, Vass realized, but simply ended, Iversen hanging up with the economy of a man who had said exactly as much as he intended to say and not one syllable more.

Vask sat with the phone in his hand for a full minute. The tin plate caught the lamplight. The cards stayed face-down. Outside, the light was going blue and then black, and the temperature was falling through the sort of numbers that killed batteries and fingers with equal indifference.

Kovac stirred on his bunk. "Well?"

"He refused."

Kovac sat up. "Then we take the ground. Shoot the porters' loads off them at the moraine, take the satchel off the—"

"No." Vask set the phone down on the table, deliberately, the way a chess player sets down a piece he has decided not to move yet. "We wait. They have to come through. And when they come through, they'll be four days hungrier than they are tonight, and hunger does what threats can't—it makes refusal expensive."

He believed it. That was the trouble with Soren Vask; he always believed his own arithmetic, right up until the mountain corrected it. He lay down on the bunk farthest from the stove, fully dressed, boots on, because he was not a man who slept deeply in occupied positions, and he listened to the wind come down the valley and thought about the old expedition, and the summit push, and the three names he had never once spoken aloud in fifteen years because speaking them would have made them real.

Rennick. Osei. Marchetti.

He'd ordered them up. Iversen had led them. Both of those sentences were true, and for fifteen years Vask had arranged his life around the space between them—the space where blame lived, elastic, negotiable, for sale. Tomorrow, or the day after, at a moraine bend four hundred meters below this hut, that space was going to close. One way or another, it was going to close.

He fell asleep to the sound of the stove ticking, and dreamed, briefly, of a white slope he had never actually seen, because he had watched it from base camp through binoculars, from the warm side of the decision.

* * *

Dawn came gray and hard. Vask was up before his men, checking the trail through the frosted window glass, and for an hour he saw nothing, and the nothing was its own kind of information: they hadn't descended in the night. They were resting, or rationing, or waiting him out in turn. Two predators, each convinced the other would blink first.

At midmorning the radio crackled. Not Iversen this time. A relay burst, automated, from the beacon network—one of his own beacons, the ones Dev Okafor had been feeding for two weeks before destroying them. The network was dead; Vask knew that. What pinged him now was a maintenance handshake, a machine greeting a machine, meaningless except that it confirmed the mast was still up and the world was still listening.

Then, just past noon, movement.

Two figures on the open ground below the moraine bend. Small, through the window glass, but unmistakable—moving slow, roped, breaking trail through knee-deep snow that had fallen fresh in the night. Behind them, at intervals, the rest of the party strung out like beads on a wire: a figure carrying a litter, another hauling a sled made, Vask guessed, from the frame of a torn-down tent, and at the rear a large man favoring one arm, walking sweep.

Vask watched through binoculars for twenty minutes without lowering them. He counted six. He catalogued gaits. The big man at the rear moved like a cook, not a fighter—heavy, deliberate, protecting one side. The woman on the front rope moved well, better than a client had any right to, which confirmed what Vask had suspected since the beginning: Ilse Brandt was not cargo. She was crew.

And August Iversen was on point, breaking trail, doing the work of the strongest man in the party because he was the strongest man in the party, and because that was who he had always been—the one who went first, the one who took the mountain's first hit so the others wouldn't have to.

Vask lowered the binoculars and felt something he had not expected to feel, which was a flicker of the old respect, arriving eleven years late and thoroughly unwelcome. He crushed it. Respect was ballast too.

"They're coming," he told his men. "Positions. Nobody fires. Nobody speaks to them except me. When they reach the flat below the hut, we go out and meet them—all four of us, rifles slung, not aimed. This ends with a conversation or it doesn't end today."

Pell asked the question again, the one from last night, the one that lived in him like a splinter: "And if the conversation fails?"

Vask pulled on his gloves and looked at the young man with the patience of a man who had buried enough of his own mistakes to know how they were made.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we find out what August Iversen is willing to lose."

He stepped off the porch into the blue shadow of the ridge, and the cold took his breath for a moment, sharp as a debt called in. Below him, tiny and inevitable, the party kept coming down—out of the white silence, onto the open ground, into the narrowing place where the valley, and the truth, and the end of everything waited together.

He had offered August Iversen one word's worth of escape, and been refused in one word's worth of answer. Now there was nothing left between them but the moraine, and the morning, and fifteen years of arithmetic that no one—no one—was going to walk away from clean.
