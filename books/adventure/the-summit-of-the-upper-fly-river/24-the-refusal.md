## 24. The Refusal

The radio crackled at dawn, and August was already awake to hear it.

He had slept, if the word could be stretched to cover it, sitting upright against the pack wall with his boots on and the .30-06 across his knees, in the shelter hut's single dry corner. The others lay in the shapes sleep had left them: Renny curled tight around his splinted wrist, Mara with her back to the wall and her face to the door, Dev breathing shallow and even, his ruined leg propped on a rolled sleeping pad. Ilse slept last and woke first, always, the way soldiers and the children of powerful men learn to do. She was watching him when the radio hissed.

He had kept Vask's set charged for exactly this. It sat on the shelf between a candle stub and a tin of matches, and when the voice came out of it—dry, reasonable, almost warm—August did not touch it. He let it speak to the whole room.

"August. It's morning on my side of the valley too. I'll keep this short, because you were always a short man when it mattered." A pause, the sound of wind against a transmitter. "My men hold the lower hut and the ford below it. The descent route you're looking at is a funnel with me at the bottom of it. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to do the arithmetic you're good at. Hand over the girl and the satchel. Walk out the west side by noon tomorrow, and you're a rich man with no name and no record. Nobody in Kessik ever has to remember what you are. Nobody in the low country ever has to learn it. You go back to being nobody, August. I know how much that's worth to you."

The voice paused again, and in the pause August could hear fifteen years of it—the letters, the ledger, the inquest, the way men at trailheads went quiet when his name came up, the way Tomas had looked at him the last time, in the doorway of the Kessik office, with the burned letter's ash still in the grate.

"You've followed orders your whole life," Vask said. "Even the bad ones. Even the one that killed three men. That's all this is—another order. The only difference is who's giving it and what it pays. Think about it. I'll call at dusk."

The radio clicked off. The wind filled the silence the way water fills a footprint.

Nobody in the room had moved. August looked at their faces one by one—Mara's level and waiting, Renny's fever-bright, Dev's carefully blank, Ilse's unreadable in the grey light—and then he stood, set the rifle against the wall, and said the only thing he had been carrying since the voice began.

"No."

Just that. One word, flat as a stone set down on a table. He picked up the kettle and went to the stove to melt snow, because a man's hands needed something to do after a thing like that, and the room could do what it liked with the word he'd left behind it.

* * *

The debate started while the water was still heating, because of course it did. August had counted on that. A crew that argued was a crew still deciding to stay; a crew that went silent had already started leaving, in their heads, one at a time.

"You should have let him finish," Mara said. She was lacing her boots with the unhurried economy she brought to everything, but her eyes stayed on him. "The west route. Is it real? Could we take it?"

"It's real. It's also a lie." August set the kettle on the flame. "He doesn't want us walking anywhere. He wants us split up. Hand over Ilse and the satchel, and the rest of us become witnesses he can deal with one at a time, on ground he chose. The west route is the hook. The offer is the bait. You don't take a hook because the bait looks like food."

"And if the funnel is real too?" Renny's voice came out cracked, the fever still working in him, but his mind was clear; it was always clear, that was the thing about Renny, the body failed and the arithmetic didn't. "Because I've been thinking about the lower hut. He said his men hold it. If that's true, we're not choosing between his offer and freedom. We're choosing between his offer and a fight."

"We're choosing," August said, "between what he wants and what's true. The truth is there's a ranger station at the valley mouth with a radio in it, and a descent route he can't watch every meter of, and a night coming, and six of us. That's the true arithmetic. His is a story with a price tag on it."

"Six of us," Dev said quietly, "one of whom can't walk."

The room went still around that. Dev said it without self-pity, which somehow made it heavier, and August met his eyes and held them. "Six," he said again. "I did the sum before he called. It doesn't change."

"You'd carry me," Dev said. Not a question. "Down a valley where a man with a rifle is waiting, and every hour costs food we don't have."

"I'd carry you," August said. "Or Renny would, or Mara, or Ilse, or all of us in shifts, which is how it would actually go. That's not sentiment, Dev. That's the only arithmetic that's ever held on a mountain. You leave a man behind to save weight, and you find out later that what you saved it for wasn't worth the carrying. I know. I've paid that bill."

He hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. It came out of the place where the old climb lived, the sealed room in him that he'd opened once already in the storm cave and had not managed to close again since. The ghosts were looser this year. They walked through the walls of him at odd hours, and they had opinions.

Tomas had an opinion now. Tomas, who wasn't dead, who was a hundred miles away in Kessik probably arguing with a supplier about flour prices, but whose voice had been in August's head for two days like a man leaning on a doorframe. *Refuse it,* Tomas had said, back at the beginning, the night of the letter. *You know what that country does. You know what those people do. You built a life out of staying small, and small is safe, and you hate it, but it's safe.* And then, the other Tomas, the one from fifteen years ago, standing at the basecamp radio with the storm coming in and Vask's voice on the line ordering the push: *Somebody has to say no up here, August. Somebody always has to say no, or the mountain collects.* Nobody had said no. August had been the closest, and he hadn't been close enough, and three men had gone into the white and not come back, and the inquest had needed a name to hang it on, and his had been the one that was available.

*You've followed orders your whole life,* Vask had said. And the worst of it, the part that sat in August's chest like a swallowed stone, was that it was true. He had followed Vask's order up that mountain. He had followed the blacklist's order into Kessik. He had followed his own fear's order for fifteen years, and the fear had been a harder master than Vask had ever been, because at least Vask paid.

"No," he said again, to the room, to the radio, to Tomas, to the dead. "We're not selling her. We're not selling it. We go down the valley our way, and we put those documents in front of people who can use them, and if Vask is standing at the bottom of it, then Vask can learn what I learned fifteen years ago about what happens when you order men into country that doesn't take orders."

Mara finished lacing her boot and stood. "Good," she said. "Then let's talk about the night route, because if we're doing this, I want to be moving before his dusk call."

* * *

They argued the route for two hours, and it was the best kind of argument, the kind where everyone in the room knew the mountain. Mara wanted the glacier's east margin, fast and exposed to the icefall. August wanted the west edge, slower, hidden under the lateral moraine, within rifle range of the lower hut for one bad quarter mile. Renny, propped on his elbow with his splinted hand in his lap, traced the line on the map and found the one place where the moraine narrowed to a chute—the same chute Vask's beacon had tried to steer them into days ago, though only four people in the hut knew that—and said, in his mild way, "He'll expect us here. It's the only line that makes sense. Which means it's the last line we should take."

"So we don't make sense," Mara said.

"So we make sense," August said slowly, "but we make it at three in the morning, in the dark, when his men are cold and stupid, and we're through the narrows before first light shows them where to aim." He looked around the hut. "It's not a good plan. It's the plan that's left. I want everyone to say it out loud that they understand what's left, because I'm not leading anybody one more meter on a maybe."

One by one they said it. Renny first, because Renny always went first when the going was worst, which was the whole secret of him. Then Mara. Then Dev, who took longer, and who said, "I'm the reason he knows where we are. Every camp, every pace. I want you to know I know what that makes me, and I'm not asking to be carried out of it. I'm asking to be carried through it, and then you can decide what I'm worth." August held his gaze and gave him nothing and everything: a single nod, the kind you give a man on a rope with you.

Ilse spoke last. She had been quiet through all of it, sitting with her back straight against the log wall, and when she finally did, it wasn't about the route.

"You understand what he offered you," she said. "What it would have meant. Your name clean. The inquest reopened on your terms, maybe, or just—forgotten. Fifteen years of Kessik ended in one walk down a valley. And you said no before he'd even finished the sentence."

"It wasn't a hard sentence to finish," August said. "I've heard it before. He gave a version of it to three men on a mountain, and they said yes, and that's the whole story of how I got here."

"That's not the whole story," Ilse said, "and you know it. The whole story is that you could have said yes just now, and nobody out there would ever have known you didn't. That's what it cost you. Not money. *Being nobody.* You've spent fifteen years buying that, and you just spent it in one word." Her voice didn't rise; it did the opposite, went quiet and precise, the way she did when something mattered. "I grew up in a house where everything had a price and my father taught me the numbers before he taught me to read. I know what an expensive word sounds like. That was one."

August looked at her and found he had nothing to hide behind, which was, he was beginning to understand, the condition she inflicted on everyone. He could have given her the clipped answer, the guide's answer, *it's just the right call, don't make it a sermon.* Instead he heard himself say, "My partner Tomas told me to refuse the job before it started. Told me the high country had taken enough from me. And he was right, and I went anyway, and I still don't fully know why." He turned his hands over, looked at them—rope-scarred, frost-nicked, the hands of a man who had spent a decade and a half pretending they were only good for river work. "I think I know now. It's the same reason I just said no. There's a version of a man that survives anything, and it isn't worth surviving as. I met him. He's the one who followed Vask up the mountain because the order came from a man with a reputation, and he's the one who came down and let the inquest have my name because fighting it meant being seen, and I have been that man for fifteen years and I am done being him. That's the whole speech. There isn't more."

"There's always more," Ilse said softly. "But that's enough for tonight."

* * *

Dusk came down blue and hard, and at the hour Vask had named, the radio hissed alive again.

August let it ring through its first call, then its second, the whole hut watching the little box like a snake in the corner. On the third call he picked it up, and did not key it, and held it a moment, feeling the weight of it, the weight of the voice that would come through it, the weight of everything a single word had to hold.

He keyed it.

"No," he said. "And don't call again."

He set it down, and took the set's battery pack in both hands, and walked out into the blue dusk and the rising wind, and put it under his heel on the frozen stones until the plastic cracked and the dark inside it leaked out and was done.

When he came back in, the hut had changed. It was a small thing, hard to name—lanterns trimmed, packs being re-sorted, Renny testing his splinted wrist against a strap, Dev sitting up with his leg braced and a rope coil already in his lap. A crew that had been waiting for a decision was now a crew that had one.

Ilse was watching him from the doorway, the wind pulling at her hood, and she didn't say anything at all. She didn't need to. She had heard the word twice, and she had heard what it cost, and she had understood it the way he was only beginning to understand her: that being seen was not the punishment he had always taken it for, that some things were only survivable out loud.

"Two hours to full dark," Mara said, checking her watch against the light dying off the ridge. "Then we move."

August looked once more at the map, at the moraine's long grey finger pointing down into the valley, at the narrow place where a man with a rifle would be waiting for men who did the sensible thing at a sensible hour.

"Then we move," he said.
