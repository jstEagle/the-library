## 25. Night Movement

Ilse had never known darkness could have a texture until she learned to move through it on a glacier.

It was not the darkness of her father's house in the city, where blackout curtains only softened the streetlights into amber smears and the dark was something you slept inside. This was a darkness with weight and grain, a darkness that pressed against her eyes like wet wool. The moon was down—August had waited for the moon to set, she understood that now—and the stars alone lit the ice, and the ice gave back almost nothing. It swallowed light the way the sea did. What reached them came as faint silver on the ridgelines, enough to silhouette the peaks against the sky but not enough to show what lay underfoot.

She walked because Dev Okafor walked in front of her, and she put her boots where his boots went.

That was the whole of the technique, August had told them at dusk, crouched over a map he would not let anyone else hold: *You don't look at the ground. You look at the man ahead of you. You trust him. If you look at the ground you'll see nothing anyway, and then you'll start imagining things, and imagination is what kills people at night on ice.* He had said it flatly, without drama, the way he said everything that mattered, and Ilse had noticed that when he finished speaking, no one argued. Not even Mara, who argued with August about everything from rope coiling methods to how much sugar belonged in trail tea.

They were six: August leading, then Dev, then herself, then Renny with his splinted wrist strapped across his chest like a sling-bound bird, then Mara at the rear with the heavy pack and the rifle she'd taken from Vask's abandoned cache two days ago. The horses were gone—one lost in the chute, the others left hobbled at the bivouac cave with half their feed, a calculated sacrifice August had made without ceremony. They carried everything themselves now. Ilse's shoulders had stopped hurting somewhere around midnight of the second day; they had passed through pain into a kind of numb obedience, and she suspected the rest of her body was following.

The satchel rode against her chest, strapped tight over her down jacket, its weight distributed by the harness August had rigged for her personally. Inside it were the survey cores' companion documents—the original field ledgers, the falsified readings annotated in her father's own project managers' hands, the chain of custody that could unravel Brandt Mining's entire environmental case. She had carried it for three days now. She slept with it. She had begun, in some private corner of herself, to think of it as a heartbeat.

*Don't romanticize it,* she told herself, stepping where Dev stepped. *It's paper. It's evidence. It's worth more than your life only because other people's lives depend on it.*

But the mountain didn't care about the distinction, and neither, tonight, did the cold.

* * *

They had left the bivouac cave at 2300 hours, or what August called 2300 hours, though none of them had seen a watch face in days. The plan was simple and terrible: Vask's men held the lower exit hut and the safe descent route through the eastern valley—the route any sane party would take. August's answer was to be insane. They would traverse west along the lateral moraine of the glacier itself, in darkness, above the crevasse fields, and descend the far side through terrain so broken that even Vask's local guide had reportedly refused to scout it.

*He thinks we'll hole up and negotiate,* August had said, rolling the map closed. *He's counting on us being rational. Rational people wait for daylight, and daylight is when his men can see us.* A pause, and then, in the tone of a man confessing something: *I've done this before. Night movement on the Fly. It's how we got down after—*

He hadn't finished. Renny had looked away. Mara had looked at him. Ilse had understood that the sentence ended in the place where three climbers died, and that August Iversen carried the rest of it folded up inside himself like the map he wouldn't share.

So now they moved, and the moving was everything.

The first hour was moraine—loose rock frozen into a semblance of stability, each step a small negotiation between boot sole and stone. Ilse learned to read Dev's body for warnings she couldn't see: the slight hitch when a rock shifted, the pause before a step down, the way he swung his head left or right to catch starlight off the route markers August had placed at dusk—small wands of bamboo with reflective tape, spaced at intervals only visible one at a time, so that finding each one felt less like navigation and more like grace.

Dev said nothing. Dev had been saying very little since the night he'd destroyed the beacon and confessed everything—since he'd stood in the blue light of the storm-cave with the crushed radio in his hands and told August, voice cracking, that he'd been paid to deliver them into an ambush and had instead delivered them out of one. Since then he'd volunteered for every dangerous position. First across the ford. First on the rope. Now first on the night line, walking point through terrain that wanted him dead.

Ilse watched him and thought about what it cost to become trustworthy, and how the cost was always paid in advance, on credit, against a debt you might never be allowed to repay.

* * *

At the second hour the moraine gave way to glacier edge, and the character of the dark changed.

Ice underfoot made a different sound than rock—a squeak, sometimes, or a hollow drumming where a thin bridge spanned air. August had warned them about this too, back in the cave, drawing in the frost on the stone floor with one finger: *The glacier is a river that forgot how to move. Under the snow bridges there are cracks going down further than any of you want to think about. In daylight we'd rope tight and probe every step. Tonight we do it by sound and by faith, and if anyone hears a crunch that doesn't sound right, you freeze and you call it.*

*Call it,* Mara had repeated. *Even if it's nothing.*

*Especially if it's nothing,* August said. *I'd rather stop this line forty times than once too few.*

Ilse had never been roped to strangers before this expedition. Now she was roped to five people whose names she knew better than her own family's, tied into a line that meant that whatever happened to one happened, in some measure, to all. The rope ran from her harness to Dev's ahead and Renny's behind, and she could feel them both—not see, *feel*—as small tensions and releases traveling down the line like pulses. When Dev stepped, she felt it a half-second later and stepped. When Renny paused, she paused. It was the closest thing to telepathy she had ever experienced, and she found it strange and moving that such intimacy required no conversation at all, only nylon and carabiners and the mutual agreement not to die.

Somewhere below them, invisible, the glacier groaned. It was a deep sound, tectonic, the sound of something enormous shifting in its sleep, and it traveled up through her boots and into her teeth. The first time she'd heard it, weeks ago on the lower trail, it had frightened her badly. Now it was almost companionable. The mountain talked in its sleep. You just had to not take it personally.

*How far to the descent gully?* Renny asked quietly, his voice carrying strangely in the cold still air.

*Two hours,* August answered from the front, barely turning his head. *Maybe three. We're slower than I planned.*

No one commented on what *slower than planned* meant—dawn catching them on the ice, exposed, visible, hunted. Everyone did the arithmetic privately and kept it behind their teeth. Ilse did it too, and hated the answer, and kept walking.

* * *

The near-catastrophe, when it came, announced itself with a sound like a door closing.

They had traversed onto a broad snowfield sloping gently toward the glacier's center—a detour around a wall of seracs that even August wouldn't pass beneath in the dark. The snowfield looked innocent. It looked, Ilse thought afterward, like the safest ground they'd crossed all night, smooth and unbroken, a white plain under the stars. That was the trick of snow bridges. They were liars. They wore the same surface as solid ground and underneath they were nothing, a crust of winter over a void that went down farther than the height of the mountains themselves.

Dev was ten meters ahead of her when the world opened under him.

There was a soft *whump*, muffled, almost polite—the sound of snow giving up its argument with gravity—and then Dev dropped, and the rope snapped taut between them, and Ilse felt the full weight of a falling man arrive at her harness like a fist.

She went down onto her knees automatically, the way they'd drilled it, digging her axe in, bracing. Behind her she heard Renny grunt as the shock hit his end of the line, heard Mara shout something sharp and wordless. Ahead, below, in the black mouth of the crevasse, Dev's voice came up thin and echoing:

*I'm hanging—I'm okay—I'm hanging—*

*Don't move,* August's voice cut across everything, low and absolute. *Nobody moves. Ilse, hold. Renny, hold. Mara—anchor. Now.*

And then the machine they had become, without ever signing the papers, began to work.

Ilse held. That was her entire job, and she discovered that holding was not passive—it was a fury, a full-body argument with the mountain, her axe buried to the head, her knees driving into the slope, every muscle she owned committed to the single proposition that Dev Okafor was not going to fall while she was attached to him. Her arms shook. Her breath came in white gasps. Somewhere below her boots there was a crack in the world two hundred meters deep, and a man dangling in it, and the only thing between him and the bottom was a system of knots and friction and the willingness of four people to hurt themselves on his behalf.

Mara built the anchor in ninety seconds. Ilse couldn't see it—could only hear the metallic chatter of gear, the bite of screws into ice, Mara's clipped instructions to Renny—and then the tension transferred, smoothly, invisibly, from human bodies to the mountain itself, and August said, *All right. Holding. Nobody relaxes yet.*

Then August went forward on the line, alone, into the dark, toward the hole.

Ilse watched his headlamp—dimmed to a dying ember so it wouldn't destroy their night vision—drift toward the breach in the snowfield. She heard him probing the lip with his axe shaft. She heard him talking downward, calm, unhurried, the voice of a man ordering coffee rather than conducting a crevasse rescue at four in the morning above a glacier: *Dev. Talk to me. Where are you hurt?*

*Legs are jammed. Chest against the lip on one side. I can breathe. My headlamp died on the fall—I've got nothing below me.*

*Nothing below you means nothing to hit. That's actually good news.* A pause. The scrape of the axe. *I'm getting an eye on you. Don't pull yourself up. Don't kick. The bridge is compromised—if you load it again it goes and takes me with it.*

Ilse knelt in the snow, holding, watching the ember of August's lamp hover at the edge of the void, and she thought about how she had misjudged this man. Weeks ago, in her father's office, reading the scandal files, she had formed a picture of August Iversen: competent but cursed, a guide who froze when it mattered, who followed orders up a mountain he should have turned around on. She had thought his disgrace was cowardice wearing the costume of caution.

She had been wrong. She saw that now, kneeling in the freezing dark with a fallen man's life running through her hands. His disgrace was the opposite of cowardice. It was what happened to a man who once obeyed the wrong order and had spent nine years making sure he would never again confuse obedience with duty. Every rule he enforced—his route, his pace, his refusal to push, his infuriating insistence on turning back, on waiting, on margin—was a memorial to the dead. He had rebuilt himself out of procedure. And here, in the worst hour of the worst night, the procedure was holding a living man above a bottomless dark, and it was working.

*Okay,* August called softly. *Here's what happens. Mara's anchor has him. I'm rigging a haul. Dev, when the line comes tight you keep your legs loose, let us lift you, don't fight it. On three counts. Everyone else—stay frozen till I say.*

The haul took eleven minutes. Ilse counted every one of them by the burn in her shoulders. There was a moment—she would remember it always—when the rope went slack in a wrong way and her heart fell faster than Dev had, a horrible free-fall of the spirit, before August's voice confirmed: *He's over the lip. He's on the lip. He's up.*

Dev crawled clear of the breach on his hands and knees and lay in the snow, breathing hard, steam rising off him like a kettle. For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Renny, from behind her, in the gentle absurdity that was Renny's gift to the world, said: *Anyone else hungry? Because I'm starving, and I figure if we're going to die out here we shouldn't do it on an empty stomach.*

Laughter, small and shocked and necessary, went down the line like warmth through a cold pipe.

* * *

They bypassed the breached snowfield on a long arc, August probing every meter of the new line with his axe shaft, tapping, listening, refusing the direct route forever. It cost them another hour. Dawn was a gray threat in the east by the time they reached the top of the descent gully—a steep, broken chute of rock and ice plunging toward the western valley, ugly, unglamorous, exactly the kind of ground no one sane would choose, which was precisely why it was empty.

August stood at its head a moment, looking down into the gray dark, and Ilse came up beside him. Below them, faint and far, she could see lights. Two, maybe three, pinpricks in the valley bottom. Vask's men at the lower hut, watching the safe route, guarding a door nobody intended to use.

*They're down there waiting for us to come to them,* she said.

*People wait for what they expect,* August said. His breath plumed. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with muscles—tired somewhere older than his body. *We're not going to be expected.*

He glanced sideways at her, and in the pre-dawn gray she could see his face properly for the first time in hours: chapped, frost-burned at the cheekbones, eyes steady.

*Your cargo made it through the night,* he said. Almost a joke. Almost.

*Our cargo,* she corrected him, and saw something move behind his eyes—surprise, maybe, or the beginning of something he wasn't ready to name—and then he turned and pointed his axe down the gully and said, *Rope teams of two. Mara and Dev first, since Dev owes the mountain a rematch. Renny with me. Ilse, you're with me after. We climb down slow and we don't stop until the trees.*

The trees. Ilse said the word to herself like a prayer. Trees meant cover. Trees meant the end of the ice, the end of the voids, the end of the places where the ground lied. Trees meant the far side.

They started down as the sky went from black to iron. Behind them, on the glacier they'd crossed, the collapsed bridge yawned in the growing light—a clean-edged hole in the white plain, rimmed with blue, dropping away into green-black depths that no eye could find the bottom of. Dev looked back at it once, briefly, the way a man looks at the place where he died and didn't.

*Thank you,* he said to the line, to all of them at once, his voice rough. *For holding.*

*That's the job,* August said. And then, quieter, as if the mountain were listening and he wanted it to know he understood: *That was always the job. I just got it wrong once, a long time ago.*

They descended into the trees as the sun cleared the ridge, and Ilse, strapping the satchel tighter, caught movement far below in the valley—a small figure breaking from the treeline, running, and then another, and the cold certainty settled over her that Vask had finally understood where they'd gone, and that the last miles were going to be a race.
