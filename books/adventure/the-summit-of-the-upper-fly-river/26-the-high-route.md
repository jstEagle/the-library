## 26. The High Route

The storm broke in the small hours and left the world scoured. August crawled out of the bivouac cave into a morning so clean it hurt—the sky rinsed to a hard cobalt, the peaks across the valley wearing their new snow like fresh linen, every ridge line drawn in ink. The air had the glassy stillness that followed three days of wind, as if the mountain had shouted itself hoarse and was now listening.

He stood at the cave mouth with his hands wrapped around a tin cup of snowmelt and looked north, because there was nowhere else left to look.

The route they'd planned was dead. Vask's men held the lower hut on the eastern descent—Mara had confirmed it at first light through the spotting scope, counting figures moving between the rocks, the dull glint of a stovepipe. The glacier route south was out; the crevasse fields had been rotting for weeks even before this last dump of snow, and after Dev's bridge collapse two nights ago nobody would say the word *crossing* without flinching. West was the wall itself: four thousand feet of ice-dressed granite that no party had ever climbed, that August himself had only ever stood beneath, once, nine years ago, with a pair of binoculars and a hunger he'd spent the rest of his life trying to starve.

The shoulder. He had named it privately, all those years ago, the way you name a thing you don't intend to touch—*the High Route*—and then he had written it into his notebook, photographed it, and buried the photograph in a drawer in Kessik along with everything else from the year the world ended. He had sworn an oath over it, not in any church but in the particular way a man swears things when three of his friends are still up there under the ice: *never again*. Never again the summit push ordered by radio from a warm tent below. Never again the arithmetic that traded lives for a client's deadline. And never, ever, the shoulder—because the shoulder was where the oath would have taken him if he'd been brave enough to break it back then, the line he'd seen and refused, the line that might have brought them home.

Now it was the only way down that Vask couldn't reach.

He heard Ilse come out behind him—she moved quietly for someone who'd grown up in boardrooms, a habit she'd learned fast on the mountain—and felt her stop at his shoulder, following his gaze up the wall.

"That's it," she said. Not a question.

"That's it."

"It's never been climbed."

"No."

She was quiet for a moment. Below them, the camp was stirring: Renny's voice, rough with fever but steady, arguing with Mara about whether the stove could be spared another ten minutes of fuel; Dev lying propped against his pack, his shattered leg splinted and bound, his face gray but his eyes tracking everything. Six of them. What remained of six.

"Show me," Ilse said.

* * *

He spread the map on a flat rock outside the cave and weighted the corners with stones, and then he did what he had promised himself he would do, however it went: he told them the truth. All of it. The whole shape of the thing, drawn in cold numbers and honest margins, because whatever else the mountain had stripped from him over the years, it had taught him one law he would not break again—a leader who hides the risk steals the choice, and a stolen choice is worth nothing when the weather turns.

"The shoulder runs from here—" he put his finger on the col below the eastern buttress "—to here, the crest, about five-eight hundred meters. Nine hundred vertical feet of climbing above us, then a knife-edge traverse of maybe two kilometers, then the descent on the far side, which I have never seen except through binoculars. It faces northwest. That's good and bad. Good, because the storm hit it from the southwest and much of the new snow has been scoured off the rock. Bad, because northwest means it stays cold, the verglas won't burn off, and we'll be climbing on ice the whole way."

Renny, hunched by the stove, said nothing, but his spoon stopped moving.

"There are sections," August went on, "where a fall is not survivable. I want to be precise about that. Not *probably* fatal. Not *serious*. There are three pitches—I remember them like I climbed them yesterday, though I've only ever looked at them—where if your anchor fails or your footing goes, you fall four hundred feet onto the glacier and they will bury whatever they find. We'll fix ropes through those sections. Fixed ropes are only as good as the anchors, and the anchors will be driven into verglaced cracks by men whose hands haven't fully warmed in four days."

"And the traverse?" Mara asked. She'd come to stand at the edge of the map, arms crossed, her breath pluming.

"The crest is a cornice on its southern side for most of its length. Cornices break. Sometimes for no reason you'll ever identify. We stay on the northern edge, single file, spaced at thirty meters, and we move when I say move and we stop when I say stop." He looked up. "And the descent. I don't know what's on the other side. Nobody does. I can see the upper third—it's steep snow, forty degrees, maybe forty-five, doable with ropes and care. Below that it disappears behind a rib, and whatever's down there, we meet it tired, hungry, and carrying Dev."

Dev spoke from the cave mouth. His voice was thin but level. "You're describing a coin flip."

"I'm describing worse than a coin flip," August said. "A coin doesn't get tired. A coin doesn't carry a man with a broken leg. If I'm honest—and I'm going to be honest, because I owe you that after everything—the odds of all six of us standing in the far valley are maybe fifty-fifty. Maybe less. I won't pretend otherwise, and anyone who tells you different on this mountain is selling something."

Silence. The wind had died completely now, and in that silence they could hear the mountain itself: the deep, tectonic groan of the glacier settling, somewhere high above the crack of a serac letting go, a sound like a rifle shot rolling down the valley.

"So what are the alternatives?" Ilse asked, and her voice was calm in a way that made him understand, again, how badly he had misjudged her in Kessik.

August laid them out anyway, because honesty meant all of it. "We wait here for the passes to clear and go back the way we came, through Vask's cordon. He has eight men, rifles, and a reason to shoot. Or we try the glacier south and hope the bridges hold, knowing what happened to Dev's bridge. Or we sit here until the food runs out—which is four days, five if we're cruel to ourselves—and then none of these choices matter, because starving men don't climb."

"And the shoulder," Renny said quietly, "at least kills us doing something."

"Maybe," August said. "Or it gets us over. I've thought about this line for nine years. I know it better than any man alive knows it, which is to say I barely know it at all. But I've turned it over ten thousand times in my head—every pitch, every stance, where the rope runs, where the protection goes. Reconnaissance isn't climbing. But it isn't nothing either."

He sat back on his heels. His hands were steady on the map, and he noticed that they were steady, and understood that this too was part of the truth he owed them: that some part of him wanted this—not the dying of it, but the attempt. That the oath he'd sworn had been half fear and half penance, and penance, it turned out, had a door in it, and the door was open, and he was afraid of walking through it almost as much as he was afraid of the mountain.

But he kept that to himself. They didn't need his hunger. They needed his honesty.

* * *

"So here's how this works," he said, and stood up. "Nobody follows me up that wall because I'm the guide and they're the clients. There are no clients anymore—we burned that word somewhere around the Col of Hanging Ice. This is a crew, and a crew votes. I'm asking each of you, one at a time, alone if you want, and I don't care what anyone else says. If even one person says no, we don't go. I'll find another way or I'll die finding it, but I will not drag a single unwilling soul up that face. Understood?"

They nodded. Even Dev, from the ground.

"Mara. You first. You're the strongest climber here besides me, and everyone will calibrate against you."

Mara looked at the wall for a long time. Her jaw worked once, twice. Then she said, "I signed onto this expedition because of what you did and didn't do nine years ago. I've spent the whole trip deciding what I think about that." She turned to face him squarely. "Here's what I think. A man who refuses a line out of fear is a coward. A man who refuses it out of grief is just grieving. But a man who looks at it with open eyes, lays out every risk, and lets people choose—that man I'll follow anywhere." She paused. "Yes. I vote yes. And I'll take lead on the pitches you don't want."

August felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight since Kettle Ford.

"Renny."

Renny's wrist was swollen inside its splint, his fever down but not gone, and everyone knew what he was going to say before he said it, which was why August dreaded asking him. But Renny surprised them. "My old man worked the trawlers out of Kessik for thirty years," he said. "Forty-foot seas, engine fires, the lot. He used to say the sea doesn't ask permission and neither should you—but that's wrong, actually. The sea doesn't ask. People do. And I've been asked." He looked up at the wall, at the pale streaks of ice feathering down the buttress. "I can't climb lead. My hand's useless for anything finer than hauling. But I can haul. I can belay. I can cook at altitude if somebody carries the stove, and I will, because if we're doing this, we're doing it fed." He raised his good fist. "Yes."

"Ilse."

She answered without hesitation, and her answer carried a weight the others' hadn't, because everyone knew what she was carrying in the satchel strapped to her chest. "I have documents in my pack that can put my father in prison and Soren Vask beside him," she said. "Every day we sit in this cave is a day Vask's lawyers work, a day the filings get amended, a day the evidence gets softer. I came onto this mountain to deliver those papers, and I will deliver them if I have to crawl." She met his eyes. "But I want you to know I'm not voting yes because I have no choice. I'm voting yes because you told us the truth, and I trust the truth more than I trust safety. Yes."

"Dev."

Dev took a breath that seemed to cost him. "I'm the heaviest thing you own right now," he said, "and the least useful. By every sane calculation you should leave me here with food and a radio beacon and come back for me." He shook his head slowly. "Don't. I know what leaving feels like on this mountain. I've watched it eat this crew alive for three weeks, and I've watched what happens when nobody gets left—when Renny offers to stay and you refuse, when we rope up for a man who fell. I betrayed these people. Every one of you has a reason to leave me, and not one of you has. I'm not going to be the reason that changes." His voice cracked and steadied. "Yes. Carry me. I'll be light. I stopped eating double rations yesterday."

August hadn't known that. It landed on him like a stone.

That left himself. He realized, standing there, that he had saved his own vote for last without quite planning to, and that the others were watching him the way he'd watched them—waiting to see if the man who demanded honesty from everyone would grant it to himself.

"Yes," he said. "Obviously yes. But I want it recorded why. Nine years ago I stood under that wall with a pair of binoculars and I saw this line, and I knew—knew in my gut, the way you know a thing on a mountain—that it would go. And I didn't say so. Because Vask was on the radio demanding the summit push, and I told myself the shoulder was too dangerous, and maybe it was, and maybe I was telling the truth, but I have spent nine years suspecting that I wasn't. That I hid behind the danger because saying yes would have meant fighting him, and fighting him would have meant risking the contract, and I chose the contract. Three people died of that choice." He stopped. His voice had gone rough. "This time I'm choosing differently. Not to erase that. Nothing erases that. But because the next choice shouldn't be made by the same man who made the last one."

There was a silence after that which no one tried to fill.

Then Mara said, "Six for six," and it sounded less like a tally than a vow.

* * *

They spent the rest of the day preparing, and the preparation was its own kind of ceremony—old rituals performed with new meaning. Mara and August re-racked the ropes, coiling each one and inspecting every meter by hand, feeling for the core shots the rockfall on the col had left. They had four ropes; two were nearly new, two were scarred, and they allocated them the way surgeons allocate instruments. The rack was pitiful: eleven screws, a dozen pitons, forty carabiners, most of them battered. August counted it three times and wrote the inventory in his notebook, because on a face like the shoulder, arithmetic was prayer.

Ilse repacked the satchel, transferring the documents into waterproof sleeves and distributing duplicates—one set into her pack liner, one into Mara's, one sewn flat into the frame of Dev's litter, so that no single accident could kill the truth. When August saw what she'd done he said nothing, but he thought: *this woman has been planning for catastrophe her whole life. She just called it growing up.*

Renny cooked the last generous meal—there would be no more generous meals—and it was nothing, oats and the final slab of fat and a handful of sugar dissolved in snowmelt, but they ate it like a sacrament, sitting in a row in the cave mouth watching the light die on the western wall. The shoulder caught the sunset first and held it longest, the ice glowing amber, then rose, then ash. It looked, in that light, almost gentle. None of them believed it. That was the point. They had all learned the same lesson on this mountain, in different classrooms: the beautiful thing and the deadly thing were usually the same thing, and you didn't get to choose one without the other.

Dev asked to see the route one more time before dark. August brought the map to him and knelt, and Dev studied it with the particular attention of a man reading his own future.

"The traverse," Dev said finally. "Thirty-meter spacing. On a knife-edge. With me on a litter."

"We'll pass the litter hand to hand at the stances," August said. "It'll be slow. Slow is fine. Slow is alive."

"And if the cornice cuts loose while we're on it?"

"Then we'll find out what we're made of," August said, "which, for what it's worth, I already know."

Dev looked at him for a long moment. "I used to think you were the most dangerous man on this expedition," he said. "Vask paid me to watch you. He said you were unpredictable, that you'd crack, that nine years in a border town had made you soft." He smiled faintly, grayly. "He was wrong about all of it. You're dangerous because you tell the truth. Nobody knows what to do with that."

August folded the map. "Get some sleep," he said. "We rope up at first light."

* * *

He slept two hours and spent the rest of the night awake, which was normal now, and lay looking at the roof of the cave listening to the mountain breathe. Somewhere past midnight the wind returned, a low moan from the northwest, and he got up and stood outside and read it the way an old sailor reads water. Northwest wind. It would load the leeward side of the crest—his side—with spindrift all night. It would fill their tracks within hours of cutting them. It would make the verglas hungrier.

It also meant the storm system had rotated, which meant Vask's camp in the eastern valley was getting hammered, which meant—if God had any sense of irony left, and August had long suspected She did—the hunters were having a worse night than the hunted.

At four he woke Mara, and they stood together in the blue dark, checking knots by feel, breathing on their hands.

"You could still say no," Mara said quietly. "One night left. You could wake up and call it off and nobody would blame you. Least of all me."

"I know."

"So why aren't you going to?"

August looked up at the shoulder, a blacker black against the stars, the line he'd carried in his head for nine years like a stone in a boot. "Because Tomas used to say a man's whole life is decided by the choices he makes when he's scared," he said. "And I've been making scared choices for nine years. I'm due."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then: "Tomas would have liked this plan."

"Tomas would have hated this plan," August said, "and volunteered to lead it."

They laughed—short, startled, real—and the laugh did something to the fear, the way a fire does something to a cold room. Not banishing it. Making it bearable. Making it fuel.

At first light, gray and windless, they roped up at the base of the buttress. August led out onto the first pitch, and the ice under his tools rang like a bell, hard and clean and honest, and above him the shoulder rose nine hundred feet into a sky the color of a blade.

He looked back once. Five faces. Six votes. No clients.

"Okay," he said to the mountain, under his breath, in the language he saved for it. "Let's find out."

He swung his tool, and the climb began.
