## 28. Ilse Above the Clouds

The ledge was barely a ledge at all—a shelf of graywacke thrust out of the shoulder's flank like a bone through skin, wide enough for four bodies if they sat close and did not mind sharing their warmth with the stone. Below them, where the world should have been, there was only cloud: a white sea running from horizon to horizon, unbroken, luminous, so thick with reflected light that it hurt to look at for long. It moved. That was the thing Ilse could not stop watching. Clouds were supposed to be still when you stood among them, or so she had imagined all her life from the windows of her father's offices in Kessik and the conference rooms of the cities beyond. But this deck of cloud rolled and swelled like something alive, slow breakers forming and dissolving against the lower buttresses of the mountain, and once—only once—a peak of some distant range broke through it far to the east, a black island in the whiteness, there and then gone again as though it had never been.

She had been sitting apart from the others for perhaps twenty minutes before she noticed that August had come to sit beside her. He did it the way he did everything on the mountain: without announcement, without wasted motion, folding himself down onto the rock at her left with his back against the wall and his ice axe across his knees, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched but not quite, leaving a hand's width of cold air between them that he seemed to regard as a kind of courtesy.

Above them, Mara and Renny were rigging the next pitch. Renny's wrist was splinted now, strapped tight under his sleeve, and he worked one-handed with a fluency that would have been remarkable in a man with two good hands. Dev lay propped against a pack near the ledge's inner wall, his shattered leg bound into an improvised splint of tent poles and cord, his face gray with pain he refused to name. He had slept in fragments through the night on the ridge, waking each time with a small involuntary sound he strangled before it escaped, and Ilse had spent much of the dark hours with her gloved hand resting on his forearm, telling him stories about a city he had never seen because she could not think of anything else to give him.

But that was over now, that long terrible night, and the sun was up, and they had descended out of the killing wind onto this shelf, and the plan—the only plan—was to rest here for an hour while Mara and August scouted the down-climb, then move again until dark. An hour. Ilse looked at the cloud sea and understood that she might never in her life see anything like it again, and that this thought should have frightened her more than it did.

"You're not sleeping," August said.

It was not an accusation. He said it the way he observed weather, or rockfall, or the color of old snow on a slope—cataloguing a fact of the environment. Ilse found, to her own surprise, that she no longer minded being catalogued by him. Somewhere between the Col of Hanging Ice and the cornice, between Renny's fever and the storm wall, between the word *no* spoken into Vask's radio and the night they had spent digging themselves into the ridge with ice axes and bare hands, being seen by August Iversen had stopped feeling like exposure and started feeling like shelter.

"I slept," she said. "Some."

"Everyone slept some." He tipped his head back against the rock and closed his eyes, and for a moment she allowed herself to look at him openly, greedily, the way she had never permitted herself in the low country where looking too long at anyone invited questions. His face was burned brown and peeling across the nose; there was a week of beard on his jaw, frosted at the edges where his breath had frozen in the night; there was a cut above his eyebrow from the cornice collapse that he had cleaned with snow and never mentioned again. He looked ten years older than the man who had met her at the trailhead in Kessik with polite contempt and a handshake like a formality. He looked, she thought, like someone who had finally set down something very heavy.

"My father," she said, "used to take me up in the company helicopter when I was small. To survey sites. There was a morning—we were crossing a range not unlike this one, lower, gentler—and we came out of cloud exactly like this, and for maybe thirty seconds the whole world below us was white, and I remember thinking it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen." She paused. "He didn't even look up from his papers."

August opened his eyes. He did not say anything, which was its own kind of attention; she had learned that about him, that his silences were not absences but containers, and that what he put inside them was usually worth waiting for.

"I've been trying to decide," she went on, "and I think—I think I decided somewhere around three in the morning, holding Dev's arm, listening to him breathe—that it isn't really a decision at all anymore. Whether I go back." She turned to face him fully. "I'm not going back. Not to the house, not to the offices, not to the name. Whatever happens on this descent. Even if we walk out of this valley and the investigators take one look at me and decide I'm just my father's daughter with better manners. Even if—" She faltered, because the list of *even ifs* was long, and every item on it ended in a place she did not want to imagine. "Even if it costs me everything except this," she finished quietly, gesturing—at the ledge, at the crew, at the whole improbable fragile machinery of people who had carried each other up a mountain that should have killed them.

"That's not a decision you can make on a mountainside," August said. "People make promises at altitude they can't keep at sea level. I've watched it happen."

"I made this one at minus thirty with no food and a dead man's radio in my pocket," Ilse said. "I think altitude has earned my trust."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the geological evidence of one, the way a landscape records the river that shaped it. He looked out at the cloud deck, and she watched him decide to say whatever it was he was turning over behind his eyes, watched him weigh it the way he weighed every pitch before leading it, testing holds, reading the fall line.

"When this is over," he said, and then stopped.

Ilse held very still. In the weeks since Kettle Ford—in the weeks since she had first watched him wade into glacial water to save a stranger's pack train, and felt the floor of her assumptions shift beneath her—she had learned the grammar of his speech, and she knew that August Iversen did not begin sentences he did not intend to finish. He began this one and stopped anyway, and the stopping told her more than the sentence would have.

"When this is over," he tried again, slower, "there's a valley north of Kessik. The Sorel. Nobody runs treks there because the access road washed out eight years ago and nobody rebuilt it, which means the fishing is untouched and the hot springs are unmarked on any map worth owning. Tomas found it. We used to talk about taking clients in someday, after—" His jaw tightened. After, she knew, meant after the accident, after the blacklist, after the name became a weight he chose to carry instead of a thing he could put down. "There's a cabin. Half-built. Roof's on. Stove works. I go up sometimes in the shoulder season and sit in it and don't tell anyone where I am."

"And you're telling me now," Ilse said.

"You're going to need somewhere that isn't your father's world," August said. "And I'm going to need—" He exhaled through his nose, a short frustrated sound, the sound of a man who could read seracs and avalanche wind-slabs and the micro-expressions of frightened clients but could not read his own way through a simple sentence. "I'm not good at this part. On the mountain I know what things mean. Off it, I never have."

"Tell me what the cabin means," Ilse said gently. "That's all. Just that."

He was quiet for a long moment. Below them the cloud sea heaved against the mountain's flanks, silent, immense. Somewhere above, Mara's voice drifted down, giving Renny instructions about a anchor placement, ordinary human sound, the sound of work continuing because work was how they all stayed alive.

"It means I built half of it assuming I'd always be alone in it," August said at last, "and lately I've been noticing the other half is missing, and I don't want to finish it alone." He turned his head and looked at her then, directly, without the deflection she had come to recognize as his armor, and his eyes were the pale gray-green of glacier melt, and they did not look away. "That's the whole of it. That's as far as my vocabulary goes. You'll have to meet me the rest of the way."

* * *

Ilse had rehearsed conversations her entire life. That was what growing up in Henrik Brandt's house taught you—not arithmetic, not history, but rehearsal: the dinner-table exchange run silently in advance, the answer prepared before the question was asked, every emotional transaction pre-negotiated so that nothing true could ever catch you undefended. She had walked into this expedition with a cover story polished to a mirror shine. She had walked into the tree-line conversation about the disaster with three deflecting replies loaded and ready. She had walked into the cave during the storm with a confession already structured, beginning-middle-end, vulnerability deployed like a tool because tools were safe.

August had just handed her something that no amount of rehearsal could hold. And so she did the only honest thing available to her, which was to say the truth in the order it arrived.

"I used to think my father's world was the real one," she said. "The offices, the filings, the lawyers, the dinners where men discussed watersheds the way they'd discuss furniture—as things that existed to be arranged. I thought the mountain was the holiday. The interlude. I packed for it the way you pack for a hotel." She laughed, a small helpless sound. "I brought a cashmere sweater. Mara found it in my pack on day two and didn't say a single word, just looked at me, and I have never in my life felt so accurately described."

"Mara describes everyone accurately," August said. "It's why she's still talking to me."

"But here's what I know now, sitting here, that I couldn't have known in any office." Ilse pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, and the movement cost her—every muscle she owned had opinions about the last forty-eight hours—but she held the position because she wanted to face him squarely for this. "In my father's world, everything is contingent. Love is contingent on usefulness. Loyalty is contingent on leverage. Dev was bought. Vask was kept. I was managed. Everyone's position is provisional and everyone knows it, and the knowing poisons everything, and they call it professionalism." She shook her head. "And then I watch a man refuse money he desperately needs because refusing it was the only clean thing left. I watch a cook with a broken wrist insist on carrying his share. I watch a bought man destroy his beacon and confess rather than lead his friends into a chute. None of it was contingent. None of it was leveraged. It was just—" She searched for the word and found that the truest one was also the smallest. "Given. Freely given. That's the world I choose. If it's the size of a ledge, fine. If it's the size of a half-built cabin in a valley with no road, better."

August had gone very still beside her, the stillness of a man receiving something he had not dared to expect, and Ilse saw that her words had landed somewhere deeper than she had aimed, and that he was holding them carefully, the way he held everything on the mountain, with respect for what they could do.

"The cabin has two rooms," he said. "Roughly."

"Roughly," she repeated, and smiled, and he smiled back—actually smiled this time, brief and crooked and real, and she understood that she would carry that smile down off this mountain inside her chest, past the investigators and the lawyers and the reckoning with her father, into whatever life she built on the far side of all of it, and that it would outlast every document she delivered.

Below them, the cloud sea broke apart for a moment along its southern edge, and the world underneath appeared: forested foothills going gold with autumn, a thread of river, the faint geometric suggestion of fields. The far side. The valley they had bled to reach. It looked impossibly gentle, impossibly near, and Ilse felt her throat tighten with something that was not quite hope and not quite grief but shared a border with both.

"There it is," August said quietly. "The rest of the way down."

* * *

They rested another twenty minutes, and then the hour was spent and the mountain resumed its claims. Mara came down from scouting the next pitch with the news that the down-climb was "ugly but honest"—her highest category of praise—and they roused Dev, gave him his ration of willow bark and the last of the fever tablets, and began the slow brutal business of lowering him pitch by pitch, Renny hauling on the belay with one arm and his whole furious heart, Ilse feeding rope and calling counts, August and Mara trading leads down the rotten gneiss while the afternoon light went long and amber across the cloud tops below them.

By late afternoon the cloud deck itself began to change. Ilse noticed it first as a darkening in the south, a bruise spreading through the white, and then as texture—the smooth luminous surface roughening into towers, into anvils, into the towering cauliflower architecture she had learned, over these weeks, to dread. The air changed too. The light wind that had touched their faces all day died completely, and the silence that replaced it was not peace but pressure, the held breath of the atmosphere before it decides.

Mara stopped at the next stance and studied the southern sky for a long, unreadable moment, and Ilse watched her expression do something it almost never did: flatten entirely, the way a lake flattens before a squall.

"Weather's coming back up the valley," Mara said. "Faster than it has any right to."

August reached the stance a minute later, coiling rope with quick hands, and followed Mara's gaze to the building towers. He said nothing for a moment. Then: "How long?"

"If it moves like the last one? Dark. Maybe sooner."

Below them, far below—so far that they were specks, suggestions, a smudge of movement on the shoulder's southern flank—Ilse caught a flicker of color that did not belong to their party. Orange. Two figures, maybe three, roped together, moving upward along the same line they themselves had climbed, tiny and stubborn and mad.

She pointed. August looked. A long silence.

"He's coming up," August said at last, and his voice was flat and quiet and more frightening than shouting would have been. "After everything. After his own men walked. He's actually climbing."

The first drops hit the rock around them five minutes later, fat and cold and scattered, ticking against stone like the opening notes of something none of them wanted to hear.
