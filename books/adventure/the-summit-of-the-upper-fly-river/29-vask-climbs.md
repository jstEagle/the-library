## 29. Vask Climbs

Soren Vask stood at the mouth of the lower valley and watched the weather do what his men could not.

The storm had broken in the night, and the morning came up hard and blue, the kind of light that made everything legible at impossible distances. He could see the shoulder of the mountain from here—really see it, not the map's abstraction of it—a pale ramp of rock and ice rising out of the glacier's western flank, unmarked by any route line, unvisited by any sane party. Somewhere on that shoulder, small as punctuation, were six figures he had spent forty thousand dollars and eleven days trying to catch.

They had said no.

The word still sat in him like a swallowed stone. He had offered Iversen everything: the money, the anonymity, the clean erasure of a man who had built his whole second life on being erased. *Hand over the girl and the satchel, walk away rich and unnamed.* It was, Vask had believed, a generous offer. It was the offer he himself would have taken at thirty, at forty, at fifty. It was the offer he had taken, once, on a mountain not so different from this one, when the price had been three lives and a scapegoat's name.

And August Iversen had answered with a single word and cut the radio.

Vask turned back to his camp. It was pitched on the terminal moraine where the valley floor began its long climb toward the ice, four tents in a rough horseshoe, and it had the look of a place where a decision had been deferred too long. His men were visible in ones and twos—Halvard crouched at the stove, the Lindqvist brothers repacking coils with the deliberate slowness of men avoiding conversation, young Piet sitting on a supply crate staring at nothing. Six men. He had come into the range with eight. One had gone home over the pass a week ago with a "family emergency" that everyone had understood to be a lie told politely. And one—

One had walked out of camp four nights ago with his pack and his rifle and had not come back.

Valkonen. The quiet one, the Finn who never spoke unless the route demanded it, who had climbed with Vask on three continents and never once asked what the cargo was. Vask had woken to find his tent empty, his sleeping bag folded with a precision that was itself a message, and a note weighted under a stone: *Not this mountain. Not this job.* Four words. Valkonen had never wasted a syllable in his life.

The desertion had not surprised him, exactly. What surprised him was how much it had stung.

* * *

He called the meeting at nine, when the sun cleared the eastern ridge and the temperature climbed out of the brutal teens into the merely painful. He stood them in a line the way he had stood crews together for twenty-five years, and he told them what they already knew.

"They went up." He pointed with his chin at the shoulder, its western face catching the light like a blade laid flat. "Iversen's taken them onto the high route. The one nobody's climbed. Which means he's committed—no descent, no retreat to the valley, no way down but through. He's put himself and the Brandt girl and the documents on a line where one mistake kills them all." He let that land. He watched their faces. "We know this mountain. We know the lower routes. We go up, we cut the shoulder at the notch below the crest, and we take the satchel when they come down off the ridge exhausted. They'll have spent a night at altitude. They'll be carrying an injured man. They will be slow, and we will not be."

Silence. The wind moved through the moraine boulders with a sound like slow breathing.

It was Halvard who spoke, because Halvard always spoke, because Halvard had been with him since the Andes years and had earned the right the way soldiers earn the right to tell a general the battle is lost. "Soren." He said the name the way you'd set down something heavy. "The shoulder's a death route. Even the guide books won't put a line on it. Iversen's taking six people up it because he's got no choice. We've got a choice."

"We've got a deadline."

"We've got a client," Halvard said, "who is three thousand kilometers away and cannot see us. We've got a job that stopped being a job the moment shots were fired into scree at paying customers. And we've got—" he paused, and Vask saw him choose the word with care, "—a mountain that's already killed better men than any of us."

The Lindqvist brothers looked at their boots. Piet looked at Halvard with something close to hope.

Vask let the silence stretch. He was aware, in the way a man is aware of his own heartbeat, that this was the moment. That every crew has this moment once—on the approach, at the col, at the point where the mountain stops being scenery and starts being a party to the negotiation—and that the leader who loses it never gets it back. He had held crews together through worse than this. He had held a crew together on the Upper Fly itself, eleven years ago, when the storm came in and the radio died and the summit push had to happen or the season was lost, and he had held it together by the simple, brutal authority of a man who does not flinch.

He did not flinch now.

"Halvard," he said, and his voice was quiet, which was how they all knew to be afraid of it. "You can go down. Take the radio, take a horse, take whatever you need. I'll pay you through the season and we'll part as friends. But I'm going up that shoulder. Iversen thinks he's found the one route I won't follow. He's wrong. I've spent my whole life being the man who goes where the other man won't, and I am not going to stop on the one mountain that matters."

He watched them the way he watched weather. Halvard's jaw worked. Piet's eyes went from the shoulder to Vask and back, doing arithmetic with his life. The Lindqvists exchanged the twin-glance that was almost telepathy.

"I'm not asking for volunteers," Vask said. "I'm telling you the plan. Anyone who wants out says so now, in front of everyone, and no one thinks less of him." A lie, and everyone knew it was a lie, and everyone knew he knew, and that was the machinery of loyalty right there: a shared pretense, maintained at altitude.

No one spoke. After a moment, Halvard turned back to the stove without a word, which in the grammar of their twenty years meant yes, and God forgive me.

* * *

They climbed out of the moraine at eleven, onto the glacier's lower tongue, roped in two teams with Vask on the point of the first. The ice was old and blue and scarred with meltwater channels, and the going was good for the first two hours, and Vask used the good going to think.

He thought about Iversen. He had been thinking about Iversen for eleven years, the way a man worries a tooth.

They had been friends once. That was the part no one who knew the story ever understood, the part the industry gossip had flattened into rivalry because rivalry was a cleaner shape. They had been friends. August had been the best natural mountaineer Vask had ever seen—instinct where Vask had method, patience where Vask had appetite—and Vask had been the one who could read a client, close a contract, see three seasons ahead. Between them they had built an outfit that was going to own the northern ranges. And then the Upper Fly, and the storm, and the client who had paid for the summit and would not be refused, and the decision Vask had made in a tent at fourteen thousand feet while August argued for the descent.

*We go up. We're close. The window closes tomorrow.*

He had said it with the radio dead and the barometer falling and three of the crew already quietly against it, and August had looked at him with an expression Vask had never seen on him before or since—not anger, something worse, a kind of grief in advance—and had said, "Soren, the mountain doesn't care what we've been paid."

And then August had gone anyway. That was the thing Vask had never forgiven, and never admitted to not forgiving. August had argued, and lost the argument, and then shouldered his pack and led the push anyway, because that was who he was—because loyalty to the crew, to the plan, to Vask himself, ran deeper in him than his own judgment. And when it went wrong, when the serac came down and the cornice went and three men went with it, August had stood in the wreckage and taken the whole weight of it onto himself. *My call. My route. My fault.* He had never said Vask's name in the inquiry. Not once. He had walked into the blacklist like a man walking into a cell he'd built himself, and he had locked the door from the inside, and for eleven years Vask had lived in the strange country of a man who owed his freedom to another man's silence.

He should have been grateful. Instead the silence had become the loudest thing in his life. Every success since—the contracts, the reputation, the money, the clients who called him the most reliable operator in the north—had arrived slightly spoiled, slightly secondhand, because he knew what the record would say if August ever spoke. And so, in a way he could see clearly now and could not have articulated then, he had spent eleven years needing August Iversen to stay silent forever. Needing it the way a man needs a wound to keep its dressing on.

And then Henrik Brandt's filings had begun to unravel, and the old survey documents—the originals, with Vask's own handwriting in the margins of the falsified readings—had surfaced in the mine's archive like a body rising out of a lake, and Vask had understood that the past was not going to stay buried on its own. It would have to be buried properly. By hand.

And the courier route, the daughter, the crossing—when the plan had formed, it had seemed almost elegant. Use the mountain. Use the one man who knew it, who owed it nothing and who had every reason to stay bought and quiet. Use August's silence one last time.

Except August had stopped being silent.

Vask drove his axe into the ice and hauled over a crevasse bridge that groaned under him, and he thought: *He's not doing this for the girl. He's doing it because I asked him to be what I made him, and he's done being it.* And the thought arrived with its twin, unbidden, unwelcome: *And you would have done the same, at his age. Before you learned better.*

He killed the thought. There was no room on the shoulder for it.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the glacier steepened and the good going ended. The shoulder revealed itself in sections: first the rock band, broken and rotten, a hundred meters of scrambling where every hold had to be tested; above that, the icefield, angled at forty degrees and streaked with the blue glass of older seasons; above that, lost in cloud, the crest itself and whatever knife-edge horror Iversen had chosen to cross in the dark.

Vask climbed well. He was fifty-eight years old and he climbed better than any man he employed, and he knew it, and the knowing was the last unspoiled thing he owned. His body had been his instrument for four decades and it still answered, still held, still did what he asked of it on rock and ice that would have stopped men twenty years younger. He moved up the rock band with the economy of long habit, placing protection almost as an afterthought, and behind him he heard the crew strung out on the rope, breathing hard, and he drew strength from their effort the way a fire draws from its draft.

But the mountain was talking to him. That was the thing about the high places—they talked, if you'd spent enough of your life listening, and this one was saying what all the serious ones said, in the same flat voice, without malice: *You are being measured. Bring something.*

He had brought something once. On the Upper Fly, eleven years ago, he had brought everything he had—the judgment, the nerve, the refusal to turn back—and it had bought three graves and a ruined man. The mountain had accepted the payment and given nothing back. And he had gone on climbing anyway, because climbing was the only church he'd ever believed in, and he had told himself for eleven years that the Fly had been bad luck, a one-in-a-hundred serac release, the kind of thing that could happen to anyone.

Standing on the rock band with the icefield glittering above him and the cloud deck swallowing the crest, Vask let himself think, for the first time in eleven years, the whole thought: *It wasn't luck. I ordered the push. I knew the barometer. I knew the serac was loaded. I sent them up because the client's money mattered more than the weather, and August carried it so I wouldn't have to.*

He had come up this shoulder to take a satchel of documents. He understood, with the strange clarity that altitude gives, that the documents were almost beside the point now. What he was really climbing toward was a conversation eleven years overdue, at the end of which one of them would be right and one of them would be dead, and he no longer trusted himself to know which was which.

* * *

They camped at the top of the icefield as the light went, on a shelf of snow no wider than a tennis court, hacked level with the axes. The cloud had come up to meet them, which was both mercy and menace—mercy because it cut the wind, menace because it cut the view, and tomorrow they would climb into weather that could close on them like a hand.

The camp was quiet in the way camps are quiet when the crew has stopped believing in the plan but not yet in the man. Vask sat at the stove melting snow and watched his men. Halvard was checking the ropes by headlamp, running each meter through his hands. The Lindqvists were already in their bags, facing away from the fire. Piet sat with his back against a boulder, and Vask saw that the boy was looking down-valley, into the dark, at the route they'd come up.

"Piet."

The boy started. "Sir."

"You want to go down. Say it."

Piet's headlamp swung toward him, then away. "No, sir. I just—" He stopped. Started again. "My brother climbed. Died on the Hjalmar wall, six years back. My mother made me promise I'd never take a route I wouldn't take my own name off." He looked at the shoulder looming above them, invisible now, present anyway. "This one I'd take my name off."

Vask nodded slowly. "And yet you're here."

"Because you asked." The boy said it simply, without accusation, and somehow that was worse than accusation. "You asked, and nobody says no to you. That's the problem, sir. Nobody's said no to you in twenty years, and look where it's got us."

Vask held the boy's gaze for a long moment. Then he said, "Sleep. We move at four."

He took first watch, because he always took first watch, because a leader who slept first while his men froze was a leader halfway to being obeyed out of fear rather than respect, and fear at altitude curdles into mutiny faster than anywhere on earth. He sat against his pack with the stove ticking down and the cloud pressing close, and he listened to the mountain. Somewhere above, muffled by the cloud deck, he heard it—the deep, structural groan of ice moving under its own weight, the sound the serious mountains make when they're settling their accounts. It rolled down through the cloud and passed over the camp and faded, and the silence after it was enormous.

And in that silence, from down-slope, from the direction of the icefield, he heard the small crisp sound of a boot on frozen snow. Then another. Moving away.

Vask came off his pack with the rifle before his mind had finished the arithmetic. He swept the headlamp across the camp. Halvard's bag: occupied, breathing. The Lindqvists: both there, a tangle of bags and rope. Piet: empty.

He stood at the edge of the shelf and played the light down the route they had climbed, and for a moment he caught it—a flicker of movement far below, a headlamp winking through the cloud, descending fast and alone. Piet. Going down in the dark, on the icefield, solo, rather than spend one more hour on this mountain under this man.

Vask could have called after him. Could have fired a warning, could have threatened, could have done any of the hundred things a commander does when a soldier breaks. Instead he stood in the wind and watched the little light shrink and vanish into the white, and he felt something in his chest that he refused, with the last of his discipline, to name.

When he turned back, Halvard was sitting up in his bag, watching him. Neither of them spoke. The stove had gone out. The cloud pressed in.

"Four o'clock," Vask said, to no one in particular, to the mountain, to the dark. "We climb at four."

And above them, unseen through the cloud, the shoulder went on rising into the night, and somewhere on it, six small fires of breath and body heat held their line against the cold, and Soren Vask lay down in his bag with his boots on and did not sleep, because he had finally understood what he had known all along and spent eleven years not knowing:

Iversen wasn't running from him.

He was climbing toward something, and Vask was no longer sure he could climb fast enough to matter.
