## 30. The Cornice

The mountain had been lying to them all morning, and August knew it the way a man knows a card sharp by the way he shuffles.

The sky above the shoulder was a blue so clean it looked manufactured, the kind of blue that made you trust things you shouldn't. The wind had dropped to almost nothing after three days of trying to peel them off the face like old paint, and in that sudden hush the whole crew had gone quiet in a way that wasn't peace—it was the silence of people listening to their own hearts and not liking what they heard. Even Renny, strapped into the improvised litter between Dev's tent poles and forty meters of rope, had stopped his running commentary on the quality of Mara's belay anchors. The mountain was letting them climb. That was the part that worried August most.

They had been on the go since four in the morning, moving by headlamp through the frozen dark of the high camp, and now the sun stood two fists above the eastern ridge line and threw their shadows long and blue across the slope like a second party climbing beside them—six climbers and one ghost of a litter, all of it stretched thin as wire. Below them, a thousand meters down, the cloud deck they'd camped above lay flat and featureless as poured milk. Above them, maybe four hundred vertical meters to the crest, the snow steepened into the final ramp that would put them on the summit ridge proper.

Four hundred meters. After everything—the chute, the lost horse, the storm wall, the night crossing where the glacier had tried to swallow Dev whole and been denied only by rope and luck and Ilse's hands on the prusik—four hundred meters felt almost insulting. Like being asked to pay full fare for a journey you'd already bled for twice over.

"Snow's getting deep," Mara said from the lead. She was breaking trail now, trading leads with him every hour as they'd agreed, and she'd earned the front position honestly, wading thigh-deep through the fresh accumulation that the storm had stacked against the upper ramp. "No consolidation at all. It's like walking on bread dough."

August tested it himself with his axe shaft. The shaft sank past the basket without resistance. Underneath the new snow he could feel the older layer, wind-stiffened, but between them was a seam of weakness he could read with his boot the way other men read print. *Depth hoar,* some part of his brain murmured, cataloguing. *Faceted grains. The whole slab is sitting on ball bearings.*

"We stay roped tight," he said. "Pitch spacing. Nobody moves until the anchor's set."

Dev Okafor was carrying the satchel.

That fact had been settled two days ago, back in the cave during the storm, when Dev had laid the beacon's broken pieces out on the stone floor like evidence at a trial and confessed everything—Vask, the relay messages, the coded photographs, the orders to lead them into the avalanche chute. He'd confessed, and then he'd asked for the one job nobody else wanted: carry the documents. "If I'm the reason they're at risk," Dev had said, "then I should be the one who answers for them." August had understood the logic even then. A man carrying his own penance walks differently than a man carrying someone else's cargo. And Dev had walked well—careful, deliberate, checking his own foot placements the way a man does when he no longer trusts the ground to forgive him.

The satchel itself rode inside Dev's pack, wrapped in the waterproof liner Renny had sewn from spare tent fabric, cinched down with double buckles. Inside it were the survey cores' accompanying ledgers, the original field readings Henrik Brandt's people had falsified, the chain of custody documents Ilse had memorized and annotated by headlamp in the cave while the storm screamed outside—all of it, the whole rotten architecture of the mine's lies, weighing perhaps four kilograms. Four kilograms of paper that had already cost a horse, a career, a man's leg-to-be, though none of them knew that last part yet. Four kilograms that Vask had chased across an entire range and up an unclimbed shoulder, spending men and money and finally his own pride to reach.

"Rope's good," Dev called back. His voice came slightly breathless—not from fear, August thought, but from the altitude working on him, thinning the air another notch for every hour they climbed. "Anchor's bomber."

"Move," August said.

* * *

The last pitch before the crest was a ramp of perhaps fifty degrees, banked with wind-loaded snow against a rock rib on its left side, and open to the right onto nothing—a slope that rolled over and fell away in a smooth white curve toward the glacier basin two thousand meters below. August had seen slopes like it fail before. Every guide who'd worked the big ranges had seen it: the cornice, that great curled lip of overhanging snow that wind builds along a crest like foam on the edge of a wave, beautiful from a distance and treacherous up close because its edge looks exactly like solid ground right up until the moment it isn't.

He'd flagged it in his mind since the reconnaissance years ago. He'd flagged it again last night, lying in the snow trench watching stars wheel, running the route in his head the way he always did, placing each member of the crew on each section like chess pieces. The plan was simple: traverse left, hug the rock rib, never touch the crest itself until they'd passed beneath the corniced section and gained the ridge at its lowest, safest point. He'd drawn it in the snow with an axe tip for Mara and Ilse. *We do not walk on the wave's lip. We walk under it, close to the stone.* Everyone had nodded. Everyone had understood.

But plans are made in the valley of the mind, and mountains don't live there.

The trouble was the rock rib itself. At the top of the ramp it pinched down to a band of verglased slab no wider than a sidewalk, glazed with rime, offering poor protection and worse footing. With Renny in the litter, hauling him across that band would take hours they didn't have—the afternoon light was already leaning, and August could see the far side of the crest catching gold, which meant their side would catch shadow soon, and temperature, and with temperature the snow would change character entirely. So he made the call every guide dreads and sometimes must make: they'd cross the open slope directly, fast, spaced wide, one at a time, minimizing the load on any single point of the slab.

Ilse went first. She moved beautifully—she'd been proving that for days now, ever since the Col of Hanging Ice, and somewhere in the last week August had stopped being surprised by it and started simply relying on it. She crossed the open fifty meters in confident, economical strides, punching through to her shin, angling hard left toward the rock, and pulled onto the safe ground beyond without drama.

Mara followed. Then Renny's litter, hauled across by August and Dev working the lines together, Renny swearing cheerfully about the indignity of being freighted up a mountain like a crate of dried fish. Then August himself, crossing with the particular tension of a man who has done this arithmetic too many times and knows how thin the margins run.

Which left Dev, and the satchel, alone on the lower slope.

* * *

Later—in the hospital, in the weeks after, in the testimony room where he would stand under lights and describe this moment to strangers with clipboards—August would replay those eleven seconds frame by frame, hunting for the decision he could have made differently, the word he could have shouted sooner, the whole branching tree of choices that might have ended anywhere but here. And every time he would arrive at the same place: there was no wrong choice available. There was only the mountain, doing what cornices do.

It started as a sound. Not a crack—that was what he expected, what he'd have reacted to instantly—but a sound like a long exhalation, a soft whumpf rolling down from above, the sound of a slab settling onto its weak layer the way a house settles onto a failing foundation. August's head snapped up. He saw it happen the way you see a wave begin: a hairline fracture shooting laterally across the slope thirty meters above Dev, clean as a cut in paper, then widening, then the whole wind-loaded bank above the fracture detaching from the mountain with a shrug and starting downhill.

"DEV!" The name tore out of him raw. "SLAB—MOVE LEFT—ROCK—GO!"

Dev moved. God help him, he moved fast—he dropped the axe into self-arrest grip and lunged for the rock rib, and he might even have made it, might have reached the same safe ground the rest of them stood on, if the slab hadn't caught him mid-lunge. But it did catch him. The fracture line ran wider than August had judged, and the sliding mass took Dev's legs out from under him and carried him downslope like a leaf on a spillway, tumbling, the pack with the satchel wrenching at his shoulders, the rope snapping taut between him and the anchor August had placed at the base of the rib.

And then the cornice went too.

Whether the slide's vibration triggered it or whether it had been hanging there for weeks waiting for an excuse, August never learned. What he saw was the crest itself—that beautiful, innocent-looking white lip he'd steered them beneath—calving off in a curving mass the size of a truck, folding over and down in slow motion at first and then all at once, and the air filled with snow, and the world turned white and sideways and loud.

The rope saved Dev's life. August had no doubt of that then and no doubt now. The anchor held—Mara's placement, a buried deadman backed by a piton, bomber as promised—and when the sliding mass hit the roll-over and began to pour over the edge of the void, the rope checked Dev's fall at the last possible instant. He swung out over emptiness like a plumb bob on a line, the entire cornice debris cascading past him and down into the basin below in a white river that went on falling for seconds that lasted minutes.

But the rope also broke his leg.

The deceleration was brutal. Later, the surgeon in the valley town would explain it in terms August half understood—axial loading, the femur taking the force of two hundred pounds of falling climber arrested over less than a meter of stretch—and August would nod and listen and understand only this: the rope that kept Dev from falling two thousand meters also snapped his thigh bone like green wood. He heard it. They all heard it. A wet, deep crack that carried through the roar of the settling snow, and then Dev's scream, high and human and utterly unlike anything the mountain itself made, cutting through the white silence afterward like a blade.

When the powder fog cleared, Dev hung twelve meters below the crest, suspended above the void, his right leg folded under him at an angle legs don't make, the satchel still lashed to his pack, still intact, swinging gently in the wind like a pendulum finding its rest.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and it happened because of everything that had come before it—the chute, the crevasse, the cave, the long nights. It happened because six people who had started as strangers and suspects had become, without anyone announcing it, a team.

Mara anchored first, wordlessly, building a redundant system off the rock rib while August assessed. Ilse clipped into the haul line without being asked. Renny, one-handed, feverish, his infected wrist wrapped in Ilse's dressings, dragged himself to the anchor and took position on the brake anyway, feeding rope through his mittened hand with his teeth gritted. Nobody discussed it. There was no time to discuss it and no need. August lowered himself over the crest on the second rope, down the smooth wind-scoured face toward the hanging man, and the last thing he registered before committing his weight to the descent was Ilse's eyes above him, steady, and her voice saying only: "We've got you both."

Dev was conscious. That was the first mercy. His face was gray as the granite, lips bloodless, breath coming in shallow hitches that meant pain was eating his oxygen faster than the thin air allowed him to replace it. But his eyes found August's and locked on, and the first thing he said—before help, before the leg, before anything—was:

"Satchel's okay."

August laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but it was a laugh. "I know," he said, reaching him, steadying his spinning body against the wall of snow. "You absolute fool. I know."

"It's okay," Dev insisted, and his hand, shaking, found the strap and gripped it. "Check it. Check the seals. I checked them when we stopped. Nothing tore, nothing—" His voice cracked apart. "Please tell me it's okay. Tell me I didn't—"

"It's okay, Dev." August took the man's weight fully onto his own harness, unclipped the satchel from the pack and slung it across his own chest where he could feel it against his heartbeat. "You saved it. You hear me? Whatever happens next—you saved it."

Then they hauled. It took forty minutes of the hardest work any of them had ever done, raising Dev's dead-weight body up the face hand over hand, three on the brake and August guiding from below, Dev biting down on a strap against the grinding of the bone, passing in and out of consciousness as they lifted him. The sun touched the western peaks while they worked. The cold came down off the crest like water off a ledge. When they finally rolled Dev onto level ground behind the rock rib, out of the wind, Ilse was already cutting away his pant leg with Renny's knife, and what lay beneath made even Mara, who had set bones in climbing accidents before, go quiet.

The femur had broken through. Not much—a pale triangle of bone pressing against skin gone shiny and bruised-black—but enough. Enough to mean splinting, morphine if they had it (they had almost none), enough to mean that the strongest carrier among them would now be carried. Enough to mean the descent they'd imagined—hard, brutal, possible—had just become something else entirely.

Dev surfaced from the fog of shock once more while Ilse worked, her hands sure and quick, her medical training no longer hidden from anyone. He looked up at the circle of faces around him—at August, at Mara, at Renny, at Ilse—and he said, in a voice thinned to almost nothing:

"I'm sorry. For the beacon. For the messages. For all of it."

"You're sorry," August said. He crouched down, put his hand briefly on Dev's forehead, pushing back the sweat-damp hair. "You destroyed the beacon. You told us about the chute. You've been carrying your own coffin up this mountain for three days." He looked at the satchel against his chest, then back at Dev's pain-glazed eyes. "The ledger's balanced, Okafor. Now we just have to get you down the mountain to collect."

Above them, the broken crest stood open against the darkening sky, a ragged white wound where the cornice had been, showing the blue ice of the mountain's interior like exposed bone. Beyond it, visible now for the first time, the far valley lay in shadow, and somewhere down in that shadow—August was certain of it, the way he was certain of weather—Soren Vask was watching the same sunset and making his own calculations.

The documents were safe. The witness was alive. The price had been paid in the only currency the mountain accepted.

"Get the litter ready," August said, standing, turning to face the crest and the darkness beyond it. "We move in twenty minutes. We're not spending the night here."

Nobody argued. There was nothing left to argue with. Only the wind, rising again off the summit ridge, and below them in the gathering dark, the long, impossible way down.
