## 31. Carrying Dev

The cornice had taken Dev at the worst possible hour—mid-afternoon, when the light on the shoulder still looked generous and the cold hadn't yet begun its evening argument with everything that breathed. Renny had heard it happen before he'd seen it: a sound like a door slamming in the sky, then August's voice cracking across the radio channel they kept between rope teams, raw and immediate, *man down, man down, anchor line*.

By the time Renny reached the anchor point, hauling himself along the fixed line with his bad wrist wrapped tight against his chest, Dev was already being hauled back over the lip of the slope by Mara and Ilse working a Z-pulley off August's shouted geometry. He came up over the edge like something dredged from deep water—white-faced, lips going gray, one leg bent below the knee at an angle that made even Renny, who had cooked through two restaurant kitchens and one war-zone aid station, look away on instinct.

The leg was bad. Renny knew it before anyone said it. He'd seen that particular wrongness once before, in a man pulled from a crevasse on the Waddington approach, years ago, back when Renny had still believed mountains were honest. The tibia had gone through or nearly through; the boot was filling; and Dev, who had spent three weeks lying to all of them for money he probably needed for reasons none of them would ever fully know, was looking up at the people he'd betrayed and asking—not demanding, not bargaining, just asking—if someone could hold his hand.

Ilse held it. She didn't let go for four hours.

* * *

They couldn't stay where they were. That was the first arithmetic of the afternoon, and it was August who did it out loud, kneeling in the snow beside Dev's splinted leg with the altimeter watch in his palm, speaking in the flat, unadorned voice of a man who had learned long ago that sentiment and altitude do not mix.

"We're at five thousand eight hundred meters. Sunset in ninety minutes. The temperature is already falling through minus twenty-five, and the wind is coming around to the northwest, which means it'll be funneling straight up this face by dark. We cannot camp here. We cannot leave him here. So we carry him."

Renny stood a little apart, the way he always did when the guides were doing their math, close enough to hear, far enough not to interrupt. It was a habit from the old kitchens—the sous chef's position, the one who listened and calculated and waited for the moment his numbers mattered. And his numbers were already mattering, because August had laid out the problem cleanly but hadn't yet done the part Renny was best at: the food, the fuel, the heat, the hours.

Carrying a grown man down a mountain cost calories. Carrying him cost daylight. Every hour spent moving him horizontally instead of descending vertically was an hour the storm window closed a little more, an hour the rations stretched a little thinner, an hour Dev's shattered leg burned through body heat that no sleeping bag alone could replace. Renny ran it in his head while August talked: six carriers working shifts, four on the litter poles and two resting, rotating every twenty minutes because twenty minutes was what a human back could give at this altitude before the shaking started. Six people burning maybe seven thousand calories a day instead of four. Their remaining food, counted honestly after the chute had eaten the pack horse and half their stores, was four days at full ration, six at half, eight if they cut to thirds—and thirds meant weakness, and weakness on a descent like this meant falls, and falls meant more litters.

He said it out loud, finally, because somebody had to and because it was, in its way, the only useful thing he could contribute with a wrist that wouldn't close around a carabiner.

"August. If we carry him the whole way, we're on half rations starting tomorrow morning, and we should think about thirds by day three."

August didn't flinch. Renny had watched this man take avalanches, betrayals, and a blizzard on the chin without visible complaint, and there was something almost eerie about how little the numbers moved him. "Then we're on half rations," he said simply. "Nobody carries more than twenty minutes. Mara sets the rotation. Ilse stays with Dev on the litter—he needs eyes on that leg, and she's got the training. Renny, you're off the rotation."

And there it was. The sentence Renny had been dreading and expecting in equal measure, delivered without cruelty, which somehow made it worse.

"Off the rotation," Renny repeated.

"Your wrist is broken, or close enough. You can't grip a litter pole. You'll drop your corner on a traverse and put all of us over the edge." August wasn't looking at him when he said it; he was looking at the route below, reading the snow like a man reading a ledger he didn't trust. "You cook. You keep Dev warm. You manage the fuel. That's your job now, and it matters as much as the carrying."

Renny wanted to argue. God, how he wanted to argue. Three days ago, at the shelter hut below the chute, he had offered to stay behind himself—had laid out the logic plainly, four mouths fed better without his slowing them, the arithmetic of survival dressed up as nobility—and August had refused him with a single wordless shake of the head that Renny had understood, eventually, as the most generous thing anyone had ever done for him. Now the same man was benching him, and the benching felt like a smaller version of the same mercy, and mercy, Renny was learning, is not always comfortable to receive.

So he swallowed it. He said, "Understood," and he meant it, mostly, and then he went to work on the only engineering problem that mattered before dark: building a thing that could hold a hundred and eighty pounds of injured porter together on forty-degree snow without killing the men holding it.

* * *

The litter was Mara's idea in its bones and everyone's idea in its details, which is how the best ideas on a mountain usually work. They had two tents between them—the small dome Ilse had carried since Kessik and the battered tunnel tent that had survived the chute when the pack horse hadn't—and Mara stood over the wreckage of both with her knife out, weighing sacrifice the way she weighed everything: silently, completely, without asking permission.

"The dome goes," she said. "Poles become the frame. Fly becomes the bed. Tunnel tent keeps us alive at night. Nobody sleeps comfortable again until the valley. Agreed?"

Nobody argued. There was nothing left to argue with. The dome's poles came apart into six sections of drawn aluminum, light and slightly bowed, and Mara lashed them into a ladder-frame with the prusik cord and the remains of the haul line, cross-braced at head and foot, the joints whipped with tape stripped from the ice axes' grips. Ilse contributed the knowledge her father's money had bought and her own pride had hidden: she showed them how to pad the frame with the foam pads, how to splint the leg to the frame itself so it moved with the litter instead of against it, how to wedge Dev's pelvis so the jolts of the descent wouldn't grind bone in the wound. She worked with quick, certain hands, and she talked to Dev the whole time, low and continuous, a running commentary of nothing—names of streets in a city Renny didn't recognize, the taste of some pastry, the color of a harbor in September—a stream of ordinary human noise poured steadily into the ears of a man sliding toward shock.

Renny built the harnesses. It was the one physical task his wrist could bear: whipping cord, tying off, the repetitive work of knots that asked nothing of the left hand but steadiness. He made four carry slings from pack straps and webbing, padded where they'd cross shoulders, adjustable so the rotation could swap bodies in under a minute. He tested each one by hanging his own weight from a boulder, and when the third sling creaked at a stitch he cut it apart and rebuilt it without being asked, because a stitch that fails at the hut fails as a joke, and a stitch that fails at five thousand meters fails as a funeral.

Dev watched all of it. That was the thing Renny couldn't stop noticing. A man with a shattered leg, gray-lipped, in shock, watched his own stretcher being built out of the wreckage of his own shelter, and watched it not with fear but with a kind of terrible attention—as if he were memorizing the process, as if he intended, someday, somewhere, to owe them a debt itemized down to the last knot.

At one point, while Renny was tying the final bridle to the head of the frame, Dev's eyes found his and held.

"I'm sorry," Dev said. Just that. No elaboration, no plea, no explanation. The confession had already been made, days ago, in the cave, when he'd destroyed the beacon and told August everything. This was something else—smaller, harder. An apology addressed not to the man he'd wronged most but to the cook. To the one whose trust had been the least transactional, the most freely given, the cheapest to buy and therefore the most expensive to have stolen.

Renny's hands kept working. He finished the knot, cinched it, bit the tag end clean, and only then looked back.

"You'll carry your share again someday," he said. "That's the apology I want. Not words. Weight."

Dev's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Weight," he repeated, like a vow.

* * *

They started down at last light, which was a mistake, and they all knew it was a mistake, and they did it anyway, because the alternative was a night at five thousand eight hundred meters with a man in shock and no shelter worth the name, and a mistake you choose is at least a decision.

The first hour taught them everything the next two days would cost.

Four carriers, slings over shoulders, the litter swinging gently between them like a cradle that hated its cargo. Twenty-minute rotations called by Mara in a voice pitched to carry over wind without carrying down the slope. August on point, choosing every step for the front pair, reading the snow with that flat attention Renny had come to think of as the man's native language. Ilse riding the litter itself, kneeling beside Dev, one hand on the splint, one on his pulse, counting quietly.

Renny brought up the rear with the stove, the fuel, the medical kit, and the food bag—the entire metabolic economy of the expedition strapped to a man with one working hand. He couldn't help carry. He couldn't lead. He could count, and heat, and feed, and so that is what he did, obsessively, the way he'd once run a pass during service: everything timed, everything tallied, every calorie accounted for before it was spent.

Half rations began at dawn, as promised. Renny divided the day's food into six equal portions and then, without announcement, shaved a seventh portion's worth off his own and redistributed it—one extra handful of oats to Dev's pot, a few extra grams of cheese to the carriers on the heavy shifts. He did it secretly, at first, measuring in the dark before the others woke, and it took Mara exactly one day to catch him, because Mara caught everything, and when she did she didn't thank him and she didn't praise him. She just started shaving her own ration too, silently, matching him handful for handful, and by the second morning all of them were doing it except Dev, who fought it, and lost, and lay in the litter swallowing food that belonged to other people with tears freezing in the corners of his eyes.

That was the arithmetic nobody had planned for: that hunger shared evenly hurts less than hunger distributed fairly. Fairness said the injured man ate less, burned less, deserved less. The crew decided otherwise, unanimously, without discussion, and the decision cost them strength they did not have, and they paid it anyway, and Renny—who three days earlier had offered to remove himself from the equation entirely—understood, watching them pay it, that he had been wrong at the shelter hut in a way that went deeper than logistics. He had thought survival was a number to be optimized. It wasn't. It was a promise being kept in public, hourly, whether or not anyone felt like keeping it.

* * *

The second day was the traverse above the icefall, and it nearly ended everything.

The route required them to move the litter sideways across a slope that angled at thirty-five degrees toward a field of blue seracs, and a litter is the wrong shape for sideways. It wants to pendulum. It wants to swing downhill at every stance change, and at every swing the four carriers had to arrest it together or watch it slide, cargo and all, into the icefall's grinding throat.

Mara reorganized them into pairs anchored to the fixed line, leapfrogging anchors, and for six hours the crew moved in a rhythm so slow it barely qualified as motion: plant anchor, shift load, breathe, swing the litter ten meters, plant anchor. Ten meters at a time. Renny, walking sweep with the rope bag over his good shoulder, counted the stances the way he'd once counted covers during a dinner rush, and somewhere past the ninetieth stance he stopped counting and started praying instead, silently, clumsily, a kitchen prayer with no theology in it—*just hold, just hold, just hold*—addressed to nothing in particular, or to everything, or possibly just to the aluminum poles in his own improvised frame, which held, and held, and held.

Dev faded in and out through those hours. Shock does that; it comes in tides. When he surfaced he talked—about Lagos, where he'd grown up, about his mother's shop, about a brother who wrote letters he'd never answered—and the talking was itself a vital sign, and Ilse monitored it the way she monitored the pulse, noting the gaps, timing the silences, feeding questions into the quiet whenever it lasted too long. Once, surfacing, Dev looked up the slope at the carriers ahead of him—looked at their backs, their straining harnesses, the sweat freezing in their collars—and said, clearly, in a voice with no shock in it at all:

"I know what this costs. I'm counting too."

Nobody answered him. Nobody had to.

* * *

On the second night they dug in below a rock band at five thousand one hundred meters, and Renny did the thing he was for.

The stove went up in the lee of the boulders, shielded by packs and bodies, and he melted snow in shifts, batch after batch, because hydration was the difference between Dev's blood staying liquid in his veins and turning to something slower. Hot drinks all around, every two hours through the night, on a schedule he kept by feel because his watch had died in the storm and nobody else's hands were free enough to check theirs. He brewed the last of the tea, then the sugar water, then—when the sugar ran out—plain hot water with a pinch of the electrolyte powder scraped from the bottom of the tin, and he served it all with the same ceremony he'd once given tasting menus, because dignity, he had learned, is also a ration, and it depletes faster than food.

August sat with him at the stove through the middle watch, both of them too tired to talk, and at some point, without preamble, August said:

"At the hut. When you offered to stay behind."

"Yeah."

"That was the bravest cowardice I've ever seen. And refusing it was the hardest call I made on this whole mountain. Harder than the chute. Harder than telling Vask no." He fed another armful of snow to the melting pot. "I need you to understand why I refused. It wasn't sentiment. It was this." He nodded uphill, toward the litter, where Ilse sat awake with Dev's hand in hers. "The moment we carry one of us, we're not a crew anymore. We're a proof. Proof that it can be done—that nobody gets left, that the arithmetic loses. If I'd let you stay at that hut, I'd have lost the argument I'm making right now with my own legs. Every meter we descend with Dev on that frame is a meter that says the mountain doesn't get a vote about who we are."

Renny turned that over for a long time, the way he turned over dough, slowly and with both hands.

"And if we don't make it?" he asked finally. "If the carrying costs too much? If we're slow, and the storm catches us, and we all—"

"Then we all," August said, without hesitation, without softening, without a single syllable of comfort. "That's the deal. That was always the deal. Collective or nothing. I made the opposite choice once, on the Upper Fly, years ago—I followed orders up when every number said down, and three people died for it, and I've spent eleven years learning the difference between a hard choice and a wrong one." He looked at Renny then, directly, and his eyes in the stove-light were the oldest thing on the mountain. "Leaving you at that hut would have been easy. Easy isn't the same as right. It never has been."

Renny didn't say anything. He handed August the next cup of hot water, and their hands stayed around it together a moment longer than the transfer required, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

Dawn of the third day came up rose-colored and viciously clear, the kind of sky that meant cold, and they broke camp in silence and took up the litter, and Renny took his place at the rear with the stove and the bags and the growing, stubborn certainty that his arms were going to carry something today whether or not they were on the rotation.

It happened at midmorning, on the long moraine ramp below the icefall, when Mara's corner carrier—the young porter Joss, hollow-eyed from three nights of broken sleep—stumbled on scree and went down to one knee, and the litter swung, and for one lurching second the whole system tilted toward failure. Renny dropped the bags where he stood, crossed the fifteen meters of loose rock at a dead sprint despite the wrist, and got his good shoulder under the frame's corner rail before the swing became a slide. He couldn't grip. He didn't need to grip. He braced the rail against the trapezius and pushed with his legs and held the corner level through three steps, four, five, until Joss regained his feet and the sling came taut again and the litter settled.

Mara saw it. Of course she saw it. Mara saw everything.

"Rotation," she said that evening, revising the schedule at the halt, "goes to six. Renny takes rear-corner relief, twenty minutes, pushing only, no grip. Doctor's orders are mine to give now." And when August raised an eyebrow, she added, flatly, "He carried a corner for five steps today on adrenaline. Five controlled steps means twenty careful ones. I'm not wasting a carrier because we're sentimental about his wrist."

Renny laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound he barely recognized—and August, after a beat, nodded once, and that was how Renny Okafor-adjacent, cook, coward-turned-something-else, broken-wristed keeper of the flame, entered the rotation of the carrying.

Twenty minutes at a time. Pushing, never gripping, his splinted arm strapped flat against the frame rail, his legs doing the work his hands couldn't. It hurt. It hurt in the wrist, in the shoulder, in the old scar tissue along his ribs from a fall no one on this expedition had ever been told about. He did it anyway, every rotation, and on the third rotation of the fourth day, cresting the last moraine with the valley finally opening below them—green, impossible, real—he found himself matching stride with Dev's head at the litter's forward corner, close enough to speak without raising his voice.

"Hey," Renny said. "You remember what I told you? Back at the anchor?"

Dev's eyes opened. They were sunken, fever-bright, but present. "Weight."

"Weight," Renny confirmed. "We're almost there. Valley floor by tomorrow, ranger station by tomorrow night. And when that leg heals—and it will heal, I've seen worse heal—you're going to show up at our outfitting office in Kessik, and you're going to ask Mara for a job, and she's going to make you carry other people's loads up mountains until you've paid off every gram of this. That's the deal. That's the apology."

Dev looked at him for a long moment. Then, with the ghost of the smile from the anchor point: "I'll want references."

"From me?" Renny snorted. "I'm the cook. My reference says *eats standing up, complains about everything, shows up anyway.*"

"Best kind," Dev said, and slept.

* * *

They made the last camp that night within sight of the tree line, close enough to see individual trees, close enough to smell—actually smell—green things growing, and Renny stood at the edge of camp after the others had turned in, looking down at the darkness where the forest began, and he thought about the shelter hut, and the offer he'd made there, and the man who'd refused it.

He had thought, at the time, that offering to stay behind was the truest thing he'd ever done. He had dressed the arithmetic up as sacrifice and called it courage, and it had felt like courage, right up until the moment he'd watched five people strap a sixth to a frame made of tent poles and refuse, day after day, hour after hour, to set that frame down. Now he understood that his offer had been a test he'd set for himself and failed in the passing—that the real courage wasn't in volunteering to be subtracted from the sum. It was in staying in the equation. In being carried when you couldn't walk and carrying when you couldn't grip and eating less so the injured man could eat more and calling none of it sacrifice, because sacrifice implies a choice made once, and this was a choice made fresh every twenty minutes, by everyone, all the time, without applause.

Survival was collective or nothing. He'd known the phrase for years—somebody had said it to him once, in another life, in a kitchen that burned down. He'd never understood it until now.

Below him, the forest waited. Below the forest, the ranger station, the radio, the helicopter, the investigators, the end of everything and the beginning of whatever came next. Behind him, up-slope, far away and falling farther, there were lights—tiny, wavering, wrong-colored, moving. He counted them twice. Four lights, strung out along the shoulder they themselves had descended, moving slower than any healthy team should move, breaking apart and regrouping and breaking apart again.

Vask's men. What was left of them, coming down the hard way, too late and too proud.

Renny stood there in the dark with his one good hand jammed in his pocket and his broken one tucked against his chest, and he did not wake August, and he did not raise the alarm, because there was nothing to alarm anyone about—not yet, not from that distance, not with that much mountain still between them. But he memorized the lights. He counted them again. Four.

And then he turned and walked back to the fire, and woke Mara quietly, and told her what he'd seen, and watched her face go still and sharp as a blade laid on a stone—because whatever Vask had lost on that shoulder, whatever had broken his team apart in the night, he was still coming down. Still behind them. Still hungry.

And the valley, which had looked like the end of the story all day, had just stopped looking like an ending at all.
