## 32. Summit Ridge, Last Light

The last pitch was nothing and everything.

August had led them up the shoulder's final headwall on a line he'd picked in the blue hour of morning, when the ice had still been honest—hard, glassy, predictable. By late afternoon it had gone soft in the sun and then hard again as the light swung off it, and now they climbed through the transition zone where the mountain couldn't decide what it wanted to be. He placed each tool with a care that looked like slowness to anyone watching but was really a kind of prayer. Above him, thirty meters of steepening snow led to a crest he could not quite see over. Below him, strung out on the fixed lines like beads on a wire, were five people whose lives he had accepted as his own weight to carry: Mara, steady and silent; Ilse, climbing with a fluency that would have astonished the woman who'd stepped off the trail at Kessik three weeks ago; Renny, one-armed and furious about it; Dev, strapped into the litter rig they'd lashed between two porters' frames because his shattered leg could not take even the vibration of a boot placement; and young Pell, who had come with Vask's deserting team and chosen, two days ago, to throw in with them instead.

The wind found them before the crest did. It came around the shoulder from the west, a long smooth river of cold that had crossed a hundred miles of ice without touching anything warm enough to slow it down. August felt it first as a pressure against his chest, then as a sound—a low note, almost musical, building behind the ridge like an orchestra tuning in some cathedral of air. He knew what it meant. Every guide knew what it meant. The jet stream had dropped, or the storm wall that had pinned them in the bivouac cave for four days had a brother, and it was coming home.

He topped out at 6:41 by his watch, and the world opened.

He had stood on summits before. He had stood on this range's neighbors, on peaks with names and registered routes and rescue helicopters within reach. This was not that. The Upper Fly shoulder was not a summit in any book—it was a crest nobody had bothered to name because nobody had been fool enough to stand on it—and yet from here the whole architecture of the range lay exposed below him like the skeleton of something enormous and sleeping. To the east, the far valley: a single ribbon of grey-green threaded through brown scree, catching the last horizontal light so that it glowed like a vein of ore in dark stone. The ranger station was down there somewhere, twelve hours of descent if everything went right. Twelve hours. He stared at it the way a drowning man stares at a boat.

To the west, behind them, the clouds were already assembling. They came up the valley they'd fled that morning, layer on layer, slate-colored, organized, patient. The storm they'd outrun for six days had found its second wind.

"August." Mara's voice, close behind him, stripped of everything but function. "What are we looking at?"

He turned and looked at her honestly, which was more than he'd managed some days. "Looking at," he said, "is the valley. *Getting to* is the problem."

She understood immediately. She always did. The light was going—not fading gradually the way it did at lower altitudes but draining out of the sky like water out of a cracked basin, the alpenglow burning rose and gold on the eastern faces for perhaps ten minutes more, then gone. And when it went, the temperature would go with it. His thermometer—the old brass one, the one that had survived the disaster expedition because he'd kept it in his pocket instead of his pack, the one thing he'd saved—read minus nineteen and falling. In an hour it would be twenty-five below. In two, thirty. On an exposed ridge at nearly six thousand meters, with wind coming.

"You can't descend that in the dark," Mara said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the route from above now, the way he had: the upper glacier spilling off the eastern face in a cascade of seracs, the traverse below it crossing two avalanche paths, the moraine beyond. In daylight, with fresh legs, it was a serious but manageable descent. In darkness, exhausted, hypothermic, carrying a man with a compound fracture—it was a tomb.

"No," August said. "We can't."

Ilse reached the crest next, unclipping from the line with hands he could barely watch—they were moving too slowly, the fine motor control going, and she didn't seem to know it yet. She came and stood beside him at the very edge, where the snow curved over and became sky, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Below them the far valley held its light like a cupped hand holds water, and above them the first stars were already out, hard and white, indifferent.

"We made it," she said.

It was not a question either, but it was wrong in a way questions aren't, and August heard himself answer it anyway, gently, because she deserved gentleness and there wasn't going to be much of it tonight. "We made the top," he said. "That's not the same thing."

* * *

They dug in while there was still light enough to work, and this was the decision that would matter more than any other he made on the mountain, though he wouldn't know it until morning.

The crest itself was unusable—a knife-edge of wind-scoured ice with no shelter on either aspect worth the name—but forty meters down the eastern side there was a terrace, a shelf of old snow plastered against the rock where the wind eddied and deposited rather than stripped. August had seen it from below on his reconnaissance years ago and filed it away in the part of his mind where he kept things he hoped never to need. Now he needed it. He traversed the crew across the crest one at a time, anchored, while the light died around them in bands of violet and iron-grey, and by the time the last of them—Dev, hauled across the slope on the litter by main force, Mara and Pell hauling while August belayed—reached the terrace, the stars had taken the sky completely.

Then they dug.

There is a particular kind of labor that happens at altitude in the cold, and it is not like other labor. Every movement costs twice what it should. The snow comes away in plates and slabs that must be cut, passed hand to hand, stacked into windwalls. Your breath freezes in your scarf and your scarf freezes to your face and when you turn your head the whole stiffened fabric cracks like thin ice on a puddle. You sweat, and the sweat is poison, because it will freeze inside your clothing the moment you stop moving, and you will spend the night paying interest on every drop. August worked them in shifts—twenty minutes digging, twenty minutes in the improvised shelter of the packs—and he watched their hands, all of their hands, the way a doctor watches pulses.

Renny he pulled off after ten minutes. One-handed, feverish still, he'd insisted on cutting blocks with his good arm, and he'd done real work, but his color was going wrong, a mottled grey-white that August recognized from the worst night of the old expedition, the night before the first man died. He sat Renny down inside the half-finished trench, wrapped him in both sleeping bags they could spare, and put a stove between his knees with strict instructions to hold it and nothing else.

"I'm not finished," Renny said, with the particular stubbornness of the very tired.

"You're finished," August said. "You carried Dev up half the shoulder today. That was your shift. All of it."

Renny looked at him for a long moment, and then at Dev, propped against the growing snow wall with his leg splinted and elevated, his face grey with pain he no longer bothered to disguise because disguising it cost calories. Something moved across Renny's face—not gratitude exactly, something rawer than that, the look of a man being told he is allowed to be weak and does not know how to accept permission.

"Fine," he said. But he took the stove.

By full dark they had a trench deep enough to sit in, windwalls chest-high on three sides, a roof of the tent fly stretched between ski poles and ice axes and weighted with cut blocks. It was, by any honest standard, a hole in the mountain. It was also, by the only standard that mattered tonight, the difference between a miserable night and a fatal one. August had spent enough nights in holes in mountains to know the arithmetic precisely: exposed on the crest, in a thirty-below wind, they would have been dead by midnight, all six of them, and the documents in Ilse's satchel would have become a curiosity for whoever found the bodies in spring. Here, in the trench, with the walls breaking the wind, with shared body heat and the small yellow island of the stove, they had a chance. Not a good chance. A chance.

He did the rations aloud, because they had earned the truth and because hiding numbers from tired people breeds worse things than fear. Two days of food remained, at half-rations. Fuel for one hot meal tonight and one tomorrow morning, if the stove behaved. Water was snow, melted sparingly, because melting snow ate fuel the way fire eats kindling. Dev needed water most and could least help get it, and they fed him meltwater warmed to blood temperature, sipped, because cold water in a hypothermic gut is a tax the body cannot pay.

"Twelve hours down," Ilse said quietly, when the accounting was done. "If we start at first light."

"First light is seven hours away," August said. "And we don't move in the dark on this terrain. Not down a glacier with seracs over the route. Not carrying Dev." He paused. "I've done it before. Moving in the dark, I mean. On the old expedition we summited at two in the morning because Vask wanted the record, and we came down in the dark, and that's when Oleg went into the bergschrund."

He had never told them that detail—that specific detail, the hour, the reason. He'd told the shape of the story in the cave during the storm, the broad strokes: three dead, his fault, Vask's order. But the mechanism of it, the *why* of the hour, he had kept. Some things you keep because saying them costs more than holding them. Tonight, on another summit, in another forced bivouac, with another man broken beside him, the cost of holding it had gotten too high.

"Vask wanted the summit photo at dawn," he said. "So we climbed through the night. We were on top at two. Everyone was past exhaustion—we'd been going thirty-one hours. And on the descent, in the dark, Oleg walked off the edge of a crevasse that any of us would have seen in daylight. The rope caught him. We got him out. But Hans went back for a dropped camera an hour later, same darkness, same fatigue, and the cornice he stepped on let go, and Hans did not come back." He looked at the snow wall, at the stars above it. "Two of the three deaths happened on the descent. After the summit. After the photograph. Because we were too proud and too ordered-around to sit down and wait for daylight."

Nobody spoke for a while. The stove hissed. Somewhere above them the wind sang its one note against the crest.

"So we wait," Ilse said finally. It wasn't resignation in her voice. It was something else—recognition, maybe, the voice of a woman who had spent her life watching men spend other people's lives on schedules and symbols, hearing a man say the opposite for the first time.

"We wait," August confirmed. "We dig deeper if we have energy. We rotate watches. We keep Dev warm, we keep Renny warm, we keep each other warm. And at first light, we go down that mountain like it's the most dangerous thing we've ever done, because it is."

* * *

The watches divided the night into pieces small enough to survive. Two hours each: August, Mara, Pell, Ilse, with August taking the first and the last, because the last watch was the one where the coldest hour met the decision to move, and he wanted no one else carrying that intersection.

His own first watch, from nine to eleven, was the easiest and therefore the hardest. Easy, because the others slept, packed together in the trench like spoons in a drawer, breathing slow, and the windwall held, and the temperature bottomed out around minus twenty-eight and mercifully stabilized. Hard, because there was nothing to do but think, and thinking at altitude in the cold is a dangerous occupation. The mind, deprived of stimulation, starts manufacturing catastrophes, and each one is plausible, and you cannot check any of them. Was the windwall leaning? Was that creak in the fly the fabric or the poles? Was Dev's breathing too shallow? Was Renny's fever rising? He got up eleven times to check things he had checked ten minutes before, and each time the answer was the same, and each time the checking was not really about the answer.

At some point in that first watch, Ilse surfaced from sleep—not awake, exactly, but near the surface, the way people float near the surface of cold water—and saw him sitting upright against the packs, facing outward, watching the sky. She didn't speak. She just watched him for a while, the way he'd watched the valley, and then she reached out from her bag and put her bare hand briefly on his forearm, two seconds, three, and withdrew it back into the warmth. She didn't say anything and neither did he. There was nothing to say that the gesture hadn't said better. On a ridge at six thousand meters, in the worst cold either of them had ever known, with a fugitive's satchel between them and a hunter's name behind them, she had touched him deliberately, knowing exactly what it meant, and he had let her.

Mara took the middle watches without incident, which was its own kind of miracle. At one point she woke him—not for his shift, just to report: "Wind's backing around to the northwest. Storm's going to miss us, or mostly. Maybe graze us on the descent." She'd read the sky the way he read faces, and her reading was better. He grunted acknowledgment and lay back down and, for the first time since Kettle Ford, slept like a stone for ninety minutes.

Pell's watch produced the night's only crisis, and it was small, and small crises at altitude are how big ones practice. Around four in the morning, tending the stove, Pell let the flame gutter—his gloved fingers clumsy on the valve, his attention drifting toward the horizon where the sky was beginning, imperceptibly, to bruise with the first suggestion of dawn—and the stove went out. Relighting a stove at minus twenty-eight, with fuel chilled to syrup, at six thousand meters where the air itself barely supports combustion, is a ten-minute fight involving preheating cups and patience and profanity. Renny, awake anyway, feverish, unable to sleep properly, talked Pell through it from inside his cocoon of bags, voice only, sight unseen, cook to apprentice across three feet of frozen snow. It took fourteen minutes. When the flame caught, the little yellow bloom of it in the darkness drew a sound out of the trench that August, half-waking, heard and understood completely: relief. Not joy. Relief. The sound of people who had learned, over six days of hunger and cold and fear, exactly how much a flame is worth.

* * *

The last hour before dawn was the coldest, and August took it alone, as he'd promised himself he would.

Minus thirty-one. The thermometer's brass casing was so cold it burned his fingers through his liner gloves when he pulled it from his pocket to check it, and he had to hold it in his armpit afterward for a minute to make his hand forget. The stars had wheeled halfway around the pole since evening. The eastern sky was still dark, but the darkness had changed texture—from black to a deep, dense blue, the color of deep water, the color the sky turns when the sun is still below the horizon but has begun, secretly, to work.

He sat facing the valley and did the thing he had come up here, in some buried part of himself, to do.

Below him, materializing out of the blue-dark degree by degree, the far valley resolved. First the ridgelines, black against blacker. Then the snowfields, gaining a faint luminosity, like paper held up to a lamp. Then, as the light climbed, the valley floor itself: the river, the moraine, the scattered dark points that were trees, and finally—so small he had to squint, so small he might have imagined it—a structure. Grey-roofed. Square. The ranger station.

Twelve hours. Less, maybe, if the glacier was kind. More, if it wasn't. With Dev on a litter, realistically: fourteen. Fourteen hours of down-climbing, traversing, lowering, hauling, crossing avalanche paths in the fresh load the storm had left, and every one of those hours would be paid for out of a bank account that was already overdrawn—half-rations for two days, one night at minus thirty, legs that had carried a man up an unclimbed shoulder. He ran the numbers the way he ran them on every route, and the numbers said: possible. Barely. If nothing went wrong.

Something would go wrong. Something always went wrong. That was not pessimism; it was planning. The question was never whether the mountain would test them. The question was what they would have left when it did.

He thought about Vask. Down there, somewhere below them on the western approaches or circling toward the moraine, Soren Vask was doing his own arithmetic, and his numbers were worse—he'd lost men, lost morale, lost the moral high ground he'd never actually held—and August found, examining himself honestly in the pre-dawn cold, that he did not hate Vask anymore. He had hated him for eleven years, had built a life on the hatred the way you build a house on a foundation, and somewhere on the shoulder, in the wind, carrying Dev, the foundation had quietly failed. What was left wasn't forgiveness. Vask had ordered a summit push that killed three men and had spent a decade buying silence about it. That was not forgivable, and August was not offering forgiveness. What was left was simpler and colder: Vask was a problem with a location, and the location was downhill, and the solution was to get to the valley first and put the documents in the hands of people who could end him. Hatred was fuel, and fuel was heavy, and he had stopped carrying things he didn't need.

Behind him, in the trench, the others began to stir. He heard it before he saw it—the zippers, the small groans of joints that had stiffened in the cold, Renny's cough, the rustle of bags being beaten flat and rolled. Ilse emerged first, hair frozen into a shape that would have been funny anywhere else, cheeks wind-burned to a color between red and purple, and she looked at him sitting there facing the dawn and she didn't ask what he was looking at. She sat down beside him in the snow, pulled her bag around her shoulders like a cloak, and looked too.

"It's beautiful," she said.

"It is," August said. And then, because the honesty they'd built on this mountain deserved maintenance: "And it doesn't care that we're here. Both things are true. Hold onto both."

She nodded slowly, and he watched her do it—watched her hold both truths at once, the beauty and the indifference, the triumph and the peril, the fact that they had climbed higher than anyone had ever climbed on this face and the fact that the climb meant nothing to anything except them. She was learning the mountain's grammar. Or maybe she'd always known it and had simply never been given a place to speak it.

Mara called the crew together with three clicks of her carabiner, the signal they'd developed on the shoulder, and August laid out the descent plan in the flat, complete voice he used for briefings: the upper glacier first, roped tight, moving one at a time past the seracs while the cold still held the snow bridges firm—their one advantage, and it would evaporate with the sun. Then the traverse under the icefall, fast, spaced, no stopping. Then the moraine, where the danger changed from ice to ankle, and the pace would slow and the real test would begin: fourteen hours of walking with a stretcher.

"Questions?" he asked.

"One," Dev said, from the litter, his voice thinned by pain and altitude but level. "When we get down. When I'm in the helicopter and you're standing there watching it leave—" he stopped, gathered himself, started again. "Don't let them bury the documents in procedure. Ilse has the originals. Make sure they get read. Not filed. Read."

"They'll be read," Ilse said. "I'll read them to them myself if I have to."

"And one more question," Renny said, raising his good hand like a schoolboy, and there was something in his face that August hadn't seen before, some settled thing, some peace that had arrived sometime in the night without announcement. "When this is over. When we're all in the valley and the lawyers have the papers and the helicopter's gone. Are we still a crew? Or does the mountain dissolve us?"

August looked at him, and at Mara, and at Pell, and at Dev grey-faced in the litter, and at Ilse with the satchel strap already looped over her shoulder, and he found that the answer was already in his mouth, had been forming since the tree line, since the ford, since the letter he'd burned in a stove in Kessik.

"The mountain doesn't dissolve anything," he said. "People dissolve crews. We won't."

The sun cleared the eastern ridge at 7:12, and the whole face of the mountain went from blue to gold in the space of a breath, and far below them, catching the light like a signal mirror, the river in the valley flashed once—twice—three times, as if answering.

August clipped into the first anchor, looked down the upper glacier one last time, and gave the order.

"Rope team one. Move."
