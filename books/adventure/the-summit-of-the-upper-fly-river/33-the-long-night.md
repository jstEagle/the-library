## 33. The Long Night

### Ilse

The sun left them at 4:40 in the afternoon, and by six the cold had stopped being weather and become a presence—a thing with weight and intent that pressed against the tent fabric like a hand testing a door.

They had dug in where the ridge widened into a shelf no wider than a tennis court, August directing the work in short sentences while their breath hung around them like smoke from a fire that wasn't there. Two tents, both pitched half-buried in the snow wall they cut with shovels and ice axes and, finally, hands. Renny's wrist was useless. Dev's leg was splinted with a ski pole and two tent stakes, his face gray as the glacier below, and every hour they had to shift him to keep the cold from taking the tissue of his backside and heels, which meant hauling him through a tent door built for one.

Ilse learned the arithmetic of it quickly. Body heat against minus thirty. Fuel for exactly three melting sessions—enough water to keep them alive, not enough to warm anyone. Food: four days at half rations, now stretched to five because tomorrow would cost everything they had. And night: fourteen hours of it, maybe fifteen, before the light came back over the eastern ridge.

She sat wedged between Mara and the tent wall, Dev's splinted leg across her lap because it was the only place it fit, and she understood that this was what her father had never once given her—not comfort, but purpose. Every small act tonight mattered. Tucking the sleeping bag edge under Dev's hip. Rotating the fuel canister so the cold wouldn't thicken it. Passing the thermos lid in strict rotation, each person drinking exactly their share, no more, watching everyone else watch you do it.

"Talk," Mara said at some point. It might have been eight o'clock; time had gone soft. "Somebody talk or I'm going to start hearing things."

"Like what?" Renny asked from the other tent—he'd shout through the wall every few minutes, a habit he'd invented an hour ago and defended fiercely when Mara told him to stop.

"Voices. My mother, probably. She'd have opinions about me sleeping on a mountain."

"What kind of opinions?"

"The kind mothers have. *You didn't wear your hat.*"

Ilse laughed—a real laugh, surprising her, coming out of her like something uncorked—and felt the whole tent relax by a degree she could almost measure.

"My father kept files," she said, before she could decide not to say it. The words came out into the dark and she let them go. "On people. Guides, mostly. Anyone who'd worked for him or against him. I found them when I was sixteen. That's how I knew about August before I ever met him."

"And you still came," Mara said.

"I wanted to see if the file was true." She smoothed the sleeping bag over Dev's shin, feeling the fragile heat of him through the nylon. "It wasn't. Files never are. They're just the shape a person leaves in someone else's anger."

Dev stirred. His eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding her face as though from a great distance.

"That's good," he said quietly. "That's very good, Ilse."

Then his eyes closed again, and the cold pressed its hand against the walls, and the night went on.

* * *

### Mara

Mara counted breaths.

It was a trick her first mountaineering instructor had taught her, back when she was nineteen and broke and hungry for the high country the way some people are hungry for anything at all: when the cold gets inside your thinking, count. In for four, hold for seven, out for eight. The numbers gave the mind a rail to run on, and as long as the mind ran on rails it wouldn't wander off into the white silence outside and start believing the wind was calling your name.

She counted. Around her, the tent breathed in its own rhythm—August's slow and even, Ilse's quicker, Dev's shallow and wrong, wrong in a way she refused to name yet.

"You're doing the breathing thing," August said. He wasn't asleep either. She hadn't expected him to be.

"How can you tell?"

"Your shoulders move in fours."

A pause. Outside, the wind found a new register, a low moan that rose and fell and rose again, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"I signed on because of you," she said. The words came out flat, stripped of drama by the altitude and the dark. "After the Fly River inquiry. When your name went through the association like a disease. Clients pulled out. Permits got harder. Everyone who'd ever shared a rope with you became a question mark. I lost two seasons of work and my best partner, and I told myself I hated you for it."

"Do you?"

"I did." She shifted against the wall, feeling the cold come through the foam pad, patient as interest accruing. "And then I watched you at Kettle Ford, and I watched you read the mountain at the col, and I realized I wasn't angry at you. I was angry that the thing they said about you was the only story anyone had. Including me."

August was quiet long enough that she thought he'd gone under. Then:

"Tomas used to say a reputation is just a rumor with a filing system."

Mara smiled in the dark, where no one could see it and it cost nothing. "He sounds like he was right a lot."

"He was right about everything except the summit push." A breath. "I've had twenty years to decide whether I should have fought him harder that day. I still don't know. That's the part nobody tells you—that you don't get answers. You just get older, and the question gets quieter."

The wind screamed down off the crest and the tent poles flexed and held, flexed and held, and Mara thought: *this is what we are tonight. Poles in a storm. Worthless alone.*

In the corner, Dev coughed—a wet, distant sound—and stopped counting breaths, and started counting hours instead.

* * *

### Dev

Dev floated somewhere above his own body, and from up there he could see the whole strange architecture of his life.

He saw Lagos, and his mother's hands kneading dough at five in the morning because the bakery ovens had to be hot by six. He saw the recruitment office in Nairobi, the smooth man from Brandt's security contractor explaining that a porter's job was simple: walk, carry, observe, report. Good pay. Clean references—they'd build them for him, brick by brick, until his paper self was spotless. He'd laughed then. He'd thought he was the clever one, getting paid twice for one walk.

Now he lay in a tent at six thousand meters with a shattered leg wrapped in a sleeping bag, and the cleverness had burned away like fog in sun, and what remained was simpler and heavier: these people had carried him. Literally. Up a face that should have killed all of them, in shifts, Renny hauling hardest despite his wrist, cursing cheerfully the entire way, and Dev had lain in the litter looking up at the sky going past and understood that he loved them, and that love was not a thing you could report to a beacon.

"Ilse," he said. His voice came out thin.

"I'm here." Her hand found his shoulder.

"When I was a boy—" He had to stop. His lips were stiff. "My mother baked bread before dawn. Every day. Nobody thanked her. Nobody saw her. She did it anyway, because the bread needed making." He swallowed. "I think I understand her now. You carry what needs carrying. That's the whole job. That's—" A breath. "That's the whole life, maybe."

"Sleep, Dev."

"I'm afraid of the sleep."

"I know." Her hand tightened. "But I'll be here when you wake. I promise you that. And August is right there, and Mara, and Renny is shouting through the wall whether we want him to or not."

From the next tent, faintly, as if summoned: "*Are you talking about me?*"

"Yes!" Ilse called back.

"*Good.*"

Dev felt himself smiling, and the smile hurt his cracked lips, and he did it anyway. Then the sleep took him after all—the bad kind, gray and far away—and he dreamed of ovens, and warmth, and his mother singing in a language the mountain had never heard.

* * *

### Ilse

At midnight, by Mara's watch, they did the second melt.

It was a military operation conducted by exhausted people. August crawled out into the wind—it hit him like a door thrown open, and Ilse heard him grunt with the sheer physical insult of it—and fed the stove while Mara held a windbreak of packs around him. Water boiled in sips. Each person drank their measured share. Dev drank his slowly, Ilse holding the cup, tipping it against his lips, watching his throat work.

His skin, when her fingers brushed his cheek, was cold in a way that frightened her. Not cold like hers. Cold like stone.

"Tell us a story," Renny shouted through the wall. "A proper one. Ilse, you're rich, tell us about galas."

"There were no galas. There were board dinners where everyone pretended the fish was interesting."

"Go on."

"My father once gave a forty-minute toast about the history of the company that included, verbatim, the sentence 'and that, ladies and gentlemen, is why geology matters.' A woman cried. I've never been sure whether from emotion or despair."

Renny's laughter came through the canvas, ragged and delighted. Even August made a sound that in lesser men would have been a chuckle.

"Your turn, boss," Renny said. "Story. Go."

Silence, except the wind. Then August's voice, low, coming through the fabric like something surfacing from deep water.

"First mountain I ever climbed, I was eleven. Behind my grandfather's farm. It wasn't a real mountain—four hundred meters of scree and sheep. But I stood on top and the valley looked small enough to hold in one hand, and I remember thinking: *I could keep this. I could keep this feeling forever.*" A pause. "I spent thirty years trying. Turns out you can't keep it. You can only visit. And every visit costs."

"What did it cost you?" Ilse asked softly.

Another silence, longer. When he answered, she heard that he had chosen the answer carefully, the way he chose footholds.

"Three friends. A name. Twenty years." The stove hissed and died; the melt was done. "And it gave me this ridge, tonight, with five people I'd trust with the last of my rope. So the ledger's not settled. Maybe it never is. Maybe that's what a life is—an account you keep working on."

Nobody spoke after that. There was nothing to add to it, and they all knew it, and the knowing itself was a kind of warmth.

* * *

### Mara

By three in the morning Dev was mumbling.

Not words, mostly—fragments, names, a phrase in another language that sounded like a prayer or a recipe. His temperature was the problem. Mara had seen hypothermia before, had treated it, had once been halfway into it herself on a winter traverse in the Tetons, and she knew the spectrum: the shivering that stops being a good sign, the clumsiness, the mumbling, the terrible calm. He was moving down that spectrum, and they had no way to stop him except each other.

So they made a machine out of themselves.

August reorganized the tents without asking permission—there was no permission left to ask, only decisions. Dev went into the center of one tent, packed between Ilse and Mara, skin to skin through base layers, their combined heat funneled into him like water into a channel. August and Renny took the other tent, Renny protesting that he should be in with Dev because he ran hot, August overriding him with two words: *your wrist.* The tents' remaining gear—ropes, the empty pack frames, every scrap of insulation—went into windbreaks and floor layers.

And then they waited, and held him, and talked.

Mara told them about her sister in Vancouver she hadn't called in two years, and why, and how the reason seemed very small now, smaller than a grain of rice, and she promised the dark interior of the tent that she would call. Ilse described the sea—she'd grown up far from it, she said, and had seen it first at nineteen, and had stood on the beach and cried without understanding why, and Mara thought: *of course you cried. Some things are too big to meet without grief for the years you lost not meeting them.*

Dev surfaced once, briefly, lucid as glass.

"Is Vask behind us?" he asked.

"Gone or dead," August said from the wall. "Doesn't matter. We're ahead of him."

"Good." Dev's eyes closed. "Don't let him have the documents. Don't let him have—" His hand moved, searching, and Ilse took it. "Her. Don't let him have her."

"No one's having anyone," Mara murmured. "We're all just having a very bad night together."

The ghost of a laugh moved through him. Then he sank again, and the wind rose to fill the space he left, and the hours ground forward like a glacier, slow and irresistible and indifferent to what they carried.

* * *

### Ilse

Near the end, in the last black hour before the sky remembered itself, Ilse held Dev's head in her lap and told him about the future.

She didn't plan to. The words simply arrived, the way the best ones do, unbidden and necessary.

"There's a valley below us," she said, her lips near his ear, her voice pitched under the wind. "I've never seen it, but August has, and tomorrow we'll be in it. There are trees down there. Real trees, tall ones. And a river that isn't gray like the glacier water—it's green, he says, green like bottle glass. And a ranger station with a stove and a radio, and a helicopter will come for you, Dev, a big loud orange helicopter, and the pilot will complain about the weight of you because pilots always complain, and then you'll sleep in a real bed for a week. Maybe two weeks. With pillows. More pillows than any person needs."

His breathing was shallow but steady. Listening, or dreaming, or both.

"And I'm going to open a clinic," she went on, and discovered as she said it that it was true, that it had been true for days, growing in her like a seed in snow. "Small. Mountain town. People who climb stupid mountains and need stitching. I'll be terrible about paperwork and wonderful about everything else. And when you're walking again—if you want, if you're willing—you can carry supplies up to me. Honest cargo. Nothing sealed. Nothing coded. Just flour and bandages and letters."

The wind dropped for a moment—one of those sudden holes in the storm, a held breath—and in the silence Ilse heard, distinctly, Dev whisper:

"Letters."

"Yes."

"Good ones?"

"The best ones. Long ones. Terribly written."

His mouth curved. His hand squeezed hers, weakly, but he squeezed it. And Ilse bowed her head over him in the freezing dark of a ledge at the top of the world and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like warmth, that this was the answer to the question she'd been carrying since her father's office, since the ledger, since the trailhead: *what do people owe each other?*

This. Exactly this. To stay. To hold. To speak into the dark on behalf of someone who cannot yet speak for himself, and to mean every word.

Outside, the east was still black. But somewhere below the horizon, behind the ridgeline, behind the world, the sun was already climbing toward them, and the first pale suggestion of it touched the highest clouds like a hand reaching up a stairwell.

Mara's watch said 5:51.

"Almost dawn," Ilse whispered—to Dev, to Mara, to herself, to all of them.

And far off down the southern flank, where yesterday there had been nothing, a pinpoint of light blinked once, twice—steady, deliberate, unmistakable.

Someone was signaling from the glacier below.
