## 34. Dawn and Descent

The cold woke him before the light did.

August came up out of a shallow sleep with his breath frozen in the wool of his collar and his hands aching inside two pairs of gloves, and for a moment he did not know where he was—only that the world was hard and angled and singing with wind. Then memory assembled itself: the ridge, the dug-in snow trench, the six of them packed together like spoons in a drawer, and Dev's leg splinted with tent poles and bound in rope, Dev himself half-buried in the middle of the pile because warmth was the only medicine they had left to give him.

The sky was doing something at the eastern edge of the world.

It began as a bruise—a deep violet seam along the black teeth of the far range—and then it widened, and warmed, and the first light came up the valley below them like water filling a basin. August watched it happen from the mouth of their snow cave, too stiff and too careful to move quickly, and what the light revealed was so clean and so vast that for several seconds he simply forgot to breathe.

Below them, the cloud deck they had climbed above yesterday had broken apart in the night. The far valley lay open, terraced with shadow, the glacier running down its center like a river of dirty glass. The descent they faced was all of it visible at once: three thousand vertical feet of icefall, moraine, and scree, every hazard laid bare by dawn as if the mountain were showing its hand and daring them to play.

"August." Mara's voice, low, behind him. "How is he?"

He turned. Dev was awake. That was the first mercy of the day—that he was awake at all, after a night when his breathing had gone thin and irregular and Ilse had twice pressed her ear to his chest and frowned. His face was gray under the frost in his stubble, his lips cracked, but his eyes found August's and held them with an effort that was almost physical.

"I'm here," Dev said. It came out as a whisper. "In case anyone was counting."

"We're counting," August said. He crawled back into the trench and knelt beside him. The splinted leg was the problem. In the night the swelling had spread past the binding, and the toes of Dev's boot had gone pale, waxy. August worked the laces loose with numb fingers, eased the boot open, and was relieved—genuinely, deeply relieved—to see color come back into the skin, slow and ugly but real. Circulation. As long as there was circulation, there was a leg worth saving.

"Can you tell me your name," Ilse said, coming around on Dev's other side, "and where we are?"

Dev managed something like a smile. "Dev Okafor. And you're all insane, and we're on top of the world." He coughed, winced. "Don't ask me to stand."

"No one's asking you to stand," August said. "We're asking you to be carried. There's a difference, and it's mostly dignity."

Renny snorted from the back of the trench, where he sat cradling his infected wrist against his chest. Even now, even gray-faced himself, Renny could not let a line like that go unanswered. "Dignity," he rasped, "left this expedition somewhere around Kettle Ford."

August looked at them—all of them, wrecked and frostbitten and half-starved, packed into a hole scratched out of a ridge at five thousand meters—and felt something settle in his chest that was not quite hope and not quite fear but some third thing made of both. Yesterday they had crested the shoulder of the Upper Fly with nothing in reserve. Today the mountain would try to take it back.

"All right," he said. "Listen. The descent is the whole day, maybe more. We go down the east flank—the glissade chute first, then the ice steps, then the moraine. I lead. Mara takes rear anchor. Ilse stays within arm's reach of Dev at all times; if his color changes or he stops answering clearly, we stop, no matter where we are. Renny—" he met the cook's eyes— "you carry the stove and the last of the fuel. If we have to halt, hot water is worth more than food."

"And the documents?" Dev asked quietly.

August glanced at the satchel, still strapped across Dev's chest, where it had been since the cornice. The man had shattered his leg rather than let it go, and he had not once suggested letting it go since.

"They stay with you," August said. "You earned that."

* * *

They broke camp as the sun cleared the ridge, and the cold it brought with it was the honest kind—brutal, but dry, and without wind. That was the second mercy. A wind on a slope like this one would have ended the conversation before it started.

The glissade chute was exactly what August remembered from his reconnaissance years ago: a broad gully of compacted snow running four hundred feet down the east flank, steep enough to move a body fast, shallow enough in its fall line to spit you out onto a bench of rock instead of over a cliff. He went down it first, alone, boots-first, axe buried to the head at his hip, and rode it out in a long hissing rush that filled his hood with powder and set his heart hammering in a way that was half terror and half something older and more shameful—joy, actual joy, the body remembering what it was built for.

From the bottom he looked up and shouted the line: *center-left, ride it long, don't drag your heels.*

Mara came next, controlled and economical, spraying a rooster tail of snow. Then Renny, whooping despite himself, despite everything, a sound that echoed off the flanks and made August grin inside his frozen hood. Then Ilse, with Dev strapped to her back in the rope litter they had built through the night—tent poles, pack frame, webbing, every knot August knew how to tie deployed at once. She came down the chute slower than the others, dragging her axe like a rudder, and when she reached the bench she was shaking, and laughing, and there was blood on her lip where the ice had caught her chin, and she did not wipe it away.

"How is he?" August asked.

"Complaining," Dev said, from beneath her. "Which I'm told is a good sign."

"It's the best sign there is," Mara said, arriving last. "A quiet patient on a mountain is a dead patient."

They ate nothing—they had nothing worth the effort of stopping for—but drank melted snow from the thermos while August studied the route below. The ice steps came next: a staircase of serac debris and blue glacial ice, forty feet of it, requiring anchors and patience. Beyond that, the long lateral moraine, and beyond *that*, the valley floor, still impossibly distant, still a day's work at least with a man on a litter.

He was studying it when Mara touched his arm and pointed.

Far below—far, and off to the south, on the parallel route Vask's team had been working since the col—there was movement. Small figures, strung out along a snowfield, moving with the peculiar slow stagger of exhausted men. Even at this distance the picture read clearly: they were descending too, but badly. One figure had stopped and was not moving at all. Two others stood near it, gesturing. The rest of the string pulled ahead, spacing widening, the discipline of the column dissolving into something looser with every hundred yards.

"They're breaking apart," Mara said.

August put up his binoculars—the one piece of weight he had refused to cut, and had been right to refuse. Through the lenses the scene sharpened. The stopped figure was sitting now, pack off, and nobody was helping him put it back on. Farther down, two men had left the route entirely and were cutting straight for the valley floor, abandoning the switchbacks, taking a line that August could see was wrong—too direct, into steeper ground—and did not care.

"Count them," he said.

Mara counted. "Six. Maybe seven. They started with nine."

Desertion, injury, attrition. Pride climbing a mountain it had no business climbing, and the mountain collecting its fees. August lowered the glasses and felt no triumph. What he felt was colder and more useful: arithmetic. Vask's team was failing, which meant Vask himself was desperate, which meant whatever remained of him down there was more dangerous than ever—not less. A man with a full crew calculates. A man alone does not.

"He'll be ahead of them," August said. "Vask doesn't wait. He'll be at the moraine, or past it, and he'll know we're coming down this side now. There's nowhere else for us to go."

"So we don't stop," Mara said.

"We don't stop."

* * *

The ice steps took two hours.

August led them on, placing screws in the blue ice with hands he had to beat warm between placements, building a rappel line they could all descend on while he free-climbed alongside to retrieve the gear. The sun climbed and the ice began to weep, water threading down the surfaces, and by the time it was Ilse and Dev's turn the placements were soft and every anchor had to be tested twice.

She descended well. That was the thing about Ilse Brandt that kept surprising him—not bravado, not toughness exactly, but a kind of exact attention. She listened to the mountain the way she had learned to listen to boardrooms, cataloguing threat and opportunity, and she did not waste motion. She came down the steps with Dev on her back, placed each crampon deliberately, and when her boot punched through a rotten lip near the bottom and she swung out over the drop with a cry, she did not panic—she locked her axe, held, breathed, and climbed back onto the line before August, twenty feet below on the retrieval climb, could even shout instruction.

"You're all right," he called up.

"I'm all right," she called back, and her voice only shook a little.

At the base of the steps they halted for ten minutes—the only halt of the morning—to re-lash the litter and check Dev. His pulse was fast but regular. His speech was clear. But when August peeled back the edge of the sleeping bag wrapped around him, he saw the man's face had gone from gray toward white, and that frightened him more than the gray had.

"Talk to me," August said.

"I'm fine."

"Wrong answer. Try again."

Dev looked at him for a long moment, and then, quietly, honestly: "I'm cold, August. Down deep. The kind that doesn't leave."

Ilse heard it. August saw her hear it. She crouched, took Dev's face in both mittened hands, and made him look at her. "Then we're going to get you somewhere with walls," she said, "and a stove, and someone whose job it is to argue with me about your treatment. Hold on to that. Walls, stove, argument. Say it back."

"Walls, stove, argument," Dev whispered, and almost smiled.

They moved.

* * *

The moraine ate the afternoon.

It was brutal going even unburdened—house-sized blocks of rock heaved up by the glacier's crawl, gullies of loose stone that ran like water underfoot, a maze with no marked path where every choice cost either elevation or distance. With a litter it was worse than brutal. They formed a rotation: August and Mara carrying the front poles, Ilse and Renny the rear, trading off every twenty minutes because twenty minutes was all any pair could manage before their arms turned to rope. Dev bore it silently, which was its own kind of terrible, gripping the satchel to his chest through every jolt, and once—only once—when they dropped him over a boulder step and his broken leg swung with the motion, they heard him make a sound none of them had heard a human being make before, and then apologize for it.

"Don't you dare apologize," Renny said fiercely, mopping the man's forehead with snow-water. "Scream the whole way down if you need to. It's free."

Twice they saw Vask's people again, farther down now, closer to the valley floor, and both times the picture was worse. The column had become individuals. On the lower snowfields two figures were separated from the rest by nearly a kilometer, moving at different speeds, in different directions. One of the distant shapes appeared to be dragging a sled or a pack behind him in a straight line, ignoring the terrain entirely, the way men moved when they had stopped making decisions and started merely obeying the last order they'd been given.

"They're done," Mara said, watching through the binoculars during a carry change. "Whatever Vask spent on those men, he spent it all getting them up. There's nothing left for the way down."

"Vask isn't with them," August said.

She lowered the glasses. "You're sure?"

"I've been looking. He's not in the column. He's not behind it." August scanned the valley floor, the terminal moraine, the dark line of forest where the ranger station sat at the mouth of everything. "He's ahead. He's been ahead since the storm. He didn't bring nine men over that shoulder to escort them home—he brought them to spend them. Bodies against the mountain, buying him a clear run to the bottom."

Renny spat. "And what does a man do at the bottom with a clear run?"

August shouldered his end of the litter. What a man did, he thought, was wait—with a radio, and a rifle, and nothing left to lose except the thing he'd crossed a mountain to destroy.

"He waits for us," August said. "So we give him tired legs and a bad temper and nothing else. Move."

* * *

They came off the moraine as the light went long and gold, and the character of the world changed underneath them. Rock gave way to scree, scree to alpine turf, turf to the first stunted pines—and with the trees came a smell August had not known he'd been starving for, resin and soil and living green, the smell of the low country, of survivable altitudes. Behind them the Shoulder of the Upper Fly caught the sunset and burned orange and violet, immense and indifferent, already becoming memory.

Dev was conscious but barely. The litter carried easier here, and they made better time, trotting the gentle grades, walking the rough ones, and it was during one of these stretches—August at the front poles, sweat freezing in his collar, the station perhaps two hours out—that Ilse, walking beside him with her hand never leaving the litter rail, spoke without looking up.

"He's getting colder again."

"I know."

"When we reach the station—"

"The station has a radio," August said. "The radio brings a helicopter. The helicopter brings heat, and plasma, and surgeons who've never heard of either of us. That's the plan. That's the whole plan. Everything else is just the road to it."

She nodded. Then, quieter: "And Vask?"

August looked down the valley. The light was dying out of it in long blue bands, and somewhere down there, between them and the station, a man was waiting with a gun and a grievance and a history that August had spent eleven years trying to outrun.

"He gets his answer tonight," August said. "One way or another."

Ahead, through the first real trees, a light flickered—the ranger station window, small and yellow and absurdly domestic, like a coal dropped in the wilderness. Dev stirred in the litter and murmured something, and Ilse bent close to hear it, and whatever it was made her grip the rail tighter.

"What did he say?" August asked.

Ilse lifted her head, and in the last of the light her face was pale and set.

"He said," she told him, "that there are men on the trail ahead of us. Three of them. Moving toward the station."
