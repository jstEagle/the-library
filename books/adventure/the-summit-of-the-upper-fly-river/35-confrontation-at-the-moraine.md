## 35. Confrontation at the Moraine

Soren Vask had not slept in three days, and the mountain had stopped pretending to be on his side.

He stood alone at the terminal moraine where the Upper Fly's glacier gave up its dead—rock and grit and shattered ice deposited over centuries like a ledger of everything the valley had taken. Below him, maybe four hundred meters of broken ground, the river ran brown with rock flour, loud enough to swallow a voice. Above him, the shoulder they had climbed loomed into cloud, its ridgeline still holding last night's snow, white as an unpaid debt.

His men were gone. Halloran first, two nights ago, walking out without a word while Vask slept. Then Petrov, who left his share of the rope coiled neatly at the edge of camp like an apology no one had asked for. The last two—Kessler and the boy, Anselm—had lasted until dawn this morning, until Vask caught them packing and said nothing at all, only watched them go, because he understood by then that a man who has to order loyalty never had it.

*Four men,* he thought. *I paid four men to follow me onto a glacier, and the glacier asked them one question each, and they all answered honestly.*

That was the thing about the high country that Vask had spent thirty years refusing to learn: it conducted interviews. It didn't care about money.

He checked the pistol again. A short-barreled thing, bought in Kessik from a man who hadn't looked him in the eye, warm now against his palm where his hand had been wrapped around it for hours. He told himself it was leverage. He told himself that when Iversen came down off that ridge—and he would come down, today, because the weather window was closing and Iversen was too good a guide to waste one—he would simply explain the terms one final time. Documents. Satchel. Walk away. Everyone lives.

The lie sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He knew what August Iversen's answer had been on the radio. One word. Vask had replayed it a hundred times in the static-hiss of his memory, and every time it landed the same way—not as defiance, but as arithmetic. *No.* As if the whole transaction, the money, the gun, the thirty years between them, weighed less than a single syllable.

Vask had built his life on the principle that everything weighed something. That every man had a figure. He had watched Henrik Brandt buy senators with figures. He had sold his own signature once—a falsified discharge report, a river monitoring station that existed only on paper—for a number so large he'd had to sit down after signing. And it had worked. For years it had worked. The mine ran, the filings passed, the salmon died quietly in a river nobody sampled twice, and Soren Vask got rich in the manner of men who have decided that conscience is a luxury good.

Then Ilse Brandt had started asking questions in her father's offices. Then the investigators had come sniffing at the far-side ranger districts. And the original documents—the field surveys, the real readings, the ledger in Brandt's own surveyor's hand—had been sitting in a courier's satchel crossing the range on the back of a disgraced guide Vask had once ruined.

He had told himself it was business. Right up until the moment on the radio when August said *no*, and Vask heard, underneath the word, the sound of his own life closing like a door.

* * *

He saw them at midday.

They came down out of the cloud in single file, small against the enormity of the moraine, moving with the slow, deliberate gait of people who had spent a night above eight thousand meters and lived. Vask counted them through the binoculars with a professional's eye and felt something twist in him that was not quite envy and not quite grief.

Iversen first, of course, breaking trail, reading the ground. Behind him the Quist woman, roped to a litter—they'd built a litter, God help them, from tent poles and line, and they were carrying the injured porter down in shifts. Vask could see the rotation even at this distance: two carrying, two flanking, rotating every twenty minutes like clockwork. Renny—that was the cook's name, he'd read the crew manifest a dozen times—rode the litter with his splinted wrist held against his chest, and every time the carriers changed, someone touched his shoulder. Not a signal. Just a touch.

And behind them, unroped but never far from Iversen's elbow, walked Ilse Brandt.

Henrik's daughter. The girl Vask had met at a shareholder dinner five years ago, all sharp silence and watching eyes, whom her father had described as "decorative but harmless." She had crossed the Upper Fly range in the worst season in a decade, helped carry a wounded man off a mountain, and was now, according to the beacon fragments Vask had salvaged before Dev Okafor destroyed it, planning to hand the documents that would destroy her own father directly to federal investigators.

*Harmless.*

Vask lowered the binoculars. His hands were shaking—not cold, though he was cold; he'd been cold for days—but shaking the way hands shake when the body knows something the mind is still negotiating about.

They would reach the moraine within the hour. There was exactly one route down: the ramp of stable scree on the eastern flank, funneled between two walls of old ice, and it emptied onto the terminal moraine fifty meters from where he stood. He had chosen this ground carefully, back when he still had men, back when the plan had been a cordon and a negotiation. Now it was just him and a pistol and the last argument of his life.

He moved up-slope and took position behind a fin of glacial till, where he could watch the ramp and be seen only when he chose to be. He worked the slide of the pistol once, silently. Then he made himself stop touching it, because a man who keeps checking his weapon is a man rehearsing.

*You're not going to use it,* he told himself.

And then, honestly, for the first time in thirty years: *Are you?*

* * *

They saw him before he meant them to.

It was the Quist woman—of course it was; she had spotted his fake rescue patrol from a ridge at two kilometers and ended any hope he'd ever had of subtlety—who stopped mid-stride, head tilting, and said something to Iversen. Iversen didn't even look surprised. He looked tired, and old, and utterly unsurprised, and he kept walking.

That undid something in Vask. All these years he had imagined their next meeting as a collision—Iversen furious, Ivensen broken, Iversen at least *afraid*. Instead the man came down the scree ramp at a steady walk, leading his people past the corpse of the glacier, and when they were forty meters apart he stopped, set his poles against a boulder, and waited.

"Hello, Soren," August Iversen said.

Just that. No venom in it. And Vask, who had rehearsed a dozen openings on the climb down from his abandoned camp, found that every one of them had evaporated, and what came out of him instead was raw and stupid and true:

"You were supposed to be afraid of me."

"I was," Iversen said. "Thirty years ago. On the North Col, when you told us the weather window was open and we both knew it wasn't." He glanced at the sky, at the ridge above, at the litter his people were setting down with practiced care. "I've been afraid of you my whole adult life, Soren. You're what I see when I close my eyes at altitude. But I'm done paying rent on it."

Behind him, the crew had arranged themselves without being told—Quist slightly forward on the flank, the big porter Dev standing despite the ruin of his leg, propped on the litter frame, and Ilse Brandt stepping out from behind Iversen as if she had been waiting her whole life for exactly this geometry.

Vask raised the pistol.

It felt enormous in his hand. It felt like a prop. He leveled it at Iversen's chest and heard his own voice come out flattened, official, the voice he used in boardrooms:

"The satchel. The documents. You put them on the ground, you walk south, and by tonight you're a rich man with no name attached to anything. Last offer, August. I'm not making another."

"No," Iversen said.

The word again. That same flat, weightless syllable. Vask's finger tightened on the guard and he was distantly amazed to find how little effort it took, how easy the machinery of it was, how the entire catastrophe of a human life could pivot on half a centimeter of trigger travel—

"Don't."

Ilse Brandt's voice. She had stepped forward—past Iversen, actually past him, into the line of fire, so that the muzzle now covered her chest instead—and she stood there with her wind-burned face and her frost-cracked lips and looked at Soren Vask the way nobody had looked at him in thirty years: without fear, and without interest in his money.

"You want to know why he won't shoot me?" she said to Iversen, conversationally, horribly calm. "Because he can't. Because if he fires this gun, every version of himself he's been maintaining since 1994 dies at once." She turned to Vask. "Isn't that right?"

"Get behind him," Vask said. His voice cracked on it.

"My father keeps files," Ilse went on, taking another step, and Vask realized with a lurch that she was crying—not sobbing, just leaking, tears freezing at her jawline, and she didn't wipe them. "Did you know that? Everything. Every transaction, every favor, every man he's ever owned. I grew up thinking it was paranoia. It's not paranoia, Mr. Vask. It's inventory." Another step. Thirty meters now. Twenty-five. "There's a file with your name on it. Thick as a Bible. And do you know what's at the front of it? Before the mine, before the money, before any of it?"

"Stop."

"A climbing expedition," she said. "1994. Three clients dead on the North Col of Mount Ferren. An expedition leader who ordered the summit push against a storm warning because a client's brother-in-law was a senator and the senator's goodwill was worth more than three lives." Her voice finally broke, and she let it. "August Iversen took the blame for that. He carried it for thirty years. And the man who gave the order—" she pointed, arm shaking, at the pistol, at the man holding it, "—is standing right there, pointing a gun at both of us, on a mountain, in the snow, exactly like the last time."

The moraine was very quiet. The river roared somewhere below, indifferent. Somewhere upslope, a serac groaned and settled, the glacier clearing its throat.

Vask's arm was trembling. He could feel the muzzle tracing small circles against the gray sky, and he could not make it stop, and he understood suddenly that this was the interview the mountain had been conducting all along, and that he was failing it.

"That's not—" he started.

"It is," Iversen said quietly. "It is, Soren. Say it or don't. But lower the gun, because my people are cold, my porter is dying, and I am so tired of you holding the weather over my head."

* * *

Later—years later, in the version of events Vask would tell almost no one—he would try to reconstruct what happened inside him in those seconds, and he would fail, because it did not happen in language. It happened in the body. It happened as a collapse, structural, like a cornice letting go.

He saw the North Col. He saw Chen and the Delacroix brothers turning back into the white at his shouted order to push on, and he saw—this was the part he had buried deepest, the part the file in Henrik Brandt's safe had never contained—that he had known. Standing in the relative warmth of Camp Two with the barometer falling off the table, he had *known*, and he had sent them up anyway, and when the news came down he had performed grief flawlessly at the memorial service while privately, in some sealed cellar of himself, calculating that the senator's mining concessions were still worth more than three cremations.

He had let August Iversen hang for it. Had watched the inquiry gut the young guide's reputation, had signed the letter withdrawing sponsorship, had attended the blacklisting hearings and said nothing, because a silent witness was a controllable witness, and August Iversen's ruin had been, conveniently, load-bearing for Vask's own ascent.

Thirty years. He had built a tower on it. And Ilse Brandt, twenty-six years old, frostbitten, crying frozen tears on a moraine, had just pulled out the keystone with one sentence.

The pistol dropped to his side.

Not lowered—dropped, his arm simply ceasing to hold it up, the way a man's arm stops working when the heart misfires. He heard it clack against a stone. He did not pick it up. He found that he was on his knees in the scree, though he had no memory of kneeling, and that the sound coming out of him was not words. It was older than words. It was the sound a man makes when the invoice arrives for a debt he'd convinced himself had been forgiven, written off, lost in filing—and the amount due is everything.

He was aware, distantly, of movement. Of Iversen coming toward him—not fast, unhurried, the way you approach a spooked animal—and of the Quist woman quietly relieving the pistol from the scree and clearing it with brisk, unceremonious competence. Of Ilse Brandt not retreating but standing her ground, arms wrapped around herself against the cold, watching him kneel in the wreckage of his own account book.

"Three climbers," Vask heard himself say. His voice sounded borrowed. "Chen Wei. Marc and Antoine Delacroix. I can still—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye socket. "I sign things, August. That's what I do. Contracts, filings, orders. I sign, and other people carry them up the mountain. And every one of those signatures was supposed to be worth the paper. And theirs weren't. Theirs weren't worth anything at all, and I knew it, and I signed anyway."

"Yes," Iversen said. "You did."

No comfort in it. No cruelty either. Just the record, entered at last, witnessed.

"They'll take everything," Vask said. "Brandt will bury me before the investigators get within a hundred miles. You know what he does to—"

"He can't," Ilse said. "Not anymore." She held up the satchel—when had she picked it up?—and patted it once, almost gently. "Copies exist now, Mr. Vask. In three places you'll never find. Your signature, my father's, the whole architecture. It's already out of our hands. All of ours." Something moved across her face that was not quite mercy and not quite satisfaction—something more tired than either. "The only thing left to decide is what you are when they come for you. The man who shot four people on a glacier to protect a lie. Or the man who testified."

Vask knelt in the scree at the foot of the glacier and understood that she had given him the only exit that had ever existed, and that it led through the one country he had spent his whole life avoiding: the truth, spoken aloud, in daylight, under his own name.

Above them, the cloud on the shoulder began, very slowly, to tear.

* * *

He rose eventually. Nobody helped him, and nobody needed to; his legs remembered how on their own, the way they had on a thousand summits. The Quist woman handed the cleared pistol to Iversen, who turned it over in his hands for a long moment—Vask watched him weigh it, watched thirty years pass behind his eyes—and then, deliberately, hurled it into the ice-fall, where it vanished between two seracs with a sound like a single note struck and damped.

"For Chen," Iversen said quietly. "For the brothers. We're done paying rent."

They regrouped at the base of the moraine, six people and one broken man, and Vask stood apart while they re-rigged the litter, and it was Dev Okafor—the traitor, the bought man, the porter whose references had been too clean—who finally limped over to him with his shattered leg strapped to a tent-pole splint and regarded him with an expression of terrible, level understanding.

"They'll ask you questions," Dev said. "Investigators. They asked me some, on the radio, before I broke the beacon. You know what I learned?" He shifted his weight, winced. "The truth is heavy, but it packs down smaller than a lie. Carries easier. You'll see."

Vask said nothing. But he looked, for a long time, at the ramp they had descended—at the knife-edge traverses, the fixed lines still fluttering on the upper pitches, the whole impossible vertical mile of the shoulder they had crossed with a wounded man on a litter—and he understood that he had lost something more valuable than the documents, more valuable than the mine, more valuable than the senator's patronage.

He had lost the story he told about himself. And standing there in the wind at the foot of the ice, Soren Vask discovered that beneath the story there was almost nothing left—which meant, perhaps, that there was room to build something truer, if the courts and the years allowed it.

Ilse shouldered the satchel. August Iversen took point, reading the ground south toward the valley mouth, toward the ranger station, toward whatever waited there—radios, investigators, helicopters, reckoning.

"Walk with us," Iversen said, not turning around. It was not forgiveness. It was not even kindness, exactly. It was logistics. "It's a long way down, and no one crosses the Fly alone."

And Soren Vask, who had commanded men across three continents, fell into line at the back of the column like the last man on a rope, and began, step by step, the long descent toward the valley floor—where, beyond the bend of the river, a ranger station's antenna glinted in the clearing sky, and voices that were not theirs were already speaking their names.
