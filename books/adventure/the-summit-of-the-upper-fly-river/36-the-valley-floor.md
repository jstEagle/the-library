## 36. The Valley Floor

Ilse smelled the valley before she saw it.

For eleven days the world had been stone and ice and wind—the clean, merciless smells of altitude, nothing that lived or rotted. Then, as they came down through the last band of stunted spruce, the air thickened. Pine resin. Wet earth. Woodsmoke from somewhere far below, and beneath it all the green rot of the river flats, the slow breathing of a place where things were allowed to grow.

She stopped on the trail and let it fill her lungs until her chest ached.

Behind her, the litter swayed between Renny and Mara, Dev strapped in with the improvised rig they'd built from tent poles and rope. He had been conscious at dawn—barely, his lips gray, answering questions in whispers—but sometime during the descent through the moraine he'd slipped under again, and now he breathed in shallow, rattling pulls that Ilse counted without meaning to, the way she'd once counted her father's footsteps in the corridor outside her office door.

"Two hours," August said beside her. His voice was scraped raw from five days of wind and shouting over it. "Maybe less if the trail holds."

He was looking down-valley, and Ilse followed his gaze.

The ranger station sat at the confluence where the Upper Fly bent north out of the mountains—a cluster of low buildings with sod roofs, a radio mast leaning against the wind like an old man against a gale, a drying rack for fish, three canoes pulled up on the gravel bar. Smoke rose from the main house's chimney. A flag hung limp on its pole. It was, by any honest measure, almost nothing: a handful of buildings at the edge of the largest emptiness in the range.

Ilse felt her knees go soft anyway.

* * *

They came down onto the flats in the long gold light of late afternoon, and the station resolved into details as they approached: a woman splitting wood behind the cookhouse who straightened and stared; a dog that ran out barking and then thought better of it; a man in a ranger's canvas coat stepping off the porch with the deliberate calm of someone who had learned that emergencies in this country rewarded patience.

"You'll be off the Shoulder," the man said. Not a question. His eyes went to the litter, to Dev's gray face, to the six of them—hollowed, frost-burned, walking like people who had forgotten how flat ground worked. "We had your beacon flare two nights back. Half a signal, then nothing."

"It broke," Dev said from the litter, so faintly that Ilse had to lean close to catch it. His eyes opened. "I broke it." And then he laughed, or tried to, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him against the straps.

The ranger's name was Aldous Wren, and he moved the way good men in bad country move—no wasted motion, no wasted words. Inside twenty minutes he had Dev carried into the bunkhouse, a stove lit, a radio crackling, and tea in front of everyone else whether they wanted it or not. The wood-splitting woman turned out to be his wife, Petra, a former flight nurse with hands that found Dev's shattered leg through the blanket with a gentleness that made Ilse look away.

"The weather window is open until tomorrow midday," Wren said, keying the mic. "After that, cloud comes up the valley and we're blind for a week. I can raise the medevac out of Corren Field. They'll want coordinates and a casualty summary."

"You have both," Ilse said.

Wren glanced at her—at the frostbite healing along her cheekbone, at the way she stood, which even after everything still had the architecture of someone raised in boardrooms—and something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or the beginning of it.

"You're Brandt," he said. "The daughter."

"I'm the one holding the radio schedule," Ilse said. "May I?"

* * *

She made the call herself.

It took four attempts through the static, and each time the voice on the other end asked her to repeat, and each time she repeated, her voice growing steadier rather than less. Casualty: one, male, thirty-one, compound fracture left femur, hypothermic, stable but critical. Party of six, ambulatory. Location: Upper Fly ranger station, valley mouth. Request: medevac at first light, and—

Here she paused, her hand flat on the radio housing, feeling the vibration of it like a living thing under her palm.

"—and notify the regional environmental compliance office at Corren. Tell them Ilse Brandt is on site with original survey documentation relating to the Kettle Creek filings. Tell them—" and here her voice did not waver, because she would not let it, "—tell them I am prepared to surrender custody formally, tonight, with chain-of-custody witnessed by Ranger Wren."

Static. Then, faintly, incredulous: *"Say again, last party?"*

"Ilse Brandt," she said. "Daughter of Henrik Brandt. And I said surrender."

* * *

The documents had ridden in the satchel across the whole of the high country—through the col, through the storm, through the chute that had taken their pack horse and half their food, up the shoulder no one had climbed, across the summit ridge where they'd spent a night at minus thirty telling stories to keep Dev alive. The satchel had frozen solid twice. It had been used as a pillow, a splint brace, a windbreak. Its strap had broken on the cornice day and been replaced with bootlace.

Now Ilse sat on a bunk in the corner of the bunkhouse with it open across her knees, and for the first time since Kessik she looked at what was inside without fear.

Survey cores in wax-sealed tubes, labeled in a draftsman's hand. The ledger of readings—Mara had found it in the drums, back when the truth of the job was still a shape in the fog. Original field notebooks, the ones whose numbers had never made it into her father's filings, because the real numbers would have ended the mine before it began. Water tables poisoned on paper into purity. Tailings volumes halved by ink. Eleven years of a mountain being lied about, in a handwriting Ilse recognized because she'd grown up watching it sign her birthday cards.

Her father's handwriting. Of course it was. He had never trusted anyone else with the important lies.

"You should sleep," Mara said from the doorway. She had a mug in each hand and gave one to Ilse without asking. "You haven't slept since the ridge."

"I will. After."

"After what? They're not coming until morning."

"They're coming," Ilse said. "That's the point of after."

Mara studied her for a moment—the appraising look Ilse had learned to read somewhere around the tree line, the guide's habit of weighing a client's competence for what came next. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her. She sat down on the opposite bunk, uninvited, and drank her tea.

"My father kept files on you," Ilse said. "On all of us, I think. On August especially."

"He'd have liked August," Mara said dryly. "Men like your father always like the guides they've ruined. Makes them feel thorough."

"He kept files on me too. I found mine when I found the discrepancies. Psychological profile. *Prone to sentiment; unlikely to act against family interest.*" Ilse turned the page of the ledger in her lap. "I've spent three weeks proving him wrong and it's the best work I've ever done."

Mara laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first Ilse had heard from her since the storm cave. Outside, the light was going copper on the peaks they'd come down from. Somewhere in the bunkhouse's other room, Renny was arguing with Petra Wren about whether his wrist needed a proper splint or whether she was just enjoying the excuse to wrap it, and losing, cheerfully.

"August is on the porch," Mara said, standing. "In case you were going to pretend you didn't know."

"I wasn't going to pretend anything," Ilse said. But she waited until Mara's boots hit the gravel outside before she closed the ledger, and she noticed that her hands were not entirely steady, and she let them be unsteady, alone, for exactly as long as it took to finish the tea.

* * *

The investigators arrived by floatplane at 9:40 the next morning, forty minutes ahead of the medevac helicopter, which told Ilse everything about how the call had landed in Corren.

There were three of them: a compact woman named Osei who introduced herself as senior counsel for the compliance office and shook hands like she meant it; a younger man, Ferro, who carried a document case and said very little; and a third who stayed near the plane and watched the tree line, and whom nobody introduced, and whom Ilse chose not to ask about.

Osei set up at the table in the main house while the helicopter thundered in beyond the gravel bar to take Dev—Petra Wren rode up with him, having declared that no patient of hers flew alone, and Renny stood in the yard and watched the helicopter shrink northward with his splinted wrist pressed to his mouth, and did not stop watching until it was gone.

Then it was time.

Ilse laid everything out on the table in the order she had rehearsed in her head through most of a sleepless night: the ledger, the field notebooks, the cores, the manifest from Vask's outfitting company, the relay beacon Dev had destroyed and surrendered in pieces, and—last, placed deliberately apart—the letter she had written at four in the morning, three pages, signed.

Osei read. Ferro photographed. The unnamed man watched the windows.

"Your father will contest custody," Osei said at last, not unkindly. "He'll claim these were removed unlawfully. That you acted under duress, or worse, that you staged them."

"He can claim whatever he likes. The cores are dated. The notebooks are dated. The handwriting is his." Ilse met her eyes. "I know what I'm handing you, Ms. Osei. I grew up in that house. I'm telling you there is no version of my father that survives these documents, and I am giving them to you freely, on the record, with a witness." She nodded toward Wren, who stood by the stove with his arms folded, radiating the particular patience of a man who had seen every kind of weather arrive at his door.

Osei looked at her for a long moment. Then she took out a form, filled it in, and slid it across the table with a pen.

"Witnessed voluntary surrender of custody," she said. "Sign there. And Ms. Brandt—" she paused, pen hovering, and for the first time her professional composure thinned enough to show the person underneath, "—for what it's worth, in eleven years of this job, no one has ever walked these documents over a mountain to my table. They've mailed them. Leaked them. Sold them. You carried them."

"Somebody had to," Ilse said, and signed.

* * *

Vask came down the valley trail at noon.

She saw him first from the porch—a single figure on the moraine path, moving slowly, without the coordinated purpose she remembered from Kessik. Behind him, the trail was empty. Whatever remained of his team had scattered somewhere above the tree line; Dev had told them as much in his lucid hour before dawn, in a voice gone quiet with something that wasn't quite pity: *they stopped following him after the ridge. Men will climb for money. They won't freeze for pride.*

Soren Vask crossed the flats alone.

He had lost weight the way men lose weight fast—in the face first, so that the bones of him showed, and the rich man from the Kessik doorway, the one who had sat in August's kitchen with old power in his shoulders, looked now like a wire frame wearing expensive clothes that no longer fit. He stopped ten meters from the porch. He did not come closer. His eyes went past Ilse, past Wren, to the floatplane on the water and the government markings on its tail, and Ilse watched him understand, all at once and completely, what the valley floor held.

"There's a version of this where I run," he said. His voice was hoarse, as if he'd been talking to himself for days. Ilse suspected he had been.

"Yes," she said.

"And there's a version where I put this—" he lifted one hand, and she saw he was holding nothing, that the gesture was a ghost of a gun or a demand or simply a habit of command with nothing left to command, "—where I make it difficult."

"Yes," she said again. "But you won't."

Something moved through his face—not anger. Something older and more tired than anger. "You sound like him," he said. "Iversen. When he refused. One word, standing in the snow, and I knew—I knew right then—that I'd already lost, and I climbed the shoulder anyway, because I couldn't think of anything else to do with myself." He looked down at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "Thirty years building something and it comes down to a man saying *no* on a ridge, and me not knowing how to say it back."

Wren came off the porch then, unhurried, and stood between Vask and the house, and said, "Sir, I'm going to need you to sit down," and Soren Vask sat down on the gravel bar like a man finally setting down a load he'd carried too far, and did not resist, and did not speak again except once, quietly, when Osei crouched beside him with her forms:

"Ask Iversen about the summit push. Ask him whose order it was. He never told you the whole of it either." And then, to no one, almost gently: "None of us told the whole of it. That was the whole problem."

* * *

They took him away in the floatplane at dusk, the same plane that had brought Osei, the engine droning out over the water and lifting into the last light. Ilse stood on the gravel bar and watched until it was a speck, then a rumor, then nothing.

The valley was very quiet after. The river talked to itself over the stones. Somewhere upstream a bird called three times and quit. Petra Wren came out and lit the lanterns along the cookhouse eave, one by one, an ordinary domestic ritual performed at the exact center of the extraordinary, and Ilse found that her eyes were wet and could not entirely account for why.

August appeared beside her. He didn't say anything for a while. Neither did she. Three weeks ago she would have found the silence unbearable, would have filled it with something clever and armored. Now she let it stand between them like a third person, welcome.

"He told me to ask you," she said finally. "About the summit push. Whose order it was."

August was quiet long enough that she turned to look at him. In the lantern light his face was unreadable and then, deliberately, readable—he let her see him decide.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I've got a whole speech. Tomas helped me write half of it, God rest him, and he'd never forgive me if I rushed it on a gravel bar with a belly full of ranger stew." He glanced at her sideways, and there—faint, tired, real—was the ghost of a smile. "You'll want to be sitting down. It's a long story, and for once I'm going to tell all of it."

Inside, Osei was packing the documents into sealed cases, and Ferro was making a list of names—Renny, Mara, Dev, Wren, Petra—and at the bottom of the page, underlined twice, he wrote *Iversen, August: testimony requested,* and looked up at August across the room with a question in his face.

August met his eyes. And Ilse, watching, saw him take a breath the way a man takes a breath before a plunge into cold water—and nod.

"Yes," August Iversen said, aloud, in front of witnesses, for the first time in seven years. "I'll testify."

Outside, the Upper Fly ran dark and steady toward the lowlands, carrying the melt of the whole range down into the waiting world.
