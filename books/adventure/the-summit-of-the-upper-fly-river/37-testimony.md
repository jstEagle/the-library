## 37. Testimony

The hearing room in Meridian had once been a railway depot, and it still wore its bones: riveted iron trusses overhead, tall arched windows that let the late-autumn light fall in long, dusty columns across the rows of folding chairs. August Iversen had walked past the building a hundred times in another life, back when his name still opened doors instead of closing them. He had never gone inside. Now he was going inside on purpose, and every step from the taxi rank to the front doors felt like walking against a current he had spent nine years learning not to fight.

He had shaved. He had bought a shirt with buttons that matched. Mara had ironed it for him in the motel room while he stood there like a boy being dressed for confirmation, and she had said, without a trace of irony, *You'll want to look like someone worth believing.* He had almost laughed. Almost.

The corridor outside the hearing chamber smelled of floor wax and old paper and, faintly, of coffee that had been burning since dawn. A clerk with a clipboard met him at the door, checked his name against a list, and looked up with an expression August couldn't immediately read—something between recognition and disbelief.

"Iversen," the clerk said. "The Iversen?"

"The same."

"You're taller than your file photo."

"Nine years will do that," August said, "if you spend them outdoors."

He signed the witness register. His hand did not shake, which surprised him. What shook instead was something deeper, some load-bearing wall of himself that had been quietly bearing weight so long it had forgotten it was a wall and not just the way things were. The pen moved; the ink dried; his name sat on a government form in his own handwriting for the first time since Kessik's permit ledgers had learned to do without it.

*August Iversen,* the register read. Not a pseudonym. Not a river-run booking under Tomas's business name. Not the careful blankness he had worn like a second skin through half a decade of low water and small jobs and deliberate anonymity.

Just his name.

* * *

Inside, the room was fuller than he expected. Investigators in grey suits. Lawyers for Brandt Mining arranged along one wall like a defensive line, their briefcases squared up on the floor beside their chairs as if at attention. Journalists—only six or seven of them, but six or seven was enough; he recognized the byline of the woman who had written the original story about the Upper Fly disaster, the one that had used the phrase *guide error* in its third paragraph and never retracted it. She was older now too. Her hair was grey at the temples. When she saw him come in, she wrote something in her notebook before he said a word, and August understood that whatever he did in this room, she had already begun writing the story of it.

At the front, beneath the iron trusses, sat the panel: three investigators and a chairwoman, a heavyset woman named Ondine Farrow whose voice, when she finally used it, carried the particular gravity of someone who had read every document twice and was no longer interested in being impressed.

Ilse Brandt was already seated at the witness table, off to the side, waiting her turn. She wore a plain dark coat and no jewelry, and she watched him cross the room with an expression he had last seen on the summit ridge—the one that meant *I know what this is costing you, and I'm glad you're paying it.* They did not speak. There was nothing left to say that hadn't been said above the clouds, or in the ranger station, or in the long night huddled against minus-thirty wind with Dev fading between them. But when August took the seat beside hers, she shifted her chair two inches closer, and that was its own kind of testimony.

A bailiff swore him in. The words were old ones, worn smooth by repetition: *the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth.* August had heard them in films and courtroom dramas the way everyone had. Standing there, holding the book, he felt for the first time what they actually weighed. For nine years the whole truth had been the thing he refused to give anyone—not the inquiry board that had blamed him, not the journalists who had simplified him, not even himself, if he was honest, because a partial confession is easier to live inside than a full one. Three dead. His fault. Case closed. That was the version he'd carried down out of the mountains like a stone in his pack, and he had built a life around never setting it down.

Today he would have to set it down in public, under oath, in front of the woman whose father had helped bury the real version.

He began to speak, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

* * *

They asked him first about the crossing. This was the part the investigators understood best—the logistics, the route, the storm, the col, the shoulder. He gave it to them plainly: the unseasonal snows, the sealed drums Mara had flagged, the survey cores and the falsified readings ledger, Vask's parallel team, the interception attempt on the scree slope, Dev's beacon and Dev's choice, the cornice, the litter, the ridge, the long night. He spoke for two hours with one break, and the room listened the way rooms rarely listen—with the particular silence of people realizing that the thing they are hearing is not a story but a record.

Chairwoman Farrow interrupted only twice. Once to ask about the fuel line Dev had loosened rather than cut—"You're telling us a man paid to sabotage you chose instead to delay you?"—and August had answered, "I'm telling you a man decided, somewhere around twelve thousand feet, that he'd rather be a person than an instrument. You'd have to ask him why. I can only tell you what he did." Dev, leg in a cast, prosthetic surgery scheduled, testified the following week. His answer, August later learned, had been four minutes long and had made two of the investigators write fast enough to snap pencil lead.

The second interruption came near the end of the logistics section, and it was the one that mattered.

Farrow turned to him with a document on the bench in front of her—a page from the old expedition's communications log, recovered from Brandt Mining's archives during the investigation into the filings. It was dated the morning of the summit push, nine years ago. August had seen fragments of this log before, or believed he had; the inquiry board had received a redacted summary, and the summary had placed the order to push for the summit in ambiguous terms, weather deteriorating, judgment call, guide's discretion.

The actual page said otherwise. It said otherwise in Soren Vask's own typed words.

*"Summit attempt proceeds 0600 regardless. Client contract specifies summit. Turnback forfeits bonus clause. If guide objects, replace guide at high camp and proceed with deputy."*

August read it the way you read a sentence you have waited nine years to be allowed to read. Slowly. Twice. Out loud, because Farrow asked him to read it aloud, into the microphone, so the transcript would carry his voice saying it and not just the paper's ink.

And then something happened that he had not planned and could not have planned. He stopped being a witness describing events and became, briefly, the man he had been nine years ago—the one at high camp, radio in hand, hearing those words come through the static in Vask's clipped, confident voice, feeling the exact shape of the trap close around him: object and be replaced, comply and kill three people, refuse and end his career. He had chosen the third option, in the end—he had ordered the turnback himself, against the contract, against Vask—and the mountain had killed them anyway, because by then the window was gone and the clients were exhausted and the decision had been made for them by days of forced ascent. But the inquiry had never heard the radio log. The inquiry had heard Vask's account, delivered through lawyers, polished and plausible, and it had heard August's account filtered through the fact that he was the surviving guide and guides are presumed to fail.

"I objected," August said, and his voice broke on the word *objected*, and he let it break, because he was done repairing it in private. "I ordered the turnback at 11:40. Two of the three climbers were already showing signs of exhaustion edema. I was overruled by satellite call within twenty minutes. The replacement never came because the weather closed the route—but the order stood, and we went back up, and we went back up because the man who wrote that page told us the contract mattered more than the cold. I have spent nine years being the reason three people died. I was, at most, the last hand on a lever other men had already pulled."

The room did not erupt. Rooms like this don't. But the silence afterward had a different texture than the silences before it, and August felt the difference physically, the way you feel a change in air pressure before weather. The journalist with the grey temples stopped writing and simply looked at him. One of the Brandt lawyers leaned forward and whispered to another, and the whisper had the sound of a man recalculating.

Farrow said, quietly, "For the record, Mr. Iversen, this committee has now heard the unredacted log. The prior inquiry did not." A pause. "It will hear it again."

* * *

Cross-examination came after lunch, and it belonged to the Brandt legal team, and it was exactly as surgical and exactly as bloodless as August had prepared himself for. A senior partner named Calloway, silver-haired, courteous in the way that costs money, walked him through the crossing again looking for seams: Wasn't it true he had accepted payment from Henrik Brandt's organization? Wasn't it true he had initially refused and then taken the job anyway, which suggested the money mattered? Hadn't he opened the sealed drums without authorization, technically converting company property? Wasn't his recollection of the old expedition conveniently self-exculpating, given that the only other witnesses to the radio exchange were dead?

August had imagined this hour for weeks—on the trail, in the cave, in the motel room with Mara ironing his shirt—and the strange discovery of living through it was that none of the questions landed where they were aimed. Each one glanced off, not because he had clever answers but because he had stopped needing clever ones. The man they were trying to catch was the man who had spent nine years hiding, and that man wasn't in the room anymore.

"Yes," he said to the money question. "I took the job for the money. I was broke, my outfitting business was dying, and I signed on knowing the cargo smelled wrong. I'm not here pretending to be clean. I'm here because halfway up the mountain I found out how dirty it was, and I had a choice about what to do next, and I'm telling you what I chose." He turned slightly toward the panel, away from Calloway, addressing the room itself. "You can weigh everything else against that."

To the drums: "I opened property I was contracted to transport, because transporting it blind would have made me complicit in whatever it was. I'd do it again. If that's conversion, convert me."

To the dead witnesses: "Yes. Everyone who heard that radio call besides me is dead. That's precisely why I'm alive to sit here, and precisely why I stayed silent for nine years—because my word alone was a rope bridge over a canyon, and I didn't trust it to hold. Now there are documents. Documents don't die on mountains."

Calloway tried one last angle, the one August knew was coming the way you know a serac is going to calve: the blacklist. *Mr. Iversen, isn't it true that following the inquiry you were removed from the regional guide registry, that multiple outfitters declined to employ you, that you spent several years working under assumed arrangements in the town of Kessik—in effect, that your professional community judged you unfit?*

"It's all true," August said. "Every word. And here's what I've learned about that." He paused, and found Ilse's eyes across the room, and drew something from them he couldn't have named. "A blacklist tells you what people decided about you when the record was wrong. It doesn't tell you what you are. I kept my name out of ledgers for years because I thought invisibility was safety. Then I spent one winter on the Upper Fly learning that the only thing invisibility keeps safe is the truth of whoever benefits from your silence. I'm done being safe that way. I'd rather be visible and accountable than hidden and useless. That's not a defense. It's the whole answer."

There was no applause—courtrooms don't clap—but when he stepped down from the stand, three separate people in the gallery touched his shoulder as he passed, and the journalist with the notebook asked him for fifteen minutes outside, and for the first time in nine years August Iversen said yes to a reporter without flinching.

* * *

The fifteen minutes happened on the depot's old loading platform, where freight had once been winched off flatcars, the iron rings still bolted into the stone. The wind coming down the street had the first bite of winter in it. The reporter—her name was Anselm, he learned, Edith Anselm—held her recorder between them like a small shared lantern.

"Why now?" she asked. "Why come out of hiding for this, when you could have sent a statement?"

August looked at the iron rings in the platform stone, at the windows of the hearing hall glowing amber behind them, and gave her the truest answer he had.

"Because a statement is a shadow on the wall," he said. "I've been a shadow for nine years. Shadows don't testify. They don't take questions. They don't sit in a room and let a lawyer try to find the seam in them, and they don't feel their voice break on a word like *objected* in front of strangers. A statement protects the man who sends it. Testimony spends him. And I finally understand—that was the lesson of the whole mountain, if I'm honest—that survival was never about staying invisible. I thought it was, for years. I hid, and hiding worked, in the sense that nobody found me. But nothing healed. The blacklist didn't fade. The guilt didn't shrink. The dead didn't get lighter. All hiding ever did was keep the wound exactly where it was, at exactly the same depth, year after year, like a crevasse that never closes because nothing falls into it and nothing fills it."

He turned to face her fully.

"What filled it was being seen. Being questioned. Being cross-examined by a very good lawyer and finding out I didn't shatter. Being believed—not because people were kind, but because the documents held. That's the thing I'd tell anyone carrying something like I carried: the record is the rope. Get yourself on the record, however much it costs, because a name attached to a true account can bear weight that an anonymous grievance never will. My blacklist is going to become irrelevant—not rescinded, maybe, not ceremonially erased, just irrelevant, the way an old forecast becomes irrelevant once the weather has actually arrived. The record arrived. Let the blacklist argue with the record and see who wins."

Anselm lowered the recorder. "One more. Off the record or on, your choice."

"On," August said. "Everything's on, now. That's the point."

"Then: do you forgive yourself?"

He was quiet for a moment. Down the street, a delivery truck idled; somewhere behind the depot walls, the hearing continued without him, clerks reading exhibits into the transcript, the machinery of the record grinding forward on bearings he had helped set that morning.

"No," he said. "But I've stopped confusing forgiveness with the goal. The goal was accuracy. The world had the wrong account of what happened up there, and I let it, for nine years, because correcting it cost more than I thought I could pay. Turns out the price was payable. Turns out I'd been mispricing it all along—counting the cost of exposure and never counting the cost of concealment, which compounds, quietly, annually, like debt. I don't forgive myself yet. Maybe I will. But I'm accurate now. And accurate turns out to be a livable place to stand."

* * *

That evening, in the motel room, Mara called from the north. She had watched the afternoon session on the inquiry's public stream, crammed into the Kessik outfitter's back office with Renny and Tomas's memory hanging over the desk like a photograph that had always been there and had suddenly become a portrait of a man who deserved better than the silence August had given his own history.

"You sounded like yourself," Mara said. "Not the pre-disaster you. Not the Kessik ghost. Yourself. The one who surfaced somewhere around the Col of Hanging Ice and never went back under."

August lay on the bed with his boots off and the phone against his ear and the ceiling doing what motel ceilings do—nothing, endlessly.

"There's a letter," Mara said. "Came to the shop today. Registry seal on it."

His stomach dropped, the old reflex, the Pavlovian clench of nine years' standing. "What does it say?"

"That's the thing. Nobody's opened it. Renny wouldn't touch it, I wouldn't touch it. It's addressed to you, care of the shop, and it's either the registry reinstating you or striking you permanently, and nobody wanted to be the one who found out which." A breath down the line. "We put it in the safe. Come home and open it yourself. Whatever it says, you should be the first one to read your own name."

He hung up and sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, hands loose between his knees, thinking about envelopes and safes and the strange arithmetic of a life that had been weighed in other people's ledgers for nine years and was, at last, down to a single sealed page with his name on the outside of it.

Whatever the registry decided, it no longer decided anything that mattered. He had testified. The log was in the transcript. Vask's signature sat in the public record wearing its own words like a confession it had never agreed to sign. Three dead climbers had their deaths reassigned to the men who had ordered the ascent, and August Iversen had his name back—not granted, not restored, but *taken*, deliberately, in a room full of strangers, at the cost of a voice breaking once on a single word.

Still. He would open the envelope. Some rituals you keep even after they stop having power over you—especially then. It was a two-day drive north to Kessik, and the passes were holding, barely, and the first snows had come to the divide the way they always came, early and without apology.

He packed the shirt with the matching buttons. He left the light on in the empty room, out of some habit he didn't examine, and went down to the taxi rank in the dark.

Two days north, an envelope waited in a safe, and beyond it, spring was already moving toward the upper river whether anyone was ready for it or not.
