## 38. What Survival Costs

The scar on Renny's hand had stopped being a wound sometime in late autumn, though nobody could say exactly when. One morning it was a red seam that caught the light wrong; the next it was simply part of him, like the freckles across his forearms or the burn mark from the camp stove he'd tipped over in his first season. Mara noticed the transition because she was the one who kept noticing things—that had always been her job, before it was anyone else's. She noticed when the grip strength came back, when he could hold a whisk again without wincing, when he started humming while he cooked instead of working in silence the way injured men do.

He was making bread in the back kitchen of the outfitting house in Kessik, flour to his elbows, and he hummed something tuneless and cheerful, and Mara stood in the doorway watching him for longer than she meant to.

"You're staring," Renny said without turning around.

"I'm inventorying."

"Of what?"

"Of what we got back." She leaned against the doorframe. The kitchen smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and the particular clean cold that came off the river this time of year. "A cook with two working hands is worth more than you'd think."

Renny flexed his fingers, all five of them, and looked at them as if they belonged to someone he'd been introduced to recently. "The surgeon said I'd lose some of the fine motion. He was wrong, but only because I practiced. Every night in the hospital I'd sit there moving my thumb against each finger, over and over, until the nurse told me to stop being weird about it." He grinned. "I was being weird about it. But here we are."

Here they were. That phrase had become something of a house motto since the descent—Mara wasn't sure who'd said it first, whether it was August on the ridge or Ilse at the moraine or Dev from his stretcher, half-frozen and laughing at nothing. *Here we are.* It covered a multitude of accounting problems. It did not, however, cover all of them.

* * *

Dev Okafor came back to Kessik in November, walking with a cane he'd already begun to resent. The prosthetic arrived a week later in a crate marked MEDICAL EQUIPMENT — HANDLE WITH CARE, which Dev found funny in a dry way, because everything about his situation was either medical equipment or needed handling with care. The leg below the knee was gone—the cornice fall had done what falls do, shattering the ankle into fragments no surgeon could reassemble—and the replacement was carbon fiber and titanium, sleeker than any leg had a right to be.

He sat on the porch of the outfitting house while Mara helped him fit the sock liner, because he couldn't yet manage the angles himself, and he talked the whole time, the way he always talked, easy and unguarded, as if the last six months hadn't happened.

"They gave me a choice of models," he said. "There was one with a hydraulic ankle. Costs more than my mother's house. I took the standard one and put the difference toward—" he paused, and Mara realized he was embarrassed, actually embarrassed, which was new, "—toward flying home for Christmas. My sister's having a baby. I intend to hold that child standing up, on both legs, even if one of them is borrowed."

"It'll hold," Mara said, tightening the last strap. "How does it feel?"

"Like a very polite stranger has moved into my body and refuses to discuss rent." He swung the leg out and tested it against the porch boards. "But it holds. That's the main thing. August says the mountain doesn't care about your feelings, and the prosthetic doesn't either, but it does care about torque, and torque I can learn."

The honest employment offer had come through in October—a letter, an actual letter on actual paper, from the regional rangers' service. They wanted Dev for their equipment and logistics division. Trail maintenance scheduling, gear inspection, the unglamorous machinery that kept people alive without ever appearing in photographs. The letter cited, among other qualifications, his "demonstrated judgment under pressure," which made Dev laugh out loud when he read it, because the judgment under pressure in question had consisted of destroying a beacon and confessing treason to his own crew.

"They know," Mara said. "August told them. All of it."

"I know they know. That's why I laughed." He turned the letter over in his hands. "You understand what that means? Anywhere else, that history is a stain. Here it's a credential. Somebody down at the ranger station decided that a man who chose right once, at cost, is worth more than a man who never faced the choice." He looked at her. "Your friend August negotiated that language personally. Took him three drafts. He's not a man you'd cast as a diplomat, but he fought for every word."

"He's good at fighting for other people," Mara said. "It's himself he's terrible at."

Dev nodded slowly, and neither of them said anything more about it, because there was nothing more to say that hadn't already been said in a bivouac cave at minus thirty, in the long night, when everyone owed everyone everything and the ledger could never balance.

* * *

The business itself was a skeleton learning to walk.

Mara had spent the years after the disaster building her own guiding work in the southern valleys—small parties, low profile, clients who came by word of mouth and never asked about the Upper Fly. When August's disgrace had broken over the industry like dirty water, it had reached her too. Not formally—there was no formal mechanism for ruining a woman who'd merely trained alongside a blacklisted man—but informally, thoroughly, the way reputations contaminate. Insurers grew cautious. Outfitters stopped returning calls. A client once withdrew from a trek mid-payment, citing "concerns about the lead guide's professional associations," and Mara had understood exactly which association, and had said nothing, because saying something would have confirmed it.

So the rebuilding was personal in a way she didn't advertise. The new company—Iversen & Quist, though the paperwork still smelled of ink and compromise—operated out of the old house on the river road with a sign she'd painted herself, because hiring a sign painter felt like vanity, and vanity was a thing she'd sworn off somewhere above the tree line.

They ran their first trip in early December: four clients, lower valley, nothing technical. August led. Mara swept. It went fine—more than fine, the clients were delighted, the tips were generous—and Mara spent the evening afterward doing the books and discovering that fine was not the same as solvent. Insurance premiums for a firm with August's name on the license had doubled. The bonding company wanted a personal guarantee. Two of the porters they'd hired for the season quit inside a week.

That was the part that stung. Not the money—money was arithmetic, and arithmetic she could survive. The porters.

Tomas's nephews, both of them. Young men Mara had known since they were boys hauling gear for pocket money, sons of the family that had fed her her first hot meal in this country twenty years ago. They came to the house together, hats in hand, and stood in the kitchen where Renny was baking, and told her plainly: they would not work under Iversen's flag. Their uncle had died believing certain things about the mountains and the men who sold passage through them, and whatever the inquiry had concluded—whatever Vask's confession had shifted, whatever the record now said—they could not shake the feeling that walking behind August Iversen was walking behind a man the mountains had already judged once.

"We don't think you're bad people," the older one said, and his voice cracked on *people*, and Mara believed him completely. "We just can't. Not yet. Maybe not ever. We're sorry."

Mara walked them to the gate. The river was making its winter sound, low and glassy under the ice shelves. She told them the door stayed open. She told them she understood. She watched them go down the road with their duffels and thought: this is what survival costs, and nobody puts it on the manifest. You come down off the mountain alive and you find that some doors have closed quietly while you were gone, and the people on the far side of them are not villains, they're just people carrying their own dead, and you cannot argue with grief using evidence.

She told August that night, sitting across the table from the ledger.

"How did he take it?" she asked.

"He didn't take it anywhere. He thanked them for their honesty and shook both their hands and then went out and split wood for two hours in the dark." She could hear the axe in her memory, rhythmic and furious and controlled. "Then he came in and ate everything Renny put in front of him and said, 'Tomas would've done the same.' And that was all."

"Was it true? Would Tomas have done the same?"

August had been quiet a long moment. Then: "Tomas would have taken the job. That was the problem with Tomas. He'd have taken the job, and he'd have been right to, and he'd have carried the doubt up the mountain like everyone else, and maybe it would have killed him too." His voice had gone flat, the way it went flat when he was near something he couldn't look at directly. "Or maybe he'd have talked me out of it in the first place, the way he tried to. I keep both versions. Some nights one weighs more than the other."

Mara had written that down—not in the ledger, in the small notebook she kept for things she didn't want to lose—and she read it sometimes in the evenings, and it never got lighter. That was the thing about Tomas. Everyone on the crew had lost someone on the old expedition, but Mara had lost him twice: once to the mountain, and once to the silence afterward, when August had gone underground and taken the truth with him, and she'd spent seven years not knowing whether her friend was guilty or destroyed or both. The inquiry had reassigned the blame. The record was clean now, formally, legally. But records are paper. Grief is weather. It comes back around regardless of what the filings say.

* * *

By January the business had its rhythm, if not yet its health. Three treks booked for the spring season, all lower routes, all within their competence and their insurance. Renny had renegotiated his role—he refused, flatly, to be treated as fragile, and had designed a menu system that let him cook full expeditions with one hand braced on the counter when the scar tissue ached in cold weather. Dev left for the rangers' post in February with a handshake from August that lasted slightly too long and a promise, extracted solemnly, that he'd inspect their ropes free of charge forever, "as penance, obviously, though I prefer to think of it as gratitude."

Ilse came and went like a tide. She had taken a room above the hardware store for the winter—she'd insisted on paying market rate, over everyone's objections—and she appeared at the house two or three evenings a week with bread or documents or questions. She was studying for her guide certification, working through the navigation syllabus with a thoroughness that made Mara feel lazy by comparison. She'd sit at the kitchen table with topographic sheets spread out and quiz herself aloud, contour intervals, aspect calculations, the names of every tributary of the upper drainage, and August would answer from the stove without looking up, and the two of them had developed a shorthand that was not quite romance and not quite anything else and was, Mara suspected, better than both.

She saw it most clearly in small moments. The way August had started leaving the second cup out when he made coffee in the mornings, unprompted, weeks before Ilse's visits settled into a schedule. The way Ilse had stopped asking permission to borrow the good binoculars and simply taken them, the way family does. The way neither of them spoke of the ridge, or the long night, or the word August had given Vask across the radio—one word, *no*, the cheapest and most expensive syllable of his life—as if those things were furniture now, load-bearing, not to be discussed while standing on them.

One evening in late February Mara found them on the porch, not talking, looking at the range. The peaks were going pink with alpenglow, the Upper Fly watershed white and enormous and indifferent in the distance, and Ilse said something quiet that Mara didn't catch, and August said, "Spring, then," and that was all, and Mara understood that a plan existed, and that the plan involved mountains, and that this time nobody was running from anything.

It should have made her happy without reservation. Mostly it did. But she was an accountant of sorts by temperament, and the account had entries that wouldn't close.

Two porters gone. Tomas's chair at the spring meeting still empty, still set out of habit, still nobody's to remove. Her own seven years of exile, which the record had reversed but which lived in her body like old altitude—present in every deep breath, invisible, permanent. And underneath everything, the knowledge she'd spoken aloud once in the cave, storm-bound, when she'd told August why she'd really signed on to Vask's expedition: not for the money, not entirely, but because his disgrace had become hers, and the only way she could see to reclaim her own name was to walk back into the fire beside the man who'd lit it. She'd gotten her name back. She'd also learned exactly what it weighed.

* * *

On the first thaw day in March, when the river ice groaned and shifted and the light came in low and gold, Mara sat down with the season's budget and found, to her genuine surprise, that the numbers worked. Barely. Provisionally. With a margin thin enough to see through. But they worked, and she allowed herself ten minutes of satisfaction before returning to the habit of a lifetime, which was finding the flaw in any structure that appeared to hold.

The flaw, this time, was a letter.

It had come that morning, forwarded from the ranger station, addressed to August in a hand Mara recognized instantly because she'd seen it on the original contract eight months ago: Henrik Brandt's office. She set it on the table without opening it—it wasn't hers to open—and when August came in from checking the boat lines, wet to the knees, he picked it up and held it for a long moment without breaking the seal, the way a man holds something he expects to bite him.

"What do you think it is?" Mara asked.

"Ilse thinks it's a settlement offer." He turned the envelope over. "She's been expecting it. Her father's lawyers have finished their internal review, whatever that means, and apparently the magnate himself wants to conclude matters 'privately and with dignity.'" He said the words with a faint taste of ash. "Dignity. From that man."

"And what do you think?"

"I think a man doesn't write letters like this unless he's decided something." August set it down again, unopened, in the center of the table, equidistant from everything, like a stone placed on a map. "And I think whatever he's decided, it isn't really for us. It's for her. And she gets to decide what happens next, not him, and not me, and not—" he glanced at Mara, and something almost like humor crossed his face, "—not the two of us reading his mail by lamplight like conspirators."

"We're not reading it. It's sealed."

"We're absolutely reading the outside of it."

Mara laughed despite herself, and the sound loosened something in the room, and August picked up the letter and slid it into the drawer of the sideboard where important unwelcome things lived—the insurance correspondence, the bonding guarantee, the old photograph of the 20XX expedition team that he still hadn't decided whether to burn or frame.

"She'll open it tomorrow," he said. "Her way. On her terms. That's the whole point of everything we did up there—that she'd get terms at all." He dried his hands on his trousers and looked out the window at the river, running free now at the edges, carrying the winter downstream in pieces. "Whatever's in that letter, she walks into that room alone. That's not cruelty. That's respect. She earned the right to face him without any of us standing behind her like witnesses."

Mara thought about that overnight, and in the morning she thought about it more, and by the time Ilse arrived that evening—bread under one arm, topographic sheets under the other, the letter already opened and read twice on the walk over—Mara had formed her view, which was that August was right and also that he was lying to himself about how little he intended to be involved, because men like August always said *alone* and then stood at the end of the road anyway, pretending to check the weather.

Ilse set the letter on the kitchen table. Her father's words were brief and formal and, in their way, almost gentle—an invitation to meet, a settlement proposed, a future described in terms that assumed her return. Mara read it once at Ilse's nod and heard, between the lines, the voice of a man who had audited his losses and found, to his astonishment, that the largest one had walked away on her own legs.

"When?" Ilse asked the room generally.

"Whenever you choose," Mara said.

"No." Ilse folded the letter along its crease, precisely, the way her father's engineers folded survey maps. "When is he expecting me?"

August, at the stove, said quietly: "Thursday."

And Thursday, Mara knew, was only three days off, and the roads would be clear by then, and whatever happened in that lawyer's room in the city would come back up the river road like weather, and none of them—not August, not Renny, not Dev with his borrowed leg, not Mara with her reopened ledger—would be able to pretend they hadn't invested everything they had in the woman carrying that letter into the room where her father was waiting.
