## 39. Henrik Brandt's Reckoning

The city came back to Ilse in layers, the way snowmelt comes off a mountain—first the smell, diesel and wet stone and the green rot of ornamental lindens, then the sound, the ceaseless hydraulic hiss of a place that never fully sleeps, and last the light, which was wrong. She had not realized until she stepped out of the taxi in front of her father's tower how much she had come to trust light that arrived at an angle, filtered through timber smoke or cloud, light that had to travel over real distance to reach you. The city's light fell straight down and bounced off glass, and it asked nothing of you except that you be seen.

She had dressed for it deliberately. Not the expedition shell with its repaired shoulder seam—not that, though she had considered it, wearing the mountain into the room like a flag. And not the silk her father's tailor kept on file for her either. She wore a plain grey suit bought off a rack in Kessik's single clothing store, and boots that had been resoled twice, and she carried nothing but the letter in her breast pocket and her own name, which was the only thing she had ever truly inherited.

The lobby smelled of lilies. It always had. Someone in facilities had decided decades ago that white lilies said *wealth without effort*, and no one had ever had the authority or the courage to tell them the flowers said *funeral* instead. Ilse signed the visitor ledger herself—the pen chained to the desk, the guard's bored eyes flicking up once—and rode the elevator to the forty-first floor watching her reflection multiply in the brushed steel doors, six versions of her, all going to the same meeting.

* * *

The conference room had not changed. That was the first cruelty of it: the long table of pale Scandinavian wood, the wall of windows facing the harbor cranes, the sideboard with the coffee service and the small dish of candied ginger that her father had eaten through every negotiation of her childhood, believing it settled his stomach and sharpened his temper both. Henrik Brandt sat at the far end of the table, not at the head—he had always understood theater better than people gave him credit for—and beside him sat Counselor Vey, who had handled Brandt Mining's filings since before Ilse could read, a dry grey man whose legal signature was so restrained it looked apologetic.

Her father stood when she entered. That surprised her more than anything else in the room. He had not stood for her mother in years, as far as Ilse remembered; he stood now because the room had witnesses, and Henrik Brandt never wasted a witness.

"Ilse." He did not come around the table. He offered his hand across the corner of it instead, and she took it, and his grip was careful, the grip of a man handling something he has been told is fragile and does not entirely believe it. "You look well."

"The mountains agreed with me."

Something moved behind his eyes—she would spend the evening deciding whether it was pain or arithmetic—and he gestured her to a chair. Not the chair opposite him; a chair along the side, so that they would sit at angles rather than in opposition. He had chosen the geometry before she arrived. Of course he had.

Vey laid out papers with the soft precision of a man dealing cards he already knew the value of. "As outlined in the covering letter," Vey began, "the settlement package consists of three components. A cash disbursement against your trust, restructured to release over five years rather than thirty. A seat on the remediation board we're establishing—your father feels your field experience would be—" Vey paused, caught himself, and Ilse watched him decide not to say *useful* —"would be relevant. And third, a formal statement, drafted by this office, acknowledging the circumstances of the Upper Fly documents and your role in their delivery, framed in terms that protect the family's standing while satisfying the regulators' procedural requirements."

"You mean a statement saying I was confused," Ilse said.

"I mean a statement saying you were *filial*," Vey said, without embarrassment. "Confusion implies impairment. Filial loyalty implies only youth. Youth is forgivable. Impairment requires documentation."

Her father let the exchange run for exactly three seconds before he raised one hand, and Vey stopped as if a valve had closed. Henrik Brandt had built an empire on knowing when three seconds was enough.

"I'm not here about the money," Ilse said.

"No," her father said. "I didn't think you were. But Vey spent two weeks building that package, and he'll want to hear you decline it properly, so that his paperwork has an ending." He turned his head toward the window, toward the cranes. "Decline it properly, Ilse. Then we can talk."

* * *

She declined it properly. She made herself do it in his language, which was the hardest part—that the only way to refuse a man like Henrik Brandt cleanly was to refuse him fluently. She cited the release schedule's clawback clauses. She noted that a seat on a remediation board chaired by the same auditors who had signed the falsified readings was not oversight but upholstery. She told Vey, gently, that a filial statement drafted by his office would be worth less than the paper it inhibited, because every regulator in the province had read enough of Vey's drafting to know what a family protecting itself sounded like.

Vey took notes. He did not argue. At the end he capped his pen and said, "For the record, Miss Brandt, this is the most literate refusal I've processed in nineteen years," and there was, buried under the dryness, something that might have been respect.

Then her father dismissed him—or rather, dismissed himself through him, because Henrik Brandt did not dismiss people, he arranged exits—and the door closed behind Vey with its pneumatic sigh, and the two of them were alone in a room built for sixteen with a view of the harbor and a dish of candied ginger neither of them touched.

* * *

"He nearly died," her father said, finally. "The porter. Okafor. I read the incident report. Shattered leg above the cornice line, carried down on tent poles, four days to the ranger station." He turned the words over like core samples. "Four days. In that cold, carrying a man."

"Yes."

"And you stayed with him."

"We all stayed with him. That's what staying means. It doesn't divide."

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw what the mountains had shown her in August Iversen's face on the col—that exhaustion is a kind of honesty, and that some men can only afford it when the performance has become too expensive to maintain. Her father was seventy-one years old and he had been performing since before she was born, and the performance had cost him everything except the thing he'd built to pay for it.

"You could have called me," he said. "From the ranger station. Before the investigators. One call, Ilse, and half of what followed would have—"

"Would have been negotiated," she said. "Yes. I know. That's why I didn't make it."

He absorbed that. She watched him absorb it, actually watched the sentence go into him the way a bad reading goes into a drill core—slowly, and all the way down.

"Your grandfather," he said, "lost a mine in 'sixty-three. Flooded. Eleven men. Do you know what he did? He went to every widow in the valley and he paid them, out of his own pocket, before the insurers paid him. He ruined himself doing it. And when I asked him why—when I was twenty-two and certain I would never be so stupid—he said: *because the ledgers are how they'll judge us, boy, but the widows are how we'll judge ourselves.*" His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I built forty years on proving him wrong. And it worked. That's the obscene part, Ilse. It worked. Right up until my own daughter read my ledgers better than my lawyers did."

"The filings weren't yours alone," she said. "Vask drafted them. You knew what he was when you hired him. You'd known since the Fly."

"I've known Soren Vask since before you existed," her father said. "He is the only man I've ever met who wanted the same things I wanted and felt no shame wanting them. Do you understand what a relief that is? To sit across from someone and not watch them flinch?" He shook his head slowly. "No. You don't. You found your flinching man. The guide. Iversen. He flinches beautifully, doesn't he? All that guilt, worn like webbing."

"His guilt saved my life."

"My ledgers fed yours for twenty-six years." It came out flat, not cruel—flat the way bedrock is flat. "We are neither of us entitled to the other's gratitude."

* * *

They talked for two hours. Not reconciled—she want to be clear with herself about that, walking out—but not cruel either. They spoke of her mother, briefly, and of the house at Løkken that neither of them had opened since. He asked real questions about the crossing, and she gave real answers, and twice he laughed—a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody—and both times he looked surprised to find it in the room.

Near the end, he slid a single sheet across the table. Not a settlement document. A list. The rangers' rescue post at the valley mouth—its budget, its staffing shortfall, the helicopter retainer that kept lapsing every fiscal year because no one upstream thought eleven minutes of flight time was worth insuring.

"This came across my desk in March," he said. "I approved a token grant and forgot it. Dev Okafor would be dead if the post had been funded properly. So would you, probably." He tapped the sheet once. "I assume you're going to fund it yourself. Out of the trust you won't let me restructure."

"I am."

"Then take the list. My accountants will fight you on the transfer mechanics for months otherwise, and you'll win, but it will cost you a year." He paused. "Consider it the only honest thing I've ever put in writing."

"Why?" she asked. "Why help me do this?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a crane swung its load across the gray water, patient as a heron.

"Because you read the ledgers," he said at last, "and you still came to the table. Because you declined properly. Because you're going to spend your inheritance on strangers in a river valley, and you'll be poorer than I was at your age and richer than I am now, and I want—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I want to see whether it works. Whether power given away holds its shape. Your grandfather believed it did. I never had the courage to test it. Test it for me, Ilse. Report back. That's all I ask. No absolution. Just data."

* * *

She left the tower in the late afternoon, when the city's straight-down light had gone amber and almost forgivable. At the lobby doors she paused by the lilies—still saying funeral, still saying wealth without effort, saying both at once, which was perhaps the truest thing in the building—and she thought about her father standing when she entered the room, and what it had cost him, and what it had purchased, and whether those two sums would ever balance.

They would not. She knew that now the way she knew the weight limits of a rope. Some accounts stay open. You carry them anyway. That was the whole of what the mountain had taught her, and her father had taught her the same thing in a different alphabet, and between the two educations she had learned the only lesson that mattered: that renunciation is not the absence of power but its final form—the last move on the board, the one that cannot be countered because it gives away the very square your opponent needed to stand on.

She mailed the funding confirmation from a post office, not from her father's tower. She wanted the rangers' post to begin its second life with clean paper.

Then she walked down to the river road, where the evening buses ran east toward Kessik, and bought her ticket, and stood in the queue with her pack between her boots, watching the water move the way it moves in valleys—sideways, patiently, carrying everything downstream eventually.

Behind her, in the city, her father was presumably eating candied ginger over some new negotiation. Ahead of her, three hundred kilometers north, a man who flinched beautifully was probably rebuilding a gear shed roof, and a cook with a healed hand was probably arguing with a porter about prosthetic-friendly crampon fittings, and somewhere Mara Quist was balancing a ledger that finally told the truth.

Ilse shouldered her pack as the bus doors sighed open.

She had a report to deliver.
