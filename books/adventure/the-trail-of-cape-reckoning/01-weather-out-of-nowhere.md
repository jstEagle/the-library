## 1. Weather Out of Nowhere

The storm came out of the north like a debt collector, and I was in no position to argue with either.

I'd been watching it build for an hour over the shoulder of Mount Corben—a bruise-colored wall with lightning working inside it like something trying to get out—and I did what any sensible man does when the sky turns ugly over the Kestrel: I finished my coffee, checked my watch, and decided I had just enough time to be stupid.

"Xanthe." Marta Kessler stood in the doorway of the airfield office with her arms crossed and her reading glasses pushed up into gray hair that had never once obeyed her. "You're not flying into that."

"I'm flying ahead of it. There's a difference."

"There's a difference between brave and dead, too, and from here they look identical."

Marta had owned the Kestrel River airfield for thirty years, which meant she owned me, more or less. Not in the way banks own people—banks at least have the decency to send statements. Marta's ownership was quieter and more total. She'd fronted me the overhaul on the Goose two seasons back when my engine let go over Mile Twelve and I'd glided onto her strip with my heart somewhere around my tonsils. She hadn't asked many questions about where the money would come from. That was the trap of kindness. It doesn't charge interest; it charges attention.

"I've got penicillin going cold at Halfway," I said. "Doc Ferro's got a kid with pneumonia who doesn't care about cumulonimbus."

"Doc Ferro can drive her up in the truck like a civilized person."

"The road washed out in March."

"The road washes out every March. That's practically its personality."

She said it, but she was already walking to the fuel shed to unlock it, because that was Marta—she argued with you all the way to helping you. I signed nothing. We didn't do paperwork anymore; paperwork was how you ended up with a file, and files were how you ended up found.

That last part I didn't say out loud. I almost never said anything out loud anymore, if I could help it.

* * *

Here's what most people knew about Xanthe Okafor, bush pilot: he flew medicine, mail, and gossip up three hundred miles of river nobody else wanted, in a forty-year-old Grumman Goose held together by safety wire and stubbornness. He kept his head down, paid cash when he had it, and when he didn't have it he paid in favors and silence. He didn't drink at the Landing Road hotel because the Landing Road hotel had a dartboard and darts invited conversation. He didn't talk about before. When asked where he'd learned to fly, he said "around," which is the kind of answer that sounds like modesty if you don't look at it too long.

Here's what nobody knew: that "around" had been eleven countries, three flight schools under three names, and a mother who taught him navigation on a kitchen table with salt shakers and a spoon before he could reach the pedals. That his real surname wasn't Okafor—that was his father's gift, one true thing in a life otherwise assembled from substitutions—and that the name his mother had given him at birth belonged to a country that no longer existed, which made it either worthless or the most dangerous thing he owned, depending on the day.

Most days I managed not to think about it at all. That was the whole architecture of my life, honestly. A man can build a lot out of not-thinking. Runways, even.

* * *

The Goose lifted off the brown water at 2:14 in the afternoon with the storm still twenty miles north and closing like a fist. I'd flown this river so long I could do it blind—not legally, but in the way that matters, the way where your hands know the bends before your eyes find them. The Kestrel runs wide and slow through the lowlands, all braided channels and gravel bars that rearrange themselves every spring like a card sharp showing off, then narrows into the gorge past Mile Nine where the water goes green and mean and the cliffs stand close enough to read each other's lichen.

Halfway sat at Mile Forty-Seven, a fuel dock, a clinic, a general store, and eleven houses arranged along the bank as if someone had dealt them and lost interest. Doc Ferro met me at the dock before I'd shut the engine down, which meant the kid was bad.

"Pneumonia's turning," she said, hauling the cooler aboard before I'd finished tying off. "You're a sight for sore lungs, Okafor."

"How is he?"

"Nine years old and mad as a wet cat about being sick, which I take as a good sign." She thumped the cooler. "Antibiotics, oxygen concentrator parts, and—" she lowered her voice, though there was nobody within a mile but gulls, "—a letter for the Wren place up past the narrows. You going far enough?"

I looked at the letter. Ordinary envelope, ordinary handwriting. But the Wren place was upriver of the falls, upriver of everything, in country the maps marked with blank space and the word *uninhabited*, which on the Kestrel usually meant inhabited very carefully.

"Ferro, I fly to the narrows and no farther. You know that."

"You flew to the narrows and fifty yards farther in April."

"That was wind. Wind makes decisions for you."

She gave me the look doctors give patients who are lying about pain. "It's medicine too, sort of. Old woman up there. The family sends down for things. Nobody flies up there regular, and the mail boat stopped after the season started."

"Because the mail boat has insurance," I said, "and I have a debt."

"Same thing, really."

I took the letter. I want it noted that I told myself it was only as far as the narrows, and I want it further noted that I believed myself, which is the trick I'd gotten best at over the years. A pilot who lies to air traffic control gets fined. A pilot who lies to himself gets a whole life.

* * *

The storm caught me at Mile Sixty, which surprised me, because storms on the Kestrel are usually honest—they build all afternoon and hit at dusk like they're keeping an appointment. This one jumped its schedule. One minute I was running the channel below a sky gone the color of pewter, and the next the north wall was on me, rain coming sideways hard enough to sound like gravel on the hull, and the river vanished into a gray so complete I was flying on memory and instruments older than I was.

I put her down in Miller's Slough, a side arm I knew had room and no traffic, and rode it out at the dock of a fishing camp that had been empty since the Hendersons gave up on the river four years back. I sat in the pitching airplane with the tie-downs groaning and watched water fall on water and thought, as I always think in weather, about my mother.

Not the warm thoughts. The warm ones I keep locked up tighter than these. I mean the practical mystery of her—the way she'd lived thirty years in a house with no photographs on the walls, teaching me to navigate by dead reckoning and never once saying reckoning toward what. The way she'd burned papers in the woodstove every autumn, a small private bonfire, and told me it was drafts. The way she'd died with her hand flat on a map of a river system I'd never heard of, as if she was holding it down against the wind.

I'd spent my whole adult life assuming the silence was grief. Some people lose a country and can't talk about it; I figured she was one. It had seemed generous, even noble, my not asking. Now, with ten years' distance, I could admit the other possibility: that some silences aren't wounds. They're instructions. *Don't ask. Don't look. Don't be findable.* And I had followed those instructions so faithfully that I'd mistaken obedience for peace.

The storm broke apart as fast as it had come, the way summer storms do, trailing itself east in rags. By six the light came back long and gold across the water, and the river lay there looking innocent, which rivers always do after they've watched you learn something about yourself.

I lifted out of Miller's Slough into air scrubbed clean and glassy, and that's when I saw it.

Low water after a storm—the river had pulled back from its banks like a tide going out, and the gravel bar below Mile Seventy, the one that shifted every spring, had given up something new. From two hundred feet it was just a dark shape on pale stone, half-buried, maybe a log, maybe a drum.

Maybe nothing.

I should have flown on. Any sensible man flies on. The bar was fifteen miles short of the narrows, the light was going, and the letter in my flight bag wanted delivering, and none of that explains why I banked around and came down low over the water to look.

It wasn't a log.

* * *

I set the Goose down in the slack water off the bar with my heart doing something unprofessional, waded ashore, and dug. The chest was bronze-bound, oak beneath the bronze, heavy as sin and twice as hard to forgive, and stamped into the lid was a seal I recognized the way you recognize a face from a dream—some part of you insisting you've never seen it while another part, deeper and less cooperative, says *yes you have, you've seen it a hundred times, you grew up under it.*

A double-headed bird. Wings spread. Crowned in river pearls.

I stood knee-deep in the Kestrel with the evening coming on and my hands shaking, and I did not say the name. Saying the name would make it real, and I had built thirty-eight years on the foundation of certain things staying unreal.

But the seal already knew my hands, somehow. Or my hands knew the seal. When I gripped the handles to drag it toward the plane, they fit like they'd been fitted.

I got it aboard. Of course I got it aboard. Curiosity is just fear pointed in the wrong direction, and mine had been pointing the wrong way for a long time.

The last of the light went out over the western hills as I lifted off, and behind me the gravel bar lay empty again, the river already moving silt in to cover its tracks. Ahead, upriver, the narrows waited with their blank-map country beyond, and a letter in my bag addressed to a family named Wren.

I told myself I'd open the chest tomorrow, in daylight, like a rational man.

I told myself a lot of things. It was, as I said, my best skill.
