## 3. The Surveyor at Mile Nine

The man was waiting at Mile Nine when I came down through the rain squall, standing on the gravel spit with a canvas case at his feet and his hand raised in a gesture so precisely calibrated to wave down an aircraft that I knew before my floats touched the water that he'd done it before, and more than once, and probably to people who had no business stopping.

I almost didn't stop. That's the honest version. There's a version I tell at the Landing where I stopped because it was raining and a man alone on a gravel bar forty miles from anywhere is a drowning waiting for a headline, and there's some truth in that too. But the truth underneath both versions is simpler and less flattering: I stopped because he was standing next to the only landmark on that stretch of river, and the chest was lashed under my cargo net six feet behind my head, and I wanted to know if he'd seen me take it on.

You can't learn anything about a man by flying past him. You can learn quite a lot by letting him into your plane.

He came up the float strut like he'd been born on water, which surveyors generally aren't, and ducked into the cabin with a nod rather than a thank-you, which told me he considered the ride a transaction rather than a kindness. Medium height, mid-forties, gray going deliberate at the temples — the kind of gray a man maintains rather than earns. Wire-rimmed glasses. A canvas case that was too heavy for charts and too light for equipment, carried in his left hand while his right stayed free.

"Appreciate the lift," he said, settling into the passenger seat like he owned the lease on it. "Aldous Crane. Department of Lands and Water."

"Xanthe Okafor." I said it the way I always said it, flat and fast, a name tossed out like bait for pike — you throw it, you don't wait to see what takes it. "Where are we headed?"

"Corben's Landing. Eventually." He produced a leather folio and opened it across his knees. "I'm charting flood damage along the upper Kestrel. The storm two days ago moved more water than this river's seen in eleven years."

"That it did." I taxied out, ran up the engine, and took us off the bar into a sky the color of wet slate. Behind us Mile Nine shrank to a gray pencil stroke on a gray page.

For ten minutes he said nothing, which I appreciated, because it gave me time to study him in the rearview mirror I keep clipped to the strut wire — technically for watching cargo, practically for watching passengers, and every pilot who says otherwise is lying or rich. Crane watched the river the way an auditor watches a ledger. Not looking at it. Reading it. His eyes ticked from bank to bank, marking cutbanks and scour lines, and twice he made small notations in the folio without consulting any instrument I could see.

"You fly this stretch often?" he asked, finally, in the tone of a man opening a drawer to see what rattles.

"When there's reason to."

"And is there reason today?"

"Medicine run. Gossip run. Same thing, mostly." I banked around a deadfall the storm had left leaning over the channel like a drunk against a lamppost. "The Landing's short on both by Thursday."

"Mm." He wrote something down. I couldn't tell if it was about the deadfall or about me, and the not-knowing sat in my stomach like bad fuel.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about me, since we're apparently going to be spending a book together: I have built a life out of being forgettable. Not invisible — invisible takes work and attracts attention, same as anything else. Forgettable. There's a difference. Invisible men get remembered as mysteries. Forgettable men get remembered as weather. I fly medicine to settlements that don't ask where I trained, haul freight for people who don't ask why I live above my hangar, and pay my debts to Harlan Voss at the airfield exactly one day late every month, because a man who pays early looks like he's trying to impress someone and a man who pays late looks like he's failing, but a man who pays one day late, every month, like clockwork, just looks like a man with a system.

My mother taught me none of this. My mother taught me nothing, in the practical sense, because she died when I was nineteen and left me nothing but a duffel bag, a silence, and a last name she never once explained. Everything else I learned myself, and the first lesson was: a man with no history is a man nobody hunts. I have been no history for twenty-two years, and it has kept me alive, solvent, and hollow, in roughly that order of priority.

So when a government man starts asking precise questions in my cabin, I do what I've trained myself to do for two decades. I lie. And God forgive me, I'm good at it.

* * *

Crane waited until we were level over the long straight below the bend at Mile Twelve, engine humming its one good note, before he opened the real conversation. I felt it coming the way you feel a front coming — pressure first, then the question.

"Interesting season for salvage," he said.

"Is it?"

"The flood. Rivers give things back after a flood. Always have." He turned a page in his folio with one finger, unhurried. "Boats. Fences. Livestock, occasionally. Once, on the Alder, a church bell." He looked up at the mirror, found my eyes in it, and smiled with exactly the bottom half of his face. "You must find things."

"I find what the river leaves," I said. "Mostly it leaves mess."

"And when you find something of value? Something — significant?"

"Depends who lost it."

"Ah." He nodded, as if I'd passed a test, though I couldn't have told you what the passing answer was supposed to be. "And that's the whole of your understanding of salvage law? Depends who lost it?"

"It's served me fine."

"There's a statute," Crane said, conversational, terrible, "governing recovery of property from navigable waterways. Abandoned goods escheat to the Crown's administration after due notice. Goods with identifiable owners revert to those owners. Goods bearing the marks of a defunct sovereign entity—" he let the sentence sit there, ticking, like a bomb with good manners "—are a considerably more complicated matter."

The river ran on beneath us, brown and indifferent. My hands were steady on the yoke. Twenty-two years of practice will do that; the hands go steady even when everything behind them has caught fire.

"Complicated how?" I said, and heard my own voice do the thing it does — easy, faintly bored, a man discussing freight rates.

"Jurisdictional questions." He closed the folio. "Historical ones. Questions about whether a nation that no longer appears on maps can still own things." A pause, measured to the second. "Whether its seals still mean anything. Whether its heirs—"

"Turbine's running hot," I lied, and put the nose down toward the Landing.

* * *

I want to be clear about what happened in the next four minutes, because I've replayed them enough times to have opinions. I did not panic. Panic is loud and I am not. What I did was execute a maneuver I'd rehearsed in my head for years without ever expecting to use it: I became boring. I talked about turbine temperatures and carburetor ice and the price of avgas until the words themselves wore grooves of tedium, and somewhere in the fifth consecutive minute of fuel-flow figures, Aldous Crane stopped looking at me and started looking out the window, and whatever he'd been about to say about heirs went back into the folio with the rest of his instruments.

But here's the thing I hated, taxiing in at Corben's Landing with the rain starting again and the chest riding heavy behind my seat like a passenger with a ticket: I hadn't needed to think about any of it. The lies came out pre-loaded, smooth as piston rings. Turbine temperature — I don't even have gauges for that anymore, half of them burned out years ago. Carb ice in these conditions — marginal at best. I lied with the fluency of a man ordering coffee, and the part of me that noticed this, that stood off to the side watching myself do it with professional admiration and personal disgust, was the part my mother would have recognized.

She used to say I got the smooth tongue from her. She never said what she'd needed it for.

* * *

Crane paid for the ride in crisp bills counted out to the cent, which no honest traveler does, and then made his mistake. Or maybe it wasn't a mistake. Maybe it was a message. He stood on the dock while I tied down, and he looked past me — not at the plane, not at the town, but at the cargo hatch, at the netted shape under the tarp that any fool could see was heavier than mail — and he said:

"You'll be flying this stretch again soon, I expect."

"Whenever the river gives me reason."

"Yes," he said softly. "It does seem to be doing that lately." Then he picked up his case, nodded once, and walked up the dock into Corben's Landing like a man who knew exactly which door he was going to knock on, because he'd already decided.

I watched him go. Watched him stop at the foot of the archive steps — Miriam Sable's steps, the one building in town with a roof old enough to remember things — and look up at it the way you look at a house you've been sent to appraise.

Then I climbed into the cabin, put my hand flat on the tarp over the chest, and felt the bronze binding cold through the canvas, and understood that the quiet part of my life was over. I didn't know yet what had replaced it. But a man knows when he's been measured, the way livestock knows. Somewhere between Mile Nine and the Landing, Aldous Crane had taken my full dimensions, and somewhere upstream or downstream or in a file cabinet in the capital, there was now a page with my name on it — spelled correctly, which frightened me most of all, because I'd spent twenty-two years making sure nobody bothered to spell it right.

That night I didn't sleep. I sat in the hangar with a lamp and the letters, and by the third one I wasn't reading them anymore.

I was recognizing them.
