## 4. Letters in a Dead Hand

The lamp on my workbench has a cracked shade and a habit of buzzing like a trapped wasp, which is why I usually read by daylight or not at all. But it was past midnight, the storm had wrung itself out over the hills, and the hangar was so quiet I could hear the river deciding what to do with its new gravel bars.

I'd laid the letters out in a row on an oilcloth, oldest-looking first, the way you'd sort tools before a job you didn't want to start. Fourteen of them. Heavy paper gone soft at the edges, sealed with wax that had once been some kind of crest—a tower above waterlines, if I had to guess—and stamped with a word in a script I didn't know but that my eyes kept trying to pronounce anyway: *Vessania*.

Here's the thing about names from your childhood. They don't sit in memory like facts. They sit like splinters. You forget they're there until something presses on them.

Vessania was a country the way a ghost is a tenant. It had been on maps when I was six. By the time I was eight, it wasn't. No war anyone could point to, no treaty signed in front of cameras. Just—gone. Erased with the bureaucratic tidiness of a pencil line, replaced by a surveyor's note about *unincorporated territories* and *resettlement administration*. The adults in Corben's Landing talked about it the way you talk about a neighbor whose house burned down: carefully, briefly, and never twice.

My mother never talked about it at all.

She died when I was nineteen, in a hospital two hundred miles from this river, and the only instruction she left me was a lawyer's envelope containing a deed to a strip of riverside land nobody wanted and one sentence in her handwriting: *Keep flying, Xanthe. Don't go looking for anything.*

I had spent eleven years obeying her. It turned out to be the easiest obedience of my life, because I was good at not looking. Not looking was practically a trade skill. You learn it young when your mother flinches at certain words on the radio, when she burns papers in the woodstove and tells you it's just old receipts, when she teaches you three languages and forbids you to speak one of them.

That third language came back to me somewhere around letter number four.

* * *

*My dearest Anselm,*

*They have given us until the thaw. Father says the treasury must stay where it is—that a vault moved is a vault surrendered—but Mother is already sewing documents into our coats, and I have begun to understand that the men who argue loudest about staying are the men with boats ready.*

*I am fourteen and I have been asked to carry nothing, which means I will carry everything. The seal goes into the hem of my blue dress. If we are stopped, I am to be a child going to visit an aunt, and children are searched last, if at all.*

*I hate them all a little for making me complicit. I love them all entirely for trusting me. I don't suppose those two things can be separated; I suspect that is what a nation is.*

*—Amara*

Amara.

My mother's name was Amara Okafor. She'd married a geologist named Daniel Okafor, who taught me constellations and then left us the ordinary way, through death, and after that it was just the two of us and the river and her silences, stacked like cordwood against winter.

But Amara. Born Vessani. Fourteen years old with a national seal sewn into the hem of a blue dress.

I sat back on my stool and the stool creaked and the lamp buzzed and none of it felt real, which is how I knew it was. Real things feel unreal first. That's the body protecting you, buying time, the way a floatplane buys altitude before it commits to a turn.

I picked up letter five.

* * *

*Anselm,*

*We made the crossing. I will not write what the crossing cost; the ink would run and anyway there are things paper should not have to hold. Say instead: we made it, and the seal made it, and the ledger of holdings made it, and I made it, though "made" is a generous word for a girl who watched her city renamed from a hillside while soldiers burned the archives and called it sanitation.*

*Father is dead. He stayed with the vault, as he said he would. His last instruction to me was the same as his first: the treasury belongs to the people, not to the people who claim to speak for them. Remember the difference. He said most of history is forgetting the difference.*

*I am in the north settlement now. They call it nothing on any map. There are forty-one of us and a blacksmith and a schoolteacher who cries when she writes the alphabet, because every letter she teaches is a small act of treason.*

*I am learning to be invisible. It turns out I am good at it. This frightens me more than the soldiers did.*

*—A.*

Invisible. Good at it.

I looked down at my own hands, grease under the nails, scar across the left knuckle from a prop strike in my bush-pilot apprenticeship—the hands of a man who hauled medicine and gossip up a river and kept his head down and filed no flight plans he didn't have to and answered questions with weather talk. A man who had built a whole life out of being unremarkable, and had always assumed he'd chosen it, the way you choose a jacket.

Eleven years of exile, and I'd thought I was the author of it.

Turns out I'd inherited it. Like the land. Like the name. My mother hadn't raised me to be careful because caution was a virtue. She'd raised me to be careful because caution was camouflage, and somewhere out there, the people who erased a nation were still erasing, still tidy, still thorough—and the hem of a blue dress with a seal in it had passed, eventually, to me.

Or so the letters implied. I wasn't ready to believe implications yet. Implications are cheap. I wanted dates, names, something I could check against the world.

Letter nine gave me dates.

* * *

*Anselm,*

*The council met again tonight and again they spoke of the treasury as though it were a weapon. Open it, they say, and we can buy arms. Open it, they say, and we can buy recognition. Open it, they say, as if the vault were a door and not a grave with our fathers' signatures inside it.*

*I told them what Father told me. The treasury is proof, not purchase. The moment we spend it, we become exactly what they accused us of—a cabal of hoarders hiding gold from the lawful world. The moment we show it—all of it, the treaties, the censuses, the decree with its forged countersignature—we become something they cannot erase: documented.*

*They voted me down. Eleven to four. Elder Wren's son says I think like an archivist and not like a survivor, and perhaps he is right, but I have watched survivors, and what they survive into is often worse than what they survived.*

*I have decided to leave. I know what this makes me in their eyes. Let them think it. I will take the letters and the location and I will keep faith the only way I know how: alone, and stubbornly, and in writing.*

*If you receive these, Anselm, it means I trusted you with the one thing I never trusted the council with. Find the source. The river remembers what the maps were made to forget.*

*—Your Amara*

The river remembers what the maps were made to forget.

I read that line four times. Then I got up, because sitting still had become impossible, and walked out into the wet dark and stood on the dock looking upriver, toward the gorge, toward the country beyond the last charted bend where the Kestrel comes down off the plateau in waterfalls nobody has bothered to name because naming things invites ownership and ownership invites men with warrants.

The stars were out, hard and cold after the rain. Somewhere up there, according to a dead woman's letters, was a vault holding the complete record of a murdered country—and my mother had carried its location out of the fire in the hem of a child's dress, and had spent the rest of her life not telling her own son, and had died leaving him one sentence of instruction: *don't go looking.*

Why? That was the question that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Not *where*, not *what*—those answers were coming clear enough. Why the silence? What had she seen, in that council of forty-one souls and a crying schoolteacher, that made her decide her own boy was safer ignorant than informed?

I went back inside and finished the letters. All of them. Some were fragments—a page here, half a page there, one that was mostly burn marks with a few surviving lines about *the forged countersignature* and *Elder Wren's vote*. The last one was different. Shorter. Older handwriting, shakier, dated the year before she died.

* * *

*Anselm,*

*I am tired, and the tiredness is not of the body. For thirty years I have carried a country in a coat seam, and tonight I find I want to set it down, if only on paper.*

*I have a son. He flies. He laughs easily. He asks nothing about my past, and I have come to understand that this is not indifference—it is courtesy, the deepest kind, the kind that watches a woman burn receipts in a stove and pretends to believe her. He has been keeping my silence for me without ever being asked. I do not deserve him.*

*The chest is safe. The letters are safe. I have arranged for both to travel after me, slowly, the way cargo travels, so that no one watching will mark their passage. If you are reading this, the arrangement held, and my son has found what I could not bring myself to give him while I lived: a history.*

*Tell him—I cannot tell him, so tell him—his exile was my cowardice and my wisdom in equal parts, and I never learned which weighed more. Tell him the treasury is at the source, beneath the cape where the river meets the sea, and that opening it is a decision no one should make alone, least of all an Okafor.*

*Tell him his grandfather's name was Anselm too. Tell him he was named for a man who believed that proof is heavier than gold, and that heavy things are carried, not spent.*

*Tell him I loved him past the edge of sense, and that everything I did—including the silence, especially the silence—was done inside that love, even when it looked like abandonment from the outside.*

*Especially then.*

*—Amara*

I put that one down last, face-up, and I did not touch it again.

Then I did what any reasonable man does at three in the morning with the foundations of his life quietly removed: I made coffee on the hangar burner and sat with it and let the vertigo have its way. Because that's what it was—not grief yet, grief takes longer to arrive, it travels slow freight—but vertigo, the specific nausea of a man discovering the floor he's stood on for eleven years was a trapdoor someone else installed.

*Don't go looking for anything.* I'd taken it as a mother's quirk, a private superstition, filed it away with her burning of receipts and her flinch at certain radio words. Now it read differently. Now it read like a woman standing between her son and a fire, holding him back with one hand while the other hand arranged for the fire to reach him eventually anyway—after she was gone, when she couldn't watch, when she wouldn't have to see whether the inheritance saved him or killed him.

She couldn't decide. That was the truth of it, and I loved her for it and was furious at her for it, both at once, in the same breath, the way the letters themselves couldn't separate hating and loving. She couldn't decide whether the truth would free me or bury me, so she chose the only option available to a woman who couldn't decide: she delayed. She made the mail slow. She made the past travel like cargo.

And the past, being patient, had waited for low water and a storm and a gravel bar, and had let the river hand it to me personally.

I laughed. It came out wrong, more cough than laugh, and echoed in the empty hangar, and I decided I wasn't going to do that again.

Practical matters, then. Practical matters are how I pray.

Fourteen letters, plus whatever else was in the chest—I hadn't inventoried it properly, and a man who doesn't inventory doesn't survive long in this business. I spread everything out under the buzzing lamp: the letters; a folded map drawn on linen, hand-inked, showing the upper Kestrel with a stretch of the river labeled in that same unpronounceable script and a small tower symbol at the river's mouth marked with a tide table; a leather ledger, water-stained, its columns of figures headed by the word *TREZOR*; and a flat tin box, sealed, heavier than it looked, which I did not open. Not yet. Some doors you open one at a time.

The map I understood immediately, the way you understand a familiar face in an unfamiliar room. The source. The cape. My mother's geography, drawn by her father's hand, sitting in my hangar like a passenger who'd finally arrived after forty years of delays.

Which raised the question I'd been circling since letter one: who was Anselm, and why had my mother been writing to a man dead—or absent—for decades? Letters need recipients. These had been sealed, addressed, kept. Either she'd written them for herself, which the tone argued against, or there was a living thread I couldn't see yet, someone who'd held these, guarded them, and put them in a bronze-bound chest on a river that drowned things and occasionally gave them back.

And underneath that question, quieter, colder, the one I really didn't want: the chest hadn't been lost. Lost things don't get wrapped in oilcloth and weighted to sink shallow. Someone had put that chest in the river intending it to be found—someday, by someone—or intending it to be hidden, and had failed, and either way, somewhere upstream or downstream, there might be a person missing a chest.

Or a person glad one was missing.

I banked the lamp, wrapped the letters in the oilcloth, and stowed them in the plane's aft locker, behind the emergency kit, because the plane was the one place on my property I checked daily and knew intimately. Then I stood in the hangar doorway and watched the sky go grey at the edges and thought about Miriam Sable.

Miriam ran the archive in Corben's Landing—a converted chapel full of county records, shipping manifests, and the miscellaneous paper sediment of fifty years of river towns. She was seventy, sharp as a fishhook, and had once told me, apropos of nothing, that the most dangerous thing a government can do isn't burn a book, it's burn the *catalog*, because then no one knows what's missing.

I hadn't thought much about that remark at the time. I thought about it now, standing in the wet dawn with a nation's paperwork in my airplane.

Because if a country could be erased once—decreed away, archived into ash, struck from maps with bureaucratic tidiness—then somebody, somewhere, had done the striking. And people who strike countries don't retire. They delegate. They file. They wait, the way the past waits, for low water.

First light. Time to fly. I had medicine for the trading post at Mile Twelve, gossip for everyone, and a debt to Dex Harrow at the airfield that I intended to keep dodging on principle.

And I had a stop to make in Corben's Landing, where a seventy-year-old archivist was going to earn her reputation, because I needed to know one thing before I decided what kind of fool to be:

Whether the name Vessania still scared anybody besides me.

I fired up the engine and the floats slapped the current and the river took me, and somewhere behind me in the aft locker, my mother's voice rode in the dark, thirty years late, telling me the one thing she'd never told me herself:

*Find the source.*
