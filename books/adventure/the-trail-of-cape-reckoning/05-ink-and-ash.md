## 5. Ink and Ash

*Miriam Sable had kept the archive of Corben's Landing for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned three things: that people lie most fluently about their own names; that water destroys paper slower than fire but more thoroughly; and that when a man walks into an archive on a Tuesday morning asking careful questions, he is never a tourist.*

*The surveyor had come the previous Thursday. He'd worn his government lanyard like a costume he hadn't yet grown into, and he'd asked about flood-damage records for the upper Kestrel — a reasonable request, delivered with unreasonable precision. He wanted dates. He wanted chain of custody. He wanted to know who else had consulted the dissolution-era holdings, as if the holdings were a patient and he was tracing an infection.*

*She had shown him nothing. She had smiled her librarian's smile, the one that says* I am happy to help you *and means* I have already decided what you will find here, which is nothing. *He'd left a card. The card was still in her drawer, under the paperknife, where she kept things she intended to think about twice.*

*And now this morning there was a second man at her door — younger, river-worn, with a floatplane pilot's squint and a canvas satchel held against his hip like something he'd rather not be seen carrying. He said his name was Xanthe Okafor.*

*Miriam Sable did not believe in omens. But she believed in filing systems, and somewhere in the deep stacks, in a box marked only with a faded V, there was a folder she had promised a dying woman she would keep until someone came asking. She had assumed no one ever would.*

*"Come in," she said, "before you're seen," and locked the door behind him.*

* * *

The thing about small towns is that they are excellent at two things: forgetting, and pretending to forget. Corben's Landing had been pretending for forty years, and it had gotten very good at it. The courthouse had a plaque about the timber boom. The museum had a case of arrowheads and one sad paddlewheel. Nowhere — not on any wall or monument or drunk old man's tongue — did the word Vessania appear.

I'd grown up downriver from that silence. My mother had taught me letters in a language she told me was "just something her grandmother liked," songs she called "nothing, hush, sleep now." I'd thought it was eccentricity. It turns out it was camouflage.

Miriam Sable's archive sat above the hardware store, up a staircase that complained in four dialects, behind a door with a brass plate so tarnished you could read it only by wanting to. Inside: the smell every real archive has, dust and glue and cold iron, and a woman in her sixties with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair, looking at me like I was a document she'd already catalogued.

"Okafor," she said.

"That's me."

"I know. Sit down." She nodded at a chair positioned, I noticed, where she could see both me and the window. "You've brought something you don't want to show me, and you want me to tell you what it is before you decide whether to show me. Am I close?"

"You could give tours."

"I do, Thursdays." She took off her glasses. "Show me."

So I showed her. One letter only — I'd kept the rest in the plane, because I'm paranoid, not stupid, though Miriam would later argue those aren't mutually exclusive. The envelope was heavy cream stock gone tea-brown at the edges, and pressed into the wax was a seal: a tower above three waves, ringed by words in that alphabet my mother used to write in the margins of my schoolbooks.

Miriam looked at it for a long time. Long enough that I heard the hardware store open below us, the bell over its door, somebody buying something ordinary.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get this?"

"River gave it back. Gravel bar below the Mile Twelve shallows, after the storm." I shrugged, which is a liar's gesture and I knew it even as I made it. "Low water. Bronze bands, cedar chest, no markings except that."

"No markings except that," she repeated. "Mr. Okafor. That seal is the Great Seal of the Provisional Government of Vessania. There are perhaps nine of them in existence that anyone admits to. And you found one floating down a river in a cedar chest."

"The river's done stranger favors."

"Has it." She set the letter down with the care people reserve for infants and explosives. "Do you know what happened to Vessania?"

"They told us it didn't exist."

"It didn't. That was official." She stood, went to the window, checked the street with a glance that was quick and total, a woman who'd been checking windows longer than I'd been alive. Then she disappeared into the stacks, and I heard boxes moving, and she came back with a ledger bound in green cloth gone soft as felt.

"Vessania was a river nation," she said, sitting again. "Chartered 1891. Population, at its height, eleven thousand along the upper Kestrel and the tributaries. Timber, tin, a mint that printed its own currency — good currency, better than the provincial scrip, which was half the problem. Forty-one years ago the territorial government issued an Emergency Dissolution Decree. Financial irregularities, they claimed. Sedition, some said. The decree dissolved the charter, voided the citizenship of every soul in the territory, and ordered the national records seized and burned."

"Burned."

"All of them. Registries, treaties, land grants, censuses. A nation isn't just people, Mr. Okafor. It's proof. Burn the proof and the people become residents of nowhere, with no claim on anything. The resettlement program moved them downriver, scattered them, reissued their papers under new names. Within five years there was no such place. Within ten, there was no such word." She tapped the green ledger. "Except."

"Except."

"Except that governments burn records, but families keep them. Baptismal certificates sewn into coat linings. Land grants rolled inside fishing rods. Photographs, diaries, seals. Whatever a family could carry out ahead of the soldiers, they carried. My own grandmother carried a hymnal with the census pages for two whole villages pasted between the psalms." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I am, technically, Vessani. So are maybe four hundred people in this valley, and none of them will say so out loud, and I include myself in that count on most days."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you walked in here carrying a seal, and because your face is doing something complicated whenever I say the word 'census,' and because—" she paused, and chose her next words like a woman crossing ice, "—because forty-one years ago, a young woman came through this town heading downriver with a leather case she never let out of her hands. Dark-skinned, river-tired, frightened in a way that made everyone around her pretend not to notice. She stayed one night. She asked me — I was a girl then, minding the counter for my father — whether the ferry ran on Sundays. I said yes. She gave me a coin with a tower on it and told me that if anyone ever came asking after the Sable family's papers, I should remember that kindness runs both directions."

The room got very quiet. The bell downstairs rang again. Somebody laughed on the street, far away, in another country.

"My mother," I said.

"Amara Okafor," said Miriam. "Born Amara Vessani-Wren, of the Wren line, New Vessa settlement, heir presumptive to offices I won't name in daylight. Yes. Your mother."

I want to tell you I took it well. I want to tell you my hands were steady and my voice was level and I asked intelligent follow-up questions. What actually happened is that I looked at the wax seal until it blurred, and thought about a woman singing nonsense syllables over a crib, and understood — all at once, like a float dropping through fog into rock — that none of it had been nonsense, and none of it had been for me, and she had spent her whole life downstream of a burning building passing herself off as smoke.

"She never said."

"They never do," Miriam said gently. "Saying it is how people get erased harder."

"And the chest? The letters?"

"Then I can't help you, because I haven't seen them." She folded her hands. "But I can tell you what the decree was, because I've read the surviving copy, and the surviving copy has a problem. The Emergency Dissolution Decree required countersignature by the Vessani Council of Kept Things — that's their governing body, the elders, the keepers of the seal. The version in the territorial archive bears that countersignature. The version my grandmother smuggled out does not. Same date. Different ink. One of them is forged, Mr. Okafor, and the forgery is the one the whole world believes."

"So the dissolution was illegal."

"So the dissolution was *invented*. Which means every resettlement, every seized grant, every burned record rests on a document nobody actually signed." She leaned forward. "You understand why a chest full of sealed correspondence bearing the Great Seal might make certain people extremely interested in you."

"I'm starting to."

"Good. Because here's the part you're not going to like." She reached into her desk drawer, took out a card, and laid it on the green ledger between us. White card, embossed lettering, a government crest. *A. Crane, Surveyor, Territorial Waterways Commission.*

"He was here Thursday. Asking about flood damage to the upper Kestrel holdings. Very thorough. Very polite. Wanted to know about salvage claims, chain of custody, consultation records." She looked at me over her glasses. "Surveyors don't ask about consultation records, Mr. Okafor. Archivists ask about consultation records. He also asked — casually, the way men ask when they've already been told the answer — whether anyone had recently come in asking about Vessani materials."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him our Vessani holdings were destroyed in the burning, same as everywhere." She smiled her librarian's smile, and for the first time I saw the steel under it. "Which is true of everything he'll ever be permitted to see. But he left this card, and he wrote something on the back before he left, and I want you to see it, because I think it was meant to be found."

She turned the card over. In small, precise, drafting-room handwriting:

*Some rivers give back more than water. Some debts compound. — A.C.*

I stared at it. "That's not a surveyor."

"No," Miriam said. "It isn't. And whoever he works for has been asking about Vessania in every town on this river for three weeks, and last month a dealer in the capital offered my cousin absurd money for anything with the tower seal on it, and two weeks ago a man I didn't recognize photographed the outside of this building for eleven minutes on a Tuesday." She took the card back, returned it to the drawer, and closed it softly. "The past has living enemies, Mr. Okafor. They were patient for forty years. Something has made them impatient."

Outside, the hardware store bell rang, and boots started up the stairs — heavy ones, unhurried, the tread of a man who had never once needed to hurry in his life.

Miriam's eyes went to mine. Then to the letter. Then to the back window, which I now noticed had a rope and a pulley and a drop onto the alley roof of the feed store, installed, I suspected, long before I was born.

"Back door," she murmured. "Now. Take your letter. Come back Thursday, after hours, and bring everything — and Mr. Okafor?" 

I was already moving.

"Whoever finds that chest next," she said, "shouldn't be him."
