## 6. Crane's Ledger

I keep my logbook the way some men keep their sins: in a locked drawer, under a false bottom, written in a hand that isn't quite mine.

It's not that the entries are lies. Every hour in the air, every landing on every gravel bar from Corben's Landing to the Kestrel's mouth, is recorded with the dull fidelity the aviation board demands. What's false is the man the entries describe. The Xanthe Okafor of my logbook is a ferry pilot with no history, no debts worth naming, and no mother worth mourning. He hauls medicine and gossip and the occasional crate of contraband coffee, and he asks no questions, because questions leave ink trails, and ink trails get followed.

So when I came back to my hangar at Mile Nine that evening and found the drawer's false bottom sitting two millimeters proud of true, I did not reach for the shotgun over the door.

I reached for the light switch, which I'd wired to the hangar's exterior floods years ago, and I turned the night white.

* * *

Crane stood at my workbench with my logbook open in one hand and a small camera in the other, the kind with the bellows and the quiet shutter, the kind a man buys when he wants to steal paper without leaving fingerprints. The flash had already fired at least twice; the air still smelled faintly of it, that burnt-sugar tang of magnesium doing its dishonest work.

He didn't flinch when the floods came on. I'll give him that. Most men caught in another man's hangar at night with another man's property in their hands perform some small theater of surprise—a stumble, a raised palm, a laugh. Crane simply lowered the logbook to the bench, squared its corners with the edge, and looked at me the way a bank clerk looks at a signature he's already decided to question.

"Evening, Okafor," he said. "Nice wiring. Most pilots run their floods off a switch by the door."

"I like to see what I'm shooting at."

"A sensible habit in this country." He set the camera down beside the logbook, deliberate as a man laying down cards he intends to pick up again. "I hope you don't mind. I came by to confirm our flight plan for tomorrow and found the door unlatched. I was concerned about security. There's been a lot of flood damage in the district. Opportunists."

"The door was latched."

"Then I'm pleased to hear it," he said, "because that means whoever latched it did so *after* I arrived, which suggests you were watching me for some time before you chose to make yourself known. Which is itself interesting."

I'll be honest with you, since the logbook can't be: I had been watching him for eleven minutes. I'd seen him try the drawer, find it locked, and go at it with something slim and bright from his breast pocket, working the lock the way a surgeon works a stitch, patient and unhurried, like a man who'd done it before and expected to do it again. I'd watched him give up on the lock and simply lift the whole false bottom out of the drawer, because the false bottom was mine and mine were never as good as I told myself they were.

I'd watched all of it from the shadow of the fuel drums with the shotgun's cold weight against my palm, and I'd thought: *here it is. Here's the moment the invisible man becomes a target.*

And then I'd put the shotgun down, because a dead surveyor raises more questions than a live one, and questions were the one cargo I'd spent forty years refusing to haul.

"Crane," I said, "you're a long way from flood lines."

"Floods," he said, "get everywhere. That's rather their nature." He turned the logbook toward me with one finger, open to the page from three days ago. My own handwriting looked back at me, small and careful: *Chest, bronze-bound, salvaged gravel bar below Mile Four. Contents: personal papers. Held pending owner inquiry.* The entry I'd made at two in the morning because some stubborn clerk in my spine insisted the truth, even a shaped truth, was safer than no truth at all.

"Personal papers," Crane read aloud. "Pending owner inquiry. You're a scrupulous man, Okafor. Most salvagers would have said *contents unknown* and left it at that."

"Most salvagers don't write their own logbooks in a hand that isn't quite theirs."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The place where a smile would go if he ever authorized one. "No," he said. "I imagine they don't."

* * *

He made us coffee, which was the most menacing thing he could have done. There's a tin of grounds and a burner in my hangar for cold mornings, and Crane found both without being told, and while the water came up he talked about salvage law the way other men talk about weather—fluently, idly, with the certainty of a man reciting scripture he wrote himself.

"Admiralty jurisdiction runs to the high-water mark and a bit beyond," he said, pouring. "River salvage is provincial. Provincial salvage is a finder's-keepings affair, *except* where the goods bear the marks of a sovereign entity, in which case they revert to the Crown's successor-in-interest, and the finder is entitled to a salvage award of—well, it varies. Ten percent is customary. Fifteen for hazardous recovery." He handed me the cup. "Bronze-bound chests recovered from the Kestrel, bearing the seal of a dissolved state, would fall under the second category. The finder's award would be substantial."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course not." He sipped his own coffee and looked at the chest where it sat under a tarp in the corner, because I hadn't moved it, because I'd told myself the tarp was enough, because a man who has spent his whole life being invisible starts to believe the world has agreed to the arrangement. "You don't know what I'm talking about. You're a ferry pilot. You haul medicine and gossip. The chest is a rumor you started yourself in your own logbook for no reason whatsoever."

"Crane—"

"Two thousand," he said.

I stopped.

"Two thousand, cash, tonight, and I've never heard of a chest, and neither have you. The entry in your logbook becomes a clerical error. Clerical errors are forgiven all the time in this district. I'd see to it personally." He set his cup on the bench. "Or we can continue this conversation at the rate of five thousand, which is what I'm authorized to go to, and which I'd rather not spend, because authorization leaves paper trails, and I dislike paper trails almost as much as you do."

The hangar was very quiet. Outside, the Kestrel ran low and clear over its gravel bars, the storm's leavings, and somewhere downriver a loon was making its idiot complaint, and I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Two thousand dollars was a year of flying. It was the debt to Harrow at the airfield, paid, with the interest and the insult. It was new floats for the old girl, whose left one had been drinking river water all season like a sailor on leave. It was freedom, of the specific, purchased kind I'd told myself I didn't want.

It was also the price of my mother's handwriting, sold by weight.

"Who sent you?" I asked.

"The Crown's successor-in-interest," Crane said, "doesn't discuss its correspondence."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's better than one. An answer can be checked."

I looked at him across the workbench, at this precise, patient man in his government khaki with his bellows camera and his scripture of salvage law, and I understood two things at once, the way you understand a storm when you see the anvil cloud stacking up over a clear horizon. First: he wasn't a surveyor. Surveyors don't carry authorization limits. Surveyors don't work locks with a slim bright tool and photograph other men's logbooks by magnesium light. Second, and worse: whatever he was, he wasn't finished, and the money was the polite part of the interview.

"Here's my problem, Okafor," he went on, before I could answer, in the tone of a man confiding a burden to a friend. "I report that the chest exists and is in your possession, or I report that it doesn't. If I report that it exists, then the machinery starts. Warrants. Inspections. Seizure orders signed by men in offices who've never smelled the Kestrel. Your aircraft would be impounded as evidence—did you know that? Instrumentality of a salvage violation. You'd walk, probably. The finder's-keepings law is forgiving. But your certificate of airworthiness would be suspended pending review, and your commercial license reviewed in turn, and reviews of that kind take"—he consulted some inner ledger—"eighteen months, in a good year. In a year with an election, longer."

"And if you report it doesn't exist," I said.

"Then it doesn't," he said simply, "unless someone else finds it. And someone else is looking. I'm not the only letter the machinery sent up this river, Okafor. I'm just the one who prefers to do his work with a checkbook instead of a crowbar. The others—" He shrugged, a small economical shrug. "The others are why the archive at Corben's Landing has been visited twice this month by men who claimed to be from the provincial records office and whose boots were wrong. Government archivists wear government boots. These men wore river boots. River boots are for men who expect to run."

The coffee had gone cold in my hand. I set it down next to his, and the two cups sat there like a pair of witnesses.

"You're threatening my certificate," I said.

"I'm describing the administrative process," Crane said. "Threats are for the men in river boots. I'm offering you the only civilized exit from a situation you created when you wrote *bronze-bound* in your own logbook like an honest man. Honesty, Okafor, is a luxury of the unobserved. You've been observed now. The question is only what the observation costs."

* * *

Here's the thing about a life built on invisibility: it works fine right up until the moment someone shines a light on it, and then every shadow you ever stood in becomes a place you can be found.

I thought about my mother that night, standing in the floodlight's spill with a government man's offer cooling between us. I thought about how she never told me where she came from, how she'd answer questions about her childhood with weather—*it rained a lot where I was born, it was green, the water was loud*—and how I'd taken that silence for shame, or grief, or the ordinary amnesia of people who emigrate. I'd built my whole self in the space she left empty. No history. No debts worth naming. A man with a mother from nowhere, flying a plane registered to nobody in particular, hauling other people's medicine up a river that forgot things on purpose.

She hadn't been silent because she was ashamed. I knew that now, from the letters, from the seal on the wax, from the word *treasury* in her careful hand. She'd been silent the way you're silent when the thing you know can get people killed. She'd been invisible on purpose, and she'd taught me the trick without ever saying a word about it, the way you teach a child to swim by carrying him in the water until one day he's doing it himself.

And here was a man in my hangar offering to buy the trick's ending for two thousand dollars.

"Crane," I said. "Let me tell you what I know about salvage law, since you're so fond of it."

"Please."

"Nothing." I picked up my logbook and closed it. "I know nothing about salvage law. I know about weather, and loading weights, and which gravel bars lie about their depth. And I know that a man who works locks with a slim bright tool and photographs other men's handwriting isn't buying a chest. He's buying the *absence* of a chest. And governments don't pay two thousand dollars for the absence of a thing unless the thing is worth a great deal more, and things are only worth a great deal more when they're dangerous, and papers are only dangerous when they're true."

The place where his smile would go moved again. "You should write pamphlets."

"I haul medicine and gossip," I said. "That's what my logbook says."

"Your logbook," Crane said, "also says *pending owner inquiry.* Has there been an inquiry?"

"Not yet."

"Then the entry remains accurate, and I remain a man who found nothing." He gathered his camera, his hat, his slim bright tool, and paused at the hangar door with the floodlight making a hard white edge of him. "You have until I finish my survey of the upper gorge, Okafor. A week, perhaps ten days. After that, my report writes itself, with or without your cooperation. The men in river boots will not offer you a checkbook." He put on his hat. "Consider the arithmetic. Duty is a fine word. So is survival. Most men find they can only afford one."

He stepped out into the dark, and the night took him the way the river takes a dropped oar—without a sound, without a ripple, as if he'd never been there at all.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the hangar with the floods off and the chest under its tarp and my mother's letters in their oilskin wrap, and I did the arithmetic he'd assigned me, honestly, the way I do everything in that logbook.

Duty: the letters named a treasury at the river's source. The letters were written by a woman who'd died keeping their secret, and somewhere upriver, if the seal on the wax meant anything, there might be people who'd spent forty years keeping faith with her. Surrendering the chest bought me a certificate, a license, a year of invisibility—and bought them nothing but the machinery's patience, and the machinery, by Crane's own account, had already sent men in river boots.

Survival: two thousand dollars, a dry hangar, a left float that didn't drink. A life with the lights off.

I'd been living with the lights off for forty years. I could tell you it was peaceful. I could tell you it was free. What it was, was small, and it had a false bottom, and a man with a bellows camera had just photographed the whole arrangement in two flashes of magnesium light.

The trouble with invisibility, I thought, is that it's a debt like any other. You can carry it a long time. But it compounds.

Toward dawn I took the letters out of the chest, wrapped them fresh, and looked at the fuel tanks of the old girl sitting on her mooring in the grey first light, and at the false tank I'd built into her belly years ago for runs I don't log, and I understood that I'd already made the choice somewhere between Crane's checkbook and his arithmetic, and that the man in the logbook—the ferry pilot with no history—was about to be retired.

I just needed to know how to get upriver before the machinery did.

And there was exactly one person on the Kestrel who'd run cargo for people like my mother, who owed her a debt, and who owned a boat slow enough and ugly enough and armored enough to survive what was coming.

I filed nothing. I told no one. I went to find Ilsa Reyes.
