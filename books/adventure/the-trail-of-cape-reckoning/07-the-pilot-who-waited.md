## 7. The Pilot Who Waited

I have been called many things in forty years of river work—captain, smuggler, widow, fool—and the only one that ever fit was *waiter*. Not the kind who brings you your dinner. The kind who sits at a dock with her hands folded and watches the water for somebody who said they'd come back.

Selene Okafor was supposed to come back.

That's the debt, if you want it plain. She came downriver one October night with eleven families packed into my hold like wet firewood, paid me in coin that had no country anymore, and asked me to remember her face so that if anyone ever came asking after her, I'd know whether to lie or not. I told her I'd remember. She laughed and said that wasn't what she meant. She meant: remember me well enough to know when someone is wearing my name like a stolen coat.

Then she went upriver toward whatever she was running from, and the river kept her, the way it keeps everything eventually, and I stayed at my dock in Corben's Landing being the pilot who waited.

So when a floatplane came chugging up the channel on a gray Tuesday morning, too slow and too careful, flown by a man who circled my mooring twice before committing to a landing—I knew whose son he was before his wheels touched water. He had her way of looking at a place like he was asking its permission.

* * *

I was mending net on the deck of the *Stubborn Magdalene* when he climbed aboard without asking, which I'll admit scored him points. A man who waits to be invited onto a barge is a man who can be kept waiting forever.

"You're Ilsa Reyes," he said.

"Last time I checked, which was this morning, in a mirror that flatters nobody."

"Xanthe Okafor."

"I know." I set down the netting needle and looked at him properly. Forty years will do things to a face, but not all of them are insults. Selene had gone into the ground at what, sixty? And here was her jaw, her stubborn brow, her habit of standing with his weight on his heels like a man ready to leave any room he's in. "You fly like her too," I said. "Like you're apologizing to the air."

Something moved behind his eyes—surprise, then grief, then the quick reflexive smoothing-over that children of secrets learn before they learn their times tables. "You knew my mother."

"I carried her once. Eleven families, one night, no lights, no questions. That was the arrangement. No questions." I picked the needle back up. "So ask yourself why you're breaking it."

He didn't flinch. Points again. "Because the questions stopped being safe for other people about four days ago. Because a government surveyor named Crane has been asking them instead, and he's better at it than I am. Because there's a chest on my floor with a seal on it that hasn't legally existed since I was six years old, and letters inside it in my mother's handwriting that say—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "That say things I need to take upstream. To wherever she came from. If it still exists."

"And you came to me because?"

"Miriam Sable said you were the only captain alive who ever ran the upper gorge with cargo that mattered."

"Miriam Sable," I said, "talks too much for an archivist. Archivists should be like oysters. Everything goes in, nothing comes out until somebody pries."

"She also said you owed my mother a debt."

I put the needle down for good this time. "Sit down, boy, before you fall down. You look like a man who hasn't slept since the storm."

He sat on my cargo crate like it might be wired, which showed sense. The *Magdalene* has been wired twice in her life, both times by people who regretted it.

"Here's what I owe her," I said. "Not money. Money's easy; money gets spent and forgotten. What I owe her is a night in 1989 when I told her I'd know her face again, and I've spent forty years keeping that promise to a dead woman, watching every dock, every passenger manifest, every young man with her chin, waiting. Do you understand what that does to a person? It makes them a waiter. I retired from the river and forgot to retire from the vigil." I leaned forward. "So yes. I owe her. And now you've come along and paid yourself out of my account without even knowing it, and I resent that deeply, and I'm going to help you anyway, because that's how debts work when they're real ones."

He blinked. "That's the most roundabout yes I've ever heard."

"I'm seventy-one years old. Roundabout is the only road I travel straight."

* * *

We drank coffee that could have stripped paint off a gunboat, and he told me the shape of it—the chest, the seal, the letters, Crane with his precise little questions about salvage law, the logbook photographed, the threat dressed up as paperwork. I listened the way I used to listen to engines: not to the words but to the rhythm underneath, where the trouble lives.

"You're going to fly it up," I said, when he finished. "In that tin duckling of yours."

"It's the fastest way to the source."

"The fastest way to the source is also the most visible way to the source. That plane makes noise for ten miles in every direction, and the upper gorge has exactly one flight corridor, which means anyone watching—which Crane will be, the moment you're missed—knows precisely where you are at all times. You'd be a cork floating down a rifle barrel." I tapped the deck with my boot heel. "Whereas the *Magdalene* here looks like exactly what she appears to be: a slow, ugly supply barge hauling diesel and flour, same as forty other barges on this river. Nobody photographs a barge. Barges are furniture."

"She's also," he said, glancing around, "armored."

"She's *stubborn*. There's a difference. Armor is plate steel bolted on by optimists. Stubbornness is double-hulled oak, iron-banded, built by my late husband for running cargo through places where cargo isn't welcome, and maintained since by a woman who had nothing else to do with her grief." I let that sit a moment. "The plating came later. Grief accumulates."

"Can she make the gorge?"

"She made it eleven times with refugees in the hold and soldiers on the banks. She can make it with one anxious heir and a chest full of paper." I stood, joints announcing themselves as usual. "But we do it my way. My boat, my rules, my schedule. First rule: we don't go tomorrow. We go the day after, under a fuel run, bills of lading for Corben's Landing and two stops above, boring as communion wafers. Second rule: the chest rides in the hold under the diesel drums, and the letters ride somewhere else entirely, because a smart runner never keeps the treasure and the proof in the same place. Third rule—"

"You're the captain," he said. "I remember. My mother mentioned you had rules."

"My mother," I said sharply, "your mother had the audacity to call me *complicated*, which from her was a compliment, and I've been living down to it ever since." Then I caught myself using the present tense and had to stand there a second with my mouth open while forty years rearranged themselves. "Was. Had. Damn it."

He didn't say anything. That was right, too. There's nothing to say to a person mid-slip, and everything to say afterward, and none of it words. He just reached over and steadied the coffee mug my hand had started to betray, and held it until my hand remembered itself.

"You really loved her," he said quietly. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite anything else either. It was the thing people say when they've found the edge of a country they didn't know was there.

"I loved her the way you love weather," I said. "From a distance, with respect, knowing it'll kill you someday and hoping it's worth it." I took the mug back. "It was worth it. Now get off my boat and come back day after tomorrow at four in the morning, and bring that chest, and don't be followed, and don't tell Miriam Sable the schedule, because that woman means well and meaning well has sunk more ships than rocks."

* * *

He was back in ninety minutes. I knew it before I saw him, the way you know rain coming—something in the pressure of the afternoon changed. I was below, checking the bilge pumps, and when I came up the ladder he was standing at my gangway talking to a man in a canvas jacket who had the particular loose-shouldered posture of a fellow who carries a sidearm the way other men carry a fountain pen.

Crane. It had to be. Surveyors don't visit retired captains; governments don't chart flood damage by interviewing barges. But there he was, smiling his file-folder smile, and there was Xanthe, wearing the smooth liar's face I'd already learned to recognize on him—the face that said *nothing here, move along* while the eyes did arithmetic.

I stayed in the shadow of the wheelhouse and listened.

"—just routine, Mr. Okafor. We're cataloguing all commercial traffic above Mile Nine this season. Insurance liability, you understand."

"Of course."

"And this vessel? Registered owner..."

"Reyes. Ilsa Reyes. Retired. She hauls supplies when she feels like it, which is rarely. I was asking about chartering her for a fuel run, but honestly she's half mad and the boat's older than both of us combined. I'll probably just fly it."

"A fuel run," Crane said, tasting each word like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "Upstream?"

"Downstream, mostly. Fuel's cheaper at the depot."

"Mm." The smile widened by a millimeter, which on that face was a shout. "Well. If you do decide to go up, Mr. Okafor, do file a flight plan. The gorges are unforgiving this time of year. And the department would hate to lose track of you."

"I'd hate that too," Xanthe said, and shook the man's hand like they were old friends at a funeral, and Crane walked off down the dock with his loose shoulders and his pen-gun, and I came out of the wheelhouse.

"He knows," I said.

"He suspects. Knowing comes later, with paperwork." Xanthe exhaled through his nose, long and controlled. "He followed me here. Or had me followed. Either way, our four a.m. departure is now a performance."

"Good," I said.

He stared at me. "Good?"

"Boy, I ran refugees past checkpoints for six years. Do you think I ever once left port when I said I would? The announced departure is the decoy. We'll load tomorrow night, quiet, and slip out on the pre-dawn dark two days early, running black, no lights, no radio, on the ebb. Let him watch this dock on Thursday morning. By then we'll be past the narrows." I looked at the sky, judging the moon the way other women judge a suitor. "Which reminds me. Rule four: from tonight until we're above the falls, you're not Xanthe Okafor, pilot. You're my nephew, Tomas Junior, helping your aunt with a run because her crew quit. Nephews don't argue with aunts. Practice looking dumb."

"I have experience being invisible," he said, and there was something under the wryness that hurt to hear—a man describing his own life the way you'd describe a scar.

"Not anymore, you don't," I said. "Invisible was your mother's strategy and look where it got her. Dead in a valley with a secret, and her son raised on the silence like it was milk. This trip, you're visible. Loudly, stubbornly, inconveniently visible. That's the whole point of what we're carrying." I jerked my chin at the gangway. "Now go home, pack like a nephew, and burn anything with your mother's name on it that you don't want Crane reading over your shoulder. And Okafor—"

He turned at the bottom of the gangway.

"When we make the gorge, and the *Magdalene* starts talking to the rocks, and you're certain we're going to die—remember that she's made this run eleven times, and I've made it thirteen, and neither of us has lost a passenger yet." I smiled, and I felt it go crooked the way smiles do when they're carrying weight. "Fourteen's always the hard one."

He almost smiled back. Almost. Then his face did the thing I'd seen it do twice already—went somewhere else, some private room behind the eyes where he kept the letters and the seal and the mother-shaped hole—and he nodded and walked up the dock toward town, toward whatever he had to burn.

I watched him go. Then I went below, pulled the false panel behind the water tank, and took out the thing I'd kept there for forty years without quite admitting why: a Vessani signal pennant, blue and bone-white, folded small enough to hide in a fist. Ilsa Reyes, retired, waiting at her dock.

I spread it flat on the chart table and smoothed the creases with my palm.

Day after tomorrow, I thought—or tomorrow night, if the moon cooperated—we'd find out whether the people upstream still remembered what it meant.

And whether they remembered the name Okafor.
