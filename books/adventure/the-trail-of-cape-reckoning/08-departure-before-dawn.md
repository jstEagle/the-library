## 8. Departure Before Dawn

There is a particular hour on the Kestrel River when the dark stops pretending to be night and starts rehearsing for morning. The birds haven't committed yet. The mist lies on the water like something that died of boredom. And a man loading a barge under a lie has the whole gray theater to himself.

I'd told Ilsa I'd be at her berth at four. I'd told the airfield owner, Gus Ferro, that I was running a supply charter downriver—flour, diesel, spare parts, nothing worth remembering. Lies, both of them, though only one was dangerous. Ilsa knew what we were really doing. Gus believed what he'd been paid to believe, which is the most reliable kind of belief there is. The lie that worried me was the one I was telling myself: that I could still walk away. That if the letters turned out to be nothing but a dead woman's sentiment sealed in wax, I could hand the chest to the river and go back to hauling insulin and gossip up a river that had never once asked me who I was.

The letters were not nothing. I'd spent two nights with them, and by the second night my hands had stopped shaking and started doing arithmetic instead. My mother had been born Vessani. My mother had been an *heir*. And somewhere upriver, at the source the maps refused to name, there was a treasury with her name on it—and, apparently, forty years of somebody's patience waiting to collect the debt in the other direction.

But all of that was for later. Right then, at three in the morning, my job was simpler: load Ilsa's barge with enough legitimate cargo to survive a customs glance, and get out before the river woke up and started talking.

* * *

Ilsa's barge was called the *Marta B*, after a woman she refused to discuss, and she was moored in the backwater below Corben's Landing where the water moved so slowly it seemed to be thinking. She was an ugly, honest vessel—steel-hulled, diesel-powered, armored in the piecemeal way of a woman who'd run cargo through three different wars and trusted none of the peace treaties that ended them. There was plate welded over her wheelhouse windows that could be swung open in fair weather and slammed shut in the other kind. Ilsa had the other kind in mind. She just hadn't said so out loud yet.

I came down the bank with a hand truck and the first of the crates, and I stopped.

The hangar light was on.

Now, I want to be fair to myself here: I am not a jumpy man. You don't survive twenty years of bush flying by spooking at every crow that flaps off a sandbar. But that hangar light was wrong the way a smile is wrong on a man who isn't laughing. I'd shut it off when I left. I always shut it off. Electricity on the Kestrel costs more than conscience, and I economized on both.

I set the hand truck down in the grass and stood in the dark and listened. The river said nothing, being a river. Somewhere a loon made its idiot complaint. The hangar sat at the top of the bank, a corrugated tin box with my whole working life folded inside it—the *Kestrel Queen*, my floatplane, my tools, my logbooks, my mother's letters in their bronze-bound chest.

The letters I'd brought to Ilsa's boat two nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth, because I hadn't liked leaving them at the hangar. I hadn't liked it the way you don't like leaving a door unlocked when you've seen a stranger walking the fence line. Call it paranoia. I've been called worse by better-informed people.

It turned out paranoia had been doing my risk assessment for me, and doing it well.

* * *

I went up the bank the slow way, off the path, through the alders, because if there was someone in my hangar I wanted to arrive as a rumor rather than a headline. The door was closed. The padlock was in place. The padlock was also hanging at an angle that suggested it had been recently persuaded, and there were fresh scratches around the hasp, bright as new coin in the spill of the hangar light.

Somebody had picked my lock and locked it again behind them. That's not a burglar. Burglars are in a hurry. That's a professional, or a government, which in my experience is the same animal with better stationery.

I pushed the door open with two fingers and stood in the doorway for a long moment, letting the scene explain itself.

The *Kestrel Queen* sat on her mooring dolly exactly where I'd left her, but her tie-downs had been retied with the knots facing out. My tool chest had been closed in the wrong order—the small drawers first, then the big ones, the way a tidy stranger does it, not the way I do it, which is chaos with a system somewhere underneath. A floorboard near the workbench sat a quarter inch proud of its neighbors. I lifted it. Underneath, where I kept my emergency cash and my father's old service revolver, everything was still there, but the cash had been counted. I could tell because I fold bills in fives and someone had refolded them in tens. A small thing. The kind of small thing that tells you everything: they'd had time, they'd had light, and they'd had a list.

The chest was where I'd left it, under a tarp behind the plane's spare float. The tarp had been refolded. The chest's lock—the original Vessani bronze, older than the nation that had supposedly never existed—bore scratches of its own. Someone had gone at it with picks and given up, or been interrupted, or decided that a locked chest in a searched hangar was evidence enough and the contents could wait for a warrant.

Aldous Crane. I didn't have proof. I had something better: the shape of it. Crane had sat in my plane two days ago asking precise, polite, poisonous questions about salvage law—*who has claim to river-borne property, Mr. Okafor, and how would a man document such a claim?*—while his eyes did inventory of my cargo bay. Crane, who charted flood damage for a ministry that had never once, in my memory, charted anything as far upriver as my mooring. Crane, whose government-issue boots were polished to a shine no flood surveyor could afford.

I stood in my own hangar, in my own doorway, and felt something I hadn't felt in twenty years of careful, self-erasing, invisible living.

I felt *found*.

And here is the strange part, the part I'd only admit under oath, which no one was administering: underneath the fear, there was a kind of relief. Like a man who's been holding his breath so long he's forgotten he's doing it, until something makes him gasp. I had spent my whole adult life being no one in particular—Xanthe Okafor, bush pilot, no history, no people, no claims on anyone. It had been a strategy and a wound and a habit, all wearing the same coat. Now someone had gone through my things, and the going-through had a message in it: *you matter to somebody, and not in a way you'll enjoy.*

My mother had run from Vessania to keep me out of its arithmetic. She'd died still running, still silent, and I'd inherited the silence like a second surname. Well. The silence was over. The men who erased a nation from the maps had come to my hangar in the night, and there was no version of the future in which I went back to being nobody by morning.

* * *

I loaded the hangar's legitimate cargo anyway—crates of engine parts, a drum of oil, the theater of an ordinary supply run—and I did it with my back to the water and my ears doing the work my eyes couldn't. Then I brought the chest down to the barge, and Ilsa met me at the gangplank with a lantern and a face like a closed account.

"You're late," she said.

"Someone was in my hangar."

She didn't ask who. That's the thing about Ilsa Reyes—she rationed her questions like a woman crossing a desert, and she'd already spent her ration on bigger ones. She looked at the scratches on the chest's lock, then at me, and something in her jaw set the way it must have set forty years ago, running refugees up this same river with the same men behind her.

"Inside," she said. "Bring the light."

In the *Marta B*'s hold, among the crates we'd loaded as cover—flour, diesel filters, a consignment of boots for a trading post that would be very surprised to receive them—Ilsa hung the lantern from a hook and watched me open the chest. The letters were still there, still sealed in their wax, still bearing the seal of a country that officially did not exist: a heron standing in water, a crown above it, the old motto worn nearly smooth—*The River Keeps What Is Given.*

"They went through everything," I said. "Counted my cash. Retied my knots. They didn't get the chest open."

"They didn't need to." Ilsa crouched beside the chest, not touching it. "A searched hangar is a message, boy. It says: *we know, and we want you to know we know.* Next time there won't be a message. Next time there'll be a warrant, or a fire, or a quiet man at your door at an hour when quiet men are at their best." She looked up at me. "So. Are we leaving tonight or are you going to stand there looking tragic until the sun comes up?"

"Tonight."

"Then the letters don't travel in that chest."

I'd been thinking the same thing, but hearing her say it made it real. "The chest is the proof," I said. "The seal, the bronze—"

"The chest is a target. Anyone who sees it sees Vessania. You want to carry a nation's seal up a river full of the men who drowned it?" She shook her head. "The letters travel where no man looks, because no man wants to look there. You have a false tank on your plane, don't you? The one you use for smuggling whiskey to the mission at Two Rivers?"

"That's *medicine*," I said, with dignity. "Communion wine. Mostly."

"Xanthe."

"It's a false fuel tank. Eight gallons of space, drains through the water separator, invisible from the outside. Yes."

"Then the letters go in the tank, wrapped in something that doesn't mind fuel fumes, and the chest travels on the barge where it can be found—because it *should* be found, if it comes to that. Let them open it and find what? Old letters? Let them find letters." Her eyes had gone narrow and bright. "Let them find letters that say nothing. You have paper? You have a hand that can write like a frightened heir?"

I understood then, and the understanding went through me cold and clean, like river water in April. "A decoy."

"A decoy," Ilsa said. "You write a letter in your mother's voice—grieving, vague, full of love and no locations. You seal it with wax from a candle, any wax, and you put it in the chest with a few blank pages and maybe a christening gown or some such family rubbish. Let Crane burn half the river looking for a treasury in a bundle of sentiment."

"And the real letters?"

"Go in the tank with the communion wine. God can look after them. He's got a better record than governments do."

* * *

It took two hours, and it was the strangest two hours of my life, which is a competitive field lately.

I sat in the *Marta B*'s galley with the lantern turned low and a sheet of good paper in front of me, and I wrote a letter from a dead woman to her son. I had her real letters beside me for the handwriting—I'd inherited her slant, her way of crossing her t's like she was striking them through, her habit of pressing hard enough to emboss the next page. I had her voice in my head, which was either a gift or a wound depending on the hour. And I wrote:

*My darling boy. If you are reading this, then the river has finally given back what I gave to it, and I am sorry for what it costs you. There is nothing in this chest but love and old paper. Whatever they tell you I was, whatever they say I carried, I carried only you, and that was treasure enough for one life. Burn this and be free. Be ordinary. Ordinary is a country no one invades.*

It was good. That was the awful part. It was good because it was true—every word of it except the one that mattered. There *was* something in the chest besides love. There was a nation's whole testimony, and a treasury at the river's source, and my mother's name in a ledger of heirs, and forty years of men with polished boots hunting her bloodline. My mother had wanted me ordinary. The river had other paperwork in mind.

I sealed the decoy with red candle wax, pressed a coin into it because I didn't have a heron seal and a coin at least looked official to a man reading by lantern light, and carried it down to the hold. The real letters I wrapped in oilcloth, then in a rubberized tool roll, then in a canvas bag that had spent twenty years smelling of avgas, because fuel fumes are the best burglar alarm ever devised. Eight gallons of space in a false tank. The letters went in. I sealed the fitting, wiped it down, and stood back and looked at my plane in the pre-dawn gray and felt the strangest thing: safer. The most dangerous cargo I'd ever carried, and I felt safer, because for the first time in my life I wasn't pretending it wasn't there.

Then I went back up the bank to the hangar, and I burned the evidence that I'd ever been careful.

That sounds backwards, so let me be precise. In the hangar's cold stove I burned the envelope my mother's letters had originally been mailed in—the one with a postmark from a river town that no longer appeared on any map, the one physical thread connecting the letters to a place. I burned a page of my own notes, the arithmetic I'd done on the letters' dates and names. I burned the scrap of wrapping paper that had come with the chest, printed with a freight mark in a script I couldn't read but which Miriam Sable, the archivist, had gone pale looking at. Anything that said *I know what this is* went into the stove, and I stood there watching it curl, and I thought about Miriam's warning—*someone else has been asking*—and about her archive full of smuggled records, and I felt the first cold drop of a guilt I'd be carrying for a while yet: that by taking the chest, I had painted a line on a map that ended at other people's doors.

The stove ate it all. Ash doesn't testify.

* * *

We cast off at first gray light, which on the Kestrel in that season is less a color than a rumor. Ilsa took the wheel. I took the deck, and the *Marta B* backed out of the backwater with all the grace of a filing cabinet learning to swim, and then we were in the current and the current was going the way we were going, upriver, toward the source, toward the place my mother had fled and the place the letters said her whole life had been an argument with.

The chest rode in the open, under a tarp, in plain sight, exactly as Ilsa had ordered. Bait and ballast. The letters rode in my fuel tank with the communion wine, and I caught myself, twice in the first hour, going aft to check a tank fitting that I had personally sealed and had no reason to doubt. Paranoia, I've decided, is not a flaw. It's a skill set. It's just that most people practice it on the wrong things—on strangers and storm clouds—when the reliable dangers are the ones that wear your own government's boots and ask polite questions about salvage law.

Gus Ferro's airfield slid past on the south bank, dark and shuttered. I'd left the charter payment and a note in his office door: *Supply run, back in a week, don't let anyone touch the Queen.* The note was true, which I figured earned me the lie. The *Kestrel Queen* stayed tied down in her hangar with her retied knots and her counted cash, looking innocent, which is more than I could say for myself anymore.

Ilsa brought us alongside the fuel dock at Corben's Landing at half past five to take on diesel—legitimate, logged, boring—and while the hose ran I walked the dock with a coffee I wasn't drinking, watching the town wake up. The baker's chimney was going. The ferry clerk was unlocking his kiosk. And at the end of the dock, where the government launch was moored, a man in a coat too good for the weather was standing very still, looking not at me but at the *Marta B*, at the chest-shaped tarp amidships, with the expression of a man confirming a delivery.

He didn't see me see him. Or he let me think he didn't, which with Crane would be the same trick wearing a different hat.

I walked back to the barge at an ordinary pace, poured my coffee over the side like a libation to whatever god handles men who've just passed their own point of no return, and climbed aboard.

"Ilsa," I said. "Our surveyor's on the dock."

"I know. He's been there since four." She didn't turn from the wheel. "He watched us load the diesel. He wrote something in a little book. Men who write in little books at four in the morning are either poets or problems, and I've met his poetry."

"He knows we're going upriver."

"He knows we're *loaded* for upriver. Flour and boots don't go downstream, boy, they come from there." She eased the throttle forward and the *Marta B* leaned into the current, and Corben's Landing began, dock by dock, to slide away behind us. "Question is whether he follows us now or lets us run and reports where we're going. A smart man lets us run."

"And Crane?"

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. The river took the silence and did what rivers do with silences, which is make them longer.

"Crane," she said finally, "is not a smart man. He's a careful one, and there's a difference. A smart man would have opened your chest by now. A careful man is waiting to see where it leads him." She glanced at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—something older than the war stories, something almost like pity. "So we're going to show him. And then, at the narrows, we're going to show him something else."

"Which is?"

She turned her face back upriver, toward the gray water and the gray morning and the country that wasn't on any map, and she smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight.

"Which is that the river remembers," she said, "even when the maps don't. Hold on to something. The current gets honest about a mile up."

The *Marta B* took the first bend, and Corben's Landing was gone, and there was nothing behind us now but everything we were running from, and nothing ahead but everything my mother had died keeping quiet.

I stood on the deck of an armored barge full of decoy flour, guarding an empty chest, carrying a nation in a fuel tank, and for the first time in twenty years I was exactly, unmistakably, somebody.

It was too late to be afraid about it. So I wasn't. Mostly.
