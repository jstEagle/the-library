## 9. Rapids and Reckonings

I've run the Kestrel for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching: the river always wins eventually, the river respects nobody's schedule, and any man who says he isn't afraid of the upper gorge is either lying or hasn't been there.

Xanthe Okafor said it on the second morning out of Corben's Landing, standing at my rail with his coffee going cold in his hand, watching the water turn from brown to a kind of furious green as the valley narrowed. "I'm not afraid of the gorge," he said. "Respectful. Not afraid."

"Lying," I told him, and took his coffee away before he could spill it. "Or stupid. You don't strike me as stupid."

He had that pilot's face — the one they all get after ten years of weather making decisions for them. Calm on top, arithmetic underneath. I'd known him since he was a boy hauling crates off my deck at Corben's Landing with his mother watching from the truck, and I'll tell you what I saw then and see now: a man built like a locked room. Everything important kept behind the door.

Well. The gorge has a way of opening doors.

* * *

The upper gorge doesn't begin so much as it commits. One moment you're in wide water with herons standing around like disapproving clerks, and the next the banks pull in tight as a fist and the whole river drops into a series of steps it has no business taking. My barge, the *Marta Reyes*, named for a woman who never once forgave me anything, is forty feet of riveted steel with an armored wheelhouse and a diesel heart older than Xanthe. She was built to take punishment. The gorge was built to give it.

I had her at half ahead, then two-thirds, reading the water the way you read a face across a crowded room — looking for the lie in it. Green tongues mean rock just under. A smooth V pointing upstream means a snag holding its breath. The boil lines were wrong all morning, shifted east by whatever the storm upcountry had dumped into the headwaters, and every mile I kept thinking: this is not the river I memorized. This is the river wearing the river's clothes.

Xanthe worked the forward winch with me, paying out and taking in the guide line we ran to shore through the calmer seams. It's old practice, older than either of us — you can't trust engines alone in the gorge, so you put a line ashore where you can and let the land help hold you honest. He learned fast, which annoyed me. Men who learn fast make me feel my age, and I'm not ready for that particular grief.

"Talk," I said, because the silence between us had gotten heavy enough to affect the trim.

"About what?"

"About why a bush pilot who hates owing people owes me a favor big enough to burn a week of flying season." I fed line through the fairlead, hand over hand. "Your mother. That's the shape of it, isn't it? She ran cargo for us. For the ones coming downriver, back when 'resettlement' still sounded like a polite word."

His hands didn't stop moving. Good sign. A man who stops moving when you name the dead is a man you have to handle carefully.

"She never talked about it," he said finally. "Vessania. I found out the word existed when I was nine, from an atlas with the page razored out. Somebody in our house had done it. Neat cut, right along the spine. I used to think she did it herself."

"Did she?"

"I asked her once. She made pancakes." He almost smiled. "That was her answer to everything hard. Pancakes. As if flour could settle a debt."

"It can't," I said. "But it buys time, and time is what most debts actually want."

The *Marta* bucked through the first real drop — six feet of green water folding over itself — and came out the far side shedding spray like a wet dog. Xanthe grabbed the rail, recovered, pretended he hadn't. I let him pretend. Courtesy matters more in a gorge than courage.

"My mother," I said, because fair was fair, "ran guns for a government that later denied knowing her name. So I don't judge anyone's silence. I inherited mine furnished."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way pilots look at weather they intend to argue with. "Furnished?"

"You'll understand by the falls."

* * *

The Narrows above Mile Eleven are where the gorge stops pretending to be navigable. The channel runs maybe thirty feet wide between walls the color of old bruises, and the current comes through at a speed that turns floating logs into artillery. Every trip, I tie off to the iron ring some forgotten soul drilled into the north wall in 1911, and every trip I say a small thank-you to whoever he was, because dead men's hardware has saved my life more often than living men's advice.

We came into the approach too fast. I knew it a half-second after I knew it, which is how these things always go — the knowledge arriving like a bill already overdue. The storm-swollen current had moved a mid-channel rock, or uncovered one, and the seam I'd planned to ride was now a boiling white scar with a hole under it.

"Hard to port!" I shouted, and threw the wheel myself, and the *Marta* groaned over onto her beam ends like a woman being dragged somewhere against her will.

We missed the rock. We did not miss the consequence. The stern swung wide, the guide line went bar-taut from the shore anchor, and the winch drum — thirty years of salt and rust doing their patient work — gave a sound I will hear on my deathbed. Not a snap. A gunshot. The cable parted at the fairlead and came back aboard like a whip with a grudge, and the loose end, sixty feet of quarter-inch steel under full load, went over the side and wrapped the deck cargo.

Which was lashed down. Mostly.

The crates of supplies shifted. One went over the rail, then the tarp, then the whole starboard stack began walking toward the water with the calm inevitability of things that have decided. And tangled in the whipping cable, dragging it toward the fairlead block, was the line itself — and if it fouled the propeller shaft, we were done. Dead in the worst water on the river, with walls too steep to climb and a current that files paperwork fast.

I did the arithmetic in about a heartbeat and hated the answer. Someone had to go over the rail on a tether and clear the cable before it reached the shaft. Someone light, someone who could work a knife in moving water, someone expendable.

I was reaching for the tether when Xanthe was already over the rail.

No hesitation, no speech, none of the heroics men use to talk themselves into dying. He just clipped the safety line to his harness, knife in his teeth like something out of a cheap novel, and dropped into the flooded well-deck where the water was running knee-to-waist and rising.

"Hold me short!" he yelled. "Don't let me go slack!"

So I held him. That was my job, and I want that recorded somewhere: Ilsa Reyes held the line while the Okafor boy fought a steel cable in white water, and my arms have not forgiven me since.

He got the wrap off the fairlead on the third try. I know because I counted, the way you count when counting is the only prayer you remember. On the third try the cable slithered free, the pressure came off, and the *Marta* straightened her skirts — and that's when the backwash hit.

A gorge backwash is the river changing its mind. All that water piled against the narrows comes rebounding off the walls, and it doesn't care what's in the way. It took Xanthe off the deck like a hand picking up a coat. His safety line snapped taut, then — I saw it, I will always have seen it — the carabiner gate, bent in the earlier chaos, let go.

The river took him.

* * *

People think drowning looks like the pictures. It doesn't. It looks like nothing. A dark shape in green water, gone between one wave and the next, and your own voice making sounds you don't recognize as yours.

I got the *Marta* around on screaming engines, because the alternative was to keep driving downstream away from him, and I would have sooner scuttled her. I threw the ring buoy twice and missed. The third throw I waded to the rail myself, tethered to the mast by my own belt because I am old but not stupid, and I went in after him.

The water was cold enough to stop thought. That's the mercy of it. No fear, no plan, just the body doing what thirty years of rivers teach it: roll, don't fight, find the eddy behind the rock, breathe when the river lets you. I caught his jacket on the second dive. He was conscious — I felt him grab me, grip like a drowning man's, which is exactly what he was — and we came up together in the lee of the mid-channel rock, both of us coughing up half the Kestrel, and I got my arm through the ring buoy line some providence had drifted down to us, and the *Marta*'s crew — meaning me, meaning us, meaning two stubborn people — hauled hand over hand until the steel deck was under us again.

He lay on the planks vomiting river water and shaking, and I lay next to him doing the same, and the gorge roared past us like a crowd that had lost interest.

"Well," he said, when he could.

"Don't," I said.

"I was going to say thank—"

"Don't." I sat up. My hands were bleeding from the line. I found I didn't care. "You jumped without orders, without a plan, without asking whether I could hold your weight in that current. That's not brave. That's the same thing as careless, wearing better boots."

He turned his head and looked at me, water still running off his face, and there was something in his expression I hadn't seen in the boy or the man — something unguarded, door open, lights on.

"You went in after me," he said. "Tethered to a mast. With your belt."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because I'm the captain. It's in the job description."

"It is absolutely not in the job description."

"It's in mine," I said, and discovered I was laughing, or crying, or some shameful combination of the two, sitting in a puddle on my own deck with a drowned pilot, while the river we'd cheated carried on without us. "It's in mine specifically. I wrote it."

* * *

We tied up that night in the slough below Devil's Elbow, a backwater deep enough to hide in and quiet enough to hear yourself think, which neither of us particularly wanted to do. I rigged the lantern in the wheelhouse, put a pot of coffee on the little stove that has survived three fires and one divorce, and we sat shoulder to shoulder watching the moths commit suicide against the glass.

"You froze," he said, after a while. "Back there. When the line parted. Half a second."

"I assessed."

"You froze, and then you moved anyway." He turned the coffee mug in his hands. "My mother did that. I watched it once, at the airfield. A fuel fire, small one, and everyone ran except her. She stood there for — I don't know, a second? Two? Like she was deciding something. Then she moved, and she put it out, and afterward she made pancakes and never mentioned it again."

"What was she deciding?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Whether to be the person the fire needed or the person she'd been trying to be since she ran. Invisible. Safe. I think she chose the fire every time, and I think it cost her, and I think—" His voice did something complicated. "I think I've spent my whole life choosing invisible, and today I jumped over a rail without deciding anything at all. First time. Felt terrible. Felt wonderful."

"Felt like her," I said.

He looked at me.

"I knew her, Xanthe. Before you were born. She came downriver with the last of them, spring flood, nineteen years old, carrying a satchel she wouldn't let out of arm's reach. I asked her what was in it. She said, 'Everything my country was.' I offered to lock it in my strongbox. She said no. Said if it left her hands, it would end up bargaining for somebody's survival, and survival bought with other people's truth wasn't survival at all." I sipped the coffee. It was terrible. It was perfect. "She paid her passage working the deck. Better than most men I've hired. And when she stepped off at Corben's Landing, she shook my hand and said, 'If anyone ever comes asking after me, tell them I died.' I told her that was a hell of a thing to ask. She said, 'It's a hell of a thing to need.'"

The lantern hissed. Somewhere upriver, the gorge muttered to itself.

"She's dead," Xanthe said. "Two years now. Cancer. Fast." He set the mug down. "And now I'm carrying her satchel, more or less, and every mile upstream I keep waiting to feel like a thief or a hero, and instead I just feel like her son. Which is worse. Which is better. I haven't decided."

"You won't," I said. "Deciding's overrated. Ask the river."

"And you?" he said. "What did you inherit furnished?"

I should have deflected. Thirty-one years of practice; I'm very good at it. But the lantern was warm and the coffee was bad and the man beside me had nearly died holding a cable in white water for cargo that wasn't his, and some doors, once opened by a backwash, don't close all the way.

"My father was Vessani," I said. "Harbor pilot at Cape Reckoning. I was eight when the decree came through. They put us on barges — 'resettled,' the papers said — and my father stood on the dock in his uniform hat and made me memorize the recognition signs before they took him. All of them. The toll signs, the safe-passage knots, the phrases. He said, 'One day the river will ask you the question, and you must know the answer.' I thought he meant soon. It's been forty years, and the river hasn't asked until this week." I looked at him. "When we come to the fortified narrows, and we will, I'm going to show them a knot my father taught me in 1961. And God help me, I don't know what happens after."

Xanthe put his hand over mine on the rail. Rough hand, rope-burned, steady. Neither of us moved it.

"Ilsa," he said. "Whatever happens after — I'm glad it's your barge."

"That's the fever talking."

"Probably."

"Good," I said. "Because if it weren't, I'd have to have rules about it, and I'm fresh out. Used the last of them on the gorge."

He laughed — really laughed, the first I'd heard out of him, and it changed his whole face the way dawn changes a valley. And I sat there in the moth-battered lamplight, an old woman with bleeding hands and a full heart, thinking: oh, this is trouble. This is exactly the trouble I swore off at fifty. And the river, which forgives nothing, appeared entirely delighted.

Then the radio crackled.

Static first, the long dry hiss of distance. Then a voice, thin and warped, calling Corben's Landing, calling any station, and though the words broke apart in the atmosphere, two of them came through whole and cold as the gorge water:

"—fire—"

and

"—Sable—"

Xanthe was on his feet before the static closed over it, and I saw the door in his face slam shut, and I reached for the throttle, because whatever had happened at Corben's Landing, we were a hundred miles upriver, and the river does not forgive, and it does not wait.
