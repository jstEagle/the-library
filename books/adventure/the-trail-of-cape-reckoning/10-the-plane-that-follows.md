## 10. The Plane That Follows

There is a particular sound a floatplane makes when it's looking for you, and once you've heard it you never mistake it for anything else. It isn't the engine—that's just noise, honest noise, the same drone every Cessna on the river makes when some geologist or fish-and-game man is bored enough to go flying. It's the *pattern*. A plane going somewhere flies straight. A plane hunting you circles wide, drops low over the water, swings back the way it came as if it had forgotten something. It reads like a sentence spoken by someone who doesn't want you to hear it.

I heard that sentence at half past six in the morning, two days out of Corben's Landing, while Ilsa was arguing with the coffee pot.

"Your barge," she said, "has one setting, and it's *deliberate*. If your friend in the government wants to find us, all he has to do is follow the smell of diesel and stubbornness."

"He's not my friend."

"Then why does he keep showing up in your life like a bill?"

I didn't have an answer for that, so I drank my coffee instead, which is what I usually do with questions I don't like. We were tied up below the second set of riffles, waiting on light—real light, not the gray suggestion of it—and I was standing on the cargo deck with a mug in my hand when the sound came down the valley. High, thin, wrong-angled. I looked up through the mist coming off the water and saw it: a high-wing single, cream-colored with a blue stripe, running the river at maybe four hundred feet.

I knew that stripe. I'd fueled that airplane twice at Corben's Landing and complimented its owner on his propeller, because that's what pilots do—we lie to each other about each other's airplanes the way farmers lie about weather, politely and without conviction.

"That's the charter outfit out of Fort Merrow," I said. "The one Crane would've called."

Ilsa didn't look up from her engine. "You sure?"

"I'm sure he'd use them. They're the only outfit north of the gorge with a plane rated for this stretch, and they're the only outfit whose owner owes money to people who make him ask no questions." I watched the aircraft slide upstream, unhurried, a man strolling past a window he intended to look through later. "That's not survey work. Survey planes fly lines. That thing is flying *us*."

The barge's wake curled away behind us, brown and patient. There was nowhere to hide a hundred and forty feet of armored riverboat, and we both knew it.

"Well," Ilsa said, wiping her hands on a rag that had been black since before either of us met her, "then I suppose we'd better be somewhere interesting by the time he looks again."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being followed from the air: the ground stops being ground and starts being board. Every bend of the river becomes a move. Every gravel bar, a square. For thirty years I had flown this country the way you walk through your own house—without thinking, without watching, without ever once considering which rooms could be seen from the street. Now I stood on Ilsa's deck with a pair of binoculars I'd bought off a dead prospector's estate and watched the sky the way I used to watch the fuel gauge, and I hated how natural it felt. Hated how quickly the old instinct came back. Because here's the other thing nobody tells you: hiding isn't a skill you learn. It's a skill you *remember*, and mine had been waiting under thirty years of honest work like a knife in a drawer.

The charter plane made three passes that morning. Wide, lazy, professional. On the third pass it climbed, turned northeast, and disappeared over the ridge line toward Fort Merrow, and any reasonable man would have said, *well, that's that, he was probably checking flood damage after all.*

I am not a reasonable man. I am a man who learned at eleven years old that the people who leave are sometimes leaving *for* you, and that the ones who come back come back with paperwork.

"He'll refuel and return by early afternoon," I told Ilsa. "Charter rates mean he can't loiter. He'll come back fast, low, and mean, and he'll expect us to be exactly where we are now, because we're a barge and barges don't do anything surprising."

"And what will we be doing instead?"

I'd spent the morning studying the chart, and there it was, the way answers always are once you stop flinching long enough to read: the Miren Cut. An old logging tributary that entered the Kestrel eight miles upstream through a gap in the bluffs, ran dead straight for six miles into country so worthless even the loggers gave it back, and ended in a marsh that appeared on exactly none of the charts worth trusting. Dead-end water. Fog trap. The kind of place a pilot looks at once and files under *never*.

"It ends in a marsh," Ilsa said when I showed her.

"Yes."

"You want to take a hundred and forty feet of steel up a creek that goes *nowhere*."

"The fog comes off the marsh around four o'clock. I've seen it a dozen times from above—it rolls down the cut like smoke and swallows the whole tributary. If we're inside it when he comes back, he can't see us. And if he tries to come in after us—" I tapped the chart where the bluffs pinched the entrance to less than sixty yards. "He can't turn around in there. Not in fog. Not in a loaded plane."

Ilsa studied the chart the way she studied everything, with the expression of a woman who had already found three flaws and was deciding whether to mention them. "And if the fog doesn't come?"

"Then we're sitting in a dead-end creek explaining ourselves to a man with a warrant." I shrugged. "But the fog comes. It always comes. The marsh breathes out in the late afternoon; it's thermodynamics, not luck."

She looked at me for a long moment. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about a lot of things since Mile Nine."

"*Thermodynamics,*" she muttered, and rang for full ahead.

* * *

We entered the Miren Cut at twenty past three, and I will say this for the plan: it worked better than I deserved, which is the highest praise I give anything.

The bluffs closed over us like a door. The Kestrel vanished behind rock, and suddenly we were in another country entirely—a narrow green corridor of dead water, logged-out eighty years ago and growing back in birch and alder, the current so slack that leaves sat on the surface like they'd been arranged. Ilsa brought the barge in with a delicacy I hadn't known she possessed, feathering the throttles, walking us between the drowned pilings the way you'd carry a full cup through a crowded room.

"Depth," she said.

"Nine feet under the keel." I was sounding with a weighted line off the bow, hauling and reading, hauling and reading. "Seven. Six and a half. Channel bends to port."

"Barges don't bend."

"This one's about to learn."

The fog began at four o'clock exactly, because thermodynamics keeps appointments. It didn't roll—it *poured*, down off the marsh in slow silver sheets, and within twenty minutes the world was the width of the wheelhouse and nothing else. I stood on the bow and listened to the charter plane arrive.

It came in high first—I heard it, a drone circling somewhere above the ceiling, patient, methodical. Then lower. Then much lower, and the sound changed, flattened out, the way an engine sounds when the pilot is flying on instruments of hope. He was under the fog now, or trying to be, feeling his way up the cut toward the mouth of the Kestrel, looking for a barge that wasn't there.

What happened next happened fast, and I reconstruct it partly from sound and partly from faith. The engine note rose—climbing, turning—and then there was a crack of displaced air, close, too close, the sound of an airplane passing overhead in fog at an altitude that could generously be called *intimate*. Branches rained down. Something large and hollow banged off the water fifty yards astern of us. And then the engine note climbed and climbed and kept climbing until it faded eastward over the bluffs, fast, the particular urgency of a pilot who has just remembered every reason he took this job.

Ilsa killed the engines. We drifted. Somewhere in the white, a loon laughed at us, and honestly, fair enough.

"He'll report the barge gone," I said quietly. "Gone means lost, and lost means he widens the search. We bought a day. Maybe two."

Ilsa was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read in the fog. "Xanthe. What did we hit?"

"We didn't hit anything."

"Something hit *us*. Listen."

I listened. Under the loon and the drip of the fog there was a new sound from the stern, faint but rhythmic: a soft, wet knocking. Wood against water. I went aft along the deck with a flashlight, and the beam found it immediately—the starboard float of my floatplane, lashed aft among the deck cargo, riding low. Too low. During the run up the cut, in the dark under the branches, something underwater—a deadhead, a piling, the river's own opinion of my plan—had caught the float and cracked it along a seam, and it had been quietly drinking the Miren Cut ever since.

I stood there a while with the water dripping off my hat brim, doing arithmetic. A cracked float meant the plane was grounded until I could patch it, and patching a float properly meant glass, resin, and twelve hours of dry weather, none of which the upper river offered in generous supply. It meant that if things went badly—if Crane's superiors sent more than one plane, if the river turned on us, if I needed wings—I might not have them.

The cost of defiance, itemized. One float. So far.

"Well," I said, when I got back to the wheelhouse, "the good news is the fog hid us perfectly."

"And the bad news?"

"The bad news is also the fog, technically, but mostly it's the rocks."

Ilsa laughed—not her public laugh, the gravelly bark she used on dockhands, but something shorter and realer, surprised out of her—and then she did a thing I'm still thinking about days later. She put her hand flat on the chart, over the Miren Cut, and said, "Next time you have a plan that ends in a marsh, you tell me the whole plan. Including the part where your airplane drowns."

"There wasn't supposed to be a part where my airplane drowns."

"There's always a part where something drowns. That's what rivers are *for*." She rang for slow ahead, and we felt our way deeper into the cut to anchor for the night, and I noticed that she'd moved closer to me at the rail than the work required, and that neither of us mentioned it, and that the fog held us both like it was in on something.

* * *

It was too easy. That's what I kept coming back to, lying awake in the fo'c'sle with the lantern turned low and the letters in their oilcloth bundle six inches from my head.

Run the numbers yourself. Crane books a charter within hours of our departure—he knows our heading, our speed, our fuel. His plane finds us on the open river, confirms us, and then loses us in a tributary I picked off a forty-year-old logging chart in an afternoon. One pass in the fog, a near-miss, and gone. No second attempt at dawn. No radio traffic I could raise on the set. No gunboat waiting at the mouth of the cut when we slid back out into the Kestrel the next morning under a sky scrubbed clean and innocent.

A man who photographs your logbook does not lose you that easily. A man who threatens your certificates does not spend his agency's money on one afternoon of flying and go home. Either Crane was stupider than he looked—and he looked like a man who alphabetized his socks—or we hadn't escaped him at all. We'd been *allowed* to feel like we had.

Because there was a third possibility, and it sat in my chest all night like a swallowed stone: that Crane didn't need to follow us anymore. That he already knew where we were going. That everyone on this river who remembered Vessania knew what lay at its source, and that the only person who'd believed this journey was a secret was the man poling the barge.

I got up in the gray before dawn and checked the false fuel tank where the letters rode. Sealed. Dry. Untouched. I stood there in the cold with my hand on the cap fitting and made myself say it plainly, the way Ilsa had taught me to say things plainly:

They're letting me carry it to them.

Then I went forward, woke Ilsa, and lied to her. Not about anything important—just about how well I'd slept. Some habits keep a man alive, and some of them are just the old knife in the drawer, and I no longer pretended to know which was which.

By seven we were underway, the Miren Cut falling away behind us into its marsh, and ahead the river narrowed and lifted and turned white over the first stones of the gorge country. From the top of the wheelhouse, glasses up, I swept the ridgelines for the flash of a wing and found nothing but mist and spruce and distance.

Nothing at all. Which is, I have learned, exactly what the beginning of a trap looks like from the inside.

At noon, Ilsa's radio—which she swore was tuned to nothing but weather—crackled once, twice, and spoke.
