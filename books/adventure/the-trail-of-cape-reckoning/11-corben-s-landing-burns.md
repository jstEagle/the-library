## 11. Corben's Landing Burns

They came for the archive at four in the morning, which told Miriam Sable two things before she ever saw their faces: they were professionals, and they were afraid of witnesses.

She knew this because she had spent forty years cataloguing the habits of men who burned things. Emergency decrees were always signed at night. Records were always destroyed before dawn. There was a whole taxonomy of it, and she had once—back when she still believed scholarship was a form of armor—written a paper on the administrative choreography of erasure. *Note the hour of the warrant. Note the absence of fire brigade notification. Note how the neighbors are warned away by men who do not wear uniforms.*

So when the smell of kerosene woke her in the cot behind the reading room, she did not scream or run for the door. She ran for the shelf.

Not the famous shelf—the locked case with the Vessani material that any competent arsonist would go for first—but the third shelf of the geography stacks, row F, where a mislabeled folio of river charts held what she had actually smuggled out of the dissolution office thirty-nine years ago, wrapped in oilcloth and disguised as surveyor's waste. She had it in her hands, down the back stairs, and into the rain barrel behind the building before the first window went orange.

Then she stood in the alley with the folio pressed against her chest like a child, watching forty years of other people's rescued paper catch light, and permitted herself exactly eleven seconds of grief. She counted them. She was precise about grief; it was the only thing she had never been able to file properly.

"Dr. Sable."

The voice came from the mouth of the alley, and she turned with the slow dignity of a woman who refused to be caught doing anything as vulgar as fleeing.

He was young. That was the insult of it. Grey coat, no insignia, gloves in this weather, and the patient posture of someone being paid by the hour rather than the result.

"You're standing in my smoke," she said.

"I am." He stepped closer, unhurried. "There's a car at the end of the lane. You'll want your coat. It's cold out here, and I'm told you're valuable."

"Valuable." She looked past him at the reading room window, where the fire had found the card catalogue and was eating it alphabetically. Somewhere in there was the drawer marked VES, and somewhere in that drawer was the index card she'd written in her own hand at nineteen years old: *Vessania—see restricted.* Forty years of *see restricted*, and now everyone could see exactly how restricted. "Young man, do you know what you're burning?"

"A structure of no official record," he said, "containing materials of no official record, belonging to an archivist of no official record. You've been very thorough, Doctor. We admire thoroughness. It's why we need you alive."

"And if I decline the car?"

"Then we take the folio out of the rain barrel myself, and you can watch us read it without you." He glanced at the barrel, and she understood with a cold settling in her stomach that he had known about the barrel before she'd reached it. They had watched her carry it out. The whole rescue had been performed for an audience of one.

She gave him her coat from the hook by the alley door, because a woman should choose something, even if it was only a coat.

* * *

The room they kept her in had a table, a chair, a lamp, and a window painted over—which meant it had been prepared in advance, which meant Corben's Landing had been burning on a schedule since before the match was struck. They sat her at the table and put tea in front of her, which she refused on principle, and then a second man came in carrying a folder, and she understood that the interrogation would be conducted as a seminar, because they believed she would find that flattering.

They were right, which annoyed her more than the ropes would have.

"The Okafor woman," the folder-carrier said. "Seraphine. Tell us about her daughter's son."

"There is no Okafor woman in my records. Names require provenance, and provenance requires paper, and you burned my paper."

"You kept duplicates."

"Of course I kept duplicates. I'm an archivist, not an amateur." She folded her hands. "But duplicates live in duplicate places, and you haven't found them yet, or you wouldn't be wasting tea on me."

The two men looked at each other in a way that confirmed it. Good. Let them chew on that all week.

"Then let's try the living archive," the younger one said, and set a photograph on the table between them.

It was Xanthe. Taken from above and behind—a floatplane at a dock, a bronze-bound chest half-lifted to the cargo door—and printed with the casual cruelty of a man who wanted her to look at it a long time. She made herself look at it a long time. She noted the date stamp. She noted the dock. She noted, with a scholar's detached horror, that the chest was open in the photograph, and that whoever had taken the picture had been standing close enough to read the seals.

"You know him," the older one said. Not a question.

"I know every pilot who lands at Corben's Landing. I sell them stamps and flattery and bad coffee. It's called commerce."

"He asked you about Vessania."

"He asked me about a dissolved jurisdiction's salvage regulations, which is a question any man who pulls furniture out of a river ought to ask, unless he enjoys donating his finds to the Crown." She sipped nothing. "I gave him the standard answer. Everything is property of the state. Nothing survives dissolution. Go home."

"And yet he flew upriver."

"People fly upriver constantly. It's the only direction the river goes that isn't down."

The younger one smiled for the first time, and it was worse than the older one's silence. "Doctor Sable. We don't need you to confess. Confession is for amateurs and courts. We need you to write a letter."

"To whom?"

"To the pilot. A friendly note. An old colleague, worried about his safety, suggesting a place where he might wait out the weather." He slid a sheet of paper across the table, and on it were coordinates, written in a hand that mimicked hers almost well enough. Almost. The descenders were wrong. Everyone's descenders were wrong; it was the first thing she taught her apprentices—*your handwriting is a fingerprint, guard it accordingly.*

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we stop needing you alive," the older one said, without heat, the way a man reports the weather. "And the letter gets written anyway. We have forty years of your correspondence to copy from. Your hand is very consistent, Doctor. It's one of the things we admire about you."

Miriam looked at the coordinates. She knew the water under them—she had charted that bend herself, in another life, before the maps were amended. Dead-end slough, deep banks, fog that pooled there until noon. A place where a floatplane could land and a gunboat could wait, and where the fog would make sure neither party saw the other until it was far too late to matter.

They hadn't chosen it because it was clever. They'd chosen it because it was *hers*—a bend she'd described in letters, fished in photographs, mentioned in the margins of a hundred marginal notes. They were using her memory as a weapon, and the only defense against a weapon made of yourself is to become someone else quickly.

She picked up the pen.

She wrote the letter. Her real hand, her real phrasing, warm and dry and faintly bossy, exactly the voice Xanthe Okafor had heard across her counter twice. And in the fourth line, in the space between two perfectly ordinary words, she made a descender wrong on purpose—one letter, one small deliberate forgery of her own forgery, invisible to anyone who didn't know her hand better than its owner.

Xanthe wouldn't see it. Xanthe had never studied her hand.

But Ilsa Reyes had bought stamps from her for twenty years, and Ilsa Reyes read everything twice.

"There," she said, setting down the pen. "Now will someone tell me whether the girl who runs the chandlery got out? She sleeps above the archive."

"No one sleeps above the archive anymore, Doctor," the younger one said gently. "That was the point."

* * *

I heard the news the way bad news always arrives on the river: sideways, in pieces, wearing the costume of ordinary traffic.

The radio crackle Ilsa had sworn was weather turned out to be the Corben's Landing harbor net, because Ilsa Reyes lied the way other people breathed—constantly, and only when necessary. What came through wasn't the harbor master. It was Denny, the chandlery girl, sixteen years old and talking so fast the words fell over each other: fire, the whole east wall gone, the catalogue gone, Miss Sable gone, no body, no note, and a man in a grey coat asking questions at the dock about floatplanes heading upstream.

Ilsa killed the volume and looked at me across the wheelhouse, and I looked at the river, and for a while neither of us said anything, because some arithmetic you have to do alone.

Forty years of rescued paper. Gone in an hour. And the woman who kept it—gone with it, into a grey coat and a prepared room, because she had answered my questions honestly. Because she had told me about the decree, and the burnings, and the families who smuggled things out, and I had walked out of her archive grateful and careless, leaving footprints a blind man could follow.

"They watched me leave," I said. "That's how they knew. They've been sitting on that town since Mile Nine, waiting for me to lead them to whatever mattered."

"Or they've been sitting on her for years and you were just the excuse to finally strike the match." Ilsa's hands stayed easy on the wheel, but her voice had gone flat, which I was learning was the sound of Ilsa Reyes furious. "Don't flatter yourself, pilot. Men like this don't burn archives for strangers. They burn them for debts."

"It doesn't matter whose fault it is. It matters that it's mine now." I pulled the letter from my jacket—I'd collected it at the last fuel stop, along with diesel and a lecture about my carburetor—and smoothed it on the chart table. *An old colleague, worried about his safety.* Miriam's dry, bossy warmth, transcribed faithfully. Coordinates in the middle of it, offered like a shelter from weather.

"She wants you to go there," Ilsa said.

"She wants me to think she wants me to go there. That's different." I tapped the coordinates. "This is a dead-end slough off the Miren Cut. Fog sits in it till noon. A floatplane lands fine. So does a gunboat. And the fog makes sure you meet the gunboat before you know it's there."

"So we don't go."

"We don't go *there*." I spread the river chart and weighted the corners with the teapot, the logbook, and my fist. "Look at the shape of it. The slough's a cul-de-sac, but the ridge above it—" I traced the contour lines with a finger, feeling the old habit of thinking in three dimensions wake up and stretch. "There's a logging spur, abandoned, comes off the fire road. You can see the whole slough from two hundred feet up. If I'm in the air and you're on the water, nobody springs anything. We look. We learn what they sent. And maybe—" I stopped, because the next part was the part I didn't want to say out loud, and saying it would make it a plan instead of a fear.

"Maybe she left something," Ilsa finished for me. "Archivists leave things. It's a disease with them."

"She corrected her own handwriting." I showed Ilsa the letter, the fourth line, the single letter with its descender bent the wrong way. "Miriam Sable does not make mistakes. She taught me that herself, in about nine seconds flat. This is a message in a bottle, and the bottle is addressed to anyone who knows her hand well enough to doubt it."

Ilsa took the letter and held it to the lamplight for a long time. When she spoke again, the fury had settled into something quieter and more dangerous.

"Twenty years I've been buying stamps from that woman," she said. "Twenty years, and she never once wrote me a letter, because she said letters were liabilities and friendship should be conducted in person like civilized people." She set it down. "First letter she ever writes me is a trap with a hinge in it. That's Miriam telling us she's alive, and telling us she knows we're smart enough to check her work."

"Or it's them being cleverer than we think."

"Nobody's that clever." Ilsa rang the engine telegraph up to half ahead and the barge leaned into the current. "Clever people assume everyone else is stupid. Miriam assumes everyone else is paying attention. It's why she's insufferable, and it's why she's still—"

She stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, over the white water.

"—she's still writing," I finished. "Yes. That's what we hold onto."

We ran the rest of the afternoon in shifts, me at the glasses on top of the wheelhouse, Ilsa working the gorge channels she clearly knew by heart, the barge threading white water with the unhurried arrogance of something too heavy to be afraid. Twice I thought I saw aircraft light flash on the ridgelines and twice it was sun on wet stone, and both times my heart did something undignified, and both times I hated it a little less than I expected to. Fear, I was discovering, was just caring with nowhere to hide.

At dusk we tied up in a spruce hollow below the abandoned logging spur, engines down, running dark. Ilsa cooked something with onions in it and pretended it wasn't comfort food, and I cleaned the floatplane's spark plugs by lamplight because my hands needed a job that couldn't argue with me.

It was while I was gapping the third plug that I finally said it.

"If they hurt her, it's because of me."

Ilsa didn't look up from the stove. "If they hurt her, it's because of them. But yes. You're the reason and they're the cause, and those aren't the same thing, and a man who confuses them ends up either useless or dead." She stirred the pot. "You want to be useful? Then tomorrow we fly that spur, we read that slough, and we find out whether Miriam Sable left us a door or left us a grave. Either way, we come back with something they didn't plan on. That's the only apology she'd accept. She'd tell you so herself, and then she'd charge you for the stamp."

I laughed—actually laughed, once, ugly and surprised—and went back to my plugs.

Above the hollow, the first stars came out over the gorge country, and somewhere downstream, in a room with a painted-over window, an old woman was presumably making her captors regret the tea. I held onto that image the way you hold onto a rail in rough water.

Because the radio had crackled once more before Ilsa shut it off for the night—a fragment, half static, half voice, the harbor net relaying something from further upstream. Most of it dissolved into hiss.

But three words came through clean, in a calm official cadence I already knew too well:

*"—survey vessel Crane—"*
