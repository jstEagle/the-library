## 12. The Toll of the Narrows

Forty years on the Kestrel and its daughters, and I'd learned one law older than any government: the river narrows, somebody builds a wall, and somebody else pays for the privilege of passing. Usually with coin. Sometimes with blood. The smart ones pay coin.

The Narrows at Sorrow Gap weren't on any chart I'd ever trusted, which is how I knew somebody was still living there. Charts cost money, and money follows the truth like gulls follow a fish boat. Where the charts go blank, that's where folks have decided they'd rather be left alone than counted.

"Kill the running lights," I said.

Xanthe killed them. The barge slid on through the dark with the engine at half, the gorge walls rising black on either side, close enough that I could've reached out and dragged a glove along the rock. The water hissed against the hull, patient and mean, the way it gets when it knows the channel better than you do.

"You've been through here before," he said. It wasn't a question. The boy had spent three days learning to read me, and he was getting too good at it for my comfort.

"Twice," I said. "Once running diesel and canned peaches for people who didn't exist. Once running the other way, with people who didn't exist anymore either. Both times there was a chain across the water and a voice asking who I was."

"And you told it?"

"I told it the truth. That's the trick of it. In a country that's been erased, the truth is the best disguise there is. Nobody's looking for it."

He didn't laugh. He'd stopped laughing at my jokes somewhere around the gorge, which I took as a sign he was starting to take the whole business seriously, or starting to take me seriously, and I hadn't decided which one worried me more.

Ahead, the gorge pinched to a throat maybe forty meters wide, and across it hung a shape my eyes remembered before my brain caught up: a timber boom, black against the blacker water, with lanterns strung along it like a carnival that had died mid-celebration. Two of the lanterns were moving. Men walking the boom.

"There it is," I said. "Toll gate."

"Those aren't government," Xanthe said. "No flag. No markings."

"No," I agreed. "They wouldn't fly one. A flag is a promise you can be found by."

I brought the barge down to a crawl and sounded the horn twice, short-short, then waited. Then I did the thing I'd done twice before and never once since: I took the old signal lamp from its bracket, uncovered the red lens, and blinked a pattern into the dark. Three long, two short, one long. Any surveyor on the river would read it as garbage. Any Vessani would read it as a sentence.

*The river remembers. We are its children.*

I hadn't blinked that pattern in forty years, and my hands shook doing it now, and I told myself it was the cold.

The lanterns on the boom stopped moving. A long silence, the kind of silence that has people inside it, thinking. Then a voice came across the water, amplified by the gorge walls into something huge and hollow.

"Who signals the old tongue?"

"Captain Ilsa Reyes," I called back. "Master of the barge *Perseverance*, out of Corben's Landing. I'm hauling freight and two passengers upriver. I'll pay your toll and I'll pay it fair, but I'm not turning around."

"Freight," the voice said, flat. "What freight?"

"Diesel, salt, rope, and a crate of rifles I'd rather not discuss in the open." There weren't any rifles. But a toll gate respects a lie about guns more than a truth about salt, and I'd learned that lesson the hard way in a place I won't name.

Another silence. Then, closer now, a lantern swinging down from the boom on a line, carried by a figure in an oilskin slicker who came alongside in a skiff with two more men, all three of them moving with the particular economy of people who have handled small boats in bad water their whole lives. The lantern came up. The light hit my face, then swept the deck, then stopped on Xanthe standing at the rail with his arms crossed and his jaw set like a man at his own trial.

The lantern-holder was a woman, I saw then. Sixty, maybe older, gray braid down her back, a face like a river stone — worn smooth, hard underneath. She looked at me a long moment.

"Reyes," she said. "You're older than your reputation."

"Everything about me is older than my reputation. Who's asking?"

"Sentinel Wren's garrison." She said it the way a priest says a saint's name, or a customs man says a tax code. "You blinked the children's sign. Nobody's blinked that sign in a decade. The last ones who tried were Crane's people, and we kept them in a cellar for a week before we turned them loose to walk home."

"Crane's people," Xanthe said quietly, beside me, and I felt him go still the way water goes still over a snag.

The woman's lantern turned toward him. "And who's this?"

"Passenger," I said.

"Passenger," she repeated, tasting it. "Passengers don't stand like that when a stranger's light hits them. Passengers don't flinch at the name Crane." She stepped closer, boots steady on the wet skiff gunwale as her man held it against the barge. "Passengers also don't have that face."

"What's wrong with my face?" Xanthe said.

"Nothing's wrong with it," she said. "That's the problem. I've seen it before. On a woman who ran this same water ahead of soldiers, the year the world ended for us. She had your chin. She had your way of standing like the question was beneath her." The lantern didn't waver. "She had your name, too. Okafor. Amara Okafor."

I heard Xanthe breathe in. I've heard a lot of men breathe on my decks — the gasp of a man who's slipped, the grunt of a man lifting, the rattle of a man dying. I'd never heard that particular sound: a man hearing his mother's name spoken by a stranger in a gorge in the dark, forty years after the world agreed she'd never existed.

"How do you know that name," he said. Not a question either. The Okafor voice, demanding the world make sense.

"Because we keep it," the woman said simply. "That's the toll, if you're asking. Not coin. We stopped taking coin years ago — coin spends, and spending leaves a trail. The toll is an answer. Who are you, and why does the old sign come out of your lamp?"

I could have lied. Don't think I didn't consider it. Forty years of running cargo for people the maps forgot teaches you that lies are just ballast — useful, heavy, and the first thing you jettison in rough water. But I'd blinked the sign myself. I'd put my own hand on the old sentence, and there's a rule about that, older than any government: you don't speak the river's language and then swear at it in the government's.

"His name is Xanthe Okafor," I said. "Amara's son. And the sign came out of my lamp because I owed his mother, and because I'm tired of pretending I don't."

The woman looked at me. Then at him. Then she did something I didn't expect, and I'll admit it knocked me sideways: she laughed. Short, rough, almost a cough.

"Amara's son," she said. "On the *Perseverance*, with Ilsa Reyes at the wheel, blinking the children's sign at my boom. Forty years I've stood this gate, and the stories all said the bloodline was dead or scattered or bought." She spat over the side, ceremonially, into the river. "The stories were wrong. They usually are. That's why we keep the gate."

"Then open it," Xanthe said.

"It opens when the sentinel says it opens, and the sentinel will want to see you himself." She was already turning to her men, snapping orders in a language I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was a young woman running peaches to ghosts — Vessani, the river dialect, clipped and musical, and hearing it made my chest hurt in a way I resented. The boom began to groan. Chain links the size of my fist rattled up out of the black water, and the gate swung.

"Ride through slow," the woman said. "There's a snag on the left bank where the flood moved it, and we haven't had the men to shift it. And Captain Reyes—" she looked up at me, and for a second the river-stone face cracked and something younger looked out, something that might have been a girl on a dock watching a cargo barge full of refugees go by, "—my mother shipped with you. The winter crossing. She never forgot it. Neither did I."

I didn't trust my voice for a moment, so I just nodded, and put the wheel over, and took the *Perseverance* through the gap.

* * *

Past the boom the gorge opened into a long lake of a reach, water flat as poured lead, and the sentries fell in alongside in their skiff to guide us — which was either courtesy or custody, and I'd been on this river long enough to know it was both. They ran a line ahead of us through the snag field without being asked, which told me they wanted us through, and they posted a man at our stern rail without asking either, which told me they weren't fools.

Xanthe found me at the wheel. He'd been quiet since the gate, and quiet wasn't natural to him; three days in my company had taught him that silence on a boat is just noise waiting for a reason.

"They knew her," he said. "Not the name. *Her.* The way she stood."

"They knew her the way a congregation knows a saint," I said. "Which is worse, for the saint. Saints don't get to be tired or wrong or afraid. They just get remembered."

"She never told me any of this. Forty years of maps with a blank spot on them, and she never once said, *by the way, son, there's a nation in that blank spot and it knows my face.*"

"Would you have believed her?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. I watched him do the arithmetic every child of a secret-keeper eventually does, and I watched the sum come out ugly.

"No," he admitted. "I'd have thought she was touched by the sun. I'd have been kind about it, which is worse."

"Then she did you a kindness, lying by omission. And she did herself one too." I eased the wheel a degree as the reach bent. "People who carry secrets don't keep them because they enjoy the weight, boy. They keep them because they've measured what happens when the secret gets loose, and they've decided they can carry it longer than the world can survive it. Your mother measured. She decided. Don't stand at her grave calling it cowardice."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were a little bit."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him, which had been the point. Then it faded, and he said, "The woman at the gate. She said Crane's people came through. Tried the sign. Were kept in a cellar."

"They did. A decade back, she said." I'd been doing the same arithmetic. "Which means Crane's outfit has known about this gate for ten years and hasn't flattened it."

"Because they can't find it twice?"

"Because a gate you can find is a gate you can burn," I said. "And a burned gate tells you where the town is. No — they let it stand because a gate that tests travelers tells you more than a burnt one. Every stranger who blinks the sign wrong is a lead. Every one who blinks it right is a thread they can follow home." I looked at him. "Your surveyor isn't hunting a chest, Xanthe. He's fishing. And we just swam through his net and told the fish where the boat is."

"He didn't know we'd come this way."

"Crane strikes me as a man who plans for other people being smarter than he expects." I spat to leeward, out of habit. "Which means somewhere behind us, a man who reads rivers is looking at a gorge with a boom across it, and he's not surprised at all."

* * *

The sentries took us into a side channel an hour before dawn, behind a screen of deadfall so artful I had to admire it — from the water you'd see nothing but a log jam and a hillside. Behind it, a basin, a rough dock, and a village that wasn't there.

That's the only honest way to put it. A village that wasn't there. Forty houses, maybe more, climbing the slope in terraces, roofed in split cedar, chimneys cold because it was summer and wood smoke travels. No road in. No wires. A sawmill, a drying shed, a dock with three skiffs and a boat that had once been a gunboat and was now a greenhouse, which told me more about these people than any speech could have. They'd taken a weapon and made it grow things.

And on the gable of the largest house, facing the water where every arriving boat would see it, a seal. Bronze, green with age, big as a wagon wheel: a river coiled through a crown, and letters around the rim in the old script. The same seal that was stamped on the letters riding in a false fuel tank on my deck.

Xanthe stood at the rail and looked at it for a long time.

"It never died," he said finally. "All this time. They just — kept going."

"They kept *going*," I said. "That's different from living, and it's different from dying. It's a third thing, and it costs more than either." I killed the engine and let the barge drift the last length to the dock, hands catching our lines before I'd finished throwing them. "Brace yourself, boy. Whatever you're expecting — the welcome, the tears, the long-lost cousins — halve it, and add suspicion. Towns like this don't survive on love. They survive on discipline. And discipline looks a lot like cold until you've earned the other thing."

The woman from the gate — her name, I'd learned, was Dagny, and she was the sentinel's sister — walked us up the dock and through the streets as the sky went gray behind the ridges. People came out to look. They stood in doorways and beside garden fences, and they looked at Xanthe the way you look at a photograph you'd been told was destroyed. Nobody cheered. Nobody wept. A few touched their collars, some old gesture, and one old man took off his hat and held it against his chest until we'd passed.

"They know," Xanthe said quietly. "They all know who I am."

"Dagny signaled ahead. And a face like yours doesn't need a signal." I nodded at a girl of maybe ten, standing on a fence rail, staring with her whole body. "That one will remember this morning for the rest of her life, and she'll tell her grandchildren she saw the heir come up the river. You'd best decide soon what story she'll be telling."

"I'm not an heir. I'm a bush pilot with a debt."

"Sure," I said. "And I'm a retired captain with a barge."

Dagny stopped us at a long low house at the top of the terraces, built older and better than the rest, with the seal worked into the door lintel in carved wood gone silver with weather. Two guards at the door, young, armed with old rifles kept in beautiful condition.

"Sentinel Wren will see the Okafor man," Dagny said. "Alone."

"The hell he will," I said. "He doesn't go anywhere alone. That's not stubbornness, that's seamanship."

Dagny looked at me, and then at Xanthe, and something almost like humor moved behind the river-stone face. "Your captain doesn't trust us."

"She trusts nobody," Xanthe said. "It's her best quality. She comes, or I don't go in."

"Then the captain comes," Dagny said, "and the captain keeps her mouth shut, and if the sentinel asks her a question directly, the river itself couldn't stop her from answering, I'd wager."

"You'd win that wager," I said.

They took us inside, into a room with a long table and a map on the far wall — a map, I realized after a moment, of the river from source to sea, drawn by hand, with Vessania on it. Whole. Named. Every town marked, including three I'd delivered cargo to in my youth and had spent forty years telling myself I'd imagined.

And at the head of the table sat an old man with white hair and his mother's eyes — Xanthe's mother's eyes, which is to say his, which is to say I understood in one lurch why Dagny had known his face on sight.

Tomas Wren looked at Xanthe Okafor for a long, cold moment, and then he said the words that turned my blood to river water:

"Before you say anything, boy, you should know we've had a radio message. A government surveyor, asking after a barge and a floatplane. He's two days behind you. And he's not coming alone."
