## 13. Settlement Above the Falls

I have been keeper of the gate at New Vessa for eleven years, and in that time I have turned away prospectors, missionaries, a very persistent botanist, and one man who claimed to be selling encyclopedias to a town that does not appear on any map ever printed. None of them frightened me. What frightens me is not strangers at the falls; it is strangers who arrive already knowing the way.

The boy at the watch post came down the switchback trail at a dead run, which is against every rule we have, and shouted the word *Okafor* across the lower terrace, which is against every other rule we have. Old habits die hard in this settlement, and some of them we sharpen rather than bury.

I climbed to the overlook above the falls myself, because if the world had finally found us I intended to see it with my own eyes and not through the breathless report of a fourteen-year-old who had never in his life seen anything worth running about.

There was a barge coming up the pool below the cataract, riding high and stubborn, with a woman at the wheel who took the eddy line like she'd been born on it — which, as it happened, she had. And standing at her rail, holding onto nothing, looking up at our hidden town with an expression I could read even at that distance, was a young man with his mother's face.

That was the trouble, you understand. Not the barge. Not the seal stitched into the letters he carried — though we would learn of those soon enough. The trouble was the face. Forty years I have kept this town alive by forgetting, and here came a boy built entirely out of things I had spent my life trying not to remember.

* * *

Let me explain what kind of place New Vessa is, since no map will do it for you.

We sit in a hanging valley above the last great falls of the Kestrel, where the river drops two hundred feet into a gorge no sane boatman runs twice. The falls are our wall and our moat and our lie. Every survey that has passed within fifty miles has recorded the upper river as unnavigable, because for forty years we have made sure the surveys believed it. We moved channel markers. We let deadfalls accumulate where a chainsaw could clear them in an afternoon. We taught our children to speak plain trade-tongue in public and Vessani only indoors, behind shutters, after dark. We kept the seal — oh, we kept the seal — pressed into wax on documents nobody outside these walls would ever see, stamped into the lintels of houses under coats of whitewash, worked into the patterns of quilts where a government man would see only pretty geometry.

A drowned nation keeps its head above water by learning to look like driftwood.

I was thirty when the decree came down. Emergency dissolution, they called it, as though a nation were a flooded mine or a rabid dog. Soldiers burned the registry offices in Corben's Landing while the archivists stood in the street and watched their life's work go up in ash. My father carried one box of family papers out the back of our house and walked upstream until his boots gave out, and the people who followed him became this town. He used to say we were not refugees. Refugees, he said, are people going somewhere. We were keepers — going nowhere, holding everything.

He died still holding it. Most of us do.

So when I say I looked down from the overlook and saw trouble, understand that I did not see a stranger. Strangers are weather; you wait them out. I saw a face, and faces are history, and history is the one cargo we have never once managed to refuse at the dock.

* * *

They brought him up the cliff path in the late afternoon, the pilot and the river captain both, and I received them in the long room of the council house because that is where unwelcome news is traditionally delivered.

Ilsa Reyes I knew. She had run the refugee barges in the bad years, before most of the present council could walk, and there are perhaps six people alive who know the old toll-signs, and she is one of them. She had flashed the recognition at the narrows sentries below — I heard about it within the hour, relayed by men whose hands were still shaking, not from fear but from something older and more confusing, something like hope, which in my experience is far more dangerous. When Ilsa Reyes shows up at your gate after twenty years of silence, she is either fleeing something or bringing it. Usually both.

But it was the young man I watched. Xanthe Okafor. Even the name was a wound reopened — Okafor, the name of the woman who had refused us.

You will want to know about her, so I will tell you, briefly, the way we tell it here, which is to say incompletely and with feeling. Sera Okafor, born Sera Vessani, daughter of the treasury's last sworn steward. When the decree fell, the elders of that generation made a decision that has sat in this town like a stone in a boot ever since: they would use the treasury — the records, the vault beneath Cape Reckoning, the proof of what had been done to us — as a bargaining chip. Amnesty, recognition, a negotiated restoration. Trade the truth for permission to exist.

Sera said no. She said the truth is not ours to sell; it is ours to keep, and keeping means guarding it, not spending it. The council overruled her. So she took what documents she could carry, went downriver in the night, married a bush pilot, raised a son in exile, and never wrote us a word.

We told ourselves she was dead for thirty years. It was easier.

And now her son stood in my council hall with river water drying on his boots and a canvas satchel under his arm, and I knew — I knew the way you know weather coming in your knees — what was in that satchel, and I knew it would be the death of somebody.

* * *

"You're Tomas Wren," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the man who decides whether you sleep indoors tonight," I said. "The rest is decoration."

He almost smiled. That was her in him too, God help us — that flicker of amusement at pomposity, that refusal to be impressed. Sera had it. Half the fights she lost with this council, she lost laughing.

"Your mother's name is spoken here," I told him, "about once a year, usually as a curse."

"So I've heard," he said. "Ilsa briefed me on the boat." He glanced at Reyes, who was studying our rafters with the fixed attention of a woman pretending not to listen. "Though the briefing ran short on details. Something about a vote, forty years ago. Something about a treasury."

"There is no treasury," said Elder Marsh from his chair by the window, too quickly, the way a man says a thing he has said so often it has worn smooth and means nothing anymore.

"There's a vault," Xanthe said. "Under Cape Reckoning. There are letters in my possession written in my mother's hand saying exactly where it is and what's inside. And there's a government surveyor named Crane who wants it badly enough to burn an archive and take an archivist to get it." He set the satchel on the table between us, gently, the way you set down something that might go off. "So when you're ready to stop lying to each other, I'll start talking."

I have been elder of this town through floods, fires, one famine scare, and the slow grinding erosion of everything my father built, and I will tell you plainly: no one has spoken to this council like that in forty years, and no one has needed to. We had grown polite in our fear. Politeness, I have come to believe, is what courage looks like when it has been left out in the rain too long.

Marsh started to bluster. I stopped him. Some part of me — the part that remembered Sera standing in roughly that spot, roughly that age, with roughly that expression — wanted to hear the boy out. The rest of me wanted him on the next barge down, gone before dark, taking his face and his satchel and his terrible inheritance back to the low country where they belonged.

I did neither. I did what councils do. I called for the room.

* * *

We argued for three hours. I will spare you the shape of it, because you will hear its echo again soon enough and I grow tired just rehearsing it. But the bones were these:

Marsh and the older faction said what they always say. Heirs bring soldiers. Every story we have of the outside world touching this town ends in smoke. Let the boy stay the night, feed him, hear him out, and put him back on the river with a warning and a blessing, and pray whatever wolf is chasing him stays down in the valley where the falling water hides our smoke.

And Ilsa Reyes — who had no seat among us and took one anyway, dragging a chair to the table like a woman boarding a hostile vessel — said the thing that none of us wanted said. She said the wolf was already climbing. She said a gunboat doesn't come up the Kestrel for flood surveys. She said Miriam Sable's archive hadn't burned itself, and that a man who burns archives in Corben's Landing will not be deterred by a waterfall, however scenic.

"He's not chasing the boy," she said. "He's chasing the same thing the boy's carrying. The difference is the boy hasn't decided yet what it's for."

"And what is it for?" Marsh demanded.

"That," said Ilsa, "is the question you've been avoiding for forty years."

I will confess something here, privately, between me and this page, that I have never said aloud in session: I agreed with her. I agreed with her the way a man agrees with a diagnosis he has been refusing to get checked. For forty years we have called ourselves keepers, and somewhere along the way *keeping* had quietly become *hiding*, and hiding had become a kind of slow-motion surrender in which we preserved the seal but surrendered everything it stood for. We were a museum that had locked its own doors from the inside. My father would have spat on what we'd become, and he would have been right to.

But agreement is cheap. Fear is expensive, and we paid it daily, and the ledger balanced, more or less, for four decades. Along comes a boy with his mother's eyes and a bag full of consequences, and suddenly the ledger won't balance at all.

* * *

While the council dithered — I use the word with affection; dithering is a craft and we are masters of it — I did something I had not planned. I walked him up through the town myself.

Not the official tour. The real one. Past the meetinghouse with the seal ground off its cornerstone but still legible if you know where the shadow falls at dusk. Past the school where the children learn Vessani songs as "old harvest rounds" and sing them without knowing what they mourn. Past the well where the census rolls of the first winter are buried in a sealed copper tube beneath three feet of stone, because paper burns and copper waits.

He walked quiet beside me, and I watched him look, and watching him look was the hardest work I had done in years. Because everywhere he turned he found his mother. Not literally — she had never set foot in this town; she was born downriver, in the old country proper — but in the architecture of the faces. The set of the ferryman's jaw. The widow Anselm's way of listening with her whole body tilted forward, hungry for detail. A girl of maybe ten, hauling water, who glanced up at the stranger and dismissed him in exactly the half-second of cool appraisal I remembered from a young woman who once stood in my council hall and told us all we were cowards in a voice like a struck bell.

He stopped at the well. He put his hand flat on the stone, on the spot — he couldn't have known it was the spot, but his hand found it anyway, the way blood finds blood — where the copper tube sleeps.

"They really kept everything," he said. Not a question either. This one seemed to notice he wasn't asking questions, and to find it funny.

"We kept everything," I said, "and it kept nothing safe. That's the arithmetic nobody warned us about."

"My mother—" He stopped. Started again. "She never talked about any of this. Not once. Twenty-six years, and I got a woman who sang songs in a language she claimed was from her grandmother's side, and cooked food she called 'river food,' and never, ever answered a question straight about where she came from." His thumb moved on the stone, small circles, unconscious. "I thought she was ashamed of something. I built my whole life around staying invisible so I wouldn't have to find out what."

"She wasn't ashamed," I said. "She was angry. There's a difference, and it matters. Ashamed people hide themselves. Angry people hide the thing they're protecting." I looked at him properly then. "She hid you, boy. From us as much as from them. She didn't trust this council with the truth, and she didn't trust it with her son, and forty years on, having watched what this council does with truths, I am forced to conclude she was right."

He looked at me then with those eyes — hers, unmistakably, the exact grey-brown of storm water over slate — and I understood that I had just handed him a knife and pointed it at my own chest, and that he was deciding, right there at the well, whether to use it.

He didn't. Instead he said, quietly: "Then help me be worth the risk she took."

And damn him, damn his mother, damn the whole drowned nation and everyone who ever loved it — I felt the old thing move in my chest. Hope. The dangerous one. The one we had spent forty years teaching our children to pronounce carefully, indoors, behind shutters.

* * *

The council reconvened at moonrise and voted, and the vote went the way votes go in frightened towns: we would shelter him one night, hear the full account of the letters in the morning, and decide nothing else until we had decided it slowly, in committee, with subcommittees, possibly until the gunboat arrived to simplify the agenda.

It was cowardice dressed as procedure, and I cast the deciding vote for it, and I have not slept since, and I am writing this by lamplight in the small hours because a man should at least be honest with his own page if he can manage honesty nowhere else.

Because here is what I know, and what the committee structure exists to prevent anyone from saying plainly: the choice is already upon us. Crane is coming. Ilsa says so, the river says so, the boy's bruised and hunted face says so. One night, two at the outside, and then the outside arrives at our door with warrants or with shells, and every careful word we have ever spoken behind shutters will count for precisely nothing. The era of keeping-by-hiding ended the moment that barge touched our landing. We are keepers now or we are nothing, and keeping, as a dead woman tried to tell us forty years ago, means standing in front of the truth, not sitting on it.

Tomorrow morning the boy will open his satchel in this hall and show us letters in his mother's hand, and I will have to look at her handwriting for the first time since she walked out of our lives rather than let us spend what was not ours to spend.

And I have just realized — sitting here, lamplight guttering, the falls muttering their one endless argument below the terraces — that there is a light burning in the guest house where the boy sleeps, and that he is awake in it, and that through the shutter-crack I can see him bent over the satchel, reading, sorting, photographing page after page by the light of a small lamp, working with the urgent methodical patience of a man who knows exactly how little time the world intends to give him.

He is not waiting for our permission. He never was. His mother didn't either.

Dawn is three hours off, and I find I am afraid of it — not of the gunboat, not of Crane, not of soldiers or erasure or any of the forty years of fears I have kept like tools in good repair. I am afraid of a stack of letters in a dead woman's handwriting, and of what they will prove, and of the fact that when they do, this council will finally have to choose between the town we built by forgetting and the nation we might become by remembering.

The boy's lamp is still burning. I think I'll go down and stand in the doorway of that guest house, and ask to see the letters now, tonight, while my hands are still steady enough to hold them.

Some inheritances you meet standing up.
