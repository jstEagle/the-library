## 16. Crane Comes Upstream

I have always believed that a warrant is a beautiful thing, in the way that a loaded gun is a beautiful thing: precisely machined, legally sanctified, and utterly useless against the one problem it was built to solve. A warrant can compel a man's body. It cannot compel his silence, and it certainly cannot compel the particular stubborn silence of a bush pilot who has already decided what kind of man he intends to be.

I stood on the deck of the *Meridian* as she came around the last bend above Corben's Landing, and I watched the smoke of the burned archive still hanging over the trees like a question nobody downstream wanted asked aloud.

Let me be honest, since honesty is about to become the most expensive commodity on this river: I did not burn Miriam Sable's archive. But I knew who had, and I said nothing, and there are days—more of them lately—when I cannot tell the difference between those two facts.

* * *

You should understand something about how I came to be standing on that gunboat, wearing a government surveyor's credentials over a government agent's conscience, if indeed I have one. My mother used to say I was born twice. Once in Vessania, in a river town so small the census-takers spelled its name three different ways, and once in the capital of the nation that erased it, in an office with green walls where a kindly man with a ledger explained to my parents that their son would have a future only if his past ceased to exist.

They called it resettlement. That was the word on the documents, in the elegant bureaucratic script of people who had never heard a shot fired in anger. Resettlement. As though a nation were a flock of geese, easily moved, easily counted, easily forgotten. My parents signed. What else does a man sign when the alternative is spelled out for him in the margins?

What they did not know—what the kindly man with the ledger did not tell them—was that the program had a second tier. Children of certain families, families with names that appeared in certain records, were not merely resettled. They were *kept*. Raised in state schools, taught the manners and accents of the majority, and then, when they came of age, quietly pointed back toward the communities of their birth like compass needles. We were to find what remained. Catalogue it. And deliver it up for destruction.

There were eleven of us in my cohort. I am, as far as I know, the only one still employed. The others broke—one drank himself into the river outside Corben's Landing two years ago, a man named Ferro whose funeral was attended by four people, three of whom were watching him for signs of sentimentality. Sentimentality, in our trade, is a terminal diagnosis.

I tell you this not to earn your pity. Pity is another thing I've learned to do without. I tell you this because you will want to believe, by the end of this chapter, that Aldous Crane is simply a villain, and villains are so much easier to shoot.

* * *

The *Meridian* made good time against the current. She drew too much water for the upper gorge—Ilsa Reyes would have told me that for free, and charged me for the lecture—and so we anchored below the narrows and came up the last twelve miles in the launch, six men and myself, all of us armed, none of us, I suspected, entirely clear on what we were arming for.

The men were river police, seconded from the provincial garrison. Good enough fellows. One of them, a corporal named Dade with a chin like a shovel, kept asking me questions I didn't want answered honestly.

"Surveyor Crane," he said, squinting at the cliffs, "you ever been up this far before?"

"Once," I said. "Years ago."

"Pretty country."

"It is," I agreed, and hated how much I meant it. The gorge narrowed ahead of us, walls going up and up, the water gone the color of bottle glass. Somewhere beyond the falls above us was New Vessa. Somewhere beyond even that was Cape Reckoning, and beneath the cape, if half of what I'd spent nine years assembling was true, was the vault.

Nine years. Let that number sit with you a moment. Nine years of filing reports nobody read, of requesting transfers to river districts nobody wanted, of cultivating informants who turned out to be drunks and drunks who turned out to be informants. All of it pointed here. All of it pointed at a bronze-bound chest that a bush pilot had pulled off a gravel bar by accident, because the river, which forgets nothing, had decided to give something back.

When I read the intercept report—the airfield owner, that vulture Hargrove, sold Xanthe Okafor's movements to anyone with cash and a grudge—I felt something I hadn't felt since childhood. Not triumph. Recognition. Because I knew that name. Okafor. It was in the records I'd been raised on, the ones we memorized and burned in sequence, the ones that named the families of the old treasury wardens. His mother had been born Vessani. Her name was in the third register, the one I'd been assigned to find and never found, because her family had smuggled it out before the burnings and hidden it somewhere no decree could reach.

The chest was the somewhere. I understood that before I understood anything else about the affair, and understanding it changed me in ways I'm still auditing.

Because here is the thing they don't put in the training manuals: when you are raised to hunt your own people, you learn their names before you learn to hate them. I grew up reciting Vessani place-names as lullabies. I knew the words to songs whose meanings were classified. There was a year—I was nineteen, stationed in the capital, learning to forge documents convincingly enough to plant them—when I dreamed every night in a language I officially did not speak.

The kindly man with the ledger would have called that a security concern. I call it the only inheritance I was ever given.

* * *

We came up on the settlement at dusk, or rather, we came up on the falls below the settlement, and the launch couldn't go further, and that was the first lesson of the day: geography itself had been enlisted on the side of the drowned nation. Every approach to New Vessa funneled through water that ate propellers. Whoever built the town had built it behind a lock, and the key was the river.

I left Corporal Dade and three men with the launch and took two up the portage trail, and we walked into New Vessa in the last light like a delegation from a country that no longer existed, which, in every way that mattered, we were.

Elder Tomas Wren met us at the head of the trail with his arms crossed and his face arranged into the expression of a man greeting tax collectors. Behind him stood perhaps thirty townspeople, and I did the arithmetic automatically—it was trained into me, counting the descendants of listed families, estimating the size of unregistered settlements—and the arithmetic told me something the reports never had: they were more numerous than the files claimed, better organized than the files admitted, and watching me with a collective attention that made my neck prickle.

"You have papers," Wren said. It wasn't a question. He wanted to see them the way a man wants to see the fangs of a snake he's about to handle.

I gave him the warrant. It named Xanthe Okafor, cited the salvage statutes, demanded the surrender of recovered property of uncertain provenance pending adjudication. Elegant language. I'd drafted a version of it myself, months ago, filed away for exactly this contingency. Seeing it in print, stamped and sealed, I felt the way a forger feels looking at his own work hanging in a museum: proud, and sick, and unable to say which feeling was winning.

Wren read it twice. Then he looked past me, at the two armed men dripping on his trail, and said, "And if we decline?"

"Then this becomes a matter for the provincial garrison," I said, "and the garrison has gunboats, and the gunboats have guns, and none of us wants the afternoon that follows."

He looked at me a long moment. There was something in his face—not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has seen my kind before, in whatever earlier decade my predecessors came up this river with warrants and left it with ash.

"The pilot isn't here," he said finally. "He flew out at dawn."

That was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, because I'd watched the fog all morning and no plane had come down the gorge, and a floatplane with a cracked float doesn't fly anywhere except into a hangar. But I let the lie stand, because lies between professionals are a form of courtesy, and because what I actually wanted could not be obtained with a warrant anyway.

"I'll wait," I said.

* * *

They quartered us in a guest house at the edge of the settlement, which was either hospitality or containment depending on your reading, and I found it was both. The room had a bed, a chair, a window facing the falls, and a door that locked from the outside, which the corporal discovered at midnight and reported to me with some indignation.

"They're treating us like prisoners," he said.

"We're six armed strangers in a town full of people with everything to lose," I said. "How would you treat us?"

He didn't have an answer for that. Dade was a decent man; that was going to be a problem later, and I noted it in the mental ledger where I keep such things.

I didn't sleep. I sat by the window and listened to the falls and thought about my mother, who died believing she had protected me by forgetting, and about the letters in Okafor's chest, which I had never seen but could describe from the intercept summaries down to the watermark. Vessani rag paper. The seal pressed in wax mixed with river sand, a forgery-proofing trick the old chancellery used. My own handlers had shown me specimens during training, handled them like relics of a plague.

At some hour past two, a shape crossed the yard below my window. Broad-shouldered, moving carefully, carrying something under one arm. I watched it resolve into Xanthe Okafor, heading toward the boathouse, and I understood that the negotiation I'd been waiting nine years for was not going to happen across a table.

It was going to happen in a boathouse, at night, between two men who had every reason to lie to each other and, as it turned out, exactly one reason not to.

* * *

I slipped the lock on my door—it was an old lock, and locks are a hobby of mine—and went down through the settlement without meeting a soul, which told me the sentries had been pulled back deliberately. Someone had decided to let the meeting happen. Wren, probably. Or the council. Men who keep a nation alive in secret for forty years develop a taste for controlled exposures.

The boathouse smelled of pitch and cold water. Okafor was waiting inside with a lantern turned low, and when I came through the door he didn't reach for the rifle leaning against the wall beside him, which surprised me, and then didn't, because I had already begun to suspect that this was a man who did his arithmetic before his fear.

"Surveyor Crane," he said. "Or should I use the other name?"

The other name. My name. The one on the third register, the family line, the one I had buried so deep under twenty years of assumed identity that sometimes I startled when I saw it written. *Vessani-born.* He knew. Of course he knew—the letters in the chest would have carried the treasury wardens' correspondence, and wardens' correspondence names its counterparts, and somewhere in that paper my grandfather's signature sat next to his great-grandmother's.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, deciding whether to be insulted or relieved, and settled, as usual, on both.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Since the letters." He turned the lantern up a notch. "Your family name appears in the warden rolls. Twice. Your grandfather countersigned the transfer order for the northern treasury. Which means either your people helped build the vault—" he paused, letting the sentence hang, "—or they helped bury it."

"Both," I said. "In sequence. That's rather the whole history of Vessania in miniature. Build, then bury. Sign, then deny. My cohort was the third generation of the denial."

"And now you've come upstream with a gunboat to finish it."

"No," I said, and here is where I ask you to extend me the same credit I extended him, which is to say: almost none, pending evidence. "Now I've come upstream because the people who sent me are not the people I work for anymore. If they ever were."

He studied me the way he'd study weather, I imagine—reading pressure, reading light, deciding whether to fly into it. Then he said, "Tell me about the archive fire."

And there it was. The question I'd been dreading since Corben's Landing, delivered without heat, without accusation, in the flat tone of a man who already knows the answer and is measuring what I'll do with it.

"I didn't set it," I said. "But I knew it would be set. I was consulted on the operation. I recommended delay. My recommendation was noted and overridden, which is the standard career arc of a conscience in my service." I sat down on a coil of rope, because my legs had opinions about standing. "Miriam Sable is alive. She's being held at the provincial depot, not the capital—they're keeping her close because they think she knows where the vault is, and they're right, and she hasn't told them, and every day she doesn't tell them is a day she pays for in ways I won't describe to you because you'll do something stupid."

"Like what?"

"Like trading yourself for her. Which is what you're thinking. I can see it. Don't. They wouldn't honor the trade. They never honor trades. That's practically the agency motto."

He was quiet for a while. The falls filled the silence, that enormous patient white noise, the sound of a river that had drowned a nation and kept its receipts.

"Why are you telling me this?" he said finally.

"Because I'm tired," I said. It sounded inadequate even as I said it, so I made myself say the rest, the part I had rehearsed for nine years and never once performed aloud. "Because I was four years old when they took my name, and I have spent my entire adult life being very good at a job whose purpose is to make sure that no one like me ever exists again. Find the remnants. Burn the records. Close the accounts. And every year I got better at it, and every year some small auditor in the back of my skull filed objections, and last spring, when the intercept report crossed my desk with the name Okafor on it—a name I knew, a name from the registers, a name that meant the third register had surfaced after forty years—I understood that I was being handed the end of my own assignment. Deliver the chest, and the job is done, and the job being done means the erasure is complete, and the erasure being complete means that everything my mother surrendered me for was for nothing."

"So you want the vault for yourself."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation, and it was, in the technical sense, accurate, and I let him have it because precision was the only currency I had left.

"Yes," I said. "I want the vault. Not for the ministry. Not for the garrison. For the record. Do you understand the difference? The ministry wants the documents destroyed because destroyed documents can't testify. I want them *read*. Every page. Every census entry. Every forged signature exposed to daylight. I want the dissolution decree held up next to the originals it falsified until the difference is visible from orbit. I have spent nine years building the case that would do it, and I am missing exactly one thing, and it is sitting under Cape Reckoning, and you are the only man on this river who can open it."

"Why me?"

"Because the vault answers to blood, or to keys, or to both, and I have neither. Because the elders trust you—or distrust me more, which functions identically. And because, Mr. Okafor, you pulled that chest off a gravel bar by accident, and I have watched you carry it since, and you have never once treated it as loot. You treat it as mail. Undelivered mail, forty years late, addressed to people who were told they no longer exist." I leaned forward. "I know what that is. I was raised by the people who wrote the return-to-sender stamp."

* * *

He didn't answer for a long time. Outside, the falls. Inside, the lantern burning down, and two men doing the arithmetic of trust, which is the slowest arithmetic there is.

"What happens at dawn?" he asked eventually. "Your corporal expects an answer. The warrant expects an answer. The gunboat on the river expects an answer."

"At dawn I report that the property named in the warrant could not be located," I said. "That the settlement denied knowledge of it. That pursuit continues downstream. It buys you days, not weeks. My superiors are not patient, and the man who actually runs this file—a deputy director named Corvus, whom I have met exactly twice and disliked both times—will come upriver himself if I stall too long. When Corvus comes, he doesn't bring a warrant. He brings a company."

"And Miriam?"

"If the vault opens, Miriam lives. That's not a promise, it's a mechanism. Hostages are only useful while the secret holds. The moment the record goes public—courts, newspapers, the works—she stops being leverage and starts being a witness, and witnesses to a forged decree get lawyers, and lawyers get her out. It's the only exit from that depot that doesn't run through a wall."

"You've thought about this."

"For nine years," I said. "I've thought about little else. It's why I drink alone. Deliberate planning is very bad for the social life."

He almost smiled. I catalogued it, the way I catalogue everything, and then I made myself stop cataloguing, because a man who catalogs everything trusts nothing, and I had just bet nine years on the possibility that trust might occasionally be warranted.

"Dawn," he said. "You give me until dawn. And then what? You sail away and I open a vault and mail photographs to newspapers and hope your deputy director doesn't burn the valley down before the ink dries?"

"That is approximately the plan," I said. "I've had worse. Most of them were mine."

"One condition." He stood, and the lantern threw his shadow tall against the boat slats, and for a moment I saw it—the resemblance I'd been briefed on and never believed until now. His mother's jaw. The warden line, written in bone. "When it opens, you're there. You see it with your own eyes. And when you find your grandfather's name in the rolls—if it's there—you read it aloud. To the elders. To everyone. No anonymity. No deniability. If I burn my name down, Crane, yours burns with it."

I had expected money, or immunity, or the chest. Instead he asked for the one thing I had spent twenty years protecting, and I found—to my genuine surprise, the first real surprise of my professional life—that giving it up felt less like a loss than like a return.

"Agreed," I said. "My grandfather's name was Aurel. Aurel Crane-Vessa. They made me drop the second part when I was six. I've been waiting forty years to say it in front of witnesses."

He looked at me the way men look at each other across a card table when the last card turns and neither of them bluffs.

"Dawn," he said again, and offered his hand, and I shook it, and the handshake of two conspirators is a strange document—unwritten, unwitnessed, binding as a deed.

* * *

I climbed back through my window at half past three, lay down on the bed without undressing, and listened to the falls until the corporal knocked at six.

"Well, Surveyor?" Dade asked, when I'd dressed and splashed water on my face. "The pilot. The chest. What's the word?"

"The property could not be located," I said. "The settlement denies all knowledge. We're dropping back downriver to continue the survey."

He frowned. "That's it? We came all this way?"

"All this way," I agreed, "and further, before it's done. Get the launch ready, Corporal. And when the radio traffic starts—and it will start—remember that you heard nothing from me that you couldn't have guessed."

He went off down the path, puzzled and obedient, a good man being wasted on a bad errand, and I stood at the window and watched the light come up over the falls, gray going to gold, and I thought about my grandfather's name, and my mother's silence, and the vault under the cape filling and emptying with the tide like a heart that had been beating in the dark for forty years, waiting for someone to count the beats.

Until dawn, I had promised him. But dawn was only the deadline. What came after dawn was the reckoning—for the ministry, for the settlement, for the drowned nation, and for a man named Aldous Crane, who had spent his whole life being a forgery and had, sometime in the last twelve hours, stopped.

The tide would turn at midday. So would everything else.
