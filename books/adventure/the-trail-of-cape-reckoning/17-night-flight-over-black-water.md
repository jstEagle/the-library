## 17. Night Flight Over Black Water

The floatplane came apart in my hands and went back together in theirs.

That's the honest accounting of that day. I'd landed her hard on the rocks two nights before—cracked the left float, bent a strut, dinged a propeller blade that would need more than a file and a prayer—and I'd taxied her into New Vessa's hidden lagoon expecting to do the work alone, by lamplight, the way I did everything. Alone was how I'd built my whole life. It was load-bearing architecture. Take it out and you weren't sure what stood.

But by dawn there were six men around her, and by noon there were twelve, and someone's grandmother had sent down coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in, and a boy of maybe fourteen named Pell who talked to engines the way other boys talked to horses had already diagnosed the strut without being asked.

"She'll fly," he said, flat as a man reporting weather. "Float's the problem. We don't have birch ply."

"We have birch ply," said an old woman named Hesta, who turned out to be the settlement's entire shipwright trade compressed into one small furious person. "We've had birch ply since before your mother was born, boy. What we haven't had is pilots who land on rocks."

"Fair," I said.

She glared at me for another full second, which I learned was her way of deciding she liked me, and then put everyone to work.

I want to be clear about what that afternoon actually was, because at the time I thought it was just repair. It wasn't. It was the first time in my life I'd let strangers touch something of mine. Every plane I'd ever owned, every engine I'd ever rebuilt, I'd done it with the hangar doors shut. My father taught me that way—"a man's work is his own business"—and after he died I kept the lesson like a superstition. Let people help you and they learn where you're soft.

But these people already knew where I was soft. They'd known my mother. Some of them had known her as a girl; Hesta remembered her running along this same dock with her hair loose, laughing at nothing, before the world decided Vessania was a word that shouldn't exist anymore. They knew my name—not Okafor, the name I'd flown under for twenty years, but the other one, the one that made old faces go careful and quiet when they said it.

So I stood in the shallows holding a float section steady while strangers riveted, and I felt something I didn't have vocabulary for yet. It took me until dark to name it. It was the feeling of not being watched. Of being *known* instead. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

The plane flew by dusk. Pell insisted on a test circuit over the falls, and I took him up because refusing would have been ruder than risking him, and because some part of me wanted witnesses when she lifted off clean. She did. She climbed out of the lagoon like she'd been insulted by the question.

Then I went looking for Ilsa.

* * *

I found her at the barge, coiling line in the dark by feel, which is a thing river people can do and I never learned. She didn't look up.

"You're flying tonight," she said. Not a question. News travels fast in a town with no roads.

"Reconnaissance. Crane's gunboat is sitting off Cape Reckoning, and tomorrow the council votes, and I'm not walking into either one blind."

"And you need a navigator."

"I need you."

She set down the coil slowly, like she was buying time to decide whether to be annoyed. "Xanthe. You've flown that coast alone since you were nineteen."

"Not at night. Not over water I've never charted. And not with a gunboat down there that has a searchlight and opinions." I crouched next to her. "You ran cargo past Cape Reckoning for ten years. You know where the water lies about itself. That's not navigation, that's survival, and I want it in the right-hand seat."

She was quiet long enough that I heard the river working against the hull below us.

"There's a rule," she said finally. "Mine. I don't fly. Thirty years, never once. Bad enough being on the water when things go wrong. In the air there's nowhere to put your hands."

"So stay ashore."

"That's not what I said." She looked at me then, and whatever she saw in my face made her exhale through her nose, the sound she made instead of admitting things. "What I said is: if I come, you fly the way I say. Slow approaches. No showing off. And if I tell you to climb, you climb first and argue later."

"Deal."

"It's not a deal. It's terms." She stood, shouldered a canvas bag with charts older than both of us rolled inside it, and walked toward the dock without waiting for me, which was also her way of admitting things.

* * *

We went up the coast in full dark, no lights but the instrument glow, the moon down behind cloud. The engine at cruise sounded like a held breath. Below us the sea was the blackest thing I've ever seen—not dark, black, a black with weight to it, the kind of water that doesn't reflect so much as absorb. The locals called it the Black Water, the stretch between the falls and the cape, and they spoke about it the way sailors everywhere speak about the patch of ocean that has taken their friends.

Ilsa navigated with a stopwatch and a wrist compass and a chart she kept folded to a rectangle the size of a hymnal. She called headings without being asked, corrected for a current I couldn't see, and twice told me to climb thirty seconds before the ridgeline loomed out of the murk exactly where she said it would.

"How?" I asked, the second time.

"Your mother's grandfather drew that chart. He marked the updrafts." A pause. "Everyone in New Vessa can read that coastline. It's the one thing they were allowed to keep."

The cape itself announced itself by absence—a place where the surf noise changed pitch, where the black water went suddenly pale around a reef line. And there, riding at anchor just beyond the reef, the gunboat: a gray shape with a single light burning amidships, its searchlight hooded, its silhouette wrong in the way military silhouettes are always wrong, all function and no grace.

I throttled back to near-stall and let the plane drift downwind, engine idling, a leaf falling toward a pond. It's a trick for getting eyes on something without your engine note carrying. Ilsa held the stopwatch and counted our drift against the current while I leaned over the window and memorized.

Two launches tied astern. Deck gun forward, covered. Searchlight aft, hooded but mounted, and the mount was trained shoreward—which meant they expected trouble from the land, not the sea. A watch fire on the cape itself, small, disciplined, positioned to signal the boat. Four figures around it, maybe five.

"They're watching the cliff paths," Ilsa murmured. "Not the waterline."

"Why?"

"Because nobody's stupid enough to come by water." She almost smiled. "Except us."

I brought us around in a slow silent arc and that's when I saw it—the thing I'd flown a hundred miles to find and hadn't dared believe was real. At the base of the cape, where the black water met the rock, there was a seam. A vertical darkness in the cliff face, maybe four meters wide, half-drowned, invisible unless the light lay along it exactly right. The swell breathed in and out of it. A cave. A sea cave, tidal, entered only when the water allowed.

And cut into the rock above the seam, worn nearly smooth by forty years of salt, a mark. I couldn't read it from the air. But I knew its shape the way you know a face in a crowd you've been searching your whole life. The seal. The same seal that was stamped on the letters in my mother's chest. The same seal on the charter now folded in my map case.

"The vault entrance," I said.

"Under their searchlight," Ilsa said. "Inside their patrol radius. Under the guns of a boat that thinks the answer is buried somewhere it can dig." She was quiet a moment. "They've been shelling guesses. The truth's been under their anchor chain the whole time."

I laughed—couldn't help it—and immediately hated myself for it, because the sound carried over water at night farther than you'd think. Ilsa's hand found my arm and squeezed, not gently, and I throttled up and climbed away south before the echo finished dying.

* * *

We made three more passes that night. On the second, the gunboat's searchlight woke and swept the cloud base, lazy, bored, a man checking a fence he didn't believe needed checking. I put the ridge between us and let the engine sing low. On the third pass I photographed the cave mouth with a camera I'd rigged to the door frame, timing exposures between swells, and Ilsa timed the tide tables against her grandfather's corrections and gave me the sentence that mattered more than any photograph:

"Low tide, tomorrow night, twenty-one-forty. Window of ninety minutes. After that the cave closes for eleven hours, and the next window comes too late."

Ninety minutes. To go in, open a vault nobody had opened in forty years, and come out ahead of the water. With a gunboat at anchor overhead and a man named Crane who had until recently been my friend, or close enough to it that the difference still hurt to think about.

On the run home, with the cape falling behind and the black water sliding beneath us like poured ink, Ilsa reached across and rested her hand on top of mine on the yoke. Not steering. Just weight. Warmth through leather.

"I've been thinking about your rules," I said.

"Don't."

"You have three. Don't fly, don't owe anybody, don't—" I stopped, because I'd been about to recite them back to her and realized I only knew two for certain, and inventing a third felt like theft.

"Those are the two," she said. "There used to be a third. I retired it."

"What was it?"

"Don't love anyone the river can take." Her hand stayed where it was. "I retired it the night your mother's chest came out of the water, though it took me until now to file the paperwork."

I didn't say anything. There's a kind of silence that's better than speech and I've spent my whole life living in the other kind, so I know one when I fall into it. The engine hummed. The stars came through a tear in the cloud, indifferent and enormous. Her hand stayed on mine.

"I'm saying it plainly," she said, "because plain is the only honest currency left in this story. I love you, Xanthe Okafor. Whatever name you were flying under. Both names. The one the maps get to keep and the one they don't. I love you the way I love the river, which is to say: knowing exactly what it's capable of, and choosing it anyway."

Over black water, between passes, with a gunboat at my back and a vault under my keel and a council vote coming that might split the only people who'd ever known me whole—I laughed again, quietly this time, and turned my hand over under hers so we were palm to palm.

"I filed my flight plan honestly for the first time last spring," I said. "It listed you as next of kin. Nobody caught the error. I decided not to correct it."

"That's not an error."

"No," I said. "It's the truest line I ever wrote."

We flew home the long way, along the coast she'd charted and her grandfather before her, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing left that needed saying and everything left that needed doing. Behind us the cape stood black against blacker water, keeping its secret one more night, the seal worn smooth above the drowned door, patient as stone, waiting on the tide.

Twenty-one-forty tomorrow. Ninety minutes. One chance.

At the lagoon, wheels—or floats—down, I cut the engine and we drifted the last hundred meters in silence, and Ilsa said, looking at the dark shoreline where lanterns were already moving to meet us:

"Whatever the council decides, the tide doesn't wait on votes."

"No," I said. "It never has."

And somewhere behind us, out on the black water, the gunboat's searchlight swept the clouds one last time and went dark—like something settling in to wait, too.
