## 18. The Vault Beneath the Cape

The tide table said we had four hours. The tide itself, when I stood at the mouth of the sea cave and watched the water peel back from black stone like a curtain being drawn by a bored stagehand, looked less like a schedule and more like a threat being politely postponed.

"Four hours," Ilsa said, checking her watch against the horizon the way river people do, as if the ocean were a subordinate who might lie to her. "Three if the swell's wrong. Two and a half if everything goes the way it usually does when you're involved."

"I'm standing right here."

"I know," she said. "That's my point."

Tomas Wren said nothing at all. He had not said much since New Vessa voted to split down its old fault line—half the council wanting to hand the letters over and buy peace, half wanting to open the vault and gamble on truth—and he had volunteered himself for this trip in the tone of a man volunteering for a firing squad so that nobody younger has to stand against the wall. He carried a storm lantern in one hand and a coil of rope over his shoulder, and he walked like a man visiting a grave he'd helped dig.

We went in with the water at our knees.

* * *

I want to tell you the cave was dramatic. I want to give you stalactites like teeth and an echo that whispered your name back at you. What I can give you instead is honest: it was cold, and wet, and smelled of kelp and old salt, and the ceiling came down low enough in places that Ilsa had to duck without letting go of the lantern, which she managed with the economy of a woman who had spent forty years moving cargo through spaces cargo was never meant to fit.

My mother had grown up twenty miles from this coast. She had flown out of it, paddled out of it, lied her way out of it, and died in a rented room two thousand miles north with a lockbox full of letters she never explained. I had spent thirty-eight years being angry at her for the silence. Standing in the throat of the cave she must have known about since childhood—the vault beneath Cape Reckoning, named in her own handwriting in a diary I'd found under a floorboard—I felt the anger go out of me like air out of a punctured float.

She hadn't been hiding the truth from me. She'd been carrying it alone because everyone else who might have shared the weight was either dead or bought.

"You're doing the thing," Ilsa said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The face. Where you look at nothing and have a whole argument with somebody who isn't there."

"My mother."

"Yeah." She didn't ask if I was winning. She knew better than anyone alive that you don't win those arguments; you just get far enough into them to hear the other side clearly.

Tomas held up a fist. We stopped. Ahead, the passage bent left, and around the bend came a gray, sourceless light—not daylight, not lantern light. Water sound, too, but different: a deep, patient breathing, like the cave itself was asleep and dreaming about the sea.

"That's the antechamber," Tomas said. "My grandfather brought me here when I was nine. Told me if I ever told anyone, the sea would take our name off the earth the way the decree took Vessania off the maps." He paused. "I told no one for fifty years. Then your mother's letters turned up in a chest on a gravel bar, and it seemed the sea had other plans."

The gray light was coming through a fissure near the ceiling—a crack wide enough to show sky, narrow enough that no aircraft would ever spot it unless it already knew where to look. Below it, the floor shelved down into a pool, and beyond the pool rose a wall of fitted stone.

Not natural stone. Fitted. Cut blocks, mortared, dressed—the kind of work you do once, carefully, when you intend what you build to outlast the country that ordered it built.

"There," Tomas said, and his voice did something I hadn't heard it do before. It cracked.

* * *

The wall had a door. Of course it had a door. My mother's letters had described it in the flat, inventory-like prose she used for anything that mattered: *iron-bound oak, three locks, keys with the family papers.* Three locks. The letters had contained two keys, small, brass, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the chest. The third key—

"The third key is mine," Tomas said. He took a cord from around his neck. On it hung a key so old the brass had gone the color of dark honey, worn smooth as a river stone by three generations of skin. "Warden's key. There are supposed to be seven families who hold them. Four lines ended. One fled south and was never heard from again. One—" he looked at me— "was your mother's. Which makes you either the seventh key or the proof that the system failed."

"No pressure," Ilsa murmured.

"Every pressure," Tomas said. "That's rather the point of a vault."

The locks fought us. The first opened clean, as if it had been waiting. The second needed Ilsa's hands—she had a touch with stubborn mechanisms, a patience I lacked, working the key in small increments, listening to the tumblers the way you listen to an engine that's running rough. The third lock, the warden's, turned for Tomas with a sound like a knuckle cracking after a long silence.

Then the door simply swung, and the gray light from the fissure fell across the interior, and none of us moved for a long moment.

No gold.

I'll admit it: some part of me, the part raised on salvage law and the arithmetic of debts, expected gold. Chests are for gold. Seals are for gold. Nations kill each other over gold. But the vault beneath Cape Reckoning held shelves. Row upon row of them, cut into the living rock, and on the shelves, in oilcloth and lead-lined boxes and sealed jars rendered watertight by some forgotten craft, lay paper.

Paper, and more paper, and ledgers with brass corners, and scrolls rolled tight and sleeved in copper, and—on a central table, under a glass case furred with forty years of condensation—a single document laid open, its ink still legible, its seal still red.

The Great Seal of Vessania. Pressed into wax the color of dried blood. Around it, in a formal hand I now recognized from my mother's letters, ran the words of the founding charter of a nation that officially had never existed.

"It's all here," Tomas said. His voice had gone strange and young. He walked the shelves like a man in a church, touching nothing. "The treaties. The land grants. The census rolls—all of them, every household, every year." He pulled a ledger free with hands that shook. "The tax records. The shipping manifests. Correspondence with—" He stopped. Read. Stopped again. "Correspondence with the colonial office. Dated *after* the dissolution decree."

Ilsa and I exchanged a look over the lantern.

"After?"

"They dissolved us in an emergency session," Tomas said slowly, "and then they kept writing to us. Requesting census updates. Confirming trade terms." He set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that could detonate. "You don't send trade confirmations to a country that doesn't exist. Unless the country does exist, and the decree is—"

"A forgery," I finished. "Or at least a fiction. Something signed by men who needed Vessania erased on paper while keeping it useful in practice."

The word landed in the cave and echoed off the stone. Somewhere above us, water dripped into the pool with a regular, indifferent *plip*, keeping time like a metronome for a verdict.

My mother had known. That was the vertigo of it, standing there among the shelves. She hadn't fled a dying country. She'd fled a *murdered* one—one killed on paper by men whose signatures she could probably name, whose crime sat forty feet below the tide line in boxes her family had helped seal. And she had refused, per her diary, to let the elders use that truth as a bargaining chip, because a truth used as a chip stops being true and starts being currency, and currency gets spent.

"So we don't sell it," I said.

"We photograph it," Ilsa said. "Every page. Before the tide comes back, before Crane comes back, before anybody's superiors get any cleverer than they already are."

I had the camera. I'd carried it since the night flight, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heartbeat. We worked fast and silent—Tomas lifting documents, Ilsa turning pages with cotton-wrapped fingers, me shooting frame after frame, the flash strobing off wax seals and iron gall ink until the cave flickered like a lighthouse in a storm. Treaties. Censuses. The forged decree itself, found in a box marked with the warden's mark, alongside the draft versions with the corrections visible—the honest pencil marks of dishonest men.

Somewhere in the third hour, my hands stopped shaking.

That's the thing nobody tells you about inheritance. You spend your whole life thinking it's money, or property, or a name on a deed. You dig through a drowned chest expecting treasure and find letters. You fly through the night over black water expecting a vault of gold and find a library. And somewhere in the middle of cataloguing the evidence of your own country's murder, you understand that the treasure was never the point. The record is the point. Somebody wrote all this down because they believed someone, someday, would need it—and the whole architecture of the vault, the locks and the wardens and the seven families, existed for one purpose only: to keep faith across time with a reader who hadn't been born yet.

I was the reader. Forty years late, but present.

"Xanthe." Ilsa's hand closed on my arm. Not gently.

I followed her eyes to the entrance passage.

Lantern light. Moving. Coming toward us along the flooded floor, low and steady, the walk of a man who knew the ground.

* * *

He stepped around the bend with his pistol held low and his coat dark with seawater to the waist, and Aldous Crane looked—for the first time since Mile Nine—exactly like what he was: tired, wet, and out of official script.

"Tide turns in fifty minutes," he said. Conversationally. As if we'd all arranged to meet.

"How did you find the fissure?" Tomas demanded.

"I've known about this cave since I was six years old," Crane said, and something in his voice made the hair on my arms stand up. "My father used to bring me to the cape. He'd point at the rocks and say, *underneath all that is your country, boy, and your job is to make sure it stays underneath.*" He raised the pistol—not aiming, just holding it, the way a man holds a tool he hasn't decided to use. "I was raised to be the seventh key too. Just on the other side of the lock."

Ilsa shifted half a step, putting herself between the muzzle and Tomas. I put a hand on her shoulder. Not yet. The tide was a clock running for all of us, and Crane knew it as well as I did.

"You've seen it, then," he said, nodding at the shelves. "The correspondence. The drafts." His jaw worked. "I read enough of it on the way in to understand what my government sent me to bury. And I am asking myself, standing in my ancestors' cellar with a gun in my hand, why exactly I'm holding it."

"Because dawn's your deadline," I said. "And because men like your employers don't raise sons to ask questions."

"No," Crane agreed quietly. "They don't. They raise them to hold tools." He looked at the glass case, at the founding charter sleeping under its fur of condensation, and for one long moment Aldous Crane—the surveyor, the ledger-keeper, the man who photographed logbooks and threatened certificates—looked like a boy of six again, pointing at rocks.

Above us, faint through the fissure, came a sound neither tide nor drip could account for.

An engine. Out on the water. Getting closer.

Crane heard it too. His grip changed on the pistol, and whatever he'd been about to decide, the deciding suddenly got urgent.

"That's early," he said. "That's much too early."

The water in the entrance passage began, very softly, to rise.
