## 19. Cape Reckoning

I have held a gun on three people in my life, and I want to say something honest before the water finishes its argument: it never once made me feel powerful. It made me feel like a man standing very still on a floor that was already moving.

The cave beneath Cape Reckoning was a cathedral that had been built by accident—vaulted black stone, walls slick with forty years of salt spray, and at its center, half-swallowed by shadow, an iron door set into the rock like a tooth. The Vessani seal was worked into the iron: the gull over the crossed oars, worn nearly smooth but still legible to anyone who had been taught to read it as a child and then forbidden, on pain of everything, to ever read it again.

I had been taught. That is the joke nobody in this story will find funny except me.

"Step away from the door," I said.

Xanthe Okafor turned slowly, hands rising, and even now—even with my pistol leveled at his chest and the sea coming for all of us—he had the gall to look *amused*. "Crane. You brought a gunboat to a library."

"I brought a warrant."

"To a nation that doesn't exist." He tilted his head toward the iron door. "You can't serve paper on a ghost. Believe me, I've tried being one."

Behind him, Ilsa Reyes stood with her back against the vault door itself, arms spread across the old seal like she could hold it shut through sheer stubbornness, which knowing her, she probably believed she could. And behind her, Tomas Wren—the elder, the careful man, the one who had voted to bury the truth rather than dig it up—stood holding a lantern that shook just enough to make the shadows breathe.

Three people. One lantern. One gun. Mine.

And outside, beyond the mouth of the cave, the grey morning light and the greyer water, where the gunboat *Meridian* sat at anchor with her deck gun trained on the cape because Director Hale did not send me anywhere without insurance. The tide was turning. I could hear it in the throat of the cave—that low, wet swallowing sound, the ocean clearing its sinuses. We had perhaps twenty minutes before the passage behind us went under, and everyone in this room knew it except, possibly, me, who had known it since yesterday and come anyway.

"Open the vault," I said.

"No," said Wren.

"Yes," said Xanthe, at exactly the same moment.

They looked at each other. Even now, even here, they couldn't agree on anything. It would have been funny if my hands weren't starting to ache from gripping the pistol too hard.

"You want it open?" Xanthe said. "Fine. But you should know what you're pointing that thing at first." He reached, slow, deliberate, into the oilskin satchel slung across his chest, and I thumbed the safety off and he stopped and gave me a look of such profound, patient disappointment that I felt nineteen years old again, caught smoking behind the academy boathouse.

"Photographs," he said. "Paper copies. I flew over this cape last night while your gunboat was playing lighthouse. I mapped your approach, your anchor point, and the fact that your crew takes their coffee break at 0540, which, Aldous, honestly. Professional standards." He drew out a folded sheaf. "But that's not what I want to show you."

"Don't," Wren said quietly. "Boy, don't."

"He deserves to see it," Xanthe said. "He's earned it. He rowed all the way upriver for it."

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, and what I told no one in that cave, and what I am telling you now only because the water has already taken the original and there is no one left to lie to:

My mother sang me to sleep in a language the government had decreed had never existed. She sang it under her breath, after checking the door, the way other mothers checked for monsters. When I was seven, men in grey coats came to our flat in the resettlement district and asked her questions about her grandmother's maiden name, and she answered them in flawless standard speech with a smile like poured concrete, and afterward she washed her hands for a long time and didn't sing for a month.

She told me we were from nowhere. She told me the past was a debt collectors' trick. She married a surveyor's clerk and changed her vowels and raised her son to be so thoroughly, catastrophically loyal to the state that erased her family that he grew up and took a job erasing things professionally.

I found out what she was when I was thirty-one, going through her effects after the funeral. A christening gown with a name embroidered inside the hem. A name that appeared in none of the census rolls I was authorized to search, because the rolls had been burned, because the decree had been signed, because a country the size of a county had been unwritten in an afternoon and everyone who remembered it had learned to be from nowhere.

I reported the gown. I want that on the record too. I put it in an evidence bag and I filed it, because that is what I was, that is what I had been built to be, and the file went into a drawer in Corben's Landing and I told myself the drawer was dark enough.

Then Okafor pulled a bronze-bound chest out of a river, and the drawer opened.

* * *

"Show him," Ilsa Reyes said. Not to me—to Xanthe. Her voice was gravel and river water. "Show the man what he came all this way not to find."

Xanthe unfolded the photographs and held them out. I didn't take them. He stepped closer—I let him, God help me, I let him—and laid them on a shelf of rock between us, weighting the corners with two stones, like a merchant setting out wares.

Census pages. Photographed in lamplight, crisp, legible. The great registry of New Vessa, kept in duplicate for sixty years by people who understood exactly what kind of fire was coming and refused to be surprised by it.

"Read it," Xanthe said. "Or don't. But you should know that I know why you're really here, and it isn't Hale's warrant."

"The warrant is real."

"The warrant is real," he agreed. "Warrants usually are. That's what makes them useful." He tapped the top photograph. "Page eleven. Look at page eleven."

I looked. Of course I looked. I had been looking at that page since Mile Nine, since the archivist Sable told me someone else had been asking, since the moment I understood that the chest in Okafor's hangar was the key to a door I had spent my whole life pretending wasn't in the wall.

The column headings were in the old script. Family names, districts, ages, trades. And there, halfway down, in a clerk's careful hand:

**CRANE, ALDOUS — b. Vessa Harbour, aged 3 months. Mother: Crane, Mirela (née Vashti). Father: unknown, listed.**

My mother's name. My mother's *real* name, the one under the concrete smile. My own age, corrected by four years, because she had shaved them off at the border the way you shave weight off a smuggling boat.

The lantern shook harder in Wren's hand. Ilsa hadn't moved. The sea was louder now, filling the lower passage with a sound like a crowd murmuring before something terrible happens.

"That's not possible," I said. Which was a stupid thing to say, because I had photographed those same pages myself, in another life, in a drawer in Corben's Landing, and I had recognized the name then too, and I had filed it, and the filing had not helped. Filing never helps. That is the entire secret of my profession: we do not keep records because records help. We keep records because the alternative is remembering.

"She registered you herself," Xanthe said. His voice had lost its edge. That was worse. "After the decree. She walked into New Vessa with a baby and asked Elder Amara to write you down, so that someday—" He stopped. He looked at the gun in my hand, and then at me, and something in his face shifted from anger to a kind of terrible gentleness. "Someday somebody would come looking. She just thought it'd take longer than this."

"My mother defected," I said. "My mother cooperated fully with the resettlement authority."

"My mother ran," Xanthe said. "Same war, different trench. You think either of them got to choose what we became?"

The water was at our ankles now. Cold as a verdict.

* * *

I want to describe the next ninety seconds accurately, because later—in the inquiry, in the newspapers, in whatever version of this story survives—I will be reduced to a single decision, a hinge, a turn. But decisions aren't hinges. They're long corridors you've been walking down for years, and the door at the end was open the whole time; you just had to walk far enough to see it.

I raised the pistol. I did it because it was the last move in a game I had been losing since Corben's Landing, and because Hale was on that boat, and because if I did not bring him the vault I would need to bring him something else, and I had run out of somethings.

"Aldous," Ilsa Reyes said. Just my name. First name, which she had no right to, which she had gotten from Xanthe somewhere on the river, and which sounded in that cave like a hand on the back of my neck.

"Captain Reyes."

"You were born here." She said it flatly, no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse than accusation. "Vessa Harbour. There's a bell in the harbour chapel with your grandmother's donation cast into the rim. I used to ring it, some mornings, for the boats coming home. Did you know that?"

"I knew nothing," I said. "That was my job."

"And how's the job?"

The water was at mid-shin. The gunboat's engine note changed outside—a deeper thrum, the ship coming around, and I understood with a lurch that Hale had not waited for my signal. He had seen the lantern-light in the cave mouth, or he had simply decided that a witness who hesitates is a liability, and the deck gun was traversing, and the sound of it traversing was the most articulate thing I had heard in twenty years of government service.

"They're going to shell the cape," Wren said. He said it calmly, the way a man says *it's raining*, and began gathering the photographs off the rock shelf with hands that were not calm at all.

"Hale won't fire with me inside," I said.

"No," Xanthe agreed. "He'll fire with the *cave* inside. There's a difference, and it's measured in tons of rock." He looked at me across the rising water, and he did not plead, and he did not threaten, and I understood suddenly why the river had chosen this man to carry its dead country's mail. "You can stand there and be evidence, Aldous. Or you can walk out of here and be a witness. Those are different jobs. I've done both."

The first shell hit the cliff above us and the whole world became percussion. Dust fell in ropes. The lantern died and relit. Ilsa grabbed Wren's collar; Xanthe grabbed the oilskin satchel; and I—

I stood in the flooding dark with a pistol that had become, in the space of one shell-blast, the single most useless object on the coast of this continent, and I thought about a woman washing her hands for a long time, and a christening gown in an evidence bag, and a bell in a harbour chapel that I had never heard ring.

The second shell hit closer. Water surged to our knees, black and furious, carrying grit and something soft and unidentifiable, and the vault door groaned—not opening, settling, dying, the whole mountain deciding what to keep.

I holstered the pistol.

I want to be clear about what that cost. Not courage—there was no courage in it. What it cost was the only identity I had. For thirty-eight years I had been a man who served papers on ghosts, and ghosts, it turns out, when you finally meet them face to face, are just people with better archives.

"This way!" Ilsa bellowed, and pointed to the high ledge along the eastern wall—the route out, the one Xanthe had mapped from the air, the one I had dismissed as a smuggler's fantasy. "Crane! Move or drown, pick fast!"

I moved. I am many bad things, but I have never once been slow.

* * *

We climbed. The water climbed faster. Behind us the iron door went under, and I heard it go—a deep, final chord, like a church organ drowning—and Tomas Wren made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, short and cracked, a man hearing his whole history swallowed in one gulp.

At the ledge, Xanthe stopped. Turned back. The water was at his waist and the third shell was incoming and the man turned *back*, into the flood, because the satchel strap had snagged on a spur of rock.

I went in after him.

Not heroically. Not redemptively. I went in because if he drowned, the photographs drowned, and if the photographs drowned, then my mother's name existed nowhere in the world except inside me, and I had spent thirty-eight years learning what happened to names that lived only inside people. They became nowhere. They became from-nowhere. I would not carry her into nowhere.

I tore the strap free. The shell hit. The cave exhaled. We came up coughing in each other's grip, and for one absurd moment we hung there in the black water, heir and instrument, holding a bag of photographs between us like the world's most waterlogged relay baton.

"Your mother," Xanthe gasped, "registered you so someone would come home."

"That's not what this is."

"Sure," he said. "Whatever you need to call it to keep swimming."

* * *

We came out of the cave mouth into grey light and driving spray, onto the rocks below the cape, and the *Meridian* lay off our starboard beam with her gun still elevated, and through the smoke and rain I could see Hale on the bridge wing, binoculars up, watching four heads bob in the surf where there should have been either silence or surrender.

There was a moment—I want to be honest, there was a real moment—when I considered raising my hands. Coming in. Telling Hale the vault was flooded, the documents destroyed, mission accomplished, let's all go home and lose the paperwork. It would have worked. It might even have worked for years.

Instead I stood up on the rocks, in full view of the bridge, and I shouted—over the surf, over the guns, in the old script that the government decreed had never existed, the words every Vessani child learned before they learned to hide them:

*"The sea keeps what the sea is given!"*

Hale lowered the binoculars. I watched his face do the arithmetic. Witness, not evidence. A Crane, on the rocks, singing drowned-country hymns at his director. I watched him decide, in real time, that I had gone native, gone mad, or gone home—and I watched him understand, with the particular horror of a bureaucrat confronting an unscheduled variable, that all three were true.

The gun did not fire again. The *Meridian* weighed anchor. Whether Hale was retreating, regrouping, or simply unwilling to shell a serving officer in view of four witnesses, I did not know, and I did not wait aboard to learn.

I swam for Ilsa's barge.

* * *

Later—much later, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of diesel and river, watching the cape recede behind us with its crown of smoke—I sat on the deck of the *Marta Rey* beside the man whose life I had spent a season trying to dismantle, and he handed me a flask, and I drank, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

"You know what's funny," Xanthe said eventually.

"Nothing. Nothing about any of this is funny."

"The vault's gone. Originals, treaties, censuses, everything Hale wanted burned or drowned—it's all under forty feet of black water." He patted the oilskin satchel, dry, safe, lashed to the deck box. "Except I spent last night photographing every page. Twice. In case the first set got wet."

I stared at him. "You had copies the entire time."

"I had copies the entire time," he agreed pleasantly. "Tomas thinks I'm reckless. Ilsa thinks I'm lucky. You, of all people, should appreciate it: I'm thorough. It's the family trade."

The flask made another round. Ahead of us the river opened toward the settlements, toward courts and newspapers and a reckoning forty years overdue, and behind us Cape Reckoning smoked gently against the sky like a candle going out.

I thought about my mother's hands, and the long washing, and I finally understood what she had been scrubbing at all those years. Not the past. The *silence*.

"So what happens to me now?" I asked.

Xanthe looked at me for a while. Then he smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and it was not a cruel one either. It was the smile of a man reading a flight plan.

"Now? Now you testify."
