## 20. What the Tide Takes

The water came for the vault the way debts come—slowly, then all at once.

We were still in the cave when we heard it, that low grinding hush beneath the shelling, the sea shouldering itself into passages it had carved over ten thousand years and had every intention of reclaiming. The lantern light wavered. The floor of the vault, dry stone a minute before, went glassy with a film of seawater that reflected our three faces like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Out," Ilsa said. Not a suggestion. Ilsa Reyes did not make suggestions; she made weather.

But I couldn't move. My hands were full of paper.

Treaties. Censuses. The dissolution decree with its forged signature and its ink gone brown as old blood—the document that had unmade a nation while my mother was learning to walk, the lie that had chased her across half a continent and finally into silence. Forty years of proof that Vessania had not died. It had been murdered, and here was the murder weapon, and here was the confession, and the sea was coming up the stairs to drown them both.

"Xanthe." Tomas Wren's voice cracked on my name. The old man stood hip-deep already where the tide had found a lower channel, holding an armload of ledgers above his head like a man surrendering to something. "The documents can't be saved. *You* can."

He was right. I knew he was right. And I knew—I had known since two o'clock that morning, hanging out of my floatplane's window over black water with Ilsa calling bearings from the right seat—that knowing right and accepting it were different countries entirely.

Because I'd photographed them. Every page. Two hundred and eleven exposures on film wrapped in oilcloth inside my jacket, taken by lantern light through the cave mouth while Crane's gunboat sat fat and blind in the offing. Ilsa had held the pages flat against the chart table; her hands hadn't shaken once. Mine had shaken so badly on the first dozen frames that I'd re-shot them, and the re-shots were better, and somewhere in the middle of that long cold work I had understood that this was what inheritance actually meant—not gold, not a seal stamped on bronze, but the willingness to stand in a freezing cave at two in the morning copying down the truth before anyone could burn it again.

So why couldn't I let go?

Because these were hers. Because my mother had carried some version of this knowledge across borders and decades and finally into a grave, and had never once told me, and the only apology she could ever make me now was this paper, and the tide was taking it.

Ilsa waded back for me. She didn't shout. She put her hand flat on my chest, over the oilcloth packet, and said, quietly, under the roar: "You already have them. All of them. Let the river have the rest."

The river. She always said the sea like it was just another reach of the Kestrel, wider and saltier and no more entitled to her respect.

I let go.

Not gracefully. I dropped the treaties first, then the censuses, page by page, watching each one darken and lift and turn like a bird deciding against flight. The decree I held longest. Then I set it on the rising water and pushed, gently, the way you'd push a boat off from a dock, and the sea took it without ceremony, because the sea has never cared what anything means.

Tomas was weeping. I want to be honest about that—he stood in the flooding vault of his ancestors and wept openly, without shame, an old man crying for a country that had been dead longer than I'd been alive. But when Ilsa took his arm and turned him toward the passage, he went. That was the difference between Tomas Wren and the men who'd erased Vessania, and I understood it fully for the first time: they would have burned the world to keep their secret, and he let the ocean keep his, because the people mattered more than the paper.

We came out of the cave mouth into grey morning light and shell-smoke, and that's when I saw Crane.

* * *

Aldous Crane lay on the shingle below the cape where the last surge had dumped him, one leg bent wrong beneath him, blood in his hair going tacky in the salt wind. His pistol was in the shingle six feet away, and I reached it before he did, which tells you everything about how much fight was left in him.

Behind him, anchored off the point, sat the gunboat—grey, ugly, flying a survey pennant that fooled nobody—and behind *it*, the whole machinery he served, waiting for word to level Cape Reckoning and write one more line in one more ledger.

Crane looked up at me with the tide running red around his leg, and he didn't beg. I'll give him that forever. He looked at me the way a man looks at a mirror he's been avoiding for forty years.

"You have copies," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd watched us work the cave too long.

"I have every page."

Something moved through his face then, and I've spent weeks trying to name it. Relief is closest. The relief of a man who has carried a thing so long that hearing someone else say they'll take it feels like being set down.

"Then it doesn't matter what happens to the originals." He laughed—a short, broken sound, half cough. "Do you know what I was told? When they sent me upriver? *Recover the documents. Deny the settlement exists.* Two sentences. My entire life reduced to two sentences, and I recited them in my sleep." He turned his head and spat seawater. "My grandmother's name is in your census, Okafor. Page forty-one. I checked. God help me, the first night I had the logbook open in front of me, I wasn't looking for salvage irregularities. I was looking for my own family."

The gunboat's loudspeaker crackled across the water, thin and distorted: *Survey One, report status. Survey One.*

Crane closed his eyes.

I crouched beside him. The tide was climbing the shingle in fast, greedy licks now, and somewhere above us the cave was finishing its meal. "They'll shell the town if you don't call it off," I said. "There are children up there. There's a schoolhouse older than your government."

"I know what there is." He opened his eyes, and they were clear—clearer than they'd ever been at Mile Nine, asking his precise little questions about salvage law. "Radio," he said. "Belt pouch. Left side. My arm doesn't—" He tried to demonstrate and the arm refused, and Ilsa knelt and got the radio out for him, and for a moment the three of us were arranged around him like nurses, this man who had hunted us two hundred miles of river.

He raised the handset himself. His voice came out level, official, the bureaucrat's instrument playing its last tune:

"Survey One to gunboat *Meridian*. Raid is compromised. Cave flooded before recovery. Originals destroyed, repeat, destroyed by tidal action. No recoverable evidence at site. Settlement location unverifiable from seaward. Recommend immediate withdrawal pending review." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "Error attributable to survey leadership. To me. Aldous Crane, sole responsibility. Stand down. That is an order I am authorized to give, and I am giving it."

Static. Long enough that my heart stopped doing its job. Then the *Meridian*'s anchor chain began to rattle home, and the grey hull swung its blunt bow toward the open sea, and the guns that had been walking their fire up the cape all morning went silent so completely that we could hear gulls again.

Crane lowered the handset and lay back on the shingle and looked at the sky like a man who had finally been permitted to stop.

"They'll court-martial you," I said.

"They'll do worse than that." He smiled, and it was the first honest expression I'd ever seen him wear. "But they'll have to find me first. And I know this river now. Better than you think." He looked at Ilsa. "Captain Reyes. Your barge. I'm told you once ran cargo for people like me."

"People like you," Ilsa said, "were the cargo."

"Yes." He said it without flinching. "Well. I'm asking."

* * *

We got him up to the barge on a hatch cover, Ilsa directing with the economy of a woman who had hauled worse men off worse rocks, and Tomas following with his face still wet and his hands still empty and somehow standing straighter than I'd seen him stand since we'd arrived. The tide had the cave now, all of it. From the deck, looking back at Cape Reckoning, you'd never have known anything had been kept there but stone.

The chest was gone too—swept out with the rest, or buried, or sitting in some deep channel where salmon would spawn over the seal of Vessania without ever knowing it. I thought I'd feel the loss like a wound. Instead I felt lighter than I had since the day I'd hauled it dripping out of the Kestrel. Some things you're only meant to carry until you understand them.

Inside my jacket, against my ribs, the oilcloth packet rode warm as a living thing. Two hundred and eleven photographs. Treaties signed by men whose grandchildren were alive in New Vessa. A census with Crane's grandmother on page forty-one and, I suspected, names on other pages that would make other important people very tired very suddenly. And the decree, photographed front and back, the forgery plain as day once you knew the ink.

Miriam Sable had told me once, in her archive that smelled of dust and lamp oil, that records were the only immortality worth trusting. They'd burned her building for saying it. She'd have laughed herself sick to learn the sea had done their burning for them, and it hadn't mattered at all.

Ilsa came and stood beside me at the rail. Her hands were cut from the winch line back in the gorge and the salt had gotten into the cuts, and she wasn't complaining, because Ilsa Reyes had never once complained in her life, a fact I intended to spend considerable time trying to correct.

"So," she said. "Copies."

"Copies."

"And the originals are fish habitat."

"The originals are fish habitat."

She nodded slowly, watching the cape fall away astern. "Your mother would have called that a fair trade. Paper remembers. People survive. Everything else is decoration." She glanced sideways at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't earned yet but intended to. "You did all right, Okafor."

Coming from Ilsa, that was a medal with oak leaves.

Below decks, Crane was unconscious again, fever climbing, his bad leg splinted with a barge pole section and wrapped in a Vessani tablecloth that Tomas had produced from somewhere with great ceremony and greater satisfaction. The man who had come to erase them, bandaged in the flag they weren't supposed to have.

The *Meridian* was a smudge on the horizon. The fog was building in the west, the way it did every evening, folding the river into itself. Somewhere downstream, Corben's Landing was rebuilding around a hole where Miriam's archive used to be, and Miriam herself was—alive, I had to believe it, alive and memorizing everything she'd lost the way only Miriam could, and waiting.

Two hundred miles of river between us and her. A wounded defector in the hold. A nation's entire testimony in oilcloth against my ribs. And spring coming on, when the courts sat and the newspapers woke hungry and the men who had erased Vessania would discover, to their considerable surprise, that you cannot drown a photograph.

Ilsa put her hand over mine on the rail.

"Course?" she asked.

I looked upstream, toward the falls, toward New Vessa, toward everything that had no business existing and existed anyway.

"Home first," I said. "Then the truth. In that order—for once."

The barge swung her blunt bow upriver, and the fog took us kindly, and no map anywhere marked where we were going. Which suited me fine. I was done with maps.
