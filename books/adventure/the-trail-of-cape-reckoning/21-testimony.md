## 21. Testimony

They kept me in a room with a window for the last three days of my captivity, which I understood to be a courtesy, the way you might give a condemned man a decent last meal. The window looked out on a stand of birches going gold, and beyond them a fence, and beyond the fence a road that no one traveled. I counted the birches. There were forty-one. I named them after archivists I had known, most of them dead, all of them stubborn, and I told them stories about the books they would never get to burn.

You learn things about yourself when someone takes your archive away and sets it on fire. You learn, for instance, that you are not nearly as indispensable as your filing system suggested. You learn that grief for paper is still grief, and that it sits in the chest like a swallowed stone. And you learn—this was the one I hadn't expected—that fear has a floor. For two weeks I had assumed it went down forever. It doesn't. Somewhere around the fourth day of not knowing whether Miriam Sable of Corben's Landing would see another autumn, my fear hit bottom and just lay there, flat and exhausted, like a man who has run out of stairs.

That was when I started taking notes. In my head, because they'd taken my pen. Mental cataloguing: the faces of the men who came, the make of their truck, the way the tall one said *dissolved* with a little flourish, like a magician producing a dove. If I got out, I would testify. Not because I was brave—I have never once in my life been brave—but because spite ferments beautifully under pressure, and mine had turned to something with real proof behind it.

* * *

The man who finally opened the door was not one of my captors. He was a provincial constable with a mustache like a wet brush and an expression of profound embarrassment, and he said, "Madam Sable? There's been a mistake," which was the truest sentence anyone had spoken to me in a month. A mistake. Eleven thousand documents reduced to ash, a woman locked in a farmhouse, a town terrorized—and a mistake. The euphemism was so perfect I almost laughed. Almost. What I actually did was sit down on the edge of the cot and let myself shake for a while, because forty-one birches will only carry a person so far.

They drove me back toward Corben's Landing in the same truck my captors had used, which felt like being returned to the scene of a crime by the criminals' landlord. The river towns along the way had heard something—the news moved upriver faster than any government dispatch, the way news always does, mouth to mouth at fuel docks and church suppers. At every stop, people knew. The ferryman at Two Sisters Crossing asked if it was true about the decree. The woman who sold us coffee asked if it was true about the boy with the airplane. Both times I said yes, and both times the asking itself was the answer: the story had escaped its cage and gone feral, and there was nothing left to do but feed it.

Corben's Landing smelled of wet char when we arrived. My archive—the building I had inherited from a man who inherited it from a woman who smuggled Vessani records out in flour sacks during the dissolution—was a black ribcage against the sky. The roof was gone. The walls stood at the angles of a drunk trying to look sober. Rain had gotten into everything, which meant the ashes had set into a kind of grey paste across the floor, and standing in the doorway I could see, here and there, the curled edges of pages that had burned only halfway. Half-burned letters are worse than ash. Ash at least commits. A half-burned page shows you exactly what you lost and exactly what survived, and it does this with the smugness of a coin landing on its edge.

I knelt in the paste and lifted one. A shipping manifest, 1931, the year of the resettlement. Cargo listed as "household goods, assorted." The word *assorted* had survived; the rest had not. I put it in my coat pocket anyway. An archivist learns early that fragments are not consolation prizes. Fragments are the job.

* * *

The hearings were held in the provincial capital, in a courtroom designed for disputes over fencing lines and unpaid grain debts, which is to say it was far too small for what walked into it. They had to bring in folding chairs. Reporters sat on windowsills. A stenographer with forearms like a stevedore took down every word, and I watched her fingers and thought: this is the new archive. This woman's hands, moving at ninety words a minute, were doing what my shelves had done for sixty years. Paper burns. Testimony has to be carried out of the room person by person, and it is heavier than paper, and it does not fit in flour sacks.

I testified on the third day. They had offered me a delay—a doctor, a rest, all very kind—and I declined, because I had learned in captivity that waiting is a weapon they hand you and ask you to point at yourself. So I stood up in my one good dress, the one I'd worn to my own mother's funeral, and I told them what I knew.

I told them about the emergency decree of 1931, and how the copy in the provincial registry bore a signature from a minister who had, according to his own ministry's calendar, been in a hospital bed with typhoid on the date of signing. I had found that discrepancy eleven years ago and reported it, and had received in reply a form letter thanking me for my vigilance and assuring me the matter was closed. "Closed," I said into the microphone, "is a word this government uses the way a gravedigger uses dirt."

I told them about the families. About how, in thirty years of running the archive, I had taken depositions from ninety-four people born in Vessania or born to Vessani parents in exile, and how every single account agreed on the essentials: no vote was held, no compensation paid, no notice given except the smoke on the horizon where the records office had been. Ninety-four testimonies, and the state's entire counter-evidence was one signature that could be proven forged by a calendar. I want it noted that I did not raise my voice. I had spent a month in a room with forty-one birches learning patience, and I deployed it now like ordnance.

And then I told them about the night of the fire, or rather the night before it, when two men came to the archive asking after "river salvage" and "a bronze-bound chest" and whether I had ever handled documents bearing the Vessani seal. I described their faces. I described the truck. I described, with the precision of a woman who had nothing left to lose and a mental notebook full of entries, the exact wording of the tall one's threat: *Old women who read too much end up as cautionary tales.*

The gallery went quiet in a way galleries rarely do. Even the reporters stopped writing. Because everyone in that room understood, suddenly and all together, what I was saying: that the erasure of Vessania had not been a historical event with dust on it. It was ongoing. It had a budget, and personnel, and a current operational tempo, and it had tried to kill me six weeks ago in a river town that most maps didn't bother labeling correctly.

When I stepped down, my legs shook, and I pretended it was the stairs.

* * *

Xanthe Okafor testified the following week, and I will say this about him: he is a better liar than he thinks and a worse actor than he hopes, and the combination makes him devastating on a witness stand. He told the truth with the cadence of a man reciting a flight plan—heading, altitude, weather—and the plainness of it disarmed everyone. The government's counsel tried to trip him on salvage law, on chain of custody, on why a bush pilot with debts would keep photographs instead of selling them, and Xanthe answered each question the way he lands a plane on short water: without flourish, without apology, and slightly ahead of where you expected him to touch down.

He produced the photographs. Every page of the vault record, shot during that night flight over black water—Ilsa Reyes navigating, he said, and the room noted the name, and I saw several older faces in the gallery go carefully blank, because half this province's river families had done quiet business with Ilsa Reyes for decades and had no wish to be reminded of it under oath.

The photographs did what photographs do. The census pages especially. Rows of names, births, deaths, marriages—forty years of a nation that officially did not exist, documented in a hand too careful to be anything but desperate. The forged decree unraveled in nine days. I kept count, because counting is what I do instead of praying. Nine days from first publication to the minister's resignation, and the resignation statement contained a phrase I have since committed to memory out of pure professional appreciation: *irregularities in the historical record.* Irregularities. As if the burning of a country were a clerical matter, a smudge, a thing to be corrected in the next printing.

But then, I remind myself, that is precisely how it began in 1931. Not with soldiers—with a form. Erasure is always administrative before it is violent. Which means the reverse is also true: restoration begins with paperwork. And so, when the negotiations opened—New Vessa's existence to enter formal discussion rather than continued denial—I volunteered for the records committee, because somebody has to keep the minutes, and I have opinions about minute-keeping that border on the liturgical.

* * *

Miriam, Tomas Wren said to me when I visited New Vessa in October, you look terrible. I told him captivity agrees with no one's complexion. He laughed, and it cost him something to laugh, and I understood that too, because I had seen the settlement's ledger of losses: two men detained, one boat confiscated, a winter's worth of trust in the outside world spent down to the dregs. But the children were in the schoolyard learning a song in Vessani, and the seal was carved fresh over the meetinghouse door, deeper this time, cut to last. Fear had not left that valley. It had simply been given a shape with edges, and shaped fear is easier to carry than fog.

Tomas walked me past the house where Xanthe's mother had grown up. A girl's diary sat in a glass case now, open to the page where she wrote that she would not let the elders spend the treasury like a coin. I stood in front of it longer than Tomas probably wanted me to. Here was the whole argument of the age, in a child's handwriting: survival against truth, kept safe against given away. She chose truth and paid exile. Her son chose truth and paid a chest, a hangar, a cracked float, a name dragged through every newspaper in the province. And the settlement, which voted to split down the middle, got both outcomes at once—negotiation instead of erasure, exposure instead of safety—and is now living inside the arithmetic of that split, doing sums nobody can finish yet.

On my last evening there, Ilsa Reyes fed me stew in her galley and asked, casually, in the tone of a woman asking about the weather, whether I thought the courts would move fast. I said governments move at the speed of their embarrassment, and right now theirs was considerable, so: moderately fast, by their standards, which is glacial by anyone else's. She nodded and refilled my bowl and said nothing further, but her eyes went upriver, the way river people's eyes do when they're thinking about someone.

* * *

Crane disappeared the week the hearings recessed.

Not fled—disappeared. There is a difference, and the difference matters. A fleeing man runs from something. Aldous Crane walked away from everything at once: his commission, his pension, his rented rooms in the capital, his own name, which had become by then the most examined name in three provinces. The gunboat crew was questioned and released. His superiors disavowed him with the smoothness of men wiping their hands on a towel. The newspapers wanted him for a week and then wanted other things, because newspapers have the memory of mayflies.

But I am an archivist, and I know what a clean disappearance looks like, and Crane's was not clean. It was curated. He settled his landlady's bill. He left his uniform folded. He mailed one envelope—one—to the hearing clerk, containing a signed confession to the fire at my archive, naming his two accomplices, accepting full criminal responsibility, and requesting, in a sentence that has kept me up more nights than I admit, that "the record reflect that the undersigned acted throughout under lawful orders he believed at the time to be lawful."

He believed. Past tense. That verb choice is a whole autobiography.

The constables went looking, naturally. They found his trail as far as Two Sisters Crossing, where he bought passage on a mail barge going upstream, and then nothing. Upriver is where trails go to die in this country. The water gets narrow, the banks get steep, the map gets honest about how little it knows. Some in the capital said he'd drowned himself. Ilsa Reyes, when I asked her, looked at me with those river-grey eyes and said, "Men like that don't drown. Men like that paddle."

And she was right, of course, though not perhaps in the way either of us meant at the time. Because in November a package reached the records committee—no return address, postmarked from a village so small it shares its name with a rapids—and inside was a sheaf of documents in a careful bureaucratic hand: internal memoranda, requisition orders, the operational file of the "resettlement survey" going back twenty years. Evidence that the hunting of Vessani bloodlines had never stopped being policy, merely stopped being written down. Someone with access had copied it all and sent it to the one committee in the province guaranteed to preserve it.

The covering note was four lines long. No name. No apology. Just a citation number matching a census page from the vault photographs—a family, a line of names—and beneath it, in different ink, added later, smaller:

*They were mine too.*

I have handled a great many confessions in my career. Most arrive screaming. This one arrived like a man setting down a load at the top of a stair and walking quietly back down into the dark. I entered it into evidence, sealed the copy, and sat for a long while afterward in what remains of my reading room—temporary quarters above the general store, smelling of lamp oil and damp timber—thinking about the difference between forgiveness and acknowledgment, and how the second costs the giver everything and buys the receiver almost nothing, and how we built a whole nation's worth of archives on transactions exactly that bad.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it does when it wants to make a point. The river began thinking about ice. Corben's Landing battened down, and I unpacked crates in my temporary rooms, rebuilding the catalogue from memory and from the ninety-four testimonies, which the fire had not touched because they lived in me and in the stenographer's transcript and in the heads of every family that had trusted me with them. An archive, it turns out, is not a building. I had suspected this for years. Now I had the scar tissue to prove it.

In late November, a letter arrived for me, forwarded twice, smelling faintly of engine oil. Xanthe Okafor's handwriting, block capitals, the lettering of a man trained to write things legible at speed and upside down. He thanked me—for the warning I'd given him in September, for the testimony, for keeping the minutes. Then he wrote a paragraph that made me put the letter down and walk around the room once.

*I've filed a flight plan,* he wrote. *First one under my full name. Xanthe Emeka Okafor, all three names, no abbreviations. Ilsa's coming with me. We're flying medicine to the upper settlements before freeze-up, and the charter tucked in my map case is the draft of New Vessa's recognition petition—you'll get the fair copy by courier. The river forgives nothing. I've stopped asking it to.*

I read it twice and then a third time, because archivists reread things, and on the third reading I understood what I was holding. Not a letter. A deposition. Thirty years from now, some successor of mine will open a box and find this page, and it will be the primary source for the moment a man who spent his whole life flying under false convenience finally filed himself honestly into the record. First-person testimony. Dated. Signed. Witnessed by the weather.

I catalogued it that same night. Accession number 1931-V-0001, the first entry of the rebuilt collection, because a new archive should begin where the old one ended: with a name the state tried to erase, restored in the subject's own hand.

Outside, the first snow was starting, fat and slow, the kind that means business. And down at the fuel dock, I was told later, a floatplane sat roped and ready against the spring, and beside it a woman with river-grey eyes checked a cargo net twice, and neither of them looked back at the town.

Upriver, the ice was already forming at the narrows. And somewhere beyond it—past the falls, past the cape, past the edge of every honest map—a man with no flag and no name was paddling toward the one place in the world that keeps faith with old promises, carrying a confession heavy enough to sink a lighter boat.

The river, as I have cause to know, gives nothing back for free.

But it keeps everything.
