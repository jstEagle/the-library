## 22. Flight Plan, Filed Honestly

The ice went out of the Kestrel on a Tuesday, with a sound like the whole river clearing its throat after a long winter of holding its opinions.

I was on the dock at Corben's Landing when it happened, coffee in one hand, a clipboard in the other, and I'll admit I flinched at the crack of it—some part of me still braced for gunboats, for shelling, for the world deciding to come apart again. But it was only the ice. Only the river, doing what rivers do: moving on without apology.

"Jumpier than a float on glass," Ilsa said beside me. She'd brought two coffees and drunk hers already, which was her way of telling me mine was in danger if I didn't get on with it.

"It's been a year," I said. "You'd think a man could hear ice break without reaching for a fire extinguisher."

"A year since the cape." She looked out at the brown water shouldering its way downstream, carrying winter's debris like a landlord clearing out an evicted tenant. "Some years it takes longer. Some things take longer."

She wasn't talking about the ice, and we both knew it.

Here's what a year looks like when you've spent it being honest, which I can now report is a genuinely novel experience: it looks like paperwork.

The photographs I'd taken in the vault—every treaty, every census page, every line of the forged dissolution decree—had gone to a judge in the capital, then to a newspaper with more courage than advertisers, then to a courtroom that smelled of floor wax and history being rearranged. Miriam Sable testified for six days with a burn scar on her left hand and a voice that never once wavered, and when the government's lawyer tried to suggest she'd invented the archive, she looked at him the way a librarian looks at a man who's dog-eared a first edition, and he sat down and did not get up again.

The decree unraveled. Vessania—struck from the maps in my childhood, a word that had been contraband my whole life—was suddenly a matter of negotiation. Boundaries. Recognition. A charter, drafted in a schoolhouse above the falls by people who'd spent forty years keeping a seal alive in secret, now being argued over by men in good coats.

And me? I got my name back. All of it. Xanthe Okafor, son of Adaeze Okafor, born Vessani, heir to a treasury that turned out to contain no gold at all—just the truth, which proved heavier and worth more. My mother had known that. She'd died knowing it, alone, silent, protecting a people who never learned she'd been protecting them. I'd spent a year angry about her silence. I'd spent the winter letting it go, which is slower work than flying and considerably more dangerous.

The clipboard in my hand was the reason I was standing on that dock in the first light of spring, feeling like a man about to jump off a diving board into water he'd only ever looked at.

A flight plan.

Filed honestly.

* * *

You have to understand what that means, in this country, to a man like me. For twenty years I'd filed plans as "X. Okafor" or "X. Ok." or, in my more paranoid seasons, under a name belonging to a dead man from Miriam's records whose paperwork I'd inherited along with a debt to the airfield. I'd kept my routes vague, my cargo vague, my whole existence filed under the heading of Nobody In Particular. Invisibility had been my religion, and I'd kept the faith harder than most converts.

Now I stood at the counter of the Corben's Landing airfield office—rebuilt after the fire, new wood smelling of sawdust and second chances—and the clerk, a round young man named Pettibone who'd been hired in the autumn and knew nothing of my history except the parts printed in newspapers, waited for me to fill in the form.

Name of pilot.

I held the pencil over the blank and felt the whole year balance on the point of it.

"You're the cape fellow," Pettibone said helpfully. "The one from the articles. My mum saved them."

"That's me."

"You could just write X," he offered. "Everyone does."

I wrote *Xanthe Adaeze Okafor*. My mother's name in mine, where it had always been, whether the forms admitted it or not. Then the aircraft registration, then the route: Corben's Landing to New Vessa field, above the falls, with a stop at the narrows to drop mail for the sentries who'd once looked at my name and gone quiet with recognition.

Purpose of flight: *Home.*

I almost wrote "supply run." Old habits. But the form had a line for it, and I had a year of practice now at telling the truth in small spaces, so that's what went down.

Pettibone read it, stamped it, and slid it back with a receipt. "First time anyone's put that," he said.

"There's a first time for everything honest," I said, and took my coffee out to the dock before the moment could get any more sentimental.

* * *

The *Kestrel Queen*—Ilsa's armored barge, slow as a sermon and twice as stubborn—sat at her mooring with fresh paint on the rails and the old Vessani recognition sign cut into the pilothouse door, no longer hidden under a coat of gray but burnished, official, hers. She'd been offered a government commission for the mail run upriver. She'd told the minister, in words I won't repeat here but which involved his ancestry and a mule, that she'd carried Vessani cargo for free when it was contraband and she'd be damned if she'd take wages for it now that it was legal.

So she carries it anyway, for free, on principle, which Ilsa would tell you is the only cargo worth carrying.

The plane sat beside her on its mooring—my old girl, new float patched where the rocks had cracked it in the fog chase, engine rebuilt over winter by a settlement mechanic named Ferro who talked to carburetors the way other men talk to horses. On her tail I'd painted, that March, in paint Miriam sourced from the capital, a small bronze seal. The seal of Vessania. Not hidden. Not flaunted. Just there, like a name on a door.

"You're staring at it again," Ilsa said.

"I'm inspecting it."

"You're staring. You do it every morning like it might have flown off in the night." She took my coffee, drank half, and handed it back, which in the grammar of Ilsa Reyes is roughly equivalent to a sonnet. "Come on. Mail won't fly itself, and the narrows sentries will eat it if we're late. They eat everything up there. I swear they'd eat the mailbag if Ferro didn't sew it out of canvas."

We loaded together: mail, medicine, seed potatoes for the terraces above the falls, a crate of books for the school—real books now, with Vessani history in them, printed in the capital, sold openly, which still made the schoolteacher cry a little every time a new box arrived. And into the map case, flat and oilcloth-wrapped, went the document that had traveled with me all winter: the settlement's charter, signed by forty-three hands, including mine—my first signature under my full name since I was a boy, when I'd signed my mother's birthday card and thought nothing of it.

Tomas Wren had pressed it on me in December, at the council house, with the stove ticking and the whole fractious, stubborn, surviving town crowded in behind him. "You argued for truth when it would have been easier to argue for peace," he'd said, in that voice of his that could frost glass or warm it as the occasion demanded. "The vote split. You lost the room for an afternoon and won it back by morning, and I've been in politics long enough to know that's the rarest arithmetic there is. The charter goes with you. A settlement isn't a place, Okafor. It's a promise, and promises need carrying."

I'd argued with him, because arguing with Tomas Wren is the settlement's principal winter sport, but I'd taken it. It rides in the map case now, between the sectional charts and the tide tables for Cape Reckoning—tide tables, for a man who once couldn't find the cape on a map. The vault is flooded and sealed now, by council decree, a tomb for the original documents the tide took. The photographs live in three places, and one of them is a fireproof box in Ilsa's cabin, because Ilsa trusts banks the way she trusts ministers.

* * *

We took off at mid-morning, into a sky the color of rinsed glass, the river unrolling below us brown and bright and busy with ice-out traffic. The Kestrel forgives nothing—I've said it before and the river has never once sued me for libel—but flying above it, with Ilsa in the right seat working the radio and calling the current like she was reading it a bedtime story, I felt something I'd stopped expecting to feel.

Unwatched.

Not invisible—there's a difference, and it took me a year to learn it. Invisible is a man hiding. Unwatched is a man with nothing left to hide. Crane had taught me that, in his broken, tide-scoured way. He'd gone upriver in the autumn, paddling toward the settlement with his confession, and the council had done with him what councils do: listened for three days, argued for two more, and then given him a cabin on the far ridge and a job splitting wood, on the theory—Tomas's theory, and I've come to believe it—that a man who betrays everything and then carries the betrayal home on his own back has at least proven he can carry something. I see him some Sundays at the far end of the common hall. He doesn't talk much. He's teaching the schoolchildren surveying, of all things, and he's good at it, and the children have decided he's mysterious, which children will forgive anything.

The narrows came up green and rushing, the fort rebuilt with a proper flagpole now, and on the pole—this still stops my breath, every time—the seal, flying in the wind like it had always been there and the last forty years were the anomaly. The sentries waved us through with the old sign and the new one both, and dropped a mailbag of their own replies into the net I trailed, and shouted something up at the plane that I didn't catch and didn't need to.

Above the falls, New Vessa appeared the way it always does: suddenly, wholly, a town that had spent two generations learning to be invisible and was now, tentatively, learning to be seen. Smoke from chimneys. The schoolhouse roof Ferro and I re-shingled in the fall. Terraces going green. The common hall with its new shutters. And on the dock, waiting, a crowd that had turned out not for me—I'd finally cured them of that, mostly—but for the mail, which in a river town is the closest thing to a festival that happens on a Wednesday.

I brought her down on the water smooth as a signature, taxied to the dock, and cut the engine, and the silence afterward was the good kind, the kind with people in it.

* * *

That evening, after the mail was sorted and the potatoes hauled and the schoolteacher had cried over her books in the best way, Ilsa and I walked up to the ridge above the town, where the graves are. Not the old ones—those are kept secret still, some habits outliving their reasons, and the council says let them. The new one. One stone, granite, carved by a stonemason from the capital who'd wept through the whole commission.

**ADAEZE OKAFOR**
*Born Vessani. Heir to the truth. Returned.*

We'd brought her home in the autumn, from the pauper's plot outside Corben's Landing where the world had buried her under the wrong name and no history, and the whole settlement had come down to the dock to meet the boat, and Tomas Wren—who had known her, who had been a boy when she'd fled—had said the words over the grave in Vessani, the old language that forty children in the schoolhouse are learning now as a matter of course, the way you learn a thing that was never going to be taken from you again.

"She'd have hated the fuss," I said.

"She'd have hated it enormously," Ilsa agreed. "That's why we did it while she couldn't object."

We stood a while. The river ran below, silver in the dusk, running north toward the cape, where the gunboat's scars had weathered to nothing and the sea-cave mouth slept behind its curtain of tide, keeping its one secret now, which is no secret at all: that there is nothing in it. That the treasure was the paper, and the paper was the people, and the people are up the hill cooking dinner.

"I filed the plan this morning," I said. "Full name. Purpose of flight: home."

Ilsa didn't say anything for a moment. Then she took my hand, which she does rarely and precisely, like everything else she does, and said: "Took you long enough."

"Twenty years."

"I've waited longer for cargo." She turned to face me, and the light was going out of the sky and into the windows below, and she had that look she gets when she's decided something and is merely informing the universe. "Next season, the Queen runs the whole river. Mail, cargo, passengers. And the plane flies the high route with us both in it. That's not a proposal, Okafor, it's a schedule. Objections can be filed with the council."

"I'd win."

"You'd lose. Tomas likes me better."

"Tomas is frightened of you."

"Same thing." She kissed me, briefly, in the businesslike way of a woman with a barge to run at dawn, and then we walked back down into the town that no map can erase, because it isn't on the maps anymore—it's on the charts, in the ledgers, in the court records, in the mouths of children learning a language the world was told was dead.

* * *

Morning came up clear. I filed the day's plan—full name, honest purpose, the whole routine of it already ordinary, which is the whole miracle of it—and Ilsa had the Queen's engine turning over before I'd finished my coffee, and Ferro waved us off from the dock with grease on his hands and sentiment in his throat that he'd deny under oath.

On the takeoff run, the river came at us the way it always does: fast, brown, indifferent, keeping everything and forgiving nothing. And I found I had stopped asking it to forget. Forget is a coward's prayer. What I asked, throttles forward, floats kissing the current one last time before the air took us, was only what I'd earned:

*Remember.*

Behind us the falls roared, and above them the seal flew, and in the map case between my knee and Ilsa's, the charter rode home.

The river, as I have cause to know, gives nothing back for free.

But it keeps everything.

And this year, at last, so do I.
