## 1. Fathoms and Miles

The wreck of the *Corvane* sat in forty fathoms off a coast that had no name worth remembering, and Jude Joubert loved it for exactly that reason.

She went down the anchor line hand over hand, letting the cold close over her like a door. Above her the morning light broke into coins on the surface, and below her the hull waited in its coat of green-black weed, patient as a held breath. Forty fathoms was nothing. She'd worked deeper. But there was something about this depth — deep enough that the world above stopped mattering, shallow enough that she could still feel the sun as a rumor on the back of her neck — where Jude did her best thinking.

Or her best not-thinking, which amounted to the same thing.

The *Corvane* had gone down in '61 carrying machine parts nobody needed anymore, which meant the hold was full of exactly what salvage divers loved best: heavy, worthless metal that paid by the ton. The navy had restricted the site two years ago — something about unexploded ordnance in the aft magazine, though every diver on this stretch of coast knew the real reason was that the navy didn't like people poking around its old mistakes. Jude had read the notice posted at the harbor office, photographed it with the mild interest of a woman memorizing a menu, and then gone out anyway, because the fine was three hundred dollars and the cargo was worth four thousand.

She was not a reckless woman. She wanted that understood, even if only by herself. Reckless divers died. Careful ones just occasionally got fined.

Inside the hold, the water was darker, thick with drifting silt that turned her lamp beam into a soft gray fog. She found the first pallet by touch, her gloves reading the shapes the way other people read print. Bolts of steel rod, bundled and crusted. She clipped the lift bag to the bundle, checked the shackle twice — once for the bundle's sake, once for her own — and fed air into the bag until it strained upward like a dog on a leash.

Good. One down. Four to go.

She'd been diving since she was nineteen, which meant fourteen years now, and in those fourteen years she had worked eleven coasts in six countries and could not, if pressed, tell you which one felt like home. This was not an accident. People assumed rootlessness was a wound — that a woman who never stayed must be running from something — and maybe they were right, but they had the direction backwards. She wasn't running from anything. She was running toward the specific pleasure of being a stranger.

A stranger got asked simple questions. Where are you from? What do you do? And you could answer in ten seconds flat: the coast, mostly; salvage, mostly. A stranger's whole life fit in a duffel bag and a tool roll, and nobody ever looked at you with that particular expression — the one that said *I know more about you than you'd like* — because nobody knew anything at all.

It was comfortable, being unknown. It was the emotional equivalent of neutral buoyancy: no effort, no drag, just hanging in the water column of the world, going wherever the current of the next job took her.

Jude finished the second pallet and paused against the bulkhead to check her gauges, and while she hung there in the gray-green dark, she did the arithmetic she always did, the one that kept her honest.

Fourteen years. Eleven coasts. Roughly nine thousand miles of coastline worked, if you counted the crossings. She measured her life in miles the way other people measured theirs in years, because miles were verifiable. You could point to them on a chart. Years were slippery things — they accumulated whether you earned them or not, like barnacles, and told you nothing except that time had passed while you weren't paying attention.

Miles, at least, you chose.

Her dive computer ticked over to twenty-two minutes of bottom time. Time to start up. She kicked out through the cargo hatch, past a school of pollack that parted around her with the bored indifference of fish who had seen a hundred divers and been impressed by none of them, and began her slow ascent up the line, watching the light above strengthen from rumor to fact.

Halfway up, hanging at her safety stop, she let herself do the thing she only allowed herself underwater, where no one could see her face: she thought about the mountains.

Not often. Maybe once a season, the way you might touch a scar to check it still hurt. Fifteen years since she'd left the Kessler range, and she had built her whole wandering life on the simple principle that the sea was flat. Oh, it had weather, it had moods, it had wrecks and currents and the occasional navy patrol with opinions, but it was fundamentally horizontal. You could always see what was coming. Mountains, in her experience, hid things. Mountains had valleys behind valleys, and people in those valleys who remembered your name and your brother's name and the night both names became a problem.

The sea forgot fast. Salt water was forgiving that way. Mountain towns remembered everything, and they kept the memory somewhere dry.

Jude exhaled a slow stream of bubbles and watched them climb away from her, silver and orderly, each one rising toward a surface it trusted. She did not trust surfaces. That was the whole trick of her, if anyone had bothered to learn it: she lived just below everything. Just below the waterline, just below the conversation, just below whatever version of herself the moment required. It was a good way to stay alive and a quiet way to stay alone, and she had never once, in fourteen years, lain awake at night regretting it.

Almost never. There had been one night in Port Callas, three years ago, after a storm took a friend's boat with him aboard, when she'd lain awake until dawn and realized she had no one to call who would already know why she couldn't sleep. Everyone she knew would need the story explained first. Who was Marcus? What boat? Were you close? By the time she'd finished explaining, the sun was up and the grief had gone stale, like bread left out.

She'd moved to a new coast the following week. Naturally.

* * *

By noon the *Corvane*'s deck was littered with lifted pallets and the harbor master's office had received, via the marine band radio, a very formal inquiry from a very bored naval lieutenant regarding whether anyone had perhaps been diving a restricted site this morning. Jude, sitting on her own boat's rail eating a sandwich, listened to the exchange between the lieutenant and the harbor master with the serene attention of a woman listening to weather.

"Can't say I've seen any traffic out that way," the harbor master said, which was technically true, since he'd been asleep.

"Very well. Out."

Jude finished her sandwich. The fine, if it ever materialized, would come out of the cargo money and still leave her ahead. This was the kind of math she enjoyed: risk priced honestly, consequences budgeted for, nothing left to chance except the sea itself, and the sea she trusted more than most people. The sea at least told you what it intended. It showed you the swell an hour before it arrived. People smiled at you right up until the moment they didn't.

She spent the afternoon stripping copper pipe from the *Corvane*'s engine room on a second dive, working by feel in the dark with her lamp off because the batteries were dying and she hadn't gotten around to buying new ones. There was a rhythm to work like this, a metronome of hands and breath, and somewhere around the fourth length of pipe she stopped being a person doing a task and became just a pair of hands in the dark, which was, if she was honest, her favorite way to exist. Hands didn't have histories. Hands didn't have names people remembered wrong.

She surfaced at dusk into a sky the color of a bruise healing, hauled herself over her gunwale, and was halfway through lighting the stove when she saw the boat coming across the harbor.

It was a charter launch, the expensive kind, twin engines, polished rails, moving with the unhurried confidence of money that had never once worried about a mooring fee. Jude watched it idly, the way you watch anything on water, cataloguing without meaning to: forty feet, glass canopy, single figure at the helm standing rather than sitting, which told you the helm thought of themselves as a captain and not a passenger.

Then the launch cut its throttles and swung alongside her mooring with a precision that made her sit up straighter, because there was exactly one person she'd ever known who docked like that — showing off, but showing off correctly — and that person was supposed to be four hundred miles south running dive charters for tourists who wanted their photographs taken next to lobsters.

The figure at the helm pushed back the canopy, and the last of the daylight caught a face Jude had spent fifteen years successfully not thinking about.

Cass Merrow.

Of course. Of course it was Cass Merrow. Jude stood very still at her stove with the match burning down toward her fingers, because this was the moment to decide what her face was going to do, and faces, she had learned, were like dives: you planned them before you committed, because improvising at depth got people killed.

Cass stepped onto her deck without asking, which was so perfectly Cass that Jude almost laughed. Same lean build under better clothes than the last time. Same jaw set at the angle of a woman who had never once entered a room through the door everyone else used. Older, obviously — they both were — but older the way driftwood is older: weathered into something harder, not softer.

"Joubert." Cass said it like a verdict.

"Sterling," Jude said automatically, and then corrected herself, because she'd dropped the alias two coasts ago. "Joubert. Old habit."

"I know your habits." Cass looked around the little boat — the coiled lines, the stacked lift bags, the copper pipe gleaming wet on the deck — with an appraiser's eye. "Still working restricted sites, I see. The *Corvane*?"

"The *Corvane* has machine parts in it. Machine parts don't care who salvages them."

"No," Cass agreed. "But navies do." She helped herself to the crate that served as a seat, sat down across from the stove, and watched Jude with an expression that Jude recognized and had hoped never to see again: the look of someone who had done their research and was enjoying knowing more than you did.

"What do you want, Merrow?"

"A job."

"I've got a job."

"You've got a pallet of scrap iron. I'm offering you the one we swore off." Cass leaned forward, and the stove light caught her eyes, and there it was — the thing underneath the polish, the same hunger Jude remembered from the old crew, from the years before Captain Voss had made them all swear on rope and salt that some jobs stayed dead. "The *Kessler Star*. The strongbox. I need a diver who doesn't scare, and I need her before the snows close the range, and there is exactly one diver alive who knows that wreck's original survey."

Jude kept her voice level, because keeping her voice level was the one skill she'd practiced harder than diving. "Ilsa forbade that job. Ilsa's dead, and her word isn't."

"It isn't," Cass said simply. "That's rather the point."

The match burned down to Jude's fingers at last, and she shook it out, and in the small dark that followed she felt the old geography shift under her — the flat, safe, horizontal world tilting suddenly toward mountains she had spent half her life making sure she'd never see again.

Cass smiled, slow and knowing, and said the thing she had clearly crossed four hundred miles of cold water to say.

"There's a name inside that box, Joubert. Your family's name. And I think you've been measuring your life in miles because you're afraid of what happens if you ever stand still long enough to be *known*."

Jude looked at her — really looked, the way she looked at a hull before committing to the penetration — and understood that the stranger's life was over. Someone had come looking. Someone had found her.

Someone knew her name.
