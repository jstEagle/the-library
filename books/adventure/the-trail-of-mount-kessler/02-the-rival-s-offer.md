## 2. The Rival's Offer

Jude Joubert was three fathoms down and having a very good morning when the universe decided to ruin it.

She was working the hull of a sunken trawler off Gullwater Point, cutting loose a fouled net that some poor deckhand had lost twenty years ago, and there was a rhythm to it — torch in one hand, knife in the other, the cold pressing against her like an old friend who didn't ask questions. Down here nobody wanted anything from her. The fish didn't care about her name. The wreck didn't remember her brother. It was, she often thought, the closest thing to peace a person could buy with a tank of air.

So of course that was when she saw the boat.

Not her boat — she didn't own a boat, owning things was for people who planned to stay — but the dive tender she'd chartered from a man named Hollis who charged fair rates and never asked where she was going next. Someone was standing on Hollis's deck who had absolutely no business standing on Hollis's deck. Even at three fathoms, even through green water and distortion, Jude knew that silhouette. She'd have known it in the dark. She'd have known it upside down, blindfolded, dead.

Cass Merrow stood like she was posing for the cover of something — weight on one hip, arms crossed, chin up — and the last time Jude had seen that posture, they'd been shouting at each other across a dock in Kessler Town while Captain Ilsa Voss watched from the wheelhouse with the expression of a woman deciding which of them to throw overboard first.

That was fifteen years ago. Apparently Cass had been practicing.

Jude finished the cut. She was not going to rush a job because of Cass Merrow; she had more dignity than that, or at least she intended to acquire some. Then she surfaced slowly, hung on the ladder for a moment letting the water stream off her, and climbed aboard with what she hoped read as mild inconvenience rather than the cold drop of dread actually sitting in her stomach.

"Cass," she said.

"Joubert." Cass looked her over the way she always had — like an appraiser at an estate sale, cataloguing what had held up and what hadn't. "You've got gray in your hair."

"You've got crow's feet."

"I've got *character lines*."

"They're crow's feet."

Hollis, a round and cheerful man who had survived forty years of charter work by mastering the art of being elsewhere during conversations, chose this moment to become intensely interested in his fuel gauge. Jude appreciated him deeply.

Cass Merrow had not changed as much as the years demanded. She was still tall in that way that made rooms feel smaller, still dressed like she was on her way to somewhere better than wherever she actually was — today a wool coat too clean for a working dock, boots that had never seen honest mud. Her hair was shorter. Her eyes were the same: quick, brown, and always slightly amused, as if the whole world were a joke she'd gotten early access to.

Fifteen years ago, Jude had hated those eyes with a purity of feeling she'd never managed since. It had been almost restful, hating Cass. Very focused. Very simple.

"Nice dive?" Cass asked.

"Productive."

"Productive." Cass smiled. "You always did have a way with words, Joubert. Ilsa used to say you could make a eulogy sound like a weather report."

"Don't." The word came out harder than Jude meant it to, and she saw Cass register that — saw the amusement flicker and bank itself down to coals. Ilsa Voss had been dead four years now, and neither of them had been there, and neither of them had said much about it since, and apparently they were going to keep not saying much about it right up until they couldn't anymore.

The silence sat between them on the deck, salt-stiff and awkward, until Cass broke it by doing the thing Cass did best: she produced paperwork.

"Before you say no," she said, unfolding a leather folder with a flourish that suggested she'd rehearsed the unfold, "hear me out."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to. You get a look. Your jaw does this thing." Cass mimed it — a small forward jut of the chin — with insulting accuracy. "I've known you since we were nineteen. I know all your faces. It's very annoying for both of us."

Jude took the folder because refusing to look at a contract was the kind of dramatic gesture that only worked if you could afford to walk away, and she couldn't yet tell whether she could. Inside was a salvage agreement, typed, real, with letterhead from a maritime firm in the capital and everything. Cass Merrow had gone legitimate-ish. That alone was worth a second look.

The terms were straightforward enough. Salvage and recovery of one sealed strongbox from the wreck of the *Kessler Star*. Compensation generous — genuinely generous, the kind of number that made Jude's eyebrows move, and her eyebrows were professionals who didn't move for just anyone. Crew costs covered. Equipment covered. A bonus clause tied to delivery date that read less like optimism and more like a threat wearing a nice suit.

And then, section nine, subsection two, in language no lawyer should have approved:

*Recovery site: the Kessler Star, high glacier shelf, Aldous Range.*

Jude read it twice. The paper didn't blink.

"That's not a dive," she said finally. "That's a mountain."

"It's a shipwreck that happens to be on a mountain."

"Ships don't happen to be on mountains, Cass. Ships are *put* on mountains, or mountains *move*, and either way somebody's lying to me."

"Nobody's lying to you." Cass leaned against the rail, casual as Sunday, though Jude noticed her knuckles had gone pale around the folder's edge. "The Kessler Star went into the range sixty years ago. Flood season, the old river shifted, took half a hillside with it — the whole valley's built on stories like that, half of them true. By the time anyone figured out where she'd ended up, she was forty miles inland and two thousand meters up, frozen into a glacier shelf above the snowline. The company surveyed it in the fifties. Sealed her holds, posted a claim, and then — nothing. Sixty years of nothing."

"Why?"

"Because the navy patrols the approach. Because the Aldous Mining Consortium owns every legal road within thirty miles. Because everyone who's ever tried has either turned back or stopped turning back." Cass shrugged, and the shrug was too smooth, the shrug of a woman who had answers to questions she wasn't asking aloud. "But mostly because Captain Voss forbade it."

There it was. Jude felt it land in her chest like a dropped anchor.

Every crew has its one law. On Voss's boats it had been this: *nobody touches the Kessler Star.* Not for salvage, not for curiosity, not for the money that would have set them all up for life. Ilsa had never explained it, not once, not drunk, not dying, not in the long grey watches when captains tell their crews everything else. She'd just said *we don't do that job,* and because she was Ilsa Voss, that had been the end of the conversation for eleven years and two boats and one entire generation of divers who came up under her.

Jude had signed that oath at nineteen, standing beside Cass Merrow, both of them still wet behind the ears and certain that rules were for people without imagination.

"And she's dead," Jude said quietly. "So now the oath expires."

"The oath doesn't expire, Joubert. Oaths don't work like milk." Cass straightened. "But oaths can be *released*. And I'm telling you she released it. End of the last season, before she got sick — she called me in. Just me. Sat me down in her kitchen with tea going cold between us and told me that someday someone would come asking about the Star, and that when they did, I shouldn't stand in their way." Cass's voice did something complicated on the word *someday.* "I asked her why. She wouldn't say. She poured herself a drink instead, which for Ilsa was practically a confession."

"You expect me to believe that."

"I expect you to believe that I have no reason to lie to you about this particular thing, because lying to you about this particular thing gets me nothing you weren't already going to give me." Cass tapped the contract. "The money's real. The job's real. The wreck is real — I've seen the survey photographs. Forty pages of them. A ship sitting in ice like a fly in amber, intact, holds sealed, exactly where the old charts say she should be."

Jude looked at the photographs. They were good photographs. The *Kessler Star* lay canted on her starboard side, half-swallowed by blue ice, her name still legible on the stern in flaking gold leaf. Somebody had loved that lettering once. Looking at her made Jude feel the specific vertigo of seeing a grave that shouldn't exist — a drowned thing breathing mountain air.

It was beautiful. It was wrong. She wanted it and distrusted wanting it, which was, she was beginning to suspect, the whole design.

"What's in the box, Cass?"

And here Cass paused. Not the theatrical pause — Jude knew those, could time them, had watched Cass deploy them across a hundred negotiations. This was different. This pause had weight in it. Cass looked out at the water for a moment, at the grey-green swell, and when she turned back her face had rearranged itself into something older and less amused.

"That's the part I should have led with," she said. "And the part I nearly didn't say at all."

"Cass."

"There's a name inside that box. Stenciled on the strongbox itself, according to the survey manifest — the original manifest, the one from the consortium's own archive, which I paid a great deal of money and committed several degrees of light illegality to obtain." Cass's jaw tightened. "The box was consigned to the *Kessler Star*'s final run under seal. Private cargo. The consignor's line reads: *Joubert.*"

The wind picked up off the water and Jude didn't feel it. She was aware of the dock beneath her boots, the gulls arguing over something down the pier, Hollis's engine idling somewhere behind her — all of it happening at a distance, muffled, like sounds through a hull.

Her family name. On a sealed box. In a forbidden wreck, on a forbidden mountain, in the one place Ilsa Voss had ever drawn a line in the world and defended it with silence.

"Fifteen years," Jude said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she'd expected it to crack. "Fifteen years I've stayed away from that range. Fifteen years I built my whole life around never going back — different coastlines, different names on the charters, never staying anywhere longer than a season so nobody could know me well enough to ask. And you're telling me my family's name has been sitting inside that wreck the entire time."

"Yes."

"And Ilsa knew."

Cass said nothing for a long moment. Then, carefully: "I think Ilsa knew a great many things she never said out loud."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Cass agreed. "It isn't. But it's the only one I've got, and I've carried it for four years, and I'm tired of carrying it alone." She held out a pen. Not dramatically — just held it out, the way you offer someone a handrail. "Sign or don't. Walk away and I'll find another diver, because I will see this done, Joubert. But there's nobody else alive who dove with me under Voss, nobody else who knew the captain, nobody else whose name is on that box. So I'm asking you first. I'm asking you *only*, if I'm honest, because if you say no I'll probably spend the rest of my life wondering whether you were right to."

Jude looked at the pen. She thought about all the things she was good at not thinking about.

She thought about her brother.

She had not said his name aloud in eleven years. Not to anyone. There had been a night, near the end, when he'd walked up into the range ahead of a storm and not come back, and the search parties had found nothing, and the town had closed around the loss like water closing over a stone, and Jude had left before the ice melted. Everyone in Kessler believed she'd run from grief. Some of them believed worse. She had let them believe whatever was easiest, because the truth was a room she kept locked even from herself, and the key was buried somewhere she refused to dig.

And now here was Cass Merrow on a dock in Gullwater Point, holding out a pen and a contract and a name, saying: *the answer's in the ice. It's been in the ice the whole time. Come and get it.*

It was, Jude reflected, a diabolical piece of work. Cass hadn't offered her money — money she could refuse. Cass had offered her the locked room. The key. The one door Jude had nailed shut from the inside, presented as a salvage job with a bonus clause.

"I hate you," Jude said, with genuine warmth.

"That's the spirit." Cass's grin came back, sharp and bright, and for one unguarded second she looked nineteen again, standing on a colder dock in a harder wind, both of them certain the sea owed them something. Then it faded, and what was underneath was tired and human and afraid, and Jude realized with a small internal shock that Cass Merrow was afraid of this job too. Had been, probably, for years.

"One condition," Jude said.

"Name it."

"We do it properly. Full crew, real expedition, no half-cocked dash up the range because you got impatient. I pick half the team. We go in late summer, before the snows, and if the window closes, we turn around — no heroics, no pushing past the weather because the bonus clause says so. I've pulled bodies out of mountains, Cass. I know how they got there."

"Agreed."

"And I want the survey photographs. All forty pages. Tonight."

"Already in your cabin."

Jude blinked. "My—"

"Charter's booked through the week. Hollis is charging me double, which he deserves." Cass nodded toward the small berth compartment aft, where, Jude now noticed with mounting alarm, a duffel bag she did not recognize sat neatly stowed beside her own. "I took the liberty."

"You broke into my cabin."

"It's a charter cabin, Joubert, nobody breaks into—"

"You went through my things."

"I put a folder in it. I didn't touch anything else." Cass paused, then added, with the honesty of a woman who knows she's already lost the moral high ground and might as well sightsee from down there: "Your knot-tying book fell open. Page forty-one. The double fisherman's. Very tidy work. Ilsa would be proud."

"*Get off my boat.*"

"It's Hollis's boat."

"*GET OFF HOLLIS'S BOAT.*"

Cass laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound carrying down the dock and startling the gulls — and it was such an ordinary, human, infuriating sound that Jude found, to her considerable irritation, that some tight thing in her chest had loosened half a turn. She hadn't laughed with anyone in a long time. She hadn't let anyone close enough to laugh at her. Cass Merrow had managed it in under ten minutes, which was either a testament to their history or an indictment of it.

Jude signed the contract. She told herself it was for the money, and for about four minutes she almost believed it.

Then Cass packed up her folder, wished Hollis a pleasant afternoon, and stepped down onto the dock — and as she did, she turned back, and her voice dropped to something quiet and careful, meant only for Jude, pitched under the wind.

"For what it's worth," she said, "when I opened that manifest and saw the name — I sat in my car for an hour before I drove to find you. I couldn't make my hands let go of the steering wheel." She looked out at the water, then back. "Whatever's in that box, Joubert — whoever put it there, and why — I don't think it's going to be what any of us expect. I think it's going to be worse. And I think you need to hear that from someone before you're two thousand meters up a glacier with no way home but the truth."

She left. Jude watched her go, coat too clean, stride too confident, all the way down the pier and out of sight.

Then she sat down hard on the charter's rail, stared at her own signature drying on the page, and understood that she had just done the one thing she had spent fifteen years making impossible: she had agreed to go back.

* * *

That night, in the cabin, Jude spread the survey photographs across the bunk and studied them by lamplight until the fuel ran low.

The *Kessler Star*. Three masts, iron-bowed, built heavy for river freight and heavier for secrets. She'd gone missing with a full consignment — mining equipment outbound, private cargo inbound — and the consortium's own surveyors had noted, in a hand that grew shakier toward the end of the report, that the sealed hold had been *inaccessible due to ice ingress* and that *further examination was not recommended.*

Not recommended. Not forbidden. Just — not recommended. As if the men writing the report had looked at that hold the way you look at a door in your own house that you suddenly realize you've never opened.

Jude turned to the last photograph. It showed the stern, close up, the gold lettering half-flaked away. And beneath the ship's name, cut into the ice shelf itself — recent, shallow, made with something small and sharp, invisible unless you knew to look —

Someone had scratched four letters into the glacier.

*K-E-S-S.*

She sat with that a long time. The lamp guttered. Outside, the harbor lights bobbed in the dark, and somewhere beyond them the range stood waiting under its early snows, patient as a creditor.

Four letters. An unfinished word. Or an unfinished message. Or a hand that had been interrupted mid-scratch — by cold, by dark, by something with teeth.

Jude lay back on the bunk and did not sleep, and when morning came she was already packing, because there was a crew to hire, and she knew exactly the kind of people she needed: strangers. Good ones. Strangers who would become something else on the ice, whether she was ready for that or not.

Starting, she supposed, with whoever answered the first advertisement.

She just hoped none of them would turn out to be anyone she already knew.

The universe, as established, had other plans.
