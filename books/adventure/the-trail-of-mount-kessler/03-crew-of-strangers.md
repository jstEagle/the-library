## 3. Crew of Strangers

The first thing Jude learned about Okafor was that he had taken apart her winch before she'd finished introducing herself.

She came around the corner of the dock shed with two coffees and found a broad, silver-haired man sitting cross-legged in the guts of the machine she'd rebuilt herself only last spring, tools laid out on a folded cloth with the neatness of a surgeon's tray. He did not look up. His hands moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once met a mechanism that frightened him.

"That's a double-block reeving," he said, to the winch or to her, hard to say. "Whoever rigged this was showing off."

"I rigged it."

He looked up then. He had a face like a well-used anvil — dented, dependable, impossible to knock over — and eyes that went from her face to the coffee in her hand and back with open arithmetic.

"Then you were showing off," he said, and held out one palm for the coffee.

Jude had planned to interview him. She had questions prepared, written out the night before in her blunt sailor's shorthand: experience at altitude, cold-weather tolerances, references. She watched him drink her coffee — no permission asked, no apology offered — and decided the interview was already over and he had passed.

"Adaeze Okafor," she said, reading the name off the application crumpled in her pocket. "You spelled your first name wrong."

"Everyone assumes Adaeze." He wiped his hands on the cloth, carefully, tool by tool, before folding it away. "It's Adaezé. The accent matters. My mother spent forty years correcting people and I promised her I'd keep doing it after she was gone. It's the only promise I've ever kept without effort."

"What are you running from?"

Most people flinched at that question. Okafor just smiled, slow as sunrise, and began packing up the rest of his tools.

"Nothing, Ms. Joubert. That's the problem. I retired two years ago from the rail yards. You know what retirement is? It's a chair, and a window, and a wife who loves you, and nothing in the whole world that needs fixing." He stood, joints cracking like small-caliber fire. "I fixed the window latch twice. Then I fixed it again even though it wasn't broken. She told me to go find something that needed me before I started taking apart the piano. So here I am. I don't need money. I need a machine that will try to kill us, so I can refuse to let it."

Jude tested him the way she tested rope: she looked for the flaw, the fray, the place where the fibers gave under load. She found none. That unsettled her more than a flaw would have. Perfect rope made her suspicious; perfect rope usually meant someone hadn't pulled hard enough yet.

"We're going into the range ahead of the snows," she said. "Cold enough to crack steel. If the winch fails on the glacier shelf, we lose the salvage and possibly the climbers."

"If the winch fails on the glacier shelf," Okafor said, "it will be because I let it, for reasons I will explain afterward, at length, whether anyone wants to hear them or not." He stuck out his hand. "When do we leave?"

She shook it. His grip was exactly what his face promised.

* * *

Tamsin arrived four hours early for an interview scheduled in three days.

Jude found her sitting on the mooring bollard outside the shed at six in the morning, a rucksack between her boots and a thermos balanced on her knee, watching the harbor fog burn off with the expression of someone who had read about harbors in books and was determined not to look too delighted by the real thing. She couldn't have been more than twenty-two. She had the kind of face that hadn't decided yet what it wanted to be — all angles and eagerness, freckles across the nose like a constellation still being named.

"You're early," Jude said.

"You're observant." The girl hopped down off the bollard and stuck out her hand, then seemed to remember something and adjusted the grip to something less crushing. "Tamsin Vale. Climber. Two seasons on the eastern faces, one winter traverse of the Col Route, solo, which everyone says is impossible, which is why I did it."

"Everyone says it's impossible because three people died trying it."

"Two died," Tamsin said. "The third turned back. And I'm not everyone." She said it without bravado — that was the strange part. No swagger in it at all. Just a plain fact delivered the way you'd report the weather, as if she'd checked it against instruments.

Jude looked for the flaw. With the young ones it was always the same flaw: they loved the mountains more than they feared them, and the mountains could smell the difference. Fear kept you alive. Love got you killed at altitude, smiling.

"Why this job?" Jude asked. "The pay's decent but there's easier climbing that pays better."

Something crossed Tamsin's face then — quick, gone again, like a fish under ice. "I want to work with you," she said. "You're Joubert. The diver. Everyone on the eastern faces knows the story — the woman who went down into the *Meridian* wreck when it was already collapsing and brought out two people and a ship's bell. You held your breath for—" She stopped herself, visibly recalibrating. "Anyway. I've read everything written about you, which isn't much, and I've asked around, which got me more, and I decided if I'm going to learn high-altitude rigging from anyone, it should be from someone who treats rope like it's a living thing."

"It's not a living thing."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But you talk to it anyway. The dockhand told me. You check every line twice and you touch it when you do, like you're taking its pulse."

Jude hadn't known anyone was watching her do that. She filed the discomfort away for later inspection.

"Show me your knots," she said.

Tamsin showed her eleven knots in ninety seconds, fingers flying, narrating each one in the breathless tone of a person reciting scripture. On the twelfth — a load-release hitch, the kind you tie when someone is hanging in space and every second has a price — her hands slowed. She tied it perfectly. Then she untied it and tied it again, slower, and looked up.

"My brother taught me that one," she said. "He's the reason I climb. He stopped. I didn't."

There it was. Not the flaw Jude had been looking for, but a flaw nonetheless — grief braided into competence, which was either the strongest or the most dangerous material a climber could be made of. Jude knew that weave intimately. She was wearing it herself.

"Pack for cold colder than you think," Jude said. "We leave when the navigator says we leave."

"Reyes?" Tamsin's eyebrows went up. "You hired Reyes? Nobody hires Reyes. Reyes *permits* you to hire him. There's a difference. Ask anyone who's tried."

"I don't need to ask anyone," Jude said. "I sent him a letter. He answered with coordinates and a tide table and no message at all."

"Yeah," Tamsin said, with something close to reverence. "That's him accepting."

* * *

Reyes appeared the way weather appears: no announcement, just a change in the quality of the air.

Jude stepped out of the shed at dusk to find a lean, gray man standing at the end of her dock, hands clasped behind his back, studying the water as though it were a document he'd been asked to proofread. He wore a coat older than Tamsin and carried nothing — no bag, no gear, nothing. Either he owned almost nothing or he trusted the journey to provide, and Jude suspected both were true.

"Mr. Reyes."

"Ms. Joubert." His voice was quiet and dry, like paper turning pages. "Your route is wrong."

"Good evening to you too."

"The coastal approach adds nine days. The storm window closes early this year — the barometric record says so, and the birds agree with the barometer, which is when you believe it. We go through the Kettle Narrows on the third day, not the fifth, and we resupply at Kessler Town whether you want to or not, because there is nowhere else." He finally turned to look at her. His eyes were pale, nearly colorless, and entirely unbothered. "Also, your mooring line will chafe through in the next blow. Port side, third cleat. Fix it tonight."

She had checked that line that morning. She checked it again after he left, holding it the way she held all rope, feeling for its pulse. The chafe was there, hidden under the serving where the line crossed the fairlead, invisible unless you knew to put your thumb exactly where he'd said. She stood on her dock in the dark, splicing in new line, and thought: *this one sees everything and says almost none of it.*

That was either the best kind of navigator or the worst kind of crewmate, and there was only one way to find out which.

"Why do you want this crossing?" she asked him the next morning, when he materialized aboard without knocking. "Everyone else wants something. Money, glory, a mountain to argue with. What's yours?"

Reyes considered the question with the seriousness other men reserved for chess.

"I navigated a survey expedition into the high range eleven years ago," he said. "Six of us went in. Four came out. The official report blames weather." He paused. "The report is wrong. I have spent eleven years knowing it's wrong and being unable to prove it, because proving it requires going back through country nobody sane crosses. Your route goes through that country." He picked up her chart, studied it, and set it down a quarter-inch straighter than she'd left it. "I don't need the strongbox. I need the passage. The truth I'm looking for is in the terrain itself."

"And if the terrain kills us instead?"

"Then the terrain will have been honest with us," Reyes said, "which is more than I can say for most people."

Jude laughed — short, surprised out of her. It was the first time she'd laughed since Cass Merrow had walked onto her dock with a contract and a name, and it felt strange in her chest, like a muscle remembered from a previous life.

* * *

They assembled for the first time as a full crew on the last mild morning of the season, around the scarred table in the shed, charts spread and pinned at the corners. Cass sat at the head because Cass always took the head of any table within reach, and Jude let her, because it cost nothing and bought peace, and because watching Cass perform command was instructive in the way that watching a card sharp is instructive.

"So," Cass said, tapping the route. "Narrows on day three, Kessler Town by day six, the wreck by—"

"Day nine," Reyes said, "if the wind cooperates, and the wind owes me a favor."

"—the wreck by day nine," Cass amended smoothly, "and the summit approach inside the window. Questions?"

Okafor raised his hand, which was somehow both sincere and mocking. "One. When the winch freezes — and it will freeze, madam, steel gets shy below minus thirty — who makes the call? The engineer who knows the machine or the captain who owns the contract?"

"The engineer," Jude said, before Cass could answer. "Always the engineer. A captain who overrides her mechanic on a glacier deserves what she gets."

Cass's eyes flicked to her, quick and unreadable, and something passed between them that wasn't quite warmth and wasn't quite war. Fifteen years of history compressed into a single glance. Cass nodded once, and moved on.

Tamsin, meanwhile, was vibrating. Jude could see it — the girl had been introduced to her childhood legend an hour ago and was managing, heroically, to say almost nothing about it, which meant the pressure was building toward some eventual catastrophic release. Jude decided to get ahead of it.

"Vale. You'll run point on the ice approaches. You'll also carry the backup rigging kit, which means when I tell you to place protection, you place it where I say, how I say, even if it looks wrong to you. Especially then."

"Yes ma'am." A pause. The dam cracking. "Can I ask — the *Meridian* dive. The bell. Did you really—"

"Tamsin," Reyes said mildly, without looking up from his charts, "she'll tell you on the glacier, when there's nothing else to do. That's how captains work. They ration their stories to match the boredom."

Jude caught Reyes's eye. He returned to his charts, innocent as a closed book.

Okafor chuckled into his coffee. "Oh, I like this crew," he announced, to no one in particular. "This crew is going to try very hard to kill us, and we are going to be extremely rude about refusing."

Outside, the light had begun to change — that thinning, amber, end-of-season light that mountaineers dread and poets forgive. Jude rolled up the charts and felt the old familiar itch in her feet, the one that used to feel like freedom and lately felt like something with a shape she could almost name.

Four strangers at a table. Each one carrying a reason, wrapped and sealed, like cargo they'd sworn not to declare. A man who needed something to need him. A girl climbing after a brother who stopped. A navigator hunting a lie the landscape was keeping for him. And a rival-captain with a contract and a secret, funding the whole enterprise for leverage she wouldn't explain.

And Jude herself, who had signed on for a name in a box.

*Test them like rope,* she reminded herself. *Look for flaws.* But she'd pulled on all three of them now, hard, and what she'd found instead was load-bearing fiber all the way through — flawed, yes, every strand of it, frayed in places, spliced in others, but sound. Sound the way a good line is sound: not because it's unbroken, but because someone bothered to mend it and trusted it again afterward.

She hated how much she already liked them. Strangers were safe. Crews were not. Crews became family, and family was the one cargo she'd sworn off shipping years ago.

That night, as she locked the shed, she noticed the envelope wedged in the door — heavy cream paper, wax-sealed, bearing a crest she recognized from the company storefronts in every catalog that reached the coast: a pick crossed with a lightning bolt. The Aldous mark.

Inside, a single card, in handwriting so elegant it was practically a threat:

*Ms. Joubert — Before you commit to the mountain, allow me to buy you dinner. Some cargoes are better declared than discovered. — E. Pike*

Jude stood in the dark a long moment, the card in her hand, the seal broken, the harbor slapping gently at the pilings below.

So the dynasty already knew. Of course it did. She looked back through the shed window at her crew of strangers, laughing at something Okafor had said, and understood that the crossing had already begun — not on the water, not on the ice, but here, tonight, with an invitation she couldn't afford to refuse and couldn't imagine surviving.

She tucked the card into her pocket, next to the contract, next to the four letters her brother had scratched into steel.

Dinner, then.
