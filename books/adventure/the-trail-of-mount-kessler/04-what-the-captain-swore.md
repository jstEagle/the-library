## 4. What the Captain Swore

The galley of the *Merrow's Fortune* smelled of diesel and burnt coffee, which was to say it smelled like every boat Jude had ever worked, and she found the sameness of it comforting in a way she didn't examine too closely. Okafor had claimed the stove and was performing some kind of alchemy with a can of tomatoes and a suspicious quantity of garlic. Tamsin sat on the bench with her boots off, feet steaming gently by the heater, narrating her blisters like they were war stories.

"Left heel. Right big toe. A bruise the exact shape of Luxembourg," she said, wiggling her toes for emphasis.

"That's not a country," Reyes said mildly, from his corner, where he was doing something quiet and precise with a chart and a pencil.

"It is if I say it is."

Jude almost laughed. Almost. There was a hitch in her chest that kept catching on things lately — a laugh, a breath, a memory — like a fishhook snagging on old netting. She'd been feeling it since she signed Cass's contract. Since the name *Kessler* had come out of Cass's mouth in that dockside office, casual as a weather report, and something in Jude had gone very still and very cold.

She'd told herself it was just the money. She was good at telling herself things.

Okafor handed her a bowl without being asked, which was his way. "Eat," he said. "You've got that look divers get when they're thinking about depth instead of dinner."

"I'm always thinking about depth."

"Yes, but usually you're *smiling* about it."

The stew was better than it had any right to be. Jude ate mechanically, watching Tamsin argue geography with Reyes, watching Okafor defend his garlic choices against all comers, and thought, not for the first time, that this crew was dangerously easy to like. That was the problem with people. You liked them, and then they knew things about you, and then you couldn't leave cleanly anymore.

Fifteen years of clean exits. She'd gotten very good at them.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep in her bunk — too narrow, too warm, too much like staying — Jude went up on deck. The anchorage was calm, the water black glass, the stars doubled perfectly in the reflection so that she seemed to float in space rather than on sea. She sat on the rail with her back against the shrouds and let the cold find the gaps in her jacket.

And because there was nothing else to do, because the mountains were waiting at the end of this route like a debt collector, her mind did what it always did when Kessler came up.

It went back to the night everything ended.

* * *

Fifteen years ago. The Voss house, up past the tree line road, the one with the crooked porch that Ilsa never fixed because she said a straight porch invited complacency. The whole crew had been there — Cass, twenty-two and already wearing her ambition like armor; Okafor, younger then, still calling everyone "sir" until Ilsa broke him of it; Reyes, who even at nineteen had spoken maybe forty words a day and made each one count. And Danny. Danny Joubert, nineteen years old, laughing on the porch rail with a beer he was mostly holding for show, because Danny had never quite learned to drink and never quite admitted it.

And Ilsa Voss, standing in the kitchen doorway with a piece of paper in her hand, looking like a woman who had been handed a live grenade with the pin already pulled.

It was an offer. Of course it was an offer. It was always an offer, in those days; the Aldous Mining Company had its fingers in everything in the valley, and salvage was no exception. A wreck on the high glacier shelf — the *Kessler Star*, a supply plane that had gone down thirty years before with a sealed strongbox aboard. The company wanted it recovered quietly. They were prepared to pay obscenely well for quiet.

Ilsa read the letter twice. Then she folded it along its original creases, which Jude would remember later as the most frightening part — the care of it, the deliberation, a woman putting something away the way you put away a thing you intend to think about for the rest of your life.

"No," Ilsa said.

Cass, who had been angling toward the offer since the moment she'd heard about it, said, "Ilsa—"

"No." Ilsa set the letter on the table and put her coffee mug on top of it, a paperweight of finality. "We don't take Aldous money. We don't go near that wreck. We don't discuss it again."

"Why?" Jude had asked. She was seventeen, and seventeen-year-olds ask why the way hammers ask nails.

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment. Jude remembered that look — the captain's gray eyes going somewhere else entirely, somewhere behind her own face, and coming back changed.

"Because some wrecks aren't cargo," Ilsa said. "Some wrecks are graves. And some graves are kept on purpose."

Nobody asked what that meant. Nobody except Cass, later, privately, and whatever answer Ilsa gave her, Cass had swallowed it whole and never mentioned it again — which, Jude understood now, standing on the deck of Cass's own boat fifteen years later, meant Cass had understood it perfectly and simply decided she could live with the arithmetic anyway.

But that night, that first night, the swearing-off had been formal. Ilsa lined them up in the kitchen like a firing squad in reverse — the captain facing the crew instead of the other way around — and she made each of them say it aloud.

"I won't take the Kessler job," Okafor said, solemn as a church deacon, and then ruined the gravity slightly by adding, "even though the pay would've fixed my truck."

"I won't take the Kessler job," Reyes said, in four words exactly, because that was how Reyes did everything.

"I won't take the Kessler job," Cass said, and her voice was smooth and convincing and completely hollow, and Jude had known it even then, known it the way you know a smile that doesn't reach the eyes. But she hadn't said anything. Seventeen-year-olds don't call out their elders. They file it away.

Danny said it last, grinning, throwing in a salute. "I won't take the Kessler job, Captain, cross my heart and hope to — what was it — hope to dive dry?"

"Drown," Ilsa corrected, without humor. "Hope to drown."

"Cross my heart and hope to drown," Danny repeated, delighted with himself.

Jude said it too. She said it easily, glibly, the way you swear off things you have no intention of wanting anyway. What did she care about some plane wreck in the ice? She cared about the water, the warm coast water, the work she was good at. Mountains were other people's territory.

She said: "I won't take the Kessler job."

And Isla nodded, once, and poured herself three fingers of whiskey, and that should have been the end of it.

* * *

It wasn't the end of it. Because Danny wouldn't let it be.

Danny had questions. Danny always had questions — he was the curious one, the one who took apart radios and put them back together with extra parts, the one who asked the Aldous foreman why the mine shafts ran east when the ore ran west. Their parents had died when Jude was twelve and Danny fourteen, and Ilsa Voss had taken them both in the way you take in strays: without discussion, without softness, and without ever once making them feel like charity. Danny had adored her. Jude had — well. Jude had needed her, which was a different animal, and one with sharper teeth.

So when Danny started asking, over the following weeks, why the captain was so certain, why the company wanted that box so badly, why a grave needed keeping — Jude had listened, half fond, half impatient. Danny chased mysteries the way other dogs chased cars.

"He's going to get himself hurt," Cass told her once, in the boatyard, low, under the smell of epoxy. "You should rein him in."

"You rein him in, then. You're the one who likes giving orders."

"I give orders people follow. He doesn't follow anyone's."

That should have been a warning. Instead it had felt like a competition, and Jude, being seventeen and stupid in the specific way that clever people are stupid, had treated it as one.

Because here was the thing Jude had never told anyone, the thing that lived in her like a splinter working toward the heart: Danny had come to her, two nights before he disappeared, with a plan. He'd found something — a record, a name, a thread — something about the strongbox, about what was inside it, about whose fortune it really was. He'd been lit up like a lantern about it. He'd said, *Jude, if this is what I think it is, it's ours. It's ours, and Ilsa knows, and she's sitting on it.*

He'd said, *I'm going up to look. Just to look. I'll be careful.*

And Jude — seventeen, terrified, loyal to Ilsa Voss in a way she couldn't have explained and didn't try to — had done the worst thing she has ever done, which is also the simplest thing, which is the way the worst things always are.

She'd told Ilsa.

Not to stop him, she'd told herself. Not to betray him. To protect him. Because Ilsa would handle it — Ilsa would sit him down with that gray stare and that crooked-porch authority and she would make Danny understand, the way she made everyone understand, that some doors stay shut. That was all. A word to the captain. A family matter, handled in-house.

Ilsa had gone very quiet when Jude told her. Quieter than Jude had ever seen her. And then she'd said, "Thank you for telling me," in a voice that thanked and accused in the same breath, and she'd left the house that night without her coat.

Danny left the next morning, before dawn, with a daypack and his grandfather's compass and a note on the kitchen table that said *Back in three days, don't tell Ilsa* — which was funny, horribly funny, because Ilsa already knew, and because the only person Danny had thought he was hiding from was the one person who'd already given him away.

He didn't come back in three days.

He didn't come back at all.

* * *

They searched. Of course they searched. Ilsa organized it like a military campaign — grids, radio schedules, the works — and the valley turned out volunteers, and for eleven days the mountains rang with Danny's name shouted into couloirs. Jude climbed until her hands split. Cass flew supply runs. Okafor drove the rescue truck on roads that weren't roads. Reyes walked ridgelines alone, which nobody understood until much later, when they understood that Reyes processed grief by processing terrain.

On the twelfth day, Ilsa called it off. She stood in the boatyard with snow in her hair and said, "The mountain keeps what it takes," and something in her had closed like a door.

And Jude — Jude had stood there knowing. Knowing that Danny had gone up because of her. Knowing that Ilsa had known where he'd be, or close enough, and had gone after him too late or not at all or — the not-knowing was the poison, the not-knowing, fifteen years of it. Had Ilsa tried to intercept him? Had she arrived too late? Had she chosen not to arrive? Jude had never asked. Asking would make it real, and real things require answers, and answers require someone to be guilty, and Jude had spent fifteen years arranging her life so that no one could ever be guilty of anything near her again.

Including herself. Especially herself.

Because here was the exile's arithmetic, the sum she'd been carrying since she was seventeen: if she had never told Ilsa, Danny might have gone up anyway — he was going up regardless, that was Danny — but he'd have gone up as a free boy chasing a mystery, with the whole crew behind him when he failed to come home, with no shadow between him and his captain. Instead he'd gone up hunted. Instead the last thing between Danny and the mountain had been the knowledge — if he ever learned it, and Jude had never stopped believing he'd learned it somehow, up there, alone, reading it in the wind — that his own sister had sold his route.

You can't drown in that. Drowning would be a mercy. You can only sail away from it, harbor to harbor, contract to contract, never staying anywhere long enough for anyone to learn your middle name, let alone your sin.

* * *

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Tamsin's voice, from the companionway. Jude hadn't heard her come up. The kid moved quietly for someone who narrated her own blisters.

"Tides," Jude said. Automatic. The lie of a woman with fifteen years of practice.

Tamsin came and leaned on the rail beside her, close enough for warmth, far enough for respect. She looked out at the doubled stars and said nothing for a while, which for Tamsin was a personal record.

Then: "Can I ask you something? About the job?"

"Ask."

"The wreck. The *Kessler Star*. It's on a glacier shelf. Fourteen thousand feet. There's no water anywhere near it." Tamsin turned to look at her, young face earnest in the dark. "How do you dive a wreck that's above the snowline?"

Jude looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — really laughed, the hook in her chest finally letting go of something — and said, "Kid, that is an excellent question, and the honest answer is that I've been asking myself the same one since I signed."

"So you don't know."

"I know frozen hulls behave like hulls. I know ice behaves like water that's forgotten itself. I know that somewhere in the back of my head, a nineteen-year-old boy is laughing at me for agreeing to climb toward him after fifteen years of climbing away." She said it lightly. She almost meant it lightly. "Beyond that, we'll find out together."

Tamsin grinned. "Together's my favorite kind of finding out."

"I know," Jude said. "That's what worries me."

Because that was the other half of the arithmetic, the half she'd only recently begun to suspect: Tamsin liked her. Trusted her. Was building, brick by oblivious brick, the same altar of belief that Danny had built, and Jude knew now — had known since the dock, since the name, since the cold — what happened to people who believed in Jude Joubert.

The kid yawned hugely, apologized for yawning, and went below. Jude stayed on the rail a while longer, watching the anchor light tremble in its own reflection.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *Tomorrow we make for the range.* And somewhere ahead, under early snow, under thirty years of ice, a strongbox waited with her family's name inside it — and beside it, maybe, scratched into metal by a dying boy's hand, an answer to the question she'd been sailing away from since she was seventeen.

She was going to have to stop running long enough to read it.

She just hoped, God help her, that whatever Danny had written down, it wasn't her name.
