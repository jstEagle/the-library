## 5. The Navy Won't Discuss It

Reyes had a rule about harbors: you left them before the people in them finished their coffee. A harbor was a place where other people's opinions of your vessel accumulated like barnacles, and by mid-morning every dockhand, customs clerk, and retired fisherman with nothing better to do had formed one. So when the *Merrow's Folly* — Cass had named the boat, and Reyes maintained this explained a great deal about her — slipped her lines at 0540 with the gulls still arguing over the fish market scraps, he considered it a professional courtesy to everyone involved.

He had the watch alone. The crew wouldn't surface for another hour, and he liked it that way. Navigation was best done in the quiet, when the numbers were still honest — before the day got loud enough that a man started rounding them off to suit his mood.

He plotted their first leg with a pencil worn down to a stub, the way his grandmother had taught him on a fishing smack off the southern islands, back when charts were paper and the sea was a language you learned by drowning in it a little at a time. *Course two-seven-five true. Wind northwest, freshening. Tide turns at 0712.* Simple arithmetic. He'd been doing simple arithmetic his whole life, and it had never once gotten him shot at, which was more than could be said for most of his former professions.

The patrol boat appeared at 0630.

Reyes noticed it the way he noticed everything — sideways, without turning his head, because turning your head was how you told the world what you'd seen. It came out of the naval anchorage two miles south, a gray hull with a clean wake and no fishing gear on its decks, and it settled onto a course that was, after eleven minutes of small corrections, exactly theirs.

Not converging. Not crossing. *Matching.* Two hundred yards off their starboard quarter, close enough to read their name, far enough to be polite about it.

Reyes made three marks on the chart and drank his coffee, which was terrible, because Okafor had made it, because Okafor believed coffee was an engineering problem solved by applying more heat.

*Professional interest,* he thought. Not law. Law came at you head-on, lights blazing, hailing you by name on channel sixteen. This was something else. This was a man walking beside you in a market, matching your pace, waiting to see where you shopped.

By 0700 the rest of the crew had come up in ones and twos, drawn by the smell of nothing in particular and the instinct, common to all working sailors, that if someone else was awake and calm, they might as well get up too.

Tamsin arrived first, hair like a startled hedgehog, carrying a mug she'd stolen from the galley and would deny stealing. She squinted astern. "Are we being followed?"

"We're being accompanied," Reyes said.

"Is there a difference?"

"One of them ends with us in prison."

Okafor emerged next, filling the companionway the way he filled everything, a big man who moved through the world as though it had been slightly under-built for him. He looked at the gray hull, looked at Reyes, and raised his terrible coffee in something between a toast and a question.

"Navy," Reyes said.

"I can see it's navy. I'm asking why navy."

"That," said Reyes, "is the interesting part."

Jude came up last, as she seemed to do everything — last, quietly, without announcing herself, so that you only realized she'd been there for a while when you turned around. She stood at the rail and studied the patrol boat for a long moment with an expression Reyes was beginning to catalog. He thought of it as her *measuring* face. She did it to rigging, to weather, to people. Everything got measured, and very little of it passed.

"They've been on us since 0630," Reyes told her. "They adjusted course four times to stay parallel. That's not a patrol route. Patrol routes don't bend around a forty-foot salvage tug."

"What is it, then?"

"An escort nobody asked for."

Cass appeared from below already dressed, already annoyed, in the manner of a woman who had been informed of events and found them presumptuous. "Anyone want to tell me why we've got company, or should I just assume the obvious and start panicking in an orderly fashion?"

"The obvious," Reyes said, "would be premature."

"I love it when you say things like that. It makes me feel like I'm on a television program."

The morning went on. The patrol boat went on. They ate breakfast; it matched their speed. Tamsin went up the mast to check the spreaders; it held station without wobbling, which took real seamanship, and Reyes noted that too. Whoever was driving that boat had done this kind of thing before, and recently, and not against fishermen.

It was past nine when the radio crackled.

"*Vessel Merrow's Folly, vessel Merrow's Folly, this is Naval Patrol Craft Alder Three-Seven.*"

Reyes picked up the handset. "Alder Three-Seven, Merrow's Folly. Go ahead."

There was a pause — short, but Reyes caught it, the way you catch a false note in a hymn. Then:

"*You are approaching a restricted exercise area. Advise you alter course to the south and maintain well clear.*"

Reyes looked at his chart. There was no restricted exercise area on his chart. There was no restricted area on any chart he owned, including two that were current within the year. He keyed the mic. "Alder Three-Seven, Merrow's Folly. Can you give me the coordinates of the restriction? My charts don't show it."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"*The restriction is in effect. Advise you alter course south.*"

Same words. Nearly the same cadence. Reyes had heard men recite things before — confessions, alibis, last rites — and he knew the sound of language that had been drilled until it lost its meaning. This officer wasn't describing a restriction. He was *performing* one.

"Alder Three-Seven, we are proceeding north-northwest along the coastal shipping lane. Is there a specific hazard you're advising us of?"

Pause.

"*The restriction is in effect.*"

Tamsin, hovering by the radio, mouthed: *the restriction is in effect,* in a fair imitation of the officer's flat delivery, and Okafor snorted coffee through his nose, which served him right.

"Understood, Alder Three-Seven," Reyes said pleasantly. "We'll take it under advisement. Merrow's Folly out." He set the handset down with great care, the way you set down a thing you'd rather throw.

"Well?" Cass said.

"Well what?"

"What's your professional opinion, navigator? You collect them like stamps."

Reyes folded his hands. He liked being asked. He liked even more taking his time answering, because a rushed opinion was worth exactly what it cost. "Three observations," he said. "One: there is no such exercise area. I have the charts, the notices to mariners, and thirty years of knowing every lie this coastline tells. Two: a real restriction gets enforced once, loudly, with a boarding party if necessary. This one is being *suggested*, repeatedly, gently, by a boat that would rather follow us than stop us. Three—" he tapped the chart, where the patrol boat's track lay penciled alongside their own, neat and patient as a signature— "whoever gave that officer his orders didn't tell him why. You can hear it. He's reading lines he doesn't understand. Somebody above him does understand, and they're not navy."

The cockpit had gone quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"So it's not law," Jude said.

"It's wearing law's coat," Reyes said. "But law doesn't rehearse."

* * *

They ran north all day, and the gray hull ran with them, patient as tide.

Around noon Okafor cooked — the man treated cooking the way he treated engines, with excessive heat and total confidence — and they ate on deck because nobody wanted to go below and miss anything. Tamsin narrated the patrol boat's every visible activity through a pair of binoculars she'd found in a locker and claimed, unconvincingly, were hers.

"He's eating lunch. Now he's standing up. Now he's — okay, now he's pretending he wasn't looking at us."

"Everyone pretends they weren't looking at us," Cass said. "It's our most attractive quality as a group."

Reyes let the banter wash past him and kept his eye on the water and the sky and the slow accumulation of data that was, to him, the actual cargo of any voyage. The wind was backing as forecast. The barometer had dropped six points since dawn, faster than the forecast suggested, and the high clouds to the northwest had developed that bruised, organized look he didn't care for. The weather windows were closing early this year. Everyone said so. Everyone said it the way people say a thing they're tired of saying, and Reyes trusted that kind of repetition more than he trusted forecasts, because it meant the mountains themselves had voted.

And behind them, always, the gray shape, holding two hundred yards, holding its silence.

At 1400 it changed.

The patrol boat slowed, fell back half a mile, and then — deliberately, unmistakably — turned broadside to them and stopped. Showing them its full length. On its aft deck, a figure stood at the rail, and even at that distance Reyes could read the posture: not threatening, not casual. *Waiting.*

"They want to talk," Tamsin said.

"Or they want us to look at them while somebody looks at us," Reyes murmured. He had his binoculars up. The figure at the rail was an officer, junior, young enough that his uniform still fit like it had been issued yesterday. And behind him, half-hidden in the shadow of the deckhouse, stood another man — civilian clothes, wrong build for a sailor, hands clasped behind his back in the stance of someone who had never hauled a line in his life and never intended to.

Reyes passed the glasses to Jude without a word.

She found the man in the shadow and went very still. It lasted perhaps four seconds. Then she lowered the binoculars and her face was smooth again, but Reyes had spent a career reading faces across negotiation tables and interrogation rooms, and he knew a door closing when he saw one.

"You know him," he said. Not a question.

"No," Jude said.

"You know *of* him."

A longer pause. "Maybe. Hard to tell at that distance. Could be anyone in a bad coat."

"It could," Reyes agreed, and let it go, because pressing her was like pressing on a knot in rope — you either knew where the end was or you made it tighter. But he filed it away, in the drawer where he kept everything: the civilian aboard a naval patrol craft, the rehearsed warning, the restriction that existed nowhere on paper. Someone had hired the coat. Someone was inside it.

Cass watched the patrol boat through her own glasses for a long time, and Reyes noticed that she wasn't looking at the officer at all. She was looking at the civilian, and her expression was doing something complicated — recognition and calculation fighting for the same square inch of face.

"Cass," Reyes said mildly.

"Hm?"

"Your turn. You know him."

"I know his *type*," Cass said, lowering the glasses. "Expensive shoes, government boat, hands like a piano teacher. That's not enforcement, that's *liaison*. That's a man whose job title has the word 'relations' in it." She smiled, and it was a good smile, and Reyes noted that it reached her eyes not at all. "Somebody's employer is taking a personal interest."

"Whose employer?" Tamsin asked.

"Darling, if I knew whose employer, I'd know whose money, and if I knew whose money, I'd know whose grave to start digging." Cass said it lightly, breezily, a woman tossing off a joke — and Reyes watched her knuckles whiten on the rail as she turned away, and added that to the drawer too.

* * *

At dusk the patrol boat finally broke off, peeling away south with its running lights coming on one by one, and the relief on deck was immediate and audible — Tamsin whooped, Okafor declared it beer o'clock, Cass vanished below to do whatever it was Cass did with ledgers and grudges.

Reyes stayed at the helm and did not feel relieved.

Because here was the thing he hadn't said aloud, the observation sitting at the bottom of the drawer under everything else: the patrol boat hadn't followed them north all day just to turn around at nightfall. Boats that shadowed you wanted you to know they could. Boats that *left* you, at dusk, on a heading that pointed directly toward the mountain approach — boats like that had already reported where you were going, and didn't need to watch anymore. The watching had moved somewhere ahead of them. Into the valleys. Into the towns. Into whatever waited at the end of the route he'd penciled on the chart.

They weren't being chased. Chasing was for things trying to escape.

They were being *herded* — no. Worse. They were being *scheduled*. Somewhere ashore, a man with expensive shoes had looked at their heading and their speed and written down, in tidy figures, the date and hour at which they would arrive somewhere inconvenient. And everything that happened between now and then would happen according to someone else's arithmetic.

Reyes hated that. Arithmetic was supposed to be his.

Jude came and stood beside him at the wheel as the light died. For a while neither of them spoke, which Reyes found agreeable; she was one of the few people he'd met who understood that silence could be a shared workspace rather than a problem to fill.

"They'll be waiting for us," she said eventually. "On the way in. At the resupply stops. Maybe worse."

"Yes."

"And you think it's the Aldouses."

"I think," Reyes said, "that mining dynasties don't buy patrol boats. They rent influence, and influence comes wearing a uniform and reading lines it doesn't understand." He glanced at her. "You recognized the man in the bad coat."

Jude was quiet long enough that the wheel hummed under his hands, twice, three times.

"There was a man like that," she said at last, "around Kessler Town, when I was a girl. He came to my father's office twice a year. My father called him 'the accountant.' He wore good coats and he never got his hands dirty, and the year after my father died, the Joubert name wasn't on anything anymore." Her voice stayed level, but Reyes heard the load-bearing strain in it, the sound of a beam holding more than it was built for. "If that's who's on that boat, then this isn't about a wreck. It's about making sure certain names stay dead."

"And yours is one of them."

"My brother's was," Jude said, and then she went below without another word, and Reyes stood his watch alone with the stars coming out and the barometer falling, and thought about drawers full of things nobody had said yet.

He woke Cass near midnight. She came up rubbing her eyes, saw his face, and sobered instantly, which he gave her credit for.

"Change the plan," he said.

"How much?"

"Not the route. The timing." He tapped the chart, where his pencil line ran north into the white blankness of the range. "We're on someone's schedule now. So we break it. We come into Kessler Town a day early, quiet, before whoever's arranging our welcome has finished arranging it. We resupply fast and we're gone before the ink dries on their memo."

Cass studied the chart, then him. "You think we can move that fast?"

"I think we can move *unexpectedly*," Reyes said. "Fast is optional. Unexpected is mandatory."

Something flickered across her face — that calculation again, quick and hungry, there and gone. "All right," she said. "One day early. We tell the others at dawn."

She went below. Reyes remained at the wheel, alone with the falling glass and the rising wind, and far off to the north, where the mountains would be if the clouds ever lifted, he imagined a man in a bad coat writing tomorrow's date in a ledger, confident and unhurried, certain the sea would deliver them on time.

"Let's disappoint him," Reyes said to the dark, and put the helm over, and the *Merrow's Folly* leaned north into the night, a day early and a lifetime late, toward the town that had eaten her family's name and the mountains that had swallowed her captain's oath — and somewhere behind them, faint on the horizon, the gray hull's lights winked out one by one, like a man closing a book he'd already finished reading.
