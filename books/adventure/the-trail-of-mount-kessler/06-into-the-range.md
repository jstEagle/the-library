## 6. Into the Range

Tamsin had packed her pack eleven times.

She knew this because she'd counted, the way other people counted sheep. Every reorganization was a small act of faith: if she arranged the gear just so — rope coil at the bottom like a foundation stone, stove nested in the pot like an egg in a cup, the emergency chocolate hidden in the lid pocket where no one would ever think to look, which was the entire point of hiding it from herself — then the mountain would have to respect her. Mountains respected preparation. Her father had told her that, back when he still climbed and she was small enough to ride his shoulders to the mailbox, and she had built her whole life on it the way you build a house on a saying.

Eleven times, and each time the pack came out heavier and more honest than the last. The first version had been all ambition: two ropes, a rack of hardware she mostly knew how to place, a book about altitude she hadn't read yet. By the eleventh it was mostly socks. Socks and humility, which weighed about the same.

They were leaving the coast on a Tuesday, because Reyes said Tuesdays were statistically the calmest crossing days out of Grayharbor, and Reyes said things like that with a straight face until you checked and found he was right, at which point you stopped questioning him forever.

Tamsin stood at the truck bed watching Jude Joubert load gear, and felt the specific joy of a person watching competence happen in real time. Jude handled coils of rope the way other people handled laundry — without ceremony, without looking, hands moving on some private rail line of habit. She never tied a knot where everyone could see her do it. She just turned away for four seconds and turned back and there it was, finished, beautiful, load-bearing.

"Watch this," Okafor said, appearing at Tamsin's elbow with two mugs of something that claimed to be coffee. "Ask her how she tensioned that anchor."

"How did you tension that anchor?"

Jude glanced over. "I didn't."

"You did. I watched you."

"I moved a rock," Jude said, and went back to the truck.

Okafor sipped his terrible coffee with the satisfaction of a man who had won something. "Three weeks," he said. "That's how long it took me to stop asking her questions. You'll get there faster. You've got the advantage of youth."

"The advantage of youth?"

"You still think answers are free."

* * *

The coast fell away behind them by mid-morning, and Tamsin pressed her face to the window like a child even though she was twenty-three and had specifically promised herself not to do that.

She had grown up in the hills south of here — real hills, honest hills, the kind you could walk up before lunch and be proud of after. But the Kessler Range was a different animal entirely. From the coast road it didn't look like geography. It looked like weather that had decided to hold still. The peaks stood up into a sky that seemed to lean on them, white-capped already though it was barely autumn, and between the peaks ran valleys so deep and blue-shadowed they looked carved rather than eroded, as if someone had come through with intent.

"That's the approach?" she asked.

"That's the *view* of the approach," Reyes said from the driver's seat. "The approach is what it does to your knees."

Cass Merrow sat up front with the map open across her knees, though Tamsin had noticed she consulted it less than she consulted the windshield, and the windshield less than some interior ledger only she could see. Cass had a way of looking at mountains that wasn't admiration. It was more like a lawyer sizing up opposing counsel.

"Weather window's closing early this year," Cass said. "Station at the last fuel stop says the jet stream dropped two weeks ahead of pattern."

"Two weeks early." Okafor turned this over. "Mountains don't usually run ahead of schedule."

"This one's making up for lost time," Jude said quietly, from the back.

And there it was — the thing Tamsin had been waiting three days to catch, and caught it now the way you catch a fish you weren't supposed to be fishing for. Because Tamsin had spent the whole coast leg studying Jude Joubert the way seminary students study scripture. She knew the diver's calm, the economy of motion, the way Jude looked at open water with an expression Tamsin could only describe as *home*. She'd assumed the mountains would be more of the same. A woman like this — competent everywhere, afraid of nothing, the person Okafor called "the answer key" when he thought nobody heard —

Jude was afraid of the mountains.

Not the way civilians were afraid, jumping at drop-offs and gasping at exposure. It was quieter than that and worse. It was in the way Jude's hands went still when the range filled the windshield — genuinely still, not relaxed-still but held-still, the stillness of a hand hovering over a stove burner deciding whether to touch. It was in the way she'd answered Cass about the weather, one beat too fast, one degree too flat, a voice doing its job while the person inside it stepped back from the controls.

"You've climbed here before," Tamsin said. It came out as a statement because she couldn't make it a question without sounding like what it was, which was hungry.

"Once," Jude said.

"When?"

"A long time ago."

"What was it like?"

The pause lasted long enough that Okafor stopped pretending to nap. Jude looked out the window at the white shoulders of the range, and Tamsin watched her do something complicated and quick with her face, folding it down into nothing, and said:

"Cold."

Then she got out of the truck at the next pull-off to check the tie-downs, which did not need checking, and stayed out there for six minutes, which was five minutes longer than tie-downs required.

* * *

They camped that night at the treeline's last outpost, a flat shelf of granite above a stream that sounded like applause. Tamsin pitched her tent in record time purely so she could stand around looking available in case anyone needed anything, and was rewarded when Jude waved her over to help sort the climbing rack.

"Tell me what this is," Jude said, holding up a cam.

"A number two cam. You place it in a crack and it—"

"And this?"

"A nut. You wedge it—"

"And you trust your life to these?"

"To the placement," Tamsin said. "The gear's only as good as the rock you put it in."

Jude turned the little aluminum cam over in her fingers, and Tamsin saw her weigh it — not its ounces, something else. "Same with anchors on a wreck," Jude said finally. "The hull doesn't care what your equipment cost. It cares whether you read it right."

"So you *have* done this kind of thing. Vertical work."

"I've done work where being wrong is permanent." Jude handed the cam back. "Different skill. Same math."

It was the most she'd said all day, and Tamsin took it like a gift and unwrapped it slowly all evening by the fire. Same math. The answer key was telling her that diving and climbing shared a grammar, and Tamsin lay in her tent constructing sentences in that grammar until sleep took her mid-clause.

* * *

The second day the road gave out, as roads do when they meet mountains with opinions, and they shouldered packs and started walking.

By noon Tamsin understood that everything she knew about walking was a rumor.

The trail — and *trail* was generous; it was a suggestion the mountain occasionally humored — climbed through pine and then through scrub and then through nothing at all, switchbacking up a face of gray stone with the valley dropping away below in long green folds. Tamsin had trained for this. She'd trained hard, hill repeats with a weighted vest, and she was twenty-three and light and she passed Okafor twice in the first hour and made sure he knew it.

By hour three the mountain had introduced her to a new category of tired, one that lived in the thighs and the lungs simultaneously and also, inexplicably, in the wrists. By hour four she had stopped passing people. By hour five she was drafting behind Reyes like a cyclist, matching his metronome step-step-step because her own rhythm had dissolved somewhere below the clouds and his hadn't.

"Water," Reyes said, without turning around, and handed her his bottle backward over his shoulder like a relay baton. He'd been rationing her without her noticing. She drank and hated how grateful she was and loved it too.

Above them, Jude moved like water finding its level. That was the thing Tamsin kept coming back to, watching her climb: there was no performance in it at all. No bravado, no counting of steps aloud, none of the little ceremonies Tamsin used to keep fear organized. Jude just went, placing her feet with a diver's certainty, reading the rock the way she'd said you read a hull. When the route steepened she didn't speed up or slow down. She was a constant. Tamsin had never seen a human being be a constant before. She wanted to be one when she grew up, and she was starting to worry about the timeline on that.

At the col they stopped, and the world opened.

Below them: the coast, a thin silver seam impossibly far away, the whole ocean they'd left folded flat under haze. Above them: the range proper, peak after peak marching inland, white and blue-shadowed and utterly indifferent. Wind came over the col with nothing in front of it for a hundred miles, and it smelled like nothing — no salt, no pine, no people. Just cold and distance.

Tamsin stood there with her heart hammering and her legs shaking and felt the purest happiness of her life.

Jude stood apart, ten steps on, facing the high country, and did not look happy at all.

Tamsin drifted over, casual, the way you drift toward a fire you're worried about. "You okay?"

"Fine."

"You're very quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a held breath right now."

Jude looked at her then, and Tamsin braced for the deflection, the *cold*, the wall going up. Instead — and this was the part Tamsin would turn over for nights afterward — Jude held her gaze for a moment, and something moved behind her eyes like weather seen through glass. Not weakness. Recognition. The look of someone standing in front of a door they'd once walked through and never walked back out of the same.

"It's beautiful," Jude said. And then, after a breath, in a voice so low Tamsin almost lost it to the wind: "It knows that about itself, too."

Then she shouldered her pack and led on, and Tamsin stood a moment longer at the col, happiness cooling in her chest by degrees, trying to decide what the sentence had meant, and why it had sounded like a warning.

* * *

*

They made the high bench camp by late afternoon, a sheltered hollow below the first glacier, and the cold arrived with them like a fifth crew member who paid no share. Okafor got the stove going with the fussiness of a man feeding a temperamental animal. Reyes circled the site twice, silent, reading wind and drainage, and planted the tents where he wanted them with the mild tyranny of a man who was always right. Cass studied the sky.

"Cloud's building over the western peaks," she said. "Early."

"Everything's early this year," Okafor muttered.

Tamsin helped Jude rig the food hang, working the rope between trees, and found herself talking the way you talk when your hands are busy and your courage is up.

"My dad used to say the mountains don't care if you live or die. That they're not cruel, they're just not interested."

"He half right," Jude said, cinching the haul line. "They're interested. Just not in you. They're interested in gravity, and weather, and time. You're a rounding error." She tested the hang, satisfied. "Best thing you can learn up here is how to be a good rounding error. Small footprint. Clean habits. Don't give the mountain a reason to notice you."

"You sound like you learned that the hard way."

The rope creaked. Somewhere above, ice ticked against stone, a sound like a clock in an empty house.

"Yeah," Jude said. "I did."

And she smiled — a real one, brief and crooked, gone as soon as it came — and Tamsin realized she'd been given something again, another scrap of the map of this woman, and that the scraps so far traced a shape she didn't like. Coast Jude was an answer key. Mountain Jude was a person standing very still in front of something old and large, keeping her voice level so it wouldn't hear her.

* * *

Night came down hard and clear, stars in brutal abundance, temperature falling like it had somewhere to be. Tamsin crawled out of her tent at midnight to pee and found Jude sitting up alone at the edge of camp, not sleeping, not quite awake, watching the glacier catch moonlight.

Tamsin should have gone back to bed. Instead she sat down nearby, close enough for company, far enough for dignity, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"Can't sleep either?" she offered eventually.

"Don't sleep much up high."

"Bad dreams?"

"Worse ones when I don't dream."

Tamsin chewed on that. Around them the mountain made its small night noises — settling stone, wind combing the scree, the glacier groaning somewhere in its sleep like a large dog dreaming. It occurred to Tamsin, sitting there in the cold with her hero ten feet away, that she had signed onto this expedition for the climbing, for the resume line, for the chance to learn from the best. She had not signed on to watch a fearless person be afraid. Nobody warned you that was worse. Fearless people being afraid meant the thing was real.

"Whatever happened to you up here," Tamsin said carefully, "you don't have to tell me."

"No," Jude agreed. "I don't."

"But we're going in anyway."

"We're going in anyway."

"Okay." Tamsin pulled her knees up to her chest. "Then I'm glad it's with you."

Jude looked over at her, moonlit, unreadable, and for a moment Tamsin thought she might say something enormous. Instead she reached out and flicked Tamsin's boot sole, twice, the way you'd tap a hull to check it was sound.

"Get some sleep, kid," she said. "Big day tomorrow. Weather window's closing, and I want the next valley behind us before it does."

Tamsin went back to her tent and lay awake a while, listening to the glacier tick, replaying the boot-tap. Sound, it had said. You'll hold.

In the morning the cloud had thickened overnight into a gray lid pressing down on the western peaks, and the air had a smell to it — iron and snow, the smell of a season changing its mind ahead of schedule. They broke camp fast. And at the edge of the hollow, where the trail bent down toward the long valley that led inland, Tamsin stopped.

Because there, faint in the frost beside the path, was a print. A boot print. Fresh — the edges still crisp, hours old at most — and pointing the same direction they were headed.

Someone was on the trail ahead of them.

Reyes crouched over it without a word, touched the edge, and looked up at the gray sky with an expression Tamsin had learned to translate: he was recalculating everything.
