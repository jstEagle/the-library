## 7. Kessler Town

The road into Kessler Town was the kind of road that didn't trust you. It narrowed twice without warning, crossed a creek on a bridge that groaned like an old man standing up, and then climbed through a cut in the hills as if it were sorry to let you in at all.

Jude drove the last stretch while Okafor navigated from the passenger seat with a map he kept folding wrong and Tamsin hung halfway out the back window like a dog.

"Is that smoke?" Tamsin asked, pointing.

"That's the smelter," Okafor said. "Company runs it day and night. You can set your watch by the shift whistle."

"You can't," Reyes said mildly from the back seat. "The whistle's been off by eleven minutes since spring."

Jude said nothing. She was watching the valley open up ahead of them — the long green floor of it, the river stitching through, the town sitting in the bend like something placed there by a hand that hadn't cared whether it fit. Smokestacks. Tailings piles pale as scars. A church steeple, white and new-looking, rising above rows of company houses all painted the same tired gray-blue.

She had seen photographs of this valley her whole childhood. Her mother had kept one on the dresser, a black-and-white print of the Joubert house with its wraparound porch and its absurd number of chimneys, taken back when the family had still believed the mountain belonged to them because they'd found it first.

Finding a thing first, Jude had learned by seventeen, means nothing. It only means you're there when someone stronger arrives.

"There," Okafor said, tapping the windshield. "That rise, past the tailings. That'd have been your people's ground."

"It was." Jude took the turn toward town harder than she meant to. "Everything on the north side of the river. The house stood where the assay office is now."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Tamsin, who had the particular gift of the young for saying exactly the wrong right thing, asked: "What happened to the house?"

"They burned it." Jude heard herself say it flatly, the way you say things you've said so many times they've worn smooth. "Officially it was a boiler accident. Unofficially my grandmother died in it, and the Aldouses bought the land from the estate before the ashes were cold."

Tamsin went quiet. Even Okafor stopped fidgeting with his map.

* * *

Kessler Town announced its ownership before you were two blocks in. The first sign read ALDOUS CONSOLIDATED — WELCOME, which was a strange thing for a mining company to feel entitled to say. The second sign, smaller, nailed to a post outside the general store, read NO CREDIT EXCEPT IN SCRIP, and beneath that, in smaller letters somebody had tried to scratch out: THE COMPANY PROVIDES.

The store itself was clean and well-stocked and quietly menacing, the way a well-run prison is quiet. Shelves of flour and tinned peaches and wool socks. A counter where a clerk with a company pin on his lapel weighed everything twice and smiled at no one. And behind him, framed on the wall like a portrait of a saint, a photograph of old Marcus Aldous himself — jaw like a shovel blade, eyes that looked at the camera the way a man looks at property he intends to keep.

"Morning," Cass said to the clerk, laying their supply list down. "We'll need hardtack, lamp oil, rope — good rope, not the shelf stuff — and whatever dried meat isn't a rumor."

The clerk looked at the list, then at the four of them, then at Cass again, longer. "You're the salvage outfit," he said. Not a question. "Came up the coast road."

"News travels fast."

"Company news travels fastest." He began pulling items without being asked, which should have been efficient and instead felt like being handled. "You'll be settling in scrip."

"Scrip?"

"Company money." He produced a small paper note, gray-green, with Aldous Consolidated printed across the top where a nation's name might go. "Good at any company store in the valley. Better than cash, honestly. Cash attracts thieves."

"Does it," Cass said.

"Out here it does." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd mention the weather.

Jude watched Cass take the scrip, watched her count it, watched her smile the smile she used when she was filing something away for later use against somebody. Jude had known that smile for fifteen years and it had never once meant Cass was happy.

Outside, Tamsin was reading the notice board by the door with the fascination of someone encountering a new species. POSTED BY ORDER OF SHERIFF DULAC: CURFEW AT TEN. NO ASSEMBLY OF MORE THAN SIX PERSONS ON COMPANY GROUND. NOTICE IS HEREBY GIVEN THAT THE EAST RIDGE ROAD IS CLOSED FOR REPAIRS UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.

"The east ridge road is the one we need," Tamsin said.

"It's been closed for repairs," Okafor said, "every year I've come through here, for nine years."

"Maybe they're very thorough."

"Maybe," Reyes said, "they don't want anyone walking east."

* * *

They split up to resupply faster — Cass and Okafor to argue with the clerk about rope, Reyes to find a farrier for the mule, Tamsin dragging Jude along on the pretext of needing someone tall to reach a shelf in the chandlery.

The pretext lasted about forty seconds. Then Tamsin abandoned the shelves entirely and fell into step beside Jude like a duckling with a climbing harness.

"I want to see the church," Tamsin said.

"Why?"

"Because it's new. Everything else here is old or falling down, but the steeple's got fresh paint. That tells you what they care about."

Jude had to admit the kid wasn't wrong. They walked up Company Row — the houses marching along in identical ranks, each with its little fenced yard, each fence the same height, each gate the same latch — toward the white building at the end of the street. Along the way Tamsin waved at a woman hanging laundry, and the woman looked at Tamsin, then quickly at the street corners, then went inside.

"She didn't finish her washing," Tamsin observed.

"No," Jude said. "She didn't."

The Church of the Provident Vein — Jude remembered the name from childhood, remembered her mother saying it with a laugh that had no humor in it — stood at the head of the row like a teacher watching a classroom. Fresh clapboard. New bell. A sign listing service times, and beneath the times, in letters of equal size as though it were scripture itself: ATTENDANCE RECORDED.

"Recorded by who?" Tamsin asked.

"By whoever reads the record," Jude said.

Inside, it smelled of pine shavings and floor wax. Empty pews in perfect rows. At the front, where another church might have put a cross, there was instead a large carved relief of the mountain itself — Mount Kessler rendered in blond wood, idealized, its dangerous faces smoothed away, with a vein of gold leaf running through it like a blessing.

Beneath the mountain, carved into the altar rail: THE VEIN PROVIDES. THE FAITHFUL ENDURE.

Tamsin stood in the aisle a long moment. "It's a church shaped like a payroll office," she said finally.

"It's a payroll office shaped like a church," Jude said. "My great-grandfather helped build the first one on this spot. Before the Aldouses. It was a different kind of church then. This one went up eight years ago. Marcus Aldous donated the timber himself."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I grew up on stories about this valley the way other kids grow up on fairy tales." Jude ran her thumb along a pew back, feeling the varnish, factory-smooth, nothing in it handmade. "Except in ours, the dragon won."

* * *

They came out of the church into a street gone suddenly busy — shift change, the ten o'clock whistle finally blowing its eleven-minutes-late announcement. Men came up out of the pithead in files, gray-faced, blinking at daylight like creatures raised underground, which in a sense they were. They moved with the economy of the exhausted. None of them spoke. A few nodded to the sheriff's deputy posted at the corner, and the deputy nodded back, and the nod had the texture of a transaction.

Jude stood at the edge of the flow and let it move around her, and something in her chest did the thing it always did in this valley — a slow tightening, grief and anger braided so tight she couldn't always tell which strand was which.

There had been Jouberts in this valley for four generations. Jouberts who found the seam. Jouberts who built the first stamp mill. Joubert money had paid for the schoolhouse before there was a company to name it. And now the name existed nowhere in town except — she checked, because some part of her needed to know — nowhere. Not on a street sign, not on a plaque, not in the church's donor board. The Aldous name was on everything: the store, the bank, the boarding house, the water tower, the small brass plate on every lamppost reading ALDOUS CONSOLIDATED — LIGHT PROVIDED.

Her family hadn't just been ruined. They'd been uninstalled.

"Hey." Tamsin's voice, quieter than usual. "You've got your diving face on."

"My what?"

"The face you make when you're under. Cass does it too. Like you're somewhere else and the somewhere else is heavy." Tamsin shifted her pack. "You okay?"

Jude considered lying, considered deflection, and then — because Tamsin was twenty-three and earnest and had a way of looking at you that made lying feel like kicking a dog — told a piece of the truth.

"When I was a girl, this street had a festival every fall. Harvest thing. My mother ran the pie table. There was music, actual music, people played fiddles badly and everyone danced anyway." She looked down the row of identical houses, identical gates, identical latches. "Now there's a curfew and a whistle and a man on the corner counting heads. That's what was taken. Not the mine. Not even the money. The part where people belong to themselves."

Tamsin was silent for a second, and then she said, with the blunt clarity that was either her worst habit or her best one: "So we take it back."

"We're taking a box," Jude said.

"Yeah, but—" Tamsin gestured at the whole gray street, the smoke, the counted heads. "Whatever's in that box, it scares these people enough to close roads and send fixers. That seems worth taking back too."

Jude didn't answer. But she filed it, the way Cass filed things, and hated noticing that she'd learned the habit from her.

* * *

They regrouped at the boarding house at noon, and that was where the day turned.

The boarding house keeper was a wide, careful woman named Mrs. Ansel who served stew and asked no questions, which in Kessler Town was practically a declaration of independence. She set five bowls down — five, though they were four — and when Reyes raised an eyebrow she said only, "For the mule," deadpan, and Jude decided on the spot that Mrs. Ansel was the bravest person in the valley.

They were halfway through the stew when the sheriff walked in.

He was not what Jude expected. She'd pictured the company sheriff as a thug with a badge — some cousin of the pit bosses with knuckles like ore. Instead Sheriff Dulac was a thin, neat, gray man with spectacles and the manner of a schoolmaster, and he pulled up a chair to their table uninvited and sat down in it like he'd been invited years ago.

"Travelers," he said pleasantly. "Welcome to Kessler Town."

"Sheriff," Cass said, matching his pleasantness note for note.

"I understand you're bound over the pass. Recreational party, is it?"

"Salvage survey," Cass said. "Historical interest."

"Mm." Dulac folded his hands. "The east ridge road is closed."

"We'd heard. Repairs?"

"Long-running project." He smiled thinly. "I mention it because hikers sometimes get confused, and the weather this year is turning early. The company would hate to read about any of you in the spring thaw." He paused, and his eyes — mild, myopic, utterly unfooled — moved across each of them and settled, briefly, on Jude. "You have the look of a local, miss."

"I used to be," Jude said.

"Used to be." He tasted the phrase. "Kessler has a way of keeping what it takes and losing what it gives. Family name?"

"Joubert."

Something flickered behind the spectacles — not recognition exactly. Recalculation. "Old family," he said. "The old families mostly moved on."

"The old families were mostly moved on," Jude said, "is how I'd put it."

Dulac held her gaze for a moment longer than courtesy required, and then he stood, straightened his coat, and thanked them for their time as if they'd given him any. At the door he paused. "One more thing. A gentleman's been asking after your party. Out-of-towner. Well-dressed. Goes by Pike." He adjusted his glasses. "If he finds you, do try to stay polite. He takes impoliteness personally."

And then he was gone, and the boarding house was very quiet, and Mrs. Ansel refilled their bowls without being asked, which was her way of saying she'd heard everything and would forget all of it.

"Well," Okafor said. "That was ominous."

"He knows the name Joubert," Reyes said, watching the door.

"Everyone old enough to matter knows the name Joubert," Jude said. "They just spent sixty years making sure nobody says it out loud."

Cass was smiling her filing-away smile. "Pike's already here. Ahead of us. Which means he didn't follow us up the coast road — he knew our route before we left harbor."

The words landed on the table like a dropped tool. Jude looked at Cass, and Cass looked steadily back, face smooth, and for one cold second Jude thought of the navy patrol boat in the fog, the officer with his rehearsed warning, the sense of being hunted before they'd begun — and wondered, not for the first time and not for the last, how the hunter had gotten their heading.

But that was a question for later, on higher ground, with fewer ears. For now there was stew, and a mule to shoe, and a closed road to get around.

"We leave at first light," Jude said. "East ridge, around the closure, through the timber. Tamsin — you scouted a route?"

The kid lit up like a lamp. "There's an old drover track above the tailings. My guidebook calls it impassable. My guidebook also thinks switchbacks are a personality flaw. We can be over the shoulder of the ridge by tomorrow night."

"Then we sleep," Cass said, "and we leave before the town wakes up."

"And if Pike's watching the road?" Okafor asked.

"Then we aren't on the road," Jude said.

* * *

She took the last hour of light for herself. It was a habit she'd never managed to break — walking a town alone before leaving it, learning it with her feet the way she learned a wreck with her hands.

She walked up past the church, past the lampposts with their little brass plates, up the hill behind Company Row where the ground roughened into scrub and the town's neat geometry fell apart. From up there she could see the whole valley laid out: the smoke, the rows, the river, the tailings pale in the evening light like a glacier of waste. And on the north side of the river, the rise Okafor had pointed out, where the assay office sat on the footprint of her family's house.

She could almost place the porch. She could almost place the chimneys. Fifteen years was long enough to lose even the shape of a thing you burned to keep.

Somewhere below, the shift whistle blew again — early this time, or late; Reyes would know — and the men came up out of the dark of the pithead into the dark of the evening, and the lamps along Company Row came on all at once, provided, recorded, owned.

Jude stood in the scrub with the wind coming down off the mountain and let the grief finish sharpening itself into the thing it had been becoming all day. Not sorrow. Sorrow was for things that were over. This was the other thing — the live wire, the current, the cold clear knowledge of exactly what had been taken and exactly who held it and exactly how long they'd held it.

Her brother had come back to this valley to dig up the proof. Her brother had died in these mountains, or near enough, with a map scratched into a bulkhead and a strongbox hidden in the ice, and the dynasty's grandfather had put him in that grave, and the captain she'd loved like a mother had helped bury the truth beside him.

And tomorrow she was going to walk east on a closed road, into the range that had taken him, and finish what he started.

Not for leverage. Whatever Cass wanted the box for, that was Cass's hunger, and Jude understood hunger better than most. But hers had changed shape somewhere between the church and the hillside. It wasn't power she wanted anymore. It was the name. Just the name, spoken in daylight, in the valley, true.

She turned back down toward the boarding house, where her crew was arguing amiably about rope coils through the lit window, and Mrs. Ansel's stew was still warm, and for one strange moment the town her family had lost felt less like a wound and more like a destination.

Behind her, at the edge of the lamplight, a well-dressed man stepped out of the shadow of the assay office and stood a moment, looking up the hill at her, unhurried, as if he had all the time in the world and intended to spend it on her.

Jude didn't run. She just noted the direction of the wind, and started planning how to be gone before he finished his dinner.
