## 8. The Fixer

The Kessler Town hotel had a dining room that tried very hard to be somewhere else. Chandeliers shipped up the valley road in pieces and reassembled by men who had never seen one lit. Wallpaper printed with a pattern of birds that did not exist within four hundred miles of the range. A piano nobody played, tuned twice a year out of principle.

Cass Merrow appreciated the effort. She'd spent enough years in rooms like this to know they were never really about comfort. They were about message: *we have money, we have time, and we are not going anywhere.* The Aldouses had built the hotel the same decade they'd built the church and the sheriff's office, and all three buildings said the same thing in different fonts.

She arrived early because she always arrived early. If you walked into a negotiation second, you were already negotiating from behind. So she sat at the corner table with her back to the wall, ordered a glass of the local wine — which tasted like a rumor of grapes — and watched the room fill through the long windows while she arranged her face into something pleasant and unreadable.

The crew was upstairs. She'd told them to stay there. Okafor had wanted to come down "for moral support," which Cass understood to mean *to argue with me in front of witnesses*, and Tamsin had wanted to come down for reasons Tamsin would not articulate but which clearly involved wanting to see what a dynasty fixer looked like up close. Reyes had said nothing, which was his way of agreeing with whoever was right.

"You'll do fine," Jude had said, which was the closest thing to kindness the woman could produce without visible strain. Then she'd added, "Don't sign anything," which was closer to love.

Cass hadn't signed anything in eleven years. Not a lease, not a contract, not a marriage license when a man in Anchorage had asked nicely. Paper was how the Aldouses held things. She knew that better than anyone at this table was ever going to know.

Edran Pike came in at seven o'clock exactly, which told her he'd been waiting outside for ten minutes so as not to arrive early. He was a compact man in a gray suit that probably cost more than Reyes's boat, with a face like a man who had been handsome once and had decided against it. He carried no briefcase. Fixers who carried briefcases were amateurs; everything important fit in a head.

"Miss Merrow." He sat without being invited, which was its own kind of opening move. "Thank you for making time."

"Mr. Pike." She smiled the smile she used on harbormasters. "I'd say it's a pleasure, but we haven't started yet."

He laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised, and Cass filed that away. Men who laughed genuinely at small jokes were either confident or nervous, and she'd spend the evening finding out which.

* * *

The waiter came and Pike ordered for both of them without consulting her, which should have been insulting and instead was almost impressive. He ordered the lamb, two of them, and told the waiter the wine list was a tragedy and brought the bottle he'd evidently brought himself, because of course he had.

"My wife thinks I'm in Fairbanks," he said, pouring. "I tell her these trips are boring. Mostly they are."

"Do you often buy people out over dinner?"

"Often enough that I've stopped calling it that." He lifted his glass. "I call it *clarifying options*. Everyone leaves my dinners knowing exactly where they stand. It's a service, really. Most people go their whole lives without anyone telling them the truth."

"And the truth tonight is?"

"That you're sitting on something the family wants, and the family pays well for patience, better for discretion, and best of all for delivery."

Cass sipped the wine. It was excellent, which annoyed her. "You're assuming we found something."

"I'm assuming you went to a great deal of trouble and expense to cross a mountain range ahead of the snows," Pike said mildly, "and that nobody does that for scenery. Miss Merrow. You've been in salvage your whole adult life. You don't climb glaciers for fun. Well—" he paused, and something flickered behind his eyes, "—perhaps you do. We'll get to that."

The lamb arrived. It was also excellent. Cass cut into it and kept her expression pleasant while her stomach performed a slow, cold roll that had nothing to do with altitude.

*We'll get to that.* Nobody said *we'll get to that* unless they had a file.

* * *

He started gently, the way a surgeon starts with the skin.

"Your engineer. Okafor, Adaeze — no, wait." He closed his eyes briefly, retrieving it. "Emeka Okafor. Forgive me, I mix up first names. Emeka. Trained at the polytechnic in Lagos, worked the deep rigs off Bonny until the incident with the pressure valve. There was a settlement. Confidential. The company paid generously and he agreed to leave the industry, and instead he's been patching together engines for expeditions like yours ever since, under names that aren't quite his." Pike spread his hands apologetically. "None of this is a threat. I simply want you to understand that I've done my reading. If the family needed to make trouble for him — visa complications, an old employer with a grievance and a lawyer — it would be trivially easy. But we're not here to make trouble. We're here to prevent it."

Cass chewed. Swallowed. Kept her voice level. "You've been busy."

"It's my job to be busy." He refilled her glass unasked. "Your navigator, Reyes. Silas Reyes. Twenty-two years running supply routes the navy pretends not to notice. He's never been caught, which is remarkable, and the reason he's never been caught is that for six of those years he was carrying cargo for a man named Ferro Delacruz, whose operation the family bought out quietly in '09. There are ledgers. Mr. Reyes's name appears in three of them." A small shrug. "Again — history, not leverage. Unless it needs to become leverage."

"And the girl?" Cass asked, before she could stop herself, and hated how fast the question came out, because it showed him exactly where the soft place was.

"Tamsin Cole. Nineteen. Ran away from a foster placement in Kelowna at sixteen, crossed the divide alone in spring melt — genuinely impressive, by the way, the rangers still talk about it — and has been climbing other people's mountains ever since. No debts. No secrets worth buying." Pike smiled, and it was almost warm. "Some people are simply clean. It's refreshing. Though I'll note she idolizes your diver, and your diver is the reason we're having this dinner at all."

There it was. The scalpel coming out of the tray.

"Jude Joubert," Pike said, and pronounced it carefully, like a man who had rehearsed. "Fifteen years ago she left the valley overnight and never came back. Do you know why?"

"I know she doesn't talk about it."

"Of course she doesn't." He tore a piece of bread, unhurried. "Here's what's interesting, Miss Merrow. Neither does anyone else. A seventeen-year-old girl disappears the same week her brother disappears into the high country, and the town simply... agrees not to discuss it. That kind of silence isn't natural. Silence is purchased. Someone paid for it, or someone frightened enough people that payment wasn't necessary." He looked at her across the candlelight. "You've known her longer than I have. Has she ever told you what happened?"

"No," Cass said.

"Has she ever told you what she *did*?"

The word landed on the table between them like a dropped knife.

Cass set down her fork. She was aware, distantly, of the chandelier doing its best, of the nonexistent birds on the wallpaper, of the piano waiting for hands that would never come. And underneath all of it, cold and clear, the thought she'd been refusing to think since the dock:

*He knows more than he should.*

Not more than a fixer should. More than a fixer *could*. The Okafor material — fine, that was records, that was money talking to money. The Reyes ledgers — that was old cargo paper, findable if you knew where to dig. But the shape of the thing with Jude, the *silence is purchased* line — that wasn't research. That was memory. That was someone who had been in the valley fifteen years ago, close enough to the fire to feel the heat.

"How old are you, Mr. Pike?" she asked lightly.

"Forty-six."

Which made him twenty-nine or thirty the summer the Joubert children went into the mountains. Old enough. Working for somebody, even then.

"You were with the family already," she said.

"I was driving trucks." He said it easily, and she believed him, and the ease itself was the warning. Men lied elaborately about important things and truthfully about small ones. "I hauled ore concentrate down the valley road. I remember that summer. Bad year for the company. Bad year generally." He dabbed his mouth with the napkin. "But we're getting away from the purpose of the evening."

"Are we? It feels like the purpose of the evening."

He laughed again — that real laugh — and then set it aside like a coat. "Fair. Let me be direct, since you appreciate it. The family believes your expedition is attempting to recover certain documents from the wreck of the Kessler Star. Documents concerning the founding of the company. The family's position is simple: whatever those papers say, they are historical curiosities at best and forged provocations at worst, and either way their circulation would injure hundreds of innocent employees to gratify a handful of grievances. The family is prepared to pay for their recovery and destruction. Generously. To you personally, Miss Merrow — not to the crew, not to Ms. Joubert. You negotiated the contract. You hold the route. You are, if I may say, the reasonable one on this expedition."

*The reasonable one.* Cass turned the phrase over slowly, tasting it. It was flattery wearing a wire.

"And if I'm not reasonable?"

"Then the family becomes unreasonable, and I promise you nobody enjoys that, including the family." He leaned back. "You'd be amazed how much grief can be prevented by one sensible person in the right room. I've built a career on it."

* * *

Dessert came — some dark cake dense as ballast — and Cass ate it because refusing food at a negotiation was a tell, and she was not going to give this man a single tell he hadn't earned.

She ran the arithmetic while she chewed. Pike had named every debt on her crew except hers. He'd circled Jude like a man circling a locked door, testing hinges. And he had said *you hold the route* — which was true, and which meant he'd read her contract with the outfitter, or heard it described, which meant someone in Kessler Town talked, which meant half the town talked, which she'd already assumed.

But here was the thing that kept snagging, the splinter under the skin: he hadn't named *her* price. Every fixer she'd ever met led with the target's own weakness. Your debts, your secrets, your sister in the hospital, your ex-wife's lawyer. Pike had laid out everyone else's ledger like a sommelier presenting vintages and then skipped straight past his host.

Unless he didn't need to. Unless he considered her an open book.

Or unless he was saving it. Holding the last card the way she herself held cards, playing the meal long, letting her sweat through dessert and coffee to see what she'd offer unprompted. That was her move. That was *exactly* her move, and watching a man run it on her was like hearing her own voice on a recording — uncanny and faintly nauseating.

"Tell me about yourself, Mr. Pike," she said, because attack was sometimes the politest form of defense. "How does a truck driver become the family's... clarifier of options?"

"Slowly, and by being useful at inconvenient moments." He turned his coffee cup without drinking. "I'm not an interesting man, Miss Merrow. I'm a mirror with good manners. I reflect what people actually want, and most people are grateful, briefly, to see it." He glanced up. "Though since you ask — my mother worked the Aldous house. Cook. Thirty-one years. I grew up in the kitchen behind the big house on Company Hill, listening to conversations through the door that a boy shouldn't hear." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "So yes. I was in the valley that summer. I was fourteen."

Cass's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Fourteen. Not twenty-nine. She'd done the math wrong — deliberately wrong, she realized, because she'd wanted him to be a peer, a contemporary, a man she could read by her own rules. Fourteen-year-olds didn't drive trucks. Fourteen-year-old boys in kitchens behind big houses heard everything and forgot nothing.

"You were a child," she said carefully, "in that house. The summer the Joubert boy went into the range."

"I was a child in that house for many summers." His voice stayed smooth as poured cream, but something moved beneath it, the way something moves under ice. "I heard a great deal over the years. Some of it I understood late. Some of it I understood immediately and wished I hadn't." He set the cup down. "Which brings us, at last, to you."

And there it was. The card, finally, face-up on the felt.

"Miss Merrow. Merrow." He said the name like he was checking it against something. "It isn't the name you were born with, is it."

The dining room went very quiet around her, or perhaps it only seemed to. The chandelier hummed. Somewhere below, the piano waited.

"No," Cass said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It isn't."

"I didn't think so." Pike folded his napkin into a precise square, a man closing a meeting he considered concluded. "I'm not going to ask what it is. Not tonight. Tonight I only want you to sit with the knowledge that I could ask. That I likely already know. And that when the family's offer expires — and offers from this family do expire, Miss Merrow, usually with the snowline — I will make a second one, privately, to you alone, and it will be shaped around whatever it is you've been running from for the last twenty years." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and smiled down at her with genuine, terrible warmth. "Think about which of us the past has a firmer grip on. You might be surprised by the answer."

He left cash on the table — too much cash, a flourish — and walked out through the lobby like a man leaving a church he didn't believe in.

* * *

Cass sat alone for a long time after he was gone, watching the wax pool in the candle, doing the thing she did instead of panicking, which was planning.

Upstairs, she knocked on Jude's door first. Jude opened it with wet hair and an expression like a drawn bolt, and behind her Tamsin was cross-legged on the bed eating crackers, and Okafor was oiling something that did not need oiling, and Reyes stood by the window saying nothing in his customary way.

"Well?" Jude said.

"He knows everything," Cass said. "About all of us. Except—" she paused, and chose the lie the way she chose rope, testing the load first, "—except what's in the box. He doesn't know what's in the box. He only knows we're looking."

Jude studied her for a moment, the way she studied everything, like a knot she intended to undo. "And what did he offer *you*?"

"Money. Separately. Privately. After the snows close."

"And what did he say about you?"

Cass hesitated. There was a true answer available and a safe answer and they were not the same answer, and for one long breath she stood in the doorway weighing them like a woman at a tiller in weather.

"He said I wasn't using my real name," she said finally, "and that he'd make me a second offer shaped around whatever I'm running from." She shrugged, casual as a card trick. "Which is nothing. I changed my name because my father was a drunk and I liked mine better. He's fishing."

Jude nodded slowly, and Cass couldn't tell whether the nod meant *I believe you* or *I believe that you believe I believe you*, and that uncertainty — that thin, humming wire of doubt — was the most dangerous thing Edran Pike had left on the table.

Because he *did* know. She was nearly sure of it now. And somewhere south of the snowline, in a gray suit with no briefcase, a man who had grown up listening through kitchen doors was holding the one card Cass Merrow had spent twenty years making sure nobody dealt.

The snows were coming early this year. Pike had said so, almost kindly.

Offers from this family expire with the snowline.

Cass looked at the box strapped under her bunk, and for the first time since the dock, she wondered whether she was carrying it — or it was carrying her.
