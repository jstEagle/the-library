## 9. The Wreck Above the Snowline

The Kessler Star should not have been there.

That was the first thing Jude thought when they came over the shoulder of the moraine and saw it — a ship's hull, black and barnacled, sitting on a shelf of blue ice two thousand meters above the sea like a whale that had wandered inland and died of embarrassment. She had spent half her life inside wrecks, and every one of them had at least had the decency to be wet.

"There she is," Cass said, and her voice had the particular hush people reserve for churches and shipwrecks.

"She's out of water," Okafor said flatly, as if this were a problem he intended to take personally. He was already doing it — taking it personally, that is. Jude could see it in the set of his jaw, the way his eyes went over the hull the way other men looked at an insult. "She's *dry*. Do you understand what that does to my systems? Forty years of freeze-thaw cycling. Her frames will be sugar."

"Her frames were sugar before we got here," Tamsin said cheerfully. "Look at her. She's been through more weather than a scarecrow."

Reyes said nothing, which was how Reyes said most things, but he marked their position on the map with a small precise X, and Jude noticed he drew the X slightly off from where the wreck actually sat. Navigators' superstition. You never put the true location where anyone else might read it. She found herself liking him a little more for it.

They made camp in the lee of the hull itself, which was either prudent or sacrilegious depending on who you asked, and by evening the wind had come up off the icefield with that thin whistling note that meant it intended to stay a while. Jude stood outside long enough to look at the wreck properly.

The Kessler Star had been a coastal freighter once, three hundred tons of her, and someone — decades ago, in weather nobody had bothered recording — had driven her up here. Not run her aground. *Driven* her. The bow was pointed uphill. The rudder was hard to starboard. Whatever her last captain had been trying to do, he had done it with intent, and the intent had failed, and the mountain had kept the evidence ever since.

Jude knew the story everyone told. Storm surge, freak wave, a ship lifted bodily and set down on the shelf like a toy on a mantel. She'd heard it in dockside bars her whole childhood, always from men who'd had enough to drink to make the physics work. But standing under the hull now, running her hand along plates still furred with salt crystals that had no business existing at this altitude, she felt the old story wobble. Salt didn't climb mountains. Ships didn't surf onto glaciers. Somebody had brought the Kessler Star here, or somebody had needed her here, and neither possibility said anything kind about the people involved.

"You're staring at her like she owes you money," Tamsin said, appearing beside him with two mugs of something hot and terrible.

"She might," Jude said. "Everything up here might."

Tamsin laughed, and handed her the mug anyway.

* * *

Inside the hull, the next morning, the cold was a different animal. Outside, the wind screamed and you fought it. Inside, everything was silent, and the silence was worse, because silence meant the cold wasn't fighting you — it was just waiting, patient as arithmetic, for you to stop moving.

Jude had rigged her lines the night before, anchored into the old cargo rings, and she went down into the hold the way she went down into any wreck: slow, hands first, reading the structure before trusting it. Okafor had wanted to come, but his knee had gone bad on the approach — "forty years of telling other people to respect load limits," Cass had said sweetly, "and none of them were his" — so it was Jude alone in the dark, with Tamsin topside on the belay, chattering down the rope like a sports announcer.

"Depth twelve meters, holding steady, air's fine, your hair looks great—"

"Tamsin."

"Sorry. Habit. Reyes says I'm not allowed to talk during actual dives so I practice on the dry ones."

Jude laughed into her mask, which fogged immediately, which served her right.

The hold was a cathedral of rot. Cargo battens hung like ribs. Her lamp found the ghost of painted lettering on a bulkhead — *KESSLER STAR, PORT OF* — and then rust, and then nothing. She moved aft, toward the lazarette, because that was where a strongbox would live if whoever sealed it had any sense. Small space, awkward access, one way in. A smuggler's instinct or a coward's; with the Aldous fortune involved, the two were probably the same thing.

The compartment hatch was there. Sealed. Not rusted shut — *sealed*, deliberately, with lead sheeting hammered over the frame and what looked like wax worked into every seam. Someone had wanted this closed against time itself.

Jude's heart did something complicated.

She'd opened a hundred compartments in a hundred wrecks and never once felt anything about it beyond professional satisfaction. This was different. This was the reason she'd signed Cass's contract, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before saying so. Somewhere behind that lead was a box with her family's name in it. Fifteen years of miles, all of them pointed here, whether she'd admitted it or not.

She worked the seal with a pry bar and a patience she usually reserved for knots. The lead gave in flakes. The wax crumbled. And then the hatch swung inward with a groan like an old man getting out of a chair, and her lamp swept the compartment, and —

Empty.

Not damaged-empty. Not looted-empty. Empty the way a room is empty when someone has left it *on purpose*, folded things, taken what mattered, closed the door behind them. No box. No dust drifts disturbed in forty years except one clean channel across the floor plate, wide enough for a chest, narrow enough for a man carrying one.

And scratched into the inside of the hatch — scratched from *within*, which meant someone had sealed themselves in with the box and then gotten out again, or sealed the box in after climbing out, and either way had paused, in the dark, with a tool in hand, to write:

**NOT WHERE YOU THINK. ASK THE MOUNTAIN. — E.J.J.**

Elias Joubert Joubert. Her brother's initials. Her brother's handwriting — she'd know it anywhere, that heavy-handed schoolboy scrawl he'd never grown out of, the letters leaning like they were tired. She'd seen it last on a note pinned to the kitchen door fifteen years ago: *Gone up. Don't wait up.* Except she had waited up. She'd waited up for years, in a way, without ever calling it that.

Jude sat down inside the compartment, in the dark, in the cold, and let the vertigo come.

It came the way it always did at altitude — not as fear exactly, but as a sudden honest accounting. Here she was, sealed inside a steel box on a glacier, reading a message written by a dead man who had been seventeen years older than her and had known, somehow, that someone would come looking. Had *counted* on it. Had left a map to himself in the only place guaranteed to be found by exactly one kind of person: a diver, a Joubert, someone who'd open a sealed hatch rather than report it.

He'd built a lock only she could pick. From beyond his own disappearance.

"Jude?" Tamsin's voice, tinny down the line. "You've been still for four minutes. That's either very good or very bad."

"It's very good," Jude said, and discovered her voice had gone strange. "I found something."

"What?"

"A map."

"To the box?"

"No." She ran her thumb over the scratches one more time, memorizing them, though memorizing was unnecessary — she could have drawn those letters blindfolded, drunk, asleep. They were carved somewhere deeper than skin. "To Elias."

* * *

Topside, she showed them, because showing them was easier than explaining, and explaining was easier than feeling, and she was saving her feeling for later, when there was a wall between her and the wind.

Cass read the scratched letters twice and said nothing for a long moment, which from Cass was practically a eulogy. Then: "Your brother was in this hull."

"He sealed the compartment. He carried the box out. He wrote that knowing — " Jude stopped. Started again. "Knowing whoever found it would be looking for the box, and would need to be pointed past it."

"Ask the mountain," Okafor repeated. He'd limped over despite the knee, and he was studying the hatch frame with his engineer's squint. "That's not poetry. That's instructions. There'll be a cairn, or a cut mark, or a cache marker — mountaineers leave signs the way sailors do. Your brother learned the language."

"When?" Tamsin asked. "I mean — the wreck's been up here forever, right? But when was *he* here?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Jude turned it over while the others argued about dates and weather records and whether the navy's charts showed any survey party in these years. Because the timeline everyone assumed — Elias vanishing into the range the same season Captain Voss forbade the Kessler job, presumed lost within weeks — had just developed a hole in it big enough to drive a freighter through. If Elias had reached the wreck, emptied it, and left directions, he hadn't died quickly. He'd survived. He'd planned. He'd expected pursuit, or at least expected *her*, and had laid a trail accordingly.

A trail that had waited fifteen years for someone stubborn enough to follow it.

"You okay?" Tamsin asked quietly, when the others had drifted back to camp. The kid had a gift for asking the right question at the wrong-seeming moment.

"I'm standing on a glacier reading mail from my brother," Jude said. "Define okay."

"That bad, huh."

"That good, actually. That's the problem." Jude looked west, where the range climbed white and featureless into cloud, and felt the pull of it like a current. "Fifteen years I told myself he was gone. Gone was simple. Gone didn't need answers. Now there's a trail, and trails are — " she rubbed her nose, which was going red in the wind — "trails are promises. He promised me he went further. So I go further."

Tamsin nodded solemnly, then ruined it completely by grinning. "For what it's worth, I think it's the coolest thing I've ever seen. A secret message inside a haunted ship on top of a mountain. If you don't follow it I'm going to follow it myself and get eaten by something."

"There's nothing up here to eat you."

"The mountain counts."

"The mountain counts," Jude agreed, and surprised herself by smiling.

* * *

That evening the wind dropped, briefly, the way it sometimes did at altitude — a held breath between weather systems — and the light went gold and sideways across the icefield, and the Kessler Star threw a shadow longer than any ship deserved. Jude walked her perimeter alone, boots crunching, and let herself think the thoughts she'd been rationing all day.

Elias had been here. Her brother, who couldn't swim, who'd gotten seasick on the ferry to the mainland, who had laughed at her for choosing salt water over snow — Elias had climbed above the snowline, broken into a wrecked ship, and carried its heaviest secret further up. On foot. In whatever year it had been, with whatever he was running from.

Running from. The phrase snagged in her like a fishhook, the way it always did, and she pulled it out and looked at it and put it back, the way she always did. Later. Later she'd deal with what he'd been running from, and whether the answer had her own name on it. Tonight there was only the trail, and the gold light, and the strange bright ache of hope — real hope, the first she'd felt in years, hope with a direction and a bearing and her brother's tired handwriting pointing into the high country like a finger.

Okafor found her at the bow, checking the anchor lines one last time before dark.

"Tomorrow we move," he said. "Weather window closes day after. Reyes says the col is passable for maybe thirty hours, then not again until spring."

"Then we pass it."

"With a knee like mine and supplies for six days instead of eleven."

"With all of that," Jude said. "Elias didn't leave a map for people who waited for good conditions. He left it for us."

Okafor considered that, and then, being Okafor, immediately began listing reasons it was a terrible idea, and then, also being Okafor, concluded by agreeing to it, because underneath the grumbling he was the kind of engineer who understood that some loads you carry because they're yours.

At the last light, Jude stood at the rail of the dead ship — an absurd sentence, a rail on a glacier, a woman at a ship's rail with no sea in any direction — and looked up at the col, white against the darkening sky.

*Ask the mountain,* her brother had written.

She was starting to suspect the mountain had been answering for fifteen years. She just hadn't known how to listen.

Behind her, at the edge of camp, Cass stood apart from the fire with the satellite phone in her hand, screen lit, speaking to no one — or to someone — in a voice too low for the wind to carry. Jude watched her for a moment, told herself it was nothing, a check-in with a supplier, a route confirmation.

Told herself.

And then the wind shifted, and carried one word of it up the hull to where Jude stood, one word out of context, one word that didn't fit anything Cass had said in three weeks of planning:

*"— Aldous —"*
