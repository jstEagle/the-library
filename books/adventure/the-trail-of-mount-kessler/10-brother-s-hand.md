## 10. Brother's Hand

The map was scratched into the inside of the strongbox compartment, and it was terrible.

Jude had spent the better part of an hour crouched in the Kessler Star's frozen hold with a headlamp and a strip of ration foil, rubbing charcoal across the etched lines the way she'd once lifted engravings off brass fittings for identification plates. The technique worked. The result looked like a spider had lost a fight with a compass.

"That's either a map," Tamsin said over her shoulder, "or modern art."

"It's a map."

"It's a *bad* map."

"He wasn't a cartographer." Jude tilted the foil toward the light. "He was nineteen and freezing to death on a glacier shelf inside a wreck that shouldn't exist. I'd forgive him worse than bad linework."

Tamsin went quiet at that — the particular quiet of a young person recalibrating. Jude felt it happen and didn't turn around. There was a version of this conversation where she explained who Elias Joubert had been, and what he'd been doing here, and why his handwriting made her chest feel like a hull under pressure. She was not ready for that version. Maybe by the col.

She traced the lines instead. A ridge — she knew this ridge now, they'd crossed its shoulder two days ago, Tamsin complaining about it with the cheerful stamina of youth. Below it, a squiggle that could be a stream or could be Elias giving up halfway through drawing a stream. Then a shape like a tooth, or a fang, or —

"Okafor," she called. "Come look at this and tell me I'm not seeing things."

Okafor came, wiping grease from her hands onto a rag that was itself mostly grease. She studied the foil for a long moment.

"Tooth-shaped peak. There's one of those about six kilometers northeast of here. The locals used to call it the Molar." She tapped the squiggle. "And if that's Miller Creek, it runs right past its base."

"Miller Creek," Jude repeated. The name landed somewhere old. "Our father fished there. Before the company bought the water rights and fenced it."

"So your brother drew you a fishing spot."

"My brother drew me a *treasure* map." And then, because grief needed somewhere to go and humor was the nearest exit: "He always did have dramatic tendencies. When we were kids he buried a coffee can of marbles behind the barn and left me a note in code. Took me three days to crack it. The marbles weren't even good ones."

Tamsin laughed — then caught herself, glancing at Jude's face, as if laughter might be contraband here. Jude shook her head.

"No, laugh. He'd have wanted that. He'd have wanted to be *legendary*, actually. Dying mysteriously and leaving coded maps in shipwrecks? He'd be insufferable about it if he were alive. He'd have made us call him Captain."

The laugh again, smaller this time, and Okafor snorted, and for a moment the three of them stood in the dead ship's belly laughing at a dead boy's flair for theater, and it did not feel blasphemous. It felt like warming hands at a fire.

Then Jude looked back down at the foil, at the small dense cluster of scratches near the Molar's base — a square, and beside it, pressed hard enough to gouge the metal, four letters:

*J.J.*

Her initials. Or his. They'd shared them, growing up — Joubert juniors, their mother's joke, though only one of them was technically a junior. Elias had signed things *J.J.* too, out of solidarity or laziness; she'd never determined which.

"He hid it," she said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew it mattered. "He got here. He found the compartment, he saw whatever Voss stashed, and instead of taking it down the mountain he carried it deeper into the range and buried it. Six kilometers, in winter, alone."

"Why?" Tamsin asked. "Why not just take it home?"

Jude stared at the scratched square. Home. What had home been, for Elias, in the last weeks of his life? What had he known — or believed — that made a hole in a glacier safer than the town where they'd been born?

"I don't know yet," she said. It was the truest sentence she'd spoken in days.

* * *

Tamsin's journal — because everyone kept journals on crossings, Reyes insisted, paper ones, batteries died but graphite didn't:

*Day 19. We're moving tomorrow, northeast, toward a peak Okafor calls the Molar because apparently mountaineers name things like dentists.*

*I watched Jude copy that map for an hour tonight and I've decided something I'm not going to say out loud yet, which is that she's scared. Not of the mountain. Of the map. Of what's written between the lines of it. She holds the foil like it might testify against her.*

*Cass is being weird too but Cass is always being weird so that's baseline.*

*Reyes checked my rope knots twice today without being asked, which either means he thinks I'm sloppy or he thinks something's coming. With Reyes those are the same thing.*

*Jude says her brother was dramatic. She smiled when she said it. It was the first real smile I've seen from her and it was about a boy who's been dead fifteen years, and I don't think that's sad, exactly. I think it's the opposite of sad. I think some people are only reachable through the people they've lost, and the rest of us just have to wait at the edge of that country until they come back out.*

*Tamsin*

*P.S. The Molar better not actually look like a molar. I will lose my mind.*

* * *

They broke camp at first light, and the weather gave them a gift: a high blue window, cold and clean, the kind of day the range offered maybe twice in a season. Reyes studied the sky, then the barometer, then the sky again, as if checking the mountains' homework.

"Window holds maybe thirty-six hours," he said. "After that, a system comes up from the southwest. Big one."

"Then we move fast," Jude said.

They moved fast. The route dropped off the glacier shelf through a rock gully Okafor pronounced "structurally offensive" but passable, then along a moraine spine where the going was good enough that Tamsin ranged ahead like a dog off-leash, and Jude let her, watching the girl's bright jacket bob along the crest and feeling something she refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting she'd started thinking of the crew as hers.

By midday the Molar rose ahead of them, and — Tamsin was vindicated — it looked exactly like a molar. A great gray crown of granite with two cusps, rising out of a saddle, with Miller Creek's frozen ribbon winding away from its base toward the lowlands.

Jude stopped walking.

It wasn't the peak. It was the creek. She'd stood on this bank before. Not this exact spot — the water had shifted, the banks rebuilt themselves every spring — but this *country*. She was eight years old, and Elias was eleven, and he was showing her how to read the water, where the fish held in the cold seams, and their father was upstream pretending not to watch them, and the valley belonged to nobody, or to them, or to whoever was strong enough to hold it, which back then had still meant the Jouberts.

"Fifteen years," she said aloud, without meaning to.

"What?" Tamsin called back.

"Nothing. Keep moving."

But her feet had gone heavy, and the map in her pocket had gone heavy, and the whole morning's lightness drained out through the ice beneath her boots. Because here was the thing she'd been circling since the wreck, the thing the charcoal rubbing wouldn't resolve no matter how many times she traced it:

Elias had come *here*. After the night everything happened. After the fight she barely remembered and the door slamming and the empty bed in the morning. He hadn't gone down-valley to the sheriff, or across the pass to the coast, or anywhere a runaway goes. He'd come *up*. Into the worst terrain in the range, in early winter, at nineteen, with no gear worth the name — and he'd climbed to a wreck that half the valley thought was a myth, and he'd taken what he found, and he'd hidden it, and then —

And then nothing. No body. No trace. Just absence, and a family that broke apart along the fault line of him, and a sister who'd spent fifteen years making sure no place ever learned her name well enough to miss her when she left.

Everyone in Kessler had said the same thing, all those years ago, in the gentle voices people use for the bereaved: *he ran away, Jude. Boys do that. The mountains took him.* Everyone had agreed the story was that Elias Joubert ran from something.

She'd never once let herself ask what.

Now she stood on the bank of the creek their father had fished, looking at a peak her brother had climbed while running, and the question finally turned around and looked back at her.

*Running from what, Eli?*

The wind came down off the Molar and pushed at her like an answer she couldn't hear yet.

* * *

They made the base of the peak by late afternoon, and the scratched square on the map resolved into reality: a shelf of rock beneath the eastern cusp, sheltered from the prevailing wind, with a tumble of boulders at its foot. Old campsite. Okafor found the fire ring within ten minutes — stones stacked with a teenager's optimism rather than a mountaineer's sense, blackened inside despite the decades.

"He camped here," Jude said. Her hand rested on one of the fire stones. "At least one night. Maybe more."

"Long enough to scout a hiding spot," Reyes said. He'd been quiet most of the day, reading the terrain the way he read weather, and now he nodded up-slope, toward a dark seam in the rock face where snow couldn't seem to settle. "There. Cave, or deep chimney. If I were hiding something I wanted found later — by someone specific — that's where I'd put it. Visible, defensible, and you'd see anyone coming for a kilometer."

"You've thought about hiding things," Tamsin said.

"I've thought about everything," Reyes said placidly, and started up the slope.

The seam was a cave — shallow, dry, floored with fine gravel that hadn't been disturbed by anything larger than a bird in years. And twenty feet in, behind a slab that had been levered aside and never quite replaced, they found it.

Not the strongbox. Not yet. First, a tin — tobacco tin, rusted at the seams — and inside the tin, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at the touch, a notebook. Cheap spiral binding, swollen with damp and age, the cover soft as cloth.

Jude didn't open it right away.

She knelt there with her brother's journal in both hands while the others withdrew, tactfully, to the cave mouth — even Tamsin, who had to physically restrain herself, Jude could see it, the girl vibrating with curiosity like a struck tuning fork — and the cave was silent except for the wind worrying at the entrance, and Jude understood that she was about to hear a voice that had stopped speaking fifteen years ago.

She opened it.

His handwriting. God, his handwriting — the same looping scrawl from the marble-burying era, matured slightly, worsened slightly, unmistakably *him*. The first entries were mundane and unbearable: gear lists, route notes, complaints about the price of rope in Kessler, a sketch of the Molar captioned *looks like a tooth, ha.*

Then the entries changed.

She read until the light failed, and then she read by headlamp, and the crew set camp below without being told, and no one came up to hurry her, and later she would understand that this too was a kind of love — the practical, unspoken kind, the kind that builds your tent for you while you sit in a cave with a ghost.

What the journal held, in Elias's increasingly hurried hand, was this: the founding fortune of Kessler had been stolen. Not earned — stolen, from a claim-jump and a murder and a fire that had been set, not accidental, and the man who'd done it had founded the dynasty that now owned the valley, and the documents proving it — the ledger, the deed, the letter in the dead man's own hand — had passed through several sets of guilty hands before ending up sealed in a strongbox aboard the Kessler Star, where Captain Ilsa Voss had put them herself, deliberately, on her last run.

Voss had known. Voss had helped bury it. And Elias had found out — the journal didn't say how, not in the pages Jude could bear to read slowly — and had come up the mountain to take the proof back.

The last full entry was dated four days after the night Jude remembered as the end of the world. Four days. He'd been alive four days after she last saw him, and he'd spent them climbing.

The final entry was a fragment, pencil pressing hard enough to tear the page:

*If I don't make it back down, the box is safe where I've put it. Whoever finds this — check the seam below the east cusp, and then trust the mountain. Don't trust people. I trusted someone and it's why I'm writing this by candlelight in a hole in the ice.*

*I trusted someone.*

Jude sat with that line for a long time.

Because she knew — had always known, in the locked room at the back of her mind where she kept the things that couldn't be looked at directly — that the last thing she'd done before Elias vanished was talk to the Aldouse house. She'd been seventeen and frightened and certain that Ilsa Voss was planning something reckless with her brother, something that would get him killed, and she'd gone to the big house on the hill and told the Aldouses' man where Elias would be, believing — needing to believe — that she was pulling him out of danger.

Two days later, Voss had sworn the whole crew off the Kessler job forever, white-faced and iron-voiced, and Elias had been gone.

She had told herself for fifteen years that those facts were neighbors, not relatives. That the timing was coincidence. That her brother had run off on his own, the way boys did, the way the whole town had kindly agreed he must have.

But he hadn't run *away*. The map proved it, the journal proved it, the fire ring and the cave and the four days of hard climbing proved it. He'd run *up*. He'd run from the valley, from the Aldouses, from Voss — from everything except the truth, which he'd carried higher and higher until the mountain took the rest of him.

From everything except—

"Oh," Jude said aloud, to the empty cave, and her breath fogged the lamplight. "Oh, Eli. You were running from *me*."

It was the first time she'd ever said it. Fifteen years of silence, and it came out small, and it echoed anyway, off the gravel and the stone, the way the truth does when it finally gets loose.

Below her, camp lights bloomed in the dusk — Okafor's stove, Tamsin's lantern, Reyes's steady silhouette on watch. Cass's tent, already pitched, already zipped.

Jude wiped her face with the heel of her glove, closed the journal, and stood up.

Tomorrow she would tell them. Not all of it — not yet, not the part with her own name in it — but the shape of it. That the box was real, and findable, and that her brother had died carrying it, and that the job had stopped being salvage somewhere back on the glacier and become something else entirely.

She was halfway down the scree when she saw the light.

Not camp light. Down-valley, far off, at the edge of the blue dusk where the moraine met the lower slopes: a point of white, moving. Then a second. Headlamps, spaced like a patrol, climbing the exact route they'd climbed today.

Reyes saw them too. She heard him stop, heard the particular silence of a man doing arithmetic with lights and distances.

"Jude," he said quietly. "How many people know where we're headed?"

She watched the distant lamps thread upward through the gathering dark, patient and unhurried, men who knew exactly where they were going because someone had told them.

"Too many," she said.
