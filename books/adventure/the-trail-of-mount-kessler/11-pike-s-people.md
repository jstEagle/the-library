## 11. Pike's People

Reyes had always believed that pain was a language, and like any language it was best learned young, in small words, before you needed it for anything important.

He'd learned his first words of it at nine, on his grandfather's boat, when a winch handle spun backward and caught him across the knuckles. His grandfather hadn't rushed over. He'd looked at the hand, looked at Reyes, and said, *Well. Now you know what that sounds like.* Then he'd wrapped it, fed him soup, and put him back on watch. By fifteen, Reyes could name a hundred varieties: the bright wire-sing of a rope burn, the deep bell-tone of a bruise still deciding how bad it meant to be, the flat gray ache of cold working its way into bone. Pain, properly attended to, told you where you were. It was a kind of navigation.

So when he woke at two in the morning to the sound of a boot finding the cook pot — a specific, unmistakable clang, aluminum on stone, followed by a man's muffled curse — Reyes knew exactly where they were before his eyes were open.

*Here,* said the pain-language of the world, *is trouble.*

He didn't shout. Shouting spent breath and warned the wrong people. He rolled out of his bag low and quiet, found the lantern Cass insisted on keeping lit at the edge of camp — "so we don't look like we're hiding," she'd said, which Reyes had privately translated as *so they can find us faster* — and pinched it dead. Darkness dropped over the camp like a lid.

Three shapes moved among the tents. No — four. One by the supply cache, one near Okafor's tent, and two flanking the trail down toward the glacier shelf, cutting off the obvious retreat. Professional spacing. Company spacing.

"Pike's people," Reyes breathed, more to himself than anyone.

Then Tamsin shouted anyway, because she was nineteen and had not yet learned that fear is a poor broadcaster.

"HEY—"

A flashlight beam snapped on and caught her mid-word, and Reyes did the arithmetic he hated doing: four men, five of them, but the men had lights, batons, and the particular confidence of employees who knew their employer would pay for lawyers afterward. The crew had rope, stoves, and each other.

It should have been enough. It nearly wasn't.

* * *

The first man went down hard because Okafor's tent was pitched over a gear trench the engineer had dug that afternoon for no reason he could articulate except "the ground felt wrong." Reyes heard the man's ankle turn inside the trench and filed the sound away — a wet pop, a short cry — *there*, that was a word of pain he recognized, and it meant one attacker was now a limper.

The second man came for the cache, and Cass met him there. Reyes saw only silhouettes: her small frame stepping into his reach instead of away from it, the sharp crack of her knee driving up, the man folding around it like a closing book. She took the cache tarp with her as she fell back, dragging half their food into the dark behind a boulder, which was either brilliant tactics or pure spite, and knowing Cass it was both.

That left the third man, and the third man was fast.

He came through the middle of camp straight at the tents, at the sleeping bags, at the thing dynasties always want most, which is leverage — and Jude Joubert hit him from the side like weather coming off a ridge. Reyes had watched her dive, watched her rig lines, watched her do a hundred competent things with a hundred quiet hands, but he had never seen her angry, and anger turned out to be the largest thing she carried. They went down together in a tangle of limbs and gravel, and the man's baton skittered free across the stones.

Which left the fourth man, the one Reyes hadn't been watching, because Reyes had made the classic navigator's error: he'd charted the visible currents and forgotten the eddy behind the rock.

The fourth man had come up the low trail, the one Reyes himself had picked that evening as the softest approach, and he arrived at Reyes's back the way bad news arrives — already there.

The baton caught him across the shoulder blade first. Then, when he turned, across the ribs.

Reyes sat down. Not dramatically; his legs simply resigned from the committee. He registered the ground through his palms, the taste of blood where he'd bitten his cheek, and above all the pain itself, arriving in full sentences now, fluent and detailed: *two ribs, maybe three, left side, deep breath denied.* He catalogued it the way he'd catalogue a reef — hazard marked, route adjusted — and while he was cataloguing, Tamsin hit the fourth man with a cook pot.

Full swing. Two-handed. The clang of it rang off the valley walls and came back twice.

The man staggered, blinked at this small furious person in a wool hat, and made the mistake of reaching for her. That was when Jude arrived, having finished with the third man, and what happened next took under four seconds and involved an arm bar Reyes genuinely intended to ask her about later, if there was a later, if his ribs permitted questions.

Then it was over. Four men: one limping downhill, one unconscious, one groaning into the gravel with Cass crouched on his chest going through his pockets with the efficiency of a customs officer, and the last one running, crashing downslope through the scrub with the pot-clang still echoing after him like an accusation.

Silence settled back over the camp, thin and ringing.

"Well," said Okafor, emerging from his ruined tent holding his sleeping bag like a wounded friend. "I want everyone to know I dug that trench on instinct."

"You saved us," Tamsin panted.

"I saved *me*. You all just happened to be standing nearby."

Reyes lay on his back and looked at the stars and laughed, which was a mistake, and the mistake told him everything he needed to know about the ribs. He laughed again anyway, smaller, because some things deserved the cost.

Four heads appeared above him, backlit against the sky, wearing four versions of the same expression.

"Oh no," said Tamsin.

"I'm fine," Reyes said.

"He's fine," Cass translated, "which means he isn't."

Jude knelt without a word, and her hands — those quiet hands — found his ribs with a diver's touch, reading him like a hull. She pressed once, gently, and Reyes's breath did something involuntary and undignified.

"Cracked," she said. "Not floating. Probably."

"'Probably' is my favorite word in medicine," Reyes managed.

"It's mine too. It means nobody has to carry you yet." She looked up at the others. "Get the shelter tarps. We're moving camp. Now, before his friends regroup or report."

* * *

They moved him the way he would have moved cargo he couldn't bear to lose — which was to say, badly, and with too much talking.

Okafor rigged a litter from trekking poles and the good tarp within eleven minutes, muttering load calculations under his breath like prayers. Tamsin insisted on taking a corner of it immediately and was permitted to, mostly because refusing her would have cost more energy than the corner weighed. Cass scouted ahead with the flashlight hooded, and Jude walked backward beside the litter the whole way, one hand braced against Reyes's uninjured side, catching every stumble before it became a jolt.

They climbed forty minutes up-scree to a hollow beneath an overhang that Jude seemed to know about, though no one asked how, and rebuilt camp in the dark with the economy of people who had recently discovered what losing things felt like.

Because they had lost things. The inventory, taken by lantern light at three in the morning, read like an obituary: half the food gone with the cache tarp Cass had dragged to the boulder (and half of *that* abandoned in the scramble), the spare radio soaked when a man had knocked the cook water across it, two tents slashed beyond repair, and — Okafor announced this one with genuine grief — the espresso attachment.

"They took the *bambino*," he said, cradling the empty leather case.

"They took half our calories and you're mourning the coffee toy," said Cass.

"That toy is the reason morale exists. Morale is a structural component. Ask any engineer."

"Morale," Reyes said from the litter, "is currently lying down."

Everyone looked at him. He raised one hand a few centimeters off his chest, which was all the gesture his ribs would finance, and gave them a thumbs-up.

Tamsin burst out laughing, then clapped both hands over her mouth like she'd sworn in church, and the laugh leaked out between her fingers anyway, high and helpless, and one by one the others caught it — even Jude, who smiled the way icebergs smile, which is to say ninety percent below the surface, but Reyes saw it, and counted it.

*There,* he thought, drifting a little as the pain-language softened into dialects of exhaustion. *That's the sound of a crew becoming a crew.* It didn't happen on the good days. Any group of hires could share a good day. It happened at three in the morning, in the wreckage of an inventory, over a stolen espresso maker. Grief, shared, stopped being private property. That was all family ever was, in his experience: people who had lost the same things and kept showing up anyway.

* * *

By dawn the storm front they'd been dreading showed its first cloud on the western ridgeline, fat and gray and unhurried, like something that owned the mountain and knew it.

Reyes sat propped against the overhang — sitting up was a negotiation he won in increments — and did the navigation he could still do, which was all of it, actually; his legs worked fine, his eyes worked fine, it was only breathing deeply that cost extra. He spread the map on his knees and plotted.

"We lost the resupply margin," he said, when the others gathered. "At current rations, we have six days of food. The col crossing, weather permitting, is four days. Weather—" he nodded westward "—will not permit."

"Say it plain," Cass said.

"The storm buys Pike's men time to regroup and closes our window. We cross early, in the leading edge, or we don't cross at all. And we cross hungry."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Reyes watched them the way he watched weather — not the words, the pressure. Okafor's jaw worked through calculations he didn't say aloud. Tamsin looked at Jude, then quickly away, the way you look at a lighthouse. Cass's face did its usual excellent impression of a locked door.

And Jude stood looking west at the storm with her arms folded, and Reyes thought: *she's not afraid of Pike's men. She's afraid of that.* Of the range. Of whatever waited for her in it. He'd known her eleven days and understood this completely, the way you understand a current by the way debris moves in it, and he understood also that she would go anyway, and that this — the going-anyway — was the truest thing about her.

"Six days of food," Jude said finally. "We split it five ways and move light. Okafor, can the stove run on the white gas we've got?"

"White gas, yes. Espresso, tragically, no."

"Then we'll suffer," Jude said, and there it was again, that ten percent of a smile. "Reyes. Route?"

Reyes looked down at the map, at the contour lines crowding together below the col like teeth, and gave them the honest version, because these people had bled for the honest version tonight and he owed them nothing less.

"There's a line through the eastern moraine. Steep, exposed, but it stays in shadow longer — we buy six hours before the storm hits the ridge. We start tomorrow at first light and we don't stop until we're through." He tapped the map. "One catch. There's a stretch where I can't be carried. Everyone climbs. I climb too."

"You have cracked ribs," Tamsin said.

"I have *probably* cracked ribs. Jude said probably. I'm choosing to believe her, because the alternative is staying here, and staying here means being here when Pike's people come back with better numbers and worse manners." He met her eyes. "I've navigated through worse than sore ribs, little one. I once crossed the Serrano shoals with a broken hand and a captain who sang sea shanties off-key the entire way. This will be easier. Quieter, at minimum."

Tamsin's mouth twitched. "You call me *little one* one more time and I'm telling everyone about your shanty captain."

"His name was Bruno and he rhymed 'forecastle' with 'disaster.' I have suffered, Tamsin. Respect your elders."

She laughed, and Okafor snorted, and even Cass's locked door opened a crack — a huff of air that might, in a generous wind, have been called a laugh.

Reyes folded the map and let his head rest back against the stone, and let the pain talk, and listened to it the way you listen to an old crewmate: not with fear, but with familiarity. *Two ribs,* it said. *Left side. Six days of short rations. A storm coming. A pass in shadow.*

And underneath all of it, quieter, in the register he'd only started hearing since the coast, since the patrol boat, since the tents and the trail and the terrible espresso-less mornings: *these people. These particular people.*

He had navigated for captains who measured worth in tonnage and clients who measured it in gold, and he had never once, in thirty years of charts and reefs and weather windows, been part of a crew that someone else's men had come in the night to break — and failed to.

Failed, because a girl with a cook pot and an engineer with a hole in the ground and a woman with a knee like a dock cleat and a diver with weather in her fists had declined, collectively, to be broken.

"First light," Reyes said aloud, and closed his eyes. "Sleep while you can."

Behind him, the western clouds ate the first star, and somewhere down-valley, faint as a rumor, a second set of boots began to move.
