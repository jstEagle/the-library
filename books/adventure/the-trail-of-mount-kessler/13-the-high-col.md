## 13. The High Col

Tamsin had grown up on stories of the high passes, the way other children grew up on lullabies. Her grandmother used to say the col above Kessler was a doorway that only opened for people who didn't need it too badly — which Tamsin had always taken as poetry until she stood at its foot and understood it as weather advice.

The snows were early this year. Everyone said so. The marmots had gone quiet two weeks ahead of schedule, and Reyes — who navigated by a hundred small signs no one else even noticed — had been frowning at the sky since dawn like it owed him money.

"We go today or we don't go," he said, spreading the map on a flat stone and weighting the corners with pebbles. "Storm window closes tomorrow afternoon. After that, we're camp furniture."

Jude stood at the edge of the group, arms crossed, looking up at the pass the way a person looks at a letter they've been avoiding. Tamsin watched her do it. She'd been watching Jude Joubert do things for eleven days now — tie knots, read ice, drink coffee black enough to qualify as geology — and she had catalogued every gesture with the devotion of a scholar and the discretion of, well, less discretion than a scholar should have.

"You've been up there before," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question.

"Once." Jude's voice was flat as the glacier below them. "A long time ago."

Okafor looked up from checking his crampons. "Good trip?"

"No."

He waited for more. None came. Okafor had learned that about Jude the way everyone did: you could stand at the edge of her silences all day and they'd hold your weight without complaint.

"Well," Okafor said cheerfully, "I'm sure it'll be different this time. Statistically, most trips are different from other trips. That's practically science."

Reyes rolled the map away. "Rope up in ten. Tamsin, you're second. Jude anchors."

Tamsin's heart did something embarrassing in her chest. Second rope behind Jude Joubert. She kept her face neutral through sheer force of will, the way she'd practiced in the mirror back in Kessler Town when she was fourteen and practicing neutrality for exactly this kind of moment.

* * *

The first hour was kind to them. The glacier rose in long blue shelves, wind-scoured and honest, and Tamsin found her rhythm — step, breathe, plant, step — the rhythm that made the world simple. She liked simple. Simple was why she climbed. Up here there were no debts, no towns where everyone knew what your father had done, no company scrip in your pocket reminding you that you were worth whatever the Aldouses said you were worth. There was just the mountain, which judged you fairly, by results.

She glanced back once and saw Jude three lengths behind, moving with an economy that made everyone else look like they were walking through soup. Even now. Even with whatever the mountain was saying to her that made her jaw set like that.

Because Tamsin had seen it. On day six, crossing the moraine, when the range first came fully into view — she'd caught Jude's face before Jude could arrange it into something professional. For half a second it had been open and raw and afraid, and then it closed like a shop shutter, and Jude had said something dry about Okafor's packing technique, and that was that.

Tamsin hadn't told anyone. Some things you keep, even from yourself, until you understand them.

The trouble started after the midday halt, past the serac field, where the glacier bent around a shoulder of black rock and the surface changed character. New snow lay over old, and under the old snow — invisible, patient — were the bridges. Snow bridges over crevasses, the mountain's lies, pretty white sentences covering empty blue grammar.

"Spacing," Jude called back. "Wide spacing. Test everything. If I go down, sit on the rope and shout, don't be a hero."

"I would never be a hero," Okafor said. "I've read about heroes. Their life expectancy is terrible."

They moved. Step, probe, step. Tamsin's pole went *thunk* into good snow, again and again, and she let herself relax by degrees, which was, she would think later, exactly the mistake the mountain was waiting for.

It happened between one step and the next.

There was no warning sound. That was the thing nobody told you — the movies gave you a crack, a groan, some dramatic overture. Reality just deleted the floor. One moment Tamsin's weight was on solid ground; the next, the ground was a rumor, and she was falling into a blue dark that swallowed her headlamp whole.

Her axe bit nothing. Her boots hit a wall of ice and glanced off. The rope snapped taut around her harness and wrenched her sideways, and she swung, penduluming in a cathedral of cold light, screaming something — she never remembered what — while the world above shrank to a coin of white sky.

Then it stopped. She hung. Alone, spinning slowly, thirty feet down in a crevasse that breathed cold at her like something alive.

"Hello?" she said, to the crevasse, to herself, to whoever might be listening. Her voice came back doubled off the ice walls, mocking her.

Above, faintly, she heard it: *"Hold! Everybody hold!"* Jude's voice, cracked raw with a fear Tamsin had never heard in it.

And then, worse — silence. Long silence. The kind of silence where a person is doing arithmetic they hate.

"Tamsin." Jude's voice again, closer to the lip now, controlled with visible effort. "Talk to me. Are you hurt?"

"My pride," Tamsin called up, because her hands were shaking too hard for anything true, "has sustained catastrophic injuries."

A pause. Then, drifting down through the white coin of sky, absurd and impossible: "Noted. Anything structural?"

"My left ankle took a hit on the way down. It works. Mostly. Ask it again in an hour."

"Copy." Another pause. Tamsin could hear the crew conferring above, voices overlapping — Okafor's low rumble, Reyes's clipped Spanish counting something out. And underneath it, one voice that wasn't conferring at all. One voice that had gone quiet in the specific way of someone facing the exact thing they'd been dreading for fifteen years.

Tamsin hung in the blue dark and listened to Jude Joubert be afraid, and felt the strange vertigo of it — not the falling kind, the other kind. The kind where the ground truth of a person shifts beneath you.

* * *

Here is what Tamsin had believed, until that morning, with the unexamined certainty of the young: that Jude Joubert did not fail. That competence, in Jude, was not a skill but a substance, like bone. That the woman who'd pulled her out of trouble twice already — once on the scree below Kessler Town, once in the river crossing where Tamsin had misjudged the current and very nearly become a statistic — simply *did not lose* to mountains.

She had built a shrine to that idea without ever noticing she was building it. And shrines, Tamsin was beginning to understand, are heavy things to carry, both for the worshipper and for the god.

Because the rescue, when it came, was not clean.

She heard the work of it — the anchor going in, the rope running, Jude's breathing through the radio-cable tension of the line itself. And then the first attempt failed. Tamsin felt it fail: the rope went slack, hauled up, and stopped, and stopped, and she heard Jude swear — actually swear, actually lose her temper at the ice, at the anchor, at herself — a short hot burst of frustration that echoed down the crevasse and broke against the walls around Tamsin like spray.

"Problem?" Tamsin called up, aiming for light and landing somewhere nearer to pleading.

"The anchor placement was wrong," Jude said. Flat. Furious at herself. "My fault. Resetting."

Silence again. Work again. Tamsin hung there, swaying, and did the bravest thing available to her, which was nothing. She waited. She let the rope hold her instead of pretending she could hold herself, which was, she suspected, a lesson the mountain had been trying to teach her since the day she was born.

Second attempt. The rope came alive, and this time it stayed alive, hauling her up in slow jerks past walls of ice striped blue and green like old bottle glass. Her shoulder scraped the lip of the crevasse, and then Okafor's hands were on her harness, and then she was lying on the surface of the glacier, gasping at the sky like a landed fish, alive, ridiculous, saved.

Saved messily. Saved by a woman who had gotten the first anchor wrong and said so out loud.

Tamsin turned her head. Jude was crouched a few feet away, re-coiling the rope with quick, angry precision, her face doing something complicated. Not triumph. Nothing like triumph. Something closer to shame wearing triumph's coat.

"You okay?" Tamsin asked.

"Fine." Too fast. "You?"

"Mostly fine. Ankle's complaining but it'll walk."

Jude nodded and kept coiling, and Tamsin watched her, and understood — really understood, for the first time — that the fear she'd glimpsed on the moraine wasn't about altitude or avalanche or any ordinary danger. It was older than that. It lived deeper than gear. Whatever the mountains had taken from Jude Joubert, they'd taken it personally, and every anchor she placed was placed with her whole history in her hands, and sometimes her whole history shook.

The great Jude Joubert was afraid. The great Jude Joubert got things wrong. And she said so — not easily, not gracefully, but honestly, which was maybe harder.

Tamsin lay on the ice and felt her shrine quietly dismantle itself, and discovered that what remained underneath was better. Not a goddess. A person. A person who was afraid of this place and came anyway, who failed and admitted it and fixed it, who had hauled a foolish nineteen-year-old out of a hole in the world because that was the job and she did the job.

Trust, Tamsin realized, wasn't believing someone couldn't fail. It was watching them fail and come back for you anyway.

* * *

They moved again within the hour, because the storm window didn't care about anyone's feelings. Tamsin walked on her complaining ankle with Okafor's trekking pole donated to the cause ("It has served me honorably; it deserves a promotion"), and the col rose ahead of them, a notch of violent white between two black shoulders, wind streaming off it like smoke.

They reached it at four in the afternoon, with the light already going the color of weak tea.

From the top, the world split in two. Behind them, the glacier they'd crossed, their own trail a stitched line of small human decisions. Ahead — the far side of the range, dropping away in shadowed folds toward valleys Tamsin had only ever seen from below, from town, from the wrong end of her whole life. The wind hit them at the notch like a hand shoved flat against the chest, and Reyes had to clip everyone to the fixed line he'd rigged along the crest, shouting instructions over the roar.

And Tamsin, clipped in, leaning into the wind, looked sideways at Jude.

Jude was standing at the top of the pass with the whole broken skyline pouring past her, and her eyes were closed. Just for a few seconds. Just long enough. Her lips were moving — not words, or words too quiet for the wind to spare — and her hand, bare for a moment before the cold forced the glove back on, was pressed flat against a rock, as if greeting it, or apologizing to it, or saying goodbye to somebody.

Then her eyes opened, and she was Jude again, and she shouted, "Move! Storm's coming in faster than Reyes promised, which if anything makes him more insufferable—"

"It makes me *correct*," Reyes bellowed back, delighted. "Being correct early is still being correct!"

They crossed the col in a ragged, laughing, terrified line, and dropped down the far side as the first flakes came in sideways, fat and fast, the sky closing behind them like a book.

* * *

Camp that night was a scramble of frozen tent poles and Okafor narrating his own suffering for morale ("I have lost feeling in my left ear; the ear and I had our differences, but I want it back"). They got the shelter up, got the stove hissing, got Reyes's ankle wrapped and Tamsin's ankle iced with a stuff sack full of snow, and sat in the orange glow doing the arithmetic of what the storm had cost them.

Half a day. Maybe more, if it blew overnight.

"We're behind," Cass said. She'd been quiet since the col — quieter than usual, which for Cass was saying something, since her usual quiet was already a loaded weapon. She sat sharpening her knife with long, even strokes, and her eyes kept going to the tent flap, to the storm, to something beyond both.

"Behind is survivable," Reyes said. "Behind and injured is manageable. Behind, injured, and hunted—" He glanced at the flap. "That's the version I don't love."

Nobody loved that version. Nobody said the rest of it either, the part hanging in the tent air like breath: that Pike's men were somewhere behind them on the glacier, and that the storm that slowed the crew slowed the hunters not at all, because hunters don't need windows, only direction.

Tamsin flexed her ankle inside its wrapping and winced, and Jude, across the tent, looked up from the rope she was re-rigging — always re-rigging, always checking, as if the mountain might forgive her if she checked enough times.

"How's the ankle?"

"Honest answer? It'll carry me. It won't thank me."

"That's all any of us can ask of our bodies," Okafor said solemnly. "Or our ears."

Jude almost smiled. Almost. But Tamsin caught it — the real one, the flicker underneath — and thought: *there.* That was the person under the legend. That was worth following, more than the legend had ever been.

Later, when the others slept, Tamsin heard Jude and Reyes talking in low voices by the stove, planning tomorrow, arguing gently about the descent route. And she heard Jude say something she must have assumed everyone was asleep for:

"If it comes to it, take Tamsin down the low route. Her ankle. Don't wait for me."

"And you?"

"I'll catch up."

Reyes was silent a moment. "You said that once before, I think. To someone."

Another silence, longer, colder. Tamsin lay still in her bag, eyes open, listening to the storm lean on the fabric of the world, and heard Jude finally answer — barely above the wind:

"Yeah. I did."

Tamsin stared into the dark of the tent and understood that the story she'd been telling herself — the story of the fearless Jude Joubert, the woman who doesn't fail — had been a child's version all along. The true story was still being written, right here, in a storm, by a woman carrying something up this mountain that weighed more than any strongbox.

And tomorrow, Tamsin thought, drowsing, trusting, they would follow her anyway — not because she wouldn't fail, but because she'd already shown them what she did afterward.

Outside, the wind shifted, and brought with it, faint and wrong, a sound none of them had trained themselves to hear yet: the far-off whine of an engine climbing the valley they'd left behind.

Someone was coming up the low route.

Someone who hadn't needed a storm window at all.
