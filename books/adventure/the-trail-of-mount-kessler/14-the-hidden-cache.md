## 14. The Hidden Cache

The map her brother had scratched into the hull plating was not, Jude had to admit, a good map.

She'd spent three days of the crossing turning the rubbing she'd taken — graphite on sailcloth, the lines faint as breath — this way and that in the lantern light, while the crew offered increasingly unhelpful suggestions.

"That's a mountain," Okafor said, tapping a jagged stroke.

"That's a scratch."

"That's a mountain-shaped scratch."

"That," Jude said, "is a scratch shaped like my brother's impatience. He never could draw. Mama made him take art lessons for two years and all he produced was one lopsided horse and a lifelong grudge."

"Which one is the horse?"

"Okafor."

But there were things in it she couldn't argue with. A double peak with a notch between, drawn twice, insisted upon. A line descending from the notch that hooked east — a glacier's flow, she realized, the way ice rivers bend around a shoulder of rock. And near the bottom, small and deliberate, a shape she knew in her bones before her head caught up: a cave mouth, marked with the symbol he'd invented when he was nine years old and they'd made a treasure map of the garden. An X with a circle around it, like an eye. *Here. Look here. I'm looking at you.*

She hadn't thought of that symbol in twenty years. She thought of it now at three in the morning, sitting up in her bag with her heart going like a winch drum, and had to sit with her head in her hands until the shaking stopped.

*He was alive. He got to the wreck, he got inside, he took the box out, and he walked it deeper into the range — away from the town, away from the Aldouses, away from —*

Away from her.

She put that thought back in the box it lived in and closed the lid, and in the morning she showed the crew the route, and nobody asked why her eyes were red, because they were kind, or smart, or both.

* * *

The glacier took two days to give them what they wanted.

It was Tamsin, of course, who saw it first — the girl had eyes like a surveyor's instrument and the patience of a cat at a mousehole. They were roped in a line across the icefall, the light going blue and strange as the afternoon aged, when her voice came crackling over the radio, too calm the way she got when she was excited.

"Jude. Your eleven o'clock. In the rock."

The shoulder of stone the glacier bent around rose out of the ice like the hull of a ship that had decided to become a mountain. And low on its flank, half-drowned in fifty years of freeze and thaw, there was a darkness. A mouth.

Jude stood on the ice and looked at it and could not make her feet move.

She'd dived wrecks in zero visibility. She'd gone down a flooded mine shaft once, on a bet she still considered the stupidest thing she'd ever done, and she'd gone down smiling. But this was different. This was a door her brother had walked through, and he hadn't walked out the other side, and some animal part of her understood that doors like that only open one way.

"Jude?" Tamsin again, gentler now.

"Coming," she said, and made her feet move, because that was the whole trick of her life, really. You just made your feet move.

The approach was ugly — a moraine slope of ice-glued boulders that shifted underfoot like a deck in a swell, and Reyes, favoring the arm he'd taken the shrapnel of Pike's ambush in, climbed it with the grim economy of a man who had decided pain was a coworker he'd simply stopped speaking to. Jude caught herself watching him the way she watched weather. He never complained. He just recalculated. She was beginning to understand that this was his whole philosophy: you didn't fight the terrain, you navigated it. Including the terrain of other people.

"Stop staring at me," Reyes said, without looking at her. "It's unsettling."

"Sorry."

"You're not sorry."

"No," she admitted. "But I said it nicely."

The ghost of a smile crossed his face and was gone, and they climbed on.

* * *

The cave was bigger inside than the mountain had any business allowing.

They went in with headlamps only, Okafor insisting — correctly — that no open flame went anywhere near whatever was in there until he'd had a look at the air. The ice had grown down from the entrance in frozen cascades, translucent and blue-green, so that walking in felt like descending into the inside of a wave. Jude had spent her whole adult life inside waves. She hadn't expected the mountains to remind her of them. It loosened something in her chest, and she hated it for that.

The cave opened into a chamber, and the chamber held a camp.

It was small. A flattened bedroll gone to felt. A tin cup. A candle stub fused to the rock by decades of cold. And on a natural shelf of stone, centered with the care of a man arranging a message he knew would be found — because it *was* a message, it had always been a message — sat the strongbox.

Beside it, a leather journal, its cover bleached grey.

Nobody moved for a moment. Even the wind outside seemed to hold its breath.

"Okafor," Jude said, and her voice came out in pieces. "The air."

He lit his tester, watched the flame stand steady and blue. "Clean. Cold, but clean." He looked at her. "Take your time."

But she found she couldn't. Her hands went to the box first — the strongbox, the whole reason for all of it, the thing Cass had hired her for and the dynasty would kill for — and it was lighter than she expected, and the lock was already open. Her brother hadn't had the key. He'd simply worked it, the way she would have, the way anyone with their blood in their hands would have. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers. She didn't read them. Not yet. Her hands had already gone past them, already closed on the journal, because a strongbox was a job and the journal was a voice.

She sat down on the ice, in the cold, in front of her brother's camp, and she opened it, and she read her brother's handwriting for the first time in fifteen years.

* * *

He wrote the way he'd talked — fast, slantwise, funny, with the jokes doing the work of armor. The early pages were almost easy to bear. His crossing of the range at seventeen, alone, furious, alive. He'd been headed for the wreck of the Kessler Star because Ilsa Voss had told him what was in it.

That was the first blow, and it landed before Jude understood it was a blow. *Ilsa.* Their captain. The woman who had gathered the crew after his disappearance and made them swear off the Kessler job with tears in her eyes and iron in her voice — *that mountain's taken enough of us* — the woman whose grief Jude had measured her own against for fifteen years, the one grief she'd allowed herself because her own was unspeakable.

Ilsa had told him where to go. Ilsa had told him what the box held. Ilsa had *sent* him.

Jude read on, and the cave got colder, or she did.

The pages from the wreck were careful, methodical, their father's son to the bone. He'd found the compartment. He'd found the papers. And the papers were worse than anyone had guessed, worse than Cass's talk of leverage, worse than the dynasty's willingness to kill for them had suggested. Because they weren't just proof that Aldous the founder had stolen the Joubert claim.

They were proof of what he'd done to keep it.

*He didn't cheat my grandfather,* her brother had written, in letters that got harder and harder as the ink froze and thawed and froze again. *He killed him. There's a deposition in here — a witness, a mule driver who saw it, who took payment and kept silent until his conscience got too loud and he put it all on paper and hid it in the one place Aldous couldn't buy: the wreck of his own ship, the one he'd insured and scuttled for the money. The Kessler Star wasn't an accident. Aldous sank his own ship to drown the truth, and the truth learned to swim.*

And below that, the line that stopped Jude's heart and then, after a long moment, started it again at a different rhythm:

*Voss knew. Voss has always known. She was there. She was crew on the salvage that "found" nothing. She took Aldous money to say the strongbox compartment was empty, and she's been carrying it for thirty years, and I think that's why she forbade the job — not grief. Guilt. She sent me because she can't go herself, and because she thinks a Joubert holding this paper is a debt she can finally pay. She's using me. She's using all of us. She's been using Jude since the day she signed her on.*

Jude lowered the journal.

The chamber was silent except for the small sounds of ice settling, the mountain's slow breathing. Tamsin had drifted closer without seeming to move, the way she did, and was watching Jude's face with open, unguarded worry. Okafor had taken off his hat, which was what he did in place of prayer. Reyes stood at the cave mouth, on watch, giving her privacy the only way he knew how — by guarding it.

"Jude," Tamsin said softly. "What is it?"

"She knew," Jude said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Ilsa knew. All of it. She helped bury it — took their money, swore the compartment was empty — and then she spent thirty years being *revered* about it. Being the captain who lost a boy to the mountain and forbade the job out of love." She laughed, and it came out wrong, and she heard Tamsin flinch. "We swore it off for her. We grieved for her. I built my whole — I left the mountains, I left *everything*, and half the reason was that I couldn't stand to look at her grief, because I thought it was mine to answer for—"

She stopped. Because the next thought was coming whether she said it or not, and it was coming fast.

*And she sent a seventeen-year-old boy up the range alone with the truth in his hands. And when he didn't come back, she never told anyone what she'd sent him for. She let us think the mountain just took him. She let me think —*

She let me think it was my fault.

Jude looked down at the journal, and the last pages were the hardest, and she knew it the way you know a wave is about to break, and she read them anyway, because she was her father's daughter too.

The last entry was dated the day before the date the town had always used for her brother's death. The handwriting had gone ragged.

*Something's wrong. I found a note in my camp this morning — someone's been up here. Ilsa's writing. She says Jude told the Aldouses where I'd be. She says my own sister sold me to them to protect me from her, and that I should come down and hear it from her before I believe it.*

*But I don't need to come down. I already know it's half true. I saw Jude with the Aldous man in the spring. I told myself it was nothing. And Ilsa wouldn't lie about the note — she's a lot of things but she doesn't lie, that's the whole tragedy of her.*

*So either my sister turned me in, or someone made it look like she did, and either way I can't go down that mountain. If I go down, I'm a loose end. The papers are the only thing keeping me breathing. I'm going deeper. There's a route east over the high col that nobody watches in the shoulder season. If I make it, I'm gone, and I'm taking the truth with me, and someday I'll come back and set it on fire in the middle of Kessler Town.*

*If I don't make it — Jude, if you ever read this — I need you to know that I decided to believe you. Not because I have proof. Because when we were kids you broke the garden fence with a croquet mallet and you stood in the wreckage and told Mama it was you before she'd even asked, and you were so proud of yourself for being honest that you forgot to be scared. That's who you are. That's the person I'm choosing to believe in.*

*Don't come looking for me. Set it on fire for me instead.*

The journal slipped from her hands onto the ice, and Jude Joubert, who did not cry, who had not cried in fifteen years, who had built an entire life out of the material of not crying — put her face in her hands and wept in front of her crew in a blue cave under a glacier, and found, to her astonishment, that the world did not end.

Tamsin's arm came around her shoulders, awkward and fierce. Okafor stood with his hat in his hands. And Reyes, from the cave mouth, said nothing at all, which was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for her.

* * *

Later — she didn't know how much later; the light through the ice had gone the color of dusk — she wiped her face with a sleeve that had been dry that morning, and picked up the journal, and put it inside her jacket, against her skin, where she intended it to stay.

"The papers," Cass would want the papers. The thought arrived uninvited and sat down. Cass had hired her with one word doing all the work — *your family's name is inside it* — and Jude had signed because of that word, and now she sat in her brother's grave-chamber holding the thing Cass had paid for, and she did not know what she was going to do with it, and she was afraid, in a small cold corner of herself, that she already did.

"Jude." Reyes's voice from the entrance, quiet, stripped of everything but the fact. "You need to see this."

She went to the mouth of the cave. Below them, far down the valley they had climbed out of, lights were moving. Not the scattered lights of a town. A line of them. Orderly. Spaced like men who had done this before, climbing through the dark with the patience of people who knew exactly where they were going.

Tamsin raised her binoculars, and after a moment lowered them, and her young face had gone very still.

"They're not following our track," she said. "They're following *the* track. The low route. Straight to this glacier." She turned. "Jude, nobody knows about this cave. Nobody *could* know. Except—"

Except the map was scratched on the hull of the Kessler Star. Except anyone who'd been inside the wreck could have found it. Except the navy had warned them off with rehearsed words, and Pike had named their debts over dinner like a man reading a menu, and someone, somewhere, had known where this trail ended before they'd ever set boot on it.

Jude stood in the cold mouth of the mountain, her brother's journal warm against her chest, and watched the lights come on up through the dark like a slow, deliberate fuse.

"They knew," she said. "They've known all along."
