## 16. Pike Closes In

Cass Merrow had heard a great many voices come through radio static in her life — her father's, drunk and distant; a border patrol officer's, bored; her own, once, recorded and played back to her by a man who wanted her to understand how easy she was to find. She liked to think she could read a voice the way Reyes read weather. And the voice coming through the handheld that morning, crisp as a banknote against all that snow-static, belonged to Edran Pike, and it was *pleased*.

That was the worst part. Not the threat. The pleasure.

"Good morning, camp," Pike said. "I hope everyone slept warm. I know I did."

Reyes held the radio out from his body like something venomous. Around him the crew had gone still in the way people go still when a floorboard creaks at three in the morning. Okafor's hands stopped mid-repair on the stove. Tamsin stood in the cave mouth with frost in her lashes. Jude sat on a supply crate with her brother's journal closed on her knee, and her face was doing nothing at all, which Cass knew from long experience meant everything.

"I'll take that," Cass said, and held out her hand for the radio.

Reyes looked at Jude. Jude looked at Cass. Something passed between them — not trust, not yet, but the memory of a storm shelter and a confession, which was its own kind of collateral. Reyes handed the radio over.

"This is Merrow," Cass said. "You're transmitting on an open frequency, Edran. Very sloppy of you."

"On the contrary." A pause you could pour into a glass. "I want them all to hear this. Especially the diver."

Cass kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used across negotiating tables when the other side thought they'd already won. "Then say your piece."

"Amnesty," Pike said, and the word came out round and generous, a man handing out bread. "Full amnesty. No charges, no questions, no consequences for trespassing on restricted range. The Aldous family is prepared to be gracious. All we require is the strongbox and its contents, delivered intact, and each of you walks away whole. There's even compensation. Mr. Okafor's workshop lease in Copper Bend comes up for renewal in March, doesn't it? The bank has been patient, but banks are like glaciers — slow, and then suddenly not."

Okafor made a small sound, half laugh, half wound.

"Miss Tamsin," Pike continued warmly, "your mother's medical debt is a matter of public record, and the Kessler clinic is, after all, a company clinic. And Mr. Reyes — well. Mr. Reyes, I'm told, has a sister in Port Amity who would very much like to hear from him. It's been what, four years? Families worry."

Cass watched Reyes take that one. He didn't flinch, exactly. He did the thing he always did with pain, folded it up small and put it somewhere behind his eyes and went on navigating. But his jaw worked once, twice, like a rudder biting water.

"And Ms. Joubert," Pike said, and here his voice changed — softened, which was somehow worse than any threat he'd made yet. "Ms. Joubert, you have nothing to lose and everything to gain. The box contains proof of what was done to your family. We're offering you the chance to hand it to someone who will *use* it. Isn't that better than letting it rot in the ice? Justice needs an executor, Ms. Joubert. We're volunteering."

"You're volunteering to bury it deeper," Cass said.

"Ms. Merrow." And now Pike laughed, low and genuine, and Cass felt the ground tilt under her before she understood why. "You of all people should appreciate our position. After all — you sold us the route."

The wind filled the silence. Snow ticked against stone. Cass became aware, distantly, of four people looking at her, and of the fact that her face — trained in boardrooms and back rooms to give away nothing — had given away something anyway, because Tamsin's brow had started to fold.

"He's lying," Cass said. "It's a wedge. It's the oldest play there is. Divide the crew, cheapen the prize—"

"March the fourteenth," Pike said pleasantly. "The Ferryman's Rest, back room. You gave my office the col approach, the resupply schedule, and the name of every hire. You asked for double because, quote, 'the diver is sentimental and sentiment makes people unpredictable.' We paid half up front. I have the ledger page right here, Ms. Merrow. Shall I read it aloud? I have excellent reception today."

The radio crackled, almost companionable.

Cass stood very still. Fifteen years of practice said: deny, deflect, counteroffer. Her mouth opened on the old machinery. What came out instead was nothing, because she was looking at Jude.

Jude wasn't looking at her. That was the thing. Jude was looking at the middle distance, at some point down-valley where the pass was, and her expression hadn't changed at all — and somehow that was more terrible than rage. Rage would have been a door slamming. This was a door being quietly, carefully not opened.

"Jude," Cass said.

"Don't," Jude said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. Which was worse. "Not yet. Let me get to the end of the sentence first."

She turned back to the radio. "Pike."

"Ms. Joubert."

"The answer's no. For all of us. You can tell your employers the valley's founding money stays buried, or gets burned, or gets nailed to their church door — anything except handed over. And you can tell them Merrow's ledger page is worth exactly what they paid for it, because the route changed after she sold it. Ask your men about last night." A pause. "Oh. Right. They haven't reported in yet, have they."

Static. Then, without any warmth left in it at all: "You're making a mistake, diver."

"Fifteen years of practice," Jude said, and clicked the radio off.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The stove hissed. Somewhere above them the wind worked at the glacier with the patience of a locksmith.

It was Okafor who broke it, because Okafor could never stand a silence longer than a kettle takes. He set down the stove part, wiped his hands on his thighs, and said, "Okay. Let's just — let's all take a breath and be adults about this."

"Adults," Tamsin repeated. She'd turned from the cave mouth. There was snowmelt on her cheeks that might have been snowmelt. "He just — he listed us. Like invoices. He knew about my mum's *debt*, Cass."

"He knows things," Cass said. "That's his job. Knowing things and making you feel known so you'll trade. Half of what he said was research and half was theater—"

"Which half was the ledger?" Reyes asked quietly.

And there it was. Cass had faced down creditors, sheriffs, two separate men named after saints who were neither saintly nor men, and she had never once lost her footing in front of witnesses. But Reyes asked his questions the way he did everything — without force, just precision, a compass needle finding north — and she felt her footing go.

"Yes," she said. "I sold the route."

Tamsin made a sound like she'd been struck.

"Months ago. Before any of you were hired. Before I found—" Cass gestured vaguely at the journal on Jude's knee, at the whole impossible architecture of the last three weeks. "Before any of this. I took Aldous money because I needed Aldous money, and I gave them the approach because that's what I had to sell, and I told myself it didn't matter because I didn't believe any of you would actually make the crossing." She heard how it sounded even as she said it. She kept going anyway, because stopping halfway through a confession is worse than finishing one. "I was wrong about that. I've been wrong about a number of things. I'm telling you now because Pike just told you worse, and if you only ever hear it from him, you'll hear it shaped like a knife."

"It *is* shaped like a knife," Tamsin said. "You sold us. You sold *Jude*. You knew — God, you knew what the box meant to her, and you sold the road to the people who stole it."

"I knew what it meant to her," Cass said, "because I've spent thirty years wanting something that much myself. That's not an excuse. It's a diagnosis."

Okafor had put his head in his hands. "We lost supplies covering a retreat from these people. Reyes took shrapnel—" He caught himself, glanced at Reyes's bandaged shoulder. "Sorry. Poor phrasing."

"Graze," Reyes said. "But thank you."

"The point stands."

"The point stands," Cass agreed. "And I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to weigh it. Everything I've done since the shelter has been real. The confession was real. The climbing was real. Whatever else I am, I've carried this crew over ice for three weeks and I intend to carry it down again."

"That's the problem," Jude said.

Everyone turned. Jude had been silent through all of it, turning the journal slowly over in her hands, and when she looked up at Cass her eyes were dry and very clear, the way water gets right before it freezes.

"That's the problem," she said again. "Because I believe you. That's the second time in my life I've believed someone right up until the moment the bill arrived, and both times the person was you."

"Cass," Tamsin said — and Cass realized with a lurch that the girl had been talking to her, possibly for some time. "When? When did you sell it?"

"Does it matter?"

"Yes," Reyes said. "Actually. It matters enormously. Because if you sold it before you learned what was in the box, that's desperation. If you sold it after—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

Cass looked at the floor of the cave, at the trampled snow, at the scuff marks where the strongbox had sat since the ice cave, wrapped in Okafor's tarp. She thought about lying. It would have been effortless. It was always effortless, that was the whole tragedy of her, the lie arrived fully dressed and smiling before the truth had found its boots.

"After," she said.

The word dropped into the cave and cracked like lake ice.

"After I read the journal," she went on, because apparently she was doing this now, honesty, like a woman taking up a new and punishing sport late in life. "After I understood what the Aldouses actually did. That's when I called it in. Because I realized the box wasn't worth anything to me sitting in a glacier, and it wasn't worth anything burned, and the only version of it that changes anything is the version somebody powerful is afraid of. I told myself I'd use it better than they would. Everyone tells themselves that. It's practically the motto of my entire generation."

"So the confession in the storm shelter," Tamsin said slowly, "about your family, about leverage keeping you alive—"

"Was true."

"—and you were carrying the beacon frequencies in your pocket the whole time."

"Also true." Cass spread her hands. "I contain multitudes. Most of them are liabilities."

Nobody laughed. Even Okafor, whose humor was less a trait than a respiratory function, didn't laugh. Jude stood up, and the movement was unhurried, deliberate, a woman coiling rope — and Cass recognized the motion from fifteen years ago, from a dock in another life, and understood that she was watching the exact gesture that had preceded every goodbye Jude Joubert had ever said.

"Jude," she said. "Say something."

"I'm deciding whether to," Jude said. "That's new for me. Usually I just leave." She tucked the journal inside her jacket, against her chest, where Cass had noticed she'd taken to keeping it, the way other people carried letters or knives. "Here's where I am. Pike's men are a day behind us, maybe less, and the pass behind is gone, and the man on that radio just offered every one of you a clean exit, and one of us sold him the map to this exact moment. So nobody decides anything tonight. Nobody storms off. Nobody does anything brave. We sleep, we move at first light, and we decide on the descent, where decisions can actually be survived. Agreed?"

There was a general murmur that in a healthier room might have been called assent.

"Agreed," Reyes said, for all of them, because that was also something Reyes did.

Jude nodded once, picked up her pack, and crawled out toward the snow ledge where she kept her watch. The curtain of white swallowed her.

Cass sat back down by the stove. Tamsin wouldn't look at her. Okafor had resumed fixing the stove with the focused energy of a man who trusted machines more than people and was currently having that faith rewarded. Reyes met her eyes for exactly one second — not forgiveness, just acknowledgment, a bearing logged — and then went back to the radio, sweeping frequencies, listening for the next bad news.

Cass looked at the strongbox under its tarp.

It was a plain thing, iron-banded, older than any of them, dented along one edge where the glacier had chewed it. Inside it were documents that could unmake a dynasty or crown a new one, depending entirely on whose hands held them, and for thirty years Cass had believed that the only sin was letting such power sit idle. Power was a tool. Tools rusted. Someone ought to pick them up.

She had believed that right up until she'd watched Jude's face go clear and cold as the ice outside, and understood that she had done to Jude precisely what the Aldouses had done to the valley: taken something living and turned it into leverage.

The stove hissed. Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did now, winter pressing down on the range like a thumb on a scale.

Cass waited until the watch changed. She waited until Okafor's breathing went long and even, until Tamsin curled around her own knees in the far corner, until Reyes, on last watch, turned his attention outward to the dark where it belonged.

Then she got up, quiet as snowfall, and lifted the tarp.

The strongbox was heavier than it looked. Everything true was.

She paused at the cave mouth with it in her arms, and looked back once at the sleeping shapes of the only people in twenty years who had heard the whole truth of her and hadn't reached for a weapon — and she hated herself with a fluency that was, at least, honest — and then she stepped out into the storm, and the mountain closed behind her like water.

Down-valley, a single light answered the dark. She walked toward it, box in her arms, telling herself — one last time, in the old words, the comfortable words — that she was the only one strong enough to carry it.

The blizzard, which had opinions about this, said nothing, and took her.
