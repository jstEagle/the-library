## 17. Split

Okafor had spent thirty years keeping machines alive in places that wanted them dead, and he had learned one law above all others: everything breaks, and the ones who survive are the ones who plan for it. He kept a spare of every part the winch could lose. He carried two knives because one knife is a tool and two is a philosophy. And when Reyes came back from the radio call pale as a man who'd read his own obituary, Okafor did what he always did. He sat down, made tea on the little camp stove, and started counting.

The radio call had come at dusk. Pike's voice, smooth as machined steel, offering amnesty — full amnesty, supplies restored, a helicopter off the glacier if they surrendered the box within forty-eight hours. And then, almost as an afterthought, delivered like a man setting down a card he'd been holding all game: *Ask Cass Merrow how you found the route through the eastern col. She sold it to us in March.*

Nobody asked Cass anything. That was the trouble with a camp full of people who'd learned to trust each other by nearly dying together — when something like this landed, nobody knew where to put their hands.

Okafor poured four cups and set them out on the crate they used as a table, and waited, because he'd learned that too: people tell you who they are when there's nothing left to fix.

* * *

Tamsin spoke first, which surprised no one. The girl had the emotional range of an avalanche — long buildup, sudden release, everything downhill from there.

"She wouldn't." Tamsin's voice cracked on the second word. "She *wouldn't*. We've been — she pulled me out of a whiteout in Kessler Town, she—" The sentence collapsed under its own weight. "She's been teaching me navigation. Why would she teach me navigation if she was going to sell us?"

"Because knowing where you are helps her leave," Reyes said quietly, and Okafor flinched at how clean the cut was. The navigator didn't waste words. He never had. Even now, with his ribs strapped and his arm in a sling from the night Pike's men hit the camp, he spoke like someone conserving fuel for a long burn.

Cass was across the camp, pretending to check the tent lines. She was doing it badly. Okafor had watched her rig those lines a dozen times; her hands knew the work without her eyes, and tonight her eyes were anywhere but the tents. They were on Jude. On the box. On the darkening col to the south.

Jude said nothing. That frightened Okafor more than shouting would have. Jude Joubert was a woman who argued with weather; silence from her was not calm, it was pressure building behind a valve.

"We should hear her out," Okafor said, into the quiet, because somebody had to be the adult and apparently it was going to be him again. "One accusation on a radio call isn't a verdict. I've been accused of worse by better-informed men."

"You haven't," Reyes said.

"I once shorted out an entire hospital wing," Okafor said. "There were accusations."

"That was an accident."

"It was a *learning experience*, and I resent the distinction." But the joke landed flat, even for him, and he let it die. There were nights for humor and nights for arithmetic, and this was shaping up to be an arithmetic night.

Because here was his arithmetic, and he turned it over in his head while the others talked: the deal was good. It was, objectively, *good*. Amnesty. Supplies. A ride off this ice shelf before the storm that was already chewing the western ridgeline finished its approach and buried the pass until June. Four people walked away alive. Whatever was in that box stayed with the Aldouses, yes — the dynasty kept its stolen fortune, the town of Kessler kept its chains — but the crew kept their pulse rates, and Okafor had buried enough friends in enough remote places to know exactly how much a pulse rate was worth.

He was fifty-eight years old. His knees announced themselves each morning like a man clearing his throat before a speech. He had a daughter in Lagos he hadn't seen in three years and a granddaughter he'd never seen at all, and every day on this glacier was a day subtracted from the account he'd sworn he'd start repaying when this job ended.

So when the argument finally came — and it came around the stove, after dark, with the wind picking at the tent flies like a curious thief — Okafor made his case the way he made all his cases: plainly, with diagrams if necessary.

* * *

"Let me say the ugly thing first," he told them, "because somebody has to and I'm the only one here old enough not to flinch at myself."

The stove hissed. Snow ticked against the fly. Tamsin hugged her mug like it contained something more precious than chamomile, which, at altitude, it did.

"Pike's offer keeps us alive," Okafor said. "That's the whole argument. Everything else — justice, leverage, the truth about the founding fortune, whatever Cass thinks she's going to do with it — all of that is worth nothing to dead women and a dead engineer. I have done the math on this mountain. We are nine days from the coast road at our current pace, we've lost half our food, Reyes can't carry a full pack, and the forecast window closes in five. Maybe four. The deal gets us down. The mission doesn't. I have loved this mission. I have also loved engines that I eventually had to admit were beyond saving, and continuing to crank them only ever cost me more parts."

He looked around the circle. "I'm not saying it's right. I'm saying it's *survivable*, and right is a luxury that requires survivors."

Tamsin set her mug down so hard the tea jumped. "You want to give it back to them."

"I want to give *ourselves* back to ourselves," Okafor said. "The box was never ours. It belonged to your brother, Jude, and he's dead, and the Aldouses have been dead inside for a hundred years anyway. You don't rob a corpse by stealing its gravestone."

"The box has proof," Tamsin said. "Proof of murder. Proof of theft. If we hand it to Pike, we're handing the Aldouses the last evidence that they ever did anything wrong. They'll burn it. They'll burn it and the town stays asleep for another century, and everyone in Kessler keeps breathing company air and tithing to the company church and dying in company mines, and we'll be home safe and *complicit*." Her voice went up and up. "Safe and complicit! Is that the trade? Because I can't — I didn't come up here to be safe, Okafor. Nobody comes up here to be safe. Safe people stay in towns!"

"Some safe people have granddaughters," Okafor said mildly, and watched the girl's face do something complicated. Good. Let her sit with the fact that other people had things to lose. That was the beginning of wisdom, in his experience, though it usually took another twenty years to finish arriving.

"And the truth," Tamsin pressed on, undeterred, because she was nineteen and the concept of strategic retreat hadn't yet reached her. "The truth matters. It *matters*. Jude's family—"

"Is dead," Okafor said, gently, and immediately wished he hadn't, because Jude looked up then, and her eyes were terrible.

* * *

Jude had barely spoken all evening. Now she set her cup aside with the deliberate care of a woman disarming herself, and said, "Say the rest of it, Okafor. You've earned the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"There's always a rest of it with you. You think in footnotes."

He sighed. "The rest of it is Cass," he admitted. "If the radio call is true — and Reyes heard it, and Reyes doesn't hear things wrong — then Cass sold our route months ago. Before she hired any of us. Which means this entire expedition, every frozen night, every rationed meal, Reyes getting hurt covering our retreat — all of it happened inside a plan she built before we existed. And if that's true, then the question isn't whether we take Pike's deal. The question is what else she's already traded, and to whom, and whether the box is still in her possession or already spoken for." He spread his hands. "I fix machines. Broken machines at least break honestly. People lie about their own diagnostics."

Reyes nodded slowly. "The route detail bothers me most," he said. "Pike named the eastern col. Not the main pass — the *eastern* col, the one I found in March, the one I showed exactly one person." He looked at the fire, not at Cass. "I've been telling myself there's another explanation. There usually is. But I stopped finding explanations around the time he offered us a helicopter, because dynasties don't send helicopters for boxes. They send helicopters for *problems*, and they only know we're a problem if someone told them precisely what kind."

Across the fire, Cass stood very still.

And then, because she was Cass Merrow, she came into the light and sat down among them, and began to talk — smoothly, reasonably, laying out a version of events in which the route had leaked through a map dealer in Kessler Town, in which Pike was bluffing to fracture them, in which loyalty was exactly what the dynasty wanted them to doubt. She was good. Okafor gave her that. She was so good that for almost four minutes he believed her, and he could count on one hand the number of people who'd ever managed four minutes.

Then Jude said, "March," in a voice like a dropped anchor, and Cass stopped talking, and the silence afterward had a texture Okafor had only heard once before — the night a turbine let go on the *Kestrel* and everyone aboard understood, simultaneously, that the sound meant the same thing.

Cass didn't deny it. That was the thing Okafor couldn't stop thinking about later. She just sat there in the firelight with her hands open on her knees, like a woman showing she wasn't holding anything, which was either honesty or the most advanced form of lying he'd ever witnessed, and he genuinely could not tell, and neither, he suspected, could she anymore.

"I need you to understand why," Cass said finally, and her voice, for the first time since Okafor had known her, wasn't smooth. "I need you to understand what the Aldouses did to my family, and what it costs to live in their shadow for thirty years, and why a person starts to believe that the only way to survive a monster is to hold something the monster wants." She looked at Jude. "I was going to tell you. When we had it. I was going to show you everything and let you decide, because I promised myself I'd never decide *for* you. Not after what they did to yours."

"You decided for us in March," Jude said.

"Yes," Cass said. "Yes, I did."

* * *

The argument that followed lasted two hours and settled nothing, which Okafor recognized as the standard yield of arguments conducted past midnight at altitude. Tamsin cried, briefly and furiously, wiping her eyes with the efficiency of someone who resented the tears more than the grief. Reyes stated his position in four sentences — survival first, truth second, vengeance never — and then declined to elaborate, citing pain levels, which Okafor privately suspected was half true and half a diplomatic exit. Jude said almost nothing, which was somehow louder than everything.

And Okafor argued for the deal, and kept arguing for it, because that was his role tonight and someone had to hold the unpopular position or the debate collapsed into unanimity, and unanimity built on exhaustion was just panic wearing a group costume.

But somewhere in the second hour, arguing, defending, diagramming survival odds against the storm front on the back of a supply manifest, Okafor felt something shift under him — the way ice shifts, invisibly, catastrophically, a half-second before it goes.

It was this: he noticed that he had stopped talking about the crew and started talking about the *box*.

When had that happened? Somewhere between the amnesty terms and the helicopter logistics, the center of gravity of his own argument had slid. He'd begun the evening arguing that four lives outweighed one artifact, which was defensible. But by midnight he was arguing about custody. About who held it. About what Pike would pay, and what Cass would take, and whether the Aldouses' claim was stronger than Merrow's. He was negotiating, in his head, in the dark, alone — dividing power like it was salvage to be split on a dock.

And the box wasn't salvage. The box was a murdered man's testimony and a stolen family's name, and here he was, the pragmatic one, the reasonable one, mentally auctioning it off because the auction felt like control.

*Power corrupts,* he thought. *Fine, old news, everybody knows that one.* But what nobody warned you about — what thirty years of engines hadn't taught him until this exact hour — was that corruption didn't wait for you to seize power. It got into the *plans*. Into the righteous plans, especially. The moment you started scheming to take power away from bad men, you had to decide what you'd do with it, and the deciding itself changed you, quietly, before you ever touched the prize. He had watched it happen to revolutionaries and union men and one memorable bishop. Tonight he watched it happen in a tin cup of tea, in his own hands, on a glacier, to himself.

He looked at Tamsin, nineteen years old and burning alive with the conviction that truth was a load-bearing wall. He looked at Reyes, who wanted nothing except to get everyone down the mountain alive and meant it with his whole scarred body. He looked at Jude, silent, carrying more weight than any of them could see the bottom of.

And he thought: *every one of us is right, and that's the disaster.* Survival was right. Truth was right. Justice was right. And they could not all fit through the same closing window, and the choosing was going to break something no rope could rig back together.

Around one in the morning, the argument burned down to embers. Tamsin crawled into her tent mid-sentence, still talking, the way children fall asleep. Reyes took first watch without being asked, moving carefully around his ribs. Jude sat by the dead fire a long time, feeding it sticks she didn't seem to notice she was holding.

Okafor checked the generator, checked the winch anchors, checked the cache lines — his nightly liturgy, the ritual that kept the world feeling like a machine instead of a gamble. Then he lay down in his tent and listened to the wind reorganize itself for the storm, and did not sleep.

* * *

He woke at some black, unscheduled hour to a sound he catalogued automatically, professionally, the way he catalogued all sounds: boots on packed snow, moving away from camp. Two sets. Light tread. Someone who knew how to walk quietly and someone walking beside them.

He lay still and counted. Thirty seconds. A minute. The sound faded toward the southern col — toward the eastern col, toward the route only Reyes had found and only one other person had been shown.

He should wake the camp. That was procedure. That was obviously procedure.

Instead he lay there for ninety full seconds, staring at the roof of his tent, running the calculation one final time. If it was Cass, and if it was the box, then the choice he'd been arguing for all night — the deal, the survival, the sane surrender of an unwinnable position — had just been made for them, unilaterally, by the one person none of them trusted anymore. And a small, cold, shameful part of him, the part that had done the arithmetic at dusk, noticed that the outcome matched his recommendation, and wondered, with genuine horror, whether that made him complicit or merely correct, and whether there was any longer a difference.

Then the better part of him won, the way it usually did, late and at cost. He rolled out of the bag, boots on, headlamp low.

By the time he had Jude awake — and waking Jude Joubert was like waking a bear that had already decided to be angry — the tracks were twenty minutes cold and filling with snow, and the box was gone from the cache line, and so was Cass's pack, and so, Okafor noted with the grim precision of his profession, was the emergency beacon.

Jude stood at the edge of camp looking down the col, and the wind came up out of the south carrying the first taste of the real storm, and she said one word, quietly, in a voice Okafor had never heard from her:

"Gone."

He got the stove lit. Somebody had to make tea. Somebody always had to make tea.

And then, over the hiss of the burner, his radio crackled once — a single burst, signal-weak, coming from high on the southern ridge, from somewhere above the cloud line that was even now swallowing the mountain.

A beacon transmission. Short. Addressed, by its frequency, to exactly one recipient.

Edran Pike was already climbing.
