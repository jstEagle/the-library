## 18. The Summit Chase

The storm did not come down the mountain. It came *up* it, which was wrong, which was the mountain's way of telling you it had opinions about your plans.

Jude felt it first as a pressure in her ears, then as a wind that arrived from below like a tide reversing, and by the time she'd clipped her axe to her harness and told Okafor — who was still holding two cups of tea with the expression of a man who had not been listened to — that she was going up, the cloud had eaten the southern ridge whole.

"You can't climb into that," Okafor said. "Nobody climbs into that."

"Then nobody will be up there," Jude said, "and I'll have climbed into nothing."

"That's not — that is not how weather works, Jude—"

But Tamsin was already at the gear line, pulling rope without being asked, because Tamsin had learned somewhere in the last three weeks that when Jude Joubert went quiet in a particular way, argument was a door you knocked on politely while she climbed through the window. Reyes stood too, favoring his bandaged side, and said only: "I'll watch the radio. If the beacon moves, I'll call it."

"It won't move," Jude said. "Cass doesn't move once she's decided where to stand. That's the whole problem with Cass."

She took the lightest rack, the short rope, four hours of food she would probably not eat. At the edge of camp she stopped, and looked back at the three of them standing in the wind like people seeing off a ferry they weren't sure would make the crossing, and something in her chest did a thing she didn't have a name for.

"Fifteen years," she said, "I've been climbing away from things. This is the first time I've ever climbed toward one."

Tamsin grinned. Okafor shook his head slowly, the way he did at engines that were going to run anyway. And Jude turned into the white and started up.

* * *

Cass Merrow had been on the summit shelf for forty minutes, and in that time she had done three things: opened the strongbox, read everything inside it twice, and lit the beacon.

The beacon was the part she kept looking at. A small brass-cased transmitter, Pike's own design, humming faintly against the snow, its antenna throwing a thread of signal south through the storm to a man who was, right now, climbing toward her with thirty hired men and a contract for her soul that she had signed months ago in a warm room because she'd been cold and tired and certain the world ran on leverage and always would.

*You sold them out before you ever loved them,* she thought. *That's the order of operations. That's always been the order.*

The box sat open at her feet. Inside: ledgers. Letters in a founder's hand so confident it looked like handwriting had been invented specifically for him. The 1891 agreement between Elias Aldous and the Kessler family — the Jouberts — trading mineral rights for a debt that had never existed, witnessed by three men whose names were now street names in Kessler Town. And beneath it all, the letter from Ilsa Voss, twenty years old, confirming receipt of payment for silence.

Cass had spent her whole life believing that documents were the only honest things in the world. Paper didn't flatter you. Paper didn't say *this time will be different.* Paper just lay there and told you what people had actually done.

So why did the paper feel heavier than the box?

Because of Tamsin's face, that was why. Because of Reyes bleeding and saying *it's fine, it's fine.* Because of Okafor making tea at one in the morning because somebody always had to. Because of Jude standing in the storm-shelter doorway after the confession, after everything, and not leaving. Not leaving. Cass had built an entire architecture of survival on the assumption that everyone leaves, and Jude Joubert had stood there in the lamplight with her brother's journal in her hands and stayed.

And Cass had taken the box anyway.

She heard the crunch of boots on ice and did not turn around. She'd known for the last hour, watching the slope below, that someone was coming. Only one person would climb into this. Only one person alive was stupid enough and stubborn enough and — there was no third word, and that absence was the whole truth of it.

"You're late," Cass said.

Jude came over the lip of the shelf with ice in her eyebrows and blood on her knuckles where the rock had taken its toll, and she looked — Cass noted this with the dispassion of long habit — terrible, and magnificent, like something the mountain had tried to keep and failed.

"I stopped to enjoy the view," Jude said.

"There's no view. There's a wall of white and us."

"Exactly my taste in views."

They faced each other across six feet of summit snow and fifteen years of history, and the wind screamed past them both like it had money on the outcome.

* * *

Jude's lungs burned. Four thousand feet of vertical in a rising storm, at altitude, on adrenaline and half a biscuit — her body was presenting her with an itemized bill and she intended to ignore it until the account was settled.

The box was open. That was the first thing she saw, and it landed in her like a stone dropped down a well: Cass had already read it all. Cass knew about the ledgers, the forged debt, Voss's silence. Cass knew everything Jude knew, plus the thing Cass always knew, which was exactly how much anything was worth.

The beacon hummed between them.

"You called him," Jude said. Not a question. Her voice came out flat and strange, stripped by the wind of everything except structure.

"I called him weeks ago," Cass said. "Tonight I just reminded him where to stand."

"Why?"

"Because if I don't hand it over, his men take it, and they take it rough, and we're all dead on this mountain instead of just cold on it." Cass's jaw was set in the old way, the dockside way, the way Jude had hated across a hundred harbors. "This way I negotiate. This way I walk away. This way everybody lives."

"Everybody lives," Jude repeated. "That's new vocabulary for you."

"Don't." Something flickered across Cass's face, fast as a fish under ice. "Don't do that. Don't stand there and judge me like you wouldn't have done the same."

"I did do the same." Jude stepped closer. The wind shoved at her and she let it shove and kept her feet. "Fifteen years ago I gave my brother to the Aldouses because I thought I was saving him. I traded a person for a plan. So don't tell me I don't know the arithmetic, Cass. I know it better than anyone breathing. And I'm telling you it doesn't balance. It never balances. You put a life on one side and a plan on the other and the scale just laughs at you."

"This isn't a life. It's paper."

"It's my family."

"It's *leverage*," Cass said, and her voice cracked on the word, split open like river ice, and underneath the crack was something raw and old and drowning. "You think I don't know what it is? My father worked Aldous Number Three for nineteen years and died owing the company store eleven dollars, and they charged my mother for his lamp. For his *lamp*, Jude. They billed her for the light. I was nine years old and I swore that I would never again be the person holding the invoice. Never. Whatever it cost. Whoever it cost." She looked down at the box. "And now I'm holding the invoice, and it turns out I've become the company."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat through wool, the radio in Jude's pocket crackled — Reyes, checking in, checking on her, and she couldn't hear the words, only the fact of them, and the fact was enough.

"Cass," Jude said quietly. "Look at me."

Cass looked at her. And there it was, the thing neither of them had ever said across all those years of harbors and rivalries and jobs almost shared: they were the same animal. Two girls from stolen families, raised by the same hard teacher, who had both concluded that the world was a ledger and safety was a balance you held by holding something over someone else. Jude had run from it. Cass had armed herself with it. But it was the same hunger, the same wound, wearing different coats.

"I used to hate you," Jude said. "Every harbor, every job, every time you outbid me or outfought me or out-*lied* me. Fifteen years of hating you, and do you know what I finally figured out on this mountain? I wasn't hating you. I was hating the mirror."

"Don't you dare psychoanalyze me at fourteen thousand feet."

"Somebody has to. You picked the venue."

And Cass laughed — a short, broken, furious laugh, startled out of her like a bird flushed from cover — and in the laughing her shoulders dropped half an inch, which on Cass Merrow was a confession the size of the ledgers in the box.

"Pike's men are six hours behind me," Jude said. "Maybe five. When they get here, what's your negotiation worth? You think a man like Pike honors contracts on summits? He brought thirty men and a storm is covering his approach. He doesn't need to buy the box, Cass. He just needs to arrive."

Cass said nothing. But her eyes went to the beacon, and Jude watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic, always the same arithmetic — and this time, for the first time in Cass Merrow's calculating life, the numbers refused to come out in her favor.

"He'll want proof of death," Cass said at last, very quietly. "If I turn on him. He'll want something."

"He'll want ash," Jude said. "Ash is easy to provide."

* * *

Cass looked at the box and saw, laid out in her mind with terrible clarity, the two futures.

In one, Pike arrives. She hands over the ledgers, collects her amnesty, descends the mountain rich in protection and poor in everything else, and spends the rest of her life in Kessler Town being the dynasty's well-paid knife, walking streets where Tamsin will not meet her eye and Okafor will make tea and not offer her a cup. She has seen that future before. She has *lived* in that future. It has excellent heating and no windows.

In the other, there is fire, and loss, and a woman with ice in her eyebrows who climbed four thousand feet into a storm not for the box but for *her* — and Cass could not, would not, examine that sentence too closely, because if she examined it she would have to act on it, and acting on it meant burning the only shield she had ever built.

"You understand what I'm losing," Cass said. "Tell me you understand that."

"I'm losing it too," Jude said. "My family's name is in that box. My brother died carrying it. Every reason I came up this mountain says burn it and every scar I own says keep it, and I am choosing the fire, Cass, and it is costing me more than you will ever be asked to pay. So yes. I understand. Get your lighter."

Cass knelt at the box. Her hands — steady through boardrooms and betrayals, steady through every transaction of her adult life — were shaking.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"The journal stays with you. The rest burns. All of it. Even the parts that hurt *them*. Especially those." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and defiant about being wet. "If we keep even one page, we're not ending it. We're just moving the vault."

Jude crouched beside her. Below them, far below, the storm was full of movement — lights maybe, or imagination, torches or stars or Pike's column snaking up the southern ridge. Time was a closing fist.

"Together?" Cass asked. And it was the strangest word either of them had spoken on that summit, stranger than any confession, because it was not a transaction.

"Together," Jude said.

They fed the ledgers to the flame piece by piece, the founder's confident handwriting curling and going black, the forged debt of 1891 becoming smoke, Voss's confession of silence becoming heat and then nothing, ninety years of stolen fortune dissolving upward into a sky that had never once cared about it. The beacon Cass smashed with an axe butt, three blows, methodical, until the humming stopped.

And when it was done there was nothing left but a scorch mark on the snow and a small tin box, empty, and two women kneeling above the clouds holding nothing at all between them except the decision they'd just made.

"Well," Cass said hoarsely. "Now we're just two broke women on a mountain with a killer coming up the ridge."

"Now we're just two broke women on a mountain," Jude corrected, "with a *crew* coming up behind us, and Reyes navigating, and Okafor's stove, and Tamsin's rope work." She stood, and pulled Cass up, and the pull was firm and unforgiving and did not let go afterward. "Pike has thirty men and a grudge. We have something better."

"What's that?"

"We have nowhere left to hide and nothing left to lose, and that, Cass Merrow, is the most dangerous armament on this mountain."

Below them, out of the white, the first torchlight resolved — not one light but many, a slow ascending constellation, and leading it, unmistakable even at distance by the unhurried certainty of its climb, the man himself.

Edran Pike had reached the summit ridge.

And found nothing waiting but ash, and wind, and two silhouettes standing shoulder to shoulder against the storm.
