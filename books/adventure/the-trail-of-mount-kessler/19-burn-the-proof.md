## 19. Burn the Proof

The storm broke at eleven thousand feet, the way storms do — all at once, like a door slamming.

One moment Jude was climbing through white chaos, rope biting her palms, the world reduced to the six feet of mountain she could see and hate; the next, she hauled herself over a shoulder of granite and the wind simply stopped, as if the mountain had decided she'd suffered enough for one day. Above her the sky opened into a blue so clean it looked manufactured. Below her, clouds rolled out to every horizon like a second sea, silver-gray and endless, and she stood on an island of black rock poking through them.

She had spent fifteen years on actual seas. She knew what it was to be surrounded by nothing. But this was different. This was standing *on* the nothing.

"Jude."

Cass Merrow sat twenty feet up the ridge, back against a boulder, the strongbox open in her lap. Her climbing shell was torn at the shoulder and crusted with ice where blood had frozen and thawed and frozen again. The beacon beside her blinked its small red heart, patient as a metronome, telling Edran Pike exactly where they were.

Jude's legs were shaking. She let them shake. No point pretending otherwise this high up; the mountain already knew.

"You followed me," Cass said. "Through that. In the dark. You're either the bravest person I've ever met or the stupidest."

"Fifty-fifty," Jude said. "Ask anyone who's known me longer than a week." She picked her way up the ridge, testing each hold the way she tested everything — weight first, then trust. "You cut the rope line at camp. Took the box. Left Tamsin's spare stove so we wouldn't freeze, which was decent of you, in a thieving sort of way."

"I'm a complicated woman."

"You're a thief with a conscience. I've dived with worse."

Cass almost smiled. Then she looked down at the box, and the smile went wherever smiles go when there's no work left for them.

Fifteen years of rivalry sat between them on that ridge. Dockside arguments, contested contracts, the long cold silence after Captain Voss died and neither of them cried where the other could see. And underneath all of it, older than any of it: two girls from the same valley who had both lost their families to the Aldous name, and who had each decided, separately, in the dark of their own heads, that the only way to survive losing everything was to never need anything again.

Jude sat down across from her. Deliberately. Making herself comfortable on frozen rock because sitting down meant she wasn't leaving, and she wanted Cass to understand that.

"Open it all the way," Jude said.

"It is open."

"Then show me."

Cass hesitated — and that hesitation told Jude more than anything else on this whole miserable mountain. Cass Merrow did not hesitate. Cass Merrow barged, bluffed, charmed, and stole. Hesitation meant shame, and shame meant that whatever was in that box, Cass hadn't fully let herself look at it yet either.

She lifted the lid back and tilted it toward the light.

Documents. Bundles of them, oilcloth-wrapped, dry as bone after sixty years in the ice. Ledgers with the Kessler Mining Company's stamp. Letters in a copperplate hand. A deed of transfer signed by Ezekiel Aldous himself, his signature fat and confident as a man signing for a horse he'd already stolen. And beneath it all, thin as a wafer, the original survey claim — filed not by any Aldous but by a name Jude had only ever heard in fragments, in her mother's lowered voice, in the pauses where family stories used to be:

*Joubert.*

Her own name. Sitting in a box on top of the world.

"There it is," Cass said quietly. "Sixty years of theft, itemized. Enough to hang the whole dynasty. Enough to take the valley back." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and hungry and terribly tired. "Enough to make them beg us. You and me, Jude. We walk down that mountain holding their throats in our hands, and they'll give us anything. Anything. Land, money, the town itself if we want it. They'll spend the rest of their lives paying us to stay quiet, and we'll spend the rest of ours deciding whether they've paid enough."

The beacon blinked. Red, red, red.

"That's what Pike's coming for," Jude said.

"Pike's coming for what he thinks he can buy. He doesn't understand yet that this isn't merchandise." Cass touched the papers the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "This is a lever long enough to move the whole valley. My grandfather worked their mines until the dust ate his lungs. My mother scrubbed their floors. I have spent forty years being *small* to these people, Jude, and I am done. Done. This—" she pressed her palm flat against the documents "—this is the last time anyone in my family is ever small again."

The wind had started up again at the edges, moaning around the boulders, but up here it was almost gentle, almost conversational. Jude found she was cold in a distant way, the way you're cold in dreams.

"My brother carried this," she said. "Up here. Alone. He was nineteen."

"I know."

"He hid it instead of using it. He could have walked into Kessler Town and burned the Aldouses' whole world down with this, and instead he scratched a map into the inside of a wreck and put it in a hole in a glacier." She shook her head slowly. "I used to think he didn't use it because he was scared. Or because he didn't understand what he had."

"And now?"

"Now I think he understood exactly what he had, and that's why he couldn't do it." Jude looked out at the cloud-sea. Somewhere under all that white was the range, the passes, the wreck of the Kessler Star, the little storm shelter where Reyes was probably swearing at his splinted ribs and Okafor was probably reorganizing the remaining supplies for the fourth time because organizing things was how Okafor prayed. Somewhere down there was Tamsin, young and stubborn and alive because of a rigging job that had gone wrong before it went right. "He knew that once you pick up a weapon like this, you don't get to put it down. You just become the next person pointing it."

"So we don't point it," Cass said. "We *sell* it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." Cass's voice hardened. "The difference is which side of the ledger you're on."

* * *

They argued for a long time. Jude would remember that afterward — not the burning, which took four minutes, but the argument, which took an hour, and which neither of them won.

Cass laid it out the way she laid out contracts: precise, reasonable, relentless. The Aldouses were not going to reform out of guilt. Power didn't evaporate because you felt bad about it; power only moved. If good people refused to hold leverage, then only bad people ever held it, and the valley got exactly the masters it deserved. She'd seen it. She'd *lived* it. Every year the dynasty tightened another screw — the scrip, the sheriff, the church with the company logo stitched into the altar cloth — and every year some idealist announced that fighting dirty made you no better than them, and every year that idealist's surrender bought the Aldouses another decade.

"You want to be clean," Cass said. "Clean is a luxury. I can't afford it. My dead can't afford it."

And Jude — Jude heard all of it, and most of it was true, and that was the trap. Because the answer wasn't to argue the facts. The facts were on Cass's side. The answer was the thing her brother had understood alone on this same ridge with the same box in his hands, the thing he'd written about in the journal now zipped inside Jude's jacket, against her heart, the one thing she was keeping:

*If the only proof that we were wronged is worth more than the wrong itself, then they've already won. They've taught us to price ourselves.*

You couldn't blackmail your way back to belonging. That was the whole disease, right there. The fortune had been stolen by men who believed everything had a price, and every plan to take it back by force or by leverage was just the same belief wearing a different coat. Change the hands, change the names — the grip stayed the same. Cass wanted justice and thought leverage was how you got it, and the tragedy of Cass Merrow was that she genuinely couldn't see those were opposite directions.

But Jude also knew — God help her, she knew — that she couldn't say that from above. Not from the moral high ground, which on this mountain was literal and freezing and insufferable. So she said it from the low ground instead. From the truth.

"I told them where my brother would be," Jude said.

Cass went still.

"The night he disappeared. I was seventeen. Ilsa had a plan to move him out of the valley, and I thought — I *knew*, the way seventeen-year-olds know things — that Ilsa was going to hurt him. So I went to the Aldouse office and I told Pike's predecessor where Tam would be crossing the river, because I thought the company would protect him. Keep him safe. Talk sense into him." Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "They went to arrest him. He ran. Into the range. In October." She gestured at the clouds below them, at all that killing white. "That's what leverage does, Cass. That's what it did to me. I held a piece of information over my own brother's head without even knowing I was doing it, and it cost him everything, and it cost me fifteen years of miles. Fifteen years of never staying anywhere long enough for anyone to know what I'd done." She laughed, short and joyless. "You know what I figured out on this mountain? Being unknown isn't freedom. It's just exile with better scenery."

The wind gusted. The beacon blinked. Down in the clouds, faintly, came a sound that might have been voices or might have been wind, and both women pretended not to hear it, because they weren't finished.

"If you burn it," Cass said finally, and her voice had gone very quiet, very controlled, the voice of a woman standing at the edge of something, "then the Aldouses win. Forever. Nobody ever knows. Ezekiel steals a valley and dies rich in his bed and history calls him a founder."

"Somebody knows," Jude said. She touched her jacket, over the journal. "I know. The crew knows. Tamsin will tell people. Stories erode them, Cass. Slowly, generation after generation, the way water does. It's not a lever. It's slower than that. But it doesn't change hands, and it doesn't run out, and nobody can buy it off us." She paused. "Also, honestly? Ezekiel Aldous dying rich in his bed while everyone slowly finds out what he was — that's funnier. Leverage gets you paid. Truth gets you *remembered*. I know which one I'd rather do to that man's grave."

Something flickered across Cass's face — anger, grief, and underneath them both, horribly, reluctantly, recognition. The look of a woman seeing her own hunger from the outside for the first time.

"You'd really do it," Cass said. "You'd burn sixty years of proof. Your own family's name. Everything you climbed this mountain for."

"I climbed this mountain for my brother," Jude said. "And he's right here." She touched the journal again. "Everything else is just paper."

* * *

It was Cass who struck the match, in the end. That surprised them both.

Later — much later, years later, on docks and in kitchens, whenever the story got told — Jude would say she wrestled the box away and burned it herself, because that was cleaner and it protected Cass, and Cass would roll her eyes and say nothing, which was its own kind of gift. But the truth was that after one last long silence, Cass had looked at the beacon blinking its red heartbeat toward Pike's men, and at the papers, and at Jude, and had said, in a voice sanded down to bare wood:

"He really wrote that? About pricing yourself?"

"Word for word."

Cass nodded once. Then she struck the match off the box's edge, and held it out, and said, "Together, then. I'm not letting you take all the credit for being noble. I've been noble for entire minutes at a time in my life."

They fed the fire together.

The oilcloth caught first, flaring green-blue, and then the ledgers, the copperplate letters curling like leaves, the great confident signature of Ezekiel Aldous browning, blistering, gone. The deed of transfer burned slowest, as if sixty years of stolen weight resisted even now, and Jude held it at the corner and watched her family's name glow orange and then black and then lift off the page entirely as ash, rising on the thermal, scattering over the cloud-sea, drifting down toward the valley where it had always belonged.

It should have felt like loss. Some part of her braced for it — the grief of the diver watching something valuable sink beyond reach. But what rose in her chest instead was lighter than grief, stranger than relief. It was the feeling of setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying. Fifteen years of miles, and the whole time the heaviest thing she'd been hauling was the *maybe* — maybe someday, somewhere, the proof would surface, and then she'd finally be allowed to stop running and start collecting. Burn the maybe, and all that remained was what was true: her brother had lived, and fought, and forgiven her in ink before she'd ever confessed aloud. The crew was below her in the storm, waiting. Her name was her own.

Liberation and loss in the same breath, exactly as promised. Mostly liberation. A little loss. The loss smelled like smoke.

"Four minutes," Cass said, watching the last of it. "Sixty years of dynasty-ending leverage, four minutes of fire. There's a lesson in efficiency there."

"Don't let Okafor hear you say that. He'll build a spreadsheet."

Cass laughed — a real laugh, cracked and startled out of her, the first one Jude had heard from her since they were girls throwing rocks at the company reservoir signs. It echoed off the rocks and died in the blue.

Then Cass kicked the beacon off the ridge.

It tumbled, blinking, into the clouds, and the red winked out, and the summit was suddenly just rock and sky and two battered women and the smell of burnt paper.

"They'll still come," Cass said. "Pike doesn't need the beacon anymore. He knows the route. He knows we're up here. Ash is evidence too, Jude — he'll find the fire ring, and he'll know what we did, and he is going to be *furious*." She squinted down at the boiling white below them. "And we've got maybe an hour of light, a storm coming back in behind us, and a descent that nearly killed Tamsin in daylight."

Jude pulled the journal tighter inside her jacket and stood, knees cracking, and looked down into the clouds where the voices — real voices now, no mistaking them — were getting closer.

"Then we'd better start lying to him convincingly," she said. "It's your specialty."

Below them, out of the white, the first lantern of Pike's party swung into view.
