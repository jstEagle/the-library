## 20. Descent

Cass Merrow had negotiated her way out of worse situations than this — she'd once talked a customs officer out of confiscating an entire boatload of contraband tea by complimenting his mother's jam recipe — but she had never done it at fourteen thousand feet, in a whiteout, standing over a box of ashes.

The beacon was still lit. That was the first problem. She'd watched Jude strike the match, watched forty years of leverage curl and vanish into a sky that couldn't tell the difference between burning paper and burning snow, and in all that chaos neither of them had thought to kill the transmitter. It pulsed away on its little tripod like a lighthouse for the damned, and somewhere below it, Edran Pike was climbing toward them with six men and a grudge.

"Jude," Cass said.

Jude was still looking at the fire pit, where the last corner of a document — she could see the Aldous crest, half-eaten by flame — had gone to gray flake. Her face did something Cass had never seen it do in twenty years of rivalry: it went quiet. Not blank. Quiet, like a room after everyone finally stops shouting.

"Jude."

"I heard you." Jude crouched and pulled the beacon's power cell without looking at it, the way other people swatted flies. "I'm deciding what to say to Pike when he gets here. I've narrowed it down to nothing or something with a lot of swearing in it."

"He'll have guns."

"He always has guns. He's a man whose entire personality is a holster." Jude stood, and Cass watched her take inventory of the summit the way she took inventory of everything — hulls, ropes, people. "You could still run. Storm's coming up from the west col. He won't see you go."

"I know," Cass said. "That's rather the point of me."

Jude looked at her then, really looked, and whatever she saw made her mouth do that thing — not quite a smile, not quite forgiveness, something parked between them like a truck with its hazards on. "You sold us out in spring. Before we ever left the coast."

"Yes."

"And you came up here planning to hand him the box and buy your way back into whatever you lost."

"Yes," Cass said again, because lying now would be like spitting into the wind she was about to walk into. "And then I sat here holding the thing every nightmare I've had since I was nineteen has been about, and it felt like holding a dead man's wallet. Heavy and warm and not his anymore, and none of my business either." She paused. "Also Jude burned it, which simplified matters considerably."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped Jude — one syllable, quickly recaptured. "Don't make me forgive you yet. I'm not finished being furious."

"Take your time. We have perhaps twenty minutes before Pike arrives to ruin the mood permanently."

* * *

The storm arrived first.

It came up the western face the way Tamsin had predicted it would back at the high col — fast, vertical, personal — and within ten minutes the summit was a gray room with no walls. Cass had spent enough years in mountains to respect weather the way she respected creditors: completely, and only when it was too late. She got the tent's remains rigged against a rock fin while Jude anchored the rope line down toward the eastern chute, the one route off this peak that didn't require wings.

"Pike's men will come up the standard route," Jude shouted over the wind. "South ridge. It's the only sane approach in this."

"So we let them arrive, and then what? We're two women, a box of ash, and my considerable charm."

"You forget who else is on this mountain."

Cass hadn't forgotten. She'd been carefully not thinking about Okafor, Reyes, and Tamsin since the night she'd walked out of camp with the strongbox under her arm like a thief in a morality play. Reyes had seen her go. He'd been on watch, and he'd watched her go, and he'd said nothing — which in Reyes's language was either forgiveness or a very long receipt he intended to present later.

"They'll come," she said, surprising herself with how much she wanted it to be true.

"They'll come," Jude said, and there was no doubt in it at all, and Cass understood suddenly why people followed this woman into frozen wrecks and dynastic feuds. Not because Jude Joubert was certain. Because she made certainty look easy enough to borrow.

* * *

Pike's men reached the summit forty minutes later, roped in pairs, looking like six identical grievances in matching parkas. Pike himself came last, which told Cass everything: a man who led from behind was a man who intended to survive whatever happened to the people in front.

He stamped snow off his boots with the delicacy of a man entering a drawing room, which would have been funnier if Cass's hands weren't shaking inside her mittens.

"Ladies." His voice carried beautifully even through wind; she'd forgotten how much practice he must have had, delivering bad news in loud rooms. "I confess the beacon brought me hoping for better news than this." He nudged the fire pit with his boot toe. Ash scattered. Somewhere in it, the founding fortune of Kessler Valley became indistinguishable from somebody's old campfire.

"The box is empty," Cass said. "Has been since the ice cave. The documents were never in it."

Pike smiled. It was a good smile, maintained annually like a yacht. "Ms. Merrow. You were always the reasonable one. Let's not perform for each other — we've both read the same ledger. The Aldous family will pay generously for silence about certain historical matters, and punish expensively for noise. Those are the only two products I sell." He turned to Jude. "You I understand less. You have nothing. You came from nothing. What exactly did you imagine burning those papers would win you?"

Cass watched Jude consider the question honestly, the way she considered everything, and braced herself.

"Nothing," Jude said. "That's the whole point. It wins nothing. It just means nobody gets to own it." She shrugged, and the shrug was so genuinely unbothered that one of Pike's men actually laughed before catching himself. "Your employers built a town on a theft and called it heritage. I was going to sell you the receipt. Now there isn't one. Go home."

"There are six of us," Pike said pleasantly, "and two of you, and a storm, and I have radios."

"You have a radio," said a new voice, "and I have a crevasse field and excellent hearing."

Tamsin came over the south ridge with a coil of rope over one shoulder and an expression of such radiant menace that Cass nearly applauded. Behind her, moving with the unhurried economy of men who had decided the fight was already over, came Reyes and Okafor. Okafor was carrying the big pry bar he used on wreck plates, which was not technically a weapon but had ended several arguments anyway.

"Four," Tamsin corrected, arriving at Jude's side. "Sorry we're late. Somebody's rope anchors were a nightmare to follow. No offense."

"Some taken," Jude said.

Pike assessed the situation with visible recalibration. Cass knew that look intimately — she'd worn it herself across a hundred negotiating tables when the numbers stopped cooperating. Six professionals against four exhausted climbers on a mountaintop in a building blizzard was mathematically fine and practically insane, and Pike was many things but not stupid.

"Ms. Merrow," he said, turning to her with the smile reinstalled. "Last chance at the generous product. Walk down with us. Your debts vanish. Your name comes home. Whatever you think these people can offer you—"

"They offered me nothing," Cass said. "That's rather the appeal."

She meant it, and saying it out loud felt like setting down luggage she'd carried so long she'd grown a hump. Nineteen years old, family farm seized over a scrip debt that had been engineered like a bridge, and she'd learned the lesson the valley taught everyone: what you can't protect, you steal the blueprint to. Leverage had kept her alive. It had also kept her alone, and alone turned out to be a very expensive way to live.

Pike studied her for a long moment, and something almost human crossed his face — recognition, maybe, of a fellow professional reading a market that had crashed. "You understand," he said, "that I still have to report something."

"Report the truth," Jude said. "The documents burned. There's nothing to buy and nothing to bury. Tell them their secret is safe forever, because it's worthless. That's the one outcome money can't fix."

For the first time, Edran Pike looked tired. "They won't believe it."

"No," Cass agreed cheerfully. "They'll spend years and fortunes trying to find out what we kept back. Paranoia is the gift that keeps giving. Consider it our parting invoice."

* * *

The turn-back, when it came, wasn't dramatic. That was the strange part. Pike weighed the storm, the numbers, the futility, and did the arithmetic that had kept him employed for thirty years: a firefight on a summit gained him a box of ash and possibly a corpse he'd have to explain. He gave the order with the same smooth courtesy he'd used at dinner in Kessler Town, and his men turned downhill into the white like iron filings following a magnet.

But at the ridge edge he stopped, and looked back at Cass, and said, quietly enough that only she heard it:

"They'll want you handled, Merrow. You know too much and you've chosen the wrong side twice now. I can slow that down. For old times' sake, I'll slow it down. But I can't stop it."

"I know," Cass said.

"That's it? No counteroffer?"

"What would I counter with? My charm?" She smiled at him, and it cost her more than the climb had. "Go home, Edran. Beat the storm."

He went. And Cass stood watching until the white swallowed the whole rope team, feeling the strangest sensation in her chest, which after some consideration she identified as terror — clean, honest terror, the kind that comes from having finally run out of things to trade.

* * *

They descended as a crew of five, which was four more than Cass had any right to.

Okafor set the pace, complaining about it professionally the entire way ("If anyone asks, I want it noted that I opposed this entire expedition from the dock, and also that I'm starving"). Reyes moved like water finding its level, checking anchors, saying nothing, occasionally glancing at Cass with that long receipt still folded behind his eyes. Tamsin floated between rope teams chattering about the burn — "Did you SEE it? She just — whoosh — forty years of dynasty, gone, I'm going to do that to my student loans" — and generally behaving as though nearly dying in a crevasse last week had been a spa treatment.

And Jude climbed ahead of her, steady as a metronome, and Cass watched her back and thought about the word *descent*, and how everyone assumed it meant falling.

Halfway down, at the shelter rocks where they'd waited out the worst of the wind, Reyes finally spoke to her.

"You could have gone with them," he said. Not accusatory. Just placing the fact on the table between them, the way he placed everything.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Cass looked at her hands. Rope-burned, frost-nipped, fifty-one years old, and for the first time in decades not holding anything. "Because a woman burned forty years of my life's work on a mountaintop and called it freedom," she said, "and I wanted to see what that felt like from the inside instead of the outside."

Reyes considered this with the gravity he gave all navigation problems. Then he handed her the thermos — the good coffee, the hoarded coffee, the coffee he guarded like state secrets — and said, "Drink. You'll need it. Lower glacier's a mess in fresh snow."

From Reyes, it was absolution. Not the full kind — the receipt stayed filed, she could feel it — but a payment plan. She drank the coffee and found her eyes stinging and blamed the wind, thoroughly and out loud, until Tamsin mercifully started singing something obscene about a mule.

* * *

They made the tree line at dusk, and camped under the first honest pines Cass had seen in weeks, and it was there, over a fire that smelled like actual wood instead of fuel tablets, that Jude sat down beside her with two tin cups and the last of Okafor's contraband whiskey.

"For the record," Jude said, "I haven't forgiven you."

"Noted."

"But you turned Pike around up there. He had numbers and you talked him down a mountain. That's the most useful thing anyone's done on this trip, including my rigging."

"Flattery. From Jude Joubert. Write the date down, someone."

"I mean it." Jude looked into the fire. "In Kessler Town, tomorrow — they're going to ask questions. The town. About what we found, what we burned. And there's no paper trail anymore, no proof, no leverage. All that's left is telling the truth out loud, in front of everyone, with nothing to gain from it." She glanced sideways. "You could still slip away tonight. Nobody would blame you. Well — Okafor would bill you."

Cass laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been rusted shut since 1971. "And stand in the square and say my family's name out loud for the first time in thirty years, with no angle, no exit, no price attached?"

"Terrifying, isn't it."

"Utterly." She drained her cup and held it out for more, and when Jude refilled it, their hands didn't flinch apart the way they had for twenty years. "All right, Joubert. One truth, freely given, no leverage involved: I'm coming down that mountain with you. Into the square, into whatever mess follows. Consequences and all."

"Consequences and all," Jude agreed, and touched her cup to Cass's with a small ceramic click that sounded, in the enormous quiet under the pines, like a door opening.

Above them, far up in the dark, the storm kept falling on the summit where the fortune had burned — erasing the fire pit, the beacon tripod, the boot prints, every trace — and below them, three days down, Kessler Town lay waiting with its company church and company sheriff and its long-held breath.

Tomorrow they would walk into it with nothing but the truth.

Cass found, to her astonishment, that she couldn't wait.
