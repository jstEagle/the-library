## 21. Homecoming, Unwanted

Tamsin had imagined this moment a hundred times on the descent: the crew walking into Kessler Town with the truth in their pockets like a lit fuse, the whole valley rising up, the Aldous name cracking down the middle like lake ice in spring.

What actually happened was that they arrived on a Tuesday, smelling of woodsmoke and each other's unwashed gear, and nobody noticed at all.

The town was busy. That was the first surprise. She'd expected Kessler to be cowed, gray, holding its breath — but there were wagons in the square and kids chasing a dog between them and the bakery queue spilling out onto the boardwalk, because it was nearly winter and nearly winter meant everyone buying flour like the snow itself might eat it.

"They don't know," she said.

"Course they don't know," Okafor said. "We haven't told them anything yet."

"I thought it would be — " She gestured vaguely at the mountains behind the town, where they'd burned a fortune above the clouds. "Louder."

"Truth usually isn't," Reyes said. He said it mildly, the way he said everything, his arm still strapped against his ribs from Pike's people and his face betraying nothing about how much the ride down had cost him. "It's more like water. Gets in everywhere eventually. You just have to keep pouring."

"You've been saving that one for days," Cass said.

"Since the col."

* * *

They took rooms at the Kettle & Crown, which was the only inn, which meant it was also the best inn, which meant its proprietor — a broad, unbothered woman named Hettie Vane who looked at six filthy mountaineers the way a farmer looks at weather — charged accordingly.

"We can't afford this," Tamsin whispered.

"We can't afford *not* to look like we can," Cass whispered back, and paid in coin that Tamsin strongly suspected had been earmarked for something else, and smiled at Hettie Vane with the smile that had once made Jude say, flatly, *that's the smile of a woman selling me a bridge I'm standing on.*

But Hettie Vane just counted the money, bit one coin out of old habit, and said, "Baths are extra. So is not asking questions, and you'll want both."

Tamsin wanted the bath so badly she nearly wept into it.

She lay in the water afterward until her fingers pruned, listening to the town through the window. Church bells — company church, company bells, cast with an A on them that everyone pretended not to see. The sheriff's boots on the boardwalk, unhurried. A man arguing cheerfully about the price of nails. It sounded like any town. That was the thing she hadn't understood until now: it didn't take chains to hold a place. It took scrip and sermons and a sheriff whose salary came from the same ledger as the church bell, all of it so ordinary you'd have to be mad to call it what it was.

Or, she thought, wringing out her hair, you'd have to come down off a glacier with ash on your hands and nothing left to lose.

* * *

Jude didn't want to do it publicly.

That was the argument that filled their shared room that first night, conducted in the fierce whispers of people who'd spent three weeks shouting over wind and couldn't seem to recalibrate.

"The town meeting," Cass said. "Thursday. Everyone attends. It's the one institution here that isn't theirs."

"It's exactly theirs," Jude said. "The Aldouses let it happen because it makes people feel heard while deciding nothing."

"Then we make it decide something."

"With what? We burned the proof, Cass. That was the whole — " Jude stopped. Tamsin, cross-legged on her bunk pretending very hard to mend a sock she had no intention of mending, watched the two women arrive at the same silence from opposite directions.

"With what," Cass said quietly, "do you think a town is made?"

Tamsin put down the sock. "Stories," she said, before she could stop herself.

Both of them looked at her.

"My mum used to say it. You can't burn a story. You can't buy it back once it's loose." She felt her ears go hot. "You said it yourself, on the mountain. The journal doesn't need to exist for people to know what it said. We know what it said. We're — " she gestured at all of them, at Reyes half-asleep in the corner chair, at Okafor's oil-stained hands folded patiently in his lap — "we're the proof now. Walking around. Talking."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"That," Okafor said finally, "is either very wise or very dangerous."

"Usually both," said Reyes, eyes still closed.

* * *

Edran Pike found them Wednesday morning, because of course he did.

He didn't bring men this time. He brought pastries. He set the box of them on the inn's common table like a peace offering between nations and sat down uninvited, and Tamsin — who had watched this man's employees shoot at them in the dark — discovered she was furious mostly about the pastries, which smelled incredible.

"Miss Joubert," Pike said. "Mr. Okafor. Miss Merrow. Mr. Reyes. And the young climber — Tamsin, isn't it? You did well on the col. My people were impressed. They don't impress easily; it's a hiring problem I've been meaning to address."

"You tried to kill us," Tamsin said.

"I tried to *stop* you," Pike corrected gently. "Killing you would have been wasteful and, frankly, bad for morale. May I?" He opened the pastry box without waiting. "I'll be brief, since I suspect brevity is all any of us can afford. The documents are destroyed. We know. Ash doesn't require forensics. Which means the dynasty's exposure is now theoretical rather than documentary — and theoretical problems are my specialty. So: walk away, and everyone keeps breathing comfortably. Stay, and start talking in public, and things become — " he selected a pastry, considered it — "*complicated.* For all of you. Individually. In ways tailored to fit."

"You already tried tailored," Reyes said. "In the shelter. It didn't fit."

Pike smiled at him with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse than everything else. "Everyone has a price, Mr. Reyes. Some people's is simply very strange."

"Our price," Jude said, and her voice was quiet in the way Tamsin had learned meant bedrock, "is that this town stops paying yours."

Pike looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw there, he stood, buttoned his coat, and left the pastries.

"Don't eat those," Cass said.

Okafor ate one. "If he wanted us dead he wouldn't cater it," he said, chewing. "Also, almond. Worth the risk."

* * *

The town meeting was held Thursday evening in the grange hall, under a roof built by Aldous timber money, on benches planed by an Aldous mill, and Tamsin thought: *even the room is theirs. Good. Let's rob it.*

Sheriff Brannock ran the meetings, because the sheriff always ran the meetings, standing at the front with the gavel and the patience of a man who had done this two hundred times and never once seen anything change. Old grievances came up the way they always did — the water rights, the school roof, the price the company store paid for wool. Each one got a sympathetic nod, a note taken, a promise to "raise it with the office," and the slow deflation of everyone who'd spoken.

Tamsin sat in the back row between Reyes and Okafor and felt her heart hammering like it had on the col, except this time there was no rope.

Cass went third.

She walked up the aisle like she owned the building, which Tamsin suspected was a skill rather than a feeling, and asked for the floor on "a matter of the founding of this town," and Sheriff Brannock, sensing nothing yet, gave it to her.

What followed was not a speech. Tamsin had expected a speech. What it actually was, was Cass telling a story — about Elias Aldous arriving in the valley with less than anyone, about the Joubert claim staked first, about the fire at the Joubert homestead that official record called an accident, about the deed signed by a dying man whose signature, Cass noted pleasantly, appeared in three other places that same week in three different hands.

"And how would you know all this?" someone called out.

"Because I've spent twenty years buying up every scrap of paper that mentions it," Cass said, "hoping to sell it back to the family that should never have needed to buy its own name. And because last month I climbed a mountain with Elias Aldous's granddaughter's fixer shooting at me to get the rest of it." She paused. "Ask Miss Joubert. She's sitting right there. Her family owned this valley before it owned them."

Every head turned. Tamsin watched Jude stand up — slowly, like the climb was costing her something even on flat ground — and walk to the front, and stand next to Cass, these two women who'd spent fifteen years sharpening themselves on each other, shoulder to shoulder under the company-built roof.

"I'm not here to give you a fortune," Jude said. "There isn't one. We found the proof of what was stolen and we burned it, because I won't hand this town a new master just because the master has my surname." Her voice carried to the back without effort; Tamsin realized she'd probably been born projecting across valleys. "I'm here to tell you what happened. That's all. It turns out it's enough."

And then she told it. All of it — the murder, the cover-up, Captain Voss's part in it, her own brother's flight, her own long exile. She told it plainly, without theater, and when she got to the parts about herself she didn't flinch, and Tamsin understood suddenly why the crew had followed her up a frozen hull: not because she was fearless, but because she refused to lie even when lying would have been kinder to everyone, including her.

The hall did not erupt. That was the second surprise. The hall went very, very quiet — the particular quiet of several hundred people doing arithmetic all at once. Tamsin could almost hear it: the sheriff's salary, the store's prices, the bell on the church, the deed to the house, the grandfather who'd signed something he shouldn't have, the grandmother who'd never talked about the old country, the water rights, the school roof.

Sheriff Brannock banged the gavel twice and said the matter would be "reviewed," and the meeting moved on to the road levy, and Tamsin's stomach dropped with disappointment so sharp it felt physical.

But outside, afterward, in the cold, the town had changed texture.

People didn't shout. They lingered. A woman Tamsin didn't know pressed a jar of preserves into Jude's hands and said only, "My gran always said the Jouberts were robbed," and walked off before anyone could respond. Two men stood by the well talking too long and too low. The baker — the same baker from the queue — reopened his shop at nine at night, which no Kessler baker had ever done, and left the lamp burning in the window, which everyone understood without being told.

"Nothing happened," Tamsin said, discouraged.

"Everything happened," Reyes said. "Slowly. Watch."

* * *

It went like water, just as he'd promised.

By Saturday, the company store had its first day of empty shelves in forty years — not from shortage, but because eleven families bought their flour in Millbrook instead, walking the extra four miles in the cold, together, in a line, like a small patient parade. By Sunday, the company church preached to half its pews; the other half held a service in the grange hall with no bell and no collection plate, led by a farmer who read aloud from the founding charter, the real one, the one Cass had produced from twenty years of hoarded paper, which named the original signatories, four of whom were Jouberts.

The sheriff posted a notice about "outside agitators" and someone — Tamsin never learned who, though she had her suspicions about Hettie Vane — nailed a copy of the founding charter right over it, side by side, so the wall read like a question.

Pike's men were still in town. You could see them at the edges, not doing anything, being paid to be visible. But visibility cuts both ways, and by Tuesday a boy threw a snowball at one, and by Thursday nobody looked away from them at all, which is a kind of defeat that leaves no bruises and wins no battles and loses the war anyway.

"They'll adjust," Okafor warned, in the evenings, over cards. "Dynasties don't die of embarrassment. They rebrand."

"Let them rebrand," Cass said, laying down a winning hand with insufferable grace. "Rebranding means conceding the story. The story's ours now."

"Whose story?" Jude asked, and there was something in it — not suspicion exactly, more like an old scar testing itself in cold weather.

Cass met her eyes across the cards. "The town's. I'm learning to live with that. It's remarkably uncomfortable. You'd think I'd be used to discomfort by now."

Tamsin laughed, and then everyone did, even Reyes, which was rare enough that Okafor marked the occasion by losing the next hand on purpose.

* * *

On their last full day in Kessler Town, Tamsin walked up alone to the cemetery on the rise above the east fields, because she'd promised herself she would and because some errands you don't delegate.

She found the stone easily. It was newer than the others, cleaner-edged, paid for — she'd checked with Hettie Vane — anonymously, years ago, by a woman who'd passed through town once and never explained why.

**ELIAS JOUBERT**
*He reached higher than the mountain*

Tamsin crouched and brushed the snow off the letters and talked to him for a while, the way you do, awkwardly, out loud, because the dead deserve the dignity of being addressed directly.

"She stayed," she told him. "Your sister. She's fixing a mooring line at the coast like it's a religion, and Okafor's teaching her engine things she pretends not to care about, and Cass is — well, Cass is Cass, but she's our Cass now, which nobody predicted, least of all her. And your map got us there. So. You reached higher than the mountain, all right. Just not the way anyone planned."

She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone for a moment, then stood, knees cracking, and looked down at the town.

Smoke rose from chimneys that weren't company chimneys, because none of them were, really — they'd just been told whose they were for so long they'd believed it. Down in the square, the baker's lamp was lit again, early. Somewhere a dog was winning its ongoing war with the same wagon. Small things. Slow things. A town beginning to breathe after decades of holding it.

Justice, she decided, watching the smoke, wasn't a summit. It was more like the descent — unglamorous, hard on the knees, done in stages, impossible to do alone.

Behind her, boots crunched on frost. She turned, expecting Jude or Reyes.

It was neither. It was a girl of maybe sixteen, coat too thin, standing at the cemetery gate with her chin up in a way Tamsin recognized from mirrors and from mountain ledges alike.

"You're the climber," the girl said. Not a question. "The one who fell in the crevasse and lived."

"Fell *into,*" Tamsin said. "Lived *out of.* Details matter."

"I want to learn." The girl's breath fogged. "Climbing. Real climbing. Nobody teaches it here — nobody's taught anything here that the company didn't approve. But they say you're staying through the thaw."

Tamsin looked at her — at the hunger in her, familiar as her own heartbeat — and then down at the town, and then at the mountains behind it, white and waiting and, for the first time in her life, feeling less like a wall and more like a door.

"Well," Tamsin said, and stuck out a rope-scarred hand. "First lesson's free. Everything after that costs a jar of your mother's preserves. I hear they're excellent."
