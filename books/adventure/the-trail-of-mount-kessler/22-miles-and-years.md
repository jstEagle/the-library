## 22. Miles and Years

The mooring line had been fraying since February.

Jude noticed it the way she noticed everything now — slowly, without flinching, the way you notice a loose thread in a sweater you intend to keep. She'd been diving the same stretch of coast off Harrow Point for three months by then, and she had learned its moods the way she'd once learned tide tables in a dozen foreign ports: the way the light came down through the kelp in April like green cathedral glass, the way the sandbars shifted after a storm and rearranged her landmarks overnight, the way the harbor seals had opinions about her anchor placement and expressed them at length.

She surfaced that morning with a fistful of old chain and found Tamsin sitting on the dock above her, legs dangling over the water, eating an apple down to nothing.

"You've got a crab on your shoulder," Tamsin said.

"I know. He's been there since the second reef. We're negotiating."

"Who's winning?"

"He is. They always do." Jude let the crab drop back into the water with more ceremony than it deserved, then hauled herself up the ladder and sat beside her, dripping. "Line's going to need replacing."

"The mooring?"

"Mm." She'd been saying that for six weeks. The difference was that this time she meant to do something about it.

Tamsin looked at her sidelong — she still did that, still checked Jude's face for weather before committing to a conversation, though the storms were long gone now. "You said that in March."

"I know what I said in March."

"And in April."

"Tamsin."

"I'm just noting," Tamsin said, with the scrupulous fairness of someone who had nearly died in a crevasse and therefore considered herself entitled to note things forever, "that you used to fix things the day you noticed them. Boats, rigs, arguments. Now you let them sit."

Jude thought about that while she wrung out her hair. It was true, and it wasn't the kind of true that hurt. Somewhere between the summit and the spring, she had stopped treating every imperfection as an emergency. A fraying line could wait until the hardware store opened. An argument could wait until everyone had slept. A life, apparently, could be lived in one place long enough for its small failures to become familiar rather than alarming.

"I'm staying," she said. It sounded strange in her mouth, so she said it again, testing the weight of it. "I'm staying. Through summer, anyway."

Tamsin grinned and tossed her the apple core, which was the closest thing to a hug either of them managed before ten in the morning.

* * *

The harbor town was called Salt Hollow, and it had exactly one street, two bars, and a chandlery run by a woman named Odile who had opinions about rope the way Cass Merrow had opinions about dynasties — total, unshakeable, and delivered without apology.

Jude had been buying her line from Odile since March, which meant they had been arguing since March.

"That's not the right lay for your cleat," Odile said, when Jude came in for the replacement spool. "You want three-strand. You keep buying braided because it's prettier."

"It's stronger."

"It's prettier. Strength isn't your problem, Joubert. You rig like a woman who expects the world to fall on her. You want line that forgives you."

Jude looked at the coils on the wall. There had been a time — most of her adult life, honestly — when she would have taken the insult as a diagnosis and left town over it. A stranger noticing how you held yourself was the first step toward a stranger knowing why, and knowing why was the thing she had spent fifteen years outrunning.

Now she just leaned on the counter and said, "Fine. Three-strand. But I want the good stuff, not whatever you sell the tourists."

"The tourists don't buy rope. The tourists buy postcards." Odile cut the length anyway, generous by half a fathom, and waved off Jude's protest with the air of someone settling an account neither of them would name. "There. Now go tie something down properly, for once in your life."

It occurred to Jude, walking back down the single street with the coil under her arm, that she knew the names of eleven people in this town now. Twelve counting the seal. That was more than she'd known in any port since she was seventeen years old, and it hadn't cost her anything except the habit of leaving.

* * *

Reyes came through in May, the way migratory birds come through — quietly, on his own schedule, staying exactly as long as he intended to and not a day more.

He had a boat now. Of course he had a boat. Reyes without a boat was like Okafor without a grudge against entropy; the universe didn't allow it. He anchored outside the breakwater, rowed in, and sat across from Jude at the fish shack without ordering for a full minute, which from Reyes was a speech.

"You fixed the mooring," he said finally.

"Took me long enough."

"You did. Most people wouldn't have." He ordered then — the same thing he always ordered, whatever the special was, no questions asked, because questions about food were a waste of breath better spent on navigation. "I read the chart of this harbor before I came in. Your mooring is the only one set right. Everyone else anchors like they're parking a car."

"That's very flattering, Reyes."

"It's not flattery. It's data." He ate. He watched the water. He told her, in the unhurried way he told everything, that he was heading north in June, up the inside passage, alone, because some part of him needed the empty water the way she'd once needed the empty map. And then he said the thing he'd actually rowed in to say, which was: "I'll be back in September. Save me a stool."

Jude nodded. That was all it needed. With Reyes, a nod was a contract, and contracts with Reyes held.

But she caught herself smiling after he'd gone, standing on the dock in the last of the light, looking at his anchor light bobbing out past the wall. Fifteen years ago she had built a life out of people who never expected her to stay, because that way nobody could ever expect anything of her at all. Now here was a man who expected her to save him a stool, and she had every intention of doing it, and it felt less like a debt than like a door left open on purpose.

* * *

Okafor arrived in a truck, which surprised no one who had ever heard him describe boats as "rusting lies we tell ourselves about mobility."

He'd taken a job in Kessler Town — actual paid work, supervising the refit of the company's ancient ore tramway, because the dynasty's hold had cracked wide enough in the winter that even the Aldouses' own engineers were quitting and somebody had to keep the mountain from falling on the town. He complained about it constantly and magnificently. He complained about the bolts, the budget, the foreman, the weather, the coffee, and the fact that the tramway had been built by "an optimist with a slide rule and no respect for gravity."

"You love that job," Tamsin told him over the phone, and Okafor, who could argue with a load-bearing wall, could not argue with Tamsin, because she had seen him work and knew the truth. "You complain about it the way I complain about my boots. Because complaining is how you say you care."

"I care about torque values," Okafor said. "That's different."

"It isn't."

He drove out to the coast twice that spring, both times ostensibly to deliver parts, both times actually to sit on Jude's dock and eat whatever she'd cooked and inspect her mooring knot with a jeweler's loupe he'd repurposed from mining equipment. On the second visit he pronounced the knot "adequate, given the materials," which from Okafor was practically a blessing, and then he stood at the end of the pier watching the sunset and said, quietly, without any argument attached:

"You know what I figured out up there? In the storm shelter, when we were all deciding whether to take Pike's deal?" He shook his head at the horizon. "I argued for the deal because I thought it was the safe choice. It wasn't. It was just the choice where I didn't have to trust anybody. Took me twenty years to learn the difference."

Jude handed him another beer instead of answering, because some conversations are better conducted in silence and carbonation.

Before he left, he fixed her dock light — the one that had been flickering since March, the one she'd been letting flicker, the one she hadn't gotten around to. He did it in ten minutes, muttering the whole time about wiring that violated several laws of physics, and when he was done the whole harbor glowed warm and steady, and Jude stood under it and felt something she couldn't quite name. Not gratitude exactly. More like the feeling of a house when someone else has carried firewood in without being asked.

* * *

Cass Merrow did not arrive. Cass was simply there one morning, sitting on the sea wall, feeding chips to the seals in open violation of three posted signs, looking thinner than winter and sharper than ever.

Nobody had seen her since the descent. She'd turned herself in to the authorities in Kessler Town — walked into the company sheriff's office, of all places, with the ash-stained confession of everything she'd sold to Pike and everything she'd done to stop him afterward, and let the consequences land where they landed. Six months of supervised restitution, working the valley's legal aid office for the families the dynasty had squeezed. She'd served four by the time she showed up in Salt Hollow, on a weekend pass she almost certainly bent the rules to get.

"You look terrible," Jude said, sitting down beside her.

"You look smug. One season in one harbor and suddenly you're a person who belongs places." Cass threw another chip; a seal caught it with insulting grace. "Disgusting. I taught you better than contentment."

"You taught me leverage. Turns out I didn't want it."

"No," Cass said. "Turns out you wanted something worse. You wanted to be known." She said it without bitterness, which was new, and maybe cost her more than bitterness would have. For a while they just watched the water. Then: "The trial coverage starts next month. The Aldouses are fighting the exposure suits with everything they have left. My testimony's part of it. So is yours, if you'll give it."

"I already gave it. In the town square, in January."

"A deposition, Joubert. Paper. Something with a stamp on it. The kind of thing that survives even if we don't."

Jude looked at her hands — diver's hands, scarred, steady. She thought about the summit, about the box burning above the clouds, about how she had spent one whole night believing she'd destroyed the only proof her family's name had ever been worth anything. She'd kept only Tam's journal — her brother's journal, the one that recorded everything, including the night she'd betrayed him and the night Ilsa Voss had covered a murder and the whole terrible architecture of how a fortune gets built on top of a family that trusted the wrong people.

The journal was in a waterproof case under her bunk. It had been there all spring. Some nights she took it out and read a page. Some nights she couldn't.

"I'll give the deposition," she said. "But I'm not doing it for leverage, Cass. Not even for justice, really. I'm doing it because Tam wrote the truth down, and truth that stays in a dry bag under a bunk isn't truth anymore. It's just grief with a lid on it."

Cass nodded, and for once didn't try to turn the nod into strategy. "That's the healthiest thing anyone's ever said to me," she muttered, "and I hate it."

They agreed to meet in Kessler Town in July. Cass finished her sentence in August. Nobody talked about what came after that, because nobody needed to. Some alliances don't need terms. They just need showing up.

* * *

June came in soft, and with it the anniversary.

Fifteen years since Tam had walked into the range and not walked out. Except now Jude knew he had walked much farther than out — all the way to the wreck of the Kessler Star, all the way to the ice cave, all the way to the truth, carrying it in a strongbox and a notebook because he'd believed, right up to the cold taking him, that his sister might someday come looking. She had. Fifteen years late, but she had.

On the anniversary morning she dove the deep reef alone, down past where the kelp gave out, to a ledge where the rock split open into a small blue cave. She'd found it in April and been coming back ever since, though she'd told no one, because some places are private even when they're public.

She brought nothing down with her but the journal page she'd copied out in her own hand — the last entry, the one where Tam wrote that if anyone ever found this, they should tell Jude he understood. *Tell her I understand,* he'd written. *Tell her I know what she thought she was doing. Tell her it doesn't make it okay, but it makes it human, and I loved her human.*

She'd burned the original documents on the summit. She'd kept the journal. And on the ledge, in the blue quiet, forty feet down where no wind could reach and no dynasty could follow, she weighted the copied page with a stone and left it, the way you leave flowers, the way you leave a light on.

Then she hung in the water a long time, breathing slow, watching the light move on the sand below, and let the anniversary be what it was — not a wound reopened, but a debt acknowledged. She had spent fifteen years measuring her life in miles because miles didn't ask her to account for herself. Distance was a currency that bought anonymity, and anonymity had felt like safety, right up until the day she'd understood it was just exile with better scenery.

Years were harder. Years required staying. Staying required being known. Being known required the story — all of it, the betrayal and the burning and the crew who'd heard the worst of her on a storm-bound mountainside and hadn't left.

But she had those now. The years. The staying. The known-ness. And it turned out none of them had killed her.

* * *

July brought the deposition, and Kessler Town, and a courtroom that smelled of floor wax and old money's panic.

She testified for two days. The Aldouses' lawyers were exactly as expensive and exactly as cruel as she'd expected, and she answered them the way she answered current: steadily, without drama, telling the truth at the speed it deserved. When their lead counsel sneered and asked whether she expected the court to believe a salvage diver's word about events fifteen years old, she looked at him and said, "Sir, my entire profession is recovering truth from places everyone agreed to forget. This is the shallow end."

Okafor laughed so hard he had to leave the gallery. Tamsin, testifying after her about the cache and the climb, wore her expedition patch on her lapel like armor. Reyes submitted his charts of the route as evidence, annotated in his small precise hand, and the judge — an old woman who had grown up in company housing and never forgotten it — read every annotation.

The verdicts came in August, and they were not a landslide. Justice, Jude had learned, is erosion, not explosion. But the dynasty's charter was revoked, the scrip system was outlawed, the company sheriff's office was dissolved into an elected department, and the tramway — Okafor's tramway, torque values and all — passed into the town's hands. No fortune changed hands. No fortune ever does. But the town breathed, and kept breathing, and on the night the verdict came down, the church bell that the Aldouses had silenced for sixty years rang again, badly, out of tune, gloriously.

Cass stood next to Jude in the square while it rang. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Redemption, Jude had learned, doesn't come with absolution attached; it comes with showing up, again and again, to consequences you can't negotiate away. Cass was showing up. It was enough.

* * *

September, as promised, Reyes came back, and the stool was saved.

October, Tamsin enrolled in the coastal guide program and moved into the room above the chandlery, over Odile's loud objections and louder affection. November, Okafor drove out for Thanksgiving and fixed the dock light again, because apparently dock lights are like torque values and require annual attention. December, the first storm of winter came through and Jude's mooring — three-strand, properly laid, forgiving — held without complaint, and she stood at the window all night watching it hold and felt a satisfaction so complete it was almost embarrassing.

One year. She had been in Salt Hollow one full year.

She marked it the way she now marked everything — plainly, with the people it belonged to. Fish shack, all five of them, plus Odile invited by Tamsin against everyone's better judgment and turning out to be excellent company. They ate too much. They argued about knots. Tamsin recounted the crevasse fall for the hundredth time, each retelling adding roughly a hundred feet of depth, and Okafor corrected the physics, and Reyes corrected the corrections, and Jude sat back and listened to the noise of it and thought: this is what a life sounds like. Not silence. Not distance. Noise — warm, overlapping, occasionally insufferable, entirely chosen.

At some point in the evening Cass raised her glass and said, "To the Kessler Star," and Reyes said, "To the ones who didn't come home," and they drank to Tam, and to Ilsa Voss, who had been braver and worse than any of them had known, and to the version of Jude Joubert who had spent fifteen years running and never once stopped moving long enough to notice she was lonely.

"She'd be amazed," Tamsin said, nodding at Jude. "The old you. She'd take one look at a fixed mooring line and a saved stool and a town full of people who know your whole story, and she'd be gone by morning."

"No," Jude said. "She wouldn't." She turned her glass slowly, watching the lamplight come through it. "She'd want to be. But she'd stay. She stayed on the mountain, that night on the summit, when running was still on the table. She stayed for the burning. She stayed for the confession. She was always capable of it. She just didn't know yet that staying is a thing you choose daily, not once. Like diving. Like forgiveness. Like belonging."

The table went quiet in the good way, the way tables go quiet when everyone has heard something true and needs a second to fit it into themselves.

Then Okafor broke it, because Okafor always breaks the good silences before they can curdle into solemnity: "Daily, huh? So tomorrow you're choosing us again?"

"Tomorrow I'm choosing the mooring line," Jude said. "You're Wednesday."

Everyone laughed, and the evening went on, and outside the window the harbor lights burned steady over water that had finally, after fifteen years and twenty-two chapters of a life measured in departures, become the place she was from.

Spring would come again. It always did. And when it came, she would dive the same waters, walk the same street, buy the same forgiving rope from Odile, and choose it all again — the crew, the memory, the name.

Jude Joubert. Her brother's sister. Her crew's diver. Her town's neighbor.

Home, at last, in her own name — renewed daily, like a mooring knot, like a promise, like the tide.
