## 3. The Leaking Boat

Puerto Deseado smelled of fish, diesel, and the particular melancholy of towns that exist because a river gave up on the sea. I came in on the mail truck, wedged between crates of oranges and a man who talked for four hours about his kidneys. Esteban Ruiz's map rode against my ribs in its oilcloth, and the bullet rode in my shirt pocket, wrapped in gauze gone brown with blood that wasn't mine.

I'd had two hundred kilometers to decide how you tell a man his father is dead when you never met either of them. There is no good way. There are only ways that are less insulting than others, and most of those involve not leading with the bullet.

The town clung to its estuary like something washed ashore and too tired to be carried back out. Gulls screamed over the cannery. A dog with one ear watched me with the professional indifference of a customs officer. I asked at the fuel dock, at the chandlery, at the kiosk where a woman sold me coffee thick enough to patch a hull, and each time I got the same answer: Ruiz? Tomás Ruiz? He drinks at the Farolón, he works on boats, he owes money. Everyone owes money here. Ask at the water.

I found him at the slipway, elbow-deep in an outboard motor, arguing with it in Spanish so rapid and profane that I understood only the general shape of his grievances. He was maybe thirty, built like the pilings he was standing among — dark, salt-cured, immovable until something finally rotted him through. When he saw me standing there with my dust-caked pack and my archaeologist's posture, he wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago and looked at me the way men look at weather coming in off the water.

"You're looking for someone," he said. "Nobody walks like that unless they're looking."

"Ansel Coldharbour." I didn't offer a hand. "Your father sent me."

Something moved behind his eyes — not hope exactly, but the cousin of hope, the one nobody invites to funerals. "Sent you. Where is he, then?"

"He's dead, Tomás. I'm sorry. I was there."

He went very still, the way water goes still before it decides what kind of storm to be. Then he laughed, one short bark with nothing in it. "Dead. My father has been dying since 1974. Every year he comes home half-starved with some story, drinks my mother's patience down to the dregs, and leaves again. Dead is just this year's version."

"He was shot," I said. "Through the side. High fever, blood loss. I did what I could. He lasted two days."

"Did what you could." He said it slowly, tasting it. "Who are you? Really. Because my father doesn't have friends. He has debts and accomplices, and neither of them carries messages for free."

"I'm an archaeologist. I was working a salvage dig east of Gobernador Gregores when he walked into camp. He traded me something for a promise. The promise was to bring it to you."

"And the something?"

I took out the oilcloth bundle and held it without unwrapping it. "That's between him and you. But there's something else you should see first."

I put the bullet in his palm.

He stared at it the way you'd stare at a tooth you'd spit into your hand — small, wrong, intimate. ".38," he said finally. His voice had changed. All the argument had drained out of it. "He carried a .45. Said anything worth shooting deserved a bigger hole."

"It came out of him. I dug it out myself, on the steppe, by lantern light, with a penknife and a bottle of aguardiente. He told me where to cut. He'd been shot before, apparently. He knew the geography."

Tomás closed his fist around the slug. For a long moment the only sound was the estuary slapping the pilings and the gulls conducting their eternal audit overhead. When he spoke again, his voice was rough as ballast.

"What did he say? At the end."

"That you were stubborn. That you got it from your mother, which he considered the better source. And that the map—" I stopped myself. Not mine to say. "That he was sorry about the years. Not sorry enough to have done differently, he said, but sorry."

That almost broke him. I watched it happen: the jaw working, the eyes going bright and furious, the whole man leaning hard against grief like a boat against a dock in a swell. He turned away, set the bullet on the workbench with exaggerated care, and picked up a wrench as if the outboard motor were suddenly the most important thing in Patagonia.

"He said sorry," Tomás muttered. "Twenty years of nothing and he spent his last breath on bookkeeping."

* * *

He fed me that night, which I hadn't expected. Sardines, bread, wine from a box, eaten at a table scarred with knife-marks and candle-wax in a house that smelled of tar and old rope. His mother had died three winters ago; her photograph watched us from the wall with the patient disapproval of the departed. Tomás drank in silence for a long time, then said, "Show me the map."

I unwrapped the oilcloth on the table between us.

It was drawn in pencil and ink on the back of a flour sack, faded but precise — the hand of a man who'd learned drafting from necessity. River lines, contour hatches, a slot canyon marked with a symbol like a bird's foot. And at the head of it, where the plateau began, a small square annotated in a second handwriting, older, more careful: coordinates, elevations, a word I knew too well. *Sitio.* Site.

Tomás studied it for a long time. "This isn't treasure," he said at last. "My father drew river maps all his life. This is different. This is a survey."

"Yes."

"Whose writing is that?"

I could have lied. It would have been easy; lying is the family trade I inherited whether I wanted it or not. "Elias Coldharbour," I said. "My father."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Tomás set down his glass very carefully.

"The Coldharbour," he said. "The one they wrote about. The one who stole the Río Chico collection and vanished into the canyon with it. My father used to curse that name. Used to say—" He stopped. Looked at me. Looked at the map, at the two handwritings side by side, and I watched him do the arithmetic I'd been doing for two hundred kilometers. "They knew each other."

"They worked together. Once. Before everything."

"Then why does my father carry his map twenty years later?"

"Because," I said, "some promises outlive the people who make them. Your father made me one. I intend to keep it. Which means going upriver, into the canyon, during the wet season, in whatever boat you own."

He laughed again, and this time there was something in it, dark and unwilling. "You've seen my boat."

"No."

"Then you don't know what you're asking."

* * *

His boat was worse than I'd imagined, and I'd imagined generously. Twenty-two feet of patched hull and stubbornness, named *La Promesa*, which under the circumstances struck me as either prophecy or sarcasm. She leaked. Tomás admitted it the way other men admit to bad knees — it rains in the boat, always has, the bilge pump works mostly, we bailed worse in '89.

"She leaks," I repeated.

"She *weeps*. There's a difference. Weeping is character."

We were at the slipway at dawn, loading water, fuel, canvas, the map now sealed in a tin. That was when I noticed the man across the harbor. He stood by the fuel pump pretending to study a chart, and he'd been pretending for ten minutes, and his boots were wrong — too clean, too new, city boots on a working dock. Behind him, idling near the chandlery, sat a pickup with a fiberglass dinghy on a trailer and two more men inside it, doing an excellent job of not looking at us.

"Friends of yours?" I murmured.

Tomás didn't turn his head. "No."

"They've been watching since yesterday. The one at the pump asked the kiosk woman about me this morning. About a gringa asking after Ruiz."

"My father's debts," Tomás said, but his voice had flattened, and I knew he didn't believe it either. Debts don't wear city boots. Debts don't travel in threes with a dinghy rigged for open water.

The man at the pump gave up his pretense and crossed the slipway toward us, unhurried, smiling the way creditors smile. He was fifty-ish, silver at the temples, with the tan of a man who pays for his sun rather than earning it. "Señor Ruiz?" he said pleasantly. "Forgive the intrusion. I understand your father recently passed. My condolences. I represent parties with certain... outstanding interests. Perhaps we could discuss what he left behind."

"He left debts," Tomás said. "Take them up with his creditors."

"Oh, I intend to." The smile widened a fraction. "Though I'm told he also left a map. And that a young woman carried it in from the west." His eyes moved to me, weighing, cataloguing. "You'd be the archaeologist, then. Coldharbour, was it? Interesting name. Very interesting name."

The wind came off the estuary cold enough to mean something. Somewhere behind us, *La Promesa* creaked against her lines, leaking her slow character into the bilge.

"We're loading," I said. "If you'll excuse us."

"Of course. Of course." He stepped back, hands raised, all innocence. "We're headed upriver ourselves, as it happens. Such a small river. I'm sure we'll meet." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Ferrer," he said, giving us the name like a calling card or a threat, and walked away unhurried, city boots ringing on the planks.

Tomás exhaled slowly. "You know him?"

"No," I said. "But he knows my father. And he knows about the map, which means somebody in this town sold us inside a day." I looked at the gray water, the swollen brown current already carrying the first rains down from the plateau, and felt the season closing around us like a hand. "How fast can she go?"

Tomás looked at his leaking, weeping, faithful wreck of a boat, and then at the river that had already begun to eat its banks.

"If we leave in the next hour," he said, "faster than them."
