## 4. Monsoon Season

The rain came three weeks early, and it came like a grudge.

I was on the roof of my mother's cousin's boathouse nailing down a tarp when the first drops hit — fat, warm, deliberate, like someone flicking a wet finger at the back of my neck. I looked up. The sky over Deseado had been the color of dirty wool all morning, and I'd told myself it was nothing. You tell yourself a lot of things in a town that lives off the river and pretends the river doesn't notice.

By dark, the gutters were running brown. By midnight, the street outside had become a shallow, moving lake, and old Ferreyra's dog was standing on his porch howling at the water as if it had come to collect a debt.

I stood in the doorway of the boathouse with a hammer in my hand and watched the river rise, and I thought: *Papá, you impossible old man. You picked the worst season in forty years to make me go looking for you.*

Because that was the shape of things now. A woman named Ansel Coldharbour had walked into my life carrying my father's map and my father's bullet, and I had not been able to say no to either. She stood at the window behind me, arms crossed, watching the rain with the expression of someone doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"It's early," she said.

"It's early," I agreed.

"How early?"

"Three weeks. Maybe four. The old men will tell you it never used to come at all, back when they were boys, and then they'll tell you it used to come in February, and then they'll contradict themselves, and then they'll charge you for another glass of wine."

She didn't smile. She was not, I was learning, a woman who smiled to fill silence. The silence she filled herself, with plans.

"We launch tomorrow," she said. "Before it gets worse."

"Before it gets worse," I repeated. "Señorita Coldharbour, have you looked at a river lately? It is already worse. It is worse than worse. It is the kind of worse that eats boats."

"Then we launch before it eats ours."

*Ours.* She'd said it without thinking, and I noticed that she noticed, and that neither of us did anything about it. That was the arrangement, more or less. She had the map, the grief, and the stubbornness of a woman raised by a disgraced man's ghost. I had the boat, the river, and a father who was — I still half believed — waiting somewhere upstream with an explanation.

The boat was *La Chispa*, my father's old hull, twenty years of patches held together by twenty years of promises. I'd spent two days on her, and I will not pretend she was ready. I'd replaced the worst of the planking, re-caulked every seam I could reach, slathered the bilge with tar until my hands looked like a chimney sweep's confession. She was sound the way an old horse is sound — technically alive, morally committed, but you didn't ask her to gallop.

* * *

We launched at first light, which was a lie the sky told us, because there was no light to speak of. Rain fell in ropes. The river had come up a meter overnight and had taken the landing stage's bottom step with it, and the current out in the channel moved with the smooth, muscular indifference of something that had already decided where everything was going.

Ansel stood in the bow with the map in an oilcloth inside her coat and her hair plastered to her face, and I worked the old Evinrude, and *La Chispa* began, within the first hour, to leak.

Of course she did. She was my father's boat.

"Not bad," Ansel called back, when I told her.

"Not bad?"

"The last boat I was on, the leak was personal."

I bailed. That was the arrangement below decks: I drove, she bailed, and every twenty minutes I killed the motor and we listened — really listened — to the rain and the river and the canyon walls ahead of us, which were beginning to close in like a pair of hands.

It was on the second day, sheltering under a basalt overhang while the river climbed the bank a hand's width at a time, that we learned about Ferrer.

* * *

A balsa raft came down the current, riding high and fast, and the man on it was half-drowned and shouting before we could decide whether to hide. He was a fisherman from the lower settlements, and his raft had been commandeered — that was his word, *comandada* — two days upstream by a party of six men with a steel-hulled launch and too much money.

"Who leads them?" Ansel asked.

The fisherman's eyes went sideways, the way eyes do in towns where questions have owners. "A foreigner. Or half. Ferrer. He carries a rifle like he was born holding it and pays like a man spending someone else's."

"What did he want?"

"Passage. Guides. Information." The fisherman looked at me then, and his face did something complicated and apologetic. "Tomás Ruiz. He asked for you by name. He knew your boat. He knew your father's route better than your father's own letters would have told him."

The rain drummed on the overhang. The river breathed against the stones. I felt the cold come up through my boots and settle somewhere under my ribs.

"Who sold him that?" I asked. "The route. The name. Who in Deseado talks?"

"Who doesn't?" the fisherman said, which was answer enough, and then, softer: "They say he paid old Quintana at the fuel depot. They say it was a lot."

Quintana. Who had sold my father fuel for fifteen years. Who had carried me on his shoulders during the festival flood of my ninth year. Who had wept, genuinely wept, at my mother's funeral, and had apparently priced his grief at whatever Ferrer had put in his palm.

Ansel said nothing for a long moment. Then: "How far behind us is he?"

"A day," the fisherman said. "Maybe less, if the water favors steel. It favors steel."

He wouldn't take our money. He took coffee, and a strip of dried goat, and he went on down the current toward a town that had, for a handful of bills, sold us like livestock.

* * *

That night the river rose again, and I lay awake in the hull listening to it, and I want to be honest about what I felt, because a man who lies to himself about fear on a river is a man the river eventually corrects.

I was afraid. Not of Ferrer, particularly — men with rifles are a known quantity, and known quantities can be outwaited, outmaneuvered, or in the last resort outrun, though not in *La Chispa*, not leaking, not against a steel hull and the current's favor. What I was afraid of was smaller and colder: that I had spent two days and all my savings and my father's boat on a promise made to a dying stranger, and that the river was going to take the payment out of me before I ever learned whether my father was worth it.

Because that was the other arithmetic, the one I did in the dark. Esteban Ruiz had walked out of my life when I was nineteen, the same way Ansel's father had walked out of hers, and I had built a whole quiet architecture of resentment around that departure — he chose the canyon, he chose the map, he chose the dead partner's secret over his own son. And now a stranger was dragging me up that same canyon to find out what the choice had actually been, and I did not know which I feared more: that he had abandoned me for nothing, or that he had abandoned me for something, and the something would require me to forgive him.

The rain eased near dawn. I came out on deck and found Ansel already awake, studying the map by the gray light, her finger on the canyon's throat.

"He'll have bought the route," she said. "But he can't buy this." She tapped the map. "Esteban drew the side channels. The slots. The places the water goes when the water is angry. Ferrer has a steel hull and a day's lead on us in knowledge. We have this."

"And a boat that leaks."

"A boat that leaks," she agreed, "and a pilot who knows where the water is angry." She looked up at me, and for the first time since I'd met her there was something in her face that wasn't grief or arithmetic. It was almost — I want to say *trust*, but it was earlier than trust. It was the decision to trust, made in advance, the way you decide to jump before you know the depth.

"Then we go faster than fear," I said, and started the engine, and *La Chispa* coughed, and settled, and took the current.

The canyon walls rose around us, red and black and dripping, and somewhere behind us — a day, maybe less — a steel hull came upriver on water that had been paid for. I bailed. Ansel navigated. The river climbed.

And in the overhangs ahead, cut into stone older than any of our griefs, something was waiting that had already outlasted my father, and his silence, and twenty years of rain.

We just had to reach it before Ferrer did — and before the river finished deciding, as rivers do, what it was willing to carry and what it meant to keep.
