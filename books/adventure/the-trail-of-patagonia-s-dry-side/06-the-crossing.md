## 6. The Crossing

My father taught me two things about rivers, and I learned both of them too late to be useful. First: the river is always lying. It shows you a smooth brown face and tells you nothing about what's underneath. Second: when the river finally tells you the truth, it tells you all at once.

We'd been on the water since dawn, and for six hours the Río had been a liar of the first order. Smooth as poured coffee, sliding between walls of red stone that rose so high we traveled in our own shadow. Ansel sat in the bow with the map spread across her knees, calling out bends before they came, and I sat in the stern with the tiller against my hip, pretending that the trickle of water seeping through the patched seam under my feet was a problem for a future version of Tomás Ruiz.

"Your father built boats?" she asked at one point, without turning around.

"He built everything," I said. "Houses, fences, arguments."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she hadn't authorized it. "Arguments?"

"He was very good at them. He could keep one going for years with no opponent present." I watched her shoulders, the set of them. She carried grief like cargo she refused to declare. "He left me a hull like this one. Patched over and over, never replaced. He said a boat with scars floats better than a proud one."

"And you believed him?"

"I was eight."

The trickle under my feet became a thread. The thread became a finger's width of brown water coiling around my boot. I bailed with a tin cup and told myself the river was still lying.

It wasn't. By midday the sky over the canyon rim had gone the color of wet slate, and the air had that thick, animal smell — the monsoon arriving early, arriving angry, exactly as the old man at the last estancia had promised with a shrug that said *nothing to be done*. The rain came down in ropes. The river rose while we watched it, visibly, greedily, swallowing the gravel bars we'd used as landmarks an hour before.

"We should put in," Ansel shouted over the noise.

"Put in where?" I gestured at the walls. She understood. In a canyon there is no shore; there is only rock, and rock does not negotiate.

So we ran it. And I will say this for the crossing that followed: neither of us screamed. We made other sounds, worse sounds, but not screaming.

* * *

The rock found us at the worst possible moment, which is the only moment rocks ever find anyone. A bend, a standing wave taller than my head, and beneath it — invisible, patient, older than any map — a shelf of stone with an edge like a plowshare. The hull hit it with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, a long tearing groan, and then the river came in the way rivers do: not through a hole but through a decision, all at once, everywhere.

I remember three things clearly. One: Ansel turning, her hair plastered flat, her eyes wide and absolutely calm, and her hand shooting out to grab the oilcloth map off her knees and stuff it inside her jacket before anything else. Two: my own hands letting go of the tiller because the tiller no longer mattered, and grabbing the bail bucket instead, absurdly, uselessly. Three: the moment the boat rolled and the river took her.

Not metaphorically. It took her. The current peeled her out of the bow like a leaf off a branch, and for one second I saw her face above the water — not afraid, furious, furious at the river personally — and then she was gone downstream and I went in after her because apparently I had learned nothing from my father either.

The water was warm and violent and total. It did not want me to swim; it wanted me to tumble, and we tumbled together, me and the flood, over and over, the sky and the red walls trading places. I caught something — her wrist, her sleeve, I still don't know — and held on, and the river objected, and we argued about it for what felt like a year and was probably forty seconds, and then an eddy behind a fallen slab decided, for its own reasons, to let us go.

We crawled out onto a shelf of gravel like two animals the river had chewed and spat. Half our supplies were pinned in the wreck or scattered downriver or simply gone into the geology of Patagonia. The boat itself hung on the rock shelf fifty meters downstream, torn open along the starboard side like a gutted fish, thumping against the stone with every surge.

Ansel lay on her back, coughing, the map clutched to her chest with both hands. She looked at me. I looked at her. Neither of us said anything, because there is a kind of conversation that happens best in silence, and ours went roughly: *you came after me*, and *of course I did*, and *don't make it strange*.

Then she sat up and started inventorying what we'd saved, because she was that kind of person, and I loved — no. I noted it. I noted it the way you note a fact about the weather.

* * *

We hauled the wreck ashore by stages, working the hull up the gravel with levers of driftwood, taking advantage of every drop in the flood. What was left of her was beyond patching — the tear ran four feet, through the seam my father had glassed twice, through wood he had planed with his own hands twenty years ago. I put my palm flat on the broken plank and left it there longer than was practical.

"She got us here," Ansel said quietly. Behind us.

"My father would have said she died doing what she loved. Leaking."

That got the laugh again, thinner this time, frayed at the edges. We built a fire from the dry driftwood stacked high in the overhang above the gravel — the flood line of some earlier century — and laid out our losses like casualties. Food: perhaps half. Ammunition: wet, but salvageable. The tent: gone. The tools: mostly gone. The map: dry, because Ansel Coldharbour had protected a piece of paper with her life while the river tried to drown her, and I decided not to examine what that meant about how much the paper mattered, or how little.

It was Ansel who found the sign. She'd walked upstream along the high bank to see whether retreat was possible — it wasn't; the flood had eaten the trail entirely — and she came back down at dusk moving differently. Not hurrying. That was the strange part. Moving slowly, deliberately, the way people walk toward something they'd rather not see.

"Tomás." She crouched at the edge of the firelight and turned over a scrap of canvas, mud-stiffened, half-buried in the fresh silt the flood had deposited. Beside it, a cartridge case, brass gone green. And pressed deep into the mud, sharp-edged, unmistakable: a boot heel print, a military pattern, the tread of a man who buys his boots in Buenos Aires and pays someone else to break them in.

"Ferrer's men camped here," she said. "Before the flood. A day ago, maybe less. They're following the same water we are."

"A day behind," I said. "And we just lost our boat."

"They don't know about the flood." She turned the cartridge case in her fingers. "They'll have lost time too. Maybe more than us."

"Or maybe they have horses and we have feet."

She didn't argue. That was somehow worse. She fed the fire another piece of driftwood and watched it catch, and the light moved across her face, and I saw that she wasn't afraid. She was angry. The whole day — the river, the rock, the loss — had burned something out of her, and what remained was harder and cleaner.

"The map shows a slot canyon," she said. "Tomorrow. Past the narrows. My father's route — Esteban's route — up to the plateau. No boat required."

"You've been reading it?"

"I've been reading it since Puerto Deseado." She glanced at me, and for a moment something almost like humor crossed her face. "You guard your father's secrets. I'll guard mine."

"That's fair," I said. "Between the two of us we have one complete parent's worth of honesty."

She laughed properly then, sitting there in the wrecked remains of our expedition, soaked, half-supplied, hunted, and I understood that something had happened in the water that I couldn't undo and didn't want to. The river had taken our boat and half our food and nearly both of us, and in exchange it had done the one thing I'd spent a week refusing to do: it had put my life in her hands and hers in mine, briefly and completely, and neither of us had let go.

Trust, my father used to say, is just fear that's found somewhere better to live.

In the morning we'd climb. Tonight, the rain hammered the overhang and the river below roared like something alive, and across the fire Ansel slept sitting up with the map against her chest, and I kept watch, and thought about boots printed in the mud, and how a day is a very small distance when the man behind you has horses.

Somewhere upstream, a sound carried down through the rain — faint, rhythmic, almost lost under the flood.

Engines.
