## 7. Ferrer

The narrows found us before we found them. That's the thing about canyons — they don't announce themselves. One moment the river is a broad brown ribbon swaggering through open country, and the next the walls lean in like two men comparing notes about you, and the water goes from lazy to furious in the space of a hundred meters.

Tomás saw it first. He always saw the river first; I saw the land, he saw the water, and between us we managed a kind of competence that neither of us could claim alone.

"Ansel." He said my name the way you'd say *fire*. "Narrows ahead. We should portage."

"Portage around a canyon?"

"Portage *through* it, if we're lucky. There's a trail on the left bank when the water's low." He spat over the side, which is his version of punctuation. "The water is not low."

I looked at the walls rising on either side of us — red and gold sediment stacked like the pages of an old ledger — and understood why every map of this country had blank spaces in it. The river didn't want company.

We ran it anyway. Not because we were brave. Because behind us, half a day back by Tomás's estimate, Ferrer's boats were running the same water, and a leaky hull with two exhausted people beats four armed ones only when it's moving.

We came through the worst of it sideways, screaming, bailing, Tomás wrestling the tiller like a man fighting his own reflection. And then the canyon opened into a pool — deep, green, unnaturally still — and the current simply set us down in the middle of it like a hand placing a chess piece.

That's where Ferrer was waiting.

* * *

He sat on a flat rock at the water's edge with the ease of a man who has never once been late for anything, smoking a cigarette down to the filter. Two of his men stood behind him with rifles slung casually across their chests, casual as furniture. A third held their boat off the bank.

Tomás hauled us ashore ten meters downstream, and we walked the rest, because there was nowhere else to walk to. The canyon walls here were sheer. Behind us, the rapids we'd survived. Ahead of us, more of the same. Ferrer had chosen his ground the way a good butcher chooses a knife.

I knew him on sight, which surprised me. Not from life — from a photograph. My father kept a box of things he never talked about, and once, when I was fourteen and snooping the way fourteen-year-olds snoop, I'd found a picture of four men standing in front of a tent, squinting into a Patagonian sun. Three of them I recognized: my father, young and already going gray at the temples; Esteban Ruiz, whose face I now knew from his son's; and a third man whose name I'd never learned because my father had scratched it out of the caption with such force he'd torn the paper.

The fourth man was standing slightly apart from the others. Slightly apart, slightly behind, holding a shovel like he resented it.

Ferrer looked exactly like that photograph, thirty years deepened. Same jaw, same way of standing as though the ground owed him money.

"You're Coldharbour's girl," he said. His voice was low and gravelled, a voice used to being obeyed in rooms where obedience mattered. "I wondered. You have his eyes. The rest of you looks like whoever raised you, poor soul."

"And you're the one who tore your own name out of a photograph."

"I tore out the name of a dead man," he said. "Mine was never on it."

Tomás flinched. I felt it through the space between us, a small seismic event. He'd spent three days believing his father had been shot by strangers — bandits, maybe, or rival hunters, the anonymous violence the steppe hands out for free. He had not prepared himself for the possibility that the bullet had a history.

Ferrer lit another cigarette off the ember of the first and studied me the way I'd studied potsherds all those years on the dig — turning me over, looking for the seam.

"I'm going to tell you a story," he said. "You'll want to sit. There are rocks. Choose one."

I didn't sit. Neither did Tomás. Ferrer shrugged, as if our legs were our own funeral.

"Twenty-two years ago there were four of us," he said. "Your father. Ruiz. Me. And a man named Ossian Vail, who is not in any photograph because Vail did not believe in being recorded. We found something up on the plateau. Something old, something valuable, something that would have made four reputations and ruined one secret."

"What secret?" I asked, and hated myself for asking, because asking meant I needed to know, and needing to know was the family disease.

"The kind of secret that gets people shot," Ferrer said. "Vail wanted to sell what we found. Quietly, privately, to a buyer in Santiago who paid in cash and asked no questions. Your father refused. Ruiz refused. I—"

He paused. He drew on the cigarette. He let the smoke out slowly, like a confession.

"I said yes," he said. "And then I thought better of it, and then it was too late to think at all, because Vail put a bullet in Ruiz's direction and I stepped into its path. Twenty-two years carrying it." He touched his left shoulder, almost tenderly. "They dug it out eventually. But a bullet leaves a mark on the man it doesn't kill, too. Ask your father's daughter."

* * *

There it was. The whole architecture of my childhood, laid out on a rock in a canyon by a stranger with a bad shoulder.

My father hadn't abandoned us. He'd walked out of our lives, out of archaeology, out of every professional society that had ever printed his name, because a man named Vail had tried to sell a secret and my father had chosen — chosen, deliberately, at cost of everything — to stand beside Esteban Ruiz instead of beside his own reputation. And when the world called him a fraud, a looter, a disgrace, he had said nothing. Had carried it. Had died carrying it, somewhere up on that plateau, alone except for the wind.

I had spent twenty years building a career out of not being him. Careful digs. Published papers. A reputation so spotless it squeaked. And underneath it, the whole time, the conviction that he'd left because leaving was easier than staying — that he was a coward with a trowel.

He wasn't a coward. He was loyal past the point of self-destruction, which is a different disease, and possibly a worse one, and I would be lying if I said I didn't recognize it in myself, standing in this canyon, having promised a dying man I'd carry his map through a flood.

"So Vail's dead," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "And you've spent twenty-two years—"

"Hunting," Ferrer said. "Yes. Not for treasure. For the site. For proof I existed. Do you know what it does to a man, Ansel Coldharbour, to be written out of a story? To have his name torn off a photograph? Your father got to be a martyr. Ruiz got to be a legend in one town. Vail got rich and died comfortable. I got a scar and a grudge." He flicked the cigarette into the water, where it hissed and died. "I want what's up there. And I want my name in the record when it's found. That's all. That's a small thing. It's the only thing I have left to want."

"And us?"

"You have the map. I need the map — or rather, I need what the map leads to, but the map is faster than searching two hundred square kilometers of plateau before the floods finish cutting it off." He spread his hands, and for a moment the butcher vanished and something tired and almost honest looked out of his face. "Here is the bargain. The map for your lives. You give it to me, you take your father's journal if you find it, you go home, you write whatever you like. I'll even give you credit in the record — *site located with the assistance of Ansel Coldharbour.* You get your father's name cleared. I get mine added. Everyone walks away."

Tomás made a sound low in his throat, and I understood it completely. His father had traded that map with his last breath, had traded it for a promise, and the promise had a name, and the name was mine.

"And if we refuse?" Tomás asked.

Ferrer smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man showing you the arithmetic.

"Then the canyon keeps you," he said. "It's kept better than you."

* * *

I stood there with the map inside my jacket, against my ribs, where it had ridden for six days, oilcloth and all, warm as a second heartbeat. And I will be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of the afternoon: for about four seconds, I considered it.

Four seconds. That's how long the offer took to become tempting. Clear my father's name — the thing I'd wanted since I was old enough to understand it had been dirtied. Walk away alive. Let someone else do the digging. All I had to do was break a promise made to a dying man, hand a son's inheritance to the man who'd helped bury the secret, and live comfortably ever after.

Then I remembered Esteban's face in his final hour, gray as the steppe, pressing the oilcloth into my hands. *Mi hijo lo sabrá por dónde buscar,* he'd said. My son will know where to look. He hadn't said *my son will know where the treasure is.* He'd said *where to look.* As if the looking were the point. As if the map wasn't a deed to anything — it was a compass pointed at the truth.

And Ferrer's bargain, I realized, wasn't a bargain at all. It was Vail's offer, twenty-two years later, wearing a cleaner shirt. Sell the secret quietly. Take the money — or the credit, or the name in the record — and let the truth stay buried under the silt where everyone convenient wanted it.

My father had said no to that. On this plateau, probably within sight of these walls, he had said no, and it had cost him everything, and he had said no anyway.

I was not going to be the Coldharbour who finally said yes.

So I lied.

"There's a second copy," I said.

Ferrer's head tilted, a degree, like a gun dog scenting something. "Is there."

"Esteban drew it twice. He told me so. One for his son, one for insurance." I let my hand rest on my jacket, casually, the way you'd pat a pocket you weren't protecting. "Kill us, and the copy goes to the museum in Puerto Deseado with a letter explaining exactly who killed us and why. Deliver it to the wrong hands and the same letter goes out. The only version where that copy stays quiet is the version where we come home."

"That's not how I'd have drawn it up," Ferrer said.

"You weren't asked."

For a long moment the only sound was the pool lapping at the rocks and the far-off grind of the river in the narrows behind us. Then Ferrer laughed — short, genuine, startled out of him, the laugh of a man meeting an opponent he'd stopped expecting decades ago.

"Your father lied the same way," he said. "Same cadence and everything. Right before he told Vail no." He stubbed out nothing — the cigarette was long gone — and looked at me the way you look at weather coming. "All right, Coldharbour. Keep your copy. Keep your lives, for now. But the rains are closing the canyon behind you and the plateau ahead of you, and I have boats and rifles and time, and you have a leaking hull and each other. So run. Run fast."

He turned to his men. "Let them pass."

We pushed off into the green pool with his eyes on our backs, and Tomás rowed us toward the narrows ahead, and neither of us spoke until the bend swallowed Ferrer whole.

Then Tomás said, quietly, "There is no second copy."

"No," I said. "There isn't."

"Then we just gave a man with rifles a reason to make sure we never reach a place where we can mail a letter."

"Yes," I said, and looked up at the canyon ahead, where the walls narrowed again into shadow, and somewhere above them, under twenty years of windblown silt, my father's last truth was waiting to be dug out — if the water, or Ferrer, didn't dig our graves first.

Ahead, the narrows roared.
