## 8. The Dry Side

The slot canyon opened in the cliff face like a seam in a coat — a crack no wider than my shoulders, invisible until you stood directly in front of it and even then half a matter of faith. My father's map showed it as two inked lines with a note beside them in his cramped hand: *Paso del Zorro. Narrow but passable. Do not attempt in rain.* It had been raining for three days.

"We're attempting it anyway," Ansel said, reading over my shoulder. She had that look she got when she'd already decided something and was only informing me of it as a courtesy.

"It says not to."

"It also says the water behind us will be at our ankles by noon. I've done the arithmetic. Have you?"

I had. The river we'd fled up was no longer a river so much as a brown opinion moving at speed, and it had eaten the low ground where we'd slept. Ferrer and his men were somewhere on the far bank or the near one; it hardly mattered which, because the flood didn't care about sides. What mattered was that the map offered exactly one way up out of this drowned country, and it was a crack in the rock that my father had once squeezed through carrying sixty pounds of gear and, presumably, better knees.

"Passable," I said. "He wrote that twenty years ago. Men write optimistic things when they want to believe them."

"So do sons." She folded the map into its oilcloth with the tenderness other people reserve for letters from the dead, which, in a sense, it was. "Let's go."

* * *

The first hundred meters were the worst, and the worst came fast. The slot narrowed until the walls pressed at both elbows and the sky reduced itself to a blue ribbon overhead, thin as the gap between two held breaths. Water ran down the stone in cold threads that found every gap in my clothing with the persistence of debt collectors. Our packs scraped both walls at once. Ansel went ahead because she was thinner and because she'd stopped asking my opinion somewhere around the second day of knowing me, and I followed, watching her boots find holds in the dark, thinking about how my father had come through here with a man he trusted and a secret between them, and how I was coming through with his map and his son's daughter and the same secret wearing different clothes.

Halfway through, the passage pinched to something less than a passage. A slab had fallen sometime in the decades since Esteban Ruiz last passed this way, wedging itself against the wall and leaving a gap you could argue about but not comfortably use.

"I'll go through," Ansel said, "and you hand me the packs."

"And if you get stuck?"

"Then you'll have learned something about my dimensions."

She went through like water finding a crack — exhaling everything, turning sideways, a long scraping silence, and then her voice echoing back off stone: "Clear. Packs." We fed the gear through piece by piece, and then it was my turn, and I will not pretend I didn't panic in that gap. The rock held my chest the way a fist holds a bird. For four or five seconds I understood completely why my father had never come home, why men who go into canyons sometimes decide not to come out of them, and then I exhaled the way she'd told me to and the mountain let go of me all at once, rude as a wave, and I was through, lying on sloped gravel in the gray light, laughing in a way that had nothing to do with humor.

Ansel crouched beside me, saying nothing, offering a canteen. That was her way. She'd learned grief the way I'd learned boats — hands-on, without instructors — and neither of us wasted words on the obvious.

Behind us, deeper in the slot, we could hear the flood arriving. Not water yet — just sound, a pressure in the rock, a hum in our boot soles, the canyon clearing its throat. We climbed.

* * *

The slot climbed in switchbacks that the map rendered as a staircase of short dashes, and by midday we emerged onto a shoulder of bare stone where the wind hit us like a new authority taking power. Below and behind, the world we'd left was drowning. The river had jumped its banks entirely and was busy annexing the valley, a mile-wide sheet of churning chocolate water studded with the crowns of drowned trees. Somewhere under that surface was the wreck of Tomás's boat, which is to say the wreck of my father's boat, which is to say the last thing he built with his own hands, now participating in the landscape rather than traveling through it.

"There goes our retreat," Ansel said. She didn't say it sadly. She said it the way a surgeon confirms what the X-ray already showed.

"No way back," I agreed.

"No way back," she repeated, and something in her shoulders loosened, which I found strange until I thought about it longer. Some people need the door closed behind them before they can stop looking at it. My father had been the opposite, maybe. He'd kept a door open for twenty years and it had killed him slower.

We climbed through the afternoon. The plateau did not arrive so much as accumulate — first the slope gentled, then the wind dropped, then the ground changed underfoot from stone to a fine pale silt that took our footprints like wet plaster and gave nothing back. This was the dry side of Patagonia, the country the guidebooks skip between the mountains and the sea, and it was doing its best impression of nowhere. No trees. No birds after the first hour. Grass in gray tufts, spaced like teeth in an old man's mouth. The light went flat and enormous, and distance stopped meaning anything; a ridge that looked twenty minutes away took two hours, and by then another ridge had grown behind it, patient as bureaucracy.

I have worked water my whole life. I know what exhaustion feels like in the arms and the lower back and the specific muscle in the jaw you clench against cold. But walking that plateau taught me a new kind of tired, a tired of the eyes, of scanning a horizon that refused to resolve into anything. Twice I caught myself asleep on my feet, moving, dreaming with my legs.

"You should eat," Ansel said, the second time.

"You should sleep."

"I will when you do. Neither of us trusts the other enough to be unconscious first. It's very romantic."

We ate standing up, hardtack and the last of the dulce de leche, which had gone to sugar and grit in the tin but tasted, in that place, at that hour, like a decision to keep living. The sun came down huge and orange and indifferent, and the temperature fell off a cliff behind it, and we walked into the dark because stopping meant shivering and moving meant warmth, and because the map said *the site lies beneath the third terrace below the western rim*, and terraces are easier to count in daylight but we were not a pair inclined to wait for favorable conditions anymore. Favorable conditions had drowned back in the valley with my father's boat.

* * *

It was near midnight, moonless, the stars so thick they seemed less like lights than like weather, when the land finally opened.

I felt it before I saw it — a change in the wind, a smell of dust and old stone, a sense of space arriving like a tide. Then the ground simply ended ahead of us, politely, without drama, and beyond the edge lay a basin miles wide, cupped in the dark, holding the night sky the way a bowl holds water. The wind that had bullied us all day died at the rim, as if the basin swallowed it. Down there, somewhere under the windblown silt, under twenty years of patience, lay whatever my father had stayed to document and died refusing to abandon.

Ansel stood at the edge for a long time without speaking. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, and I watched her do the thing she does — take a feeling too large for the moment and file it away for later, for some night when there's fuel for the fire and time to burn it.

"My father stood here," she said finally. Not a question. A surveyor's mark.

"Your father and mine both." I unrolled the map on a flat rock and weighted the corners. By starlight I could make out the final annotations, the ones in different ink, added later, the handwriting looser — a man writing against time. *Tercera terraza. Cavar donde la piedra cambia de color.* Dig where the stone changes color. And beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic: *E.R. — si lees esto, perdóname el retraso.* If you're reading this, forgive the delay.

Twenty years of delay. My father had waited twenty years for someone to bring this map to me, and instead he'd put it in the hands of a stranger with steady hands and a hole in her life shaped exactly like the hole in mine, and somehow that had worked out to the same thing.

"The stone changes color," Ansel said, looking down into the basin, where in the dark every stone was the same color, which was none. "Tomorrow's problem."

"Tomorrow's problem," I agreed, and we made camp in the lee of the rim with no fire, because fire on a skyline is a confession, and we had no idea yet who else might be listening.

Because that was the thing I couldn't say out loud, the thing sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook: the flood had closed the door behind us, yes. But floods run downhill, and so do men like Ferrer. Every route we'd taken to get here was a route, and the water that cut off our retreat would eventually drop, and when it dropped, a man with a grudge and three rifles and a twenty-year-old betrayal to settle would come up the same terraces we had. We hadn't escaped him. We'd merely arrived first.

I said none of this. Ansel knew it anyway — I could tell by the way she checked the rifle's action in the dark, quietly, twice, the way a person checks a lock they know is already locked.

Sleep came for me like the flood had come for the valley — sudden, total, indifferent. My last waking thought was of my father's handwriting, loose and hurried: *forgive the delay.*

In the gray before dawn, Ansel shook my shoulder, and her voice had an edge I hadn't heard since the narrows.

"Smoke," she said. "Down in the basin. Someone beat us to the digging."
