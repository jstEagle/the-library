## 1. The Wreck They Won't Name

The sea off the Coral Triangle doesn't forgive you. It just waits.

I'd learned that lesson twice before I turned thirty — once with a collapsed lung in the Sulu Strait and once with a woman named Adelita who decided my line of work was better suited to men who came home. The sea took its time on both counts. It's patient like that. It lets you think you've won something, then hands you the bill with interest.

So when I rolled over the gunwale of the *Magpie* at six in the morning, breathing compressed air through a regulator older than most navies' patience, I wasn't thinking about treasure. I was thinking about the current, the visibility, and the fact that somewhere out beyond the reef shelf, a navy patrol boat had cut its engines an hour before dawn and gone quiet as a held breath.

That last part was the problem.

* * *

The wreck sat in forty meters of green water on a sloping shelf of coral rubble, and officially it didn't exist. That's not salvage-man poetry. I mean it literally. No chart marked it. No harbor office would discuss it. The navy had run three "survey exercises" over the site in the past month, each one dragging sonar across the seabed like a man combing a graveyard for something he'd dropped.

I knew about it because of a fisherman named Old Tuan, who knew about it because his grandfather had watched a ship burn there one night sixty years ago and been paid in rice to never speak of it. Tuan spoke of it for two bottles of arrack and a promise I'd take him diving someday, which we both knew was a lie, because Tuan was seventy-one and had one lung that worked on a part-time basis.

"Ship went down quick," he'd told me, gesturing with the bottle toward a horizon that held nothing but heat shimmer. "No storm that night. My grandfather said she burned from the inside."

Ships that burn from the inside are either accidents or arguments. And ships the navy won't name are almost always arguments.

* * *

Forty meters down, the world goes the color of old bruises. The light comes down thin and gray-green, and everything moves — not fast, but constantly, the way a sleeping animal twitches. The wreck lay on her starboard side, hull furred with a decade's growth of soft coral and black sponge, her superstructure broken open like a cracked walnut. She wasn't ancient. Her plates were riveted steel, her lines pre-war, the kind of coastal freighter that hauls copra and diesel and never makes the shipping pages unless something goes wrong.

Something had gone wrong.

I swam her length slowly, running my gloved hand along the rail the way you'd check a horse's flank. Brass first. That was the job. The *Magpie* ran on brass and diesel and my stubbornness, in roughly that order, and a wreck like this could carry two hundred kilos of porthole fittings, gauges, telegraph housings — enough to keep me fed through the monsoon without asking questions I didn't want answered.

But even as I worked the first porthole free with a spanner and a prayer, the questions were already queuing up.

First question: why did a coastal freighter have portholes this heavy? Naval spec. Civilian boats don't get glass thick enough to survive depth charges, because civilian boats aren't supposed to be near depth charges.

Second question: why was there no manifest? Every wreck has a paper ghost — registry, cargo list, insurance claim, some clerk's signature somewhere saying *this vessel existed*. This one had nothing. I'd spent three evenings in the port authority's back rooms with a bottle of cheap gin and a smile that cost me forty dollars in bribes, and the answer had come back the same every time: no record. Not lost record. Never-recorded. Ships don't get born without paperwork unless somebody made sure they wouldn't.

Third question, and this is the one that kept my heart rate up worse than the depth: why patrol a wreck you refuse to admit exists?

I'd seen the patrol boat's searchlight sweep the water two nights ago from the deck of the *Magpie*, slow and methodical, painting the swells silver for a full minute before moving on. You don't waste fuel doing that over an empty patch of sea. You do it when you're looking for divers. Or when you're making sure nobody else becomes one.

I put the questions away in the compartment where I keep things I can't afford to think about yet, and I got back to work.

* * *

Brass comes off a wreck easier than you'd think, if you're patient. Sixty years of coral holds fittings in place like a jeweler's setting, and you chip around them, ease them loose, bag them. It's quiet work. Meditative, almost. Down there, with your own bubbles ticking upward and the parrotfish crunching away at the coral like office workers at lunch, you can pretend the world is simple: metal down here, money up there, and nothing in between but water.

I'd cleared the promenade deck and was working along the boat deck when I found the first thing that didn't fit.

A lifeboat davit — empty. Fine. But the falls were still attached, the blocks seized with rust, meaning the boat had never launched. And beside it, bolted to the deck, a rack of signal flares, every tube fired. All of them. Somebody on this ship had stood right here and burned through their entire distress inventory, and nobody had come.

You feel that kind of thing through a dive suit. Or I do. Maybe it's superstition, maybe it's the nitrogen, but wrecks hold their last moments the way a room holds an argument after everyone's left. This one's last moment had been loud, bright, and unanswered.

I hung there in the green water and let the current push against me, and I thought about Tuan's grandfather watching the ship burn from the inside, and I thought: *accident or argument.*

Then I saw the cabin.

* * *

Every wreck has a layout you can read like a face. Bridge up top, crew quarters aft, engine room amidships, cargo holds below. This one followed the pattern except for one detail: a compartment on the port side, just forward of the engine room bulkhead, that had no business existing. Small — maybe three meters square. Steel door. And the door was sealed.

Not jammed. Sealed. Welded shut from the outside, the bead of the weld still visible under the coral like a scar that hadn't learned to blend in. Somebody had closed that door while the ship was alive, and they'd done it deliberately, and they'd done it with a torch.

Salvage men have a rule older than salvage: never open what someone else welded shut. It's not superstition exactly. It's arithmetic. A weld means somebody wanted something contained, and contained things come in two varieties — dangerous and valuable — and both varieties attract the same audience.

I should have bagged my brass and gone up.

Here's the thing about me, though, and it's the thing that's kept me alive and kept me broke in roughly equal measure: I don't like doors that lie to me. A sealed door on an unrecorded ship in patrolled waters isn't a door anymore. It's a sentence with the last word missing. And I have never once in my life walked away from an unfinished sentence.

The weld was old but not perfect. Salt had gotten into the seam, and where salt gets in, iron gives up. I worked a pry bar into the joint at the hinge side, where the bead was thinnest, and leaned into it with my legs, not my back — the one piece of advice my father ever gave me that was worth the air it took to say it.

The weld let go with a sound I felt more than heard, a deep groan through the hull that sent a cloud of silt blooming off the deck and a school of cardinalfish scattering like startled thoughts. Then the door swung inward, slow, obedient, and the dark behind it was the particular dark of a place that hasn't been opened in a very long time.

I clicked on my torch.

* * *

The beam cut through the black and showed me a room that explained nothing and promised everything.

It was an office. That was the first absurdity — a proper office, aboard a ship that officially never existed, bolted to a deck above an engine room, welded shut like a tomb. A desk, steel, bolted to the floor. A chair, tipped over. Shelving on one wall, mostly empty, but the dust lines on the shelves told you shapes had been taken out — boxes, ledgers, something rectangular and heavy, removed in a hurry or removed carefully, I couldn't tell which.

And on the desk, fused into a single lump by sixty years of corrosion, a typewriter.

I looked at that typewriter for a long time. There's a species of dread that lives underwater, and it doesn't announce itself. It just settles on you, cold through the neoprene, like a hand deciding whether you belong. The room felt read. Felt searched. Felt like the end of an investigation rather than the beginning of one — except whoever had investigated it had left in a hurry, and whatever they'd concluded had been worth welding a door over.

There were papers. Of course there were papers; there are always papers, and they're always pulp and glue by the time you find them. These were worse than pulp. They'd been soaked, dried, soaked again, and now they lay in the drawer of the desk like dead leaves pressed by someone who hated the tree. I lifted one out with two fingers and it came apart in a brown lace. Fragments of type were visible on some pieces. Numbers, mostly. Columns of numbers. One fragment had a word I could half-read:

*—transfer of prisoners—*

I held that scrap in my glove and the current moved me gently, the way the sea does when it wants you to consider your options.

Prisoners. On a freighter with naval-spec portholes that burned from the inside and whose distress flares nobody answered, in waters the navy surveys monthly and charts never. A sealed office. Removed records. And a typed phrase about the transfer of prisoners, dissolving between my fingers like it was embarrassed to be remembered.

Somewhere above me, faint through forty meters of water, I heard it: engines. Low, distant, deliberate. The kind of engine note a patrol boat makes when it isn't in a hurry because it knows exactly where it's going.

I put the paper scrap in my bag — not with the brass, in a separate pocket, though God knows why, since I couldn't have told you what I intended to do with it — and I took one last look around the little welded room. The desk. The fallen chair. The empty shelves with their clean dust-shadows, shaped like ledger books and ammunition boxes and, if I was reading one silhouette right, a rifle rack.

This wasn't a cargo ship's office. This was a command post. And commands posts don't ride coastal freighters hauling copra.

The engine note grew, unhurried, closer.

* * *

I'll say this for fear: it organizes you. My hands stopped shaking the moment they had a job to do. I kicked out of the cabin, pulled the door shut behind me — no point advertising — and swam the wreck's shadowline toward the anchor line I'd dropped off her bow, keeping the hull between me and the sound. My bubbles ticked upward. The parrotfish had gone quiet. Even the sea, forgiving nothing, forgetting less, seemed to be holding its breath.

At the line I paused, thirty meters down, and looked back at the wreck lying in her bed of rubble, furred and silent, keeping her secret the way the drowned keep everything — absolutely, and without complaint.

Sixty years, I thought. Sixty years of patrols and paperwork and a fisherman bought off with rice, all to keep one burned ship unremembered. That's not embarrassment. Embarrassment fades. That's fear. Somebody, somewhere, is still afraid of what's inside that hull — afraid enough to spend a nation's navy on guarding a grave.

And I had just picked the lock on the mausoleum and stolen a piece of the corpse's diary.

I went up the line slow, decompressing, watching the light strengthen from bruise-gray to honest green to the hammered silver of the surface. The *Magpie* bobbed at her anchor, small and ugly and mine, and the patrol boat was nowhere in sight yet — but I could hear her now clearly, maybe two kilometers off, coming on at a pace that said *routine* and meant anything but.

I broke the surface, spat my reg, and hauled myself over the transom. My deckhand, a gap-toothed Bajau kid named Yusuf who asked no questions because I paid him not to, took one look at my face and started coiling the anchor line before I said a word.

"Pull the hook," I said. "Quietly."

He pulled the hook quietly.

As the *Magpie* swung her head around and began the long, idle-looking crawl back toward the coast — because a guilty boat runs, and only an innocent one putters — I sat on the stern locker with the bag between my knees and my heart going like a bilge pump, and I took out the paper scrap one more time.

*—transfer of prisoners—*

The ink was fading even now, the way things do when the sea decides it wants them back. I memorized the shape of the letters instead. Then I looked east, toward the highlands rising blue and impossible behind the coastal haze — country I'd never had reason to enter, mountains where the maps went vague and the roads went to rumor.

I didn't know it yet. I didn't know that the sea had just handed me the first installment of a bill I'd be paying for a year, in blood and snow and other people's ghosts. I didn't know about the prospector, or the map, or the girl named Sela, or any of it.

All I knew was what every diver learns eventually, usually too late:

The wilderness forgives nothing. And I had just trespassed on somebody's secret — somebody with a navy.

Yusuf tapped my shoulder and pointed astern. The patrol boat had come around the headland, white hull catching the morning sun, and she was turning toward us, unhurried as a cat that has all day.

"Innocent," I reminded myself, and puttered on.
