## 2. A Map for a Promise

The tide brought me a dying man the way it brings everything else to this coast — without asking, without apology, and slightly ahead of schedule.

I was mending net when I heard him first: a sound like a gull with opinions, coming from the black rocks below my camp. Then I saw him, or rather saw what was left of him, wedged in the wrack line where the sea had deposited him with all the care of a drunk emptying his pockets. One arm hooked through a coil of kelp. Boots gone. Shirt gone to ribbons. And across his ribs, three holes that hadn't been made by coral.

I've pulled drowned men off beaches before. Most of them are past arguing. This one looked up at me with eyes like wet slate and said, "You're the diver."

Which was either remarkable deduction or remarkable bad luck on both our parts.

* * *

His name, when he could remember it, was Ardan. Tomas Ardan. He said it the way men say things they're not sure they'll need again — quickly, like paying a toll.

I got him up to my fire because the alternative was watching him die on the rocks, and even a man who trusts no one has standards about where corpses accumulate. My camp is a lean-to of driftwood and salvaged canvas above the tideline, half a day's walk from the port, chosen precisely because nobody comes here. The navy doesn't. The fishermen don't — they say the water's bad luck, which is their polite way of saying they've seen patrol boats circle this stretch at night and decided ignorance was cheaper than curiosity.

Ardan shivered so hard the driftwood bench creaked under him. The bullet wounds were ugly but not immediately fatal-looking, which told me the sea had been working on him longer than the bullets had. Salt water is honest that way. It gets into everything and tells you exactly how long you've been lying about being fine.

"Who shot you?" I asked, because it seemed relevant.

He laughed. It came out as a wet rattle, like a pump drawing air through mud. "Navy," he said. "Or men dressed as navy. Up in the hills there's no difference anymore."

I should have walked away then. Any sensible man would have. A stranger washes up shot by soldiers, babbling about hills and uniforms, and the correct response is to let the tide take back its property and go back to your nets. I've spent forty years learning that other people's trouble is heavier than it looks — it always weighs more once you pick it up.

But he'd said "navy." And I'd spent two weeks diving a wreck the navy wouldn't name, stripping brass off her bones while patrol boats drew lazy circles overhead like sharks that had been paid to be bored. So when a bleeding man washed onto my private beach and accused the same institution, some crooked part of me sat up and listened.

"You were up in the highlands," I said. Not a question. His hands told me — scarred, cracked, the nails blackened from cold rock. Those weren't diver's hands or fisherman's hands. They were a prospector's hands, the kind that come back down out of mountains looking like they lost an argument with the mountain and are still sore about it.

"Twenty years," he said. "Looking. Everyone looking." He closed his eyes. "Found it last month."

"What?"

He opened his eyes again, and this time there was something underneath the pain — a kind of ferocious satisfaction, the look of a man who has beaten a thing that beat everyone else. "The cache," he whispered. "Everyone's grandfather's ghost story. It's real. I found it."

* * *

Now, here is what you need to know about me, since apparently we're going to be spending time together: I don't believe in treasure.

Not the way most people mean it. I believe in brass fittings and copper sheathing and engine parts, because those have weight and price tags and can be sold to men who count money for a living. Gold in stories is always guarded by something worse than gold — a curse, a rival, a government — and the government is the worst of the three, because curses only kill you and governments keep killing you after you're dead, just to make a point.

But a dying man's conviction is a strange currency. It spends differently than a sober man's. And Ardan wasn't delirious — that was the unsettling part. Delirious men wander. They talk about wives they never had and ships that sail on grass. Ardan talked in straight lines, short and hard, like a man laying track. He knew where he'd been. He knew what he'd seen. He knew who wanted him dead.

And he knew, with the terrible clarity of the recently shot, that he was not going to survive the night.

"I need a boat," he said. "Port. Doctor."

I looked at his wounds, then at the tide, then at the sky, which was doing that bruised purple thing it does before weather. "The port's six hours by boat if the sea cooperates. It won't. And a doctor will ask how you got those holes, and when you tell him, he'll report it, and then soldiers will come here, to my camp, asking me questions I don't want to answer."

"So you'll leave me."

"I didn't say that."

"You thought it." His voice was gravel over glass. "I can hear you thinking it. Men like you think fast and feel slow."

That stung, mostly because it was true. I am not a good man. I want to be clear about that up front, because this story will eventually require you to believe I did a decent thing, and I'd rather you understood the discount it was bought at. I'm a salvage diver. I take things from dead ships. The whole trade is legalized grave-robbing with better paperwork. Trusting people is a luxury I gave up somewhere around the third partner who sold me out, the second wife who took the boat, and the first friend who took everything else.

But leaving a man to die alone on a beach — that's not caution. That's just arithmetic done wrong.

I fed him broth. He kept most of it down, which surprised us both.

* * *

It was full dark when he asked for the oilcloth packet.

"It's waterproof," he said. "Inside pocket of my coat. If the coat's still on me."

It was, barely, stiff with salt. Inside the oilcloth was a bundle wrapped in waxed linen, tied with cord that had been knotted and reknotted so many times it looked like a cat's cradle designed by an anxious man. I set it by the fire and he watched me with those slate eyes, and I could see him weighing me — the way I weigh wrecks, checking what's worth taking and what'll drag you down.

"Open it," he said.

I did. Inside was a map.

Hand-drawn. Ink on survey paper, the kind the old colonial surveys used, heavy stock that survives damp. The coastlines were confident, professional. The interior was not — it was dense with a prospector's shorthand, elevation marks and stream crossings and notes in a ciphered margin, little symbols that meant nothing to me. And in the upper reaches, circled twice, a mark like a broken triangle with numbers beside it.

"That's twenty years of my life," Ardan said. "Every false lead, every season lost, every partner who quit or died or stole from me. It's all in that paper. The route. The water sources. Where the ground lies." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it finished there was blood on his lip that hadn't been there before. "And the cache. Grid-true. I fixed it against the peaks myself. Nobody else alive knows those numbers."

"Then why show me?"

"Because dead men's secrets are worth nothing, and I'm about to be one."

I turned the map over. Blank. I turned it back. The broken triangle stared at me like an eye.

"There's gold up there," he said. "Paymaster gold, war gold, whatever you want to call it. Enough to buy this whole coastline and the honesty of everyone on it. That's the problem, diver. That's exactly the problem."

"How do you figure?"

"Because enough gold to buy everyone's honesty means someone already tried to buy mine." He shifted against the driftwood, and I heard the wet sound of the wound moving, and I made myself keep looking at his face instead of flinching. "Men came for me. Two nights ago. Professional. Not bandits — bandits shout. These ones whispered. They knew my name, my route, my daughter's village. They knew everything except where I'd cached the map."

My mouth had gone dry. "Your daughter."

"Sela." He said the name and something moved behind his face, tectonic, deep down where faces keep their faults. "Sela Ardan. Coastal village, south of the port. Kailu Bay. She keeps the lighthouse records — no, she did, years ago. She works the fish sheds now." He stopped. Started again. "She hasn't spoken to me in nine years."

"Why not?"

"Because I chose the mountains over her mother's deathbed, and then chose them again over her wedding, and then again over her son's christening, and a man only gets to make that choice so many times before his daughter stops counting it as a choice and starts counting it as an answer." He recited it flatly, like coordinates. Like a man who had surveyed his own failures as carefully as any ridge line and marked each one grid-true.

"And now you want me to carry your map to her."

"No." His hand came up, trembling, and pressed down on the oilcloth over my fingers. "I want you to carry it *for* her. There's a difference. She'll want to burn it. She should. But she won't, because she's my daughter, and stubbornness is the only thing I ever gave her besides the name." His grip tightened. "The gold isn't hers. It isn't anyone's. But the truth of how I died — that's hers. She deserves to know what killed me, and it wasn't bullets. Bullets are just how they signed it."

* * *

Here is the moment I should describe honestly, because everything afterward grows out of it like a crack grows out of a foundation.

He asked for my word. Not my help — my word. He distinguished between them, this dying man, with the precision of someone settling accounts. Help could be withdrawn. Help could fail. A word, once given, became a debt that followed you, and he wanted to know that when he stopped breathing, something of his would still be owed and therefore still alive.

"Bram Montrose," he said. "That's your name? The diver they talk about at the port?"

"They talk about lots of divers at the port."

"They talk about the one who brought up the *Corvane*'s safe single-handed. Who dives wrecks the navy pretends aren't there." He almost smiled. Blood in the creases of it. "You're already trespassing on somebody's secret, Montrose. I could see it in you the minute you dragged me up this beach. You dive where you shouldn't. You take what they don't want found. Well." He tapped the oilcloth. "Here's more of the same. Only this time it comes with a daughter attached."

I looked at the map. I looked at the fire. I looked at the sea, black and patient beyond the tideline, doing its eternal work of taking everything back eventually.

"I don't give my word," I said. "It's the only thing I own outright, and I don't spend it."

"Then spend it once," he said. "On me. I'm cheap. I'm nearly free."

The wry bastard was dying and making jokes, and I hated him a little for it, the way you hate anyone who makes virtue look easy. Because that's what it would have been, refusing him — easy. Walk away. Bury him above the tideline with a cairn and a lie about pirates. Keep my word unspent, my hands clean, my beach quiet. The sensible arithmetic.

But I kept thinking about the wreck. The one the navy wouldn't name. The sealed cabin no manifest explained, the patrol boats circling like they were guarding a grave rather than a hazard. Two weeks I'd been telling myself it was none of my business. And now the business had washed ashore and bled on my driftwood and said *Sela*, and the arithmetic had changed, because arithmetic always changes when the numbers turn out to be people.

"If I give my word," I said slowly, "it's iron. You understand that? I don't renegotiate. I don't quit halfway. If I say I'll get this map to your daughter, I'll get it there if it costs me the boat, the season, and the use of my legs. Is that what you want on your conscience, dying on my beach?"

Something in his face eased. Not peace exactly — more like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"That's exactly what I want," he said. "That's the only thing I want. Give me your word, Montrose. Word of a diver. Word of a man the sea hasn't managed to drown yet."

I gave it.

I said it out loud, to a dying stranger, on a beach I'd chosen specifically so that promises couldn't find me. "Your map reaches Sela Ardan. Whatever it costs. Whatever it leads to. You have my word."

He exhaled, long and slow, like a valve releasing. "There," he murmured. "Now I'm rich after all."

* * *

He lasted until maybe two in the morning.

We talked, in the gaps between the shivering, about small things. He'd been born inland, farm country, hated the sea his whole life and died on it anyway, which he found funny in a bitter, private way. He'd had a wife named Odile who sang badly and often. He'd once found a nugget the size of a quail's egg and traded it for medicine he ended up not needing, and he counted that as the luckiest mistake of his life, though he couldn't explain why and I didn't ask.

Near the end, the talking stopped being conversation and became something else — a man reading aloud from his own closing ledger. He made me repeat the promise twice more. He made me swear I wouldn't sell the map, wouldn't trade it, wouldn't dig for the cache myself before finding her. Each oath cost him visible effort, like a man lifting weights with his soul.

"The margins," he said, in the last hour. "The ciphered marks. Old survey code. Navy surveyors used it, thirty, forty years ago. Someone who knows the old charts can read it." His breath was coming shorter now, spaced out like a man climbing. "Find a cartographer. An old one. One who worked the coastal surveys before they burned the archives."

"Why burned?"

"Because maps are confessions, Montrose. Every line on a chart is something somebody saw and somebody else decided you shouldn't." He closed his eyes. "Remember that. When you're up there. Everything on that paper is a confession."

Then, near dawn, with the fire burned down to orange memory and the tide turning over below the rocks, he stopped talking altogether. His breathing went ragged, then spaced, then — between one silence and the next — simply didn't resume.

The last thing out of him, fainter than the surf, was the name again. "*Sela.*"

Just that. Not a sentence. Not a plea. A name, spoken the way you'd drive a survey stake — one final mark, grid-true, fixing the location of everything that mattered.

* * *

I buried him above the tideline where the sea couldn't collect him back. Took me till midmorning, cutting into ground that fought like a live thing, roots and stone and the general resentment of wilderness toward graves. I stacked a cairn over him, the way they do up in the hills he loved, though I doubt the hills would claim him. No marker. Markers attract questions, and questions attract the wrong kind of visitors.

Then I sat on the driftwood bench with the oilcloth packet in my lap and conducted the internal argument of my life.

Option one: burn the map. Promise fulfilled in the technical sense — delivered to no one, harmlessly destroyed, secret safe, my beach quiet, my season intact. Option two: sell it. There were men at the port who'd pay well for a prospector's fix on a legendary cache, and I knew at least three of them by their first names and their worst habits. Option three: find this daughter, hand her a map she'd probably hate, inherit a dead man's enemies, and spend my word like the fool he'd made of me.

I want to report that I deliberated for days. I didn't. It took about an hour, and most of that was me staring at the fire pretending to deliberate while some stubborn, submerged part of me — the part that dives wrecks the navy won't name, the part that's never once walked away from a locked cabin — quietly made the decision on my behalf.

Because here's what I learned from Tomas Ardan in his last six hours: a man's word is the only cargo that appreciates. Brass corrodes. Gold corrupts. Hulls go to worms. But a promise kept compounds, quietly, in ways you can't chart — and a promise broken sinks everything it was loaded on.

Besides. He'd said the margins were navy survey code. Old charts. Burned archives.

And I happened to know a woman at the port who used to draw charts for the very office that burned them.

I wrapped the oilcloth tight, stowed it inside my coat, and started walking toward the harbor — carrying a dead man's confession, a living girl's inheritance, and the growing suspicion that I'd just signed on for a voyage considerably longer than six hours.

The tide came in behind me and erased my tracks off the sand. The wilderness forgives nothing, but it forgets fast, and I was counting on exactly that.

In the port town, in a tavern that smelled of turpentine and regret, there was a disgraced cartographer named Vane who drank like she was trying to drown a specific memory — and I had a map with navy survey marks that needed reading, and a feeling in my gut that her memory and my map were about to become very well acquainted.
