## 3. The Cartographer's Debt

The tavern was called the Drowned Compass, which struck me as either honest advertising or a cry for help, depending on how you felt about your own direction in life. I'd been feeling ambivalent about mine for three days — ever since a dying man bled out on my tarp with my promise in his mouth and his daughter's name on his lips.

I found Isolde Vane exactly where the harbor gossip said she'd be: third stool from the end, back to the wall, a bottle of something cheap and brown in front of her and a look on her face like she was grading the world's penmanship and finding it wanting.

She was younger than her reputation. Late thirties, maybe, with ink stains worked so deep into her fingers they looked like gloves, and dark hair pinned up with what I swear was a drafting compass. Her eyes were the problem. They were clear. Sober-clear, sharp-clear, the kind of clear that made me suspect the drinking was performance, or at least had started as one and gotten out of hand somewhere along the way.

"You're staring," she said without turning around.

"Occupational habit. I salvage things."

"Then you're in the wrong trade. Everything worth salvaging in this room drowned years ago." She turned then, and gave me the once-over that harbor women give men who smell of salt and secrets. "You're Montrose. The wreck-diver. The one who works the water the navy pretends isn't there."

"Reputation travels."

"Reputation *limps* into port and drinks until someone feeds it. Sit down or don't, but stop looming. You're blocking my view of nothing."

I sat. It seemed safer than the alternatives.

"I need a map read," I said.

"No."

"I haven't shown it to you yet."

"You have a map with navy survey marks on it, or you wouldn't be here, and if it has navy survey marks on it, then reading it is how cartographers end up at the bottom of harbors with their lungs full of answers. I've seen it done." She lifted the bottle, found it empty, and set it down with the tenderness people reserve for things that have failed them completely. "So no."

"The man who drew it died in my camp two nights ago. Shot twice and half-drowned, and he held on long enough to make me promise I'd get this to his daughter." I put the oilskin packet on the bar between us but kept my hand flat on top of it. "I don't break promises. But I can't keep this one alone, because the margins are ciphered, and the cipher looks like survey work. Old survey work. Navy survey work."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not interest exactly. Interest's uglier cousin.

"And you came to me because?"

"Because everyone says Vane was the best chart-maker the Survey Office ever produced, before whatever happened happened. And because disgraced people are the only ones who'll touch disgraced maps."

"That's the first flattering thing anyone's said to me in four years, and it's still an insult." She looked at the packet the way a gambler looks at cards he knows are marked. "Show me one corner. One. And if I tell you to walk away, you walk away, and we never met."

I peeled back the oilskin enough to expose the upper margin of the map — just the border, the grid references, the little cluster of symbols stamped in violet ink that most people would take for decoration.

Isolde Vane went very still.

It wasn't dramatic. No gasp, no trembling hand. She just stopped moving entirely, the way small animals do when a shadow crosses them, and for a long moment the tavern noise — dice, arguments, a piano missing three keys — seemed to happen around her rather than near her.

Then she reached out and touched the violet mark with one ink-stained fingertip, feather-light, like checking whether a scar still hurt.

"This is a Halloway mark," she said quietly. "Trig-survey convention, third revision. We used it on the highland grids in the war years." She looked up at me, and the drunk was gone from her face so completely I wondered if she'd ever really been there. "Where did a dead prospector get a map with *my office's* marks on it?"

"*Your* office?"

"I told you I was Survey Office. You didn't ask which years, and you didn't ask why I left." She pulled the packet toward herself, and I let her, because some hooks you just have to swallow. She unrolled the map flat on the bar, weighted the corners with two glasses and an ashtray, and studied it with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of a person reading their own past written in someone else's blood.

The map itself was good work. Hand-drawn, meticulous, the coastlines confident, the interior ranges rendered in careful hachures. But it was the margins where the prospector had lived: annotations in three different inks, decades apart by the fading, and threaded through all of it a cipher of numbers and survey shorthand that meant nothing to me and clearly everything to her.

"He didn't draw this base map," she said. "He copied it. From an original." Her finger traced a line of cipher. "And these aren't random. This is grid-conversion notation. Someone encoded coordinates using the old wartime datum — the one we retired after the war because it put everything forty meters off from where it claimed to be." A short, humorless laugh. "Which means whoever made this wanted the location findable only by someone who knew the old system. Someone trained in it. There can't be more than a dozen of us alive who read this fluently, and half of us are dead or drinking."

"Can you read it?"

"I could read it in my sleep. That's rather the problem." She straightened up and looked at me fully for the first time, and I got the sense I was being weighed against something — not money, not danger, but some private ledger she kept behind her eyes. "Before I decode a single number, Montrose, you're going to answer a question honestly, because I've spent enough of my life around naval officers to know what dishonesty costs. Why you? Why does a brass-stripping salvager carry a dead man's promise through hostile waters instead of selling that map to the highest bidder? Because the highest bidder exists. Dekker's people would pay for it. Certain officers would pay more."

"How do you know about Dekker?"

"Everyone knows about Dekker. He's a tide — he comes into every port eventually and leaves nothing floating." She waved the question off. "Answer the real question."

I considered lying to her. It would've been easy; she was three bottles deep into a performance and I'm a convincing man. But there was something in the directness of her that made lying feel like cheating at solitaire.

"A man died holding my hand," I said. "His last word was his daughter's name. Sela. I told him I'd see the map delivered. I've broken every other kind of vow there is — to employers, to governments, to a woman in Suva whose name I still drink to sometimes — but I've never yet broken a deathbed promise, and I'm not going to start with this one. If delivering it means decoding it first, then decoding it first. That's the whole of it."

Isolde studied me for a long time. Then she did something unexpected: she laughed. One short, genuine bark of a laugh, quickly strangled, as if it had escaped custody.

"A deathbed promise. God help me." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "You know what I was doing four years ago, Montrose? I was signing my name to charts I knew were wrong. Not wrong because I made errors — wrong because I was ordered to move a reef ninety meters east of where it actually sits, so a transport could claim it never ran aground there, so forty-one drowned sailors could be officially listed as lost due to 'navigational error by the master.' My error, officially. I refused to sign the second version. They took my commission, my credentials, and my good name, and distributed them among more cooperative men." She dropped her hands and looked at me with terrible clarity. "So when you say the navy won't acknowledge your wreck — understand that not acknowledging things is not an aberration of theirs. It's a *discipline*. A practiced one. And I have been waiting four years for someone to walk into this tavern carrying a piece of it."

The tavern noise swelled and fell around us. Somewhere near the piano a man was losing an argument about fish.

"So you'll read it," I said.

"I'll read it." She held up a finger. "For terms. One: a full share of whatever the cache holds. Not a finder's fee — a share. I'm not renting you my eyes, I'm investing them. Two: I come along. All the way. Whatever's at those coordinates, I see it with my own eyes, because if it's what I think it might be, I want to stand on it. Three—" she hesitated, and for a moment the clarity flickered and I saw the woman underneath, the one the drinking was built to hold down, "—three, if we find what I think we might find, I get to put my name on the corrected chart. Officially. In ink. Even if it takes the rest of my life and ruins whatever's left of my reputation to do it."

"That last one might cost you more than the first two."

"It already has. That's the point." She extended her hand across the bar, ink-stained and steady. "Do we have a bargain, Mr. Montrose?"

I shook it. Her grip was stronger than the bottle suggested, and dry, and cold at the fingertips the way hands get when they've been holding pens too long in unheated rooms.

"One condition of mine," I said. "You don't decode anything past what we need for route planning until we're committed. Once I know what's in those margins, there's no walking it back, and I want us both past the point of no return before either of us has to live with the knowledge."

"Wise." She gathered up the map with surprising gentleness, re-rolled it, and slid it inside her coat, against her chest, like a letter from a dead friend. "I'll need paper, my old field instruments — they're pawned, incidentally, so add a redemption fee to my share — and a boat. When do we sail?"

"Three days. I have to find the daughter first. Sela Ardan. Last known in the fishing villages south of here."

"Sela Ardan." She said the name slowly, tasting it, and frowned. "Ardan. Josiah Ardan was a prospector who worked the highlands during the war — supplied the remote posts. There were stories about him. About what he carried besides ore samples." Her eyes came up to mine. "Montrose. The man who died in your camp. What was his first name?"

"Josiah."

Isolde closed her eyes briefly, and I watched four years of deliberate forgetting come undone around her like a knot slipping.

"Then the stories are true," she said softly, "and we're not chasing treasure. We're chasing evidence. And evidence, Mr. Montrose, is the one cargo the navy will sink whole ships to recover — including ships with us aboard." She drained the last inch of the dead bottle, stood, and steadied herself against the bar with the ease of long practice. "Get your boat ready. I'll start on the cipher tonight. And Montrose—"

"Yeah?"

"When you go looking for the daughter — be kind. People who hate their fathers hardest are usually the ones who loved them best, and grief with nowhere to go turns into a weapon pointed at whoever's standing closest." She headed for the door, then paused, silhouetted against the harbor light. "I'll be at the dock at dawn. Don't be late. I've waited four years for this. I'd hate to wait a fifth day."

She walked out into the evening, straight-backed, sober as a verdict, and I sat there a while longer with two empty glasses and an ashtray between us, thinking about how the wilderness forgives nothing and forgets fast, but people — people remember everything, and some of them spend their whole lives sharpening the memory into a blade.

I'd come looking for a map-reader.

I was starting to suspect I'd hired a fuse.

* * *

I bought back her instruments from the pawnbroker that night — a brass alidade, a box sextant, a set of drafting pens worn to the shape of her fingers — and the old man wrapped them in newspaper and said, "Fourth time she's redeemed these. First time anyone else paid." I didn't ask what that meant. Some debts you can read well enough from the margins.

At dawn, Isolde Vane was at the dock before I was, standing in the gray light with her collar up and the map already open on a crate, pencil flying across scrap paper, and she looked up at me with eyes that hadn't slept and said:

"Montrose. The cipher isn't hiding gold."
