## 4. The Runaway Heiress

I have been thrown out of better establishments than the Saltglass Tavern, but rarely with such enthusiasm.

That was two nights ago. Since then I had done what I always do when the world reminds me it has no use for me: I drank, I drew, and I waited for Bram Montrose to come to his senses and realize he needed me more than I needed him. The waiting was the humiliating part. The drinking was merely traditional.

He came at dawn, smelling of salt and bad decisions, and dropped a leather tube on my table hard enough to rattle the bottle.

"You're sober enough to read a map?"

"I'm sober enough to read a map. Whether I'm sober enough to like what it says is another question." I unrolled the prospector's chart across the sticky wood and felt that old familiar itch behind my eyes — the one that meant cipher, meant survey marks, meant someone had hidden something inside the cartography the way smugglers hide coins in a ship's ballast. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me. Navy killed him for it, I think."

"Then we're already in a charming story." I traced the margin with one finger, careful not to smudge the iron-gall ink. The symbols were old — older than the current naval hydrographic office, older than the war nobody in the ports would name out loud. "I can read this. Not tonight. Not drunk. And not for free."

"I assumed." He counted coins onto the table. Then he looked at me with those flat harbor-water eyes and said the thing I'd been dreading. "I've chartered a boat. We leave on the morning tide."

"We?"

"You and me. I need someone who can read what this map is actually saying. You need—" he glanced at the bottle, at the tavern, at the general wreckage of my circumstances, "—a reason."

I should have told him to go to hell. Instead I said, "I'll need my instruments," which is how a disgraced cartographer agrees to anything.

* * *

The boat was called the *Kestrel's Mercy*, which should have been my first warning. Nobody names a vessel Mercy and then has good things happen aboard her. She was a coastal trader, forty feet if she was generous, with a patched foresail and an engine that sounded like a consumptive uncle. Bram had hired her from a Tongan-Chinese skipper named Faleolo who said perhaps forty words a day and charged accordingly.

We loaded her through the grey of the morning: Bram's dive gear, my instrument chest, provisions, and a crate of naval-issue brass that Bram had stripped from the wreck and was smuggling with the serene confidence of a man who had decided the law was more of a suggestion. I sat in the stern with the map spread on my knees and worked the cipher while Faleolo took us out past the breakwater into a sea the color of hammered tin.

The cipher, for the record, was beautiful. The prospector — Ardan, Bram said his name was — had overlaid his cache coordinates onto a naval survey grid, the old one, the pre-war triangulation that the Hydrographic Office had formally disavowed after the armistice. Every "error" in his map was a deliberate displacement. Correct for the old datum and the numbers snapped into place like a lock giving way. I'd spent three years of my ruined career studying exactly that grid, because my father had helped draw it, and there is no irony the sea will not eventually serve you.

By the second evening I had a bearing. By the third, a heading. We were beating south along the coast toward the last port before the highlands when I noticed the water casks had been moved.

"Someone's been in the hold," I told Bram. He was splicing line, and he didn't look up, which I had learned was his way of taking things seriously.

"Rats."

"Rats don't drink from the casks and leave the stopper seated. Rats don't rearrange my chart case. Something in there has been reading my charts, Bram, and unless the rats of this coast are literate—"

He stood up. We opened the hold together, and there, wedged between a coil of anchor chain and a crate of Bram's contraband brass, was a young woman in a traveling dress worth more than the boat, asleep on a folded oilskin with the composure of someone who had done this before.

She woke the moment the hatch came off — eyes open, hands visible, a knife already palmed and pointed at nothing in particular, which told me she was frightened but trained. Then she saw Bram, assessed him, and put the knife away.

"Good morning," she said. "I was hoping to make it to the highlands before you noticed me."

* * *

Her name was Marisol Quennel, and if the name meant nothing to you, it meant everything to anyone who owned a harbor. The Quennel Line ran forty percent of the coastal shipping in the Triangle, plus the insurance, plus the warehouses, plus, if you believed the tavern talk, the customs officers themselves. She was twenty-three years old, she had a bruise on her wrist shaped like fingers, and she announced her terms before we'd even gotten her up the ladder.

"I can pay. Properly, not in tavern coins. I want passage to the highlands and I want you to not tell anyone where I went. In exchange, I work. I don't do decorative."

"You're a stowaway on a smuggler's boat," Bram said, "asking for a berth on an expedition that may get everyone killed, over a map that got one man already murdered."

"Yes. That's the sum of it. Do you want the money or not?"

I will admit I liked her instantly, in the way you like a fire that's just burned down your neighbor's house — appreciative, and slightly alarmed. Bram liked her less. He spent a full hour trying to find the flaw in her, turning her over like a suspect coin, and the frustrating thing was that she let him, answering everything with a directness that was either honesty or the best performance I'd ever seen.

"Why the highlands?" he finally asked. "There are a dozen islands in this sea where a rich girl could disappear."

"Because a rich girl can disappear on any of those islands, and a Quennel can be found on all of them. My family's reach is the sea. It's the mountains where it thins out." She rubbed the bruised wrist absently, like touching an old scar. "I'm to be married in three weeks. To a man named Corvane who owns half the pearl concessions and all of the debt my father won't admit to. I've met him four times. The last time, he took my hand to show me how a wife greets her husband's business partners, and he held it a moment longer than a demonstration requires." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes didn't. "So. The mountains."

Bram looked at me. I looked at the horizon, which is where cowards and cartographers keep their opinions.

"We're already crowded," he said.

"I've slept in your hold for two days. Crowded is established."

"We don't know you."

"No one knows anyone after two days. Ask me again in a month, if we're all still breathing." She dug into her reticule and produced a bank draft, folded with the crispness of a woman who had grown up watching her father ruin people with paper. "Half now. Half when we make the highlands. And Mr. Montrose — I know who you are. The diver who works wrecks the navy doesn't name. I grew up on shipping manifests. I know what a forbidden wreck is worth and I know what it costs. I'm not squeamish, and I'm not stupid, and I am very, very motivated."

There was a long silence in which Faleolo, at the tiller, said the most words I'd ever heard from him: "She paid me already."

Bram closed his eyes as if asking strength from a god he didn't believe in. "Fine. You carry your weight or you swim."

"Agreed," said Marisol Quennel, and shook his hand like a man.

* * *

I want to be clear about the next two days: I did not like her. She was twenty-three and had never been seasick, which is a moral failing in my view. She reorganized the galley without being asked. She asked questions — about the cipher, about the wreck, about the highlands — with the tireless appetite of someone who had been educated expensively and never once usefully. When I snapped at her, she absorbed it and came back with more questions, and I began to suspect that a girl raised in a house like hers had developed a callus over exactly the place I kept hitting.

The trouble came on the fifth day, at Port Meridian, where we put in for water and fresh food and made the mistake of believing, for one soft hour, that we were just another trader.

I saw the customs launch first — I have a cartographer's eye for vessels that are where they shouldn't be — and I said, quietly, "Bram. The brass."

The crate of naval salvage sat in the hold under a tarp that would not survive a determined inspection, and the *Kestrel's Mercy* was carrying no manifest that explained it, because there was no honest manifest that could. The launch was coming alongside with four officers in white and a bosun with a crowbar, and Bram had perhaps ninety seconds and a face like a man doing arithmetic at gunpoint.

"Overboard," I said. "We dump it."

"Two hundred pounds of brass. That's the expedition's operating money."

"Two hundred pounds of brass and a cell in Meridian, then. Choose."

And that was when Marisol stepped between us, and I watched the runaway heiress become something else entirely.

"Neither," she said. "You don't dump it and you don't hide it. You *declare* it." She was already pulling the tarp straight, squaring the crate, turning contraband into inventory with a few economical motions. "The Quennel Line ships salvage under bond all the time. There's a form — Customs Schedule Eleven, salvage declared in transit for assay at the next port of registry. Nobody uses it because nobody knows it exists. I know it exists. I grew up reading my father's clerks fall asleep over it." She was writing as she talked, fast and fluent, in a hand that belonged on a banknote. "Bram — you're now the master of a licensed salvage vessel. Faleolo, you're his mate. Isolde—" she glanced at me, and there was a glint in her eye I would come to know well and mostly dread, "—you're the assayer. Try to look like someone who owns a loupe."

"I own three loupes," I said, with more indignation than truth.

"Even better. Look expensive."

The officers came aboard at 1400. Their leader was a lieutenant with the damp handshake and apologetic eyes of a man who inspected boats the way dentists pull teeth — necessary, unpleasant, and done to someone else's mouth. He looked at our papers. He looked at the crate. He looked at Schedule Eleven, and I watched the words *Quennel Line procedure* pass across his face like weather, because every customs officer in the Triangle had signed on the dotted line somewhere beneath a Quennel, and no man alive wants to be the one who inconvenienced the family that signs his next posting.

"Salvage in transit," he said.

"Under bond," Marisol confirmed, and handed him the draft copy with the Quennel seal — she had a traveling seal, of course she had a traveling seal — pressed in wax the color of old blood. "My father would be happy to hear from you directly, Lieutenant, if there's any question about the line's compliance. He does so enjoy correspondence."

The lieutenant looked at the seal the way a man looks at a cliff edge, and stepped back from it.

They searched anyway — procedure — and here is the thing I will not forget: while they were poking through the hold, the lieutenant's junior man found my instrument case and opened it, and my charts were inside, and the Ardan map was inside *that*, folded inside a chart of the coast that would have taken him thirty seconds to unfold and one glance to make him very interested in us.

Marisol got there first. She didn't grab it — grabbing draws the eye. She simply reached past him, lifted the case, and said, "Careful with that one, it's worth more than your launch," and then, to the lieutenant, breezily: "Her survey instruments. She's assaying the salvage — you'll want accurate weights for the bond schedule, yes? The navy does so hate an inaccurate bond." And she set the case down in the stern sheets, on top of a folded sail, and stood beside it, chatting about assay procedures with the fluency of a born liar, until the officers lost interest in everything except escaping this conversation.

They went over the side at 1430. The launch puttered away. Bram stood at the rail watching it go, and then he turned and looked at Marisol Quennel for a long moment.

"That," he said finally, "was well done."

"I know," she said. And then, because she was twenty-three and had just discovered she was good at something her family never intended for her: "It felt *wonderful*."

I laughed. I hadn't meant to. It came out of me like something surfacing from a wreck — rusty, unexpected, real. She looked at me with surprise, and I looked back, and something was quietly renegotiated between us on that deck, some treaty neither of us signed aloud. I still thought she was a spoiled girl playing at adventure. But she was a spoiled girl who had just saved our expedition with a forged schedule and a family seal, and in my experience the world is run almost entirely by people like that, and it is better to have them beside you.

That night she took the middle watch without being asked, and I sat up later than I should have with the Ardan map, correcting the final displacement. The cache bearing resolved clean at last: a valley in the highlands, two days past the last village, in country the old surveys had marked with a symbol I recognized and wished I didn't.

A navy depot mark. The disavowed kind.

I didn't tell Bram yet. I wanted to be wrong first. I wanted to spend one more day being a disgraced cartographer on a small boat with a smuggler and a runaway, before the map turned into what I was beginning to fear it was.

At dawn we raised the last port before the mountains, and standing at the rail, scanning the waterfront with the focused hunger of someone reading a name off a headstone, was a young woman with her father's eyes and none of his forgiveness.

"There," Bram said quietly, beside me. "That's Sela."

The map in my case seemed suddenly very heavy, and the harbor ahead seemed suddenly very small, and I understood that we had just finished assembling something that could not be unassembled.
