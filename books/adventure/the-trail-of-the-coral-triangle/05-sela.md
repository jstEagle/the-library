## 5. Sela

The village of Tanjung Laut smelled of salt smoke and drying fish, which is to say it smelled like every coastal village I'd ever limped into with empty pockets and a full set of regrets. Isolde stood beside me on the dock with the map rolled in an oilcloth tube under her arm, looking at the crooked line of stilt houses like a woman being asked to read her own obituary.

"You're certain this is the place," she said.

"I'm certain the ferryman said Ardan lives here. Whether he meant the right Ardan is a wager I haven't placed yet."

"Your confidence is a comfort."

"My confidence is all I've got left after you drank half my rum ration last night."

"That was medicinal," she said, "and I'm still ill."

We found her at the far end of the beach, past the fish racks, in a boatyard that was less a business than a stubbornness. A half-built hull sat on rollers under a tarp patched so many times it had become more patch than tarp, and a woman was working inside it, caulking seams with a mallet and iron, each strike precise as a heartbeat. She didn't look up when our shadows fell across her.

"Whatever you're selling," she said, "the answer's no."

"We're not selling," I said.

"Then you're buying, and I don't have anything worth your money except opinions, and those are free but expensive."

Isolde glanced at me. I glanced back. Neither of us had rehearsed this part, because neither of us is the sort who rehearses anything, which is why we're both standing on beaches instead of sitting in parlors.

"Sela Ardan?" I tried.

The mallet stopped. She straightened up out of the hull, and I got my first proper look at her. Mid-twenties, sun-browned, hair tied back with what looked like a length of rigging line, hands scarred the way boatwrights' hands get scarred — not clumsily, but honestly, one honest wound at a time. Her eyes were her father's eyes, though she'd have gutted me for saying so. I knew because I'd spent three days watching that same color go out of them on my camp floor.

"Who's asking?"

"Bram Montrose." I let the name sit there. It bought me nothing; she'd never heard of me, or pretended not to have been. "I'm — I knew your father."

The change came over her face like weather. Not shock. Something older and more settled than shock, the way a coastline settles against the sea.

"No," she said. "You didn't."

"He gave me something for you—"

"No." She climbed out of the hull, set the mallet down with exaggerated care, the way people do when they're choosing not to throw it. "Whatever he told you, whatever he paid you, whatever story he spun — he did that. He was good at it. But you didn't *know* him, because nobody knew him, because there wasn't anybody in there to know. So take whatever he gave you back to wherever he's drinking these days, and tell him—" She stopped. Recalculated. "Where is he?"

And there it was, the question underneath the question, the one she'd been carrying around like ballast for years without admitting it was cargo.

I looked at Isolde. Isolde looked at the sea. Coward.

"He's dead," I said. "He washed into my camp four nights ago. Shot twice. He died about an hour later."

* * *

I want to tell you she cried. It would be tidier, and I've noticed people prefer tidy grief — the kind that comes with handkerchiefs and a clear beginning. What happened instead was stranger and worse. She went very still, the way water goes still before it decides whether to be ice or flood, and then she picked the mallet back up and went back to work, driving caulk into a seam that didn't need it, hammering the same inch of oak over and over while her shoulders shook and made no sound at all.

Isolde moved toward her. I caught her elbow. Some doors you have to open yourself from the inside, and if you push them they only jam tighter.

So we waited. The tide came in a foot. The fish racks creaked. Somewhere behind us children were racing a dog along the sand, and their laughter sounded obscene and wonderful at once, the way ordinary joy always does when you're standing next to somebody's ruin.

Finally the hammering stopped. She stayed bent over the hull a long moment, forehead nearly touching the wood, and when she turned around her face was dry and terrible and calm.

"How," she said.

"Shot. In the chest and the shoulder. He'd been in the water a while before I found him. He shouldn't have been alive at all, frankly. Stubborn man."

"Yes," she said, and something flickered through the calm — fast, gone. "That sounds like him. Who shot him?"

"I don't know yet. Men who wanted what he was carrying. Which was a map."

"A map." She laughed, and it was the ugliest sound I'd heard in weeks, and I once shared a cell with a customs officer who sang. "Of course. A map. He walked out on my mother when I was nine to go chase a map. He came back twice — twice in fifteen years — and both times he smelled of other people's camps and had stories that got longer and truer-sounding every visit, and both times he left again before the month was out. And now he dies for one." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, briskly, like swatting a fly. "What was in it? Gold? Please say gold. At least gold would be honest."

"There's a cache in the highlands. And ciphered margins Isolde here is still working on. And I think — I don't know yet, but I think — something the navy wants kept buried."

"The navy." Her jaw worked. "He used to blame the navy for everything. Bad weather, bad luck, his bad knee. We thought it was a joke."

"It may not be a joke anymore."

She looked at me for a long time. Then at Isolde, taking her measure — the fine ruined coat, the cartographer's ink-stained fingers, the eyes that had seen too much of the bottom of too many bottles. Then at the oilcloth tube.

"He really sent it to me," she said, and her voice cracked on *me* like a plank stepping wrong. "After everything. After all of it. He sent it to *me*."

"He made me promise," I said. "On his last breath. Deliver it to Sela. Those were nearly his last words. His actual last words were your name."

That broke her. Not loudly — she folded rather than shattered, sat down on the gunwale of the half-built boat like her strings had been cut, and put her face in her paint-flecked hands, and shook. Isolde sat down beside her without a word, which was the correct move, and I stood there feeling like the world's largest useless anchor, holding position, waiting for weather.

* * *

She fed us that evening, because grief in a fishing village apparently expresses itself as rice and smoked mackerel whether you want it or not. Her house was one room above the boatyard, neat as a chart drawer, everything squared and stowed. Except one corner. One corner held a shelf with a tin box on it, dented, salt-rusted, and I'd have bet my boat — such as she is — that nothing in that house had been touched less.

She caught me looking at it.

"Letters," she said. "He wrote. Twice a year, sometimes. Never an address I could write back to. Just postmarks from places that aren't on charts." She set a cup down harder than the cup deserved. "I never opened the last six. What would be the point? 'Dear Sela, sorry, love, Dad.' You can't drink apologies."

"No," Isolde said quietly. "But sometimes you can read them."

Sela looked at her. Something passed between the two women that I wasn't invited to and correctly pretended not to see.

"Why are you going?" I asked. "To the highlands. If you come. Why?"

She was quiet long enough that the lamp guttered and I thought she wouldn't answer.

"Because I hate him," she said finally. "And I've hated him so long I built my whole life on top of it, the way you build a house on a wreck — and now the wreck's gone and the house is standing on nothing, and I'd like to know what's down there. That's all. I'm not coming for treasure. I'm not coming for him. I'm coming because I have a coffin's worth of things to say to that man and no address to send them to, and the closest thing to an address left in the world is a map with ciphered margins." She looked at me, and her father's eyes were steady as lead lines. "Also, you two couldn't find your way out of a wet paper bag without me. You need someone who can build a boat, fix an engine, and lie to officials. I've watched you for ten minutes. It's painful."

"She's not wrong," Isolde said into her cup.

"I hadn't asked yet," I pointed out.

"You were working up to it. You have a face like an open ledger, Montrose. Everything gets written on it eventually."

* * *

We left two mornings later, at gray light, in a chartered ketch whose owner asked no questions because Marisol Quennel had paid him enough not to have a curiosity left in his body. Yes — the heiress was already aboard by then, discovered in the hold like a cat you didn't remember feeding, and that particular circus deserves its own telling, so I'll keep it brief here: Sela took one look at the silk-traveling-suit daughter of the Quennel shipping empire climbing out of a bilge with straw in her hair and said, "We're all going to die," and Marisol said, "Probably, but stylishly," and that was the whole treaty signed between them.

Sela stood at the stern rail as Tanjung Laut shrank behind us. She didn't wave. She watched the tin box's absence the way you watch a door you chose not to open — the six unopened letters stayed on the shelf, because she said she'd read them at the top of the mountain or not at all, and I understood that better than I let on. Some mail you deliver in person.

Isolde joined her at the rail, map tube under her arm, and said something low I didn't catch. Sela laughed — short, surprised at herself, rusty as an unused winch.

I went forward and checked the engine I already knew was sound, because that's what I do instead of having feelings, and I thought about a dying man saying *Sela* like it was the last word in the language, and I thought about how the wilderness forgives nothing, and how neither, apparently, do daughters — and how maybe that's the same thing, out here. The sea doesn't forgive. It just keeps what you give it, exactly as given, forever.

Ahead of us the coast bent north toward the reef channels, and beyond the reefs, faint as a bruise on the horizon, the highlands stood up into cloud.

Behind us, I noticed — because I notice things late but thoroughly — a boat with no lights holding station at the edge of visibility, patient as a debt.

It had been there since Tanjung Laut.

It was still there when the sun went down.
