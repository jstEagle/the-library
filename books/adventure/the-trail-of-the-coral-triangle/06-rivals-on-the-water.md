## 6. Rivals on the Water

I have been followed before. In Port-au-Prince, by a man my father paid to make sure I didn't change my mind about boarding a flight. In Monaco, by three sisters from a rival family who wanted to know which designer I was wearing so they could wear something else. Being followed is a language I speak fluently.

But nobody in Monaco ever followed me with a gun mounted on the bow.

I noticed them first because of the way Bram *didn't* notice them. That's the thing about men like Bram Montrose — they perform carelessness the way my mother performed affection, beautifully and with total commitment, and you learn to read what's underneath. He'd been at the wheel for two hours, chewing the same toothpick down to a splinter, and when the trawler appeared on our stern quarter — rust-orange hull, no name painted where a name should be, riding low the way boats ride when they're carrying more engine than they admit to — he didn't look at it. Not once. He adjusted our heading four degrees, very casually, the way you'd adjust your hat passing a stranger.

Four degrees doesn't sound like much. Four degrees, held long enough, is the difference between open water and the teeth of a reef.

"Friends of yours?" I asked, settling onto the bench beside him with the studied elegance of someone who has never once been seasick and never once been believed about it.

"No," Bram said.

"Enemies?"

"Not yet."

"Ah." I nodded sagely. "So we're in the courtship phase."

The corner of his mouth did something that on another man might have been called a smile. "That's Dekker," he said. "Rival salvager. Works out of Ternate. You'll hear him described as colorful, larger than life, a character. The descriptions come from people who've never owed him money."

"And what would you call him?"

Bram chewed the toothpick thoughtfully. "A man who's already decided how this ends and is just waiting for us to agree."

I looked back at the trawler. It had closed maybe half a mile without appearing to move at all, which is a trick good boats and dangerous men both know. On its deck, small with distance, figures moved with the unhurried economy of people who were not worried about anything. One of them raised an arm — not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. The way you acknowledge livestock.

"He knows about the map," I said.

"Everyone knows about the map," Bram said. "That's the problem with secrets. They're only secret until two people know them, and then they're just gossip with a body count."

* * *

Isolde came up from below with her charts rolled under one arm and her hangover worn like a piece of inherited jewelry — visible, heavy, hers by right. She'd been working the ciphered margins for three days, sleeping in shifts shorter than her cigarettes, and she emerged into the daylight squinting like it had personally wronged her.

"We're being tailed," she said. It wasn't a question. Isolde Vane misses nothing except, historically, the point of her own life.

"Dekker," Bram said.

"Mm." She unrolled a chart across the wheel box and weighted the corners with a coffee mug, a brass fitting Bram probably stole himself, and my elbow. "Then we have perhaps six hours before he stops being polite about it. This channel narrows at the Sula passage — here, and here. Reef on both sides, water the color of gin over coral that will open our hull like a letter. If he wanted to board us, that's where he'd do it. Or—" her finger traced north, "—he sends a fast boat ahead through the outer passage and waits for us at the far end. Pincer. Textbook."

"You've run blockades?" I asked.

"I've drawn maps of places people ran blockades," she said. "You absorb things. It's a occupational hazard of cartography — you spend your life looking down at the world from above, and eventually you start thinking like the weather. Detached. Cruel. Accurate."

"That's also just being a woman," I said, and she gave me a look that was ninety percent disdain and ten percent something warmer, which from Isolde is practically a marriage proposal.

Sela appeared at the rail. She'd spent most of the voyage the way she spent everything — silently, watchfully, carrying her father's death the way mules carry load, without complaint and without setting it down. She looked at the trawler for a long moment.

"How many men does he have?" she asked.

"Enough," Bram said.

"That's not a number."

"It's the only honest one." He spat the ruined toothpick over the side. "Dekker runs twelve, fifteen when business is good. Half of them are divers, half are muscle, and the line between the two is thinner than he'd like. His first mate is a Tongan named Faleolo who once broke a man's jaw for touching his chart table. He's loyal the way dogs are loyal, which is to say completely, and to whoever feeds him best."

"So we outrun him," I said. "This boat is faster. I checked."

"You checked."

"I grew up around ships, Bram. My family owns forty of them. I may be running away from that world, but I can still read a tonnage figure faster than you can read a chart."

Something shifted in his expression — a reassessment, quick and grudging. People underestimate me constantly. It's my single greatest asset, after the trust fund I'm currently pretending doesn't exist.

"She's right," Isolde said, surprising everyone including herself. "The *Kestrel* makes two knots better in flat water. But he doesn't need to beat us in flat water. He needs to beat us once, anywhere, and he gets to choose where."

"Then we don't let him choose," Bram said.

* * *

He chose anyway. That's the thing about men like Dekker — they don't ask the sea's permission, they just take it, and the sea, being the oldest corrupt institution in the world, lets them.

We made the Sula passage at dusk, which was either brilliant or suicidal depending on which of us you asked, and all of us asked differently. The light went the color of old brass, then old blood, and the reef rose on either side of us in dark serrated ridges, breaking white where the swell hit it, silent everywhere else. The water between was impossibly clear and impossibly shallow — you could see the coral twenty feet down, whole cities of it, purple and bone-white, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful.

Bram took us through at half throttle, eyes moving constantly: water, reef, mirror, trawler. Because the trawler was still there, holding station astern, patient as arithmetic.

"He's waiting for full dark," Bram said quietly. "Reef channels are suicide at night unless you know them. He thinks we don't."

"Do we?"

"I fished these waters for six years." A pause. "Before I learned to dive for other people's drowned secrets."

"And do you remember them? The channels?"

"I remember everything," Bram said, in a voice that made me decide not to ask what else he remembered.

Full dark came down like a lid. No moon — some phase or other; I'm an heiress, not an astronomer. The stars came out in that obscene tropical abundance that always looks fake, like a backdrop, and the reef disappeared entirely, and the only light in the world was our own stern lamp and, far behind, the orange glow of Dekker's deck.

And then a second light. Ahead. Green, low, moving — a running lamp cutting across our bow from the north, from the outer passage, exactly where Isolde's finger had traced the pincer six hours earlier.

"There's your textbook," I said.

Bram killed our lamp. We went dark, engine throttled back to a murmur, drifting in black water between black reefs while the green light swept past a quarter mile ahead, searching. Behind us, the orange glow held position. Waiting. Somewhere out there, Dekker was sitting in a wheelhouse drinking something expensive, watching two lights converge on one small boat, and I could have sworn I felt him smiling, the way you feel weather changing.

"Bram," Isolde whispered. Her voice had changed — stripped of irony, stripped of everything except the cartographer. "There's a third channel. It's not on the charts. My father surveyed this coast thirty years ago and left it off — a private mark, a smuggler's cut. Two hundred meters of water deep enough for our draft, if the sandbar hasn't moved. It threads between the reef systems and comes out in the strait beyond."

"Where?" Bram asked.

"If the sandbar hasn't moved," she said again, "then where I say it is."

"And if it has?"

"Then we're aground in shark water with two hostile boats converging, and honestly, Bram, I don't recommend it."

He looked at her for a long moment in the dark. Two damaged people, each deciding how much to trust the other — I'd watched them do this dance for days, and every time, the stakes got higher and the dance got closer to honest.

"Take the wheel," he said.

* * *

What happened next, I want to set down precisely, because later — after everything — people will ask me when it changed, when a boatload of strangers became whatever we are now, and I want to remember that it started here, in the dark, with Isolde Vane steering us through a channel that existed only because her father had been a smuggler's friend and a liar, and with her hands shaking on the wheel and her voice absolutely steady calling depths she couldn't possibly have known, and with Bram hanging over the bow in the black water-glow, reading the phosphorescence the way other men read scripture — the cold blue fire that blooms where water shoals, warning written in light.

"Twelve feet," Bram called. "Ten. Sandbar should be—"

The keel kissed it. Just kissed — a shudder through the whole hull, a grinding whisper, a moment where every person aboard stopped breathing in unison — and then we slid off, and deeper, and through.

Nobody cheered. Sela put her hand briefly on Isolde's shoulder, which from Sela is a standing ovation. And I stood at the stern rail and watched the orange glow fall behind, and the green light swing wide and confused, searching water we'd already left, and I thought: *we won.*

Which shows you what I knew.

* * *

They came aboard at three in the morning, or rather their work did. We heard nothing — no boat alongside, no splash, nothing — because whoever Dekker sent wasn't a brawler, he was a craftsman. We found out at dawn, sixty miles into the strait, when Bram turned the key to warm the engine for the day's run and the *Kestrel* answered with a sound like a man swallowing gravel.

He had the cowling off in four minutes flat. I watched him look into the guts of our engine the way a doctor looks at a wound he already understands too well.

"Sand in the oil," he said. "Grit in the fuel line. Cooling intake packed with rag — packed, not jammed. Someone took their time." He straightened up, wiping his hands on a rag with terrible calm. "They didn't wreck it. They *crippled* it. There's a difference. Wrecking says anger. This says patience. This says: *I want you limping, not sinking. I want you slow enough to catch.*"

"When?" Isolde asked.

"Sometime last night. While we were threading the smuggler's cut feeling clever." He looked at her, and she looked back, and I watched the same thought land on both of them: the sabotage hadn't been done from a boat alongside. It had been done earlier. At the port. Before we ever sailed. Which meant Dekker had a man inside the harbor yard, which meant he'd known our berth, our schedule, our engine model—

Which meant he'd known about us before we'd known about ourselves.

"They were never chasing us," Sela said quietly. She'd come up unnoticed, the way she does. "We were being herded. The chase was theater. He wanted us running, tired, committed — committed past the point of turning back. Now we're sixty miles from any harbor that isn't his, on a crippled boat, with a map he wants."

Silence, except the sea doing its indifferent percussion against the hull.

"Well," I said, into that silence, because someone had to, and because my family raised me to speak first in rooms full of men who think they're smarter than me. "I have money. Actual money, not the kind I keep pretending I've renounced. Enough to buy us a new engine, a new boat, or a small island if anyone's interested in a change of career."

"We don't need your money," Bram said.

"You need *something*, and I'm offering the only thing I have that weighs less than regret." I crossed my arms. "Look at us, Bram. A disgraced diver, a disgraced cartographer, a disgraced heiress, and a woman carrying her father's coffin on her back. We are, collectively, the most disgraceful crew ever to float. Dekker has twelve men, a gunboat, and a spy in every harbor. The navy has Verrick, whom I've met — don't ask — and Verrick makes Dekker look like a charity gala. And somewhere up a mountain there's a cache that all of them want and one dead prospector trusted to *us*. So yes, I'll spend the money. Not because I believe in the treasure. Because I've finally found four people I don't want to see caught."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Isolde, dry as chart paper: "She's insufferable."

"Completely," Bram agreed.

"But she's right," Sela said.

Bram looked at the dead engine, then at the horizon where the highlands waited, blue and patient, and then — last — at us. Something in his face settled, like a man accepting a weight he'd been refusing to pick up.

"All right," he said. "There's a fishing village eight miles south. Neutral ground. We buy an engine, we buy silence, and we make the rest of the distance on foot before the snows close the passes." He paused. "And from tonight, someone stands watch in pairs. Because Dekker didn't win today — but he showed us his hand. He can reach us anywhere we sleep."

* * *

That evening, while the others argued engine prices with a fisherman whose negotiating style owed everything to piracy, I sat on the dock with my feet in the water and let myself feel, for the first time since I jumped ship, something that wasn't relief or fear.

It was purpose. Ugly, dangerous, borrowed purpose — but mine.

Behind me, on the *Kestrel*, someone was standing watch. Ahead of me, up in the blue highlands, a dead man's map was pulling five disgraceful people toward whatever truth he'd died carrying. And somewhere out on the dark water, patient as arithmetic, Dekker was recalculating.

Let him calculate. He thinks he's racing a treasure crew.

He hasn't figured out yet that he's racing a family.

And families, I'm told, are the one thing in this world more dangerous than money — because unlike money, they can't be bought.

Only, apparently, joined.
