## 7. The Officer in White

I have been boarded by customs men twice, by pirates once — though the pirates were more polite than the customs men — and by my own father's survey crews more times than I care to count. So I know the taxonomy of boarding parties the way other women know hymnals. There is the bored kind, who want signatures and cigarettes. There is the hungry kind, who want whatever is loose enough to lift. And there is the worst kind: the courteous kind, the ones who arrive with pressed uniforms and soft voices and already know everything.

Commander Aldous Verrick was the third kind, and he wore white while doing it, which I considered a flourish of pure cruelty. Nobody wears white on a working boat. White is a costume for a man who wants you to notice that he has never had to scrub a deck in his life.

We were two days out of Sela's village, beating north along the coast with a wind that kept changing its mind, when Marisol spotted the patrol cutter. She said its name like a curse she'd learned recently and was proud of: *Corbie*. Grey hull, navy pennant, running lights burning in full daylight because regulations are a language some men speak to frighten the rest of us.

Bram stood at the wheel and watched it come for a long moment with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Could we run?" Marisol asked.

"Into what?" Bram said. "That's a naval cutter. We're a chartered ketch with a fouled bottom and a crew of four." He glanced at me. "Five, counting the cartographer."

"Counting the cartographer," I agreed, "you're doomed."

He almost smiled. Then the *Corbie* put her helm over and came alongside with the smooth, unhurried confidence of something that has never once been told no, and a voice over a loudhailer invited us — *invited*, the way a spider invites — to heave to for inspection.

* * *

They came aboard six strong, rifles slung with theatrical casualness, and then, last, the commander himself. He stepped across the gap between hulls without a hand offered or needed, landed on our deck as lightly as a gull, and looked around at our little kingdom of salt-stained canvas and bad coffee with an expression of mild, forgiving interest.

"Commander Aldous Verrick," he said. "Coastal patrol division." His eyes went around the deck and touched each of us in turn — Bram, me, Marisol, Sela — the way an auditor's finger touches line items. "You'll forgive the interruption. Routine inspection. The waters here have been... restless lately."

"Restless," Bram repeated.

"Smugglers," Verrick said pleasantly. "Wreck-strippers. Men who take things that don't belong to them." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the sort of smile that has been practiced in mirrors until it forgot how to stop. "You'd be amazed what washes up on this coast. And what walks out of the water carrying it."

I want to be clear about what happened in my chest at that moment, because I am a woman who makes her living from precision. Nothing happened. That was the problem. A man says *wreck-strippers* on open water and every honest sailor bristles on principle; every guilty one goes still. But Verrick wasn't fishing. Fishermen cast and wait. Verrick was simply reading us aloud, the way I might read a chart, checking his soundings against what he already knew was there.

His officers went through the boat. They were thorough in the way of men following a list written by someone else: the hold, the lazarette, under the bunks, behind the water tanks. One of them opened Isolde's — my — sea chest, lifted my drafting tools one by one, and set each back exactly where it had lain, which was somehow worse than rummaging. Another found our stores and counted them, lips moving.

Marisol, to her credit, played her part beautifully. She planted herself on deck with her arms crossed and her chin up and delivered a performance of aristocratic outrage so convincing that I briefly wondered whether any of it was performance. "Do you know who my family is? Quennel Shipping. My father will hear about this. Every hull we lease, every harbor contract—"

"Madam Quennel." Verrick turned to her with genuine, unhurried delight, like a man finding an old friend in an unexpected port. "Of course. The Quennels. Your family moves half the freight in these waters." He inclined his head. "I've read their manifests. All of them." Something passed under the pleasantness then, quick as a shark's shadow under a swell, and was gone. "Give my regards to your father."

Sela stood apart by the rail, arms wrapped around herself, saying nothing. She had said very little since the village, since the letters, since grief had cracked her open and she'd sealed the crack with silence and work. But I saw Verrick's gaze rest on her a moment longer than the others, and I saw him file something away behind those courteous eyes, and I did not like the arithmetic of it at all.

* * *

It was in the cabin that he finally stopped pretending.

The inspection had produced nothing, because there was nothing to produce — Bram had buried the map in the bilge keel space before the cutter was even a smudge on the horizon, wrapped in oilcloth inside a bait tin, and if Verrick's men found it they were welcome to explain to their commander why they'd spent twenty minutes elbow-deep in fish guts. So Verrick dismissed his officers topside, accepted the cup of coffee Bram offered with the grace of a man accepting tribute rather than hospitality, and sat down across the tiny chart table from the two of us as though he owned the chair, the table, the boat, and possibly the sea itself.

"You're Isolde Vane," he said.

"I'm flattered. I didn't realize the coastal patrol kept up with cartography."

"I keep up with everything in my waters, Miss Vane." He blew across his coffee, unhurried. "Your father surveyed this coast. Before the war. Fine charts. Some of them are still in use." He set the cup down. "Shame about his reputation at the end. Errors in the highland series, wasn't it? Elevation discrepancies. A career undone by a few wrong numbers."

The cabin was suddenly very small and very warm. "My father's numbers were not wrong," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and careful, and hated myself for giving him the reaction.

"No," Verrick agreed softly. "They weren't."

He let that sit between us like a mine floating just under the surface. I stared at him and he stared back with perfect patience, and somewhere above us Marisol's voice rose in another aria of protest and was smoothly talked down by an ensign.

"You know why I'm here," Verrick said, turning to Bram. "Or rather, you don't, which is why I'm here. Let me save us all some time." He folded his hands. "Three weeks ago a trawler reported salvage work on a wreck off the southern shoals. Unmarked wreck. No registry entry, no casualty record, no salvage rights filed — because there are no rights to file. That vessel does not exist, Mr. Montrose. Officially, it never existed. And yet you were diving it. Stripping brass off a ship that isn't there."

"The sea's full of unlisted wrecks," Bram said. "Storms lose records. Wars lose more."

"They do." Verrick nodded thoughtfully. "Wars lose ships, and lose men, and occasionally lose whole ledgers. And sometimes—" he picked an invisible speck from his sleeve "—a man comes ashore out of that same war thirty years late, half-mad, full of secrets, and starts telling stories in the highland villages. Stories about a depot. About what was buried up there and why." He looked up. "About a month ago, such a man was seen heading for the coast. Two weeks ago, he was shot. And ten days ago, a salvage diver pulled a stranger out of the surf at his own camp, and the stranger died holding something in his fist."

The engine noise filled the silence. I could hear my own pulse in my ears, steady and traitorous.

"Now," Verrick continued, in the tone of a man discussing weather, "I don't know what he gave you. A map, perhaps. Old paper, ciphered margins, survey marks from a war nobody discusses. I don't know, because it isn't on your boat — my men checked, and they're very good, but you struck me as careful people. Careful people hide things well." He rose, smoothing his whites, and smiled down at us with something that was almost warmth. "But here is what I do know, and I offer it freely, as one professional to another. Whatever Ardan drew for you leads into the highlands. The highlands close for winter in five weeks, perhaps six. And everything that road passes through belongs, in one way or another, to me."

"You're confessing to murder in my cabin," Bram said quietly, "and expecting us to shake your hand."

"I'm confessing to nothing. I'm describing geography." Verrick moved to the companionway, then paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back with an expression of genuine, almost paternal kindness that made my skin crawl worse than any threat would have. "Mr. Montrose. Miss Vane. Go home. Dive your wrecks, draw your charts, drink your coffee. There is nothing in those mountains but cold and old ghosts, and the men who go looking for them tend to become ghosts themselves. I would hate to read about it." A beat. "Although, of course, I read everything."

Then he was gone, up the ladder, across the gap, and the *Corbie* peeled away with the same unhurried grace she'd arrived with, her wake unrolling behind her like a signature on a document we hadn't signed.

* * *

We stood in the cabin for a long time after. Marisol came down the steps, outrage abandoned, face pale. Sela followed, and shut the door, and leaned against it.

"He knew Papa's name," she said. "Ardan. He said it like he'd said it before. Like it was on a list."

"It is on a list," I said. "It's been on a list for thirty years. We just weren't allowed to see it."

Bram hauled up the bait tin, unwrapped the oilcloth, and spread the map on the chart table between the coffee cups Verrick had touched. Under the lamp, the old survey marks in the margins seemed to shift — my father's marks, Verrick had known them on sight, had known *me* on sight — and I understood, with the slow vertigo of a woman standing on a ledge she'd drawn herself, that we had never been racing Dekker at all. Dekker was a dog off a leash. The leash had a hand on it, and the hand wore white gloves.

"He let us go," Marisol said. "Why would he let us go?"

"Because a cat doesn't eat the mice in the kitchen," Bram said. He rolled the map slowly, eyes on nothing. "It waits and follows them to the granary." He looked up at us, and I saw the last comfortable illusion leave him — the idea that this was salvage, that this was fortune, that the dead prospector had been a stray misfortune washed into his camp. "The wreck. The murder. Him. It's one thread, and we're tied to the end of it now."

Outside, the *Corbie*'s smoke was a thin grey line on the northern horizon, holding our course, holding our speed.

Not chasing us. Escorting us.

And five weeks, perhaps six, until the snows.
