## 8. Into the Green Staircase

The boats died at a place called Teras Landing, which was less a landing than an argument between the river and the sea, and the river was winning. We paid a fisherman with no questions in him to watch the *Kestrel* and Marisol's chartered tub, and we shouldered packs that weighed more than our regrets, and we started walking up.

That's how I'd describe it to anyone who asked — though nobody sane would ask. The highlands don't rise so much as they climb toward you, terrace by terrace, green stair over green stair, each step taller than the last, until you look back one evening and realize the coast is a rumor and the air has gone thin enough to taste like tin. Isolde called it the Green Staircase because she'd found it on her father's old survey charts, annotated in his cramped hand: *ascensio perpetua*. Perpetual ascent. She said it like a joke. It wasn't one.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Marisol said, forty minutes in, which by my count was thirty-nine minutes longer than I'd expected her to last.

"Because your father's shipping empire doesn't run routes through mountains," Isolde said, not turning around. "And neither does Verrick's navy. Not quickly."

"And Dekker?"

"Dekker walks," Bram said. That was me. I'd decided somewhere around the third day after Verrick let us go that talking about myself in the third person kept things honest. "Dekker walks, and Dekker hires men who walk faster than us."

Sela Ardan hadn't said anything since Teras Landing. She'd been quiet for most of a week, actually, ever since the night at the coastal village when grief cracked her open like a dropped plate and she'd swept herself back together before dawn, insisting nothing had happened. She walked at the rear of our line with her father's map folded against her chest, and every time I glanced back she was touching it through the canvas, the way you'd touch a wound to check if it was still bleeding.

We made camp the first night on a shelf of ground above a stream, under trees so old their roots had opinions about architecture. Isolde got a fire going — she was better at it than any of us, a fact she guarded jealously — and Marisol discovered leeches.

I want to be fair to her. Most people discover leeches the same way: a tickling on the ankle, a look down, a scream held mostly inside. Marisol managed the first two with dignity. The third came out as a sound like a kettle being murdered, and then she was hopping in a circle slapping at her own boots while Sela watched from across the fire with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Salt," Sela said finally. "Not fire. You'll scar."

"I *have* scars," Marisol said, with the tone of someone whose scars were all of the emotional variety.

"Not like these." Sela dug a tin of salt from her pack, the kind of practical object that told you she'd grown up poor before she'd grown up angry, and tossed it over. "Pull them off wrong and the head stays in. Then you've got a real problem."

Marisol looked at the salt tin. Looked at Sela. Something passed between them that had nothing to do with leeches — some small recalibration of what each of them thought the other was worth. It lasted maybe two seconds. Then Marisol sat down, hiked her trouser leg up with the grim efficiency of a woman performing surgery on herself, and got to work.

By the third day the jungle had stopped being scenery and become a condition. Rot under everything, including, eventually, us. Our boots never dried. Our socks achieved a state I can only describe as theological. The terraces climbed in switchbacks cut by farmers dead a thousand years, stone steps swallowed by root and moss, and we went up them in a silence broken only by breathing and the occasional curse from Marisol, who had developed a rich vocabulary of profanity that seemed to draw equally from three continents.

Isolde navigated. She carried her father's charts in oilcloth against her chest the way Sela carried the map, and I noticed — because noticing is my job, or it was, back when my job was brass and hulls — that the two women had started walking together without discussing it. Two daughters, two inheritances neither of them wanted, climbing the same stairs.

On the fourth day we heard the river before we saw it.

It came down out of the high country in a white rope, swollen with rain from slopes we hadn't reached yet, and the trail crossed it where an old bridge had been — except the bridge had been reduced to two posts and a memory. The crossing was fifty feet of water moving fast enough to carry boulders, and the far bank was a wall of stone slick with spray.

"We go upstream," Isolde said, unrolling a chart with hands that shook slightly from cold. "There's a ford marked here. Old survey."

"How old?" I asked.

She did the math. "Older than me."

The ford, when we found it, was a lie the river had allowed to survive. Water to mid-thigh, fast, over cobbles that rolled underfoot like marbles with grudges. We linked arms — Isolde refused at first, then thought better of pride — and started across in a chain, me leading, breaking the current for the others.

I was ten feet from the far bank when I heard Marisol go.

No scream this time. Just a change in the tension on my arm, a sudden lightness, and then the sound of water taking something it wanted. I turned and saw her downstream, already past the shallows, arms windmilling, her pack dragging her under and up and under again. The current had her the way a cat has a mouse — playing, patient, certain.

I lunged back into the deep channel and knew immediately I'd misjudged it; the water hit me like a door. Behind me Isolde was shouting something the river ate whole.

And then Sela Ardan went past me like a spear.

I didn't see her decide. I don't think there was a decision. One moment she was in the chain behind me and the next she was cutting diagonally through the flood with a stroke I recognized too late — the flat, economical swim of someone raised on a wild coast, someone whose father had taught her water before he'd taught her anything else, back when teaching her things was still something he did.

She caught Marisol by the collar at the lip of a drop where the river fell fifteen feet into white chaos. Caught her, turned her, and rode her — rode *them both* — into an eddy behind a boulder, where the water swirled them around twice like a hand stirring a cup and set them gently, almost politely, against the near bank.

I got to them crawling. Marisol was on her hands and knees, coughing half the river back up, and Sela knelt beside her with a hand flat on her back, saying nothing, waiting, the way you wait with a drowning man because you know the panic needs somewhere to go.

"I had you," Marisol gasped finally, between retches. "I had—"

"You didn't." Sela said it without cruelty. Just inventory. "You didn't have you. That's fine. Now you know."

Marisol stared at her. Water ran off her chin. And then she laughed — a raw, cracked, furious laugh, the laugh of a woman whose dignity had just drowned and whose body had unexpectedly survived the funeral. "You're insufferable," she said.

"Yes," Sela agreed, and offered her a hand up.

Something changed after the river. I've seen it happen on crews before, though rarely this fast and never with these people. It's not friendship, exactly. Friendship takes time and trust, and trust takes longer. What happens instead is simpler and harder to undo: people stop performing. Marisol stopped pretending the expedition was beneath her. Sela stopped pretending she hadn't chosen us. Even Isolde, who trusted maps more than people and had good reason, started saying things out loud — small things, corrections, worries — instead of writing them down in cipher and keeping them behind her eyes.

We climbed. The jungle thinned by degrees, giving way to cloud forest, moss hanging like the trappings of a drowned ship, everything dripping, everything green. The leeches gave way to cold. The rot gave way to rock. And every night we built a fire, and every night the fire did its old work — the work fires have done since the first frightened ape fed one — pulling people close, making talk possible.

It was around those fires that I learned things I hadn't asked for and couldn't give back.

Marisol talked about her family the way you'd talk about weather in a country you'd fled: precisely, bitterly, with too much detail. Her father owned forty-one ships. She could name them all. He'd named none of them after her. Her arranged marriage, she explained, wasn't even to a bad man — that was the trap of it. "He's *fine*," she said, poking the fire with unnecessary violence. "He's pleasant. He'll be a decent husband and a boring life and I'll die at sixty-two in a house with good drainage, and everyone will say what a sensible woman I was." She looked up. "I'd rather drown in a river, apparently."

"You nearly did," Sela said.

"Thank you for the reminder."

Isolde talked less, but when she talked it landed. Around the fifth fire she told us, staring into the flames, about the disgrace — how she'd caught a survey error in a chart used fleet-wide, an error her own father had signed, and reported it, and the admiralty had buried it rather than admit the great cartographer Vane had made a mistake, and her career had been the thing buried deepest. "They didn't punish me for being wrong," she said. "They punished me for being right. There's a difference, and it's the difference that ruins you."

Sela talked least of all. But one night, when Marisol was asleep and Isolde was sketching the next day's route by firelight, Sela sat across from me and said, without preamble: "He wrote to me. Every year. On my birthday."

I waited. Waiting is most of listening.

"I never opened them. I kept them in a box and I moved the box four times and I never opened them." She turned the map case over in her hands. "Now he's dead and I'm carrying his map up a mountain, and I still haven't read a single letter. Isn't that a stupid thing to be?"

"No," I said. "That's the bravest thing you could be. Reading them means deciding what you feel. Carrying them means you already know."

She looked at me for a long moment, firelight moving on her face. "What do you know about fathers, Bram Montrose?"

"Enough," I said. "Not more."

She nodded slowly, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

Dekker's scouts found us on the seventh day, though they didn't know we knew.

I'd been expecting it since the river. A crew moving that slow leaves sign a blind man could follow — broken fern, cut turf, boot prints in the clay terraces that would hold for weeks in that damp. So I'd started watching our backtrail the way I used to watch a tide: not looking for anything specific, just reading the water.

The sign I read was smoke. Two mornings running, a thread of it in the valley below us, in a place where no village stood on Isolde's charts. Then, on the eighth morning, a glint — sun off glass or steel, up-slope and behind, maybe a mile back along our own line of ascent.

"They're letting us break trail for them," Isolde said when I showed her. She'd gone pale, and not from altitude. "Why bother following quietly when we'll clear the path?"

"Because Dekker doesn't spend men he can spend ours," I said. "We stop resting where they can see the smoke. Cold camps from here. We move at first light, we eat standing, and we start making our trail ugly."

"Ugly how?"

"The way a diver makes a trail ugly." I'd spent years erasing myself from the sea floor; the forest was just another medium. We doubled back on our own tracks. We walked in streams. We took a ridge traverse that cost us half a day and a fair amount of Marisol's remaining goodwill, and by the ninth morning the glint behind us was gone, and the smoke had stopped appearing, and I made the mistake of feeling good about it.

The mistake corrected itself that afternoon.

We'd come up onto a long shoulder of the mountain, a grassy spine between two drainages, when Sela — walking rear guard now by unspoken appointment, because she had the best eyes — stopped and put two fingers in her mouth and whistled, low, the way you whistle when shouting is a luxury.

We froze. She pointed down-slope, back the way we'd come.

Three men. Small at this distance, but unmistakable: moving in a spread line, the way you move when you're hunting rather than traveling, rifles slung for quick use. They were a mile back and falling behind on the climb, but they weren't losing ground the way tired men lose ground. They were pacing us. Measuring.

"Dekker's," Isolde breathed.

"Or Verrick's," Marisol said. "How would we know?"

"We wouldn't," I said. "That's the point. Whoever they are, they want us to see them. They want us scared and hurried, because scared hurried men make mistakes in mountains." I looked at the three dots, and the dots held their distance, patient as tide. "So we don't hurry, and we don't make mistakes. We camp early tonight, behind that ridge, cold. Tomorrow we climb the steepest thing on this map and dare them to follow."

Isolde unrolled the chart with hands steadier than they'd been a week ago. Her finger found a contour line packed so tight the ink nearly merged. "The Chimneys," she read. "Her father marked it. *Bad going. Worth it.*" She glanced up at me. "His handwriting gets sloppy here. Like his hand was shaking."

"Maybe the mountain was shaking it," Sela said.

That night, cold-camped behind the ridge with no fire and the temperature dropping through the floor of what I'd call comfortable, we lay in our blankets in a row like spoons in a drawer, and Marisol whispered into the dark:

"This is the worst holiday of my life."

Nobody laughed. Then Sela whispered, "Better than the wedding," and Marisol whispered back, "Marginally," and Isolde made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth, and I lay there listening to the three of them breathe in the dark and felt something I hadn't felt since I was a boy on my uncle's deck: the particular weight of people who would, given cause, come back for you.

I'd hired none of them. I'd promised one of them a dead man's errand and acquired the other two like barnacles. And yet there they were, shivering in the dark on a mountain because I'd asked, holding a line none of us had drawn on purpose.

The wilderness forgives nothing. I'd known that since my first dive. But I was starting to learn its corollary, the one the drowned never get to tell you: it also forgets nothing. Every kindness, every cowardice, every choice — the mountain keeps them all, filed away in scree and snow, waiting to be read back to you at altitude.

Tomorrow we'd start reading ours.

At first light we moved for the Chimneys, and behind us, patient as tide, the three dots began to climb.
