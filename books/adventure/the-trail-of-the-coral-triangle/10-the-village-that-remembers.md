## 10. The Village That Remembers

The village announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet stone and something green growing where it shouldn't, at this altitude. Then the terraces appeared out of the mist like a staircase built for giants who'd lost interest halfway up, and I understood why Isolde had gone quiet for the last hour of the climb.

"Terraced fields," she'd said, twice, as if the first time hadn't taken. "Pre-colonial cut. Nobody's surveyed this valley."

"Nobody's surveyed a lot of things up here," Bram said. "That's rather the point."

I was at the back of the line, which had become my place without anyone assigning it. Marisol led because Marisol always led when there was an audience; Bram walked point because he trusted nothing, including the ground; Isolde navigated because she was the only one of us who could read a contour line without turning it into a confession about her father. And I brought up the rear carrying my father's map in a waterproof sleeve against my chest, like a letter I hadn't decided whether to open.

Six days out from the river, three days behind Dekker's scouts — Bram counted their sign every morning with the grim satisfaction of a man confirming his own paranoia — and one day ahead, if Isolde's arithmetic held, of weather that would close the pass like a door.

The village was maybe forty houses, stone-footed and timber-roofed, stacked up the valley wall in defiance of gravity. Goats regarded us from rooftops with the contempt goats reserve for all visitors. Children stopped mid-game to stare, then scattered, and I knew what that meant: runners, sent ahead. News travels faster than strangers in country like this.

An old man met us at the first terrace gate. He was small and straight-backed, wearing a jacket that had been military once and was now simply old, and he looked at us the way you look at weather coming in — assessing, unhurried, already deciding.

"You are going to the high depot," he said. Not a question.

Bram didn't flinch, which I was learning was his whole personality. "We're going into the mountains."

"The mountains have one road," the old man said, "and the road has one destination, and everyone who walks it is going to the depot." He let that sit. "You will eat tonight under a roof or you will eat badly in the cold. Come."

His name was Ekoru, though it took two days and a shared chore before anyone told us. He fed us in the longhouse — barley soup dense enough to stand a spoon in, flatbread, tea that tasted like it had opinions about us — and he asked no questions, which was somehow more unnerving than interrogation. Around the fire, the villagers watched us with a patience I couldn't parse. Not hostility. Not welcome, exactly. Something closer to *recognition*, as if we were actors in a play they'd seen performed before, and they were waiting to see whether we'd remember our lines.

It was the children who broke it open. They always do. A girl of maybe eight, bold as brass, planted herself in front of me while I washed bowls — the universal currency of guests — and stared at the strap across my chest where the map sleeve rode.

"What's in the bag?"

"Nothing interesting."

"Liar," she said cheerfully, and ran off, and something in my chest went sideways, because my father used to say *liar* exactly that way, delighted, whenever I tried to bluff him about stolen biscuits. Twenty years dead to me, that man, and a child in a village I'd never heard of dug him up in four seconds flat.

* * *

Ekoru came to me on the second morning, while the others were arguing with a mule trader whose prices suggested the mules were gilded.

"You carry the map of Ardan," he said.

The world did that thing where it tilts slightly and keeps spinning anyway. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I knew Tomas Ardan." He said the name carefully, the way you handle something with edges. "Thirty-one years ago. Sit, girl. You have his chin. He would hate hearing that."

I sat. I'm not proud of how fast I sat.

"He came through here in the spring of the year the navy built its road," Ekoru said. "A surveyor's assistant, he said, though he carried a prospector's pan and knew more about rock than any surveyor I've met. He stayed three weeks. He was waiting for someone." The old man's eyes went to the fire. "The someone never came. In the autumn, men from the depot came down instead, and after them, we never saw him again."

"What men? What happened at the depot?" My voice came out wrong, too sharp, too loud. Somewhere behind me a goat complained in solidarity.

Ekoru looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him decide something. "Come," he said again. It seemed to be the village's favorite word. "There is someone you should meet before I answer questions I've spent thirty years not answering."

* * *

Her house was the last one up the terraces, small, smoke-darkened, with a garden of medicinal herbs laid out in rows so precise they looked surveyed. She was tiny, ancient, wrapped in layers of wool, and her hands shook until she took hold of something — then they were steady as a surgeon's.

Her name was Ama Wren. She looked at my face for a long time without speaking, and I let her, because some examinations deserve silence.

"Tomas stayed in this room," she said finally. "That bed. He mended my roof and argued with me about the proper way to dry chilies and ate like a starving dog pretending to have manners." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He talked about a daughter. Sela. Who was angry with him."

"I wasn't—" The lie died before it cleared my teeth. "I was. I am. Was."

"He said he'd wronged you by leaving, and wronged you worse by staying away, and that he didn't know which sin to repent first so he intended to make money enough to apologize extravagantly for both." She snorted. "Men. They think love is a debt they can pay off in coin."

"He left because of my mother's death," I said. The words came out flat, rehearsed, the official version I'd been telling myself since I was nineteen. "He couldn't—"

"He left because the navy gave him work no honest man should take," Ama Wren said, "and then gave him knowledge no living man should carry, and he spent the rest of his life trying to decide whether to bury it or give it away. He chose to give it away. To you. Late, but he chose." She turned, slow, joints cracking like green wood, and took something from a chest beneath the window. "He left these with me. He said: *if I don't come back, keep them until someone comes asking with my face.*"

A bundle of letters, tied with cord gone soft with age, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job. Thirty-one years in a chest and the paper inside was dry.

My hands shook taking them. Hers didn't. She pressed the bundle into my grip and closed my fingers over it with both of hers, and her hands went steady the moment they held something, and I understood that this was how she'd survived — by holding things. Keeping them. Waiting.

"He wrote to you?" I managed. "From the mountain?"

"He wrote from this room, the night before he went up the last time. And he wrote others, over the weeks he waited here. Letters he never posted because he didn't know the address of forgiveness." She said it without pity, which was the only way I could have borne it. "Read them or burn them. But read them first. Burning unread letters is just arson with extra regret."

Despite everything — the altitude, the grief landing on me like snowfall, slow and total — I laughed. It startled us both.

"Why keep them?" I asked. "Thirty years. Why not send them, or destroy them, or—"

"Because the past is not buried in this valley, girl. It is *waiting*. There's a difference, and the difference is that buried things stay put, and waiting things get up." She settled onto her stool, hands folding over her stick, steady now, always steady when holding something. "And because I promised a frightened man I would wait with him. Everyone needs one person who waits."

* * *

I read the letters that night in the longhouse, off to the side, by lamplight, while Bram pretended not to watch me from across the fire and Isolde actually did watch me, openly, because Isolde has never learned the courtesy of pretending.

The early ones were what I expected. Apologies, circling. *Sela, I know what I cost you. Sela, your mother would have—* He kept starting over like a man trying doors in a dark house. But the later letters changed. Somewhere around the third one, the apologizing father disappeared and another man surfaced — a precise, frightened, furious man writing in a surveyor's shorthand I recognized from the map's margins.

*I count forty-one prisoners brought up the road in the month of the red moon. I count nine who came back down. The ledger says they were released to coastal labor. I have seen the coastal labor. There is no coastal labor.*

*I have marked the true locations in cipher because they shoot men for less than what I know. If you find this, do not come looking for money. Come looking for the crates with the navy stamp. The gold is bait. The paper is the poison.*

*Forgive me the order I chose: truth first, apology second. I wanted to hand you something clean. All I have is something true.*

I read that last line four times. Around the fifth, I became aware that my face was wet and that Isolde had moved to sit beside me without asking, which was its own kind of manners.

"My father surveyed the depot road," she said quietly. "I found his marks in the map margins. I haven't told Bram yet." She stared into the fire. "I've spent my whole life being ashamed of him. I was starting to feel righteous about it. This is ruining a perfectly good grudge."

"Welcome to the family business," I said, and heard my father's laugh in it, and didn't hate that as much as I'd have predicted.

* * *

On the third evening, Ekoru gathered the elders in the longhouse — seven of them, gray and straight-backed, including a woman who, I was told, had been a girl when the road was built and remembered the sound of the trucks at night. Bram sat across from them with his arms crossed, radiating the wariness of a man who trusts rooms the way cats trust water. Marisol had dressed down for the occasion, which for Marisol meant only one layer of expensive. Isolde had spread her copy of the map on the table between the fire and the elders, and the room went very still when she did.

The old woman — Maren, they called her — leaned forward and touched the margin cipher with one finger.

"That mark," she said. "The navy put that mark on the door of the depot. I saw it when they took my brother up the road." Her voice didn't shake. That was the terrible part. Nothing in this valley shook anymore; it had all finished shaking decades ago. "Forty-one men went up that autumn. Nine came down. The navy said fever. We buried what they sent back in boxes too heavy for the number of men inside, and too light for the weight of the story."

"We're going up there," Bram said. Flat. No apology offered for it.

"We know," Ekoru said. "Dekker's men passed through four days ago. They bought supplies and shot a goat for sport and asked the same questions you ask, except they asked them with guns showing." His eyes were level. "They went up the east trail. It is faster and it is exposed and the weather will catch them on the shoulder of the mountain, because men like that never believe the sky has opinions."

"And us?" Marisol asked.

"You we feed," Maren said simply. "You we shelter. Because you carry his face." She nodded at me. "And because someone must bring it down."

"Bring what down?" Bram asked, though I think he already knew. He had that stillness he got when the shape of a thing was assembling itself in front of him.

"The truth," Ekoru said. "For thirty-one years we have kept the letters and the lists and the names of the missing, and every year fewer of us remain to keep them. The navy burned the depot records long ago — we've had travelers confirm it. What happened on that mountain exists now in three places: in our memories, which are dying; in whatever your prospector hid up there, which we could never reach; and nowhere else." He looked around the table, at each of us in turn, and I felt the weight of it settle on my shoulders like the pack I'd been cursing for six days. "We are not asking you to avenge anyone. Vengeance goes up mountains and does not come back. We are asking you to *carry*. Carry it down. Put it where the world has to look at it."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the mules complained about existence on principle.

Bram looked at me. Not at Isolde, whose map it technically was; not at Marisol, whose money it was becoming; at me. Asking, in his wordless way, whether I could bear to walk into the place that had eaten my father, on the theory that it might cough up the truth of him.

I thought about the letters in their oilcloth against my ribs. *Truth first, apology second.*

"Yes," I said. My voice held. It surprised me.

Then Maren did something none of us expected. She rose, slow, and fetched a small bundle from her own chest — cloth-wrapped, lighter than my letters — and laid it beside the map. Inside were scraps of fabric, buttons, a child's carved bird, a wedding ring.

"The families' tokens," she said. "What they sent up with the men, hoping the men would send them back. The boxes that came down held none of these. Only stones, packed to weight." She closed the cloth again, gently. "If you find anything of the forty-one up there — anything at all — bring it down. Let us bury them properly. That is the other half of the carrying."

Isolde was writing in her notebook, fast, her pen scratching like something alive. Marisol had gone uncharacteristically quiet, doing sums of a different kind than usual. And Bram — Bram was looking at the tokens with an expression I'd started to recognize, the one from the wreck, from the dying man on the beach: the look of a man discovering that the job he'd signed up for was not the job he'd been given.

Later, on the terrace wall, he lit his pipe and stood next to me in the cold without requiring conversation, which I was beginning to understand was his highest form of respect.

"Dekker's ahead of us and Verrick's behind us," he said eventually, smoke curling into the dark. "Weather's closing the pass in three days, maybe less. And we've just volunteered to haul a graveyard's worth of truth over the top of the world."

"When you say it like that, it sounds insane."

"It is insane." He knocked out the pipe. "Get some sleep. We leave at first light, and we take the west trail — longer, meaner, but it stays under the ridgeline where the sky can't see us." He paused at the door of the longhouse. "Your father waited three weeks in that village for help that never came. Feels right that it finally showed up thirty years late, wearing your face."

He went inside. I stayed out a while longer, under stars sharp enough to draw blood, listening to the wind come down off the depot mountain — the mountain where forty-one men had gone up and nine had come down, where my father had written *truth first, apology second*, where the past wasn't buried but waiting, patient as snowpack on a steep slope.

Three days. Then the door closed until spring.

I went in, and slept, and dreamed of chilies drying wrong, and woke before dawn with my father's letters strapped against my heart and the whole crew already moving in the dark, because the mountain had stopped waiting, and so had we.
