## 11. Dekker's Bargain

I have been kidnapped twice, held for ransom once, and forced to attend my own engagement party, and I can say with authority that the rope bridge was the worst of them.

Not because of the bridge itself — though it was a horror, a hundred meters of rotting bamboo and hemp slung across a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was just a rumor of white noise. No. The bridge was the worst because we had to cross it single file, which meant that when Dekker stepped out of the treeline on the far side with six men and two rifles, there was nowhere to go and no one to blame but the geography.

"Nobody moves," he said, which struck me as redundant. The bridge moved enough for all of us. It swayed in the wind coming up the gorge, and I gripped the guide ropes and thought, very clearly and very stupidly, about the state of my boots.

Behind me, Sela made a small sound. Not fear. Calculation. I was learning to hear the difference.

Bram stood maybe four meters ahead of me, halfway across, one hand on the cable, and he did not look surprised. That was the thing about Bram Montrose — surprise seemed to be something that happened to other people. He looked at Dekker the way a man looks at weather he'd been expecting since morning.

"Dekker," Bram said. "You're a long way from salt water."

"So are you." Dekker came to the head of the bridge and stopped, planting his boots wide like a man who owned the ground under him. He was bigger than I remembered from the harbor — or maybe it was the altitude, the thin air making everything seem more solid, more permanent. He had a rifle slung casual across his back and a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "And you've got something of mine."

"I don't recall signing anything over."

"The map, Montrose." He said it patiently, the way you'd explain arithmetic to a child. "The old man's map. You picked it off his body and you've been walking it uphill ever since. I watched you do it."

"You watched us bury him," Sela said. Her voice carried flat across the gorge. "You watched us dig his grave and you followed us anyway."

Dekker's eyes flicked to her, and something passed over his face — not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Recognition, maybe. He filed her away somewhere and turned back to Bram.

"Here's my offer," he said. "Because I'm a reasonable man, and because this mountain is about to get very unfriendly. Hand over the map, and we split the cache. Fifty-fifty. My crew does the heavy lifting, your crew keeps breathing. Everybody walks down rich."

"Fifty-fifty," Isolde repeated, from behind me. She sounded almost amused, which frightened me more than the rifles did. "That's generous, coming from a man holding both ends of a bridge."

"I'm not holding the ends." Dekker spread his hands. "The mountain is. I'm just standing on it. Same as you."

I want to record, for posterity, what went through my head at that moment. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was inventory. Six men, two rifles — the rest carrying machetes and that particular hungry patience men develop when they've been paid in promises too long. One bridge, which cut both ways: they couldn't rush us without crossing it single file, and we couldn't retreat without doing the same. And one name. Mine. The name I'd spent eleven months running from, the name stitched into every shipping manifest between here and Singapore, the name my father used to sign away other people's livelihoods with a fountain pen and a clear conscience.

*Quennel.*

It was the only weapon I had that Dekker couldn't see, and I was going to hate myself for using it.

But first, Bram did what Bram does, which is stall beautifully. He shifted his weight on the cable, letting the bridge sway, and said, "Fifty-fifty assumes you know where the cache is. You don't. That's why you need the map. Which means it isn't fifty-fifty, is it? It's a hundred percent mine, minus whatever I feel like paying you."

Dekker's smile thinned. "Careful."

"I'm always careful. It's why I'm still alive." Bram glanced back at us — a quick flick, reading our faces the way Isolde read charts — and then turned forward again. "Tell you what. Come across. We'll talk like civilized people."

"I don't cross bridges I don't control."

"Then we're at an impasse, and the snows are coming, and you'll freeze on your side of the gorge wondering what you missed." Bram shrugged. "Your crew looks cold, Dekker. Look at them. They're thinking about it already."

And here is the thing — they were. I saw it. Two of the six shifted their weight, glancing up-slope where the clouds were stacking themselves against the peaks like unpaid debts. Men who follow a man like Dekker aren't following him; they're following the treasure he hasn't found yet. And a man whose men are thinking is a man in trouble.

Dekker knew it too. I watched him know it. His jaw worked, and for half a second the mask slipped and underneath was just a tired, middle-aged man whose business model was running out of mountain.

That's when I stepped forward.

The bridge swayed under me and I let it — actually, I exaggerated it, arms out, wobbling like a woman who'd never seen altitude before, and I heard Sela mutter something behind me that was probably a prayer in her mother's tongue.

"Before anyone decides anything," I said, loud and bright and awful, "you should know who you're robbing."

Dekker looked at me the way men always look at me first — the clothes, now ruined and trail-filthy, but still cut better than anything he owned; the accent I can't seem to shed any more than my shadow. "And who's that?"

"Marisol Quennel."

I gave the name time to land. Watched it land. Watched his eyebrows climb and two of his men exchange a glance that had *Quennel* in it — because everyone in the salvage trade knows Quennel Shipping, knows the fleet, knows the insurance syndicates, knows the lawyers who descend like weather on anyone who touches a Quennel hull, a Quennel cargo, a Quennel—

"A Quennel," Dekker said slowly, "trekking through the highlands with a salvage diver and a drunk cartographer."

"Disgraced cartographer," Isolde corrected pleasantly. "Get it right."

"My family has ships in every port your crew has ever drunk in," I said. "My family has lawyers in every port your crew will ever run to. If I go missing on this mountain, my father won't send soldiers. He'll send paper. Contracts, liens, bounties — he'll own your boat, your crew's boats, and the harbors they berth in, and he'll do it before the spring thaw. He's done worse to men who mattered more than you."

All of which was true, incidentally. That was the beauty of it. I hadn't lied about a single thing. I'd simply pointed at the truth and asked it to stand very still while I hid behind it.

"And if I just leave you here," Dekker said, "and take the map from Montrose's cold hands later?"

"Then you're standing in front of witnesses." I gestured at his own men, sweetly. "Six of them. Men talk, Mr. Dekker. Especially hungry men. Especially men who've started wondering whether their share is really fifty percent."

Oh, that landed wrong. I felt it land wrong even as I said it — saw the flicker in the eyes of the man on Dekker's left, a lean scarred fellow with a machete and the posture of someone who'd done the arithmetic on their shares long ago and hadn't liked the total. I'd meant to scare them into caution. Instead I'd handed them a question they'd apparently already been asking themselves.

Dekker saw it too. His face went carefully blank, the way a man's goes when he realizes the room has changed temperature.

"Here's what happens now," he said, and his voice dropped into something quieter and much worse than the bluster. "You keep walking. We keep following. And when the map stops being necessary — when you've led us close enough that we can read the ground ourselves — the conversation ends and the taking starts. Bridges are just geography, Miss Quennel. Geography runs out."

"Then enjoy the view while it lasts," Bram said, and started moving.

He crossed the last stretch of bridge fast, hand over hand on the cable, and stepped past Dekker with maybe a meter to spare — close enough that I held my breath, close enough that I saw Dekker's hand twitch toward the rifle and then stop, because shooting Bram on the far side solved nothing and told his own men that murder was on the menu. A leader can't put that on the table until he's sure of the meal.

Sela crossed next, fast and light, the way she did everything. Then Isolde, white-knuckled and swearing steadily under her breath in three languages, which I believe is a cartographer's form of prayer. I went last, backwards, watching Dekker's crew the whole way, and I will confess that my knees were not performing the confidence my mouth was selling.

We regrouped on the far side, packs on, and walked. Nobody ran. Running would have told Dekker everything.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, cold-figured and quiet, with no fire because fire was an invitation. The gorge was behind us, a black seam in the dark, and somewhere across it Dekker's crew was making its own cold camp and its own calculations.

Sela took first watch. I couldn't sleep, so I sat with her, which she permitted with the grace of a woman allowing a cat on the furniture.

"You lied well," she said eventually.

"I didn't lie at all. That's the trick of it."

"Mm." She turned her cup in her hands — empty, but she held it anyway, a habit I'd noticed and never commented on. "My father lied well too. Everyone said so."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She was quiet a moment. "You put your father between you and those men today. The name. Did it cost you?"

I thought about lying to her, and decided against it. There was something about Sela that made lying feel like littering in a temple.

"Every time I use it," I said, "it uses me back. That's how names work. That's how families work. You spend years building a wall to keep them out, and then one day you find yourself living inside it and calling it protection."

She nodded slowly, and I understood that I'd said something true about her too, about the coffin of unsaid words she hauled up this mountain alongside her share of the gear. Neither of us said anything else. The stars over the ridge were brutal and beautiful, the kind of sky that makes you feel briefly honest.

* * *

It was near dawn when the screaming started.

Not from our camp. From across the gorge — thin, torn apart by wind, but unmistakably human, unmistakably terror, and then abruptly, horribly, silent.

We were all awake and at the rim of the hollow before the echo finished dying. Below, the river muttered on, indifferent. Across the gap, torchlight bobbed among the trees, scattered, agitated — Dekker's camp, lit up like an anthill kicked open.

"What happened?" Isolde whispered.

Bram was beside me, and I felt rather than saw him go very still. "Somebody fell," he said quietly. "Or somebody was helped."

We learned the shape of it an hour later, when one of Dekker's men came alone down to the gorge's edge and shouted across the chasm, voice cracking with the effort of being heard and the effort of pretending he wasn't afraid. His name, he yelled, was Corvan. The lean scarred man with the machete — the one whose eyes I'd caught yesterday, the one doing arithmetic.

"The scout," he shouted. "Rasik. He went out before dawn to find another crossing. He—" A pause, the wind eating his words. "—he questioned the split. Asked Dekker straight out, in front of everyone, what fifty-fifty meant when Dekker kept the books. And Dekker told him to mind his place, and Rasik told him where his place could be put, and then—" Corvan's voice failed and rebuilt itself. "—then Rasik went out to scout the crossing and never came back, and there's blood on the rocks above the falls, and Dekker says it was a misstep. Dekker says a lot of things."

Bram stepped to the edge and cupped his hands. "Why are you telling us?"

"Because you offered fifty-fifty!" Corvan's laugh came across the gorge cracked and wild. "No — because nobody offers fifty-fifty! Because a man who offers fifty-fifty keeps honest books! Because I've watched Dekker for six years and I know exactly what my share comes to, and yesterday your woman stood there and said it out loud, the thing we all knew and none of us said!" He was crying now, or the wind made it sound like crying. "How much do you pay? For a man who can shoot and carry and keep his mouth shut?"

And there it was. The corruption of power, shown small — not in Verrick's white uniform, not in some admiral's office, but here, on a freezing mountainside, in a man offering to sell himself across a gorge before sunrise because his captain had killed a boy who asked about percentages.

Bram didn't answer immediately. I watched him look back at us — at Sela, unreadable; at Isolde, grimly awake; at me, and I shook my head slightly, because I knew what he was weighing and I wanted him to know I'd weighed it too. A gun on our side of the bridge. A gun that might turn again.

"We pay in shares," Bram called back finally. "Real ones. Written down. But we don't buy men who desert in the night — because a man who leaves one captain for money will leave the next one for more. Go back to your crew, Corvan. Keep your eyes open. When Dekker's arithmetic finally breaks — and it will — come to us in daylight, with your weapon slung, and we'll talk."

A long silence across the gorge. Then Corvan spat — whether at us or at himself, I couldn't tell — and turned back up into the trees, and the torches of Dekker's camp settled slowly, sullenly, like coals banking down toward something.

We withdrew from the rim. Isolde spoke first, low. "You just refused a rifle."

"I just refused a knife in the back aimed at my own crew by accident," Bram said. "There's a difference."

"There's always a difference with you. That's what makes you slow."

"That's what keeps us alive."

Sela said nothing. She was looking across the gorge at the dying torchlight, and her face had gone somewhere I couldn't follow — somewhere with her father in it, maybe, and the question of what a man owes the people who follow him. Then she shouldered her pack and said, "Snow's coming early. Smell it?"

I could. Iron and ozone and something older underneath, the mountain clearing its throat.

We walked. And behind us, across the black seam of the gorge, I heard — faintly, carried on the wind that smelled of coming winter — the sound of Dekker's voice, raised not in command but in argument, and the sound of men answering him back.

The race had changed shape overnight. It wasn't us against them anymore. It was everyone against everyone, and the mountain against us all, and somewhere above us, past the pass, a depot full of dead men's secrets waited to see which of us was desperate enough to reach it first.

By noon, the first flakes came down — fat, slow, wrong for the season, like the mountain had decided to deal itself in.

Isolde looked up at them and said, very quietly, "Three days. Maybe less."

Nobody argued. We walked faster.
