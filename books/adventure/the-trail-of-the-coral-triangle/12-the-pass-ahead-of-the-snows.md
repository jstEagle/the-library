## 12. The Pass Ahead of the Snows

The mountain told us we had three days, and it told us the way mountains do — not with words but with a wind that came down off the high snows smelling of iron and endings.

I'd been watching the sky all morning from the ridge above the village, the way a man watches a debt collector's office. The clouds that had been sitting fat and white over the eastern peaks for two days were turning grey at their bellies, stacking themselves into something with intent. Old Manoro, the headman who'd given Sela her father's letters, stood beside me and read them like I'd read tide tables.

"Three days," he said, in trade-tongue. "Maybe four if the mountain is kind." He smiled without humor. "It is rarely kind."

Below us, Isolde sat on the village wall with the map spread across her knees, the deciphered margins finally giving up their secrets — the depot coordinates, the approach routes, and the one line she'd underlined three times in pencil: *Pass closes with first snow. No winter crossing survived.*

"No pressure," she said when I came down, not looking up.

"We cross in three days or we sit in this village until spring," I said. "And Verrick's patrol is two days behind us, maybe less."

Marisol, who was attempting to charm a villager's dog with dried fish and failing — the dog had better judgment than most people I knew — looked up. "Two days? How do you know?"

"Because Dekker's scouts stopped being subtle yesterday. You don't push scouts ahead unless the main body's close enough to matter."

Sela came out of the guest hut where she'd spent the morning with her father's letters, her eyes red-rimmed in a way she'd have denied under torture. "Then we leave today," she said. "All of it. Whatever it costs."

I want it noted, for the record of my own conscience, that I hesitated. Not because I doubted the mission — by then the promise had grown teeth and I couldn't have let go of it if I tried — but because I looked at these four people, my crew, my accidental family, and I thought: *the mountain doesn't care what you're carrying.* It cares only about weight, weather, and will. And we were short on the first two allowances already.

But Sela was right, and I knew it, and the knowing settled into me like ballast.

"We leave within the hour," I said.

* * *

The problem, as always, was logistics wearing the mask of destiny.

We needed mules. We needed a guide who knew the pass. And we needed both before nightfall, which meant we needed money — more money than any of us had carried up from the coast, because coin weighs nothing in a plan and everything in a market.

Manoro took me to see the trader who ran the mule line to the pass, a leathery woman named Oksana Vay who chewed a pipe stem and named a price that made me laugh out loud, the way you laugh at a wave that's about to break over your boat.

"That's robbery," I said.

"That's winter rates," she said. "Four mules, grain for six days, my nephew as guide. Plus passage tolls at the high camps. Take it or climb on your backs."

I haggled anyway, because pride, and got nowhere, because sense. The number she landed on was roughly everything Marisol had left.

Here is what you need to understand about Marisol Quennel: she had run away from one of the great shipping fortunes of the archipelago with a satchel of cash, a fake name, and the specific arrogance of someone who has never been told no by anything except her own family. That satchel was her escape fund. It was her ticket to a new life in some port far from her father's reach once this madness ended — independence, bought and paid for, no strings, no Quennel signature required.

She counted it out on Oksana's table without blinking, and then she counted out more, buying grain we didn't strictly need and blankets for the villagers' children, because that's the kind of fool she was, the useful kind.

"You just spent your way home," I said quietly, while Oksana's nephew saddled the mules.

Marisol shrugged, watching the coins disappear into the trader's apron. "My way home was always a fiction. My father would've dragged me back the moment I surfaced anywhere with a customs house." She turned that bright, reckless grin on me. "Besides. If we find paymaster gold at this depot, I'll be rich again by Thursday."

"And if we don't?"

"Then I'll be poor in excellent company." She patted the lead mule's neck. "There are worse fates. I've dined with most of them."

Isolde, overhearing, said nothing. But later, on the trail, I saw her walk beside Marisol in silence for an hour, and when they parted Isolde touched her shoulder — briefly, awkwardly, the way a woman touches another person when she's forgotten how — and Marisol pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of grace.

* * *

The first day above the village was jungle giving way to cloud forest, moss hanging from the trees like the mountain's own mourning veils. Oksana's nephew — Piotr, nineteen, silent as his aunt's pipe — led us up switchbacks older than any nation claiming them.

Verrick's patrol closed from below. We knew because Piotr had eyes, and because on the second evening we found a cold camp in a hollow, fire ash still warm, army ration tins stacked neat as an insult. Regulars. Moving fast. Moving light.

"They're not chasing us," Isolde said, studying the ground. "They're herding us. Up. Toward the pass. He wants us committed to the crossing so he can take us at the top, or take the depot after we've opened it for him."

"Wonderful," Marisol said. "He's playing chess. Are we allowed to be pawns that bite?"

That night we made cold camp ourselves, high enough now that the air had teeth. Sela sat apart with the letters again — she'd taken to reading one each night, rationing them like medicine — and I heard her voice low, reading aloud to nobody, or to a dead man, or maybe to herself. I didn't listen. Some doors you don't put your ear against.

* * *

The frostbite found Marisol on the third morning, because the mountain takes its tax from whoever paid least attention.

She'd lost a glove somewhere in the river crossings below — we'd all lost things; the trail eats gear like the sea eats hulls — and she'd been working the rope lines bare-handed for hours without saying a word, because admitting weakness wasn't in the Quennel inheritance. By the time we halted at the tree line her fingers had gone waxy white and she couldn't feel three of them.

Piotr knew what to do. Warm water, slow rewarming, her hands cradled between his while she swore in four languages with impressive fluency. The fingers came back pink and screaming with pain, which Piotr said was good. Pain, he explained through sign and broken trade-tongue, meant living tissue.

"You could have said something," Sela snapped at her, wrapping the hands herself with a fierceness that surprised everyone including Sela.

"And slow us down?" Marisol attempted a grin through chattering teeth. "Please. I've sat through dinners with worse pain and better table manners."

"Next time you say something."

"Yes, Mother."

But something shifted there, at the tree line, with the wind keening over the rocks. Sela had joined this expedition carrying a coffin's worth of unsaid words, and I'd watched her distribute them slowly — a few to her dead father via letters, a few to the road, a few to the dark. Now she was spending them on the living. On Marisol. It was a small thing. Most things that matter are.

* * *

The near-fatal fall was mine, because apparently I'm not allowed a chapter without nearly dying.

We hit the traverse below the pass proper on the afternoon of the fourth day — a shelf of scree and old ice cut across the face of the mountain, wide enough for mules in single file, dropping away on the right into a gorge that went down long enough to give you time to reconsider your life during the fall. Which I did, briefly, when the ice shelf I was cutting steps into gave way beneath me with a sound like a door closing.

No drama. That's the thing nobody tells you. One moment I was swinging my axe, the next the world tilted and the mountain simply declined to hold me, and I was sliding, axe skittering off rock, toward the edge with the whole grey drop yawning.

Sela had me. Rope, anchored around her waist and Piotr's, and she threw herself flat and took my weight on the line, and for perhaps ten seconds the entire expedition hung on the friction of her body against stone and the stubbornness in her arms. Piotr braked beside her. Isolde shouted instructions in cartographer's shorthand that somehow translated perfectly into "dig in, you idiots."

They hauled me back onto the shelf. I lay there gasping, staring at the sky, feeling my heartbeat in my teeth.

"Well," Marisol said, peering down the gorge. "That looked expensive."

"You caught me," I said to Sela.

"Don't make it a habit." She was shaking, I noticed. So was I. She coiled the rope with hands that wouldn't quite obey. "My father fell in the highlands too, according to his letters. He wrote that someone caught him, and he never forgot it." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "So don't die. I'm not burying anyone else on this mountain."

We moved on. What else is there? The mountain doesn't pause for gratitude.

* * *

Isolde collapsed at dusk, at the mouth of the high camp, and for one black hour I thought we might lose her.

Not injury. Altitude and exhaustion and — I suspected — the vertigo of walking into her father's shadow, the depot coordinates burning a hole in her pocket like a coal. She'd been pushing harder than any of us, mapping as we climbed, double-checking every bearing, sleeping less than the rest, and the bill came due all at once. She simply folded, mid-step, into the snow beside the trail.

We got her into the shelter cave Piotr knew of, warmed her, forced broth and sugar into her. She surfaced eventually, grey-faced, furious at herself.

"I'm slowing us down," she rasped.

"You're the only one who can read the final approach," I said. "You're not slowing us down. You're the map."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — the old wound, the disgrace, the years of drinking away a career because the maps she drew were called lies by men with medals. "Bram Montrose," she said, "if I die up here, burn my survey notes. Don't let them grade them posthumously."

"Noted. Now sleep."

She slept. And I sat at the cave mouth with the wind screaming past and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding: Verrick below, closing. Snow above, coming. Isolde half-dead. Marisol broke. Sela carrying grief like ballast. And me, holding a promise that had metastasized into truth-telling, wondering how a salvage diver from nowhere had become the man responsible for five lives on a mountain.

The answer, I supposed, was that he'd given his word to a dying stranger. Everything else followed from that, the way everything follows from the first bad decision on any voyage.

* * *

Dawn of the fifth day came clean and brutally clear — the calm before the front, Piotr said, gesturing at the wall of cloud building beyond the pass like a siege engine. We had until tomorrow night. Maybe.

Isolde, moving slowly but upright, spread the map on a flat stone and traced the route one final time. "Depot's a day beyond the pass, in the high valley. If we cross today and move fast tomorrow, we make it ahead of the snow." She looked up. "If Verrick's regulars push hard tonight—"

"They'll be tired," Bram— I corrected myself; even my internal narration was getting sloppy. "They'll be tired, and we'll be gone."

Marisol flexed her bandaged hands. "Right. Final inventory. I have no money, mild frostbite, and a mule who hates me. Let's go get haunted by war crimes."

We crossed the pass that afternoon.

I won't dress it up. It was four hours of white hell, wind stripping the slope, the mules balking and Piotr singing to them in a language I didn't know, all of us roped together like beads on a string that the mountain kept trying to snap. At the crest — a saddle of wind-carved ice between two peaks too steep to name — we paused for thirty seconds, no more, and looked back the way we'd come.

Far below, tiny as insects, torchlight was moving up the switchbacks. Verrick's regulars. Marching through the night that was about to swallow the pass behind us.

"They'll make the saddle by tomorrow midday," Piotr signed. "If the snow lets them."

"Then we have a day," I said. "One day at the depot. Maybe less."

Sela stood beside me at the crest, wind tearing at her hood, and I saw her look down at those lights and then up at the high valley ahead, where her father had walked thirty years ago and never come home whole.

"He knew," she said quietly. "In the letters. He wrote that he'd found something up here the navy would kill to keep buried. I thought it was gold." She pulled her collar tighter. "It was never gold, was it?"

"No," I said. "I don't think it ever was."

Behind us, the first flakes of the storm came spinning over the saddle, dry and fine as ash. Ahead, the valley waited with whatever her father had died carrying.

We walked into it, all five of us and four mules, one day ahead of the snow and half a day ahead of the men with torches — and somewhere in the back of my mind, the wreck I'd stripped months ago turned over in its grave, patient as the deep, waiting for its story to finally surface.
