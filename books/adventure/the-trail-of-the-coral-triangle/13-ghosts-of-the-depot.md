## 13. Ghosts of the Depot

I have spent my life reading the lies that men draw into maps, so perhaps I should have known better than to trust the silence at the top of the world.

The last hour of the climb was the kind that cartographers omit. There is no symbol for a slope that hates you personally, no contour line for the way the scree shifts under your boot like it's been waiting all morning for exactly you. Bram went first, testing each step with his ice axe the way an old man tests ice on a pond, and Sela followed him with Marisol's rope around her waist, and I came last because someone had to carry the map case against my chest, and because — if I'm honest — I wanted to be the one who read the ground before anyone else touched it.

The fog lifted all at once, the way it does above the treeline, as though someone had pulled a sheet off furniture in a house long shut.

And there it was.

Not gold. Not yet. First: walls. Low stone walls, green with fifty years of lichen, laid out in the stiff geometry of military thinking — barracks, a parade square gone to moss, a flagpole with no flag and its halyard still frozen in a knot some boy's hands had tied the last morning anyone stood here. Beyond them, half-swallowed by the hillside, a great door of timber and iron set into the rock itself, and over the door, carved deep enough that even the weather hadn't dared erase it, the survey mark I had grown up tracing in my father's ledgers.

The crown. The anchor. The number seven.

My father's mark. My father's hand, or the hand of men who used his charts. I stood there longer than I meant to, longer than Bram liked, because Bram was watching me the way he watches everything — quietly, from the corner of his eye, filing it away for later — and I could feel the question forming behind his weathered face before he'd said a word.

*Isolde,* he finally said, not unkindly. *You knew.*

*I suspected.* That is the honest answer, and honesty has never once made me feel clean. *I suspected since the reef channels. The cipher in the margins wasn't a prospector's cipher. Prospectors don't learn navy shorthand. Someone taught Aldan that notation, or Aldan stole it out of a dead man's kit, and either way the numbers weren't pointing at a cave full of ore. They were pointing here.*

*And you didn't say.*

*Would it have changed anything?* I asked him, and watched him turn that over honestly, which is more than most men do with a hard question. He would have come anyway. Sela would have come anyway. The promise would have dragged us up this mountain regardless; the only difference is that I'd have been carrying my father's ghost openly instead of discovering him at eight thousand feet with my hands shaking.

Marisol, for her part, took one look at the parade square and started laughing — that bright, terrible laugh of hers, the one she deploys when she wants the world to know she isn't afraid, which is how I've learned to tell when she very much is. *It's a ruin,* she said. *We climbed through leeches and rot and a pass ahead of the snows for a ruin.*

*Wait,* Sela said.

She was standing at the great door, and her voice had gone quiet in a way I'd only heard once before — at her father's grave, such as it was, a cairn we'd built on the coast with nothing under it but stones and unsaid things. She put her palm flat against the iron hinge, cold enough to burn, and then she stepped inside first, before any of us, because whatever was in there belonged to her father's story and she had decided, somewhere on the rope bridge or in the old woman's hut, that she was done letting other people hold it for her.

The door groaned open on hinges that should have seized decades ago. Someone had oiled them. Recently. That was the first wrong note, and I heard it the way you hear a false chord — instantly, in your teeth.

Inside, the mountain opened into a vaulted gallery cut straight into the living rock, and along both walls, stacked in ranks, stood the crates.

Hundreds of them. Stenciled in fading white: ordnance, rations, medical, paymaster. The paymaster crates were the ones Bram went to first, because Bram is Bram, and I watched him lever one open with the flat of his axe and stand very still in the light of Marisol's lantern while the gold caught it — bars of it, dull yellow, stamped with a mint mark from a country that had stopped existing before any of us were born. Enough to buy a fleet. Enough to buy forgiveness, if forgiveness were the sort of thing that could be bought, which I have always doubted and now doubt more.

*There it is,* Marisol said, and her voice cracked with something between triumph and disbelief. *The whole reason. The whole stupid, impossible, magnificent reason. We found it.*

We found it. Yes. And for about four minutes, four golden minutes, we were rich — laughing, weighing bars in our palms, Marisol doing rapid arithmetic about ships and debts and the particular freedom of owing no one anything. Even Sela smiled, briefly, the way you smile at a stranger's good luck.

Then Bram pried open the second crate. The one stenciled not PAYMASTER but RECORDS.

And the four minutes ended.

*

Paper keeps differently than gold. Gold just waits. Paper waits too, but it waits accusingly, and when Bram lifted the lid off the records crate the smell that came out was the smell of every archive I've ever feared — dust, iron-gall ink, and something underneath both, faint and organic, that I recognized and wished I didn't.

Bundles. Tied with government twine. Manifests, muster rolls, court-martial summaries, and then — at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that suggested someone had hidden them rather than filed them — a stack of sworn depositions.

I read the first one aloud because my hands were steadier than Bram's and my training was better and because somebody had to, and the room went silent around me in a way rooms rarely do when I'm talking.

Prisoners. Two hundred and eleven of them, taken after a garrison action nobody official ever named, held at this depot through a wet season, fed from stores that the manifests showed were ample, and then, in the third week of a month my father's ledger marks only as *survey suspended*, marched out of this gallery in columns and not marched back.

The depositions were written by three men. A corporal, a chaplain's assistant, and a paymaster's clerk — the clerk being the man who had counted the gold, recorded the rations, and then, when the order came down, counted something else entirely and could not make his arithmetic lie about it. His deposition ran forty pages. He described the orders. He described who gave them. He signed his name, and beneath his signature, in a different hand, someone had written a single word across the page:

DENIED.

That word was Verrick's. I knew it was Verrick's before anyone told me, because I had spent two days in a cabin across from Commander Aldous Verrick during his courteous inspection of our boat, and courtesy leaves handwriting the way fire leaves smoke. The denial was dated six weeks after the massacre. The clerk's name appears nowhere in any subsequent muster roll. Neither do the other two.

And folded into the oilcloth with the depositions, a chart. My trade's own instrument, turned inside out. It showed the approaches to the coast, the wreck we had stripped in the first chapter of all this — the wreck the navy patrols and will not name — and in the margin, in survey notation I could read in my sleep, the sinking plotted. Deliberate. Cargo scuttled in deep water. Prisoners' bodies listed as *expended stores*.

They had sunk the evidence. They had sunk the men. And they had built the cover-up on charts drawn by men trained in my father's school, possibly — the notation was his era's, his habits, that particular way he had of curling a depth figure like a hook — quite possibly by my father himself.

I sat down on a crate of gold and read my father's probable handwriting describing a war crime, and I want to tell you what that did to me, but the truth is I still don't fully know. Something in me went quiet and stayed quiet, the way a clock goes quiet when you realize it was never keeping the time you thought.

Sela read the muster roll of the two hundred and eleven. She read it the way she does everything, completely, refusing to skim, running her finger down column after column of names, and near the bottom she stopped. She looked up at us with an expression I couldn't parse, and then she said, *One of these men is from the village. The elder's brother. She told us to carry the truth down the mountain, and here it is, and his name is on a list that says EXPENDED.*

Bram had not spoken in twenty minutes. When he finally did, it was to Marisol, quietly, at the edge of the lantern light, and I only heard it because I was sitting close enough and because Bram, for all his guardedness, forgets sometimes how sound carries in stone chambers.

*This stopped being about the promise a long time ago,* he said. *I gave my word to a dying man about a map. The map was true. But the man who drew it — Aldan wasn't a prospector hunting gold. Look at the margins again. He was documenting. He spent thirty years walking to this depot and away from it, drawing it, ciphering it, hiding the record where only someone stubborn and desperate and honest enough to keep a promise would ever find it. He wasn't leaving us a treasure. He was leaving us a witness.*

Marisol looked at the gold in her hands and then at the papers on her knees, and I watched her make, in real time, the calculation that will define the rest of her life. Her family runs shipping lines. Her family has moved cargo for navies. Her family, I would bet the gold I'm sitting on, has moved cargo for *this* navy, and she knows it, and she has run halfway around the world from that inheritance without ever once naming what she was running from.

*My father's ships carried things,* she said finally, to no one, to the dark vaulted ceiling. *Crates with stencils on them. I used to think the stencils were boring.*

Nobody laughed. For the first time since the customs boarding, nobody laughed at anything Marisol Quennel said, and she noticed, and something in her face settled, like a compass needle finding north after years of spinning.

*

We worked until the light failed, and by *light* I mean Marisol's lanterns, because the depot has no windows and the great door faces east and the sun had already dropped behind the ridge. We catalogued. We photographed — Marisol had a camera, because of course she did, heiresses travel with cameras — and we wrapped the depositions in oilcloth again, all of them, and packed them into the map case and two rucksacks with the tenderness people usually reserve for relics.

The gold we left.

Not all of it — Bram took three bars, and when Sela asked him why, he said, *Because the promise was real, and the man died clutching a map to a cache, and part of the cache was gold, and I won't pretend otherwise. Three bars. One for his daughter. One for the village. One for the boat that got us here. The rest stays. It isn't ours. It was never ours. It was blood money sitting in a drawer, and taking it would make us clerks in the same ledger.*

I want to be clear about what that decision cost, because I watched it cost. Marisol stood with her arms crossed for a long time, doing sums, and then she walked to the crate, picked up a bar, weighed it, put it down, and said, *Fine. Fine. But when my family disinherits me — again, properly this time, with lawyers — I'm telling them it was for this.* And she laughed, and this time the laugh was real, and I understood that Marisol Quennel had just become someone new and would never fit back inside the person she'd been on the dock at the port town.

Sela took the muster roll of the two hundred and eleven and folded it into her jacket, against her chest, where her father's letters had ridden since the village. She said, *He knew. All those years, he knew, and he sent me a map instead of a letter, because a letter can be burned and a map has to be followed.* Then she cried, briefly, without apology, and dried her face on her sleeve, and went back to work, and that is the bravest thing I saw anyone do on this entire mountain, including everything that happened afterward.

As for me, I copied the depot's position into my own field book, corrected to the second, and then I drew — deliberately, professionally, with the last clean page in the book — a false chart. A decoy. Coordinates shifted nine kilometers northeast into a bowl of glacier that will kill anyone who goes looking in earnest. If Verrick comes, let him come with my father's notation leading him into the ice. It seemed fitting. It seemed like the only thing I had to offer that was truly mine.

We made camp inside the gallery, in the lee of the records crates, and ate cold rations, and Marisol rigged the lanterns to throw shadows on the vaulted stone, and for an hour it was almost cozy, the five of us — no, four of us, I keep counting Dekker's deserters who aren't here, scratch that — the four of us sitting among the ghosts of two hundred and eleven men, richer than we'd ever been and poorer than we'd ever felt, and happier, somehow, than any of us could account for.

Bram banked the small stove and looked around at us and said, *Tomorrow we go down. Down is harder than up, with this weight. And Verrick knows these mountains better than we do.*

*How do you know he knows?* Marisol asked.

*Because the hinges were oiled,* Bram said.

I lay awake a long time after that, listening to the wind work at the great door, thinking about my father's hooked depth-figures, thinking about the clerk who wrote forty pages knowing it would cost him his name, thinking about how every map I have ever loved is a document of both discovery and erasure, depending on who holds it.

And then, sometime past midnight, the wind changed direction, and brought with it, faint but unmistakable, from the col below the ridge —

Engines.

More than one. Climbing.
