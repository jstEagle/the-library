## 14. Verrick Arrives

We heard the engines for a full hour before we saw anything, which in the mountains is its own kind of torture. Sound up here doesn't travel so much as it *arrives* — bounced off the col, folded around the ridge, arriving at your ear from directions that make no sense. Isolde stood at the depot's broken parapet with her binoculars and called out bearings like a woman reading tea leaves.

"Two vehicles on the switchback. Maybe three. They've stopped."

"They've stopped," Marisol repeated, "is not what I wanted to hear."

"It's worse than stopped." Isolde lowered the glasses. Her face had gone the color of old paper. "They're walking. Bram — they left the road. There are men on foot in the treeline below us. A dozen at least. Maybe more."

Sela came up from the cache chamber with a crate lid under one arm and read our faces the way you read weather. "How long?"

"Dark comes in forty minutes," Bram said. Which was me. I said it about myself sometimes, out of habit, the way a man taps his own chest to make sure the heart's still there. "They'll wait for dark. Verrick isn't a man who climbs mountains in daylight when he can make the mountain come to him."

We spent those forty minutes the way people spend the last of anything: badly. Marisol wanted to run for the pass and I told her the truth, which was that a dozen soldiers move faster uphill than four civilians carrying documents, and she hated the truth and said so, and then helped me bar the great door anyway. Sela stacked crates across the inner entrance with a methodical fury that told me more about her state of mind than any words would have. Isolde sat down amid her father's survey charts and began, calmly, insanely, to fold them into her coat.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"If they burn them, I want at least one set walking out of here inside someone's ribs." She didn't look up. "My father taught me that. Paper survives by dispersal. It was the only useful thing he ever taught me."

The light went copper, then grey, then gone. And in the blue dark that followed, the mountain made its sounds — wind in the door hinges, stone ticking as it cooled, the far-off groan of the glacier — and underneath all of it, boots on scree. Many boots. Patient boots.

They didn't force the door. That was the first thing I understood, crouched in the dark with a pry bar in my hands and no better weapon anywhere among us. They didn't need to. The knocking, when it came, was almost gentle. Three raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man calling on a neighbor.

"Mr. Montrose." The voice carried through two feet of timber and forty years of navy paint as if neither existed. It was a pleasant voice. That was the worst part. "I know you can hear me. I'd rather not have my men break down a historical structure. These walls have seen enough damage."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed.

"I have hot food," the voice offered. "And I have answers. And I have, if it comes to it, other options — but I find options are so rarely necessary with reasonable people. Are you reasonable people, Mr. Montrose?"

I looked at my crew. Marisol's jaw was set; she'd already decided something and wasn't sharing it. Isolde had gone very still, the stillness of a woman listening for a specific word. Sela was looking at me.

I put down the pry bar.

"All right," I called back. "But we open the door. Not you."

"Naturally."

* * *

Commander Aldous Verrick came into the depot the way a host enters his own home. He'd changed out of the whites I'd half-expected — this was field kit, grey and practical, cut well — but he wore authority the way other men wore cologne, faintly and unmistakably. Behind him came six soldiers who took up positions with a smoothness that said they'd done this before, in places whose names never made reports. And behind *them*, shuffling in the cold with their hands jammed in their pockets and their eyes everywhere, came Dekker's survivors. Four of them. Hollowed out, frost-nipped, looking at us with expressions I couldn't read and didn't like.

Verrick surveyed the chamber — the crates, the scattered paymaster gold catching the lamplight, the documents Isolde had spent a night cataloguing — with an expression of mild, scholarly interest. Like a man at an exhibition.

"Well," he said. "You found it."

"You knew it was here," I said.

"I've known about this place since I was a lieutenant, Mr. Montrose. I've spent a career making sure nobody else did." He turned, and smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Which is why I'm not angry. Do you understand? I'm not angry. Angry is for subordinates. You've simply stumbled into a situation larger than yourselves, and my job — my actual job, tonight — is to help you out of it alive."

He gestured, and a soldier set down a canvas pack and produced, of all things, a spirit stove and a kettle. The smell of coffee began to fill the chamber, absurdly domestic.

"Let me tell you what happens next," Verrick continued, warming his hands. "Option one: you resist. My men are patient, well-armed, and outnumber you nine to one, and this building has exactly one door, which you were thoughtful enough to reinforce from the inside — thank you for that, it saved me the trouble of deciding whether to use the incendiaries. Option two: you run for the pass tonight, in the dark, ahead of a storm front that will arrive within thirty-six hours, carrying crates of paper through snow that will be waist-deep by morning. I give that perhaps a twenty percent chance, and the mountain keeps the bodies warm enough that spring thaw makes them findable, which creates paperwork I'd rather avoid." He sipped his coffee. "Or option three."

"Which is?" Marisol asked. Her voice was steady. I was proud of her and terrified for her in equal measure.

"Which is that everyone in this room walks down the mountain rich." He let that sit. "There is gold in this room — paymaster gold, unregistered, unclaimed, officially destroyed in a warehouse fire twenty-two years ago. On paper, it does not exist. In practice, it is sitting here waiting for whoever is sensible enough to carry it away. Split it. All of it. I will sign nothing, remember nothing, and see nothing. In addition—" he turned to Marisol, and his smile sharpened by a degree, "—Miss Quennel. Your family has been trying to buy back a certain shipping contract since your grandfather lost it. I can restore that contract. One signature. Your mother stops writing you letters about duty; your grandmother dies content; you go home not as the runaway but as the daughter who delivered."

Marisol's face did something complicated. I watched her want it. God help me, I watched her *want* it, and I understood, because I was doing arithmetic of my own — brass and salvage and a boat free and clear, no more diving wrecks the sea wants kept shut.

"And you, Mr. Montrose." Verrick came closer. Up close his eyes were pale and unhurried, the eyes of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice. "The wreck off the coast. The one the navy won't name. You stripped her brass and you asked yourself why the patrols were so thick, and you never got an answer, so let me give you one: because she is full of prisoners this government swore never existed. Because a unit under my command was ordered to do a thing that official history says never happened, and I obeyed, and I have spent twenty-two years being the only man who remembers, which means I am also the only man who can be blamed. Do you understand what I'm offering you? I'm offering you the chance to walk away from a dead man's map and a dead man's promise and keep everything you came for. Amnesty. Wealth. Silence." He spread his hands. "It's generous because it costs me nothing. The past is only dangerous if it surfaces."

"And the prospector?" Sela said.

She hadn't spoken until then. She stood by the crates with her arms crossed, and her voice came out flat and cold as lake ice.

"Ardan," Verrick said, and something flickered behind the pleasantness — recognition, maybe, or accounting. "Yes. Your father. A difficult man. He found things he shouldn't have and sold them to people who shouldn't have bought them, and eventually he found *this*, and at that point the conversation between us became... final." He said it without cruelty, which was the most frightening thing yet. He spoke of murder the way a clerk speaks of filing. "Your father was not a good man, Miss Ardan. Whatever he told you at the end."

"He didn't tell me anything at the end," Sela said. "That was the point. He gave the telling to someone else." Her eyes came to me, and away. "Because he knew his daughter wouldn't take his money either."

"Then you understand," Verrick said gently, "that you owe him nothing—"

Dekker's survivors shifted. I'd been watching them since they came in — four men trying to look like spectators — and now their leader, a scarred little ferret named Coss, cleared his throat and stepped forward.

"Commander. About that amnesty. Me and the lads, we were Dekker's, not these people's. We never saw any documents. We'd testify to that."

Verrick didn't even turn his head. "You'll be compensated for your discretion, yes. Go stand by the door."

Coss went. And the sound of those four men crossing to the other side of the room — boots on stone, casual, done — was the loudest thing I'd heard all day. Marisol exhaled through her teeth. Isolde's hand had found the folded charts inside her coat, unconsciously, the way a child finds a pocket.

"There it is," Verrick said, almost kindly, watching our faces. "You see how it works? Loyalty is a market, Mr. Montrose. Everyone has a price; most people's price is simply fear wearing a nicer coat. I'm offering you the chance to skip the fear entirely." He checked a pocket watch, an affectation so perfect it had to be genuine. "You have until dawn. Talk amongst yourselves. No one will stop you leaving — but anyone who leaves *with* documents leaves as a problem, and I solve problems before breakfast."

* * *

They gave us the far chamber, with two soldiers at the door, which meant they weren't worried about us, which meant they were right to not be worried, and I sat against a wall in the dark and took inventory.

Marisol spoke first, barely above breath. "The contract's real. He could do it. One signature."

"Mari," I said.

"I *know*. I know what it is. I'm saying it's real, that's all. He didn't offer us fairy gold. That's what makes him dangerous — everything he offered is true." She laughed, low and bitter. "Twenty years of running from that family and he finds the one door I'd walk through myself."

"He's not offering doors," Isolde said from the corner. "He's offering windows painted on walls. The contract gets restored, and then it gets revoked the first time you're inconvenient. Amnesty means he owns you. That's what amnesty *is* — it's a debt with your name on it that never comes due until he chooses." She pulled the charts tighter against her chest. "My father took a version of that offer once. Survey work, good pay, don't ask questions. Forty pages in the cache say what he bought with it, and what it cost him. I am not going to die owning the same debt."

"Sela," I said.

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had something in it I hadn't heard before — not the anger she'd carried up the whole mountain, but something older and colder underneath it.

"When I was nine," she said, "my father left for six months and came back with a navy officer's coin on a cord. Said a friend gave it to him. I wore it for a year. I thought it was the finest thing I owned." A breath. "He was wearing it too, when Bram found him. Shot through the coat pocket where he kept it. He died holding the gift of the man who killed him." She stood. "He's downstairs offering us the same coin. Same cord. Same price."

She walked out before we could stop her — and the soldiers let her, which told me Verrick wanted an audience for whatever came next.

We followed. Of course we followed.

Down in the cache chamber, the lamps had been turned low. Verrick stood among the gold crates with his coffee, and Sela crossed the stones to him, and for one strange moment they faced each other — the commander and the prospector's daughter, twenty-two years of buried history standing upright in the shape of two people.

"Miss Ardan," he began, courteous as ever. "If we could speak privately about your father's—"

She spat in his face.

It caught him full across the cheek, and the chamber went absolutely silent — soldiers, defectors, my crew, everyone — and Verrick stood very still while it ran down toward his jaw. He didn't wipe it. That was the discipline in him; some cold engine somewhere counted to five before he even blinked.

"My father sold maps," Sela said, loud enough for every man in the room, "and it ruined him, and he knew it, and he crawled up this mountain dying to fix one thing. You sold *people*, and you've never once known it, and you climbed this mountain healthy and fed to bury it deeper. So keep your gold, Commander. Keep your contract. Keep your amnesty." Her voice cracked, finally, on the last of it, grief and fury fused into one thing. "We're taking the truth down the mountain, and every soul in these highlands is going to hear it, and there is nothing you can buy that will fit in the hole where your soul used to be."

The silence held. Somewhere outside, the wind rose against the great door, and the first flakes of the early storm ticked against the broken glass overhead.

Verrick took out a handkerchief. Wiped his cheek, once, precisely. Folded the handkerchief away. And when he looked at Sela, the pleasantness was gone — not replaced by rage, which I could have fought, but by something far worse: *interest*. The look of a man revising an estimate upward.

"Dawn," he said quietly, to all of us. "I'd reconsider the storm arithmetic, Mr. Montrose. The mountain forgives nothing. Neither do I."

He turned to go, and paused at the door, and added, without turning around:

"Oh — and Miss Quennel. Give my regards to your grandmother. She and I dined together in the capital, not three months ago. Lovely woman. Very concerned about you."

The door closed behind him. Boots faded into the dark. And Marisol stood staring at the space where he'd been, white-faced, whispering:

"He's known where I was. This whole time. He's *known*."

Outside, the snow began in earnest — fat, silent, early — and I looked at my crew, at the crates of paper, at the single door, and started doing the only arithmetic that mattered anymore.

Thirty-six hours of storm. Twelve hours of darkness. And one way down.
