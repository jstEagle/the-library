## 15. The Long Night

The navy had left us one candle.

I want to be fair about this — fairness being a thing I learned tonight, in a ruined depot at the top of the world — but it's hard to feel generous toward men who lock you in a stone room with a single candle and then post two soldiers outside to cough at each other all night. The candle sat on an ammunition crate between us, guttering, throwing our shadows up the walls like prisoners of its own. Somewhere beyond the broken roof, the snow was starting. You could hear it before you could see it: a hush, a pressure, the mountain drawing breath to hold it against us until spring.

Four of us. One candle. And outside those doors, thirty soldiers who answered to a man who smiled like a bank manager and killed like weather.

"So," Marisol said, breaking the silence the way she broke everything — cheerfully, and without asking permission. "Anyone else find it ironic that we finally found the treasure and now we're sitting on it like dragons who've been captured by other dragons?"

"It's not treasure," I said.

"It's paymaster gold, Sela. I counted it. Twice."

"You counted the gold. I read the papers." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "It's not treasure. It's evidence."

Bram looked up from where he sat against the wall, working at the rope around his wrists with a patience that would have bored a saint. He'd been at it for an hour. He didn't seem discouraged. That was Bram — a man who treated captivity as a scheduling problem.

"Evidence burns," Isolde said quietly.

We all looked at her. She was sitting farthest from the candle, which was typical; Isolde Vane arranged herself in shadows the way other people arranged themselves in light. Her shoulder was bruised where a rifle butt had found it during the surrender, and she held that arm carefully, like something borrowed.

"Verrick doesn't need to kill us," she said. "He needs one fire. Documents burn faster than people can run. Then we're four witnesses with no proof, and witnesses are just rumors with legs."

"Cheerful," Marisol said.

"I'm a cartographer. Accuracy is my profession."

* * *

They'd taken everything except the letters.

That was the strange mercy of the evening, the one detail I kept turning over like a stone in my pocket. Verrick's soldiers had stripped Bram's knife, Marisol's money belt, Isolde's instruments, even the waxed packet of documents we'd carried up half a mountain. But they'd missed the oilskin wrap sewn into the lining of my coat — or maybe they hadn't thought an old man's letters were worth confiscating, and there's a whole other kind of insult in that.

My father's letters. Forty years old, water-stained, kept for me by a woman in a village I'd never heard of until three weeks ago, by a man I'd spent twenty-six years deciding to hate.

I'd been carrying them since the village. Through the pass. Through Dekker's ambush, through the frostbite and the fall and the crossing where Marisol nearly went under the ice. I'd carried them the way you carry a debt you're not sure you owe — resentfully, and always.

Now, with nothing left to do but wait for dawn to decide what we were, I unwrapped them.

"What are those?" Bram asked. He'd stopped working on his ropes. Even his escape plans knew when to give way.

"Letters." I worked the knot loose with numb fingers. "From him. To me. He wrote them every year, apparently. The old woman said he sent them care of the village because he didn't know where I'd be. Or—" I stopped. Swallowed. "Or because he couldn't make himself send them anywhere at all."

"Are you going to read them?" Marisol asked, softer than I'd ever heard her.

"I'm going to read one," I said. "That's already more than I planned."

* * *

The first letter was dated the year I turned nine.

*Sela —*

*I don't know if these reach you. I've been told they might not, and I've decided to write anyway, because a man who stops writing is a man who's decided, and I haven't decided anything yet except that I was wrong.*

*You'll want to know why I left. I've started this letter eleven times and eleven times I've written the easy version — the fever, the debts, the wanderlust. Here is the true version, which is worse: I found something in the high country that frightened me, and instead of standing in front of it, I ran home to pack. Your mother deserved better than a man whose courage only worked outdoors. So did you.*

*I'm going back. Not for the gold — everyone thinks it's gold, and I let them think it because the truth gets men shot. When you're older, if you ever read this, know that your father was a coward twice over: once for running, and once for taking so long to come back.*

*— Papa*

I read it twice. The candle flame leaned in the draft from the broken roof, and nobody spoke, and somewhere outside a soldier coughed the way men cough when they're pretending they aren't listening.

"He never came back," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "He wrote that he was going back, and then he just — stayed away. Twenty years. Sending letters into the dark like messages in bottles."

"Maybe the dark wouldn't let him out," Bram said.

"That's generous."

"Bram's entire personality is generosity disguised as grumpiness," Marisol offered. "Don't tell him."

"I'm right here."

"You're also still picking at that rope, so arguably you're not."

I should have laughed. Some part of me registered that the joke was good and that laughing was the correct response, but I was looking at my father's handwriting — the careful, cramped script of a man who'd taught himself letters from a salvaged primer — and what I felt wasn't funny. It was the vertigo of discovering that the villain of your life story has been, all along, a frightened man writing apologies to a child who never received them.

Twenty-six years I'd built him out of absence. A drunk, the gossips said. A drifter. A man who chose mountains over his own daughter. And here was the truth, in ink faded to the color of weak tea: a coward. Just a coward. Who loved us badly, from far away, in sentences he never had the nerve to mail.

Somehow that was worse than everything I'd imagined. And somehow — this is the part I still can't explain — it was also a door opening.

* * *

I read the second letter aloud. I don't know why. Maybe because the room was too quiet, and grief does poorly in silence; maybe because Marisol and Bram and Isolde had earned the right to hear it, having bled on the same ice my father had once described.

*They were prisoners,* he wrote. *That's what nobody will say. Not deserters, not bandits — prisoners, marched up from the coast in chains, and the orders came down about what to do with them, and the officer who gave the orders signed his name with a flourish like he was signing a dinner invitation. I was hired to survey supply routes. I drew maps for a massacre. Every contour line I put on that chart was a wall around a grave.*

*I took the cache map from the paymaster's records. If anyone comes asking, I buried the truth where only a stubborn man with a good memory could find it. I hoped that man would be me. I was wrong again. I hope it's you.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the snow hissed against the stones.

Isolde made a sound. Small, controlled, the sound of a woman swallowing something sharp. When I looked at her, she was staring at the letter in my hands as though it were a chart she'd drawn herself.

"Isolde?" Bram said.

"My father surveyed the depot too." Her voice was level in the way that ice is level — flat, and hiding depth. "I told you in the village that the margins matched his hand. What I didn't say is that I recognized the notation system. The ciphered coordinates. I recognized it because he invented it, and he taught it to me when I was twelve, at the same table where he taught me to draw coastlines." She paused. "And I recognized the date. My father was there, Bram. Before the massacre. After it. He drew the depot, and he drew the routes the prisoners were marched along, and then he came home and taught his daughter mathematics and never once — not once in thirty years — said a word."

Marisol reached across the crate and put her hand over Isolde's. To my astonishment, Isolde let her.

"I built my whole career on hating him," Isolde went on. "Every map I corrected, every boundary I redrew, every time I refused to fudge a survey for a client with deep pockets — I told myself I was erasing him. Being everything he wasn't. And tonight I find out he wasn't a bad cartographer. He was a good one. Good enough to help bury forty men under accurate coordinates." She looked up, and in the candlelight her eyes were dry and terrible. "Excellence isn't virtue. That's what no one tells you. My father was excellent, and it made him useful to monsters, and usefulness is how monsters get their work done."

"And yours?" Bram asked gently. "Your excellence?"

"Mine has been pointing a gun at the truth since I met all of you." Something almost like a smile crossed her face. "I deciphered those margins weeks ago, Bram. In the village. I knew what the coordinates marked before we ever left the boats. I told myself I was confirming the translation. I was deciding whether to tell you."

"Why didn't you?" I asked.

"Because telling you meant admitting what my father was. And because—" She exhaled. "Because I wanted the gold to be gold. For one more week, I wanted to be a woman hunting treasure instead of a woman excavating her family's crimes. Selfishness, deferred. It's remarkable how long you can defer it while walking uphill."

Nobody condemned her. I watched Bram's face and saw no judgment there at all — only recognition, the look of a man cataloguing another weight he understood.

"We all brought baggage up this mountain," Marisol said. "Mine had shoes in it. Expensive ones. Let's not rank our sins by weight."

* * *

Somewhere past midnight, Bram got his ropes off.

He didn't announce it. He simply stood, flexed his hands, and began moving along the walls with the candle cupped behind his fingers, reading the depot the way Isolde read charts. We watched him test the door frame, the window casements, the joints of the roof beams. When he came back, he crouched among us and spoke barely above the wind.

"Two guards. They rotate at first light — I heard the relief discussed. The east casement is rotted through; it'll come out quiet if we take our time. Beyond it, the yard, then the treeline. Snow's coming down heavy enough to cover tracks."

"And then what?" I whispered. "Run where? Verrick owns the passes. Dekker's people are defecting to him — we watched it happen. There's nowhere to run to."

"There's nowhere to run to," Bram agreed. "There's somewhere to run *through*, though. Down-mountain, to the coast, to a radio, to Marisol's family's shipping network. We don't need to escape Verrick forever. We need to escape him long enough to make the truth louder than he is."

"They'll hunt us."

"They'll try. But the storm's ours now." His eyes went to the broken roof, to the white darkness beyond it, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not grimness exactly. Relief. "That wilderness forgives nothing. I've been saying it since the wreck. Well — it doesn't forgive Verrick either. Snow doesn't check uniforms."

Marisol grinned in the dark. "So the plan is: flee into a blizzard, carrying documents, pursued by soldiers, toward a radio we may not reach."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"How would you say it?"

"Strategically reckless."

Isolde made a sound that might have been a laugh. Then she sobered, and looked around the circle of us — the heiress, the diver, the prospector's daughter — and said, quietly: "Before we do this. I need to ask. All of you. Because after tonight I won't have the nerve."

She laid her hand flat on the crate, next to the candle.

"The gold is ten feet from where we're sitting. Under the floor planks where we found it, some of it still scattered. Verrick's offer stands — wealth, amnesty, silence. Any one of us could take it. Any one of us could buy a new life before breakfast." Her eyes moved from face to face. "So I'm asking plainly. Are we leaving with the truth? All of it? Because once we go over that wall, there's no version of this where some of us keep the secret and some of us don't. It has to be all of us, or none."

Silence. The candle flame stood very straight, as if it were listening too.

I thought about my father's last letter — the one written in a shaking hand, near the end, the one I'd read while the others slept earlier in the trek. *If you find the cache, daughter, you'll find what I couldn't carry. I'm sorry to leave you the weight. It was never gold. It was only ever the truth, and the truth is heavy because so many hands set it down.*

"I'm in," I said. "All of it. He waited twenty years for someone to pick it up. I'm not putting it back down."

"All of it," Bram said. No hesitation. As if the question had been settled for him long ago, on the deck of a wreck the navy wouldn't name.

"All of it," Marisol said, and then, wryly: "Though I want it noted for posterity that I am voluntarily walking away from a fortune, and my family will disown me, and I have never been happier about anything."

They all looked at Isolde last. She nodded once, slow, like a seal pressed into wax.

"Then we go at the change of guard," Bram said. "Quietly. Together. Nobody's a hero, nobody's left behind. We move like water — downhill, patient, finding every crack."

* * *

After that, there was nothing to do but wait, and waiting is its own country with its own weather.

Marisol slept first, actually slept, curled against the ammunition crate with the untroubled breathing of a woman who had made her decision and saw no reason to lose rest over it. Bram dozed sitting up, the way sailors do, one ear open. Isolde stared at the wall, rehearsing something — confessions, perhaps, still owed.

I read the rest of the letters.

Not all of them — there were too many, twenty years of them, a father's whole unwitnessed life in fading ink. But I read enough. The year he tried to come home and turned back at the harbor because he'd heard my mother had remarried and decided I was better off without a ghost haunting the doorstep. The year he was sick and wrote what he clearly believed was a final letter and then, offended at his own body's presumption, recovered and wrote an embarrassed follow-up. The last one, short, written when his hands could barely hold the pen:

*I don't ask forgiveness. Forgiveness is a gift and I haven't paid for gifts. I ask only this: whatever you decide about me, decide it knowing I thought of you every day, and that every day I thought of you was the best part of the day, and that I knew it was my own fault it was all I had.*

I folded the letters along their old creases and held them against my chest for a while, the way you hold something that is both wound and medicine.

And I forgave him.

I want to be precise about this, because precision mattered to him, in his way. I didn't forgive him because he deserved it — I genuinely don't know if he did; that accounting belongs to him and whatever waits past the snowline. I forgave him because I was tired. Tired of hauling twenty-six years of anger up mountains, tired of feeding it at every campfire, tired of the way it had gotten so heavy I'd mistaken it for a spine. Anger had gotten me up the mountain. It would not get me down it. Love would — the ragged, late-arriving, imperfectly addressed love of a man who wrote letters he couldn't send to a daughter he couldn't face, and kept writing anyway, because stopping was the one thing worse than cowardice.

"Papa," I said softly, to the candle, to the snow, to whatever distance holds the dead. "You absolute fool. I forgive you anyway."

The candle flame bowed, whether in answer or in draft, I couldn't say.

* * *

An hour before dawn, Bram touched my shoulder.

I came awake instantly — the mountain teaches you that — and found the others already moving, silent as the snowfall itself. Marisol pried at the rotted east casement with a slat of wood; it came free with a groan she muffled against her sleeve. Isolde had rewrapped the letters' oilskin around a copy of the key document pages she'd memorized and redrawn from memory, because Isolde Vane did not trust confiscated originals to survive, and she was right not to. Cold poured through the opened window like water.

Beyond the casement, the yard lay white and unmarked. Two guards at the main door, backs to the wind, collars up, dreaming of warm stoves. The treeline a black seam at the edge of the world.

Bram looked at each of us in turn. No speech. He wasn't a speech-making man. He just held up three fingers, then two, then one —

— and that was when the shouting started.

It came from the far side of the depot: a soldier's voice, cracked with alarm, then another, then boots, many boots, and a bell that shouldn't have existed in a ruin ringing wildly. Bram's hand froze mid-count. Through the casement we watched lanterns bloom across the yard like fever spots, and men running not toward us but *away*, toward the north wall, toward the pass road.

Toward something coming up through the storm.

Isolde reached the window beside me, and I heard her breath catch.

"Lanterns on the switchbacks," she whispered. "Dozens of them. Coming up from below." A pause, and then, in a voice gone strange: "Those aren't army lanterns. Army lanterns are brass. Those are ship's lanterns."

Bram leaned in, squinting through the snow, and I watched understanding move across his face like weather.

"Dekker," he said. "Dekker's crew. Every man Verrick bought yesterday — they're not defecting to him." His teeth showed, white in the dark, in something that was not quite a smile. "They're mutinying. And they're coming for the gold."

The bell kept ringing. The candle between us finally drowned in the draft, and in the sudden dark, Bram's whisper was the only thing left:

"Change of plan. We go *now* — and pray the thieves reach the thieves before anyone counts what's missing."
