## 16. Betrayal at the Cache

I have been held hostage exactly once before this, and that time it was by my own mother, in the sense that she'd locked me into a finishing school and called it love. So when Verrick's soldiers marched us into the depot's central hall, bound our wrists with wire, and seated us on a crate of paymaster gold like we were guests at a very grim dinner party, I want you to know I was calm. Practically serene. I even complimented the seating arrangements.

"You're taking this well," Sela muttered beside me.

"I've had worse captors," I said. "At least these ones don't ask about my posture."

It was dusk, and the depot had gone the color of old bone. Snow ticked against the broken roof slates — early snow, wrong-season snow, the kind of weather that made Bram go quiet in a way I'd learned to dread. Through a shell-hole in the eastern wall I could see the pass we'd climbed, white now to the knee, and beyond it the world where my family's ships still sailed without me. Somewhere down-mountain, Dekker's defectors were warming their hands at Verrick's fire, having sold us for whatever Verrick paid men who sell things. I'd met their type at a hundred galas. They always smiled too quickly.

Verrick himself had been almost tender about it. He'd untied Isolde's wrists personally, offered her water, told her that her father would have understood the situation immediately. She'd looked at him the way one looks at a stain on good linen. Then they'd wired her again anyway, because courtesy only stretches so far before it has to become logistics.

So there we sat: me, Bram, Sela, Isolde, in a row on a fortune, guarded by four soldiers who kept glancing at the gold crates the way hungry men glance at bread. The documents — the massacre reports, the pay ledgers, the photographs of prisoners who never came home — were stacked under canvas across the hall, near the door Verrick's officers used. He'd separated them deliberately. Gold here, truth there. A man who thinks in those terms has already decided which one he believes in.

"Marisol." Bram's voice was low, barely breath. "The wire. Your hands are smallest. Can you work it?"

"I was raised to escape things," I whispered back. "Boarding schools, engagements, family portraits. Wire is nothing."

"It's not the wire I'm worried about. It's what happens after."

Which was fair, because what happens after is always the expensive part.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being rich: you learn to read rooms the way other people read books. You learn which uncle is calculating, which aunt is desperate, which cousin is about to do something stupid for money. It's a survival skill, and it's the only thing my upbringing ever gave me that I'd have chosen to keep.

So when one of Dekker's defectors came through the side door an hour after full dark — a narrow man with a reef-scarred face whose name I later learned was Pell — I read him in about four seconds. He wasn't looking at us. He was looking at the gold. And then he was looking at the door, and then at the gold again, doing arithmetic with his eyes.

"He's going to do something stupid," I breathed.

"They're all going to do something stupid," Sela said. "It's what they do."

"No, listen." My heart had started up a quick, greedy rhythm. "He defected tonight. Verrick promised him a share, but Verrick promises everyone shares, and shares get smaller every day a man stays useful. Pell knows how this ends. He's seen Dekker's crew turn over already. He wants out *now*, and the only way out is—" I nodded, minutely, toward the crates. "—weight."

Bram went very still beside me. "You think he'll free us."

"I think he'll free whoever can carry the most."

There was a long silence in which all of us pretended not to be considering the same moral question. Then Isolde said, softly, "If he frees us, we take the documents and run. Not the gold."

"The gold buys us mules," I said. "Guides. A boat. Everything."

"The gold buys us nothing if Verrick's inquiry never happens," she said. "Paper burns, Marisol. But paper also convicts. Choose which cargo you want to be carrying when the mountain comes down."

She was right, of course. Isolde is always right; it's her most irritating quality, and I say that with enormous affection. But I want to be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now: in that moment, wired to a crate of sovereigns, watching a desperate man creep toward us through the dark, I did not feel noble. I felt the weight of the gold in my imagination. I felt how easy freedom would be with forty pounds of it in each hand. My whole life I'd been told money was the door out of every room, and part of me — the part raised on Quennel dinners where affection was denominated in allowances — believed it absolutely.

Pell reached us. His knife sawed through Bram's wire first, then mine, then Sela's, then Isolde's, all in the space of a minute, while the sentry by the east wall coughed and shifted and mercifully stayed bored.

"Gold," Pell whispered. "Two crates each. We're gone before the watch changes."

"We're gone with the documents," Bram said.

Pell's face did something complicated. "The documents are worth nothing on any coast I know."

"That's because you know cheap coasts," I said, and he almost smiled, and that was the moment everything caught fire.

Because the sentry coughed again — or turned, or heard the wire snap, history doesn't matter — and shouted, and then the depot was full of light and noise the way a struck match is full of fire. A lantern went up. A rifle went off. Somebody kicked over a crate and the paymaster gold went across the stone floor in a bright, ringing spill, hundreds of coins fanning out like a hand of cards dealt by God, and every man in that room stopped being a soldier and became a man kneeling in money.

That's the thing about treasure. You spend years imagining the moment you find it, and you imagine triumph, and what you actually get is this: grown men on their knees in the dark, clawing, shooting at shadows over coins that were minted to pay dead men's widows. I stood in the middle of it with my wrists bleeding and thought, *so this is what my family's empire looks like from underneath.*

"Documents!" Bram roared, and it was less an order than a prayer.

We ran for the canvas stack. Bullets followed us — wild ones, mostly, because half the garrison was diving for gold and the other half was shooting at the divers, discipline dissolving in real time. Pell took a crate and bolted for the side door alone, which was the smartest move available to anyone in that room, and I genuinely hope he made it, wherever he is. Sela put her shoulder through a soldier's chest and didn't stop moving. And Isolde —

Isolde got there first. Of course she did. She threw herself over the document stack just as a defector — one of Dekker's, the big quiet one they called Harrow — swung his rifle around and fired from ten feet away, not at her heart, thank God, but not away from her either. Just close enough. The bullet went through her shoulder and buried itself in the papers beneath her, and she made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: not a scream, more like the noise a book makes when its spine cracks.

* * *

People talk about moments of decision as though they arrive with trumpets. Mine arrived with gold literally rolling over my boots.

I was three steps from the spilled hoard. Three steps. Coins lay in drifts against the wall like snow that paid interest. With both hands I could come away with enough to disappear forever — no Quennel boardroom, no arranged husband named Aldous-something with a yacht, no father deciding my life quarterly. Freedom, weighed and minted. Every year of my childhood had trained me for exactly this grab.

And behind me, Isolde was on the floor with red spreading through her shirt, using her good arm to drag the documents under her body like a bird covering eggs, and the gunfire was getting closer, and nobody else was going to reach her in time.

I wish I could tell you I hesitated beautifully. I didn't. I don't even remember choosing. What I remember is my knees hitting stone beside her and my hands finding hers and thinking, with absurd clarity, *she reads maps, someone has to.* The gold was still singing at my back. I let it sing.

"Hold on to me," I said. "This is going to be undignified."

"Everything about you is dignified," she managed, which was delirious, because I was hauling a wounded cartographer across a battlefield by the armpits with my hair in my face.

Sela appeared out of the smoke and took Isolde's legs without being asked, and the three of us moved — a strange, staggering centipede — toward the shell-hole in the east wall while Bram covered us with a soldier's rifle he'd taken off a man who was too busy kneeling in money to miss it. Behind us the depot burned gold-light onto the snow, and men screamed at each other over coins, and Commander Aldous Verrick's beautiful, reasonable plan tore itself apart on the oldest flaw in any empire: the people inside it.

We came out into the cold and the dark and the falling snow, and the mountain took us the way it takes everything — silently, completely, erasing our tracks behind us as though we'd never been there at all. For once, I was grateful to be forgotten.

Isolde was shivering. That frightened me more than the blood. I pressed my coat against her shoulder and she looked up at me with fever-bright eyes and said, "You left it."

"What?"

"The gold. I watched you choose." Her mouth curved, small and crooked. "Your family would be appalled."

"My family," I said, tightening my grip on her, "is going to be so disappointed in me. I can't wait."

Behind us, above the wind, I could hear Verrick's voice rising over his own soldiers — sharp, cracking, no longer reasonable at all — and I knew, the way you know weather coming, that this night was nowhere near finished.
