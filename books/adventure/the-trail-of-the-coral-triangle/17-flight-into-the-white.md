## 17. Flight Into the White

The snow came early, and it came angry.

I'd spent enough winters in high country to know the difference between weather that meant to inconvenience you and weather that meant to keep you. This was the second kind. It came down out of the north in a grey wall, eating the ridgelines one by one, and by the time we'd loaded what mattered from the depot, the world above six thousand feet had been reduced to a rumor of white.

We left the gold.

I want that sentence on the record, because I've thought about it every day since, and because Marisol said it first, standing in the doorway of the paymaster's hut with a crate of sovereigns in her arms like an infant she couldn't decide whether to love or drown.

"We can't carry it," she said. "Not all of it. Not with Isolde down and the storm coming."

Isolde was propped against a wall with a field dressing on her shoulder where Dekker's defector's bullet had torn through, white as the snow starting to fall outside, but conscious, and — this is the thing about Isolde Vane — already arguing geography. "The pass east of the ridge," she said through her teeth. "Verrick will expect us south, toward his supply line. Nobody crosses east in November."

"Nobody crosses east in November because nobody comes back," Sela said.

"Which is precisely why Verrick won't follow," Isolde said. "He'll assume we're dead in a day. He'll want us dead in a day. Dead men don't file reports."

Bram logic, Marisol called it later. The cartographer's cold arithmetic. But I watched her say it with blood seeping through the dressing and her hand shaking, and I understood something then about why she'd drunk herself out of a career: people who see terrain that clearly can't stop seeing everything else that clearly either, including their own ruin.

We took the documents. Four oilskin-wrapped bundles of them — muster rolls, requisition ledgers, a court-martial transcript signed by men whose names had been struck from the navy's memory, photographs that no darkroom should ever have printed. And the letters from the village below, the ones the old woman had kept for forty years, written by conscripts who never came home. That was the cargo. That was worth more than the gold, though only four of us believed it at the time, and one of us was bleeding out her conviction through a hole in her shoulder.

Marisol set the crate down on the depot floor. She looked at it a long moment — her escape fund was ash already, burned buying mules and passage, so this gold was the last of anything resembling her fortune — and then she kicked the lid shut and walked away without ceremony.

"Sentimental," I said.

"Practical," she said. "Gold weighs forty pounds a case. Guilt weighs nothing until you try to climb with it."

* * *

We went up.

That's the part I still can't quite explain to anyone who wasn't there. Every instinct I own says downhill, toward water, toward warmth, toward the coast and a boat and distance. We went up instead, into the teeth of the storm, because Isolde's map said there was a col between the eastern peaks that dropped into a valley the surveys had never bothered to chart, and because going down meant walking into Verrick's arms.

Verrick had maybe thirty men. We had two mules, a wounded cartographer, a runaway heiress who'd turned out to be the toughest person aboard, a prospector's daughter who moved through high country like she'd been born on it — which, it turned out, she more or less had — and me.

And we had the storm.

Here's what thirty years of diving teaches you that no academy does: the wilderness forgives nothing, but it doesn't take sides either. The same current that drowns you hides you. The same reef that guts a hull shelters a fish. I'd spent my life reading water, and up here, for the first time, I read snow the same way. The storm wasn't our enemy. It was indifferent — and indifference, when you're being hunted by men with orders, is the closest thing to an ally God offers.

By midday the tracks behind us were gone. All of them. Ours too, which cut both ways, but Verrick's trackers would be working blind while we at least had Isolde's compass work and Sela's mountain sense. The white swallowed the depot, swallowed the mule trail, swallowed the whole sordid business of men shooting each other over crates of paper, and reduced the world to wind, slope, and the next ten feet.

Ten feet at a time. That's how you survive altitude and storm. You don't think about the summit or the valley or whether Isolde's shoulder wound is festering. You think about the next ten feet, and then the ten after that.

Sela broke trail. She wouldn't hear otherwise. "You dive," she said, when I argued. "Your body's built for pressure, not cold. I grew up at this elevation." Her father's map hung inside her coat now, wrapped in oilcloth next to mine, and I noticed she'd stopped talking about him with hatred somewhere around the rope bridge and started carrying the map like something alive.

Marisol brought up the rear with the mules, singing. Actually singing — some bawdy harbor song from her boarding-school days, pitched under the wind, and when I looked back at her she grinned through a face full of ice. "If I stop singing," she shouted, "drag me. That's the system."

"That's a terrible system," I shouted back.

"It's worked so far!"

It had, God help us. Somewhere between the rope bridge and the depot, the runaway heiress and I had stopped performing at each other. She'd chosen Isolde over a fortune in a burning room full of gunfire, and you don't argue with a person after that. You just make room for them.

* * *

The col nearly killed us at dusk.

Isolde found it — a saddle between two shoulders of rock that her father's survey marks had flagged decades ago, and I watched her stand in front of those marks, half-frozen, bleeding, and read her father's handwriting in the snow like a letter from the dead. She didn't say anything. She just touched the chiseled survey bolt once, the way you'd touch a grave marker, and then pointed east and gave us the bearing.

The climb to the saddle was two hundred yards of scree under fresh powder, and it took us an hour. The wind came across it sideways, hard enough to lean on, and Isolde went down twice. The second time she didn't get up.

Sela got there first. She hauled Isolde upright, got the woman's good arm over her shoulders, and simply carried her — this slight, sharp-tongued cartographer who outweighed nothing but her own regrets — up the last fifty yards, step by step, while Marisol and I pushed the mules and cursed the weather in two languages.

At the top, in the lee of a boulder, we made camp. No fire — heat signature, Verrick might have glass on us — just shelter cloaks and body heat and the last of the dried meat. Isolde shivered badly, which Sela said was better than not shivering, and Marisol held her and sang very quietly, the bawdy song slowed down into something almost like a lullaby.

"The documents are dry," Isolde said, from somewhere inside the blankets. It wasn't a question. It was her way of asking whether any of it had been worth it.

"Dry as a customs ledger," Marisol told her.

"Then we're richer than Verrick," Isolde said, and closed her eyes, and slept.

I sat up the first watch. Below us, far down through breaks in the cloud, I could see lights strung along the southern approach — lanterns, torches, the slow patient crawl of a column hunting for tracks that no longer existed. Verrick's men, beating the ground for us like a man searching a drained pond for fish that had already swum.

Let them search. The pond had closed over us. The wilderness that forgives nothing had decided, for once, to forgive us specifically — or rather, to forget us, which in the mountains amounts to the same mercy.

I woke Sela for second watch and lay down next to the mules, and before sleep took me I heard her say quietly, to nobody, to the storm: "Papa, look at us. Look what your map did."

The wind took it. The mountain kept it.

* * *

We crossed the col at dawn in a whiteout, roped together, Isolde navigating by dead reckoning and pure stubbornness, counting paces against the wind like a woman doing sums at gunpoint. Twice we lost the bearing and twice she found it again. When we finally came down off the saddle into the eastern valley — uncharted, silent, deep with powder — the storm broke apart overhead like a curtain tearing, and the sun came through, and none of us said anything for a long minute because there was nothing to say that the light wasn't already saying.

Below us, the valley ran green-black with pine, untouched, trackless, running down toward country no patrol would reach for weeks.

Behind us, the pass we'd crossed was already filling in. By noon it would be gone. By evening, Verrick could stand on that saddle with a hundred men and find nothing but smooth white silence.

"He'll know we went east eventually," Sela said.

"He'll suspect," Isolde corrected. She was grey-faced and feverish but her eyes were clear. "There's a difference. Suspicion takes proof, and proof takes time, and time—" she nodded down the valley "—is ours now."

Marisol slung her arm carefully around Isolde's good shoulder. "Look at you. Smug with a bullet hole. You're my favorite terrible influence."

"I'm everyone's favorite terrible influence," Isolde said. "It's the only career I have left."

We started down. And that was when I saw it — far off, at the valley's mouth, where the pines thinned toward the low country: smoke. Not one fire. A line of them. Cook fires, camp fires, laid out with military neatness along the river terrace.

A camp that shouldn't exist. In a valley no survey had ever charted, on a route Isolde swore was suicide.

Somebody was waiting for us. And they'd lit their fires bold as brass, like they wanted to be seen.

Sela raised her father's binoculars, focused, and went very still.

"Bram," she said. "They're flying no colors. But the man walking the perimeter — I've seen him before. At the rope bridge."

Dekker's man. Which meant Dekker hadn't gone to ground after the bridge, hadn't scattered, hadn't quit.

Which meant somebody had paid him to sit in the one valley we had left.
