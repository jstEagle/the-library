## 18. The Avalanche

There's a particular sound a mountain makes when it decides to kill you. It isn't dramatic — no thunderclap, no roar of warning. It's a whisper. A soft *whump* under your boots, like the mountain clearing its throat before it speaks.

I'd heard that whisper twice in my life before this morning. The first time was on a glacier in the Sunda Arc, and I lived because I ran fast and a man named Okonkwo didn't. The second time was three days ago on the pass above the depot, when Isolde's weight went through a snow bridge and Marisol screamed and we dug her out blue-lipped but breathing. Both times, the mountain had been talking to someone else.

This morning, it was talking to me.

* * *

We'd been running for two days. Two days uphill, into weather that came down off the summit like something with intent, carrying documents instead of gold because Bram Montrose, salvage diver, professional extractor of other people's valuables, had somehow become a courier for dead men's testimony. Verrick's soldiers were below us and behind us, moving faster than they had any right to, and Dekker's defected crew moved with them like jackals who'd finally found a pack worth joining.

Sela led. She'd grown up in these highlands before her father dragged her to the coast, and she read the terrain the way Isolde read charts — fluently, contemptuously, spotting every lie the snow told. Marisol carried Isolde, or half-carried her, taking turns with me at supporting the cartographer's feverish weight while she clutched the oilskin packet of documents against her chest like a second heartbeat. Isolde had taken a bullet for those papers. She wasn't going to let altitude sickness finish what a defector's rifle started.

"She's getting worse," Marisol said, somewhere past noon. Snow to our knees, wind cutting sideways, the sky the color of old dishwater. "She stopped complaining an hour ago."

"That bad?"

"Isolde complains about everything. Weather, mules, my breeding, your grammar." Marisol adjusted her grip. "When she goes quiet, she's spending everything she has just staying upright."

I looked back down the trail. Far below — maybe a mile, maybe less in that flat white light — movement. Dark figures in a white world, fanning out along our backtrail with the patience of men who knew exactly where we were going, because there was only one way down off this side of the massif before spring, and they were standing on it.

"Cornice," Sela said. She'd stopped ahead, one arm out, pointing along the ridgeline to our left. An overhang of wind-packed snow curled out over the drop like a wave frozen mid-break. Beyond it, the slope fell away eight hundred feet into cloud. "The trail cuts under it. No way around. We go under it, they follow us under it."

"And if it comes down—"

"It comes down on whoever's underneath. That's the arrangement cornices make with the world." She turned and looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before. Not fear. Arithmetic. "Bram. How many are behind us?"

I counted the dark shapes again. "Twelve. Maybe fourteen. Plus Dekker's lot."

"How many rounds do we have?"

"Between all of us? Enough to annoy them."

The arithmetic finished itself without my help. We couldn't outrun them — not carrying Isolde, not in this snow. We couldn't outfight them. And by nightfall they'd have us cornered against the pass walls, and Verrick would walk up with his white gloves and his reasonable voice and offer us the same bargain he'd offered at the depot, except this time there'd be no depot full of witnesses to complicate the math.

"There's another arrangement," Sela said quietly. "One nobody likes."

I looked at the cornice. Then at the slope beneath it. Then at the place where the trail narrowed between rock wall and drop-off — a choke point, forty yards long, directly in the kill zone.

"You want me to bring it down on top of them," I said.

"I want you to think about whether you can live with it," she said. "Because I can tell you already want to. I can see it. And wanting it is different from being able to carry it."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about vengeance: it feels exactly like survival from the inside. Same heat in the chest. Same narrowing of the world down to a single bright line. For two days I'd been telling myself everything I did was necessity — running, hiding, burning Marisol's escape fund on mules we then lost, dragging a sick woman up a mountain. Necessity, necessity, necessity. But standing under that cornice, watching Verrick's soldiers pick their way up our backtrail, I felt something that wasn't necessity at all. It was appetite.

Verrick had murdered prisoners and sunk a ship full of evidence and put a bullet's worth of lead into the air around my people. He'd stood in the depot ruins offering me wealth for silence as casually as a man offering a light for a cigarette. He'd survive the inquiry — I'd known it even as we fled — unless something buried him first.

And here was a mountain offering to do exactly that.

"Bram." Marisol crouched beside me behind the rock spur, breath steaming. Below, the first soldiers had reached the mouth of the choke. "Whatever you're thinking — think it fast. They'll be at the narrows in ten minutes."

"The cornice comes down, it doesn't check uniforms," I said. "If any of them are close to the edge when it goes—"

"They're chasing us through a blizzard to arrest us or worse," Marisol said. "You've been fair-minded your whole life, apparently. Take five minutes off."

It should have been easy. It wasn't. That surprised me more than anything else that morning. I've stripped wrecks that still had men trapped inside them — heard the tapping stop, day three, day four — and I slept fine afterward, because the sea does what the sea does and I was only collecting what it left. But this was different. This was me choosing. Me walking out onto a wave of frozen snow with a climbing axe and deciding which way gravity would fall.

Isolde stirred against the rocks where we'd propped her. Her eyes found mine, fever-bright but lucid. "The documents," she rasped, "prove twelve men were shot after surrendering. Verrick signed the order. If he dies under snow, he dies a hero. Missing, presumed lost in service to his country. There will be a plaque." She coughed. "If he lives to face what we carry — he faces it as a butcher. Think about which ending serves the dead."

Leave it to Isolde Vane to turn even an avalanche into an argument about historiography.

But she was right, and I hated it. Bury him, and the truth went into the ground with him, wrapped in a flag. Leave him alive, and every step down that mountain was a step toward exposure — toward the harbor, the broadcast, the inquiry Marisol kept swearing her family's network could force. Survival wasn't killing Verrick. Survival was outliving him loudly.

So why did my hands want the axe?

* * *

Because sometimes the plan chooses you. That's the honest answer.

We hadn't intended to stand and fight at all. The plan was to cross under the cornice fast, single file, hugging the rock wall, and push for the saddle beyond before the soldiers closed the gap. Sela went first with Isolde between us, Marisol rear guard. Forty yards of bad ground. Thirty seconds each.

We were twenty yards through when the mountain cleared its throat again.

Not for us. For them. The lead soldiers had reached the narrows — six of them bunched together, roped, moving carelessly because they thought the hunt was nearly over — and their combined weight on the loaded slope beneath the overhang made the whole face shudder. I heard it through my boots. Saw the hairline cracks spider out across the cornice lip like frost forming on glass, fast, too fast.

And I saw Verrick.

He was ten yards behind his lead element, white parka, unhurried, the way he'd walked through the depot ruins surveying our futures. He'd stopped at the mouth of the narrows to let his men bunch up — a commander keeping his own skin out of the crowded places. Even now, even here, he took no risks he could delegate.

The cornice groaned. The cracks ran. Everything happened in about four seconds, and I spent all four of them making the worst decision of my life, or the best one, depending on which ledger you keep.

I could have run. Twenty more yards and we were clear, and the mountain would have done its work or not, unassisted, and whatever it buried would be between Verrick and his God. Instead I turned, drove my axe into the cornice lip where the cracks converged, and hit it once, hard, with everything the last three weeks had put into my shoulders.

The mountain answered.

It didn't roar. That's the thing they never get right in the stories. It *sighed* — a vast, almost gentle exhalation — and then the world tilted and the entire face of the slope came unstuck from itself and went downhill at the speed of judgment. Six men vanished. The crack of it hit me a half-second after the sight, a thunderclap with the thunder arriving late, and the shockwave of displaced air slammed up the slope and knocked Isolde from between us and took my feet out from under me and slid me — sliding, sliding, arms windmilling, axe gone, the whole white world pouring past me downhill —

— and stopped me at the edge of nothing.

My fingers had found a fist of rock protruding through the snow. Barely. My boots hung over eight hundred feet of fresh-run avalanche debris and cloud, and my shoulders were coming out of their sockets in slow motion, and below me the roar went on and on, the mountain emptying its throat into the valley, burying six soldiers and most of the trail and, very nearly, the difference between justice and murder.

Then hands closed on my wrists.

Sela. Flat on her belly at the cornice edge, snow to her chin, hauling with a strength that had nothing to do with her size and everything to do with whatever furnace burned in that woman. Marisol had Isolde. Sela had me.

"Don't," she grunted, as I tried to help, "you absolute — donkey — hold — STILL—"

She pulled. I came up over the lip like a landed fish, and we lay there side by side in the snow, gasping, while below us the avalanche settled into silence and the clouds closed over the wreckage like water closing over a wreck.

"You went back," she said finally. "For the cornice. You could have kept running."

"I know."

"Why?"

I didn't have an answer that would survive daylight. Because Verrick deserved it. Because six against four was arithmetic. Because for one bright moment I'd wanted the mountain to be my weapon instead of my witness, and I'd swung the axe before the better part of me finished voting.

"Because I'm not as good a man as I sell myself as," I said.

Sela considered that. "No," she agreed. "But you're close enough to hate the gap. That counts for something. My father never hated the gap at all."

* * *

We found Verrick an hour later, and I'll say this for the devil: he was hard to kill.

He'd been far enough back that the avalanche's tongue missed him — caught instead in the churn of its tail, tumbled, broken, and spat out onto a shelf of rock above the debris field. One arm bent wrong. Blood frozen black down the side of his face. His white parka, that immaculate banner of authority, was torn and gray with snow-melt, and he lay against the rocks looking smaller than any man I'd ever seen look small.

Two of his soldiers had dug themselves out with him. They had rifles. They didn't raise them. Maybe it was the sight of their commander. Maybe it was the arithmetic — four of us, two of them, and neither of them eager to die for a man whose order had just fed six of their comrades to a mountainside.

Marisol covered them anyway, rifle steady, while Sela and I picked our way down.

Verrick watched me come. Even broken, even bleeding, the eyes were the same — patient, weighing, always three bargains ahead. When I crouched beside him, he smiled with cracked lips.

"Montrose." A wet laugh. "Did you do this? Or did the mountain?"

"What difference does it make?"

"All the difference in the world." He coughed, pink-flecked. "If the mountain did it, you're a survivor. If you did it—" his gaze drifted, deliberate, to the oilskin packet under Isolde's arm, "—then you're a man who kills, and everything you carry is just a murderer's excuse. You've thought about this. I can see you've thought about this. So let me spare you the philosophy: you hesitated. I watched you hesitate. A killer doesn't hesitate. You're not one of mine, Montrose. You never were."

He said it kindly. That was the horror of Aldous Verrick — he delivered even his verdicts like gifts.

"We're taking you down with us," I said. "You'll stand an inquiry. You'll answer for the depot, the wreck, the men your unit shot after they surrendered. All of it, in front of the whole coast."

"Inquiry." He tasted the word. "By whom? My navy? My admiralty?" But there was something new under the contempt — a hairline crack of his own, running through the certainty the way the cracks had run through the cornice. He'd heard the mountain sigh. He knew now that the world could come unstuck from itself without warning. "You'll fail, Montrose. Men like me don't fall to documents."

"Men like you fall to mountains," Sela said, kneeling to splint his arm with a ski pole and a length of rope, rougher than strictly necessary. "And to daughters. And to paper. Ask my father how the first two work."

Verrick looked at her, and for the first time since the depot, the weighing eyes had nothing to weigh. He'd buried six of his own men under a hundred tons of snow, and the woman binding his broken arm was the daughter of a prospector he'd had shot, and none of his rank or reason or white gloves meant a thing up here where the only law was gravity.

We left his two soldiers with him, disarmed, with half our food and directions to the village that remembered — the one place on this mountain where Verrick would be safe and judged in equal measure. The elders would feed him. The elders would also talk. By the time he reached the coast, limping and half a command lighter, the story of the depot would already be waiting for him, traveling faster than any soldier could march.

As we climbed away toward the saddle, Marisol fell in beside me. "You went back for the cornice," she said. "Sela told me."

"Did she also tell you I'm a donkey?"

"She mentioned it." Marisol glanced back down the slope, where the debris field lay smooth and terrible under fresh snowfall, already erasing itself. "For what it's worth — I'd have swung the axe. No hesitation. I want you to know somebody on this crew doesn't hesitate, so you can keep being the one who does."

"That's the strangest compliment anyone's ever paid me."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment." She bumped my shoulder with hers. "Come on. Isolde's asking for you. She says if she's carried down a mountain by committee, she wants to approve the grammar of everyone involved."

We made the saddle by dusk, four figures and a packet of papers, leaving the buried and the broken behind us. The snows were sealing the pass early — Sela said two days, maybe three, before the high route closed until spring. Which meant the descent, when it came, would have to happen fast, down thawing passes, with a wounded prisoner's testimony and a wounded friend's fever both racing the weather.

And somewhere below, in the white silence we'd left behind, Aldous Verrick began to crawl toward the village that remembered everything.

The mountain had spared him. The mountain, it turned out, was the only one on this peak with mercy to spare. What we did with him next was up to us — and I was beginning to understand that the hardest part of the truth wasn't carrying it up a mountain.

It was carrying it down.
