## 19. Descent

I have been carried before. Once, at seven, after I fell from the orchard wall and broke my wrist, my father hoisted me onto his shoulders and marched me home lecturing me about the load-bearing capacity of branches. It is a measure of how far my life has deteriorated since then that this time the carrying is done by Sela Ardan and Marisol Quennel, neither of whom lectures, both of whom swear continuously in two languages, and the terrain would make an orchard wall look like a billiard table.

Fever makes a cartographer of everyone, did you know that? The mind insists on drawing lines. All night — or what I took for night; the storm had opinions about day and night and none of them were official — I lay across Bram's shoulders or Sela's or whoever was fool enough to take a turn, and I mapped us. Contours of pain. Elevation lines of cold. A river of heat running through my left side where the bullet had gone through and through, which the field dressing called clean but my body called a lie.

"You're muttering coordinates," Sela told me once, her breath fogging against my ear.

"Am I right?"

"Close enough to worry me."

"Then I'm still useful," I said, and passed out again, which I recommend as a strategy for anyone who wishes to avoid the worst parts of a descent.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the mountain on the way down, assembled later from fragments the way one reconstructs a chart from water-damaged originals:

I remember Marisol burning something. Not money — she'd already burned the money, in the practical sense, trading her escape fund for mules and passage like some picaresque heroine, and if she regretted it she hid it behind a performance of aristocratic martyrdom so convincing I nearly applauded. No: she burned pages from her own journal, feeding them into a tin stove in a shepherd's hut while the wind tried to take the roof off, because the ink had frozen and the paper was the only dry thing left.

"I was writing about you all," she admitted. "It seemed wasteful."

"What did it say?"

"That you snore like a landslide."

"She's lying," Sela said. "You snore like two landslides having an argument."

I remember Bram standing watch at the hut's door with his back to us, and the particular set of his shoulders — I am a cartographer; I read topography everywhere, including men — that said he was listening for footsteps and dreading hearing them.

And I remember waking once, properly awake, in the grey hour, to find Sela sitting beside me with her father's letters open on her knees, though there was hardly light enough to read by. She wasn't reading. She was holding them the way you hold a thing you've decided not to throw into a gorge.

"He wrote to the village every year," she said, without looking up. "Did you know that? Every year. Asking if I'd come looking for him. Telling them where he'd be, in case I ever—"

Her voice did something I pretended not to hear, because we were not yet women who could afford to be gentle with each other, and pretending was the kindest currency I had.

"He was an idiot," she finished.

"Yes," I agreed. "They usually are. My father surveyed depots for massacres and signed his name beautifully in the corner. Idiocy has range."

She almost smiled. On the mountain, in that light, it counted as a festival.

* * *

The thaw came up to meet us as we descended, which sounds merciful and was not. Frozen ground is honest; thawing ground is a con artist. What had held our weight at dawn swallowed a mule to its hocks by noon, and the passes that Verrick's maps — Verrick's *father's* maps, though he doesn't know I know that, or didn't then — marked as safe became brown rivers with ambitions toward the sea.

We lost the second mule on the third day. It simply sat down in a slough and declined to participate further in our narrative, and Marisol cried over it, which told me more about how far she'd come from the Quennel drawing rooms than any confession could have.

By the fourth day I could walk, technically, in the sense that my legs performed their contractual obligations while the rest of me filed complaints. My shoulder had gone hot and then, worse, cold, and the fever had settled into a low dishonest simmer, the kind that lets you think you're fine right up until you're face-down in a snowmelt stream wondering who moved the horizon.

Bram found me there. He fished me out, and instead of the lecture I deserved he said only:

"How much farther to the tree line?"

I blinked water out of my eyes and did the arithmetic automatically, because I am incurable. "Two miles. Maybe three. Then forest cover, then the valley track, then—" I stopped.

"Then what?"

"Then the coast," I said. "Then the world. And the world has Verrick in it."

Bram looked down the valley for a long moment. Below us the mountains stepped away in green terraces, and somewhere down there, half his command buried under a cornice I'd helped chart him into, Commander Aldous Verrick was doing his own arithmetic.

"He'll come again," Bram said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen what I'd seen: a man who loses soldiers to an avalanche does not go home to write reports. He goes home to write excuses, and excuses require corpses that aren't his own.

"Then we'd best be worth catching," I said, and let him haul me upright.

* * *

The offer came at dusk, in the last village above the river gorge, and I will tell it as I experienced it: badly, from a cot, through a fever-haze, with the smell of woodsmoke and goat in my nose.

We'd taken shelter in a granary on stilts, the kind of structure highland villages build when they've learned — and these people had learned, God help them, they'd learned decades ago — that soldiers take whatever sits at ground level. Sela was boiling willow bark. Marisol was re-wrapping my shoulder with a gentleness that fooled no one, because her hands shook. Bram was outside, checking the track.

I heard the horses first. Two of them, no more. Then boots on the ladder, unhurried, and I knew the rhythm before I knew the reason: Verrick walked the way my father had walked into survey offices, with the confidence of a man who has never once been made to wait.

He came up into the granary alone. That was the performance, of course — alone, unarmed, hat in hand, courteous as a dinner guest. His uniform was filthy and his face was gaunt and half his command was dead on the mountain behind him, and he looked *better* for it, the way certain men look better ruined, because ruin finally matched the inside of them.

"Ladies." He inclined his head to Sela and Marisol, and to me on the cot. "Miss Vane. Or shall I say Miss Vane-*Verrick-survey-office*, given your recent interest in family history?"

"My family history is public record," I said. "Yours is about to be."

Something crossed his face — quick, professional, gone. "Your friend Montrose is below. I'd like a word. Alone."

"He's not yours to summon," Sela said, and put herself between Verrick and the ladder with a hatchet she had absolutely been holding all along, and I loved her a little for it.

"Of course not," Verrick said mildly. "But he'll come. Men like Montrose always come, when it's offered properly. It's why I prefer dealing with them."

And Bram came. Of course he came. I heard the ladder creak under his weight and felt the whole granary shift around the axis of what happened next.

I should confess: I listened. Feverish, half-blind, wrapped in someone else's blanket in a stolen granary, I lay very still and eavesdropped on the conversation that decided everything, and I have never once felt guilty about it. Cartographers are paid to know where things are. Where things *really* are.

Verrick kept his voice low and pleasant, the voice of a man discussing freight rates.

"Montrose. You're a salvage man. You understand value. So let's speak plainly, man to man, without the schoolmistress and the heiress and the daughter softening the arithmetic." A pause. "The documents are worthless. Paper burns. But gold is gold, and there's more of it in that depot than either of us can carry, and I am currently the only officer on this coast with the authority to make it disappear cleanly. Do you follow me?"

"I follow you," Bram said.

"Good. Here is the shape of it. You keep the gold — all of it, every bar, I'll even provide the transport, my launch, my crew, whatever's left of them. In exchange, the papers come to me, and the four of you never existed. No inquiry. No witnesses. No names in anybody's report." Another pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something almost tender, which was the most frightening register he owned. "Or — and I say this with genuine respect, because you've cost me thirty-one men and I don't offer this twice — you give me the others. The women. Tell me where they'd run, help me close the net, and walk away richer than any wreck ever made you. Either bargain ends with you wealthy, Montrose. Only one of them ends with you free."

Silence. Long enough that I heard the goats below, and the wind, and my own treacherous heart.

Then Bram laughed. Not loudly. Just a short, tired sound, like a knot coming loose.

"You want to know what I pulled off that wreck?" Bram said. "Brass fittings. Portholes. A ship's bell with the name filed off. Forty years of a navy pretending a ship never existed, and the salvage manifest says *scrap*. You know what I learned down there, Commander? Everything rots the same. Gold, ships, careers, men. It all goes back to the sea eventually. The only thing that doesn't rot is what people do when nobody's watching."

"Poetry," Verrick said. "From a diver."

"Arithmetic," Bram said. "You're offering me a cage with gilded bars and calling it freedom. I've been in cages, Commander. Cheap ones. I know the difference." And then, quieter, in the voice I'd heard him use exactly once before, over the prospector's body on the beach: "He made me promise to deliver a map. That's all. One map, one promise. And it turned into this — into you, and the depot, and thirty-one dead men on a mountain. If I trade those women to square it, then everything they died for was for nothing, and so was he, and so am I. So no. Keep your gold. Keep your launch. When we reach the harbor, the whole coast reads what's in those crates, and there is nothing you can offer, buy, bury, or drown that changes it."

I heard Verrick stand for a moment in the silence. When he spoke again, the courtesy had drained out of him and left something older and barer underneath, and I understood suddenly that we had never been speaking to a commander at all, only to a man guarding a hole in the ground with other people's lives.

"They hanged men for less than what's in those papers," he said.

"Yes," Bram said. "Some of them were prisoners. Some of them were just in the way. Your unit kept excellent records — that's rather the problem."

Boots on the ladder. Horses in the dark, going down-valley fast. And Bram climbing back up into the granary with his hat in his hands like a man returning from a funeral, finding four faces turned toward him in the lamplight.

"Well?" Marisol whispered.

"He offered me everything," Bram said. He looked at each of us in turn — at Sela with her hatchet, at Marisol with her shaking hands, at me, at the bundle of oilcloth-wrapped documents that had cost Isolde Vane a bullet and very nearly a life. "So I suppose we'd better live long enough to spend it on the truth."

Sela blew out the lamp.

In the dark, I heard Marisol exhale, and Sela laugh once, softly, and I thought: *this*. This ragged, freezing, hunted handful of damaged people was worth more than the paymaster's gold, and I had the fever to thank for being honest enough to know it.

* * *

We went down through the thawing passes by night and slept by day, moving like ghosts through country that remembered worse ghosts. The mountain gave us its mercy grudgingly — a fordable river here, a shepherd's track there, a village that fed us and asked nothing except that we finish what we'd started. The wilderness forgives nothing, Bram likes to say. But I've begun to suspect that's wrong. I think it forgives exactly one thing: people who tell the truth about it.

On the last morning, when the air turned salt and the first fishing boats showed on the grey line of the coast, Sela sat beside my mule and said, "Harbor's a day out. Verrick will have sent word ahead. Soldiers, lawyers, whatever he has left."

"He has less than he thinks," I said. "Men who follow a man into avalanches start doing arithmetic too."

"And us? What do we have?"

I looked back at our little procession — Bram in front, Marisol arguing with a mule, the documents strapped against my chest where the bullet had gone in, as if my body had been keeping the place warm for them all along.

"We have the truth," I said, "and a shipping empire's daughter who owes her family a very large favor. Between those two things, Commander Verrick is going to discover what it feels like to be cargo."

Below us, the harbor town woke along its waterfront — and at the quay, tied alongside a naval launch flying colors it had no right to fly, waited a ship I recognized from forty-year-old survey charts.

The *Meridian*. My father's old vessel.

Someone had gotten there first.
