## 21. What We Buried, What We Kept

The village gave us shovels before it gave us breakfast. I have decided this is the correct order of priorities.

I had imagined burying my father a hundred times over the years — usually in the abstract, usually with feeling, occasionally with a shovel of my own in the fantasy. What I had never imagined was doing it here, four thousand feet up in the highlands, in a graveyard the size of my childhood kitchen, surrounded by people who knew him better at the end of his life than I ever did in the middle of mine.

The old woman — Ama Wari, though she corrected me twice before I got the honorific right — met me at dawn by the long house. She was carrying the letters wrapped in oilcloth, the way she had carried them for thirty-odd years, and she set them in my hands like she was handing back something borrowed.

"He asked us to keep them," she said. "In case he didn't come down."

"And if he did come down?"

"Then we would have kept them anyway." She smiled with most of her teeth. "A man who writes letters that honest deserves to be remembered honestly. Even by himself."

I had read them all by candlelight during the long night at the depot, and again on the descent when fever made Isolde delirious and someone needed to stay awake, and once more last night in the guest room they'd given me, which smelled of smoke and drying chilies and which I already loved more than any room I'd ever paid for. The letters were not what I expected. I think I'd expected excuses. What I found instead was a man writing to a daughter he didn't know how to face, describing mountains to her because he couldn't describe love, telling her about weather and birds and the taste of glacier water because *I'm sorry* was a door he couldn't get through, so he kept leaving things outside it instead.

Thirty-one letters. He never sent one. He just climbed up here every year and left them with strangers, hoping, I suppose, that someday the mountain would do what he couldn't.

The mountain took its time. But it delivered.

* * *

We buried the letters rather than the man. His body had gone into the sea months ago, off the coast where I'd spent half my life refusing to think about him, and there was something fitting about that — the sea keeps what it takes, and the mountain keeps what's given to it, and between the two of them they had divided my father fairly.

Bram dug the grave himself. He didn't ask; he just picked up a shovel after breakfast and started, and when I went to take it from him he held on for a moment and said, "You carried him up the mountain. Let me put him down."

Which is the kind of thing Bram Montrose says when he means something larger, and then pretends he doesn't.

Isolde came leaning on Marisol's arm, her wound still angry under its bandages, her color somewhere between parchment and regret. She insisted on standing. "Your father drew his map on the back of one of my father's survey sheets," she told me. "The least I can do is stand at the burial of both their sins."

"They weren't sins," I said. "One of them was a map. The other was a mistake."

"You're being generous."

"I'm being finished," I said, and discovered that this was true, and that saying it out loud felt like setting down a pack I'd been carrying since I was nine years old.

Marisol wore black. Actual black, tailored, which she'd had made in the port town during the week we waited for Isolde to be fit enough to travel, and which she claimed was "the only useful thing my family's money ever bought, besides the mules, and the boat, and the lawyer, and—" but she stopped herself, laughing, because the list of useful things her money had bought now included a truth that had ended her family's favorite admiral's career, and there was no tidy way to say that out loud yet.

Ama Wari spoke the words of the village, old words about how nothing given to the mountain is lost, only lent at interest. Then each of us put a stone on the cairn. Bram put two. He didn't explain why, and nobody asked, but I saw him touch the second one with his thumb the way you'd touch a coin for luck, or a scar for memory.

Then it started to snow — light, dry, ceremonial, as if the mountain had been waiting for its cue — and Ama Wari declared that funerals followed by snow meant the dead man had been forgiven by the sky, and that we would all come inside and drink something hot immediately, because grief is cold work and the dead don't mind if the living are comfortable.

My father, I thought, would have liked her enormously.

That was the strange part. Standing there in the falling snow, I realized I wasn't grieving anymore. I was introducing him — this version of him, the letter-writing one, the one who noticed birds — to the people who had hauled me off a mountain and pulled me out of a river and stood between me and a naval officer's rifle. *This is my father,* I wanted to say. *He was shy. He wrote better than he spoke. He loved me from four thousand feet up because he couldn't manage it across a kitchen table.*

He was not a good man, exactly. He was an honest one, late, and honesty arriving late is still worth more than most things that arrive on time.

* * *

The inquiry, when it finally happened, was everything inquiries are: slow, airless, and obsessed with procedure over truth, until the moment the documents hit the table and procedure suddenly became very interested in truth indeed.

Marisol handled it, naturally. She'd spent her whole life watching her family bend institutions like wire, and it turned out all that observation had taught her exactly where the joints were. She routed copies of the depot evidence through three separate Quennel shipping offices, timed to land simultaneously, so no single portmaster could sit on them, and she included a cover letter written in the tone of a woman who has nothing left to lose and finds it liberating.

"The trick," she explained to us afterward, over terrible coffee in a harbor café, "is that everyone assumes a runaway heiress wants something. Money, reinstatement, revenge on Daddy. So when I ask for absolutely nothing except that the documents be read, it unnerves them. Bureaucracies can resist greed. They cannot resist a woman who genuinely does not care."

Verrick's fall was less dramatic than I'd expected and more complete than I'd dared hope. His own officers turned first — Bram had predicted this, saying that men who follow a commander up a mountain to bury evidence are the same men who will bury the commander the moment the evidence surfaces. Two of them testified within the week. The wreck they wouldn't name acquired a name, a date, and eighty-seven prisoners who had been marched aboard and never marched off. There were memorials. There were resignations. There was, inevitably, a commission, because no atrocity is officially acknowledged without first being studied to death by men in good chairs.

Dekker vanished. Truly vanished — no port record, no crew talking, nothing. Marisol's sources said he'd gone east with empty hands and a reputation for treachery that even the worst harbors charge extra for. Bram shrugged when I asked if it bothered him.

"A man like Dekker doesn't need catching," he said. "He needs a mirror. He'll meet one eventually. They always do." Then he went back to re-caulking a seam on his boat with the contentment of a man who has decided the universe's paperwork is no longer his department.

And the gold? People always ask about the gold. Eighty pounds of paymaster's coin, scattered across a depot floor during the firefight, most of it left behind in the snow, some of it walked away in Dekker's defectors' pockets, and every ounce of it — this is the part nobody believes — formally surrendered to the inquiry as evidence, where it was presumably melted down to pay for the commission studying why it existed.

"We could have taken it," Marisol admitted once, late, when the wine had loosened whatever usually kept her fluent in denial. "Some of it. Quietly. Nobody was counting crates in that chaos."

"Yes," Isolde said. "And then every word we said about Verrick would have been worth exactly what we'd stolen. That's how testimony works. It's currency too, and it devalues."

Marisol nodded slowly, and I watched her arrive at the understanding that her family had never once arrived at: that some ledgers balance only when you leave the money on the table.

She was disowned by return telegram. She framed the telegram. It hangs in the cabin of Bram's boat now, between a chart of the reef channels and a photograph of none of us, smiling, that we do not remember taking.

* * *

We scattered the way flotsam scatters — gently, and with the certainty of meeting again.

Isolde went first, against medical advice and everyone's objections, back to the port town where she'd drunk away her career, to un-drink it. She'd been offered a lectureship — the cartographic institute had developed a sudden passionate interest in historical survey corrections, now that historical survey corrections had toppled a naval commander — and she accepted it with the wary expression of a woman checking a gift horse for government markings.

"I spent ten years hiding from my father's name," she told me at the dock. "Turns out the name was never the problem. The problem was letting him define what a Vane could be." She squeezed my hand with her bandaged one, which hurt, which seemed appropriate. "You taught me that. Up there. You stopped being his daughter somewhere on the pass and started being whoever you decided to be, and I just — watched you do it, and thought, oh. That's available. That's a thing a person can do."

"It's a thing anyone can do," I said. "It's mostly just deciding, and then surviving the deciding."

"Well," she said, "you've always been better at the second part than I have. Come lecture sometime. Bring the letters. History should hear them."

Marisol stayed longest, ostensibly to manage the legal aftermath, actually because she had discovered she liked working ships — liked the logistics, the weather math, the small daily competence of it — and had never once in her gilded life been allowed to be merely competent at something. Her family's network, post-inquiry, was in scandalized disarray, and several captains had quietly approached *her*, the disowned one, because she was the only Quennel whose name now meant anything clean.

"My mother says I've thrown my life away," she reported cheerfully. "I've written back to say that's technically impossible, since I'm keeping all of it, just not the parts she ordered."

Bram refitted his boat the way other men pray: daily, quietly, and with more faith than he'd admit to. I helped sometimes, mostly by holding things and asking questions, which he claimed was the definition of a good crew. When I announced I was going inland — taking a survey contract, actually, mapping the highland trails with Ama Wari's people so the villages could control their own access and no navy ever again moved through those passes unrecorded — he looked at me for a long moment.

"That's good work," he said.

"It's my work," I said. "That's the point."

He nodded, and handed me a coil of rope to hold, and that was the entire farewell speech, and it was perfect, because Bram Montrose communicates primarily through the assignment of tasks, and being trusted with the rope *is* the speech.

* * *

On my last morning in the village, before descending to the coast and the contract and the rest of my unlabeled life, I climbed up to the cairn alone.

Snow had half-buried it. I cleared the top stone and sat beside it the way you'd sit beside a sleeping animal, carefully, not expecting anything.

"I brought them home," I said. "Not to me. To here. I think that's what you actually wanted — you just didn't know the address."

Wind moved through the terraces below. Somewhere a village dog was losing an argument with a goat. The peaks across the valley stood in that hard morning light that makes distance look achievable.

I am not my father's daughter anymore, in the sense I used to mean it — the sense where the word *daughter* was a debt I was paying on a loan I never signed. I am his daughter in the new sense: keeper of thirty-one letters, carrier of a truth down a mountain, person who chose the mountain back. The difference between those two definitions is the whole distance I traveled, and I would walk it again barefoot.

I put a fresh stone on the cairn. Just one, this time. One was enough.

Then I heard boots on the trail below — two sets, unhurried, familiar — and I turned, and there they were: Bram with a surveyor's case slung over his shoulder like it weighed nothing, and Marisol behind him wearing expedition gear that cost more than the boat, grinning like a woman who has recently learned that being disowned is a form of freedom you can't buy at any price.

"Ama Wari says the southern trails haven't been mapped since the war," Bram said, in the tone of a man reporting weather. "Said somebody ought to do it properly."

"Did she," I said.

"And the institute sent Isolde's regards," Marisol added, "along with a request for triangulation data, and also she may possibly, allegedly, have requested leave." She produced a telegram from her coat with theatrical ceremony. "Arrives Thursday. Port of call: here."

I looked at the cairn. I looked at the two of them standing in the snow like an answer to a question I hadn't finished asking.

"Well," I said, picking up my pack. "Someone ought to tell her the trails are steep."

They were already arguing about who would carry the theodolite when I caught up.
