## 22. The Trail Continues

The boat came out of the yard in March looking like a dog that had survived a fight with a lawnmower and won on points.

I'd named her the *Cormorant* twenty years ago because cormorants are ugly, stubborn birds that dive into black water and come up with something worth having, and I'd never once regretted the choice. The navy's patrol boats had nearly made a liar of her twice since autumn — once off the reef channels where Dekker had crippled us, once in open water when a white ensign ran up too close and too fast and I spent an hour sweating through my shirt while a very young lieutenant asked me questions about cargo manifests I was technically in violation of three separate regulations for not possessing.

But that was before the inquiry. Before Marisol's family network started broadcasting depot documents from every port office between here and the capital like a woman scattering seed grain. Before Commander Aldous Verrick stood in front of a tribunal with his collar unbuttoned and his voice still calm and reasonable, explaining to men who no longer had to pretend to believe him that yes, he had ordered prisoners shot at a depot that officially did not exist, and no, he saw nothing wrong with it, and would they care to discuss the weather instead?

They took his command. They took his pension. Whether they'd ever take him anywhere worse than that was above my pay grade, but I'd learned something on the mountain about what justice looks like when it arrives late: it doesn't gleam. It just shows up, tired, and does the work.

So the *Cormorant* got her new planking, her recaulked seams, a secondhand engine that coughed like a consumptive uncle but turned over honest, and a fresh coat of paint the color of storm clouds, which felt appropriate. And I got a bill from the shipwright that made me briefly nostalgic for the days when my problems were limited to leeches, frostbite, and armed men.

I was sanding the rail on a Tuesday morning — because grief and refits both go better with your hands busy — when I heard footsteps on the dock behind me. Three sets. One light and quick, one deliberate as a surveyor pacing a baseline, one with the particular drag of someone carrying something heavy and refusing to admit it.

I didn't turn around right away. Some things you want to see properly.

"You know," I said, running the sandpaper along a seam, "most people knock."

"It's a dock," said Isolde Vane. "It doesn't have a door."

"Then most people wait to be invited aboard."

"We've been standing here for two minutes," said Sela Ardan, "watching you pretend you haven't noticed us. We decided the invitation was implied."

Marisol Quennel dropped a crate onto my deck with a thump that rattled my tea. "Also we brought provisions. Well — I brought provisions. Sela brought charts. Isolde brought instruments and an air of scholarly disapproval that I'm told is load-bearing."

I set down the sandpaper and turned around, and there they were, exactly as advertised and still somehow wrong in all the ways memory gets people wrong.

Isolde looked thinner than the mountain had left her, but the fever was gone out of her eyes, replaced by something steadier. She wore a coat that fit her for once, and under her arm she carried a leather tube of drawings the way other people carry infants. There was a scar along her forearm where the bullet had gone through — the bullet she'd taken standing between Dekker's defector and a crate of documents, because Isolde Vane, who had spent thirty years insisting she owed nothing to anyone, had thrown herself bodily over paper. She caught me looking at it and didn't bother hiding it. That was new, too.

Sela had color in her face again. On the mountain she'd gone grey as the scree, hauling me back from the cornice edge with a rope and a curse, and for a while afterward I'd wondered if the highlands had taken something out of her she wouldn't get back. But she stood now with her weight even, her jaw set in the way I'd learned meant she was deciding whether to say something true, and slung over her shoulder was a canvas map case — long, heavy, newer than anything her father had ever owned.

And Marisol — Marisol was radiant in the specific way of a woman who has been formally cut out of one of the wealthiest shipping fortunes in the hemisphere and has discovered she doesn't miss it. Her family's lawyers had sent four letters. She'd framed the first one. The other three she used to light the stove in the boarding house where she'd taken a room, paying her own way for the first time in her life and complaining about it with visible joy.

"Well," I said. "This is a surprise."

"No, it isn't," said Isolde. "You wrote to me asking about triangulation data. You knew I'd come deliver it in person rather than trust the post."

"That was a professional inquiry."

"It was a fishing line," said Sela, "and we're the fish, and you're standing there looking pleased about it, so don't insult us by pretending otherwise."

I looked at the three of them standing on my dock in the morning light, salt wind pulling at their coats, and I thought about what the old woman in the highland village had told us the night before we carried the truth down the mountain. *The wilderness forgets less than people do,* she'd said. *But people can choose what to keep.* And then she'd pressed her father's letters into Sela's hands, letters kept forty years in a cedar box by strangers who remembered a prospector the daughter had stopped remembering, and Sela had cried for the first time since learning how he died — quietly, facing away, the way people cry when they've been taught that grief is a debt you settle alone.

She wasn't crying now. She was looking at me with the expression of a woman holding a winning hand and enjoying the fact that I hadn't seen the cards yet.

"Are you going to invite us aboard or do we stand here until the tide turns?"

"The tide's already turning," I said. "Come on, then. Mind the wet paint."

* * *

We drank tea on the deck among paint tins and sawdust, and they told me what the winter had done with them, each in their own key.

Isolde went first, because Isolde always went first when there was work to report. The cartographic institute had reinstated her — not with honors, she was quick to say, but with employment, which after the scandal that ended her first career amounted to the same thing. Her monograph on the navy's pre-war survey marks had been accepted by a journal that had rejected her twice under her father's shadow. "They cited my fieldwork," she said, and there was a wonder in her voice she couldn't quite sand flat. "Not Vane senior's methods. Mine. The ones I worked out on a mountainside with frostbite." She touched the scar on her arm absently, the way you touch a thing to confirm it happened. "My father surveyed that depot, Bram. He drew the maps that helped bury it. I spent my whole life running from that, and then I walked straight into it, and it turns out the only way out was through. So." She lifted her chin. "I'm through. And the institute wants the whole Coral Triangle resurveyed. Properly this time. With the corrections the old surveys were built to hide."

"They're paying you?" I asked.

"They're paying me an insulting amount," said Isolde, "which is how I know it's serious."

Marisol's account was less institutional. "I'm disowned," she announced, with the delight of a child reporting a particularly good mischief. "Formally. There was a clause in the will. My aunt read it aloud at dinner. 'Marisol Quennel, having demonstrated a persistent disregard for the interests of this house—'"

"Persistent disregard," Sela repeated, savoring it.

"'—is hereby removed from succession and from all commercial correspondence.' Four pages. Father had lawyers draft it in advance, I think. He's been waiting years for an excuse." She wrapped her hands around her tea tin, and for just a moment the performance flickered and something real showed underneath — not regret, but the raw place where regret would have lived if she'd let it. "He offered me one more chance, actually. A letter, private, not lawyers. Come home, marry the man, and all is forgiven. And I sat with it for three days, and then I burned it in the stove, and I swear to you the fire smelled like freedom." She grinned, and the grin was real again. "Anyway, I've taken a job. Bookkeeping, if you can believe it, for a chandlery. I'm spectacularly overqualified and they adore me."

"And you walked past your family's offices on the way here," said Sela, "just to look at it."

"I strolled past," Marisol corrected with dignity. "There's a difference."

Sela went last. She always went last; she'd learned it on the mountain, letting others spend their words first so hers could find their proper weight. She unlaced the canvas case across her knees and drew out a chart — new paper, new ink, but drawn in a hand I recognized, because I'd watched her practice it night after night by campfire, copying her father's lettering until her own hand forgot itself and became his.

"I finished it," she said. "His map. The real one, I mean — not the ciphered thing he carried, the one underneath. Isolde and I worked out the margins together, and then I redrew the whole route clean. Every pass he marked. Every village that sheltered him. The depot." Her thumb rested on a point in the highlands where we'd buried the dead who'd waited forty years for names. "The institute wants it for the archive. Under both our names. His and mine." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and clear. "I hated him for eleven years, Bram. For leaving. For choosing rocks over me. And then I read those letters and found out he wrote me one every year and never sent them — thought I was better off without him, the idiot, the absolute —" Her voice caught, held, steadied. "I took them to the village. Buried them where the old woman keeps her garden. He'd have wanted that, I think. To be remembered by people who actually did remember him." She rolled the chart closed with careful hands. "So now I'm a cartographer. Apparently. Isolde says I have promise, which coming from her means I'm merely terrible instead of hopeless."

"She has promise," Isolde confirmed gravely. "Decades of suffering ahead of her. It's wonderful."

I sat there with my tea going cold and looked at the three of them, and I understood that I was being asked a question none of them had said aloud.

Because here was the thing about me: I had built my whole life on the principle that you travel light. No crew, no promises, no names on any manifest but your own. Trust was ballast, and ballast sinks ships. I'd said as much to a dying prospector on a beach, and he'd died anyway with my word in his mouth, and that word had dragged me up a mountain and into the company of a disgraced mapmaker, a runaway heiress, and a grieving daughter, and somewhere between the leeches and the avalanche I had stopped traveling light without noticing the moment it happened.

The wilderness forgives nothing. I'd known that since I was fourteen. It takes your blood, your fingers, your friends, and gives back lessons. But it had given me these three, and the strange arithmetic of it was that everything the mountain took, it seemed to have been collecting toward this — a crew assembled not by hiring but by surviving, bound not by contract but by the particular knowledge of what each of them looked like at their worst and chose anyway.

"So," I said, because somebody had to break it. "The institute's survey. When does it sail?"

"June," said Isolde. "Institute vessel. Institute crew. Institute schedule, which means six weeks of committee meetings before anyone touches water." She examined her nails. "Naturally, I intend to conduct preliminary reconnaissance well in advance. Unofficially. From a privately chartered vessel with a captain who has a documented history of ignoring naval regulations and getting away with it."

"Naturally."

"There are reefs on the eastern approaches nobody has charted since before the war," Sela said. "Father marked three of them. He called them the Teeth."

"And I," said Marisol, "have recently become extremely good at bookkeeping, which means I have noticed that your refit budget has a hole in it shaped exactly like a fourth crew share, and I have taken the liberty of arranging financing." She patted the crate she'd brought aboard. "Provisions. Charts. A spare theodolite, because Isolde will drop hers overboard within the month — don't argue, Isolde, I've watched you eat soup."

"I have never once dropped an instrument overboard."

"The sextant. In the strait. November."

"That was enemy fire."

"It was a seagull, Isolde."

I laughed. It came up out of me rough, like a rusted anchor chain, the first real laugh since the snow, and all three of them looked pleased with themselves in the way of people who had planned this moment down to the seagull reference.

I looked at the *Cormorant* — half-painted, newly planked, engine coughing, ready for nothing and hungry for everything — and I looked at the chart case in Sela's lap, and I thought about the old woman's words again. What the wilderness forgets, what people keep. The wreck they wouldn't name was named now. The depot had its dead counted and its truth delivered. Verrick was a cautionary tale in three navies, and Dekker was a rumor with empty pockets somewhere south, and the fortune we'd scattered across those crates of gold was buying nothing but the inquiry's momentum, and none of us regretted a coin of it.

Which left the horizon. Which left the next unknown, because there is always a next unknown, and the wilderness forgives nothing but it also wastes nothing — not even the people it breaks and remakes.

"All right," I said. "Terms. Nobody touches my helm without asking. Nobody paints anything on my boat without approval. And when the Teeth take a plank off us in some uncharted reef channel, nobody says I didn't warn them."

"Accepted," said Isolde instantly.

"Agreed," said Sela.

"I accept everything except responsibility for the theodolite," said Marisol.

"Then welcome aboard," I said, and heard how it sounded — not like hiring, not like charity, not like a promise made to a dying man. Like a door that had been open the whole time, waiting for them to stop knocking and walk through.

We cast off on the morning tide in June, ahead of the institute, ahead of the committees, ahead of anyone who might tell us what we were allowed to find. The harbor fell away astern, gulls arguing in our wake, and Sela stood at the bow with her father's chart open against the wind, reading the water the way he'd taught her without either of them knowing it. Isolde had the sextant out and steady. Marisol had claimed the galley and was already rearranging it to standards of chaos she described as *system*.

I had the wheel, and the wind, and the strange lightness of a man who travels heavy now — with a crew, with a promise kept, with a family that had assembled itself out of wreckage and refused, against all evidence and expectation, to sink.

Ahead of us the eastern approaches opened blue and unmarked, and somewhere out there the Teeth were waiting, and beyond the Teeth whatever else the Triangle had spent forty years keeping quiet.

"Cormorants dive into black water," Sela said, not turning around, "and come up with something worth having."

"That's why I named her," I said.

"I know," she said. "Isolde told me. She tells everyone. It's insufferable."

"Steer us east, then," I said, "and let's go be insufferable."

The wind filled in from the southwest, fair and rising, and the *Cormorant* leaned into it like a dog that has finally been pointed at the thing it was born to chase, and we ran for the open sea with the whole uncharted world ahead of us — which is, when you think about it, the only direction worth sailing.
