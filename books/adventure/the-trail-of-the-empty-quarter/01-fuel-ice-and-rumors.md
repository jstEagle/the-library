## 1. Fuel, Ice, and Rumors

The engine coughed twice before it caught, and Felix Whitlock made a private note to hate it more thoroughly later.

"Third time this week," June said from the dock, not looking up from the fuel hose. She had grease on her forearms to the elbow and a wrench tucked into the back pocket of her coveralls like a sidearm. "She's flooding on the prime. I told you the carb needs a rebuild."

"The carb got a rebuild in March."

"March was a long time ago, Felix. March was practically another country." She wiped her hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration and jerked her chin toward the sky. "You want to argue, or you want to fly? Weather window's closing."

Felix looked north. She was right, which was the most irritating thing about June Okafor — she was almost always right, and she delivered it the way other people delivered the mail, without ceremony and without apology. The ceiling over the interior was doing the thing it did in late spring, stacking itself into gray shelves that would, by afternoon, decide whether to be merely unpleasant or genuinely dangerous. The medevac call had come in at 5:40 a.m. from the trading post at Kettle Narrows: a trapper's son, sixteen, appendix, fever climbing. The nearest hospital was four hundred miles south, and the nearest surgeon was Felix's fuel bill away from being a rumor.

"Load her up," he said.

Cole Brannick was already doing it. The loadmaster had the constitution of a dock crane and roughly the same conversational range, but he moved a stretcher like it was made of eggshell and prayer, and in eleven years he had never once dropped anything that mattered. He cinched the medical pod into the Otter's belly, checked the tie-downs twice, and gave Felix the nod that meant *we are a functioning aircraft and not, at present, a collection of spare parts in an aviation-shaped bag.*

The last one aboard was Sami Rask, carrying the flight bag and a look of concentrated worry that Felix had learned to find endearing. The kid was twenty-three, fresh out of navigation school in the south, and had taken this job because — as he'd confessed on his first night, over beans — he'd wanted to fly somewhere the maps still had blank spaces. Felix hadn't had the heart to tell him that the blank spaces were blank for reasons, and that some of those reasons had names.

"Fuel state?" Felix asked.

"Full tanks, plus the drums in the pods." Sami patted the clipboard like a talisman. "Weight and balance is, um. Within limits. Barely."

"Barely within limits is how everything in this outfit works," Cole said, hauling himself into the door. "Barely within limits is our brand."

Felix took the left seat and ran the start checklist by touch, the way you do ten thousand hours in. Behind him the float base was waking up: the office light, the fuel shack, the sagging sign that said WHITLOCK AIR in paint that had been red once and was now the color of old blood. The operation consisted of one de Havilland Otter on floats, one hangar that leaned a degree east of plumb, one mechanic who was worth three of any southern outfit, and debts arranged in a delicate lattice that would collapse if anyone breathed on it. The medevac contract with the territorial health authority paid the hangar rent. The freight runs paid the fuel. The charter work paid the interest on the loan, and the loan paid for the privilege of owing money.

It was, Felix thought, taxiing out past the breakwater, a living. Barely. Barely was their brand.

The Narrows opened ahead of them, gray water stitched with the first real chop of the morning wind, and he put the nose north and let the country take them.

* * *

Four hundred miles of interior unspooled beneath the wings like a scroll nobody had finished painting.

Felix flew it from memory more than from instruments. Lakes in chains, rivers braiding and rebraiding, muskeg the color of old pewter, and then the country starting to climb — black spruce giving way to the first white shoulders of the high ground, the ridgelines still holding their snow in the folds like linen in a drawer. He'd flown this route so many times he could tell you which lake had the wrecked Canso on its shore, which river bent had a moose that hated aircraft personally, which burn scar from the fire six years back had healed into a silver forest of standing dead.

Sami navigated anyway, diligently, plotting their line on the chart with a straightedge and announcing checkpoints in the careful voice of a man showing his homework. Felix let him. The kid needed the practice, and Felix needed the sound of a young voice in the cockpit; it kept his own thoughts from settling into the old ruts.

Because there were ruts. There were always ruts. There was the bend of river at the two-hour mark where he always, always looked down, without meaning to, at a valley that wasn't on his charts anymore — not erased, nothing so dramatic, just never drawn in the first place, a gap in the contour lines where a gap in the world had been. Ten years ago he'd promised himself he'd stop looking. He still looked. He supposed you could take the man out of the country but the country kept a lease on the man.

"You're quiet," Cole said, from behind his shoulder.

"I'm flying."

"You're quiet *and* flying. Usually it's just flying."

"Enjoy it while it lasts."

June was in the back with the patient, keeping the boy comfortable and the IV running, and if the aircraft's interior smelled of avgas, antiseptic, and Cole's coffee, that was the smell of home as much as anything. The kid's father had ridden along as far as the Narrows dock and stood there on the pilings with his hat in his hands until they were a speck. Felix had seen that stance before. You spent enough years doing this work and you learned that the country gave and took with the same open hand, and all anybody could do was stand on the dock with their hat in their hands.

They handed the boy over to the ambulance at the coastal strip at 11:20, took on fuel and bad coffee, and turned south with the weather still holding — barely — and Felix felt the particular tiredness that came after a good flight done for bad reasons, which was most of them.

* * *

The letter was waiting when they got back.

It was sitting on the office desk, propped against the coffee tin where incoming mail always went, and Felix almost didn't register it — a plain envelope, postmarked from the south, addressed in a firm, slanted hand. He was peeling off his jacket, already composing the evening's list of grievances for June regarding the carburetor, when his eye caught the signature line of the enclosed page.

He stopped moving.

The hangar was loud — June had the compressor running, Sami was hosing silt off the floats — and none of that sound reached him. He stood in the doorway with the letter in his hand and the paper seemed to have weight beyond its weight, the way certain objects do, the way a stone from a riverbed you haven't visited in a decade is still just a stone until you remember which river.

The signature was a single word, in handwriting he'd last seen on an expedition manifest ten years ago: *Okonkwo.*

Dara Okonkwo.

He read the letter twice. It was short. That was like her; she'd never wasted words, not in the field, not in an argument, not in the three conversations of their lives that had mattered. It said that she was arriving on Thursday's coastal steamer. It said that she had money, a map, and a proposition. It said — and this was the line he read a third time, standing in the doorway while the compressor snarled and the floats hissed — *I know you don't want to hear from me. I know what the last expedition cost you. But there's a plane down in the high country, and I think you're the only one who can find it, and I think we both know which high country I mean.*

She'd signed it with her full name, as if he might otherwise mistake her for some other Dara Okonkwo, as if there were a whole flock of them.

Felix set the letter down on the desk with great care, the way you set down something that might go off.

Ten years. Ten years since the expedition had gone north with five men and come back with four and a story that didn't hold water and a silence that had held it for him ever since, sealed shut like a pressure bulkhead. Ten years since Tom Reyes had walked into the mist off the shoulder of the high valley and not come back, and the search had found nothing, and the coroner had written *presumed lost, remains not recovered,* and Felix had signed the report with a hand that didn't feel like his own and had not spoken of it since, not to the coroner, not to the others, not to God.

He had built a life on top of that silence the way you build a float base on muskeg — carefully, with a lot of pilings, knowing the ground underneath was soft.

"Boss?" Sami was in the doorway, wet to the elbows, holding the hose. "You okay? You look like you saw a ghost."

"Mail," Felix said. "Just mail."

Sami looked at the envelope, looked at Felix's face, and had the good sense — which Felix was beginning to suspect was his defining feature — to say nothing and go back to the floats.

Felix sat down at the desk. The office smelled of coffee, dope and fabric cement, and the particular dust of old paperwork. On the wall above the filing cabinet hung the framed certificate of airworthiness and, beside it, a photograph he'd never taken down because taking it down would have been an admission: five men in front of a Beaver on a gravel bar, squinting into a sun that was ten years gone. Tom Reyes stood at the end, arms crossed, grinning like the country was a joke he was in on.

Felix looked at the photograph for a long time. Then he looked at the letter.

The past, he thought, had knocked politely. He'd heard the knock before — in dreams, in the bend of a river, in the two-hour mark of every flight north — and he'd never answered, and it had always, politely, gone away.

This time it had left its name and address.

Outside, June's voice rose over the compressor, swearing at the carburetor with real feeling, and the ordinary machinery of his fragile, barely-affordable life ground on around him, and Felix Whitlock folded the letter once, precisely, and put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, which was either sentiment or strategy and he honestly didn't know which.

Thursday, he thought. The steamer came in Thursday.

He had three days to decide whether to open the door — or to start reinforcing it.
