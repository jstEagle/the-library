## 2. The Woman Who Left

She came on the Thursday boat, which told Felix she'd planned it, because the Thursday boat was the only one that arrived before the fog burned off, and only people who had studied the base from the outside knew that.

He was in the hangar, elbow-deep in June's carburetor war, when Cole called from the dock in a voice pitched carefully casual. "Felix. You've got a visitor. Fancy one."

Felix wiped his hands on a rag that was already beyond saving and walked out into the gray morning light, and there she was.

Ten years had done to Dara Okonkwo what ten years do to everyone — put weather on her, put lines around her eyes — but they hadn't touched the way she stood, weight on both feet, chin level, like the ground was a document she was prepared to contest. She wore a field coat too good for this coast and carried a hard-shell case in one hand and a leather folio in the other, and she looked at Felix the way she'd looked at him across a dozen briefing tables a decade ago: like he was a problem she'd already solved and was merely waiting for him to admit it.

"Whitlock," she said.

"Okonkwo." He stopped at the edge of the dock, letting the gap between them be physical. "You got my letter, then."

"I got your letter." She held up the envelope — his own careful handwriting, the one line he'd allowed himself: *Whatever you're selling, we're not buying.* "It was very dignified. I've had it framed in my memory next to the last three things you've said to me, all of which were also no."

"Consistency's a virtue in aviation."

"It's a virtue in mules, too." She stepped onto the dock boards, and the whole structure — Cole's pride, June's constant complaint — took her weight without complaint, and Felix hated that it felt like an omen. "I'm not here to buy anything, Felix. I'm here to hire you. There's a difference. Buyers take. Employers pay."

"Employers also get told no."

"They do." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had won arguments in tents and ministries and once, memorably, a customs shed in a country that no longer existed under that name. "But they get told no by people who've heard the whole offer. You haven't heard it. You've heard the shape of it through the door, which is not the same thing, and you know it isn't, because you're still standing here instead of walking back to your engine."

June's head appeared around the cowling. Sami hovered at the hangar door, pretending to inventory tie-downs. Cole had found a reason to coil a rope that had been coiled since spring. The whole base had gone quiet in the particular way of people who wanted to listen and were willing to work at it.

"Hangar," Felix said. "Five minutes. Coffee's terrible."

"I've had worse in eleven countries."

"That's not the brag you think it is."

* * *

The fluorescent tube over the workbench buzzed like something trapped, and under it Dara unrolled the satellite map across the scarred plywood, weighting the corners with a torque wrench, a socket set, and Felix's own cold cup of coffee, and Felix understood that she had chosen this room deliberately. Not the office. Not neutral ground. His hangar, his territory, so that everything she did next would happen in front of his people, under his light, where he couldn't pretend it wasn't real.

"Meridian Air Survey, flight 447," she said. "Down eleven weeks ago in the northern interior. Three aboard. Insurance paid, families compensated, and the recovery contract lapsed when the season turned. Nobody's going back for the airframe before the monsoon, because nobody can." She tapped the map, well south of where her finger's meaning actually lived. "Except you. You fly that country in your sleep."

"The country I fly in my sleep is not that country."

"The country you fly in your sleep," Dara said, "is the approach to that country, and you know the passes, and you know the fuel caches, and you are the only operator on this coast with a plane rated for the strip and a crew that trusts you enough to follow you somewhere stupid." She slid the folio across the bench. "Triple your best season's gross. Half up front. Done before the rains, or we walk away and you keep the advance."

Felix didn't touch the folio. "And the route?"

"Through the last lake. North of it."

The fluorescent tube buzzed. Somewhere outside, the compressor cycled down and the silence came in behind it like water.

"Out of the question."

"Felix—"

"That route is closed. It's been closed for ten years. I don't fly it, I don't chart it, I don't fuel people who ask about it, and I have bought that policy with money I didn't have, which you'd know if you'd ever once asked how the base was doing instead of how I was."

"I'm asking now." Her voice didn't rise. That was the thing people had never understood about Dara Okonkwo — she didn't raise her voice, she lowered the temperature, until the words came out sharp and bright as frost. "Meridian lost a plane. A company lost an asset. But I lost—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There were four of you on that expedition. Three came back. I've spent ten years not knowing which of the three I'm angriest at, and I've decided it's all of you, equally, so at least we're consistent."

"We were sworn—"

"I know what you were sworn." For the first time something cracked in the frost, just a hairline. "I read the report, Felix. All of it. The official one, the one that says Tom Reyes walked out of camp on the fourth of September and was lost to exposure, and I read between its lines too, which took longer, because between its lines there's a hole the size of a man." She put her hand flat on the map, over the blank space north of the last lake where the satellite imagery went soft with cloud, as if the country itself had hired a lawyer. "He walked into the mist. Your report says mist. In September. In that latitude. There is no mist in September there. There's fog, there's rain, there's cloud — but you wrote *mist*, which is what a man writes when he's describing a memory and not weather."

Felix looked at the map and saw, laid over the satellite false-color, a different map entirely: the one from ten years ago, grease pencil and hand-annotated, Tom's blocky capitals naming features they'd named drunk on glacier melt and relief. He saw the last camp. He saw the white wall coming down the valley on a morning that had started clear.

He saw Tom standing at the tree line with his pack on, saying something Felix had spent a decade not finishing the sentence of.

"Five minutes are up," Felix said.

"Felix."

"Get off my dock, Dara."

* * *

She didn't get off his dock.

Dara stood at the rail while the fog came back in off the water for the evening, and she let it, because fog was honest, fog didn't pretend to be anything but what it was, and she had spent the last ten years surrounded by people who were neither.

She had rehearsed the hangar scene on the boat north — rehearsed it in the shower of her hotel, on the tarmac at two connection airports, into the dark of her first-class seat while the man across the aisle pretended to sleep. In every version, Felix Whitlock shouted at her. In one version he threw the folio into the water, which she had privately hoped for, because a man who threw a contract into the sea was a man still burning, and burning men could be steered.

He hadn't shouted. He had gone gray and quiet and precise, and then he had said *get off my dock* in the voice of a man closing a door on a room where something was still alive, and that was so much worse.

Because it meant he knew something. And it meant the thing he knew had a shape, and the shape was a man walking into mist that didn't exist.

Dara had loved Tom Reyes for eleven months, twenty years ago, in a season that fit inside a single field season and had never afterward fit inside anything else. She had loved him the way you love a country you can't return to — specifically, inconveniently, with maps. When the expedition's sponsors had cut funding and the team had scattered, Tom had gone north with Whitlock's crew and she had gone south with the sponsors' money, and she had told herself for a decade that the arithmetic was clean: everyone chose their side, everyone got what they chose.

Then, fourteen months ago, a package with no return address. Inside: a topographic tracing in a hand she'd have known anywhere, of a valley that appeared on no chart she could buy, and on the back, in the same hand, four words: *I was wrong. Come.*

No signature. No date. The postmark was a coastal town she'd flown through twice and forgotten. She had spent a year and more money than she'd admit verifying that the tracing was Tom's — paper age, ink, the idiosyncratic way he drew contour lines like the country was breathing — and then she had spent another year failing to find anyone who could reach the valley and was willing to try.

Which left Felix. Felix, who had come back. Felix, who had built a wall of no out of exactly the materials she needed.

She heard footsteps on the dock boards — not Whitlock's stride, heavier, and she turned to find the mechanic, June, hands still black to the wrist, looking at her with the frank appraisal of a woman who maintained things for a living and recognized another machine under strain.

"He's not going to say yes tonight," June said.

"No."

"He might not say yes at all."

"He might not," Dara agreed. "But he'll say it in the morning, not tonight, because tonight he's going to take that folio out of the bench drawer where he put it — third drawer, under the torque wrench he used as a paperweight — and he's going to look at the map, and he's going to see the valley, and he isn't going to sleep. And a man who doesn't sleep on it has already gone."

June looked at her for a long moment. "You've known him before."

"I've known him before," Dara said. "That's the whole problem."

* * *

Felix took the folio out of the third drawer at eleven o'clock, which was exactly as predictable as she'd said, and he hated her a little for that, for being ten years gone and still reading him at sight.

He spread the map under the hangar's single work lamp and told himself he was reviewing it for errors, for exaggerations, for the tell — because Dara always left a tell, some flourish of certainty that would let him refuse her cleanly. He found the flight plan competent. He found the fuel math conservative, which was unlike her. He found the stated objective — Meridian 447, airframe recovery, remains if any — properly documented, properly insured, properly dead.

And then he found, folded into the folio's back pocket, a photograph.

Not satellite imagery. A photograph, old, creased into quarters and worn soft at the folds the way photographs get when they're carried a long time in a pocket over the heart. Four people in front of a tent, squinting into sun that was gone now from the world: Cole, young and grinning; himself, thinner, already looking at the horizon out of habit; Tom Reyes in the center with his arm around—

Dara. Dara, twenty years ago, laughing at something outside the frame, and Tom looking not at the camera but at her, with an expression Felix had spent ten years pretending he hadn't seen, because he'd seen it and said nothing and the saying-nothing had become part of the silence, and the silence had become the oath, and the oath had become the route he didn't fly.

On the back, in Tom's blocky capitals: *THE FOUR OF US. PLUS ONE WHO WAITED.*

Felix sat under the work lamp for a long time. The fluorescent tube in the main hangar had been off for hours; this was the little lamp, the yellow one, the honest one. Outside, the fog pressed against the hangar doors like something reading over his shoulder.

Tom had walked into the mist. That was the report. That was the sentence they had all signed, and sworn, and paid for with a decade of not talking about it. But Felix had been the last one to see him go, and what Felix remembered — what he had never once said aloud, not to the investigators, not to the others, not to himself in any voice above a whisper — was that Tom had not looked like a man walking to his death.

He had looked like a man walking home.

Felix closed the folio. Triple the season's gross. Half up front. And a route north of the last lake that he had forbidden on his own charts for ten years, bought with money he didn't have, and here was a woman with enough money to buy the forbidding itself.

He thought about the letter in his breast pocket, the one that had arrived Tuesday with a signature ten years dead. He thought about June's carburetor and the fuel bill and Sami's navigator's license, which the kid had earned and which the base could not, in any honest audit, afford to let him use. He thought about the arithmetic of a fragile life, and then he thought about the photograph, and the arithmetic stopped working, because some columns don't balance, they just wait.

In the morning, he would call the crew together. He would put it to them the way he put everything to them, because that was the only currency he had ever had that Dara couldn't match.

He just had to decide what he'd put to them.

Felix turned off the lamp. In the dark, the fog breathed against the doors, patient as a creditor, and somewhere north of the last lake, in a valley that appeared on no chart, the mist that didn't exist in September waited for him to come and finish the sentence.
