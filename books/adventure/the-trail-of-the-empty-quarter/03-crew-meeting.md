## 3. Crew Meeting

June Kowalski had rebuilt carburetors in worse light than the hangar's dying fluorescents, but she'd never heard a silence as heavy as the one sitting on the crew room table that night. It had weight. It had edges. It sat there between the coffee pot and the stack of charts Felix had laid face-down, like a bird with a broken wing that nobody wanted to touch.

She'd known Felix Whitlock for nine years, ever since he'd hired her off a wrecking dock in Fort Nelson because she could keep a Beaver flying on prayer and baling wire. In nine years she had watched him land on lakes that were more ice than water, fly a medevac through a thunderstorm with a bleeding child aboard because there was nowhere else to put down, and once — memorably — talk a panicked hunter out of shooting himself at ten thousand feet by describing, in detail, the taste of his grandmother's bannock. Felix did not crack. Felix bent, the way good aluminum bends, and held.

Tonight, folding his hands on the table, he looked like aluminum that had finally found its stress limit.

"Triple pay," he said. "Plus hazard. Plus a completion bonus that would clear our fuel debt with Meridian twice over."

Cole whistled low, the way he did when numbers got big enough to be dangerous. "Triple. For what, exactly? Hauling a survey team's luggage?"

"For a recovery flight." Felix's voice was flat. "Downed aircraft, northern interior. We go in, we assess, we bring back what's worth bringing back. Client's Dara Okonkwo."

June saw Sami's head come up like a dog catching a scent. The kid was twenty-three, three months into the job, and still had the habit of saying what he thought before his brain could file it properly. "Okonkwo. The Okonkwo? From the — the mining surveys? My dad used to talk about her. She mapped half the—"

"She did." Felix cut him off, gentle but final, the way you'd put a hand on someone's shoulder at a funeral.

June poured coffee she didn't want and watched Felix's hands. That was the thing about a man who'd spent his life around machines: his hands told you everything his mouth wouldn't. Right now his hands were very still, which meant they were working very hard to be still. She'd seen that stillness exactly once before, the day a float had come apart on takeoff and they'd limped back to the base with the whole left side of the plane shuddering like a dying animal. Felix's hands had been still the entire flight.

"So what's the catch?" she said, because somebody had to.

Felix looked at her. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer, and then he did something she'd never seen him do. He reached over and turned the top chart face-up.

June had been flying this country for fifteen years. She knew its charts the way she knew the lines of her own palm. The one on the table showed the high country north of the last lake — the Kettle River headwaters, the granite ramparts, the long green throat of a valley that every map she'd ever owned marked the same way: with a river, a contour or two, and nothing else. No strips. No cabins. No trails. Just emptiness, the kind cartographers drew when they'd never bothered to go look.

Except this one had a line drawn on it. A route, inked in by hand, running up the valley and into the high country beyond. And across the whole of it, in Felix's block capitals, a single word:

**FORBIDDEN.**

The ink was old. Faded at the edges, browned the way ink browns after years of sun through a hangar window. June did the arithmetic without wanting to. Ten years, give or take. Ten years this chart had hung in Felix's office, and in ten years she had never once seen him look at it. He kept it in the bottom drawer, under the spare logbooks, and she'd assumed it was just old paperwork.

"Ten years ago," Felix said, "an expedition went up that valley. Four men and two guides. Three men came back. The guides didn't come back either — they stayed, by choice, or that's what we told ourselves. The three who came out signed a paper saying they wouldn't discuss what happened up there. I was one of them."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere out on the water, a loon called, that long descending laugh they made, like the lake itself was mocking them.

"Tom Reyes was the fourth man," Felix said.

June had heard the name maybe twice in nine years, both times by accident, both times followed by Felix going quiet for a day. She'd built a small shrine of assumptions around it: a dead friend, probably. A crash, probably. Something that made Felix the kind of pilot who triple-checked tie-downs and never flew a route he hadn't flown before.

"He walked into the mist," Felix said, "and he didn't come back. We searched nine days. Then the money ran out and the weather turned and the other two — we came home." He paused. His hands, still folded, had gone white at the knuckles. "And we signed the paper. And we told everyone he was lost. And that was the last true thing any of us said about it."

Cole shifted in his chair. Cole Braddock was loadmaster, ex-forest service, built like the cargo he used to sling, and he had exactly two speeds: working and thinking about working. "So this Okonkwo woman wants us to fly back up there. Ten years later. To find a downed plane."

"A survey plane. Lost last fall, she says."

"And the route she wants?" Cole nodded at the chart. "That one?"

Felix didn't answer, which was an answer.

* * *

Sami had gone still in the corner, and June knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a kid who'd grown up around pilots and had learned early that the dangerous ones weren't the loud ones. He turned the chart toward himself, careful not to smudge the old ink, and studied the hand-drawn route.

"Felix," he said slowly, "if this plane went down last fall, why does the client have a route into the high country? Nobody flies in there. Nobody *has* flown in there. There's no strip, no fuel, no reason. So either she's guessing—"

"She wasn't guessing," Felix said quietly.

"—or she already knows where she's going. Which means the downed plane is a story." Sami looked up, and his young face had gone sharp and old. "Felix. What is she actually paying us to find?"

The silence came back, heavier than before. June watched Felix across the table and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he was going to tell them the truth — or some piece of it — and that telling it was going to cost him something he'd been carrying for a decade.

"She thinks Tom's alive," Felix said.

Cole's coffee mug stopped halfway to his mouth.

"She thinks he walked out of that valley ten years ago and stayed. She thinks he's been living up there ever since. And she wants—" Felix's jaw worked. "She says she wants to bring him home. Or bring back proof. I don't know which, and I don't know that she knows which. But there's a second thing." He exhaled. "There's something in that valley. Something the expedition found. Tom was the one who found it, and Tom was the one who said it should stay buried, and the other two of us signed a paper agreeing we'd never speak of it. She's offering triple pay because she thinks I'll lead her to it whether Tom's alive or not."

"And will you?" June asked. It came out harder than she meant.

Felix looked at her, and for the first time all night he looked tired — not pilot-tired, not end-of-season tired, but the tired of a man who'd been holding a door shut for ten years and had just heard a knock.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the honest answer, June. I've spent ten years not knowing, because not knowing was the only way I could keep flying. And now she's shown up with money and a map and his—" His voice caught, actually caught, and June felt something in her chest turn over. "With a letter. In his handwriting. Ten years old, but his."

He pushed back from the table and stood, and for a moment June thought he was going to walk out, and she realized that the crack she was watching wasn't about the money or the valley or even Tom Reyes. It was about the fact that Felix Whitlock, who decided things — who always, always decided things — had just told his crew he didn't know.

"I'll be on the dock," he said. "Vote however you want. Nobody flies that route who doesn't choose it." The door banged shut behind him.

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The coffee pot ticked as it cooled. Out on the floats of the Beaver, water lapped in its small eternal rhythm.

Cole spoke first. "Triple pay," he said, as if continuing a conversation from an hour ago. "Plus hazard. Plus completion bonus."

"You're not serious." Sami turned on him. "Cole. Did you hear anything he just said? The route's forbidden. The client's lying about what the job is. There's a dead-or-alive man at the end of it and some secret that three grown men signed papers never to talk about. That's not a charter. That's a ghost story with a payroll."

"That's triple pay," Cole said placidly, "to a crew that's two fuel invoices from parking the Beaver for good. You know what Meridian's lawyer said on the phone Tuesday? He said the word 'repossession' four times. I counted."

June had counted too. She'd been the one who took the call, standing in the office with the receiver pressed to her ear while Meridian's man recited numbers that meant the end of everything — the base, the medevac contract with the nursing station up the lake, the whole fragile scaffolding they'd spent nine years building out of stubbornness and secondhand parts. The operation didn't run on money so much as on the *absence* of debt, and the absence had run out.

"We could take other work," Sami said. "Tourist season's starting. The fishing lodges—"

"The fishing lodges pay half rates and want us on call twenty-four hours," June said. "You know that. I've done the math every spring for nine years and every spring it comes out the same: we're one bad month from dead." She looked at the chart, at the inked line and the faded word. "That's not why I'm voting, though."

Sami looked at her. "Then why?"

She turned her mug slowly on the table. How to explain it — the thing she'd seen in Felix's face tonight, the thing she'd been circling since he'd walked out? Nine years of watching a man carry something, and tonight she'd finally seen the shape of it. It wasn't grief exactly. Grief was a wound, and wounds closed. This was more like a debt. Felix had come down off that mountain ten years ago without his partner, and he'd signed a paper, and he'd told the world a story, and he'd built a life on top of the story the way you build a float base on pilings — solid-looking, until the water drops.

"He's been paying interest on this for ten years," she said. "Whatever it is. Whatever happened up there, whatever he signed away, he's been paying it every single day, and he's never once asked any of us to help carry it. Tonight's the first time he's even shown us the bill." She looked up at Sami. "I'm not voting for the money. I'm voting because he asked. He's never asked for anything."

"He didn't ask," Sami said. "He said vote however you want."

"That's how Felix asks."

Cole grunted — his version of a laugh. "Kid, you'll learn. There's men who talk about loyalty and there's men who just fly the route. Whitlock's the second kind. Ten years he's kept us alive and paid and never once put his own ghosts in the flight plan. If the ghosts are in the flight plan now, it's because he can't keep 'em out anymore." He drained his coffee and set the mug down with a click. "I'm in. Somebody's got to carry the fuel drums."

Sami looked from one of them to the other, and June watched him do the arithmetic he was always doing — the kid navigated by numbers, by headings and drift angles and the cold clean logic of where you are versus where you said you'd be. She could see him trying to plot this the same way, and she could see the moment the numbers refused to resolve.

"Everything about this job smells wrong," he said finally. "The client's story doesn't hold. The route doesn't hold. The timeline doesn't hold. If this were a flight plan, I'd refuse to file it." He paused. His jaw set in a way that reminded her, suddenly and painfully, of Felix. "But it's not a flight plan. It's Felix. So — yeah. I'm in. Against my better judgment, which I want noted for the record."

"Noted," June said. "Cole, get him a coffee. He's going to need it."

* * *

They found Felix on the dock, sitting on the edge with his boots nearly touching the water, the way he did when he was thinking about flying and couldn't. The evening was coming down gold and rose across the lake, the Beaver riding her floats at the mooring, everything so peaceful it felt like a lie.

"Triple yes," Cole said.

Felix didn't turn around. June sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and looked out at the water. Below them, in the black-green depth, she could see the shadow of the dock pilings going down and down into nothing.

"You don't have to do this," she said quietly. "Whatever she's holding over you. Whatever that letter says. We'd fly for you without it."

"I know." His voice was rough. "That's what makes it hard." He was quiet a moment. "Ten years, June. Ten years I told myself he was dead, because dead was a thing I could survive and the other thing wasn't. And now there's a woman on my dock with money in her pocket and his handwriting in her bag, and I looked at that handwriting and I thought — what if I signed a paper that buried a living man? What if I flew home and built all this—" he gestured at the base, the hangar, the whole small kingdom of their lives, "—on top of him?"

"Then you go find out," June said.

"And if what I find is worse? If what we find up there is something that can't be brought home — something that has to stay buried, like the paper said?" He finally turned and looked at her, and in the last of the light his face looked ten years older than it had that morning. "Because that's the real question, June. Not whether we go. Whether we come back with the truth or leave it there. And I've been on both sides of that choice, and I'm telling you, neither one lets you sleep."

June didn't have an answer. She was a mechanic; she fixed what was broken and she scrapped what couldn't be fixed, and the one thing she knew for certain was that nothing stayed hidden forever — not in an engine, not in a man. Sooner or later the metal fatigued. Sooner or later the crack found the stress.

"Then we'll deal with it up there," she said. "One problem at a time. That's how you taught me."

Felix almost smiled. He stood, and the motion had some of his old certainty back in it, enough to pass. "First light day after tomorrow. Weather window's narrow and she wants to beat the monsoon front in. Cole, triple the fuel drums. Sami—" he paused, and something flickered across his face, some last flicker of the man who'd forbidden that route, "—file the flight plan as a survey recovery, direct to the last lake. We'll go beyond it on dead reckoning. I don't want that valley on any paper with my name on it until we're already in the air."

He walked back up the dock toward the hangar, and June watched him go, and she thought: *first domino.* She didn't know why the phrase came to her, or what it was a domino *toward*. She only knew that the whole evening had felt like the first tile tipping — slow, almost graceful, touching the next one with a click so quiet you could pretend you hadn't heard it.

Behind them, out on the darkening water, the loon called again. And from the guest cabin at the end of the dock row, where Dara Okonkwo had been given a room, a light came on — and through the window, June could see the silhouette of a woman standing very still, holding something up to the light.

A map, June thought. Or a letter. Or something else entirely.

The tile clicked against the next one, and the next one waited.
