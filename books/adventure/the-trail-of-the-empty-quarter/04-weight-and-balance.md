## 4. Weight and Balance

Sami Okonkwo—no relation, she'd checked twice, and the coincidence still itched like a label sewn into her collar—had learned navigation from a man who believed that every disaster in aviation was really an arithmetic failure that had been allowed to grow up. *You don't crash into a mountain,* Instructor Reyes had said at the academy, back when she'd thought the name was funny. *You crash into a bad sum.* So she did her sums early, did them twice, and trusted nothing that arrived without paperwork.

Which was why, on the third morning of preparations, she was sitting cross-legged on the hangar floor with Marek Voss's equipment manifest spread out in front of her like a confession he hadn't written yet.

The manifest itself wasn't unusual. Dara had provided it through Felix, who had passed it along with the expression of a man handing someone a dead rat wrapped in gift paper. Standard expedition loadout: sample kits, core drill bits, assay reagents, rock hammers, GPS units, satellite messenger, tents, cold-weather gear. Sami had weighed all of it in her head before she finished reading the first page.

It was page two where the arithmetic went wrong.

*Item 14: Steel cable, 6mm, 200m.*
*Item 15: Carabiners, locking, x24.*
*Item 16: Climbing rack — cams, nuts, slings.*
*Item 17: Bolt kit, expansion anchors, x40.*

She read it again. Then a third time, because that was what you did when the numbers refused to behave.

Geologists carried hammers. Geologists carried hand lenses and hydrochloric acid and little canvas bags that clinked. Sami's uncle had been a geologist for the northern survey for eleven years, and the most dangerous thing he'd ever owned was a kettle that shorted out. Geologists did not carry forty expansion bolts. Expansion bolts were for fixing routes up cliff faces — permanent anchors, drilled and glued into stone, the kind of thing you installed when you planned to come back down the same line over and over. Or when you planned to haul something heavy up something vertical.

Or when you planned to make sure nobody else could follow you up and take whatever you'd left there.

She set the list down on the concrete and looked across the hangar at Marek himself, who was crouched by the Otter's cargo door, checking the lashing points with the patient thoroughness of a man counting another man's money. He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, built like a door that had been left slightly open. He'd spoken perhaps forty words since arriving two days ago, and Sami had counted them, because counting was what she did instead of sleeping.

"Mr. Voss," she said.

He looked up. His eyes were pale blue and entirely unbothered, the eyes of a man who had been woken by worse things than a rookie navigator holding a clipboard.

"Your climbing rack," she said. "Where's the objective?"

He considered her for a moment. "Terrain," he said.

"It's karst limestone and glacial till above the tree line, according to the survey sheets. There are three cliffs on the whole route and none of them need forty bolts."

Something moved behind his face — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would be kept if a man kept one. "Ms. Okonkwo. In my experience, terrain is always steeper than the survey says."

"That's true," Sami agreed pleasantly. "That's why we carry rope. We don't carry a hardware store." She held up the manifest. "This adds ninety-one kilos to our center-of-gravity envelope. Ninety-one kilos of somebody else's weight means ninety-one kilos less fuel, which means a shorter leg or a higher-risk crossing, and I'd like to know which one I'm signing my name to."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and she had the distinct sensation of being measured for something — a coffin, a uniform, she couldn't say which. Then he stood, brushed off his knees, and took the manifest from her hand with a courtesy so smooth it felt rehearsed.

"I'll speak to Ms. Okonkwo about trimming the load," he said. "Thank you for your diligence."

He walked away toward the office, unhurried, and Sami sat on the cold floor and did the other sum, the one she'd been avoiding: ninety-one kilos of climbing gear, plus a man who photographed everything twice, plus a financier who paid triple rates without blinking, plus a flight plan filed toward a region Felix Whitlock had blacked out on his own charts for ten years.

The total came to a number she didn't like at all.

* * *

By afternoon the hangar had turned into the kind of organized chaos that June presided over like a small furious deity. The Otter's floor panels were up, the new tie-down track half-installed, and Cole was underneath the fuselage swearing at a corroded fitting in a voice that would have embarrassed a stevedore. Sami climbed onto the wing walkway with the fuel burn worksheets and found June elbow-deep in the avionics bay.

"Your geologist," Sami said.

"Mm." June didn't look up. A soldering iron hissed somewhere in the wiring loom.

"He's carrying a bolt kit."

"Mm."

"And a steel cable rated past anything a human being weighs."

June backed out of the bay, pushed her goggles up onto her forehead, and finally gave Sami the full force of her attention, which was a bit like being caught in a searchlight. June was sixty-one years old and had hands like cured leather, and she had once told Sami, during her first week, that airplanes didn't kill people — people killed people, usually with maintenance schedules. "You ran the weights?"

"Twice."

"And?"

"And if we take everything on that list, we're over max gross for the second leg by four hundred pounds, or we're landing at the upper lake with minimum fuel and no reserve, which in monsoon season isn't a plan, it's a suicide note with a tail number."

June grunted, which from her was applause. "So we don't take everything."

"Dara already said the science payload is non-negotiable."

"Dara," June said, "can negotiate with the mountain when we're sliding off it." She wiped her hands on a rag that had started life white sometime in the previous decade. "Listen, kid. You flagged it, you write it up. Put it in the loading sheet, put it in front of Felix, make him initial it. That's how this works. Paper first, arguments second. That way when it goes sideways, everybody knows whose signature was on the decision."

"And whose fault it was."

"No," June said, and for a moment her voice lost its gravel edge. "That way nobody has to guess. Guessing is what kills crews. Not mountains. Guessing." She nodded at the office window, where Felix stood talking to Dara, their silhouettes sharp against the fluorescent glare. He had his arms crossed, and even through the glass Sami could see the argument in the set of his shoulders — not angry, exactly. Braced. Like a man leaning into weather he'd been promised was coming.

"Felix looks bad," she said.

June followed her gaze. For a while neither of them spoke. Out on the water a floatplane from the fishery service droned past, and its shadow crossed the hangar floor like a slow bird.

"Felix always looks fine," June said at last. "That's the problem. Ten years I've watched that man absorb things that should've broken him — the debt, the winter we nearly lost the base, the year the medevac contract dried up and he flew supply runs for a logging outfit just to keep the lights on. He absorbs it all and files the flight plan anyway." She rolled the rag between her fingers. "This is the first time I've seen him look like the thing might not get absorbed."

"You think we shouldn't have voted yes."

June snorted. "I think we voted yes because he asked us to, and because the money keeps us flying until October, and because loyalty isn't a feeling, it's a decision you make with your eyes open." She pulled her goggles back down. "Eyes open, kid. That's the whole job."

* * *

The customs officer arrived at four o'clock, which was itself suspicious, because customs officers in the interior arrived when they felt like it and this one arrived like an appointment.

He introduced himself as Inspector Brandt, Transport and Customs Authority, and he was younger than his title, with a clipboard, a tablet, and the particular crispness of a man wearing a uniform that had never seen rain. Sami met him at the dock because Felix was on the radio with the weather service and everyone else was conveniently busy, and she understood within thirty seconds that this was not an accident of scheduling.

"Flight plan revision," Brandt said, tapping his tablet. "Filed yesterday, amended this morning. North corridor, then west of the divide." He looked up. "There's not much west of the divide, Ms.—"

"Okonkwo."

"Ms. Okonkwo. There's not much west of the divide except the high country, and the high country closes in three weeks when the monsoon sets in. Search-and-rescue won't fly it after that. Neither will we." He smiled the way a locked door smiles. "Recreational party?"

"Medevac charter and geological survey," Sami said. "Contracted. All the documentation's in the office."

"Oh, I've read the documentation." He scrolled. "Charter company licensed for coastal and lake operations. Insurance rider for high-altitude operations — added eleven days ago. Cargo manifest includes drilling equipment, assay supplies—" his finger paused, delicately, "—and forty expansion anchor bolts. Interesting choice for a survey."

"We carry contingency gear."

"For fixed-rope installation?" He glanced up. "That's mining infrastructure, Ms. Okonkwo. Anchor bolts are what you install when you intend to bring heavy equipment into terrain that doesn't have roads. It's what you file when you intend to come back with a lot more than six people and a floatplane." He let that sit in the air between them like a dropped tool. "So. Which is it? Survey, or staking a claim?"

Sami kept her face doing what faces were supposed to do, which was nothing. Inside, her instructor's voice was running the numbers again, and the numbers were screaming. *A customs officer doesn't memorize your cargo manifest. A customs officer doesn't know what anchor bolts mean. A customs officer asks these questions either because somebody paid him to ask them, or because somebody wants the answers on record.*

"Inspector," she said, "if you have concerns about the nature of our operation, I can arrange for Mr. Whitlock to sit down with you and go through the contract line by line."

"That won't be necessary." He signed something on the tablet with a stylus, briskly, a man closing a folder he intended to reopen later. "I'm required to note that your amended flight plan transits airspace adjacent to a restricted mineral reserve, and that any deviation will be logged." He handed her a printed receipt, warm from the machine. "Fly safe, Ms. Okonkwo. Weather's building in the north. They say it'll be an early monsoon this year."

He walked back down the dock without looking at the airplane, which somehow was worse than if he'd inspected it, and Sami stood there holding the receipt and watching the water slap against the floats, and she thought about the word *adjacent*, and about how a man who knew where the restricted reserve was also knew roughly where they were going, and about who he might mention that to.

* * *

She found Felix in the office at dusk, doing what he always did at dusk: standing at the chart table with a mug of coffee gone cold, looking at the north country the way other men looked at old photographs. The blackout rectangle was still there on his master chart — a patch of blank paper where the valley should have been, inked over in his own hand years ago, the edges gone brown.

Sami laid the customs receipt on the table next to the manifest with her annotations, and the loading sheet with the weight figures, and waited.

Felix read. He was fast, and he read the way pilots read instruments — completely, without moving his face. When he got to the bolt kit his eyes stopped, and she watched him arrive at the same sum she had, three days late but arriving nonetheless.

"Bolts," he said quietly.

"Ninety-one kilos of them, plus the rack, plus cable. And a customs officer who knows exactly what they're for and wanted me to know that he knows."

Felix set the papers down. For a long moment he looked at the blackout rectangle, and Sami saw his thumb move across it, once, like a man touching a scar through cloth.

"When Tom and I ran the north route," he said, and his voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man reading from a page inside himself, "we cached supplies at the head of the second lake. Hauled them up a rock chute with fixed lines. Took us two days." He looked up. "Somebody's planning to go back up that chute. Or planning for somebody to come up it after them."

"The retrieval team," Sami said. "If there is one."

"If there is one." He picked up the manifest again, folded it along her crease, and tapped it against his palm. "Here's what I know, Sami, and I want you to hold it, because you're the one on this crew who counts things. Dara Okonkwo is telling the truth about wanting to find something in that valley. I'd bet the airplane on it. But she is not the only person spending money on this trip, and the other spender has sent us a man who packs like an invasion and files paperwork like a lawyer." He straightened. "Trim the load. Cut the bolts, cut half the cable, cut anything that isn't food, fuel, medicine, or rope enough to get down what we climb up. If Dara objects, send her to me. If Marek objects—"

"What if Marek objects?"

Felix looked out the window at the darkening water, at the Otter riding her moorings with her new tie-down track gleaming faintly in the last light, loaded and ready and lying about nothing yet.

"Then we'll learn something important about how quiet he stays," Felix said.

Sami gathered her papers and got as far as the door before he spoke again, and when he did, his voice had changed — lower, aimed at the chart rather than at her.

"Sami. The officer today. Did he say who directed the inspection?"

"No. He said the flight plan was flagged."

"Flagged." Felix nodded slowly, as though ticking a box on a form only he could see. "Flight plans don't flag themselves."

She went out into the evening, and behind her she heard him uncapping a pen, and the small sound of a man beginning to draw a new line on an old map — and she couldn't shake the feeling that whatever shape he was drawing, he was drawing it around all of them.
