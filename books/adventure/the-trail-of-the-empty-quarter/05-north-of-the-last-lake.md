## 5. North of the Last Lake

The last lake disappeared under the port wing at 0740, and with it went the last thing on any chart that could be called a landmark.

Felix Whitlock had flown this country for fifteen years and had never once come north of it. He'd drawn his own boundary on his working maps — a red pencil line running east to west across the fold at the lake's southern shore, annotated in his own hand: *No reason.* Ten years of no reason. Now the Otter droned across the line with six souls aboard and enough fuel to make regret expensive.

"Passing the line," Sami said from the right seat, not looking up from her chart. She said it flatly, the way you'd call out an altitude, but he heard what was underneath it: *You're sure?*

"I'm sure," Felix said, though nobody had asked him aloud.

Behind them the cabin was full — June riding shotgun on the cargo net beside Cole, both of them wedged among fuel drums lashed for the return legs, crates of medical stock they'd use as cover story if anyone ever asked, and Dara Okonkwo sitting forward where she could see the country unroll beneath them like something being slowly unrolled *at* them. Marek sat opposite her, silent as luggage, his aluminum cases strapped down with more care than the fuel drums got.

The weather ahead was doing what northern weather does in the week before monsoon: building itself a wall. From two thousand feet Felix could see the front stacked over the high country — cloud standing on cloud, gray going to bruise-purple at the top, the whole mass of it anchored to the range like a ship tied to a dock. Beneath the leading edge the valleys were still clear, green-black forest stitched with rivers the color of gun steel. It would hold for a day. Maybe two. After that the whole north would close like a fist.

"Visibility's going," Sami said. "That haze ahead — smoke or mist?"

"Smoke." Felix tipped his chin toward a thin gray smudge far to the northwest. "Somebody burning off a cut block, or somebody's camp. Hard to say at this distance."

He felt Dara's attention shift onto him like a hand laid between his shoulder blades. He didn't turn around.

* * *

An hour past the lake the terrain stopped pretending to be friendly. The forest thickened into country that had never been logged because no road could reach it and no mill would pay to find out. Rivers braided and split and came back together in silver knots. The ridgelines climbed and sharpened, and between them the valleys deepened until flying down one felt like flying down a throat.

"Chart's getting creative," Sami muttered. She held her section up to the light. "This river here is supposed to run northeast. It runs northwest. This ridge is supposed to be eight thousand. That one out there has snow on it in July, so I'm guessing it's more."

"Charts up here are suggestions," Felix said. "Fly the ground, not the paper."

"Ground's fine. Paper's lying." She made a correction anyway, penciling the river's true course onto the margin in small, disapproving strokes. "Who surveyed this, anyway?"

"Somebody who did it from a desk," Felix said, and heard how much of the truth he was giving away by saying even that much. The original survey — the one Tom Reyes had flown and walked and died inside of, officially — had produced maps. Felix had seen them once, ten years ago, spread across a hangar table while men in clean shirts argued about mineral rights over a country none of them would ever smell. The maps had been beautiful and wrong in ways that only mattered to someone actually there.

Dara leaned forward between the seats. "Bear left here," she said. "There's a canyon coming up. The river cuts through a limestone band — the walls narrow to maybe three hundred meters. You'll want altitude before you're in it, because the downdrafts off the south wall will eat you."

Felix looked at her.

She met his eyes without flinching, but he saw the half-second delay first — the small hitch of a person catching herself after stepping off a ledge. "I read the expedition reports," she added.

"The reports don't describe the canyon," Felix said. "I've read them too. They describe rock samples and river gradients. Nobody wrote down that there's a canyon."

"Then I have a good memory."

"You have a memory of a place you claim you've never been."

"Felix." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "We're forty minutes from wherever we're going. You can spend them interrogating me, or you can spend them climbing to nine thousand feet before that canyon eats your airplane. Your choice. But I'd climb."

He climbed. Because she was right, and because the south wall was already visible ahead — a rampart of gray limestone rising sheer out of the treeline, its face streaked black with a century of runoff — and because arguing with her while hand-flying an overloaded Otter toward a slot canyon was a way to die tired.

The canyon was exactly where she said. Three hundred meters wide, maybe less, the river roaring white somewhere far below the wingtips, the walls closing in until the sky became a strip. Felix flew it with his jaw set and both hands light on the yoke, feeling the air go rough as the sun-heated rock shed its morning warmth upward, and behind him nobody spoke at all. Even Cole, who narrated everything, said nothing. When they popped out the north end into open valley again, Sami let out a breath she'd been holding long enough to make a point of.

"Good memory," Felix said to the mirror.

Dara didn't answer. In the reflection he saw her looking down at the valley floor with an expression he couldn't file anywhere — not triumph, not fear. Something closer to grief wearing a work shirt.

* * *

They stopped at a gravel bar at noon to stretch their legs and argue with the fuel math. June was out of the plane before the wheels stopped rolling, walking the bar with her head down, reading the stones.

"This bar's been used," she called back. She crouched, picked something up. "Look at this."

Felix crossed to her. In her palm lay a length of steel drum hoop, rusted orange, and beside it, half-buried, the corner of a wooden crate stamped with faded stenciling: *AVGAS 100/130* and a lot number.

"That's aviation gas," June said. "Drummed, hauled in, stored on a gravel bar in the middle of nowhere." She looked up at him. "Somebody's been flying up here for years. Or somebody did, once, a lot."

"Survey crews cache fuel," Cole offered, ambling over. "Standard practice."

"Survey crews cache fuel where their charts say they're operating." June turned the hoop over in her fingers. "There is no operation on any chart within two hundred miles of this rock."

Nobody had an answer for that, so nobody made one. Marek stood apart, photographing the crate corner with his small camera — one frame, unhurried, then another from a different angle, the way a man documents evidence rather than scenery. Felix watched him do it and filed it away next to the gear list that had included a signal analyzer and a hardened satellite terminal and no rock hammer worth the name.

They lifted again at 1240. The wall of weather had grown teeth while they were on the ground; the leading edge now reached down to the ridgelines in ragged curtains, and the wind at two thousand feet had developed a muscle to it, shoving the Otter sideways in long, patient gusts.

"Front's closer than forecast," Sami said. "Whatever window we thought we had, it's shrinking."

"Then we land where we're going and stop pretending we have options," Felix said.

"And where are we going?" Cole asked from the back. "Because I've been looking at my copy of the flight plan, and the flight plan says, quote, 'strip at grid four-seven-one dash nine-zero-eight,' end quote, and I have looked at every chart we own, including the ones Felix pretends don't exist, and there is nothing at four-seven-one dash nine-zero-eight except trees."

"There's a strip," Dara said.

Everyone looked at her. Even Marek raised his head.

"You've seen it," Felix said. Not a question anymore. He'd stopped pretending it was one somewhere over the canyon.

"I've seen a photograph of it," Dara said carefully. "Taken from the air. Ten years ago." A pause, and then, quieter, the closest thing to honesty she'd offered since the float base: "Tom took it. It was in his effects. The things they sent back."

The things they sent back. Felix kept his eyes on the horizon and his hands level and said nothing, because the box they'd sent back had contained a watch, a notebook with the last third torn out, and a boot. One boot. He had spent a decade not thinking about why there hadn't been two.

"Strip should be on the east bank," Dara went on, all business again, unfolding the satellite printout across her knees. "Gravel, river-corrected, roughly nine hundred meters. There'll be a marker — a pole, maybe. And the approach from the south is better than from the north; the ground rises past the north end."

"River-corrected," Sami repeated. "Meaning someone graded it against the flood cycle. Meaning someone maintains it."

"Meaning," Felix said, "that we're about to land on an airstrip that doesn't exist, built by people who aren't supposed to be there, in a valley that three governments think is empty." He glanced back at Cole. "Anybody want to renegotiate?"

Cole grinned, which was Cole's way of being afraid. "Triple pay," he said. "And I've always wanted to be a rumor."

* * *

They found the strip at 1415, and it found them first — a pale slash through the dark forest, catching the flat light like a scar.

Felix made one pass to look, and what he saw took the words out of his mouth before he'd chosen them. The strip was real. Not a gravel bar pressed into service, not a game trail that could fool a hopeful eye — a cut and graded runway, nine hundred meters if it was one, its surface raked smooth, its edges trimmed back from the treeline, drainage ditches running along both sides with the tidy geometry of a man who'd done it more than once. At the north end stood a wind sock on a pole, faded to bone-white, hanging limp in the gusts. Someone had painted numbers on the threshold. The paint was old, but it had been renewed at least twice; you could see the ghost of the older numerals beneath.

"Nine hundred meters, east bank, graded," Sami said softly, writing nothing down. "On no chart. Maintained. Painted." She looked at Felix. "Who paints a runway nobody knows about?"

"People who expect visitors," Felix said.

"Or people who get them anyway," June said from the back, "and want them to land safely whether they're welcome or not."

He brought the Otter around in a wide arc over the valley, using the pass to read the country, because the country was reading him. The valley ran north-south, five miles wide, walled in by ridges that went up steep and gray and ended in the cloud deck. The river looped through the floor in great slow bends, and along its terraces the forest broke into meadows — and the meadows were wrong. Too regular. Old clearing edges, grown over but legible, the way old fields stay legible for a hundred years. Somebody had cleared this valley once. Somebody had lived in it long enough to leave a pattern.

And there — at the limit of visibility, upstream, where the valley narrowed — a thread of smoke rose straight up into the still air under the cloud base. Thin. Domestic. The kind of smoke a cook fire makes when nobody's hurrying.

"Smoke," Sami said. "Two miles, bearing three-four-zero."

"I see it."

"Is that our destination?"

"Our destination is an empty airstrip," Felix said. "That smoke is somebody else's problem."

"Sure," Sami said, in the tone of a woman filing something away.

He set up for the approach from the south, the way Dara had said, and the valley received them the way a host receives guests whose names he already knows. The wind came down the axis of the strip quartering left, ten knots, gusty — the terrain breathing out. The trees leaned away from the field on both sides as if making room. Felix flared the Otter over numbers that some unknown hand had repainted for strangers, and the wheels touched gravel with a sound like applause, and rolled out through a silence so complete he could hear the tires shedding small stones.

He shut down. The props ticked as they cooled. Six people sat in the sudden stillness, listening to the valley listen to them.

It was June who moved first, popping the cargo door and dropping to the gravel, and it was June who said what all of them were thinking:

"Okay. So. Who do we suppose mowed the grass?"

Because the strip's margins weren't just trimmed. They were scythed — recently, neatly, the cut growth laid in windrows at the edge of the trees, curing in the wind. Somewhere within a day's walk, someone had stood in this meadow with a blade and done honest work, and gone home, and told whoever they told.

Felix stepped down onto the strip that didn't exist. Underfoot the gravel was firm, crowned for drainage, swept clean of the larger rocks. He turned a slow circle. Forest to every side, ridgelines drowning in cloud, the river talking to itself in the middle distance. And everywhere the sense — physical, almost tactile, like a hand resting on the back of his neck — of being observed. Not watched the way prey is watched. Watched the way a neighbor watches strangers unload a truck next door: patiently, thoroughly, with opinions forming.

Marek was already at the tail of the plane with his camera. Dara stood at the threshold numbers, looking north up the valley toward the smoke, and her face had gone very still, the way faces go when a person stands in a doorway they've imagined for ten years.

"Whitlock," Cole said quietly, coming up beside him. He nodded at the tree line, fifty meters off the east edge of the strip.

Felix followed his gaze. Pinned to a spruce trunk at eye height, weathered gray but unmistakable, hung a board. On it, carved deep enough to outlast the wood's own lifetime, was a single word in a steady hand:

**WELCOME**

And beneath it, smaller, newer, the knife-strokes still bright:

**GO BACK.**
