## 6. What the River Kept

The river had a voice like a man muttering in his sleep, and by the second morning of camping beside it, June Okonkwo—no relation to anyone, thank God for small mercies—had learned its vocabulary well enough to sleep through most of it.

She woke before dawn anyway. Twenty-two years turning wrenches on airframes that other people trusted with their lives had burned the habit into her: check everything twice, then check it again, because the one time you don't is the one time it matters. She crawled out of her tent into a world the color of wet slate, breath fogging, and went down to the water's edge where the Otter sat moored between two boulders, nose pointed upstream like a dog that wanted to keep moving.

She ran her hands along the floats first. Then the struts. Then she pulled the cowling checks she'd already done yesterday evening and did them again, because the plane was the only thing standing between six people and a very long walk home, and June loved that airplane more than she'd ever loved anything that couldn't love her back.

"You talk to it more than you talk to us."

Cole, coffee mug in hand, hair flattened on one side, grinning at her from under the tarp they'd rigged as a galley. He was wearing the look he wore when he'd slept well, which was rare enough up here that she let him have it.

"The plane listens," she said. "You just hear me."

"Harsh." He handed her a mug. The coffee was the instant kind, bitter as regret, and she drank it anyway because cold mornings made compromises of everyone.

Upstream, maybe three kilometers through the alder and gravel bars, Felix was already gone. He'd left before first light with Sami and the satellite map folded into his jacket pocket, scouting the stretch of river Dara's chart marked with a small, confident X—the crash site of the survey plane, the reason any of them were freezing their fingers off in a valley that appeared on no chart worth trusting. Felix had been quiet since they'd crossed the last lake. Not angry-quiet, not scared-quiet. Something else. Like a man walking back into a house he'd moved out of ten years ago and finding all the furniture exactly where he'd left it.

June didn't like it. She liked even less the way Dara Okonkwo stood at the edge of camp each morning looking upstream like she expected the river to hand something back.

* * *

By mid-morning the radio crackled. Felix's voice came through thin and clipped, the way it got when he was trying not to feel whatever he was feeling.

"June. Bring your kit. All of it. And the good flashlight."

"What've you got?"

A pause long enough that she checked the signal meter.

"Wreckage," he said. "Bring the kit."

She packed the tool roll, the spares box, the medical bag because she always brought the medical bag, and told Cole to mind the camp. Dara was already lacing her boots. Marek materialized from behind his tent with the camera hanging around his neck and a daypack on his shoulders, and June watched him adjust the strap twice, checking it, the way she checked things. Careful people bothered her. Careful people who pretended not to be careful bothered her more.

They went upstream single file along the gravel bar, the river loud on their left, the forest walling them in on the right so thick and green-black that sound died ten feet into it. Sami met them at a bend where a side channel braided out across the stones, and the kid's face was wrong. Sami was twenty-four and had the kind of face that broadcast everything—excitement, fear, hunger, all of it live on open frequency. Right now it was broadcasting something June hadn't seen there before.

"It's not what we thought," Sami said quietly, to Felix, but loud enough that all of them heard.

"No," Felix said. "It isn't."

He led them up the bank, through a stand of alder whose leaves were just starting to turn, and then the trees opened and the river kept its promise and there it was.

The wreck lay half-buried in a gravel fan where the side channel had built itself around the fuselage over years, maybe decades. A tail section jutted up like a broken wing bone, fabric stripped to bare frame, the registration number still legible in flaking paint. The rest of the aircraft was scattered downstream in pieces sorted by weight and current, engines apart from wings apart from cockpit, the way rivers sort everything eventually.

June walked toward it slowly, and every step the picture got worse and clearer at once.

"Felix," she said. "This isn't a survey plane."

"I know."

"This is a—" She crouched at the nearest piece of wreckage, wiped mud from a data plate with her thumb. "This is a twin-engine transport. Radial engines. This airframe went out of production before I was born." She looked up at him. "And it didn't crash."

Dara had stopped moving entirely. "What do you mean, it didn't crash?"

Felix didn't answer her. He was looking at the tail section, at the row of neat round holes punched through the aluminum just forward of the rudder hinge, edges bent outward, petals of bright metal blooming into the daylight.

"Those are exit holes," June said. Her voice came out flat and professional, which was how she handled things that would otherwise come out shaking. She moved along the fuselage, counting without meaning to. Entry holes smaller, exit holes larger, spaced in a line no impact ever makes. "Somebody shot this plane down. More than once, by the grouping. And these—" she touched a scorched patch near the wing root, blackened metal under ten years or thirty of lichen, "—that's fire damage. In-flight or on impact, hard to say. But nobody walks away from this by luck. Somebody walked away from this on purpose."

The river muttered. The alder leaves ticked against each other in the wind.

"That's impossible," Dara said. "The survey flight was five years ago. A Cessna 208, single engine, two men aboard. That's what the report said. That's what I paid—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Five years ago," Felix repeated. He said it softly, and June heard the thing underneath it, the thing he wasn't saying. He turned to her. "Can you date it? Properly?"

"Give me an hour and I can tell you the last time those engines turned over, give or take." She was already pulling gloves on. "Corrosion patterns, oil residue in the cylinders, condition of the fuel lines. Metal doesn't lie about time. It's the only honest thing left in this valley."

Marek had begun to photograph the wreck.

June noticed it the way she noticed everything mechanical—peripherally, automatically, filing it without deciding to. He worked methodically. Wide shots of the whole debris field. Close shots of the bullet holes. The registration number, framed dead center, sharp. The data plate she'd wiped clean—he photographed that too, from two angles, and then photographed the serial number stamped into the landing gear strut where the paint had peeled, and June thought: *a geologist*. She thought: *what kind of geologist frames a tail number like a man framing a certificate.*

Then she thought about the gear list she'd helped load in Whitehorse. The case that weighed too much for rock samples. The antenna that didn't match anything geological she'd ever seen. She'd told herself it was government survey equipment, because the contract said geologist and contracts were supposed to mean something.

"Marek," she said. "Hand me that wrench out of my kit, would you?"

He came over obligingly, and while he dug in the tool roll she watched his eyes track the wreck over her shoulder, cataloguing, measuring, doing arithmetic. When he handed her the wrench his fingers were steady. Steadier than hers.

* * *

An hour later she sat in the open cockpit section, which had torn free almost whole and come to rest fifty meters downstream, and told them what the metal had confessed.

"She's been here a long time. Ten years minimum, probably closer to twelve. Both engines show heat discoloration consistent with running to failure—she was flying when she went down, low, and fast enough that the props were still turning when the trees took her. Fuel lines were cut clean before the fire, which means somebody shut them deliberately, which means somebody was flying this bird with intent right up until the end." She pulled off her gloves. "And she was shot at from the ground or from below, multiple passes, before she hit. This wasn't weather. This wasn't pilot error. This was a killing."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, patient, indifferent.

"Twelve years," Dara said finally. Her voice had changed. All the boardroom polish had drained out of it, leaving something rawer. "The expedition I funded—the one Felix flew on—that was twelve years ago. Tom Reyes disappeared twelve years ago." She looked at Felix. "What is this plane doing here?"

"You tell me," Felix said. "You're the one with the map."

"My map marks a *survey* crash. Five years old. Two souls lost. I have the incident summary memorized, Felix, I read it forty times looking for—" She pressed her lips together. Started again. "Someone gave me that map. Someone told me that story. And either they lied to me, or—"

"Or someone buried a real wreck under a fake story," June said, "and hoped nobody up here would ever walk far enough upstream to check." She stood, brushed gravel off her knees. "Question is who, and why, and whether the folks who shot this plane down are done shooting."

"They're not done," Felix said.

Everyone looked at him. He was standing at the treeline, not looking at the wreck anymore but at the ground near it—at the mud at the edge of the gravel fan, where the alder shade kept things soft and wet year-round.

"Sami found these." He held up a brass casing, small, rifle-sized, green with age but not ruined. Then a second one. "Old. But the ground up here keeps things old. And look at this." He crouched, parted the alder stems, and showed them a scar on a tree trunk at shoulder height—a healed wound in the bark, old wood rolled smooth around it, but unmistakable. "Bullet strike. Same vintage, I'd guess. Somebody stood right here and fired at that plane while it came down the valley, and then somebody else stood here after and picked up their brass, mostly." He straightened. "This wasn't an ambush that failed. This was a machine gun position, and it worked."

June felt the hair rise on her forearms under her jacket. Twelve years ago. The same year Felix's expedition came home one man short and sworn to silence. She'd known Felix eight years now, flown with him through weather that grounded birds and saints both, and he had never—not once—spoken about that trip except to forbid the route on the charts with a grease pencil and a word she'd never heard him use otherwise.

Now she watched him stand in front of a murdered airplane in the exact valley he'd forbidden, and understood that the silence had never been grief alone.

Grief doesn't hide brass casings.

"Felix," she said carefully. "Who else knew about this valley?"

"Too many people," he said. "That's always been the problem."

* * *

They worked the site until the light went long and gold. It was June's idea, and Felix backed it, and Dara didn't argue: document everything, touch nothing, leave the wreck exactly as the river had arranged it. If someone had gone to the trouble of building a false story around this place, then disturbing it might tell whoever built that story that visitors had come. June had spent a lifetime making wrecks look flyable; she could spend an afternoon making a crime scene look untouched.

So they photographed nothing themselves. They measured nothing. Sami sketched the layout in a waterproof notebook instead, angles and distances, and buried the notebook in the bottom of his pack. June recovered one item only—a corroded nameplate from the cockpit door, small enough to close her fist around, stamped with a company logo she didn't recognize and a serial number she memorized and then photographed mentally, letter by letter, the way she memorized torque values. Some evidence you carry. Most evidence you leave lying where history dropped it.

Marek photographed everything.

That was the part that sat wrong in her chest all the way back down the gravel bar. Because they'd agreed—out loud, together, Felix saying it plainly—to leave the site undisturbed and undocumented, and Marek had nodded along with the rest of them, and then he'd spent four hours doing the opposite with a telephoto lens and the patience of a man defusing a bomb. He'd been subtle about it. He'd angled himself away from the group. He'd shot the wreck from inside the treeline, through gaps in the alder, so that anyone glancing over saw a man studying plants.

June had spent twenty-two years around people who lied for a living—salesmen, insurers, owners, the occasional charter client smuggling something in crates marked FRAGILE—and she knew the species. Marek wasn't hiding what he was doing from them.

He was hiding it from whoever he reported to. Making sure the product was good.

At the bend in the river where the side channel braided out, she fell into step beside him. The others were ahead, voices carrying thin over the water.

"Good light today," she said. "For pictures."

"Yes," Marek said pleasantly. "The valley is very photogenic."

"Shame we agreed not to photograph the site. Rules are rules."

A pause, one stride long. "I took some landscape shots. For myself. I find it helps me think."

"Geology'll do that."

He smiled at her. It was a good smile, warm, practiced, the smile of a man who had learned that being liked was safer than being suspected. "You're very observant, June."

"I'm a mechanic," she said. "Observation is the whole job. Things tell you what's wrong with them, if you listen. Planes, engines, people." She looked at him steadily. "You've been transmitting your observations somewhere, haven't you. Since Whitehorse."

The smile stayed exactly where it was, which was answer enough.

Ahead of them, Felix stopped walking. He was facing upstream, past the camp smoke, past the moored Otter, to where the valley narrowed and climbed toward a high saddle full of shadow. The sun was dropping behind the western ridge and the shadows were pouring downhill like spilled ink, and in that spreading dark, thin and blue and almost invisible—

Smoke.

Not campfire smoke. Too high, too steady, rising off some sheltered hollow in the upper valley where the wind couldn't shred it. Smoke where no chart showed habitation, where no route showed a trail, where ten years of Felix's grease-pencil prohibition said no human being had any business being.

Sami saw it next. Then Cole, standing up from the fire ring. Then Dara, going very still in a way June was beginning to recognize as the shape of her grief.

"Someone's up there," Sami said quietly.

"Yeah," Felix said. His eyes hadn't left the smoke. His voice was the flattest June had ever heard it, and she'd heard him call maydays. "And either they've been there a long time, or they know we're here."

Behind her, almost too soft to catch, June heard the small click of a camera shutter.

Marek was photographing the smoke.
