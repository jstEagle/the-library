## 7. Dara's Version

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones, and Dara Okonkwo sat apart from the others, the way she had sat apart from people for ten years, and told herself the truth for the first time since a helicopter lifted her off a ridge that smelled of rain and kerosene.

She had lied to Felix Whitlock. Not by commission — she had never once said the words *survey plane* herself; she had let him say them, let him fill the silence with his own assumptions, and that was a skill she'd learned in boardrooms long before she'd learned it in the bush. But it was a lie, and it had a shape, and the shape was a man's name.

*Tom Reyes.*

She said it silently, the way you test a scar to see if it still hurts. It did.

* * *

They had met in the worst possible place, which was to say the best. A staging camp outside Puerto Inca, 1998, a year of mud and money, when the interior was opening like a wound and every outfit with a Twin Otter and a dream was pushing north. She was twenty-six, the only woman on a geological team that treated her like a piece of equipment that occasionally spoke, and she had walked into the mess tent to find a pilot with a crooked grin teaching a deckhand to cheat at cards.

"You're the rock doctor," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the geologist. The rock doctor is a different, more expensive person."

"Tom Reyes." He shook her hand like it mattered. "I fly for whoever's paying. Tonight that's you people."

She had hated him within a week and loved him within a month, and the order of those events had never reversed. He was the kind of man who remembered the names of every cook and fuel-hand between the coast and the divide, who carried candy in his jacket pockets for children in villages that weren't on any map, who flew weather other pilots refused and called it *negotiating*. He was also — and this was the part she had spent a decade refusing to examine — the kind of man who would walk into mist for a stranger and consider it a fair trade.

The expedition had been her company's idea: a mineral reconnaissance up the western drainages, three weeks, two aircraft, a guide named Ilsa's husband who knew the high country the way priests know scripture. Dara had designed the sampling program herself. She could still draw the traverse lines from memory — she had, a thousand times, on napkins and hotel stationery and the fogged glass of airplane windows, until the lines were less a map than a prayer.

And on the ninth day, the strike.

She would never forget the assay numbers. Not because they were good — because they were *impossible*. Copper and something else, something the lab tech had circled twice and underlined, in a formation that shouldn't have held either. Tom had stood beside her at the outcrop, turning a fragment of green-stained rock in his big careful hands, and he had said, quietly, in the voice he used for weather decisions and other irreversible things:

"This is a lot of money, Dara."

"I know."

"No. I mean it's the kind of money that brings people. Bad people. Men with helicopters." He'd looked at her, and she had seen something in his face she'd never seen before — not fear for himself. Fear *of* what she was holding. "What happens to the valley when they come?"

"Progress," she'd said, and heard how the word sounded in her own mouth, and hated herself for it, and said it anyway, because she was twenty-seven and ambitious and the numbers were singing to her like a choir.

He had smiled then — that crooked, sad smile — and put the rock in his sample bag, and said, "Then I hope we're wrong about it."

They weren't wrong about it.

* * *

The end, when it came, came fast. It always did, in her memory; she had sanded the edges off it over ten years until what remained was a sequence of images with no connective tissue. The storm front stacking up black over the divide. The radio dying in a squall of static. Tom volunteering to walk the ridge line to the emergency cache — *I know the route, it's forty minutes, I'll be back before you've got the tents pegged* — and Dara, who was expedition lead, who had the authority and the obligation to say no, saying yes instead.

She said yes because the sample crates had to get down the mountain. She said yes because the numbers were singing. She said yes, and Tom Reyes walked into the mist on a ridge above the tree line, and the mist closed, and he did not come back.

Three days of searching. Then the weather won, the way weather always won up there, and the company lawyers arrived by satellite phone before the search parties had even dispersed, and the word *liability* entered a conversation that should have held only Tom's name. The guides were paid off and scattered. The samples — all of them, including the impossible ones — were signed into a company manifest and vanished into a warehouse in the capital. The expedition report was written by a man who had never left his office, and it said *pilot error, terrain, weather*, and it said *no survivors' accounts available*, and Dara signed it because she was told that if she didn't, she would be named in the inquiry, and she was twenty-seven, and she signed it.

She had been signing things ever since. Contracts, mostly. That was what she'd become: a woman who signed things, who raised capital, who sat in glass towers and made the numbers sing for other people. She had married and unmarred a man who called her *formidable*, which she had eventually understood was a word men used for women they intended to survive. She had built a career on the bones of that mountain, and she had told herself for ten years that the money was the point, that the strike was the point, that going back was about *closing the file*.

It wasn't. It had never been.

* * *

The truth had arrived two years ago, in an envelope with no return address, postmarked from a coastal town four hundred kilometers from anywhere.

Inside was a photograph, old and water-spotted: a group of people at a river landing, trapper-rough, dressed in patched canvas and hides. And standing at the back, half-turned, a man with a crooked grin and a scar on his left forearm that Dara would have known anywhere, because she had been there when he got it, on a drill casing, laughing, in 1998.

She had sat with that photograph for six hours. She had missed a board meeting. She had missed a flight. She had, at some point in the small hours, begun to laugh, and then to cry, and had done both for a long time in a hotel room in a city that didn't know her name.

*He lived.* He had walked out of the mist and down off the ridge and found people, and he had *lived*, married into a community that the company's own report had dismissed as *non-sedentary*, and he had lived there for — the photograph was dated, roughly, twelve years after the expedition — he had lived there for years while Dara was signing things in glass towers, while she was being formidable, while she was telling herself the money was the point.

And then, she had learned — slowly, expensively, through researchers and fixers and a man in the capital who dealt in information the way other men dealt in fish — Tom Reyes had died two winters ago. Defending something. There had been prospectors in the valley before her. There had been a confrontation. There had been a death that no court had ever heard of.

So she had done the thing she knew how to do. She had raised money. She had built a cover story — a downed survey plane, a recovery operation, all of it clean and boring and fundable — and she had found the one pilot in the north who knew that country, because if anyone could get her there before the monsoon, it was Felix Whitlock, Tom's friend, her old rival, the man whose name Tom had said with warmth and hers, she suspected, with something more complicated.

She had not told Felix the truth. She had told herself it was because he wouldn't fly the job if he knew — that his grief was a locked room and she had no key. That was true. But it was not the whole truth, and the whole truth was this: she was afraid. Afraid that if she said Tom's name aloud in front of Felix Whitlock, the ten careful years would crack open all at once, and she would be twenty-seven again on a ridge, saying *yes*, saying *the crates have to come down*, and she would never stop saying it.

* * *

Across the dead fire, Felix turned in his bedroll. She watched the shape of him in the starlight — older than Tom would have been now, gray coming into his beard, carrying his own ten years of the same mountain. They had hated each other politely for a decade, and she understood now that what they had hated each other *with* was grief, hers buried and his worn on the outside like weather. Two people mourning the same man in different languages.

*He was your friend,* she wanted to say. *You got to mourn him honestly. You got to put his name on things. I had to put him in a footnote.*

Marek slept by the gear, too still, the way he always slept — or didn't. She thought of the way he photographed the wreck, too careful, too complete, and felt the first cold thread of something she should have felt weeks ago. She had hired Marek through the consortium's recommended list. She had signed things. She was always signing things.

Tomorrow, or the day after, they would find what the river had kept, and then the high valley, and then — she allowed herself, finally, in the dark, to say it plainly — *his grave*. That was all. That was the whole mission, under the cover story, under the money, under the ten years: a woman standing at a grave in the rain, saying a name she'd been unable to say.

She would tell Felix when they found it. She owed him that. She owed Tom that.

The fire's red eye guttered and went out. And in the silence that followed, from somewhere up the black shoulder of the valley, Dara heard — or thought she heard, or told herself she only thought she heard — a sound that did not belong to wind or water or any animal she knew.

It sounded, faintly and impossibly, like a man's voice, singing.
