## 8. Smoke Where Smoke Shouldn't Be

The watch schedule was Felix's idea, and like most of his ideas it was built out of old scar tissue. Nobody had argued. When a man who has flown the north for twenty years tells you that camps get watched at night, you don't ask him what for. You take the two-to-four and you drink your coffee bitter.

Sami didn't mind the night watch. It was the only hour of the day when nobody needed anything from her — no weight-and-balance figures, no fuel calculations, no Dara Okonkwo asking in her careful, educated voice whether Sami thought the weather would hold. At two in the morning the world simplified itself down to firelight, cold air, and the sound of the river doing its endless arithmetic with stones.

She sat on an overturned fuel drum with the rifle across her knees — unloaded, because Felix had said *carry it, don't load it, we're not that kind of expedition yet* — and she watched the valley breathe.

It breathed. That was the thing nobody told you about country this far north. Maps made it look dead: white space, contour lines, a blue thread of river. But live in it for three days and you understood it was less like scenery and more like an animal. It fed at dawn when the birds came off the water in clouds. It slept at dusk. And at night, if you were quiet enough, you could feel it thinking.

The camp behind her was a scatter of shapes under tarps: Cole's snore, low and rhythmic as an idling engine; June's tent zipped to the chin because June believed mosquitoes were a personal enemy; Felix sleeping the way he always slept, flat on his back on the ground with no pad, like a man lying in state. Dara's tent was dark. Marek's was dark too, though Sami had noticed — the way you notice things when your job is noticing — that Marek's tent was pitched facing away from the fire, and that he'd chosen the spot before anyone else had picked theirs.

The wreck lay a kilometer downstream where they'd found it, rigged now with alder branches so it read as just another dead tree from the air. The journal pages were wrapped in oilcloth inside Felix's duffel. Tom Reyes's cabin stood empty up the slope, its door closed by Ilsa's grandson with a formality that had felt, to Sami, like closing a book someone else was still reading.

And above all of it — above the wreck, above the cabin, above the grey shoulders of scree where the trees gave out — the high valley went up into cloud and stayed there.

That was where she saw the smoke.

* * *

It came the way smoke comes in cold country: not as a column but as a smell first, faint and wrong, woodsmoke threaded through the clean mineral smell of glacier melt. Sami's head came up like a dog's. She'd grown up in her uncle's trapping camp outside Yellowknife; she knew every honest smell a forest could make, and woodsmoke at three in the morning, upwind, from a direction where no one was supposed to be —

She stood. Scanned. The moon was a fingernail paring behind cloud, which meant the light was the bad kind, the kind that showed you movement but not shape. For a full minute she saw nothing and told herself she'd imagined it, dreamed it, that her uncle's stories about the bush getting into your blood had finally taken root.

Then the cloud lifted off the saddle at the head of the valley, and there it was.

A smudge. Not a flame — a flame would have been visible, a spark in all that dark. This was smoke rising thin and straight from a fold in the mountainside maybe four kilometers up-valley, the kind of smoke a small fire makes when it's being fed carefully, a stick at a time, by someone who doesn't want to be seen feeding it.

Someone was living up there.

Sami's heart did something complicated — a lift and a drop at once. Because Felix had said, standing over Tom Reyes's grave marker, that the man had died two winters ago. Ilsa had said it. Everyone had said it. And yet here was smoke, and smoke meant hands, and hands meant a person, and the only person who should have any reason to build a careful fire in this valley was a man everyone had buried.

Or someone watching them. Or someone waiting for them.

She looked back at the tents. Waking Felix was easy — he surfaced instantly, silent, one hand already finding his boots, and when she pointed up-valley he went very still in a way she'd learned to recognize as him thinking fast.

"Four klicks," she whispered. "In the fold below the saddle. Small fire, tended."

"Tended." He said it like a diagnosis. "Not abandoned, not blown up. Tended."

"Somebody's home."

Felix stared up at the black shape of the mountain for a long time. In the firelight his face looked older than it had in Yellowknife ten days ago, carved deeper, and Sami realized she was watching a man do arithmetic with hope, which is the cruelest kind.

"Nobody wakes Dara," he said finally. "Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because if it's who I'm afraid it might be, she deserves to sleep one more hour believing in graves." He pulled his jacket on. "Wake June. Get her on the glasses. Then wake Marek — gently — and tell him we've got a possible contact and we're holding position till dawn. Nothing more."

"Why nothing more?"

Felix's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Because I want to see what he does with the information."

* * *

June took the binoculars without a word and lay prone on the gravel bar, elbows planted, and became the kind of still that only June could become — the stillness of a woman who had spent thirty years listening to engines tell her the truth about themselves.

"Wind's quartering," she murmured after a while. "Smoke's drifting east. If it was a signal fire they'd bank it lower. If it was accidental they wouldn't feed it." A pause. "There's a structure. Maybe. Rock shadow, hard to say. Could be a cache, could be a wall."

"Could be nothing," Sami said.

"Could be." June lowered the glasses. In the dying fire her eyes were very level. "But nothing doesn't smell like spruce boughs burning, girl. Somebody cut green wood tonight. Green wood means a bed. Beds mean living." She handed the glasses back. "Go wake our geologist."

Marek's tent was cold when Sami unzipped the fly — not slept-in cold, not yet, the fabric still holding the evening chill, and the ground cloth beneath where a body should have warmed it was smooth and uncreased. She stood there in the dark feeling the small hairs rise on her arms.

He wasn't in his tent.

She found him forty meters from camp, upstream, in the alder shadow at the edge of the gravel bar, crouched with his back to her. He had something cupped in both hands and angled away from the firelight, and there was a faint blue glow between his fingers, and he was speaking — low, clipped, in a language she didn't know and then, as she drifted closer through the alders, in English:

"—negative on extraction window, the strip is compromised, I need a new—" A pause. Listening. "No. No, listen to me. There are people here. Actual people, there's a settlement, there's—" Another pause, longer, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone flatter, the way voices go when someone on the other end of a line has stopped asking and started telling. "Understood. Understood. I'll hold position. Vossen? Is Vossen coming?" A silence. "How long?"

Sami stepped on a branch.

It cracked like a shot in the cold air, and Marek moved faster than a man his age had any right to move — the phone vanishing into his jacket, the glow gone, and when he turned around his face was arranged into mild, apologetic concern, the face of a geologist caught taking a midnight stroll.

"Sami." Even her name sounded rehearsed. "I couldn't sleep. The altitude."

They weren't at altitude. They were at four hundred meters. She let the lie sit there between them, growing.

"You missed your tent," she said. "It's back that way."

"I wanted to check the sky. Weather moving in from the west." He gestured vaguely upward, at cloud cover that was, admittedly, moving in from the west. He was good. He was very good. His breathing hadn't changed, his hands hung loose, and if she hadn't seen the blue glow she would have bought it completely.

"Felix wants you briefed," she said. "Smoke sighted up-valley. Possible contact. We hold till dawn."

Something flickered behind Marek's eyes — quick, hungry, gone. "Smoke," he repeated. "Interesting."

"Yeah," Sami said. "Real interesting."

She turned and walked back toward the fire, counting her own footsteps so her pace stayed even, and behind her she heard nothing at all, which frightened her more than footsteps would have. A man following you makes noise. A man deciding something makes none.

* * *

At the fire, June was waiting. She didn't ask. She just looked at Sami's face, and then past her, at the dark shape of Marek coming up the bar, and her jaw set in the particular way it set when she was adding a rivet to a structure she'd been building in her head since day one.

"He was on a call," Sami said quietly, when Marek was still out of earshot. "Satellite phone. Said 'extraction window.' Said 'the strip is compromised.' Asked if someone named Vossen was coming."

June was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved across her face.

"Vossen," she said. "You sure?"

"That's what I heard."

June nodded slowly, the way she nodded at a gauge reading she didn't like but couldn't argue with. "Cole mentioned a Vossen once. Years back. Ketchikan days. Said he was the kind of man companies hired when they wanted a problem to stop existing." She spat into the fire, a habit Sami had never seen her do anywhere but here, as if the forest licensed certain discourtesies. "So our quiet little geologist reports to somebody, and that somebody sends Vossen."

"We don't know that—"

"We know a man lied about his gear list, photographs wrecks like he's cataloguing evidence, sleeps facing away from the group, and makes encrypted calls at three a.m. asking how long." June's voice never rose. It never needed to. "In my experience, girl, that pattern only ever adds up one way."

"What do we do?"

"Nothing." June said it flatly, and Sami stared at her. "We do *nothing*. We smile at breakfast. We hand him the coffee pot. Because the second he knows we know, we stop learning from him — and right now he's the only radio line we've got into whatever's coming." She looked up-valley, toward the smoke that had gone invisible again behind the cloud, toward whoever was tending a careful fire in the fold of the mountain. "Besides. Felix said hold till dawn. So we hold."

"And then?"

June fed a stick to the fire. Green wood, Sami noticed, cut fresh, smoking slow and deliberate — and understood suddenly that June had been making smoke of their own all along, a small honest signal saying *we're here, we see you, we're not hiding.*

"And then," June said, "somebody goes up and knocks on the door."

* * *

Dawn came grey and reluctant, the clouds lowering until the saddle disappeared entirely, and with it the smoke — which somehow made it worse, not better. An absence is louder than a presence if you know what used to be there.

Felix gathered them at the fire: himself, June, Sami, Cole rubbing sleep out of his eyes, Dara with her hair braided tight and her face already braced for news, and Marek last, arriving from the direction of the river with wet boots and a plausible expression.

"Smoke last night," Felix said. No preamble. "Up-valley, four kilometers, below the saddle. Tended fire. Possibly a structure. It's socked in now, so we can't confirm from here." He looked around the circle. "Tom Reyes is dead. We've all seen the grave. Which means either someone else lives up there, or someone's using his ground, or—" he paused, and Sami watched him decide how much truth to spend, "—or the grave is wrong."

Dara had gone absolutely white. She didn't speak. Her hands, folded in her lap, had knotted themselves together like rope under load.

"The valley people won't guide us up there," Felix continued. "Ilsa made that clear. Her people don't go above the falls, and they don't want us going either." He glanced at Dara. "So it's us. Two go, three stay with the plane and the pages. I'll lead. I want one more."

"Me," Sami said, before she'd decided to.

Felix studied her. "Why?"

Because I found the smoke, she almost said. Because I grew up reading country like this. Because I don't trust Marek and I'd rather be gone than stay and pretend. What she said was simpler.

"Because you'll want someone watching your back who isn't watching anything else."

A beat. Felix's eyes flicked — just barely, just once — toward Marek, and Sami understood that he knew too, or suspected, or had simply been flying long enough to smell the same wrongness she had. He didn't ask. That was Felix: information hoarded like fuel, burned only when necessary.

"All right. Sami and me. June has the camp. Cole—" 

"I'll rig the plane," Cole said. "If weather lifts, I want the option of lifting out."

"Marek." Felix turned to him, pleasant, unreadable. "You'll stay with June. Help her catalogue the wreck site. Photographs, measurements — the survey work you came for."

For half a second — less — something crossed Marek's face. Disappointment, or recalculation, or relief; Sami genuinely couldn't tell which frightened her most. "Of course," he said smoothly. "Whatever's useful."

Dara stood. "Felix. If it's him — if there's any chance it's him—"

"Then he's been up there alone for ten years while we all agreed to forget him," Felix said quietly. "One more morning won't kill either of us. Sit down, Dara."

She sat. But her eyes went up-valley, into the cloud, and stayed there, and Sami thought: *she knows something. She's known it since the cabin.* Whatever Dara Okonkwo had really come north to find, it wasn't a downed plane, and the smoke rising out of the high country was pulling at some thread in her that had nothing to do with minerals.

* * *

They left at first light with rifles loaded this time — Felix hadn't said anything about it, but Sami had loaded hers at the fire, and he'd seen, and he'd said nothing, and that was its own conversation.

The climb was ugly. Alder first, waist-deep and spiteful, then scree that shifted under every step like the mountain shrugging them off, then the treeline, stunted and twisted, and above that the grey. Cloud moved through them rather than past them, beading on their jackets, erasing the world to fifteen meters in every direction. Sami navigated by bearing and count — two hundred degrees, forty minutes of climbing — and twice they stopped and simply listened, because sound carried strangely up here, flattened and close.

On the third stop, Felix held up a fist.

Through the cloud, downhill and to the east, came a smell. Woodsmoke. Fresh. Close — closer than four kilometers, much closer, the fire had been moved or there was more than one — and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, another smell that made the hair stand up on Sami's neck.

Cured meat. Fish, maybe. Someone's winter stores.

Somebody wasn't just surviving up here. Somebody was *provisioned.*

Felix drew his rifle off his shoulder — not aimed, just held, the way a man holds a tool he hopes not to use — and moved forward with a low-land hunter's economy that surprised her; she kept forgetting there had been a whole other Felix before the float planes, a Felix who had walked out of these same mountains one man short.

They crested a shelf of rock and the cloud tore open for ten seconds — a rip in the grey, a gift or a trap — and Sami looked down into a hanging meadow she would have sworn no chart showed, and saw it.

A cabin. Smaller than Tom's, newer, built of driftwood-grey timber with sod chinked between the logs. A fish rack. A woodpile stacked with geometric precision. A trail worn smooth between door and stream. And from the stone chimney, rising thin and patient into the cloud, exactly the smoke she had watched all night.

Occupied. Tended. Lived-in.

And pinned to the cabin's door frame, fluttering in the wind off the icefield, a scrap of bright fabric that even at this distance Sami recognized, because she'd packed it herself in Yellowknife ten days ago, off the supply manifest, into the expedition stores.

An orange survey panel. Theirs.

Someone up here had been down at their camp. Recently. Had taken a piece of their gear and hung it on their door like a flag, or a message, or a warning.

Felix stood very still beside her, and when he spoke his voice was almost too quiet for the wind.

"Well," he said. "They know we're here."
