## 9. The Valley People

The smoke told us where to go before anyone did.

It rose from the treeline at the head of the valley in a thin grey thread, the kind of smoke that comes from green wood burned by someone who knows exactly how much they need and no more. Not a signal fire. A cooking fire. Which meant people, and people meant either rescue or trouble, and after ten years of flying this country I had learned that the two usually arrived holding hands.

We went up on foot because the river gorge wouldn't let us do anything else. Six of us — me, June, Sami, Cole with his arm still stiff from the wreck-site work, Dara, and Marek trailing behind with his camera bag held against his chest like an infant. The trail was old. That was the first thing I noticed, and it bothered me more than the smoke did. Alder cut back year after year, roots worn smooth by boots, a blaze mark on a spruce so weathered it had grown a lip of bark over the axe scar. Somebody had been walking this path for a long time. Long enough to make it theirs.

"Nobody lives up here," Dara said. She said it like she was reminding herself.

"Somebody does," June said.

The valley opened the way valleys do in the north — all at once, like a curtain yanked back. A broad flat of meadow going gold at the edges, a river braided silver through the middle of it, and on the near slope, maybe forty structures. Cabins, drying racks, a long low building with a sod roof, fish weirs staked across a channel in patterns I recognized from coast villages two hundred miles south. Woodsmoke from a dozen chimneys. Dogs — three of them, then five, coming down the trail toward us at a trot with their ears up, not barking yet, which was somehow worse than barking. Dogs that don't bark at strangers have already been told about strangers.

Nobody came out to meet us. That was the second thing. In every village I'd ever landed at, kids materialized within minutes, drawn by the sound of an engine or the sight of outsiders like gulls to a gutting table. Here the doors stayed shut. Curtains moved. Somewhere upslope a child's voice said something sharp and was hushed.

"They know we're here," Sami murmured. He had his hands visible and loose at his sides, the way his father had apparently taught him to stand in places where you weren't sure of your welcome. Smart kid.

"They've known since we landed," I said. "Maybe since we took off."

An old woman came out of the long house while the dogs circled us at a distance of ten feet, close enough to smell, far enough to matter. She was small and straight-backed, white hair braided tight to her skull, wearing rubber boots and a canvas coat gone soft as cloth-of-gold with age. She carried nothing. She didn't need to. Behind her, in the doorway shadows, I counted four shapes with rifles held the way you hold a tool you've used all your life — not aimed, just present.

She stopped six paces away and looked at us one at a time, unhurried, the way you'd count crates. When she got to Marek her eyes narrowed a fraction. When she got to Dara they didn't change at all, which told me she'd already filed Dara under something. When she got to me, everything changed.

"You're a Whitlock," she said.

It wasn't a question, and my own name sounded strange in her mouth, accented somewhere east of everywhere, flattened by decades of this air.

"Felix Whitlock," I said. "Ma'am."

She spat. Not dramatically, not with theater — just turned her head slightly and spat into the grass between us, a small precise gesture, older than any language in the valley, and then looked back at me with eyes the color of creek water over slate.

"Ilsa," she said, giving me nothing else. "You fly for the man who left us."

* * *

They took our rifles at the edge of the village, politely, the way customs takes your passport. A young man with a face full of freckles and an old man's patience held out his hands and waited until Cole unbuckled his holster without being asked twice. Nobody searched us roughly. Nobody needed to. There were perhaps sixty people in this place and every one of them had watched us walk in, and there was nowhere in forty miles of country we could run that they couldn't find us.

They gave us a cabin at the village edge — one room, iron stove, bunks built for shorter men than Cole. Through the single window I could see the river and the weir stakes and, beyond them, the dark line of the high valley where the smoke from Tom Reyes's ghost had risen two nights ago. Or where somebody's smoke had risen. I was no longer sure those were different things.

Dara stood at the window with her arms crossed. "Ilsa said *left us*. Left who? Left what?"

"The expedition had guides," I said. "Four of them. Local men out of the coastal stations, hired in Talkeetna-equivalent towns downriver. When the money ran out and the weather turned, the decision was made to push the last leg light." I heard myself saying it in the flat voice I used for cargo manifests. "Light meant leaving the guides at the last camp with half the rations and a promise that the plane would come back for them inside a week."

"And it didn't."

"The weather closed for eleven days. When we got back through, the camp was empty. We assumed—" I stopped. Ten years of assuming. "We assumed they'd walked out south. It's a four-day walk if the river's low."

"It wasn't low," Dara said quietly. "That year it wasn't low."

"No," I said. "It wasn't."

Marek sat on a bunk with his camera bag between his feet and said nothing, which was the most he'd said in three days. Sami watched him the way June watched fuel gauges — constantly, without appearing to.

June came in from checking the perimeter, which is a military way of saying she'd walked around the cabin once and counted dogs. "Sixty-one people," she reported. "Kids, elders, working-age adults. Gardens, fish racks, a smokehouse bigger than our hangar. They're not surviving up here, Felix. They're *living* up here. This is a town."

"A town that isn't on any chart," Sami said.

"A town that doesn't want to be," June said.

There was a knock — two knuckles, once — and the freckled young man put his head in. Supper, he said, at the long house, if we wanted it. His English had the rhythm of the coast but the vowels of nowhere; he'd learned it from parents who'd learned it from books and radio. He looked at each of us when he spoke, and his gaze snagged on Marek's bag the way a fish snags on a hook, and moved on.

"We'll come," I said.

"Eat what you take," he said, and shut the door. It wasn't a threat. It was worse. It was a rule.

* * *

The long house smelled of woodsmoke, spruce pitch, and fish fat, and it was warm in the way only buildings heated by the same fire for generations get warm — the warmth in the walls themselves. Benches along both sides. Food set out without ceremony: smoked char, boiled roots, a dark bread dense as ballast, berries cooked down with something bitter. We ate because we'd been told to eat what we took, and because none of us had eaten properly in two days.

Ilsa sat across the fire from us and did not eat. She watched us eat, which was its own kind of sentence being passed.

"You came up the river past the falls," she said. "In a floatplane. White, blue stripe, tail number ending four-seven."

"That's right."

"There were others. Eleven days ago. Four men, a black helicopter, no markings. They landed at the gravel bar below the falls and walked up the same trail you walked." She let that sit in the room with the smoke. "They carried metal detectors and a drill core sampler and a gun apiece, and they asked questions about the high valley, and my grandson Ansel told them what we tell everyone: there is nothing up there but rock and weather."

"What happened to them?" Sami asked. Too fast, too curious. I put a hand on his knee under the bench.

"They left," Ilsa said. "Mostly."

"*Mostly?*" Dara leaned forward.

"One of them fell into the river above the falls. The river is not kind to careless men." Her voice didn't rise or fall. "We did not push him. We did not pull him out. We buried what the river gave back, because we bury everyone. Even men like that. Especially men like that — so the ground remembers them better than they deserved."

Cole set down his bread. "And you think we're the same kind of men."

"I think you came the same trail asking the same questions," Ilsa said. "What a man is depends on what he does next. That part hasn't happened yet."

She rose, joints cracking like green wood in a hot stove, and crossed to me, and for a moment I thought she was going to spit again. Instead she took my chin in a hand dry and strong as tree bark and turned my face to the firelight.

"Edmund Whitlock," she said. "Ten years ago. Big man, loud laugh, talked about God like he owed Him money. He signed the paper that sent the guides home with nothing. Your father."

"My uncle," I said. "And yes."

"He promised the plane would return in seven days." Her grip tightened. "My son was one of the four. Karel. Twenty-six years old. He walked south into a river country in flood because your family's word was worth less than the fuel to keep a promise." She let go of my chin and stepped back, and I saw that her hands were shaking now, fine tremors, rage banked so long it had become geology. "Two of the four made it out alive, downstream, half-starved, in August. My son was not one of them. We found his pack in October. We never found him."

The fire popped. Down the benches, the valley people went on eating and talking softly, letting the old woman spend her anger the way you spend money saved for a purpose.

"I didn't know," I said. It came out small and true and useless, the three qualities of almost every apology ever offered. "The report said the guides walked out. It said—"

"I know what it said. Edmund Whitlock wrote it." She turned away from me, done, a door closing. "He wrote that the guides were released at their own request. He wrote it sitting in a hotel in the city drinking whiskey bought with the expedition's insurance settlement. My daughter wrote to him for nine years. He answered twice. Then he died, and God forgive me, I was glad."

Dara's face had gone the color of ash. Whatever she'd known about the original expedition's finances, whatever she'd suspected, this was new arithmetic to her too — I could see her recalculating ten years of grievance against me and finding the ledger wrong.

"Why tell us this?" I asked. "Why feed us? Why not turn us out?"

Ilsa paused at the door of the long house, silhouetted against the blue evening.

"Because you flew medicine into the interior for ten years," she said. "Because the radio carries more than gossip, even up here. Because my granddaughter broke her arm two winters ago and the nearest doctor was nine hundred miles away, and a Whitlock plane came through weather that would ground the angels." She looked back at me, and for one unguarded second the hatred in her cracked and showed the thing underneath it, which was grief, which was always underneath everything. "I have spent ten years hating your name, Felix Whitlock. It is a very inconvenient thing to discover that names are not men."

Then she was gone into the dusk, and the freckled young man showed us back to our cabin through a village that had begun, very slightly, to come back to life around us — a woman drawing water who nodded, a child peering around a doorpost who didn't run when I caught his eye.

* * *

June banked the stove and sat on her bunk cleaning a lantern that didn't need cleaning, which is what June does instead of talking. Sami lay staring at the ceiling beams. Cole was asleep sitting up, the way he could, breath whistling through his nose.

Dara crouched by the window, watching the dark shape of the high valley swallow the last light.

"Tom," she said, so quietly I almost missed it. "If he came here — if he lived here —"

"Then Ilsa knows," I said. "Ilsa knows everything this valley has done for twenty years. She knew who we were before we introduced ourselves. She knew about the helicopter. She knows why we came." I rubbed my eyes. "Which means she's deciding right now how much of it we get to learn."

"She hates you."

"She has reason to hate me. It's not the same thing." I looked at the window, at the valley, at the place where a thin thread of smoke had risen two nights ago from country that officially contained nobody. "Tomorrow she decides whether we're Edmund Whitlock or whether we're something else. And Dara — whatever she shows us, whatever she tells us — remember what she said about the men with the metal detectors. One of them *mostly left.*"

June set down the lantern. "You think she'd do that to us? After the medicine flight?"

"I think," I said, "that this valley has kept itself off every chart for a hundred years, and the people who found it anyway are not people it forgives easily. We're standing in the one courtroom in the world where the past actually gets read aloud. And I just heard my family's name read into it."

Sami spoke from his bunk, voice thin in the dark. "Then what happens to us tomorrow?"

Outside, far up the high valley, so faint it might have been wind or might have been memory, a dog began to bark — one animal, one voice, at something moving in the trees where nobody was supposed to be.

Ilsa's answer, when it came at dawn, would be a rifle over an old woman's shoulder and a walk upriver toward a cabin none of the charts had ever carried — and a grave that had waited twenty years for someone from outside to kneel beside it.
