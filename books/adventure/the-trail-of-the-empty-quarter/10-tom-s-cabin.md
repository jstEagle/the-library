## 10. Tom's Cabin

Ilsa did not speak on the walk. None of the valley people did, really — they spoke the way the river spoke, in short bursts and long silences, and you learned to read the current between the words. June Okonkwo-adjacent, she'd have said if she bothered with jokes, which she didn't. She walked ahead of them up a deer trail that switchbacked through alder and devil's club, her grandson Ansel trailing behind with a rifle slung loose across his back like it was an extension of his spine, and June kept her eyes on Ilsa's boots because looking at anything else meant looking at Felix.

Felix had gone quiet in the way that scared her most. Not the silence of a man thinking — the silence of a man holding something down, like cargo strapped into a bay that wanted to shift. He hadn't spoken since Ilsa had said the name at the fire that morning: *Reyes.* Just the surname, spat like a seed husk, and then her eyes finding Felix's face and narrowing, and Felix saying, in a voice June had never heard him use, "Take me to where he lived."

Now they climbed. The morning was cold enough to see their breath, and the forest dripped from last night's rain, and somewhere above them a woodpecker hammered at something dead with the persistence of a man who intended to keep working until the world ended or the tree fell over, whichever came first.

Dara walked behind Felix. She had barely slept — June could see it in the gray under her eyes, in the way she moved like someone operating on borrowed voltage — but she climbed steadily, one hand on roots, saying nothing. Marek stayed in camp under Cole's guard, his ankle wrapped and his face sullen, and nobody had argued about leaving him there. Sami had volunteered to sit on him, which told June everything about how the kid's week was going.

The trail crested through a stand of birch gone gold at the edges, and then the trees opened, and there it was.

The cabin sat on a bench of land above the river bend, built from hand-hewn spruce logs silvered by twenty years of weather, chinked with moss, roofed in shakes that had been replaced in patches — you could see the generations of repair, the way you could see the rings in a stump. A woodshed. A smokehouse. A garden plot gone to seed but still fenced, still defended against deer by a wall of woven branches. A root cellar dug into the hillside with a door of split cedar. And everywhere, everywhere, the marks of a man who had expected to stay forever: axe notches in the chopping block worn deep as a bowl, a boot-scraper carved into the shape of a loon, a child's swing hanging from the birch by the door, its rope green with moss, its seat rotted through.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

It was Dara who broke first. Not with words — with a sound, small and involuntary, like something tearing low in her chest, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth and stood there staring at the swing.

"The swing," she managed finally. "He talked about—" She stopped. Started again. "On the expedition. Nights around the fire. He used to say that when it was over, when we were rich, he was going to find some place—" Her voice failed her and she laughed, a broken wet sound. "Some place with a river. And he'd build a cabin. And he'd hang a swing."

"He did," Ilsa said.

They all turned. The old woman stood at the edge of the clearing with her hands folded, watching them with an expression that was not cruelty and not kindness but something older than both — the look of a judge who has already heard every argument the world can make and found most of them wanting.

"His wife hung it," Ilsa said. "My daughter. Katrin. She died eight years ago, in the winter fever, same winter that took two others. He raised the girl alone after." She nodded toward Ansel, who had stopped at the treeline and stood there with his rifle and his unreadable young face, watching these strangers desecrate his grandfather's clearing just by standing in it. "Ansel is Tom's blood as much as mine. More, maybe. Tom earned it."

"Where is he?" Felix asked. His voice came out scraped. "Where is he buried?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to decide something for her, though whether it decided mercy or contempt, June couldn't tell.

"Come inside," she said.

* * *

The cabin door was cedar, carved with the same loon motif as the boot-scraper, and it opened on hinges that needed oil and didn't get any complaint about it. Inside, the air smelled of cold ash and spruce and time.

One room. That was all. One room and a loft, and the room held a stove black with use, a table scarred by twenty years of knives and plates and elbows, two chairs and a rocking chair by the window with a quilt folded over its arm, faded to ghosts of blue. Shelves of books — actual books, paperbacks swollen with damp, spines cracked from rereading: Steinbeck, a Bible, a surveyor's manual, three dog-eared collections of poetry with a name written inside the front cover in pencil. *T. Reyes.*

And above the stove, nailed to the log wall, a photograph.

June saw Felix see it, and she watched something go out of his face and stay out.

The photo was old, sun-faded to sepia along one edge. Four people crouched on a glacier tongue, ropes coiled, grinning the particular grins of people who believe the worst is behind them. Young Felix Whitlock on the left, thirty pounds lighter, hair dark, laughing at something outside the frame. A younger man beside him with a wide smile and a compass in his fist — Tom Reyes, unmistakably, even after ten years and twenty more years of distance. Two others June didn't know. And at the edge, half out of frame, a woman with dark skin and close-cropped hair, caught mid-turn, not quite smiling.

Dara made the sound again, the tearing sound, and this time she didn't cover it.

"He kept it," she whispered. "Ten years. Twenty years. He kept us on his wall."

"He kept everything," Ilsa said. "That was Tom. He kept what other men threw away." She crossed to the table and lifted a tin box off it, dented, painted once in green, and set it down in front of Felix with a care that was almost ceremonial. "Two winters ago, men came up the river. Prospectors. They had a map — a bad map, a stolen map, copied from something. They came to the cabin with guns and asked Tom where the color was. He fed them dinner. He told them stories. He sent them downriver satisfied and empty-handed." Her jaw tightened. "Then they came back at night. There was a fight at the smokehouse. Tom was sixty-one years old and he put two of them in the river before the third one shot him."

Nobody breathed.

"The outside world never asked a single question," Ilsa went on, and her voice didn't rise at all, which made it worse. "No police came upriver. No investigation. We buried him ourselves, on the point above the falls, facing the water he chose. And the third man went home and told his employers the strike was a myth, and they believed him, because men like that always believe what lets them sleep." She set her hand flat on the tin box. "But Tom left this. For whoever came looking. He said someone would come, someday. He said it would either be friends or thieves, and the box would know the difference."

She pushed it across the table to Felix.

Felix's hands hovered over the lid. June had flown with this man for six years, had watched him land planes on ice the width of a rumor, had seen him calm through engine failures and whiteouts and a customs interrogation that would have broken a lesser man into confessions. She had never seen his hands shake.

They shook now.

He opened the box.

Inside: a compass, brass-cased, its glass clouded. A bundle of letters tied with twine, addressed in careful handwriting to *Felix Whitlock, Float Base, Last Lake* — never sent, never mailed, because there was no address that could receive them and no pilot brave enough to fly the route. A dried flower pressed flat as a fossil. And beneath the letters, wrapped in oilcloth, a journal. A surveyor's field book, actually, its canvas cover stiff with age, its pages dense with the two-handed slanted script June recognized from flight plans and weight-and-balance sheets in Felix's own hand — the handwriting of a man who thought in numbers and loved in margins.

Felix untied the twine on the letters first. He read the top one standing, then sat down slowly in the rocking chair as if his legs had filed formal notice of resignation, and read the rest of the page, and turned to the next, and June stood in the doorway of a dead man's house and watched her boss come apart quietly, the way ice goes out of a lake — no drama, just a slow structural surrender, sheet by sheet.

Dara read over his shoulder. Halfway down the second letter she put her hand flat against the log wall to steady herself.

"What does it say?" June asked softly. Because somebody had to ask, and it wasn't going to be Ilsa, and it wasn't going to be Ansel in the doorway with his rifle and his grandfather's chin.

Felix cleared his throat. It took him two tries.

"It says—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It says, *Dear Felix. If you're reading this, you finally grew a spine and flew north. Good. Took you long enough, you stubborn bastard.*" A sound came out of Felix that was trying very hard to be a laugh. "*I'm not going to explain why I walked into the mist that day. You've had ten years to invent reasons, and I bet none of them are true, so let me save you the trouble. I found something in the high valley that I couldn't carry out and couldn't leave behind, so I became the thing that guards it. You'll understand when you read the journal. Or you won't, and you'll be angry, and that's fine too. Anger keeps a man warm.*"

Felix's voice gave out. Dara took the letter from his hands without asking and read aloud, her voice steadier than her eyes:

"*Tell Dara I'm sorry. Tell her I should have said it at the last camp instead of writing it here. Tell her the mist wasn't an ending. It was a door, and I walked through it, and on the other side was Katrin, and she was furious with me for ten years and then she married me anyway, which tells you everything about the women in my life.*"

Dara stopped reading. She folded the letter along its existing creases with great precision, as if precision were the only thing keeping her upright, and set it on the table, and stood looking at the swing through the window for a long, long moment.

"The journal," Ilsa said. "Read the journal. That's the part that matters now."

* * *

Felix read it by the window light, turning pages with the reverence of a priest handling scripture, and June drifted through the cabin while he read because standing still felt unbearable. She catalogued the dead man's life the way she catalogued an engine room: the mended netting over the loft rail, a girl's height marked in pencil on the doorframe — *Ansel, age 6; Ansel, age 9* — climbing past the last mark and stopping, because boys stop growing before grandfathers stop measuring. A rack of fishing lures carved from bone. A medical kit, well-stocked, well-used — June recognized half the drugs, the same ones she packed for Felix's medevac runs, and understood suddenly that for twenty years this valley's fevers and broken bones had been treated by a man who'd learned field medicine on glaciers, improvising, trading, writing away for supplies through channels June couldn't imagine.

A whole life. Built in secret, sustained in secret, ended in violence, mourned in private. Ten years June had flown with Felix, and in all that time he'd never once mentioned that his partner might be alive, might be fifty miles north of the last charted lake, raising a daughter, hanging swings, delivering babies. The grief of it arrived in her sideways, late, the way grief does — not for Tom, whom she'd never met, but for Felix, who had spent a decade carrying a hole shaped exactly like this cabin and calling it discipline.

"Felix," she said. "The journal. What are we looking at?"

He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear, and that combination frightened her more than tears would have.

"I'm looking at the biggest mineral strike in the history of the territory," he said. "And I'm looking at a lie so beautiful it should hang in a gallery."

He turned the journal around and laid it flat on the table, open to a spread of hand-drawn maps. The upper drawing showed the high valley in meticulous detail — contours, drainages, sample sites marked with dates and assay values, numbers that even June's untrained eye could tell were staggering. Gold values, and beneath them, in a different ink, notes on rare earths, concentrations that read like typos.

"And this?" Dara touched the second map, the lower one.

"That," Felix said, "is the map Tom sent out. The one that got copied, and stolen, and got him killed. Look at it." He traced the contour lines with one finger. "Same valley. Same rivers. But the strike is drawn two drainages east, in country that's barren rock. Every sample site relocated. Every value deflated. He mischarted it — deliberately, beautifully, with the skill of a man who'd spent his life making maps. Anyone who followed this map would spend a season digging holes in nothing and go home telling their bosses the whole thing was a myth." His finger stopped on a line of text in the margin, and he read it aloud:

"*A lie on paper is cheaper than a war on the ground. Let them dig in the wrong valley. Let them find gravel and go home. This ground belongs to the people who buried their dead in it.*"

Silence. Outside, the river spoke its one long sentence, over and over.

"So the strike is real," Sami would have said, if Sami were here. June supplied the thought herself. "And the map everyone wants is worthless. And the only true map in existence is sitting on this table in a dead man's handwriting."

"Not the only one," Ilsa said quietly.

Everyone turned.

"Tom taught the valley to read his maps," she said. "Me. My daughter, before she died. Ansel. The strike is not a secret we keep in paper, Mr. Whitlock. It is a secret we keep in people. Burn the journal and the knowledge walks on two legs. Kill us, and it walks on whatever legs are left." Her eyes were flint. "Men have tried. You read what happened to the last ones."

Dara pulled out a chair and sat down heavily, and for a moment she looked ten years older than the woman who'd landed at the float base with money and a satellite map and an apology nobody wanted.

"My backers," she said. "The consortium. They funded me because they had the stolen map — the false one. They think I'm leading them to a strike that doesn't exist. But Marek—" She stopped. Her face changed. "Marek photographed everything at the wreck site. Marek has been reporting since day one. If he's confirmed the real valley—"

"He has," June said grimly. "He photographed the wreck like an insurance adjuster. He's been building a case since the float base."

"Then they don't need me anymore," Dara said. "And they don't need the false map. They need witnesses silenced and coordinates confirmed, and there is a retrieval team—" She caught herself, glanced at Ilsa, and finished differently. "There are people coming. Soon. Men whose job is to make problems disappear into paperwork."

Ilsa absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain. Then she looked at Felix, and June watched the old woman weigh him — the shaking hands, the red eyes, the dead friend's rocking chair still warm under him — and find, apparently, something worth weighing.

"You knew him," Ilsa said. "Before."

"Twenty-two years ago," Felix said. "We roped up together on the Serrac icefield. He pulled me out of a crevasse at fourteen thousand feet and never let me forget it." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "I spent ten years thinking I'd abandoned him on that ridge. Turns out I abandoned him at a dinner table in a city he'd already decided to leave. Either way, Ilsa — I owe him a debt I can't pay him. So I'll pay it forward or die stupid trying."

Ilsa studied him a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, the smallest motion, and June understood that something enormous had just been decided in a language the rest of them weren't fluent in.

"Then hear the law of this valley," Ilsa said. "Not today. Today you eat, and you rest, and you sit with your dead friend's ghost, because grief that isn't given time becomes poison. Tomorrow, the valley asks its question." She gathered the tin box, the letters, the journal — then paused, and set the journal back on the table in front of Felix, deliberately. "Tonight, this stays with you. Read every page. Know exactly what you're deciding to protect or destroy. That is also the law. No man chooses blind."

She left. Ansel lingered in the doorway a moment longer, looking at the photograph above the stove, at the young laughing stranger who shared his blood, and then at Felix with an expression that was equal parts challenge and hunger — the look of a boy who had grown up on stories of the outside world and never once been allowed to touch it. Then he too was gone, boots fading down the trail.

* * *

Evening came down through the birches like blue water filling a basin. Nobody lit lamps. They sat in the cabin — Felix in the rocking chair with the journal, Dara at the table with the letters, reading them one by one and laying each finished page facedown with funeral care — and June went out to check the plane's radio relay and found Sami waiting at the treeline, having hiked up alone against orders, breathless, face pale in the dusk.

"Sami. You're supposed to be watching Marek."

"Marek's fine. Cole's sitting on him." The kid caught his breath. "June — I need to show you something. On the ridge above camp. I saw it an hour ago and I've been arguing with myself ever since about whether it was real."

"Show me."

Sami led her up through the trees to a granite shoulder, and pointed north, up-valley, toward the high country where the monsoon clouds were beginning to stack themselves against the peaks like grey freight cars coupling onto an endless train.

Above the treeline, far off, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Deliberate. A pattern.

"Signal mirror?" June said. "Sun's nearly down."

"Too regular. Too bright." Sami's voice was thin. "June, that's a helo nav light. Running dark, running high, coming up the valley chain from the coast." The light blinked again — closer now, or maybe just clearer — and Sami swallowed. "They're not coming tomorrow."
