## 11. Marek Moves

Cole had never trusted a man who folded his socks.

Twenty-two years of hauling freight and bodies across the interior taught you to read people the way you read weather — not by what they said, but by the small pressures they left on the air. Felix was a man who crumpled. June rolled hers into tight cylinders and jammed them in a footlocker like she was loading shells. Sami, the kid, still balled everything up because nobody had ever told him better. But Marek — Marek pressed his socks flat along a seam, squared the fold, and laid them in his pack with the precision of a surgeon closing.

Nobody that neat was out here for their health.

Cole mentioned it to June on the third night at Tom's cabin, low, while they stacked firewood against the coming rain. She'd grunted and said she'd noticed the same thing about his boots — cleaned every evening, laces coiled just so, even when there was mud to the knee and no one watching but trees. A man who kept discipline for an audience of forest was keeping discipline for something else entirely.

So when Cole woke at 0240 to the sound of a zipper moving slow as syrup, he wasn't surprised. He was ready.

He'd taken the last watch on purpose. The cabin sat on a bench of ground above the river, one room and a loft, built from timber Tom Reyes must have felled himself — the joints hand-cut, tight as a Bible. Dara slept by the stove. Sami was dead to the world on the floor near the door, arm flung over his eyes. Felix had gone out at dusk with Ilsa's grandson Ansel to look at the portage route below the falls and hadn't come back yet; Cole told himself he wasn't worried about that either, and failed.

Marek's bedroll was empty.

Cole lay still in the dark, breathing through his mouth, listening past the sound of the river. There — the soft scrape of a boot sole on plank, near the corner where they'd built the cache shelf. Where the journal lived.

They'd found the journal two days ago in a box under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, and it had changed the shape of everything. Tom Reyes hadn't died lost. He'd died *staying* — twenty years married into Ilsa's people, and a final winter spent writing down what he knew: the strike, the false coordinates he'd fed the survey companies, the valley he'd mischarted on purpose so well that even the satellite maps still carried his lie. Felix had read it twice and then put it away like a man putting away a loaded gun.

It had been sitting on the cache shelf since yesterday, waiting for a decision nobody wanted to make.

Cole slid his hand under his own bedroll and closed it around the wrench he kept there — thirty inches of chrome vanadium, cold and familiar. He rose without sound, which at his size was a trick he'd paid for with decades of practice, and moved along the wall where the boards didn't talk.

The loft ladder creaked anyway. It always did.

Below him, the scraping stopped.

For three full seconds the cabin held its breath. Then the sounds resumed — faster now, less careful, paper whispering against paper. Pages being separated. Cole came down the ladder with the wrench low against his thigh and got his first look at Marek in the gray wash of moonlight through the window: kneeling at the cache shelf, the journal open beside him, a slim leather folder already fat with stolen pages. His headlamp was off. He was working by feel, which meant he'd done this before, in places where light meant death.

"Marek," Cole said quietly.

The man didn't jump. That was the thing Cole remembered afterward, the detail that made his skin crawl years later — Marek simply went still, the way water goes still, and then began very slowly to close the journal.

"You're up early," Marek said. His voice was mild. Mild as milk.

"Put them back."

"I'm just reviewing—"

"Your hands are shaking and you're packing pages into a folder you brought from home." Cole stepped into the middle of the room, between Marek and the door. "Geologists don't carry document folders, Marek. Geologists don't clean their boots for the trees. Put. Them. Back."

In the corner, Dara stirred. "What is—" Her voice cracked awake mid-word, and then she saw it: Marek kneeling, the folder, the journal. Cole watched her face go through three seasons in one second — confusion, comprehension, and something colder than either.

"Marek." Just his name. But the temperature in the room dropped ten degrees.

He stood up slowly, hands visible, the folder still in his left. "You should understand something," he said, and now the milkiness was gone from his voice, replaced by something flat and professional, a voice used to conference rooms and quiet threats. "This was always the mission. Dr. Okonkwo's grief was a useful instrument, nothing more. The consortium funded this expedition to recover those pages. Everything else — the plane wreck story, the sentimentality — was cover."

"Tom Reyes was my—" Dara started forward and stopped herself, fists white.

"Yes," Marek said. "Which is why they chose you."

Cole had seen men swing first and think later, and he'd seen the other kind, the dangerous kind, who talked while their hands drifted. Marek was drifting. Toward the window, Cole realized. Toward the pack leaning against the sill.

"The radio," Cole said. "It's in the pack, isn't it. You've been calling it in every night."

Marek smiled. It was the worst thing Cole had seen all year. "Every night since the lake. They're forty hours out. Maybe less, if the weather holds for them the way it won't hold for us."

Cole moved.

He wasn't fast anymore — the knees had opinions, the shoulder had history — but he was heavy and he was committed, and he crossed the cabin like a rockslide. Marek got the window open and had the pack half-lifted when Cole hit him. They went through the sill together in an explosion of splinters and old glass, out into the mud and the fern brake below the cabin, and the pack landed somewhere in the dark with a sound of breaking that Cole would remember with satisfaction for the rest of his life.

Marek fought like a man who'd been trained and hoped nobody would notice. He drove an elbow into Cole's ear that rang the whole sky, got a knee up, twisted toward the pack. Cole caught him by the collar and the belt and simply fell on him, all two hundred and sixty pounds, driving him face-first into the dirt. The folder burst. Pages fanned out across the mud like pale leaves, and both of them froze for half a heartbeat — twenty years of a dead man's handwriting, soaking in the wet.

Then Marek screamed for help. In another language. To nobody.

"He's got a transmitter!" Cole roared back into the cabin. "Sami! The pack!"

Boots on the floorboards above. Sami came down the ruined ladder too fast and half-fell into the yard, and to his credit the kid went straight for the pack instead of the fight, dragging it away into the light spilling from the broken window. Cole heard him tearing at buckles, heard a plastic case crack open, heard Sami say, in a small, horrified voice: "It's already transmitting. There's a light on. It's — oh no. Oh no, it's been on."

"Smash it," Cole said, pinning Marek's wrist against the mud. "Sami. Smash it."

A pause. Then the crack of the radio case against a stone, once, twice, a third time, until the little green light inside went dark forever and Sami stood panting among the pieces like a boy who'd just killed something.

Under him, Marek stopped struggling. Not defeated — calculating. Cole could feel the change in the man's body, the way you feel weather shift. He leaned close.

"How far out did you tell them we were?"

Marek spat mud. "Close enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get." And then, softer, almost kindly, which was somehow worse: "You should know it doesn't matter what you do to me now. The retrieval team has its own navigation. They have the original expedition records — the ones your friend Reyes thought he destroyed. My job was only ever confirmation." He turned his head and looked at Dara, standing in the broken window with the moon behind her. "He loved you, you know. He wrote about you. I read every page. It made the assignment easier."

Dara made a sound Cole had never heard from a human throat before, and for a moment he honestly believed she was going to kill him right there in the mud. Instead she turned away, shoulders rigid, and started gathering the scattered pages out of the wet, one by one, with hands that shook.

Cole hauled Marek upright. The man's ankle had turned wrong under him in the fall — Cole felt it give, felt the man's weight shift sickeningly — and Marek cried out, a real cry this time, and collapsed against the cabin wall, cradling his leg.

"Congratulations," Cole told him. "Now you're cargo."

* * *

They worked the rest of the night by lamplight, and nobody suggested sleeping again.

June splinted the ankle without tenderness, using a tent stake and a strip of canvas, while Marek sat on the floor and watched her with the detached interest of a man taking notes for a report. Felix came back with Ansel an hour after dawn, took in the broken window, the dead radio in pieces on the table, the mud-stained pages drying in rows, and asked exactly one question:

"How long?"

"Forty hours, maybe less," Cole said. "If they've got their own nav."

Felix looked at the journal for a long time. Then at Ilsa's grandson, who stood in the doorway with rain beginning to mist down behind him, patient as stone. Then at the sky stacking itself gray and enormous over the western peaks.

"Then we're leaving today," Felix said. "All of us. All of it. And Marek rides in the middle where everyone can see him."

"There's a problem with 'all of it,'" June said quietly. She nodded at the plane, invisible beyond the trees, and at the fuel drums, and at the weight math none of them had spoken aloud yet. "There's always a problem with all of it."

Felix ran a hand through his hair and, for one unguarded moment, looked ten years older than the man who'd signed Cole's contract. "Then somebody figure out what we can live without," he said. "Because I'm not leaving anything else in this valley that the wrong people can find."

Outside, the rain thickened. Downriver, faint beneath the water-sound, Cole thought he heard something that might have been thunder and might have been rotors, and decided — for the sake of the next few hours, for the sake of everyone's hands staying steady — that it was thunder.

He picked up the first fuel drum and started counting what they could live without.
