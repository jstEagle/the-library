## 12. The Consortium

They carried Marek into Tom's cabin because it was the only room with a door that shut.

June had splinted his forearm where Cole had wrenched it against the cabin's corner post — a clean break or close to it, the kind of injury Marek had probably catalogued in some risk-assessment memo before he ever boarded the Otter. He sat on Tom Reyes's old bunk with his arm bound in a sling made from a flour sack, and he did not look at the journal pages stacked on the table between them. That was the first thing Dara noticed. A guilty man would have looked. Marek looked at her instead, patient as an accountant waiting for a meeting to start.

His satellite phone lay on the table beside the pages, its casing cracked open by Cole's boot heel, the antenna bent like a broken reed. Cole stood by the door with his arms crossed and his shoulder still bleeding through a field dressing. Felix leaned against the wall beneath the window, out of the lamplight, saying nothing. Sami hovered in the doorway until June pulled him back outside — this was not a conversation for rookies, though everyone understood it was exactly that: a conversation about what they were now inside of.

Dara pulled the chair from under the table, turned it, and sat facing Marek with her knees almost touching his.

"Talk," she said.

"Dr. Okonkwo." His voice was level, faintly accented, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this moment in a hotel bar somewhere and found it went better than expected. "I want to say first that nothing I've done was—"

"Don't." She held up one hand. "Whatever it is — professional obligation, contractual necessity, following orders — save it. You're going to tell me who you work for, what you told them, and what's coming. In that order. And then we're going to decide what happens to you."

Marek glanced at the ruined phone. "You've destroyed my only means of—"

"I know what I destroyed." She let a beat pass. "That's why you're going to talk."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not fear exactly, but recalculation. Dara had spent fifteen years in boardrooms and negotiation suites on four continents, and she knew the difference between a man deciding whether to lie and a man deciding how much truth he could afford. Marek was doing arithmetic. She let him do it.

Outside, the wind had come around to the north, dragging the smell of snow off the high country even though it was barely autumn. The monsoon was stacking itself somewhere beyond the ridgeline; Felix had said so at dinner without looking at anyone, the way he said all the true things.

"You funded this expedition," Marek began carefully. "Through Meridian Frontier Capital. My employer is—"

"Your employer *is* Meridian Frontier Capital," Dara said. "I've read their filings. A holding company, a geologist, a charter contract. That's the structure I signed. So explain to me why your gear list included a radiation survey meter, a rock core extraction kit rated for hard mineral sampling, and encrypted comms. Explain to me why you photographed that wreck site the way a claims adjuster photographs evidence. And explain to me why, on our third night out, you made a call when you thought everyone was asleep."

She watched him absorb the fact that Sami had seen the call. Watched him decide, finally, that the ledger no longer balanced in favor of silence.

"My employer," he said, "is Halvard Resources Group."

The name landed in the cabin like a dropped tool. Cole shifted by the door. Felix didn't move at all, but Dara felt the quality of his stillness change — the stillness of a man listening very hard.

Halvard. She knew the name the way anyone in resource finance knew it: a private consortium, Dutch-registered, quietly enormous, famous for arriving in frontier basins eighteen months before governments realized there was anything to govern. They didn't do press releases. They did feasibility studies that became mines that became towns that became dependencies. And they had, three years ago, been named in a leaked due-diligence memo as the silent partner behind two separate "community development" initiatives in exactly the drainage basin they were sitting in right now.

"Halvard funded Meridian," she said slowly. "Meridian funded me."

"Halvard funded *you*," Marek corrected, and there was something almost gentle in it, which made it worse. "Dr. Okonkwo. Your proposal crossed my desk fourteen months ago. The downed survey plane, the lost expedition, the personal connection — it was compelling. It was also, frankly, a gift. A licensed medical and salvage charter, crewed by a pilot with ten years of history in this exact corridor, requesting overflight permits for a valley no government has surveyed since the eighties. Do you know how long it would have taken us to justify that flight plan through legitimate channels? Years. You got us in through the front door carrying a first-aid kit."

Dara heard her own pulse in her ears. "The map I brought. The route."

"Your route was genuine. We simply made sure you'd choose it." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "The archival material your proposal cited — the expedition records, the mischarted coordinates — those came to you through a research grant. Meridian administered the grant."

She thought of the archive clerk in Whitehorse, the helpful young man who had pulled the 1970s survey folios for her, who had seemed so pleased someone was finally interested. Fourteen months ago. Before she'd ever written the proposal. Before—

"The letter," Felix said from the wall. His voice was quiet and terrible. "The signature. Someone sent her a letter with Tom's handwriting on it."

Marek looked at him for the first time. "We located Mr. Reyes's effects through a probate inquiry two years ago. A death certificate filed by a regional authority. We acquired copies of correspondence. One page of his hand was sufficient." He paused. "We understood Dr. Okonkwo's history with the original expedition. We understood her belief that Mr. Reyes survived. It was... efficient."

*Efficient.* Dara stood up so fast the chair went over backward, and Cole caught her arm, and she shook him off, and stood there shaking — not with grief, though the grief was there, vast and cold underneath everything, but with something hotter and cleaner rising through it.

They had used Tom's handwriting. They had taken a dead man's letters out of a filing cabinet and mailed a fragment of him to her like bait on a hook, knowing she would follow it anywhere, knowing grief made her predictable, knowing that ten years of guilt and love could be converted into flight hours and access. She had sat in a hotel room in Whitehorse with that letter and wept, and somewhere in a glass tower in Rotterdam a portfolio manager had logged the expenditure as *reconnaissance, phase one.*

"They're coming," she said. Her voice came out flat. "You called them. Before Cole broke your radio. What did you tell them?"

Marek hesitated.

"Marek." She crouched to his eye level. "Here is what you need to understand about your situation. Your radio is gone. Your team — whoever they are — will arrive expecting a man on the ground with confirmed coordinates and a cooperative local asset. Instead they'll find a wrecked strip, a hostile crew, and you with a broken arm. Whatever story you gave them, the facts on the ground have already diverged from it. The only variable left in your favor is whether you told them the truth about the strike. Because if you lied — if you inflated it, if you sold them certainty you don't have — then when this goes wrong, and it has already gone wrong, the people who sent you will need someone to blame. And you are the only asset here they can write off."

She let that sit. Around them the cabin ticked and settled, Tom Reyes's home, built by his hands, sheltering the men who had come to strip-mine his grave.

"What did you tell them?" she asked again, softer.

Marek closed his eyes. When he opened them, the accountant was gone and something tired and human looked out.

"A vein," he said. "Cobalt-nickel association with platinum group metals, exposed at surface along a fault contact. High grade. I told them high grade, based on the samples referenced in the journal pages I photographed at the wreck site cache." He swallowed. "I told them the deposit was accessible. River barge to the lower falls, winter road potential on the east bench. I told them indigenous occupancy was minimal and unorganized — a few families, no title claims, no legal standing." He stopped.

"And?"

"And I told them the window closes with the monsoon. Ten days, maybe twelve, before weather seals the high country until spring. I recommended immediate retrieval of the documentation and a follow-up ground survey in the spring thaw." He looked up at her. "They don't wait for spring, Dr. Okonkwo. Halvard doesn't do follow-up surveys. If the data says go, they go. There's a helicopter on a barge on the river system right now — has been since we crossed the last lake. I confirmed it the night I made the call. Eighteen hours out, maybe twenty-four if the weather holds them up. Six men, a site assessor, and security."

"Security," Cole repeated from the door, in the tone of a man translating a foreign word.

"Contractors," Marek said. "Former military. They've done this before — in West Africa, in Central Asia. Their instruction set is simple: secure the documentation, secure the site, neutralize any obstacle to either." He met Dara's eyes. "Including us. Including you. I'm not certain my status was explained to them clearly."

Felix pushed off the wall at last. He crossed to the table, picked up one of the journal pages — Tom's handwriting marching across it in tight, careful lines, the numbers of a false survey woven through the prose like a code — and held it where Marek could see it.

"This," Felix said. "This is what they want. Pages describing a strike that doesn't exist — that Tom invented, mischarted, buried. Twenty years of fake coordinates. Even if they take every page, they'll drill a mountain for nothing."

"They won't believe it's nothing," Marek said. "Not immediately. Not after the money's spent. Halvard has never written off a project in its history. They'll keep coming back. Spring after spring. Survey after survey. Until the valley is roads and tailings ponds and a company town, whether the ore is there or not — because admitting the error costs more than the drilling does." He said it without pride. "I've watched them do it twice."

The lamp flickered. Outside, June's voice rose briefly — Sami asking something, June answering sharp — and fell again. Dara became aware that her hands had stopped shaking. That was the strange thing about rage, she thought. It burned through the fog of the last two weeks — the letter, the contract, the polite lies in the hangar, her own rehearsed apology to Felix that had been, God help her, partly scripted by people whose names she hadn't known — and left the landscape clear for the first time.

She had come here for a grave. For Tom. To kneel at a cairn and say the things she hadn't said on the ridge ten years ago when they'd turned back and he'd walked into the mist and she had let the expedition vote and kept her mouth shut because the money was gone and the season was dying. And instead she had delivered his valley, his family, his twenty years of quiet life and good death, into the hands of men who filed grief under *reconnaissance.*

The enemy finally had a face, and the face wore hers.

"We have eighteen hours," Felix said. He wasn't looking at Marek anymore. He was looking at the window, at the black wall of the forest, at the sky above the ridge where the stars were going out one by one behind incoming cloud. "Maybe less, if the weather moves faster than they do."

"You can't outrun a helicopter," Cole said.

"No," Felix agreed. "But we don't have to outrun it. We have to make sure that when it lands, there's nothing here worth landing for." He turned to Dara, and for the first time since the hangar — maybe for the first time in ten years — he looked at her the way he used to look at her across a chart table, back when they'd been partners instead of rivals, back when trust was a thing they spent freely because they hadn't yet learned its price. "Those pages. The real ones. Where are they?"

Dara held his gaze. "Ilsa has them. Has had them since the night we arrived. She took them out of my pack while I slept, and she never gave them back, and I never asked." She almost smiled, and it hurt. "I think part of me hoped she would."

Felix nodded slowly, as if a long equation had finally resolved. Then his expression hardened into something she recognized from the worst year of her life — the expedition's final week, the decisions made in cold air by lamplight.

"Then we have a choice to make," he said. "All of us. And not much time to make it." He picked up the lamp and moved toward the door, then paused, looking back at Marek with an expression that held neither forgiveness nor cruelty — only arithmetic. "And you're going to help us. Because the helicopter that's coming doesn't know you failed. As far as Halvard knows, you're their man on the ground. Which means, Marek, that for the next eighteen hours, you're ours."

Outside, the first rain hit the roof — fat, scattered drops, scouts ahead of the monsoon's main column — and far to the south, beyond the ridges, beyond hearing, Dara imagined she could feel the beat of rotors turning in the dark.

* * *

They gathered in the main lodge an hour later, and Dara stood before Ilsa and her people and told them everything — the funding, the forgery, the helicopter, the contractors with their simple instruction set. She expected anger. What she got was worse: nods. Ilsa listened with her hands folded, and when Dara finished, the old woman said only, "Prospectors came before. We buried two of ours and none of theirs. Now you say more are coming, with guns and a machine that flies." She looked around the firelit room at her family. "So. We decide again."

And Dara understood, standing in the heat of that fire with the rain beginning in earnest against the hide-and-timber walls, that whatever happened in the next eighteen hours would not be decided by contracts or companies or the law of any nation. It would be decided here, by these people, by this fire — and that she had finally, too late and exactly on time, chosen her side.

Ilsa's grandson Ansel was already pulling a rifle down from the rack above the door.
