## 13. Ilsa's Law

They gathered in the longhouse at dusk, and Felix understood within the first minute that this was not a meeting. It was a trial.

The building sat on stilts above the river's edge, cedar and spruce poles silvered by decades of weather, its roof thatched so thickly it seemed grown rather than built. Inside, a fire pit ran down the center of the floor, and the smoke rose through a gap in the ridgepole into a sky already bruising toward rain. Perhaps forty people filled the benches along the walls — faces from every decade, children peering between their mothers' ribs, old men with hands like tree roots folded in their laps. They had come quietly, without being summoned, the way people come to something they have decided long ago.

Ilsa stood at the head of the room with her back to the fire, and the light through the smoke made her seem carved out of it.

Felix sat where they'd been placed — the five of them on a bench near the door, close enough to leave if it came to leaving, far enough from the fire that the warmth only reached them as an accusation. June sat rigid beside him. Cole's arm was bound now, properly splinted by one of the valley women who worked with a competence that made Felix think of field hospitals, and he'd gone gray around the mouth but kept his silence. Sami watched everything with the stillness of a man memorizing exits. Dara sat apart from them, one bench over, and Felix noticed that no one had told her to move — she had simply chosen the distance herself, and the valley people had allowed it, which meant they had already measured her and found her wanting in some way he couldn't yet name.

Marek was not present. He lay in a small hut across the compound, feverish, his leg wrapped where Cole's boot had caught him, guarded by two boys who looked barely old enough to hold the knives they carried. The journal pages were gone from him — Ilsa's people had taken them before anyone thought to ask — and Felix had spent the walk here trying to decide whether that frightened him or relieved him.

It did both.

"You came up the river," Ilsa said. Her voice carried without effort, low and dry as gravel shifting underfoot. "You found his cabin. You read what he left."

It wasn't a question, but she waited anyway, and after a moment Felix nodded. "We did."

"Tomás wrote for ten years knowing someone would come." She turned her head slightly, addressing the room as much as him. "He said it would be either friends or money. He hoped friends. He prepared for money." A pause. "You are which?"

The question hung in the smoke. Felix felt the crew go still around him.

"Neither," he said finally. "Both. We came for a grave we didn't know existed. We're leaving with one we do."

A murmur moved through the benches like wind through grass. Ilsa let it run its course.

"My grandfather trapped this river before there were charts," she said. "When the survey men came, twenty-two years ago, they hired our men as guides and porters and promised shares. Shares." Something moved at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile. "Six went in. Four came back. The two who stayed — one was Tomás. The other was my uncle's son, Eero. He married here. He raised children here. When the second party came looking — prospectors, three years ago, with a helicopter and a permit signed by men who have never seen this river — Eero met them at the falls and told them the valley was empty and the rock was worthless."

She stopped. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a dog began to bark and was silenced by a hand.

"They did not believe him," Ilsa said.

No one else spoke. The silence had weight, texture, the specific gravity of a thing long carried. Felix realized his hands had closed into fists on his knees.

"He fell," Ilsa continued, "from the high ledge above the lower rapids. That is what the report would say, if there had been a report. There was no report. There was a helicopter leaving fast, and a body in the water, and a widow, and four children. The men who killed him flew home to their offices and their permits, and the world asked them nothing, because the world does not know this valley exists." Her eyes found Felix's and held them, pale and unhurried. "That is the law you are sitting inside tonight. Not your law. Ours. It says: what is hidden stays hidden, and those who carry it out answer to us, because no one else will ever come to collect."

June's breath left her slowly through her nose. Beside Felix, Sami had stopped pretending to be casual; he was watching Ilsa the way he watched weather fronts.

"We didn't know," Dara said. Her voice was rough. "About Eero. About any of it."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "But you came with a man whose gear held a radio keyed to frequencies no geologist uses, and pages torn from a dead man's book, and you did not know that either until it was too late to matter. Not-knowing is how these things always arrive. It arrived for Eero the same way. Polite men with questions." She stepped back from the fire, and the light released her face into shadow. "So. We put it to you plainly, because Tomás would have wanted it plain."

An old man rose from the front bench — Eero's brother, Felix guessed, from the family resemblance and the way the younger ones deferred — and took a position at Ilsa's shoulder. He held nothing. He needed to hold nothing.

"The mountain above us holds metal," the old man said. His English was slower than Ilsa's, each word set down like a stone. "Copper. Silver. Enough that men will burn this forest to own it. Tomás mapped it wrong on purpose, and then he hid the true map, and then he died keeping it hidden, and now it lives in the heads of maybe six people in this room and in pages that my niece has locked away." He looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, giving every face its full weight. "You have seen enough to find it again. That cannot be undone. So there are two roads, and you must choose one before you sleep."

He raised one finger.

"The first road: you swear, here, before all of us, that what you saw stays buried. You fly out carrying nothing — no samples, no coordinates, no photographs — and you never file a flight plan over this river again. In exchange, you are welcome here as long as any of us live. Your fuel comes from our cache. Your wounded man is nursed. Our canoes and our knowledge go with you when the monsoon comes, because we do not want your bones on our river any more than we want their helicopters." The finger lowered. "And if you break that oath — if a mining company files a claim on this watershed within your lifetime — we will know whose word broke, and we will know where to look for the one who broke it. Not to punish." His voice did not change at all. "To know. That is worse, for a man like you. To be known."

The second finger rose.

"The second road: you take it out. The data, the strike, the truth about Tomás Reyes and the men who shot down that plane. You sell it or you publish it or you spend it however you wish. And you will be rich, some of you, briefly. But you may never come back here. Not you, not your children, not your fuel drums, not your medicine. This valley closes to you forever, and everything in it — including the grave you came for — becomes a place you remember instead of a place you belong."

The old man sat down. The fire burned. Forty faces watched them, and none of them blinked, and Felix understood that the verdict had already been reached among these people days ago — probably the moment Marek's radio crackled — and that this whole evening was theater only in the sense that a hanging is theater. The rope was real. The drop was real. The only open question was which side of it they'd stand on.

"I want to say something," Dara said.

Isla's eyes flicked to her. "Say it."

"I funded this expedition to find a man, not a mine." Dara stood, and Felix saw her legs were unsteady and admired her for standing anyway. "I was lied to by the people who financed me, and I'll deal with that. But I want you to hear it from me, not from suspicion: whatever the others choose, I choose the grave. I choose Tom. There is nothing under that mountain I want more than I want his letter to reach the people he loved." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I'm sorry it took a dead man's journal to make me honest. But I am honest now."

Silence. Then Ilsa said, "Your apology is late by twenty years and worth having anyway," and something in the room loosened half a degree.

Felix looked down the bench. June gave him a single nod — *you already know my answer* — and Sami shrugged with one shoulder, which from Sami was a speech. Cole, gray and sweating, said quietly, "Triple pay doesn't buy a new valley, boss. First road."

Felix stood. His knees ached. Ten years ago he had walked out of this country one man short and sworn to silence, and the oath had eaten him alive from the inside ever since, and here was the strange arithmetic of it: the way out of the past was deeper into it. Deeper into the promise he should have made the first time and hadn't.

"Ten years ago," he said, "my partner walked into that mist and I told myself he was dead, because dead was easier than abandoned. He wasn't dead. He lived twenty years up here, better than I've lived, and he died protecting the same thing you're asking me to protect." He looked at Ilsa. "I don't swear oaths lightly anymore. But I'll give you this one, and I'll keep it, because I've learned what breaking one costs." He turned to face the room. "First road. All of us. What's under that mountain stays under it. And when we fly out, we fly out empty."

The old man studied him for a long moment, then looked at Ilsa, and Ilsa nodded once, and the sound that went through the longhouse was not applause — it was quieter than that, exhaling, settling, the sound of a verdict accepted by everyone at once.

Then Ilsa crossed the floor and stood in front of Felix, closer than she had come all evening, and lowered her voice so only he could hear.

"There is one more thing," she said. "Eero's death was not investigated. But the men who killed him — they logged their flight plan. They filed it in the coastal station under a company name, and my grandson copied it before they burned the rest. Vossen's company. The same men are coming back, Felix Whitlock. My boys watched their helicopter refuel at the lake yesterday." Her hand closed briefly on his forearm, hard as a trap. "You chose the first road. Good. Now understand what it costs: the oath protects you from us. It protects you from nothing else. They will be here by tomorrow night, and they will not sit in a longhouse and listen."

* * *

Outside, the rain had begun — the true rain, the monsoon's outriders, fat drops ticking against the broad leaves — and the crew stood under the longhouse eave while the valley darkened around them.

"Well," Cole said at last. "That went better than expected."

Nobody laughed. Downriver, invisible beyond the black wall of trees, thunder rolled — or perhaps it wasn't thunder.
