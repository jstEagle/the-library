## 14. Splitting the Load

June Okafor had spent twenty years making airplanes do things airplanes weren't supposed to do, and she knew the arithmetic of the sky the way a priest knows scripture: absolutely, and with the understanding that breaking it always cost somebody something.

The arithmetic this morning was ugly.

She stood on the wing of the Otter with her torque wrench still in her hand, doing it again for the fourth time because she kept hoping the numbers would change. They didn't.

"Say it out loud," Felix said from the ground, arms crossed, looking up at her.

"Full fuel is nine hundred pounds of go-juice. You, me, Cole, Sami, Dara, Marek on his stretcher — call it eleven hundred in bodies and gear, and that's before we count the journal pages, the medical kit, the radios we haven't burned yet." She wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "That's over max gross by four hundred pounds, Felix. And that's with a dry strip and calm air. We've got neither."

"And if we dump fuel?"

"Then we don't clear the pass, and the monsoon eats us somewhere over the divide. Either way we're a smoking hole in somebody's forest." She climbed down to the float dock — they'd moved the plane upriver to the gravel bar Ilsa's people used as a landing spot, a narrow shelf of stone and packed silt barely long enough, hemmed in by spruce so tall they made the sky feel like a corridor. "We can't lift everyone. Not all at once. Not with what we're carrying."

Felix was quiet for a long moment. He'd been quiet a lot since Tom's cabin, since the journal, since Ilsa put the valley's law in front of them like a blade laid flat across both palms. But this silence was different. This was the silence of a man running calculations he didn't like.

"Then we split it," he said finally. "The load. The people. The truth."

* * *

They gathered under the tarp behind the cook tent at midday, the whole strange family of them: Felix drawing lines in the dirt with a stick, June with her maintenance log open on her knees, Sami crouched forward on his heels the way he did when he was thinking hard, Cole propped against a supply crate with his arm strapped tight and his color bad but his eyes sharp. Dara sat apart, Marek's bandaged shoulder visible through the tent flap where he lay sedated on a cot. Ansel stood at the edge of the group, silent, watchful, a young man with his grandmother's jaw and none of her patience for outsiders.

"Two lifts," Felix said. "First one takes the weight we can't leave: Cole, Marek, the medical priority, minimum fuel, straight downriver to the coastal station. It's six hours of flying through weather that's getting worse by the hour, but the Otter can do it light."

"Who flies it?" June asked, though she already knew.

"You," Felix said. "You're the only one who can nurse a heavy, sick airplane through that front. And you're the only one Cole trusts to land him without breaking him worse."

Cole made a sound that might have been protest. June looked at him — at the gray in his lips, the sweat despite the cool air, the fever climbing in him like water in a flooding bilge — and he shut his mouth.

"Second lift comes back for the rest of us," Felix went on. "Me, Sami, Dara, Ansel if he'll come, whatever fuel's left. That's tomorrow morning, if the window holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Sami asked. "If the front comes down tonight and seals everything for two weeks? Monsoon season doesn't knock, Captain. It kicks the door."

"Then the rest of us take Tom's route out. River, portage, the boat below the falls. Ilsa says it's been done."

"Ilsa says it was done *once*, thirty years ago, by people who grew up on that river," Ansel said quietly. Everyone turned. He shrugged. "I'm just telling you what my grandmother said when you weren't listening."

"Which is why you're coming with us either way," Felix said. "If the plane goes, you're our guide. If the plane doesn't, you're still our guide."

Ansel held his gaze for a moment, then nodded once, the smallest possible concession.

June watched Sami during all of it. The kid had gone very still, and stillness in Sami was never calm — it was a man holding a door shut against something. When Felix finished laying out the plan, Sami raised his hand like they were back in flight school.

"There's a piece missing," he said. "Vossen's team is inbound. Helicopters, maybe tonight, maybe dawn. They're going to come to the wreck site first, because that's what their satellite imagery shows them and that's what Marek reported. If they find the wreck stripped, the journal gone, fresh tracks everywhere—" He shook his head. "They won't ask questions. They'll just start hunting. And the first thing they'll find is a gravel bar with an Otter sitting on it."

"So we don't let them find that," Felix said.

"We move the plane," June said slowly, the idea assembling itself even as she spoke. "Tonight. Fly it dark, no lights, up to the lake basin above the falls. There's water big enough to land on and trees tall enough to hide under. Vossen's people fly the valley, they see nothing but forest."

"They'll see the wreck site," Sami said. "And here's the part nobody wants to say out loud. Somebody has to stay behind after the first lift and make sure the wreck looks exactly the way Marek's photos said it looked. Undisturbed. Ten years old. Boring." He paused. "I volunteer."

"No," Felix said immediately.

"Captain—"

"It's the riskiest leg of the whole operation, Sami. Alone, on the ground, with an armed retrieval team inbound, no radio once we kill ours to keep them from triangulating—"

"Exactly." Sami's voice didn't rise; it got *quieter*, which was worse. "It needs someone who can read terrain, who knows how their satellite passes work because I'm the one who taught myself, who can fake ten-year-old brush disturbance because I've spent three years studying how forests heal. That's not Cole with a bullet in him. That's not Dara, who they might recognize. That's not you, because if you're caught, this whole thing dies with you." He looked around the circle. "It has to be someone expendable enough that losing them hurts but doesn't end us. That's me. I've known it since Ilsa's law. I've just been waiting for the right moment to say it out loud."

Nobody spoke. The river talked instead, low and constant under everything, the way it had talked for ten thousand years and would talk for ten thousand more.

June broke the silence, because somebody had to and because she'd earned the right. "He goes with a radio. A shortwave, low power, scheduled check-ins only. And he goes with a bolt rifle and Ansel teaching him every hiding place within five miles of that wreck between now and sundown." She looked at Felix. "You want to keep him alive? Then stop pretending solo means alone. This valley has people in it, Felix. People who hate Vossen's kind more than they dislike us. Use that."

Felix stared at her. Then at Sami. Then at the dirt, where his stick had drawn the whole shape of their survival and its price.

"Scheduled check-ins," he said at last. "Every six hours. Miss two, and I come back for you if I have to fly the Otter through a hurricane to do it."

"Understood," Sami said, and there was something in his face June had never seen there before — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has finally found the exact size and shape of the person he's going to be.

* * *

The caching took the rest of the afternoon, and it was the kind of work June loved best: physical, exacting, honest. No politics, no grief, just weight and space and the eternal puzzle of fitting too much into too little.

They dug the cache into the root cellar beneath Tom's old cabin, which Ilsa granted with a nod that managed to be both permission and warning. Fuel drums went nowhere — they drained what they could into the Otter's tanks and buried the empties; the full drums stayed hidden under alder brush a mile downstream, coordinates written in three copies in three pockets. Spare parts, tools, the emergency kit, two weeks of rations: sealed in oilcloth, stacked on pallet stones, covered with gravel and then living moss cut in intact squares and laid back down like sod. In eighteen months you wouldn't find it unless you knew. In ten years, the forest would own it completely.

Dara worked beside her without being asked, hauling drums with a smooth economy of motion that told June this woman had done fieldwork in places harder than this one. Twice their hands met on the same crate. Neither of them mentioned it.

"You were going to tell them eventually," June said during the third load. "About Tom. About being on the expedition."

"Yes."

"But not until you had to."

Dara set the crate down and looked out at the river. "Would you have believed me any sooner?"

June thought about it honestly. "No."

"Then we're not so different, mechanic. We both believe in checking the airframe before we trust it." She picked up the next crate. "How is he? Felix. Really."

"Falling apart," June said. "Same as the rest of us. Difference is he thinks nobody can see the cracks."

"He's wrong."

"Yeah," June said. "He usually is, about that."

* * *

The wreck site at dusk was a lesson in how much a lie costs to build.

They'd stripped it clean that morning — instruments, documents, anything with a serial number or a story — and now they had to put the wilderness back over the wound and make it look like the wilderness had never been disturbed. Sami led it, and watching him work was watching a different man than the nervous rookie who'd shown up eight months ago unable to read a windsock without narrating his own anxiety.

"Brush grows back from the edges," he murmured, half to himself, bending a broken alder stem back toward its root and pinning it with a stone. "You don't replant. You re-tension. Everything that got snapped wants to spring back — you just help it remember where it was going." He worked the stem until it stood at the angle its neighbors stood at, then scattered duff over the scuff marks with a fir bough, sweeping in long strokes, erasing boot prints the way a painter lays a wash.

June hauled the tail section's torn skin back into its torn shape and wired it loosely, rusting wire, aged-looking, the kind of repair a wreck makes of itself. Felix carried the heavy pieces — the ones Marek's photographs showed in place — and set them back within inches of where they'd lain, consulting the printed images by flashlight shielded under a poncho.

"Prop strike," Felix said quietly, crouched at the engine. "Ten years ago. Grass grown through the cowling seams. That's what they need to see. A survey plane that came down hard a decade ago and nobody cared enough to come look." He fitted a panel back into place. "Marek gave them the truth, just not all of it. So we give Vossen the same thing."

"The truth minus the point," June said.

"That's most of what anybody knows about anything."

By full dark the site was perfect. June walked its perimeter twice and couldn't find her own handiwork. The alders had closed ranks. The wreck sat in its clearing looking exactly like what it was supposed to be: old news, dead metal, a question nobody had bothered to ask in years.

Sami stood at the edge of it, pack on, rifle slung, the shortwave's antenna coiled at his hip. He looked smaller in the dark and somehow steadier.

"Check-ins every six hours," Felix said. "Starting 0600."

"0600," Sami agreed. Then, unexpectedly, he grinned — the old crooked grin, the rookie's grin. "Hey, Captain. First instrument approach I ever flew was your simulator, and I flared forty feet high and you called it 'enthusiastic.' I figure if I can survive that review, I can survive a week in the woods."

"Sami—"

"Don't." He stuck out his hand, and Felix took it, and held it a beat longer than handshake. "Bring the plane back tomorrow. Or don't bring it at all and meet me at the falls. Either way—" he shrugged, shouldering his pack, turning toward the treeline where Ansel waited with a lantern hooded to a coal-glow, "—either way, I'll see you downriver."

They watched until the lantern-light swallowed him. June felt Felix breathing beside her, controlled and careful, a man keeping his own airframe together by main strength.

"He'll be fine," she said.

"I know," Felix said, in the voice of a man who didn't know anything of the kind.

* * *

They flew the Otter out at 2300, blacked out, June's hands light on the yoke and Felix calling terrain by memory and moonlight through the broken clouds. The lake basin above the falls took them gently — glass water, mirror-still, the floats kissing down with barely a whisper — and they taxied into a slot between two deadfalls where the overhang hid them from anything above two hundred feet. June rigged the tie-downs to roots, drained a cup of water out of each float, and covered the windows with tarps.

Standing on the float in the black silence afterward, she heard it: the far-off, arrhythmic thrum rolling down from the north, felt in the soles of her boots before her ears caught it properly.

Not thunder. Thunder didn't pulse.

"Helicopters," Felix said beside her. "Two of them, maybe. North rim, heading for the valley floor."

"At midnight?"

"Men like Vossen don't wait for daylight. Daylight is for men with something to lose." He was already moving back along the float, low, fast. "Get to the shore. We hike down to Ilsa's tonight — if they overfly the cabin, I want us scattered and dark. And June—"

"Yeah."

"If those check-ins stop coming—"

"They won't," she said, and stepped off onto the rocks, and started climbing toward the treeline while the rotor-thrum swelled behind them like a second heartbeat, closer now, patient, hungry, sweeping the valley it believed was empty.
