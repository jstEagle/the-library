## 15. Helicopters at Dawn

I heard the helicopters before I saw them, which is how you know a thing is wrong. The valley had been teaching us its sounds for two weeks — the river's constant argument with itself, the wind combing the spruce, Ilsa's people moving through the trees like they were part of the trees. A helicopter doesn't fit in that vocabulary. It's a word from another language, and every living thing in the valley stopped speaking when it heard it.

I was on first watch, sitting on an overturned fuel drum by the cold fire pit, working a whetstone along my belt knife for no reason except that it gave my hands something to do. Cole Mercer, loadmaster, twenty-two years hauling freight into places that didn't want freight. In all those years I'd learned one rule: when machinery comes to the wilderness uninvited, somebody paid for it, and they're coming to collect.

The sound came up the valley from the south, doubled — two rotors, not one. I stood up slow and watched the gray light under the cloud deck, and after a while there they were: two machines flying tight and low, running lights off, riding the river line like they'd been briefed on it.

"Sami," I said, and the kid was already awake. Rookie navigator, slept like a cat with one ear up. He came out of his tent with his boots in one hand, and I pointed, and he went pale in a way I recognized. It's the color you turn when the map stops being paper.

"Felix," he said.

"Get him. Wake June. Nobody runs around. We look like a survey crew having breakfast."

* * *

Felix came out of his tent doing up his jacket, and I'll say this for the man — ten years of guilt had taught him to move calm even when everything behind his eyes was going fast. He looked at the sky, then at me.

"How far?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less if they know the strip."

"They know the strip." He said it flat, like reading off a manifest. "Vossen's been planning this longer than we have."

June was already at the Otter, pulling the covers off the engine cowlings. She caught Felix's eye across camp and he gave her a small nod, nothing more, and she gave him one back, and that whole conversation took half a second because that's how long June needs to understand a man. Then she was inside the plane, and Sami was dragging gear toward the tree line like he'd done it before, and I understood what Felix wanted: the plane gone, or looking gone, or ready to go — anything but sitting on that strip like a duck on a pond.

"Cole," Felix said. "You're with me. Coffee's on. We're four men having coffee."

"We only got three cups."

"Then we're three men having coffee and one man who lost his cup. Look natural."

"I never look natural before six."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then the helicopters came over the ridge and the morning changed hands.

* * *

They were white Bell 212s, corporate clean, no markings but a registration number and a small logo of interlocking chevrons that I didn't recognize and knew anyway. Consortium. They flared over the strip in formation, rotor wash flattening the grass and blowing our fire pit into a small tantrum of ash, and set down fifty meters out with the precision of men who'd rehearsed this landing on a model of the valley.

Four men climbed out. Not soldiers — soldiers are loud. These were quiet. Two stayed by the aircraft, arms folded, wearing jackets too heavy for the season, and the other two walked up the slope toward our camp at an easy pace, unhurried, the way a man walks when he owns the clock.

The one in front was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, dressed like he was about to inspect a vineyard. Clean-shaven, tanned, a small scar through one eyebrow that might have been surgical for all the character it had. He carried nothing. His partner carried everything — a hard case, a radio, and the particular stillness of a man who has done violence as a job and found it neither thrilling nor troubling.

"Cole Mercer?" the silver-haired man said, extending a hand. "Vossen. Henrik Vossen. I believe we've been expected."

"Nobody expects anybody out here," I said, and shook his hand anyway, because refusing would have told him more than shaking did. His grip was dry and warm and exactly calibrated. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"That's rather the point of everywhere, isn't it." He glanced past me at the camp, counting tents, counting faces, arriving at a number and filing it away. "Is Mr. Whitlock available? And Ms. Okonkwo? I've come a very long way to speak with them."

"You've come a long way to speak with whoever's paying you to."

His smile was genuinely pleasant, which made it worse. "Ah. You're the suspicious one. Good. Suspicious crews live longer." He turned as Felix came down the slope, and something passed between the two men — recognition without warmth, the handshake of professionals who'd heard each other's names in rooms where names get priced.

"Mr. Vossen," Felix said.

"Whitlock. Ten years, and you still land on strips that don't exist. Some habits are expensive." Vossen gestured, and his silent partner opened the hard case on the ground between us. Inside, nested in foam, were four satellite phones, a medical kit better than ours, and bricks of cash in currency I couldn't count fast enough. "Let's not waste the morning. You found something in this valley. My employers would like it. They're prepared to pay for the inconvenience — generously. Half now, half on your arrival at the coast. Every member of your crew walks away wealthy, and this valley returns to being a blank space on the chart, where it has lived quite happily for a century."

"And if we say no?" Dara asked. She'd come up behind us without a sound, arms crossed, and I watched Vossen reassess her — the way a man does when the file he was given turns out to be incomplete.

"Then we have a much less pleasant conversation," Vossen said, "about logistics. Fuel. Weather windows. The structural integrity of old aircraft." He said it like discussing catering. "But I'd rather not. Violence is inefficient. It leaves paperwork."

"The journal isn't for sale," Dara said.

"No," Vossen agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Nothing ever is. Everything is rented, at a rate." He looked back at Felix. "You have until I finish my coffee."

* * *

And here's the thing I keep coming back to, lying awake since: Felix stalled him. That was the plan, if you could call it a plan, and I watched it work. Felix started talking — about weather, about the monsoon building south, about how whatever data existed was cached somewhere Vossen wouldn't find it in a season — and Vossen let him talk, sipping coffee from our dented pot like a man at a garden party, because stalling ran both directions. Vossen wasn't here to negotiate. He was here to run out our clock while his other two men did whatever they were doing back at the helicopters.

I drifted toward the firewood pile, which put me at an angle to see the strip. One of the jacketed men had opened the second helicopter's cargo bay and was handing out equipment — long, dark, deliberate shapes. The other was walking the tree line with a handheld unit, sweeping it slowly, reading signal like a man dowsing for water.

Looking for the plane. Looking for us to be stupid enough to have hidden it badly.

Except June doesn't hide things badly. June hides things the way water hides — completely, patiently, everywhere at once. While Vossen drank our bad coffee and Felix fed him weather talk, June and Sami had taken the Otter upriver at first light — before dawn, before the rotors, before any of us knew — and put her down on Tom's old water strip above the falls, under alder cover, fueled just enough to matter, stripped of everything incriminating. What sat on the main strip now was a hollowed-out shell, empty tanks, logbook burned, looking exactly like what Vossen's sweepers would expect: a dead survey aircraft belonging to a failed expedition.

Or so we hoped. Hope is a loadmaster's least favorite cargo. It shifts in transit.

"Your mechanic is resourceful," Vossen said suddenly, not looking at me, and my blood went cold. "Fuel receipts in the village cache. Tire tracks. She left at oh-four-hundred, yes? We have a drone overhead since midnight, Mr. Mercer. We know where your aircraft is." He set down the cup and smiled at Felix with real regret. "Which brings me to the second item on my agenda."

He nodded once. Across the strip, both jacketed men turned toward the tree line, and the silent partner closed the cash case — not all the way, leaving it open like a door left ajar on purpose.

"The offer stands until sundown," Vossen said. "After that, my employers' patience converts to a different currency. You've seen what it buys." He straightened his cuffs. "One more thing, offered free of charge, as a courtesy between professionals. The old woman who leads these people — Ilsa — her community filed a complaint ten years ago about a death on this river. It was never investigated. My employers acquired the file last month." He smiled again, warmer. "Files can be reopened. Or buried deeper. Everything is rented, Mr. Whitlock. Even the past. Especially the past."

He turned to go, then paused, almost as an afterthought.

"Oh — and tell your young navigator that satellite phones broadcast on frequencies even children can buy scanners for. Marek's call was received. By us. Before it was received by anyone else." He walked down the slope toward the helicopters, unhurried, owning the clock, and called back over his shoulder without turning: "Sundown, gentlemen. Do think about your families. Everyone has them. That's usually sufficient."

* * *

The rotors wound up and the Bells lifted, circled once over the valley — showing us they could, showing us the falls, showing us the alder bank where a white Otter sat under cover thinking it was hidden — and went south over the ridge.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The ash settled. Somewhere upriver, a loon called, the valley finding its voice again.

"Sundown," Sami said finally. His voice cracked on it.

"He knows about the plane," June said, coming up the slope with grease still on her hands and murder in her eyes. "He knows about Marek's call. He knows about Ilsa's file. That man came here to hand us a receipt, not an offer."

"He came to make us feel bought before we're sold," Dara said quietly. She was staring down the empty sky where the helicopters had gone. "The money was real. The threat was real. Both at once — that's how it works. You take the cash, you're theirs. You refuse it, you're a problem. Either way, sundown ends the conversation."

Felix crouched by the dead fire, and I watched him pick up Vossen's abandoned coffee cup and turn it in his fingers like it might tell him something. When he spoke, his voice was the one I'd learned to trust in bad weather — low, level, no drama in it at all.

"Then we end it first. Cole — get every weapon in this camp cleaned and counted. June, I want that shell of a plane rigged so it burns spectacular if they touch it. Sami—" he looked up, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen in ten years of flying with him, something old and hard and finally awake, "—get on the radio to Ilsa. Tell her the men from the file are here. Tell her it's time to collect."

The valley had gone quiet again. But it wasn't the same quiet as before. This one had teeth in it.

Down at the strip, the empty cash case sat open in the grass, half a million dollars' worth of temptation glinting in the morning light, and nobody moved toward it.

Sundown was eleven hours away.
