## 16. The Runway Fight

They came at dusk, and they came polite.

Felix watched the helicopter come down the valley in the last grey light, running lights off, rotor wash flattening the alders along the strip like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. It set down at the far end of the gravel with the delicacy of something that had done this many times before, and four men stepped out into the gloom. No weapons visible. No hurry. Vossen walked point, hands loose at his sides, a man strolling to a dinner party.

"They're on the strip," Felix said into the radio, low. "Everybody hold."

He was crouched behind the fuel drums at the tree line, two hundred meters from where the Otter sat tied down at the near end of the runway. June was in the cockpit with the engines cold and the panels open — she'd spent the afternoon making the plane look sick, cowling half-removed, tools scattered like an abandoned diagnosis. Sami was up-slope in the rocks with the flare gun and a rifle he'd never fired at anything but tin cans. Cole had taken the left flank along the riverbank with Dara, who carried Tom Reyes's old lever-action like she'd been born to it.

And Marek lay in the tent under a tarp, his leg splinted, drifting in and out of fever from the wound Cole had given him. The radio that would have called the helicopter home was a box of shattered glass and bent circuitry buried in the muck by the creek.

So Vossen's employers knew only one thing: their man had stopped reporting. And men who stop reporting get retrieved, along with whatever they'd found.

The four figures crossed the strip unhurried. One peeled off toward the wreck of the survey plane in the alder brush; two came on toward the camp; Vossen walked straight for the fuel drums, straight for Felix, as if he'd known all along exactly where everyone was standing.

"Mr. Whitlock." His voice carried easily across the flat evening air. He was a compact man, silver at the temples, wearing a shell jacket too clean for this country. "I'd hoped we could do this without the theatrics. Come out where I can see you."

Felix stood up slowly, hands visible. Behind him the forest breathed, dark and indifferent.

"You're trespassing," Felix said. "On a strip that isn't charted, in a valley that isn't yours."

"Everything is charted somewhere, Mr. Whitlock. Everything is someone's." Vossen smiled the way a bank manager smiles. "Where is my geologist?"

"Alive. Barely. Your man tried to rob us and got hurt doing it."

"That's unfortunate. Marek was expensive." Vossen said it without irony, which somehow made it worse. "Here is how tonight goes. You give me the journal pages, any survey data, and my colleague's coordinates for extraction. In exchange, you keep your aircraft, your contracts, your licenses — yes, we know about the licensing irregularities, Mr. Whitlock, the medevac permits you've been stretching for three years — and you fly out of here tomorrow as wealthy men. There's a number attached to silence. It's a generous number."

"And if we don't?"

Vossen's smile didn't move but something behind it did, the way ice shifts on a lake before it cracks. "Then the number changes. It becomes a different kind of figure entirely."

* * *

Later, Felix would try to reconstruct the exact moment the night went wrong, and he never could pin it. That was the nature of fights like this one: no clean beginning, just a seam splitting.

It split when the man who'd gone to the wreck site called out softly from the alder brush — a single word, a name or a code, and then the crack of a branch. Sami's flare went up from the rocks a heartbeat later, whether because he'd seen movement or because his nerves simply gave out, Felix never learned. The sky turned white-green and strobing, and in that terrible flickering light everything became silhouette and shadow, and shadows were shooting at each other.

The first shot hit the fuel drum six feet from Felix's head.

He didn't decide to move; his body decided for him, slamming flat into the gravel while the second round shrieked off the drum's rim and the third punched through the metal above him with a sound like a struck bell. Gasoline began to whisper out of the puncture in a thin silver thread, pooling around his elbows, and Felix understood with perfect clarity that he was lying in the fuse of a bomb.

"June!" he roared. "Get her started! Get her moving!"

The Otter's engine coughed, caught, coughed again. Across the strip, muzzle flashes stuttered from the riverbank — Cole and Dara, giving them something else to look at. Felix rolled away from the spreading gasoline, gravel chewing his forearms, and came up behind the second row of drums. The flare light died. Darkness rushed back in, thicker than before, full of breathing and small desperate sounds.

A flashlight beam swept the tree line. Felix saw the shooter clearly for one second: young, kneeling, rifle shouldered professionally, sweeping left to right with the patience of a man who'd done this work on other continents. The beam found the rocks where Sami was.

Felix did the arithmetic without wanting to. If that beam settled, Sami was dead. If Felix shot the man, the man might be dead, and Felix Whitlock had gone forty-nine years without killing anyone and had told himself stories about why that mattered.

He grabbed the road flare from his jacket pocket — the one he'd carried since the coast, the one June had teased him about, *what are you going to do, signal Santa?* — cracked it against the drum, and threw it underhand into the gasoline pool.

The world turned orange.

Flame ran up the spill like something alive, took the drum, leapt to the grass margin of the strip, and stood up in a roaring column that lit the whole valley floor in shuddering amber. The shooter scrambled back from the heat. The helicopter pilot, fifty meters away, made the only sane decision available and began spooling up, rotor wash feeding the fire into a frenzy.

"Go!" Felix heard himself screaming, voice raw. "Sami, down here, now! Everyone to the plane!"

They converged through the smoke in pieces. Sami first, sliding down the scree on his backside, flare gun still clutched in one fist like a toy he couldn't put down. Then Dara dragging Cole, whose left arm hung wrong, shoulder soaked black in the firelight. June leaning out of the cockpit door, hair whipping, holding the plane on the brakes while the propeller hammered the air.

"Cole's hit!" Dara shouted over the engine noise. "Through the shoulder — he's bleeding bad!"

"Load him!" Felix hauled Cole up by the good arm, felt the man's weight sag against him, dead weight going heavier by the minute. They shoved him through the cargo door onto the deck plates, and Dara climbed in after him, tearing open the medical kit with shaking hands.

Vossen appeared out of the smoke at the edge of the fire line, and for a moment the two men looked at each other across twenty meters of burning ground. The fixer's composure had finally cracked — not into rage but into something colder, recalculation happening behind his eyes at high speed. Two of his men were visible now, falling back toward the helicopter, one limping. The fire stood between them all like a wall.

"This isn't finished, Whitlock," Vossen called. The rotor wash tore his words sideways.

"It never is," Felix shouted back. "That's the country."

Then the second bullet found the Otter.

Felix felt it more than heard it — a heavy *chunk* through the airframe, and the plane lurched on its gear like a horse stumbling. June screamed something from the cockpit. The last shooter, retreating, had put two rounds into the starboard main gear as a parting gift, and Felix watched the strut fold with a grinding shriek of tortured aluminum, watched the wingtip drop and dig a furrow in the gravel as the tire blew.

The Otter slewed sideways, nose swinging toward the burning drums, and June fought it with both hands and every ounce of skill she owned, standing on the brakes, feeding opposite rudder, until the plane shuddered to a stop angled drunkenly across its own shadow, starboard wing low, gear collapsed beneath it like a broken knee.

Silence except for the fire and the helicopter climbing away into the dark, its running lights finally blinking on, red and green, almost festive.

Felix stood in the smoke with his hands still raised from pushing Cole aboard, and looked at his airplane. Ten years of medicine runs and mail and mercy flights, and it lay there tilted and dying, gear folded, one tire shredded, fuselage holed twice, smelling of cordite and avgas and burnt rubber.

"Well," June said, dropping out of the cockpit onto the gravel. Her face was soot-streaked and her voice was very steady, which meant she was nowhere near steady. "That's going to need a part."

* * *

They fought the fire until midnight, because they had to — a fuel fire on a gravel strip surrounded by dry timber in the last week before the rains was a death sentence for the whole valley, and Ilsa's people would not forgive them for it, and neither would the land itself. They beat it out with shovels and wet tarps and buckets passed hand to hand down a chain that included, eventually, Ansel and two of the valley men who had come down silently out of the dark when they smelled the smoke, saying nothing, working beside them, judging everything.

By the time the last embers were drowned, Felix's hands were blistered and his throat was raw and the strip looked like a battlefield, which is what it had been. Scattered brass. A boot print in the melted tar of a flare casing. A dark stain where Cole had lain.

Cole himself was propped against the cargo door inside the plane, gray-faced, shirt cut away, the entry wound dressed and the exit hole packed by Dara with a competence that surprised nobody anymore. The bullet had gone through the meat below the collarbone, missed the bone by what June called a miracle and Cole called, through gritted teeth, "a lousy shot."

"You're welcome," Dara said.

"I'm saying," Cole managed, "if you're going to shoot me, aim better next time."

"Next time I'll use both barrels."

Sami sat nearby with his knees drawn up, staring at nothing. He was nineteen years old and he had spent four hours in a firefight, and Felix knelt in front of him and gripped his shoulder hard enough to matter.

"You threw that flare early," Felix said. "It saved us. Their plan was to walk in quiet and walk out with everything, and your flare broke it apart. Understood?"

Sami nodded. His eyes were wet and furious at being wet. "I thought I was going to die."

"Everyone thought that. That's what it means to be here." Felix squeezed once more and let go. "Welcome to aviation."

* * *

At two in the morning they held the council that had to be held, in the cargo bay by lamplight, with Cole dosed on antibiotics and half-asleep against the fuselage wall and Marek unconscious in the tent outside, guarded now by Ansel, who had appointed himself to the job with a look that dared anyone to object.

"The gear's done," June said flatly. She had the damaged strut assembly laid out on a cargo net like a surgeon displaying the tumor. "Starboard main's collapsed, the strut's bent past truing, and even if I could straighten it, the attach fitting's cracked through. I can't fix this here. I can't fix it anywhere short of a hangar with parts."

"So we're grounded," Dara said.

"We're grounded *here*." June let that sit. "Which means when the monsoon comes — and look at the horizon tomorrow, you'll see it stacking up already — we are sitting in this valley with a broken airplane, a wounded man, a prisoner, and four armed professionals who now know exactly where we are and will be back with more friends."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the creek muttered over its stones, and far off, thunder cleared its throat beyond the divide.

"There's another way out," Felix said quietly. He'd been turning it over since the fire died, turning it like a stone in his pocket. "Tom's route. Downriver. Ilsa told me — there's a boat below the falls. Derelict, but her people kept it. Canoes above the falls, portage around, boat below if it floats. River to the coast station. Four days, maybe five, if the water doesn't rise."

"In monsoon season," June said.

"In monsoon season."

"With Cole feverish and Marek on a stretcher."

"Yes."

Sami looked up. "The plane—"

"The plane stays. We make it look untouched, undisturbed, like we lost the fight and fled on foot in panic. Vossen wants the strike data. Let him find a stripped camp and an empty valley and no map worth having. Every hour he spends searching this valley is an hour we spend getting Cole to a hospital." Felix met each of their eyes in turn. "I won't order anyone onto that river. This stopped being about the contract days ago. But I'm going, and I'm asking."

June snorted. She was cleaning soot from under her fingernails with a knife point, not looking up. "You've asked. That's cute. Cole can't walk a portage, so somebody carries him. I'm the strongest, so it's me. That's not volunteering, that's load planning."

"I'll take point on the river," Sami said. His voice cracked and steadied. "Ansel says the rapids change every year. Someone young should be in the bow."

Dara said nothing for a long moment. Firelight moved on her face through the open cargo door. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough. "Ten years ago I walked out of this country and left a man behind, and I have spent every day since paying for it in ways I didn't have words for. I'm not leaving anyone behind again. Not Cole. Not Marek, God help me. Not any of you." She looked at Felix. "We go together or not at all."

Thunder rolled again, closer now, and the first fat drops of nothing-yet began ticking against the wing overhead like fingers testing a window.

Felix looked out at his broken airplane silhouetted against the bruised sky, at the scorched strip, at the black wall of forest holding all its secrets close. The cost had come due, all of it at once, and the bill was written in aluminum and blood and four days of river that would try to kill them every mile of the way.

"All right," he said. "First light, we see Ilsa. We're going to need canoes."

From the darkness outside, Ansel's voice answered before anyone else could, quiet and certain, as though he'd been waiting all night at the door:

"She already knows. She sent me to tell you — the river is rising early this year."
