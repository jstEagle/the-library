## 17. Grounded

The rain started as a rumor before it was a fact — a gray smudge on the southern horizon that Sami watched grow all morning from the lip of the wrecked strip, the way you watch a debt come due.

She'd been up since four, though nobody had asked her to be. Sleep had stopped being a thing her body trusted after the runway fight, after the sound Cole made when the bullet went through his shoulder — a short, punched-out grunt, like a man who'd been expecting the bill for years and was almost relieved to finally pay it. Sami had held him down in the mud while June cut the sleeve away, and what she remembered most wasn't the blood. It was how quiet Felix had gone. How he'd stood over Cole with his hands hanging at his sides like they'd forgotten what hands were for.

Now Cole lay in Tom Reyes's old cabin on a bed of spruce boughs, fever climbing by degrees, and the plane sat on the strip like a gut-shot bird.

Sami walked its length twice before she let herself say it out loud.

The port gear was done. Not bent — *done*. The left main strut had folded under the impact of Vossen's men using the fuselage for cover, and the tire was shredded to cord and rubber ribbon. Worse, the spar fitting above the gear well showed a crack running six inches into the metal, hairline-thin, the kind of damage that let a plane fly fine right up until the moment it didn't. She put her hand flat against the skin of the wing and felt the morning sun warming the aluminum, and underneath it, nothing. No strength left in that structure. Any loadmaster who'd ever dropped a crate wrong knew what a cracked fitting meant. You didn't fly on one. You didn't even taxi on one if you could help it.

"Tell me I'm wrong," Felix said behind her.

He'd come down the slope without her hearing him, which frightened her more than the crack in the spar. Felix Whitlock moved through country like he belonged to it; ten days ago he'd still sounded like a man visiting.

"You're not wrong," she said. "I could fabricate a strut. June's got the skill and we've got scrap. But that fitting—" She tapped the crack with a knuckle. "That's not field-fixable. That's a hangar job, a good hangar, and we're two hundred miles from a road."

Felix looked at the plane the way a man looks at an old dog he's about to have to make a decision about. "How long would a strut take you?"

"Two days if everything cooperates." She didn't bother finishing. Nothing was going to cooperate. The clouds stacking over the southern ridgeline said so in language older than aviation.

"How long do we have?"

Sami turned and faced the horizon properly. She'd grown up in a coastal town where weather was something that happened to other people's fishing boats, but ten months of flying the interior had taught her to read sky the way June read engines — not by what it looked like but by what it was doing. And what this sky was doing was loading. The monsoon didn't arrive like a storm arrived, all drama and lightning. It arrived like a tide. A wall of moisture pushed inland off the coast, day after day, until the passes filled and the rivers stood up and every strip in the high country drowned under cloud you couldn't climb through.

"Three days," she said. "Maybe four. After that, nothing flies in or out of these valleys for weeks. Maybe a month."

Felix was quiet for a long moment. Then: "So we don't fly out."

"No," Sami agreed. "We don't fly out."

* * *

They carried the news back to the cabin, and the meeting that followed was the strangest Sami had ever sat in — seven people and one wounded man crowded into a room built for two, arguing survival in voices kept low because the walls were thin and the forest listened.

June laid it out flat, because June always did. "Options are three. One, we stay and wait out the monsoon here, hope Vossen doesn't come back with more men and better aim. Two, we fix the strut, overload the plane, and pray the spar holds through the first pass — which is gambling five lives on a crack I can see daylight through. Three—"

"There is no three," Cole said from the bunk. His voice was sanded down by fever, but his mind was working. Sami could see it grinding behind his eyes. "There's never a three."

"There's a three," Ilsa said.

Everyone turned. The old woman had been sitting by the fire through the whole argument, mending a net, saying nothing, letting them exhaust themselves the way you let children run themselves out in a yard. Now she set the net down.

"The river," she said. "Tom's river. He came in on it and he went out on it, twice, before your airplanes made him lazy. There is a route. Downriver through the gorge, a portage at the falls, and below the falls there is a boat."

"A boat?" Dara said. "Out here?"

"My father's boat. Aluminum, flat-bottomed, thirty feet. It ran freight up from the coastal station for twenty years before the falls took the lower channel and made the run pointless." Ilsa's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "It has been sitting below the falls for eleven years. It will need work. But it floats. Boats want to float. It is their whole opinion about things."

"And the falls?" Felix asked.

"Are why the boat is still there," Ilsa said. "Nobody comes down the river past them. Nobody goes up. The water does the guarding that soldiers couldn't."

Sami watched Felix take that in — watched him weigh the arithmetic of it, the portage distance, the load weights, the state of the river in early flood season. She'd learned to read his silences too. This one wasn't refusal. This one was a man discovering that the map in his head had been drawn by someone else all along.

"We'd need guides," he said finally.

"You need one guide." Ilsa stood, joints cracking like green wood. "My grandson."

* * *

Ansel came down to the strip that afternoon, and Sami understood within a minute why Ilsa had chosen him and no one else.

He was maybe twenty-four, broad through the shoulders in the way of people who'd carried boats over mountains since childhood, with a face that gave away exactly nothing. He walked the length of the wrecked plane without touching it, looked at the cracked spar where Sami pointed, grunted once, and then looked out at the river instead — the real road, the silver one winding north through the gorge — and Sami realized he'd already finished the calculation the rest of them were still setting up.

"The portage is nine kilometers around the falls," he said. His English was accented but precise, a classroom English, and Sami wondered later which teacher had survived into the valley. "The trail is Tom's trail. He cut it. My family has kept it clear since." He looked at Felix. "Your grandfather flew medicine to the coast stations. My grandmother says you are not the same kind of man. She says it like it is not a compliment."

"It isn't," Felix said.

"Good. Then we agree on something." Ansel crouched, picked up a stick, and drew in the dirt: the river, the gorge, the falls, the boat. His hands moved with the certainty of someone drawing his own kitchen. "Three canoes. We carry the wounded man in a litter between two of them through the flats, then the portage. Below the falls, we repair the boat — my uncle has tools, caulking, there is fuel cached near it from before. Six hours' work if the hull is honest. Then downriver to the station, two days if the water is kind."

"And if the water isn't kind?"

Ansel looked up at him. "Then it is the same water that killed the prospectors' guide two summers ago, and we will find out what kind of people you are faster than you wanted to."

Sami felt the words land on the whole group at once. Nobody answered. There wasn't an answer. The valley had already tried them once — the ambush, the fight, Cole bleeding — and whatever verdict Ilsa's people had reached, Ansel standing here offering the river was the sentence being read out.

"When?" Felix asked.

"Tomorrow, first light," Ansel said. "Before the rain. After that, the river makes its own schedule." He rose, brushed the dirt map away with his boot — erasing the route as casually as he'd drawn it, and Sami understood that too: the plan existed only in the heads of the people in this clearing, and that was deliberate — and walked back toward the trees without waiting to be thanked.

Dara watched him go. "We wrote them off," she said quietly. "All of us. Ten years ago, the expedition treated their grandparents like pack animals with opinions. And now—" She stopped.

"Now they're saving our lives," June said. "Get used to the feeling. It doesn't wear off."

* * *

They worked until dark, and Sami worked harder than she'd ever worked in her life, because humility, she was learning, was mostly just labor performed in front of witnesses.

They stripped the plane. Everything that mattered came out: the medical stores, the radios, the surviving weapons, the food, the fuel they could carry in drums to the boat. June and Sami spent two hours making the wreck look like what it was supposed to be — a failed survey aircraft, storm-damaged, abandoned — rather than what it actually was, which was evidence. They rehung the cowling panels, scattered tool debris, dragged a wingtip through the brush to suggest a bad landing long ago. If Vossen's people or anyone else came looking, they'd find a boring story written in aluminum.

The journal pages rode in a waterproof bag against Sami's own chest. After Marek, nobody suggested putting them anywhere less personal.

Cole insisted on walking to the strip to supervise the loading, which lasted forty feet before the fever won and Sami and Dara half-carried him back. On the way he stopped, swaying between them, and looked at the mountains going purple in the evening light.

"I've flown out of worse places than this," he said. "Just never walked out of one."

"First time for everything," Sami said.

"That's what worries me." But he smiled, or the fever let him, and between them they got him to the bunk.

Near midnight, unable to sleep, Sami stepped outside and found Ansel sitting alone by the cold fire pit, sharpening a knife with long, patient strokes. She almost went back inside. Instead she sat down across from him, leaving the fire ring between them the way you leave space with a dog you haven't earned yet.

"Why are you doing this?" she asked. "Helping us. After everything."

Ansel didn't stop sharpening. "My grandmother decided," he said. "In this valley, her decision is the law."

"But you could have argued."

"Yes." He tested the edge against his thumbnail, nodded, sheathed the knife. "I argued. I said: these people brought Marek, and Marek brought the radio, and the radio brought the helicopters, and the helicopters brought bullets into our valley. My grandmother said: yes. And Tom Reyes came here with nothing and stayed thirty years and died for us, and his friends came here with nothing and got shot for us, and the difference between a guest and a thief is smaller than a knife's edge, and someone has to find out which side of it they fall on." He looked at her then, and his eyes in the starlight were very level. "You are finding out now. All of you. Downriver."

Sami sat with that. The stars over the gorge were hard and close, and somewhere south, beyond three ridgelines, the monsoon was eating the horizon one mile at a time.

"Your grandmother hates my boss," she said. "Hates Felix. Because of his grandfather."

"She hated the grandfather," Ansel said. "The grandson she is still deciding about. So am I." He stood, rolled his bedding out by the fire, and lay down facing the treeline, which told Sami everything about how much he trusted the night. "Sleep. Tomorrow we find out what kind of people you are."

* * *

First light came gray and windless, the air thick as wool, and the first drops of the monsoon's advance guard ticked against the leaves while they loaded the canoes.

Nine kilometers of portage. A derelict boat below the falls. Two days of river, a wounded man, a storm coming down out of the south like a closing door.

Sami shouldered her pack, took the bowline of the middle canoe, and felt the weight of everything they hadn't said settle onto her back along with the gear. Behind them the wrecked plane sat silent on its broken leg, telling its boring story to nobody. Ahead of them the river ran loud through the gorge, and Ansel stood at the head of the line already, patient as stone, waiting for them to be ready.

"Stay between the blazes," he said. "And whatever you hear behind you today — don't stop walking."

Sami frowned. "What would we hear?"

Ansel looked upriver, into the gorge, where the water came down out of the high country white and endless, and for the first time since she'd met him, something moved behind his face that wasn't calm.

"The falls," he said, "sing differently when the rain starts. You'll know it when you hear it. Walk faster."
