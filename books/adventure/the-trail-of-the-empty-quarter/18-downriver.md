## 18. Downriver

The river took us the way it takes everything — without asking.

We put in at first light, four canoes riding low: Felix and Ansel in the lead, me and Cole behind them, Sami and Dara third, and Marek lashed into the fourth like cargo nobody wanted to claim. His arm was splinted with a paddle shaft and duct tape from my kit, his face gray as wet ash. He hadn't said ten words since the scuffle at Tom's cabin, and I'd stopped counting them as a loss.

Cole sat in front of me because he couldn't hold a paddle anymore. The bullet had gone through his shoulder two nights ago, and by morning his skin was hot enough to feel from a foot away. I'd cleaned the wound with what was left of our iodine, packed it, wrapped it, done everything right, and it hadn't mattered. Infection doesn't care about competence. It just moves in like weather.

"Talk to me," he said, as the current picked up under the bow. "Keep me honest."

"You're honest," I said. "You're delirious."

"That's when I'm most honest."

The gorge swallowed us. Walls of black granite rose on either side, close enough that the paddles threw echoes, and the water went from brown to green to a churning white that made my forearms ache just looking at it. Ansel read it like a page. Every few hundred meters he'd raise a hand — left, right, hard left — and Felix would pass it back, and we'd dig in, and the river would try its best to fold us against the rocks and fail.

I've run water before. Nothing like this. This wasn't sport. This was a road, and we were freight, and the toll was paid in attention. One bad stroke. That's all it would take. One stroke where your shoulder says *enough* instead of *again*, and the current would do the arithmetic for you.

Cole's paddle dipped and dragged. I reached past him and corrected before the bow swung.

"Sorry," he mumbled.

"Don't be sorry. Be here."

"I'm here." A pause. The water roared. Then, quieter, almost lost in it: "June. If I'm not—"

"You're going to be not-dead in a clinic bed by Thursday complaining about the food," I said. "That's the only version I'm accepting."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a shiver, and I put my hand flat against his back between strokes and felt the heat coming off him like off an engine block.

* * *

The first portage came at midday, where the river dropped through a chute no sane person would run. We hauled the canoes up a deer trail slick with moss, gear on our backs, Cole's weight split between me and Sami. Ansel carried a canoe over his head like it was a hat.

That was where Dara happened.

She'd been quiet since the valley — quieter than her usual quiet, which was already a weapon she kept sharpened. She took the heaviest load without being asked: the fuel drums' worth of weight in tools, the medical kit, the ammo box with the journal pages sealed in wax. She took it and walked, and when she came back for more, she took mine too.

"I can carry it," I told her.

"I know you can." She shifted the strap on her shoulder. "But you're carrying him."

It wasn't an apology. Dara Okonkwo didn't apologize; she recalculated. But somewhere on that portage trail, between the second carry and the fifth, something changed in how she moved. She stopped walking like a woman who owned the ground and started walking like a woman carrying something fragile through someone else's house. When Cole stumbled at the top of the climb, she was there before I was, taking his other arm without a word, bearing half his weight down the switchbacks while he muttered about being fine, absolutely fine, never better.

At the bottom, putting back in, she looked at me across the canoe.

"He loved this country," she said. Not Tom. Not by name. She didn't need to say the name anymore; it lived in every sentence now like silt in water. "He wrote about it. Did you know that? Pages of it. Rivers and birds and the way the light comes down the valley in October. I thought I knew him. I knew a version of him that fit in a city apartment."

"The real one was harder to carry," I said.

"Yes." She looked upriver, where the mist hung. "So was this."

She got in the canoe and paddled, and I watched her arms work, and I thought: *apology is a word. This is the thing the word is standing in for.* You earn your way back into a family the same way you earn anything out here — by hauling weight, over and over, until the hauling is just who you are.

* * *

The capsizing happened at the worst possible place, which is the only place things ever happen.

A long braid of rapids below the second portage — nothing lethal, just fast, pushy water threaded with rocks that showed themselves a half-second too late. We were running it clean. Felix ahead, reading lines. Sami and Dara behind us, doing well, better than well. And then Cole fainted.

No warning. No groan. One moment his paddle was moving, the next he listed sideways like a man falling asleep in a chair, and the bow swung, and a rock took us amidships with a crack I felt in my teeth.

Cold. Total, absolute cold, the kind that erases thought. I came up gasping with my hand still locked on Cole's collar — some animal part of me had grabbed him before my brain even registered the flip — and the river was pulling us apart and I pulled back, both hands, screaming words the water ate instantly.

Sami saw it happen. Later, she told me she didn't remember deciding; she just turned the canoe. She and Dara drove their bow across the current at an angle that should have flipped them too, and Dara leaned out over the water with her whole body, one hand clamped on the gunwale, the other reaching, and I got Cole's collar up to her and she held him — held forty kilos of dead-weight man against a five-knot current with nothing but grip and fury — until Sami dragged them both over the side.

I came ashore fifty meters downstream on my own power, coughing, shaking so hard I could barely stand. Felix and Ansel were already there, already hauling the swamped canoe up onto the stones. Everything not tied down was gone: two packs, the tarp, half the food. The iodine. Gone.

Cole lay on the bank, gray-lipped, breathing. Alive. The river had nearly argued otherwise and been overruled.

"We don't stop," Felix said. Nobody had asked. He was wringing out his beard, looking upriver toward the gorge mouth, listening to something. "Rain's coming early. Ansel says the falls will tell us. We move."

"His fever—" I started.

"Is why we move faster." Felix crouched next to Cole, and for a second the commander mask slipped and I saw the man underneath, scared, ten years of guilt stacked behind his eyes. "June. I'm not losing anyone else on this river."

I checked Cole's wound while Sami wrung out our remaining clothes. Red streaks climbing his chest. The good news was I didn't have any iodine left to worry about rationing. The bad news was everything else.

* * *

We ran the last miles into dusk, Cole wedged upright between cargo in the middle of Felix's canoe, conscious in flickers. Dara paddled bow for me now, unasked, unremarked. Her hands were blistered raw from the portage and she wrapped them with strips of shirt and kept paddling.

Ansel called the halt at a gravel bar below a bend where the gorge opened just enough to show sky. We dragged the canoes up, got a fire going with damp wood and stubbornness, and I did what I could for Cole, which was almost nothing, which has to be the hardest kind of medicine there is.

He surfaced once, near full dark, and found my face above him.

"Hey," he said. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"Did we win?"

"We're not done yet."

He smiled, and his eyes closed, and his breathing went shallow and fast, and I sat there holding his wrist like I could keep time for him, and around me the crew worked without being told. Sami repacked the salvaged gear. Felix stood watch facing upriver. Dara fed the fire piece by piece, feeding it like it was alive, like it was Cole, like keeping something burning was the one thing tonight she could actually do.

And I understood, sitting in the smoke with a fevered man's pulse under my fingers, that somewhere between the float base and this gravel bar we had stopped being a payroll. There was no contract language for what Dara had done on that portage trail. No invoice line for Sami turning her canoe into the current. Whatever we were now, we'd become it the way anything becomes true out here — by weight carried, repeated, until the carrying is the identity.

Felix crouched beside me. For a long time neither of us spoke. Upstream, faint but unmistakable, the falls changed their voice — a deeper note under the white roar, like a choir adding a bass section.

"There it is," he said quietly. "The rain."

"How far behind us is Vossen?"

"If he's smart? Half a day. If he's angry?" Felix looked at the fire, at Cole, at the black wall of the gorge. "Angry men travel light."

I looked downriver, where tomorrow's route waited — the falls, the derelict boat, whatever came after. Then upstream, where the sound was building, patient and enormous, the monsoon clearing its throat.

"Felix," I said. "Whatever's coming — we're not leaving him."

He put his hand over mine on Cole's wrist.

"No," he said. "We're not."

Behind us, in the dark, the rain arrived.
