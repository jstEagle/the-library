## 19. Vossen Catches Up

The falls announced themselves a mile before we saw them — a low, continuous thunder rolling up the gorge like weather that had decided to live in one place. Ansel heard it first, or rather felt it, the way he seemed to feel everything in this country through the soles of his boots. He raised a fist and we stopped, six people and a litter and two days of portage strapped to our backs, standing in the green cathedral light under cedars older than any of our grievances.

"Portage trail starts there," he said, pointing through the trees. "River goes ugly after. We carry around, put in below."

"How long?" June asked. Her voice had gone flat and efficient, the voice she saved for engines that were trying to kill her.

"Two hours, if the rain holds off."

The rain did not hold off. It never holds off in this country; it only agrees to wait. It came in fine and sideways through the canopy, beading on the rifle Cole had insisted on carrying slung across his back with his arm in a sling, and turning the portage trail into a brown ribbon of slick root and stone. Cole walked it anyway, refusing the litter, refusing help, pale as birch bark and sweating out a fever that had nothing to do with the weather. Dara carried double loads without being asked. Nobody commented on it. That was its own kind of comment.

We were halfway around the falls when I heard the helicopter.

* * *

You don't mistake that sound for anything else. Not wind, not the river, not thunder — a helicopter has a rhythm to it, a mechanical pulse, and this one was beating its way up the valley behind us, low, following the river the way Vossen's men would follow it, because the river was the only road and they knew it as well as Ansel did.

"Off the trail," I said. "Now."

We went into the trees like animals, no talk, just motion — June hauling Cole by his good arm, Sami with the journal satchel strapped to his chest, Ansel melting ahead of us through the undergrowth with the unerring certainty of a man moving through his own house in the dark. We got maybe two hundred meters off the trail and down into a fold of the land, a mossy hollow beneath a deadfall, before the helicopter passed over. It didn't slow. It didn't need to. It was going where we were going, and it knew the way.

"They'll put in below the falls," Ansel said quietly. "Wait for us there. Or they'll send men up the portage to meet us in the middle." He looked at me. He was maybe nineteen, and he had the oldest eyes I'd ever seen on a young man. "Your choice, pilot."

My choice. I looked at my people — June crouched in the ferns with rain running off her chin, Sami white-faced and gripping the satchel like it was a life vest, Cole half-delirious and trying to stand up anyway, Dara watching me with an expression I couldn't read and had stopped trying to. Two days ago we'd been a crew. Now we were whatever this was — fugitives, witnesses, carriers of a secret that had already cost blood.

"There's a way down to the water below the falls," Ansel said. "Steep. Old climb, my grandfather used it. The boat is there. If we take it now, before they finish unloading, we pass them in the current and we're gone."

"And if they're faster than you think?"

"Then we fight in the rapids," he said, "and the river picks the winners."

* * *

The climb down to the boat pool was the worst hour of a trip that had been made of worst hours. Wet granite, a rope Ansel had left coiled in a hollow two years ago and that I trusted exactly as far as I could throw it, and below us the falls themselves — a hundred feet of white violence funneling the whole river into a throat of black rock. The pool at the bottom was deceptively calm, a green lens of water where the current gathered itself before committing to the gorge. The derelict boat sat half-sunk at the edge of it, and Ansel and Sami spent twenty frantic minutes bailing and repacking until it floated like something that might, under ideal conditions and with favorable gods, survive the run.

We were loading the last of the gear when the first of them came down the far trail.

Three men. Not four — I counted twice, because four was the number we'd fought at the strip, and one of them had gone into the fuel fire and not come out. These three moved like professionals, spaced out, rifles slung, no hurry. They came around the base of the falls and stopped when they saw us, and for a long moment nobody did anything at all. The falls roared. The rain fell. Two groups of wet, exhausted men looked at each other across forty feet of green water.

Then Vossen stepped forward, and I understood that the other two were decoration.

He looked worse than the last time I'd seen him. The night action at the strip had cost him — there was a bandage at his hairline, and something in his bearing had gone from salesman to survivor. His rifle was in his hands, not slung. The two behind him had theirs ready, casual as men holding umbrellas.

"Whitlock." His voice carried over the water without effort. "You've cost me a great deal of money."

"You came to the wrong valley," I said.

"I came to the right valley. That's the problem." He walked to the water's edge, boots in the shallows, rifle loose across his chest. Behind me I heard June shift her weight, and I knew without looking that she had the flare gun out of her pack and palmed. It wasn't a rifle. It was all we had. "Here's the last conversation we're going to have, so let's have it honestly. You have something I want. Pages. A map. Whatever Reyes left. I have a helicopter, two more men upstream, and a very long reach. Give me the satchel and the boy's boat, and you all walk out of here. I'll even fly you to the coast. I'm not a monster, Whitlock. I'm an accountant."

"You're standing in a graveyard," Dara said.

He turned his head toward her voice, and something flickered across his face — recognition, maybe, or the memory of a file he'd read. "Ms. Okonkwo. They told me you'd gone native. I didn't believe them."

"They told you a lot of things," she said. "They told you Tom Reyes was dead. They told you there was no valley. You're standing in the proof that they lie to you every time it's cheaper than the truth."

For the first time, Vossen's composure cracked — just a hairline, just for a second, a man hearing something he'd suspected and paid a great deal not to confirm. And in that second, Sami moved.

I'll never know what he thought he was doing. Maybe he saw the same crack I saw and thought it was an opening. Maybe he just panicked. He stepped toward the boat, toward the satchel, as if he could put it between himself and the rifles, and the man on Vossen's left swung his weapon up and fired.

The shot went wide — rain, nerves, a moving target — and the sound of it broke the world open. June's flare gun barked, a red star hissing across the pool, and the man flinched away from it, and Ansel shoved the boat off the bank with all his strength, and then everything happened at once and nothing happened in order.

We went into the water.

* * *

The pool took the boat and us like it had been waiting all week for the chance. I came up gasping in the churn, one hand on the gunwale, and the current already had us — that gathering, muscular pull at the lip of the pool where the green water leaned over the edge and became white. Vossen was shouting on the bank. A rifle cracked again and the bullet slapped the water a foot from Sami's head. Then we went over the lip, and the world became noise.

I have run rapids before. I have never run anything like this. The gorge narrowed to a stone throat and the river went from water to solid white argument, and the boat did what boats do in that situation, which is everything and nothing — surfing, spinning, shipping water, held together by Ansel's hands on the yoke and by whatever contract exists between desperate people and their tools. Cole was lashed into the bottom. June had him. Sami had the satchel. Dara had the bailer and was using it like a weapon against the river, bailing with a fury that had nothing to do with staying dry.

Then we hit the rock.

It came out of the white without warning, a fang of granite the size of a truck, and the boat struck it a glancing blow that spun us sideways and threw Dara out. I saw her go — one moment her hands were on the gunwale, the next the river had her, and her head came up once in the foam with an expression I will carry to my grave, not terror, just astonishment, the surprise of a woman discovering that the water was serious all along.

I went in after her.

I don't remember deciding to. That's the honest truth, and I've had a long time to be honest about it. There was no decision, no weighing, none of the careful moral arithmetic I'd like to claim. There was just my hand letting go of the boat and my body diving into water that hit me like a collapsing building. The river took me down, rolled me, scraped me along the bottom, and gave me back to the surface just long enough to see her — twenty feet downstream, going under again, a flash of her jacket in the white.

I caught her jacket on the second dive. The current fought me for her like she was made of money. I got an arm around her chest and my other hand on a submerged branch — a deadfall wedged in the rocks, a snag that should have killed us and instead saved us — and I held on while the river tried to pull my arms out of their sockets. She was limp. She was limp for too long. Then she coughed, and cursed, which in my experience are the two surest signs of life, and we dragged ourselves hand over hand along the snag to the eddy behind it, and lay there on the wet stone with the gorge roaring past, breathing like bellows.

She was crying. I couldn't tell if it was the water on her face or not, and I didn't ask. I just held onto her and the branch and let the river spend its fury on everything but us.

* * *

The boat came through without us. Ansel brought it out the bottom of the gorge on pure skill and stubbornness, shipping half its water, Cole still lashed in the bilge with June bent over him, Sami white-knuckled and alive. They found the eddy a quarter mile down and pulled us out of the river like two drowned cats, and for a while nobody said anything at all. There was nothing to say. The river had said it.

We were a mile below the falls, in the last of the light, when we heard the helicopter again — and this time it passed high overhead, going north, going home, going away. Vossen was done. I knew it the way you know weather. A man who has spent his men and his argument on a river and lost both does not come back into country like this. He goes home to his accountants and reports that the deposit does not exist.

Except he still had the maps. And the phone. And somewhere in that wet, shaking, half-drowned pile of survivors, we still had the journal.

We made camp on a gravel bar, the first dry ground in hours, and it was there, wrapping Dara in a blanket while she shivered out the river's cold, that I saw Vossen's team had left their mark on us after all — because Sami, checking the satchel with the thoroughness of a man who has been shot at over its contents, went pale.

"The dry bag's torn," he said. "The river. The maps — Felix, the route maps, the strike overlays, they're—"

"Gone?" Dara said.

"Soaked. Some of them gone. The rest—" He held one up. The ink had run into gray ghosts. The mountain's secret was dissolving in the rain, the way it had always meant to.

"Good," I said, and meant it.

But it wasn't all of it. Because at the bottom of the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth the way Tom Reyes had wrapped it twenty years ago, dry as the day he'd written it, was the journal. Every page. The strike, the false charts, the last entry addressed to whoever came looking. The one thing Vossen had crossed a wilderness to take was sitting in my lap, and the one thing I had promised Ilsa's people was that it would never leave the valley — and here it was, a mile below the falls, in my hands, going out.

Dara looked at it. Then at me.

"Tomorrow," she said quietly. "At the last camp. Before we're in reach of anyone's law but ours."

The river ran dark past the gravel bar, carrying everything it had taken and everything it had spared, and tomorrow was coming fast.
