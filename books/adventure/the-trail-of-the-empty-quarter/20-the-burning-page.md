## 20. The Burning Page

The rain had followed them down from the falls like a debt collector, patient and inevitable, and by the time Ansel called a halt at the bend where the river widened into a gravel shallows, it was falling in earnest — not the monsoon yet, not the wall Felix kept watching the northern sky for, but its herald: a fine grey drizzle that beaded on everything and refused to wipe away.

They made camp under a leaning granite overhang that Ansel said his grandfather's people had used "since before the maps." It was generous shelter as such places went — dry floor of packed sand, a fire ring of old stones blackened by generations of other fires — and they built the flames high because Cole was shivering again, the fever climbing through him in waves that June fought with the last of the antibiotics and a stubbornness that frightened Dara more than the fever itself.

Cole lay propped against a pack near the fire, his bandaged shoulder seeping rust-brown at the edges, his eyes too bright. Sami sat close beside him, holding a tin cup he only sometimes drank from. Felix stood at the mouth of the overhang with his back to them all, watching the river come down out of the dark.

And Dara sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, with Tom Reyes's journal open on her knees.

She had carried it since the cabin. Through Ilsa's valley, through the fight on the strip, through the portage and the rapids and the capsized canoe that had nearly taken Sami into the white water — she had carried it inside her jacket, against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and she had not opened it. Not once. Not even when Marek's theft left the pages torn and scattered and Cole bleeding to keep them together. Not even when Felix had looked at her across Vossen's disarmed men and asked, without words, *what are you going to do with it?*

She told herself she was waiting for privacy. For quiet. For some moment worthy of it.

The truth was simpler and worse. She was afraid that whatever Tom had written would be the last thing he ever said to anyone, and she did not want to hear it in front of strangers.

But there were no strangers left. That was what the river had done to them. Ten days ago these people had been employees, rivals, an inconvenience wearing a geologist's face. Now June was humming something low and tuneless while she changed Cole's dressing, and Sami was keeping the fire fed without being asked, and Felix was standing guard over all of them out of habit older than grief. Whatever she read tonight, she would read it among family she hadn't chosen and couldn't return.

She turned back the oilcloth.

The journal was water-swollen at one corner, buckled where the river had gotten it during the scuffle at the cabin, but most of the pages had survived. She'd seen fragments already — Marek's torn-out sheaf, recovered from his pack after the radio died, covered in assay numbers and grid coordinates written in a hand she recognized instantly, because she had spent ten years trying to forget it. The strike data. The thing three parties would burn a forest to own. It was all still here, folded into the back cover like an appendix: the location of the richest ore body any of them had ever seen, hidden inside a valley that appeared on no chart, guarded by people who had already buried one prospector for coming after it.

Tom's handwriting filled the earlier pages. Dara began at the beginning, because she could not help herself, because ten years of not-knowing deserved at least one hour of knowing.

*Day 3,* the first entry ran. *Whitlock is insufferable about the fuel margins. He counts every liter twice and then makes me count it a third time so he can pretend I got it wrong. Okonkwo says we'll starve before we run dry. She may be right. She's usually right, which is the worst thing about her.*

Dara laughed — a short, cracked sound that made Sami look up — and then pressed her hand over her mouth, because laughing hurt more than crying and she hadn't budgeted for either.

She read on. The expedition came alive in Tom's flat, wry field-hand: the weather, the arguments, Felix's obsessive route-planning, the guides who grew quieter the farther north they flew. She found herself in it constantly — *Okonkwo fixed the radio with a bootlace and an insult; Okonkwo outwalked everyone to the ridge and pretended not to be winded; Okonkwo was right about the storm.* He had noticed her. All those years she'd told herself he barely saw her past Felix, and he had been writing her name into every third page like a man keeping score of something he didn't dare name.

Then the entries thinned. The tone changed. Somewhere around day nineteen the jokes stopped and the sentences shortened, and she understood, reading between the lines, that this was where the others had started lying — where the survey became something else, where the money behind them started asking questions the answers to which could not be spoken aloud. There were gaps. Whole days missing, pages razored out long before the journal ever reached a cabin shelf. Someone had censored it once already. She wondered if Tom had done it himself, cutting out the parts that would get people killed, and decided that yes — yes, that was exactly what Tom would have done, careful to the last, protecting everyone including the men who'd abandoned him.

The final entry began on a page near the end, set off by itself, the handwriting larger and slower, as though written by a man making sure it could be read by tired eyes in bad light.

*Dara.*

Her breath stopped.

*If you're reading this, then you finally came north, and I'm sorry, because I know what it cost you to wait and I know what it will cost you to be here. I've thought about writing this a hundred ways and every one of them sounds like a speech, so I'll just say it straight.*

*I lived. You probably figured that out from the cabin. Twenty years, give or take. I married. Her name was Sera and she was better than I deserved and she's gone now too, and our boy Ansel is the best part of both of us, so be kind to him if he's the one carrying you downriver. He probably is. He can't help being decent. It's genetic.*

*I stayed because of what we found. Not the strike — you can keep the strike, I never wanted it, and neither did the people who took me in. I stayed because of what the strike would do to them. You remember what the company men were like at the end. You remember what they asked us to sign. Now imagine them with helicopters and no witnesses and a valley full of families who've never seen a lawyer. I mischarted it. Every number I sent out was wrong by enough to make it look like slag. It's the only lie I ever told that I'm proud of.*

*But I know you, Dara. I know how you love a truth. It's your best quality and it will be the death of you or someone else. So this is me asking, from twenty years away, from a grave you're probably sitting next to: let them keep it. Let the valley keep it. Some things are worth more unproven. I spent my whole second life learning that, and I'd trade every year of it to have learned it faster.*

*Tell Whitlock I'm sorry about the fuel margins. He was right to count twice.*

*Burn the numbers, Dara. Not for me. For the kid who feeds your fire right now, and for mine, and for everyone who will never know your name and shouldn't have to die learning it.*

*— T.R.*

The journal blurred. She realized she was crying only when a drop hit the page and bloomed there, swelling the ink of his signature, and she didn't wipe it away because wiping it away felt like erasing him and she had done enough erasing for one lifetime.

"Dara."

Felix's voice, low, from just beside her. She hadn't heard him cross the camp. He lowered himself onto the sand next to her, forearms on his knees, and looked into the fire rather than at her, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"How much of that did you hear?" she managed.

"Enough." A pause. "He really wrote that about the fuel margins?"

"He really wrote that about the fuel margins."

Felix huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. In the firelight his face looked carved and tired, ten years older than it had been a month ago, and she understood suddenly that he had been carrying his own version of this journal inside him the whole time — the guilt, the unanswered questions, the empty chair — and that Tom's letter had just handed them both the same weight and the same release.

"He knew," she said. "All those years. He knew we'd come looking eventually, and he — he wrote to us. To me. Like he was leaving a message on a machine he hoped we'd never call."

"That was Tom." Felix picked up a stick and turned the coals. "Always planning for the worst outcome and hoping nobody needed the plan."

"Marek has the numbers," she said. "Had. We took them back. They're real, Felix. The assays are real — I checked them against my own data the night we found the wreck, before I knew whose handwriting it was. It's the biggest strike in the hemisphere and it's sitting under a valley full of people who want nothing to do with it, and there is a consortium out there that has already sent one team of armed men, and they will send another, and another, forever, as long as the data exists to prove them right."

"I know."

"And Tom says burn it."

"I know that too."

She looked at him then. "You're not going to argue with me?"

"What would I argue?" He shook his head slowly. "I spent ten years thinking Tom walked into the mist and died alone because of a route I put on the map. Turns out he walked into the mist and lived twenty years, married, raised a son, and died defending a secret he chose — and the only reason any of us are sitting here instead of floating down that river is that he chose well." He nodded toward the journal. "That man has been right about everything for twenty years, Dara. Including the fuel margins. I'm not starting an argument with him now."

She almost smiled. Then the weight of it settled fully onto her — the actual, physical act she was about to perform — and the smile died.

Because it wasn't just paper. It was proof. It was the difference between a story and a fact, between a rumor the consortium could dismiss and a coordinate they could fund an invasion around. Burning it meant the valley's protection rested on ignorance, and ignorance was a thin wall, and men like Vossen had shovels. Burning it meant Tom's twenty years of silence would be total — no record, no vindication, no museum case somewhere with his name on it. It meant the world would go on believing he'd simply vanished, and she would go on carrying the only memory of what he'd actually become.

It also meant no one else would ever die for it. Not Ansel, not Ilsa, not some bush pilot ten years from now who stumbled onto the wrong creek bed and mentioned it on the wrong frequency.

June looked up from Cole's dressing across the fire. Sami stopped feeding sticks to the flames. Even Cole lifted his head, fever-bright, tracking the moment without understanding it. Nobody spoke. They all seemed to feel it — the camp holding its breath around a woman with a journal and a decision.

Dara stood. Her legs were stiff from the portage and her hands were shaking and she let them shake, because hiding it would have been a lie and she was done with those.

"Felix," she said. "I need you to witness this. Not to stop me. Just — to see it. So that somebody besides me knows it happened."

He rose without a word and stood at her shoulder.

She carried the journal to the fire and knelt. First she tore out the back section — Marek's stolen pages, the assay sheets, the grid coordinates, the whole glittering mathematical skeleton of the strike — and held them over the flames until the corners caught. The paper went up fast, eager, orange light jumping across her face, and she fed the pages in piece by piece, watching each number curl and blacken and lift as ash into the wet dark air where the rain swallowed it.

Then, gently, she laid the journal itself on the coals.

The cover blistered. The pages browned, silvered, caught. And as the fire took it she read what she could of the last lines one final time — *let them keep it, let the valley keep it* — until the words themselves burned, and Tom Reyes passed out of the world for the second and final time, leaving nothing behind but heat and a smell like a library after a flood.

She knelt there long after the flames had eaten everything, tears running freely now, making no effort at dignity. Felix's hand came to rest on her shoulder — heavy, steady, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the fire hadn't already said better.

Across the camp, Cole stirred and rasped, "Did we win?"

June smoothed his blanket. "Ask her tomorrow."

"No," Dara said quietly, without turning around. Her voice was raw and level and certain for the first time in ten years. "Tonight. We won tonight. Everything after this is just getting home."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and looked up at Felix, and beyond him at the northern sky, where above the ragged treeline the clouds had begun to stack — tall, bruised, silent, building themselves into the wall they had been racing for two weeks.

The first thunder rolled down the valley like a door closing.

Felix followed her gaze and said, almost gently, "That's our clock. Monsoon's coming early. We move at first light — and Dara? After tonight, we don't stop moving until we hit salt water."
