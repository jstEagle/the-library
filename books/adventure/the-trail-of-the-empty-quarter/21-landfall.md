## 21. Landfall

The monsoon did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision.

Felix saw it first as a bruise on the southern horizon at dawn, a wall of cloud stacked from the water to the stratosphere, purple-black and utterly still, the way a fist is still before it closes. By midmorning it had eaten half the sky. By noon the wind ahead of it was shoving the river flat, and Ansel's canoes were riding swells that had no business existing sixty miles inland.

"Coastal station by two o'clock," Sami shouted over the wind, holding the chart down with one knee. "Maybe. If the river doesn't turn on us."

"It already turned on us," Cole said from the bottom of the lead canoe, where he lay with his shoulder bound and his fever talking to him in a low, cheerful murmur. "Everything turned on us. That's just what rivers do."

Felix almost laughed. Ten days ago Cole would have said something like that and meant nothing by it. Now every joke came out of him like a man paying out rope, hand over hand, not sure how much was left.

The last stretch of the river widened into an estuary, brown water braiding through mudflats, and the station appeared out of the haze the way it always did — a fuel dock, three Quonset huts, a radio mast leaning like a drunk against the sky, and the long gravel strip where floatplanes and wheeled aircraft shared space with stacked drums and nobody's ambition. Port Meridian. The end of the map that mattered and the beginning of the map that didn't.

The rain hit them a mile out.

It didn't fall so much as arrive — a gray curtain sweeping up the estuary at forty knots, erasing the far shore, then the near shore, then everything but the sound of it on the water, which was less like rain than like gravel poured onto tin. Felix hauled his paddle and watched the station dissolve. Visibility dropped to fifty yards, then thirty.

"Sami!" he bellowed. "Bearing!"

"Two-seven-zero! The mast should be — there!" A shape resolved out of the gray: the radio mast, then the fuel dock, black lines swimming in white noise.

They came alongside the dock in a chaos of fenders and shouting hands, and the station hands — two men and a woman in oilskins who'd been battening hatches — grabbed gunwales and hauled them in. Felix got a line around a cleat with fingers he couldn't feel anymore and looked back down the canoes.

Cole was conscious. Barely. His face was the color of the water, and when they lifted him out he made no sound at all, which frightened Felix more than any scream would have.

* * *

The station kept a clinic the way it kept everything else: barely. One room behind the mess hut, a cot, a cabinet of antibiotics older than Sami, and a shortwave link to a doctor in Kodiak who answered questions between static bursts. The station nurse — Marta, sixty, forearms like dock rope — took one look at Cole's shoulder and started cutting his shirt off without asking anyone anything.

"Gunshot," she said. Not a question either.

"Accident," Felix said. "Hunting party. Rifle went off during a portage."

Marta looked at him over Cole's body for a long moment. Her eyes went to the wound — a through-and-through, cleaned in the field, sewn by June with fishing line and a curved needle boiled in a tin cup — and then back to Felix's face. Rain hammered the roof like applause from a hostile crowd.

"Hunting accident," she repeated.

"Portages are dangerous."

"They are," Marta said, and turned back to her work. Whatever lived behind her eyes stayed there. Stations like this survived on discretion the way trees survived on roots; you didn't ask about other people's business because someday yours would be the business not asked about. She gave Cole penicillin and quinine and fluids, wrapped him in blankets, and told Felix, without looking up, "He needs a real hospital. Kodiak, if the weather ever breaks. Which it won't, not for days."

"How many days?"

"As many as the sky wants." She finally looked up. "You people look like you walked out of the drowned world. Sit down before you fall down. There's coffee. It's terrible. It's also coffee."

* * *

The officials came at dusk, or what passed for dusk under the monsoon — a darkness that fell at four in the afternoon and never fully committed to leaving. Two of them, flown in from the regional capital before the weather closed the strip: a transport authority man named Brandt with a clipboard and a rain jacket too new to have ever been rained on, and a younger woman from the provincial office whose name tag read OKAFOR and whose eyes missed nothing, which Felix noted the way a man notes the location of every exit in a room.

They sat in the mess hut with the stove going and the whole crew arranged around the table — Felix, June, Sami, Dara, Ansel, and Marek, who sat propped in the corner with his splinted wrist in his lap and his face arranged into an expression of injured innocence that Felix had come to find almost impressive in its craftsmanship.

"So," Brandt said. "You want to file a report. Let's hear it."

"A survey flight," Felix said. "Contracted assessment of a decommissioned airstrip in the upper interior. We flew in, found the strip unusable, got caught by the early monsoon, lost our aircraft to a gear failure on landing, and came out by river." He laid it out flat and dull, each fact a brick in a very boring wall. "Nobody died. We'd like to report the aircraft as a loss and get a lift south when the weather clears."

Brandt wrote. Okafor didn't. She turned her chair slightly toward Dara.

"You're listed as expedition financier. Okonkwo. You funded a survey of a strip that isn't on any chart we have."

"It was on charts," Dara said. "Old ones. My family held mineral claims in that country twenty years ago. I wanted to know if anything was left worth salvaging." She shrugged, and it was a good shrug, tired and honest-seeming. "Nothing was. The strip is alder and muskeg. I've paid for a very expensive lesson."

"And your geologist?" Okafor's eyes moved to Marek. "Mr. — "

"Vasik," Marek said smoothly. "I slipped on the portage trail. These gentlemen helped me out. I'm eager to get home."

"I'll bet," Okafor said, and let it sit there for three full seconds before writing something.

June spoke next, and Felix could have kissed her. She gave them the logistics — fuel figures, weight calculations, the gear-failure story in technical detail so granular that Brandt's pen actually slowed down trying to keep up. Sami backed her with the navigation log, pages of bearings and drift corrections and weather observations copied out in the canoe camps by lantern light, meticulous, plausible, and purged of every heading east of the last lake. They had burned the real log on the second-to-last night, page by page in the cook fire, while Ansel stood watch and the rain came down like judgment.

Okafor flipped through the copy. "This wreck site. The aircraft you're abandoning. Anyone else likely to go poking at it?"

"There's nobody out there," Felix said. "That's rather the point of the place."

"Mm." She closed the folder. "We'll need statements from everyone individually. Standard procedure."

They gave them, one at a time, in the station office with the rain roaring on the tin roof. Felix went last. He told his version again, and it came easier each time, worn smooth like river stone, until even he could almost see it: a failed survey, a broken plane, a hard walk out. Almost.

At the door, Okafor stopped him.

"The station nurse says one of your men has a gunshot wound."

"Fishing accident," Felix said. "Speargun, on the river. He's clumsy."

"He's shot through the shoulder."

"People are clumsy in different directions."

She studied him for a while. Outside, the monsoon threw itself against the building like something wanting in. Then she said, quietly, "Ten years ago there was an expedition out of this district. Five men went upcountry. Four came back. Nobody filed a report on that either."

Felix felt the floor tilt, very slightly, the way a deck tilts in a swell.

"No," he said. "Nobody did."

Okafor nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd only suspected. "My predecessor pulled those files once. They're thin. Thin files bother me more than thick ones." She tucked her folder under her arm. "Get your man to Kodiak when the weather breaks, Mr. Whitlock. And whatever happened up there — " she tipped her head toward the north, toward all of it — "the best reports are the ones that never need filing. I've learned to recognize them. I file them myself, sometimes. As nothing."

She left. Felix stood in the doorway a long time, watching the rain, understanding that he had just been offered something that wasn't mercy exactly, and wasn't blackmail either. Something rarer. A professional courtesy between people who understood that some maps were better left blank.

* * *

They found Vossen's evidence washed up on the tide flats the next morning, or what the storm had left of it.

Sami spotted it first — a glint of aluminum among the wrack line, and went out with June and Ansel to pick through the debris the estuary had sorted for them like a patient housekeeper. A hard case, sprung open, its contents gone to soup. A satellite phone, dead, its screen a spiderweb. A GPS unit full of river. Two rifles, rusting even as they lay there, their serial numbers filed to ghosts. A waterproof document pouch, empty, its zipper torn away. And a boot. Just one, size eleven, laced, floating in a tide pool with the patience of all abandoned things.

No bodies. The river had taken the men and given back only their equipment, and the equipment told the story perfectly: a retrieval team, caught by the flood, swept away, lost with all hands. No bullet holes. No burn marks. Nothing that contradicted the clean, boring narrative taking shape in triplicate in the station office.

"Should we report these?" Sami asked, turning the dead phone over. "Say we found them?"

"And explain how four armed men came to be drowning in a river none of them had any business being on?" June shook her head. "Let the tide do the paperwork."

They carried what was useful — the rifles went into the station's lockup with a story about finding them adrift, because stations always needed rifles and asked few questions — and left the rest for the gulls and the mud. On the way back across the flats, Ansel stopped at the wrack line and looked north, into the rain, toward the country they'd come out of.

"My grandmother says the valley will forget you in a generation," he said. "But it will remember Tom Reyes forever. That's the trade she thinks is fair." He picked up a stone and skipped it into the gray water, once, twice, three times. "I think she's right. I think most trades are worse than that one."

* * *

On the third day the sky cracked open just enough. A Kodiak-bound mail plane, a battered old Twin Otter with medevac markings, squeezed through the gap and sat down on the wet gravel trailing spray. Marta loaded Cole aboard on a stretcher, IV bag taped to the ceiling rail, his fever finally broken but his color still wrong.

Cole caught Felix's sleeve as they slid the stretcher in.

"Hey, Skip." His voice was sandpaper. "The journal. The strike data. All of it. Tell me it's — "

"Gone," Felix said. "All of it. Burned or drowned. There's nothing left to sell and nothing left to steal."

Cole stared at him, and then something moved through his ruined face that might have been relief, or exhaustion, or both wearing the same coat. "Good," he said. "That's — yeah. Good." His eyes drifted shut. "Worst paying job I ever took."

"Best one too," Felix said, but Cole was already asleep, and the plane was already lifting, and the words went with it into the clouds.

Dara stood at the edge of the strip watching it go, small against the enormous sky, her duffel at her feet. She was booked on the same plane south — there was nothing left to keep her here, and everything behind her that needed facing: the consortium, the lawyers, the wreckage of a company that had used her grief as a hunting license. She'd spent two days drafting letters and making calls from the station radio, and her voice on the airwaves had gotten colder and more precise with each one, until Felix had stopped listening, because it sounded like a glacier calving.

"You could come south," she said now, not looking at him. "There'll be inquiries. Investigations, maybe. People asking questions about Marek, about the money. It would help to have you all telling the same boring story in the same room."

"We tell it fine from here."

"I know. That's not why I'm asking." She turned then, and the rain had gotten into her hair, and for a moment she looked ten years younger and ten years more tired. "Tom's compass. I'm leaving it with June, with the note. But I wanted to say — " She stopped. Started again. "He waited twenty years for someone from the old life to walk up that river. And when we finally did, it was you. Of all people. And you chose the valley over the map." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He'd have hated that. And he'd have known you were right."

"Dara — "

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Apologies are ballast, Whitlock. I'm traveling light from here on." She picked up her duffel, walked to the plane, climbed the steps, and did not look back. The Otter lifted off into the broken sky and was gone, and the monsoon closed the hole behind it like water closing over a stone.

* * *

That evening the crew sat in the mess hut while the storm worked itself hoarse outside. June had the stove stoked and a pot of the terrible coffee going, and Sami was drawing on the table in spilled sugar — new routes, the coming season's contracts, medevac runs and mail runs and fuel hauls, the small honest economy of staying alive. Ansel had a berth south with the next plane out; tonight he taught Sami a card game from the valley that had no winning, only continuing, which Sami declared was either profound or rigged.

Felix sat with his back to the wall and listened to them, and felt the strangest thing settle over him, light as ash.

They had come north with a map and a lie and come home with neither. They were poorer by a plane's landing gear, a season's profits, and Cole's blood. Somewhere behind them a valley slept under the first snows of the early storm, keeping its secret the way it had kept it for twenty years, and somewhere south a woman was dismantling a consortium letter by letter, and somewhere in a clinic bed a loadmaster was learning to shoot left-handed.

And tomorrow — or the day after, when the weather allowed — Felix would file a new flight plan. Routine. Medicine and mail, the usual circuit. Nothing worth printing.

June refilled his cup without being asked. "You're grinning," she said suspiciously. "Stop it. It's unsettling."

"I'm not grinning."

"You are. It's the grin of a man who's about to do something stupid and free."

Felix looked into his coffee, at his own warped reflection in its black surface, and found she was right.

Outside, the rain went on falling on the station, on the flats, on the whole drowned green country to the north, forgiving nothing and forgetting less — and for the first time in ten years, Felix Whitlock felt no need to be forgiven, and nothing left to forget.
