## 22. The Trail Ahead

The hangar smelled of fresh lumber and turpentine, and June Okada had decided she liked it better than the old smell, which had been oil, mildew, and ten years of Felix Whitlock's stubbornness soaked into the rafters.

She stood on the new catwalk with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the morning work itself out below. The north wall was up — new planks still pale as bone against the weathered gray of the surviving structure — and the float dock had been rebuilt two feet wider, because Cole had insisted that if he was going to nearly die on a river he'd never consented to run, the least the base could do was have a dock you could land a canoe on without swearing. Sami had painted the fuel drums a cheerful, defiant yellow. Someone had hung a windsock off the tower that hadn't flown since before the fire.

The fire had taken three days to explain to the insurance people and would take years to explain to anyone who'd actually been there. *Electrical fault in the generator shed*, the report said. *Total loss of one De Havilland Beaver's spare parts inventory, one storage annex, and approximately nothing else worth mentioning.* June had signed her name under Felix's and felt the lie settle into her hand like a callus. It wasn't even a hard lie. The hardest part was how boring it sounded. Four men with rifles and a corporate fixer named Vossen, a night of flares and burning fuel, Cole's shoulder opened by a round that should have killed him — all of it compressed down to *electrical fault* so that some adjustor in the city could sleep well and pay out.

The wilderness forgave nothing and forgot less. But paperwork, it turned out, forgot everything.

Below her, Cole sat on an upturned crate in the sun, his left arm in a sling that Sami had decorated with marker doodles — a cartoon beaver, a fish with an attitude problem, and in small letters along the strap: *PROPERTY OF THE MONSOON*. He was supposed to be resting. He was instead supervising Sami's engine work with the serene tyranny of a man who had been told by three doctors that he'd fly again by autumn and intended to hold them to it.

"Torque sequence," Cole called up to her, without turning around. "He's doing it wrong."

"He is not," Sami said from inside the cowling, voice echoing off metal. "I'm doing it *differently*."

"Differently is wrong with torque."

June smiled and drank her cold coffee. Six weeks ago this man had been gray-lipped and feverish in the bottom of a canoe while Ansel paddled them through water that wanted them dead, and now he was arguing about fasteners in the sun like none of it had happened. The fever had broken on the third day at the coastal clinic, thanks to antibiotics and what the nurse described as Cole's "unreasonable constitution." The shoulder was another matter. The bullet had gone through clean, missing everything that mattered by what the surgeon called "an insulting margin." Cole had asked him to put that in writing so he could frame it.

Felix came out of the office carrying the flight board, and June watched him the way she'd been watching him for six weeks — not anxiously anymore, but attentively, the way you watch a joint you once saw break. Something in him had come back from the north different. Not lighter. She'd expected lighter, after they burned the pages, after Dara wept over Tom Reyes's last words and fed them to the fire. But Felix hadn't gotten lighter. He'd gotten *quieter*, and there was a difference. A man who sets down a weight he's carried ten years doesn't always stand taller. Sometimes he just stands differently, discovering where his balance used to live.

He pinned the flight board to the wall by the door and stood looking at it, and June came down the stairs to see what he'd written.

*WHITLOCK AIR — SCHEDULED RUNS — EFFECTIVE MONDAY.*

And beneath it, in his square engineer's handwriting:

*MED-1: Fort Kessler — supplies.*
*MED-2: Two Rivers — Dr. Amari, rotation.*
*MED-3: North.*

Just *North.* No destination. No lake name, no strip designation, no coordinates. June looked at it, then at him.

"North," she said.

"North," Felix agreed.

"That's not a flight plan. That's a direction."

"It's the only kind I trust right now." He said it lightly, but his eyes went to the big chart on the office wall — the master regional chart, the one every pilot on the coast could read through the window — and June followed his gaze to the blank space in the upper quadrant where the high country lived. Officially blank. Unofficially, everyone knew something was up there; officially, no one asked. Ten years ago Felix had inked over that whole region on his own working copies with a grease pencil and a word: *FORBIDDEN.* The grease marks were still there, faded to a ghost-gray shadow under the new plastic laminate. He'd never cleaned them off. She'd assumed he never would.

But yesterday evening she'd found him at the chart table with a cloth and a bottle of solvent, working at the old grease line with the patience of a man restoring a painting, and when she'd asked, he'd said only: "It's not forbidden anymore. It's just far away. There's a difference, and I want the chart to know it."

Now the chart showed the region clean — terrain contours, the big lakes, the river systems, all of it accurate, all of it public knowledge, and none of it pointing anywhere in particular. The valley itself remained unmarked. It would remain unmarked until the end of June's life, if she had anything to say about it, and she intended to have quite a lot to say about it.

"You're really filing MED-3," she said. "Medicine run. Into the high country."

"Ansel sent word down with the fall freight — Ilsa wants a proper course of penicillin before winter, and the clinic chest we left them needs restocking. There's a girl with a bad lung. Tuberculosis, probably, or something wearing its coat." Felix folded his arms. "I told them we'd come."

"Did you tell them you'd come *alone*?"

"I told them I'd come carrying medicine and nothing else." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Ilsa will check. You know she'll check. She'll meet the plane with half the valley behind her and go through every crate like customs officers with grudges."

"Good," June said. "I hope she frisks you personally."

"She probably will. She spat at my surname once. She can spit at my luggage too."

There it was again — that dryness, that ease, that was new and that June was still learning to read. Before the north, Felix's humor had been armor. Now it was just... humor. She didn't fully understand what had changed in him, except that she'd watched a man spend ten years believing he'd abandoned his partner to die, and then find out his partner had lived twenty years, married, raised children into a community, built a cabin with his own hands, and died defending it — and that the abandonment had never happened at all. Tom Reyes hadn't been lost. He'd been *found*, decades ago, by people who kept him. And Felix's guilt, which had been the load-bearing wall of his whole personality, had turned out to be built on a false foundation. Guilt like that doesn't vanish when the foundation goes. It takes weeks to come down, room by room, and Felix was still clearing the debris. But the house was standing.

Sami emerged from the cowling, wiping her hands, and joined them at the board. She read MED-3 twice.

"North," she said. "Alone?"

"Just me."

Sami looked at June. June looked at Sami. This was the thing about rookies — they hadn't yet learned which silences meant *don't push* and which ones meant *push, he needs pushing.* Sami still treated every silence as an open question, which was exactly why Felix had promoted her.

Because he had. Three days after the coastal station, over bad coffee in the clinic waiting room while Cole slept off his fever upstairs, Felix had handed Sami a laminated card and said, "Navigator, full rating. You earned it twice — once in the air, once on the river." Sami had cried, briefly and furiously, and then threatened them both with death if they mentioned it. Since then she'd flown co-pilot on every med run, logged forty hours of actual navigation, and reorganized the entire map drawer by a system only she understood, which Felix claimed was insubordination and June claimed was the first sensible thing anyone had done to that drawer in a decade.

"You shouldn't go alone," Sami said now. "Monsoon season's late tail is still sitting on the passes. If you go down—"

"If I go down, I'll walk out. I've done it before." A beat. "Badly. But I've done it."

"Felix." Sami planted herself in front of him, and June recognized the stance — the same one Sami had used in the crew meeting eleven weeks ago, when she'd voted yes on the job nobody should have taken, and then spent the whole expedition being right about everything. "You taught me that a solo flight into marginal weather with a marginal strip and no filed alternate isn't courage, it's arithmetic. Your words. So do the arithmetic."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then at June. Then at the board.

"The strip's wet but serviceable," he said. "Ilsa's people keep it drained. Ansel marked it with new reflectors — orange drums, you can't miss it. Weather window Thursday, maybe Friday. One day in, one day out, medicine both ways." He paused. "And I'm not going alone. I'm going with their permission, which is more than most pilots ever get, and it weighs less than a second seat."

"It weighs more," Sami said. "Permission weighs more than anything. That's why you're scared of it."

June hid her grin in her coffee. The kid was going to be insufferable within a year, and it would be entirely deserved.

Cole got up off his crate and ambled over, sling and all, moving with the careful economy of a man whose shoulder still reported daily. He studied the board.

"MED-3," he read. "North. Solo. Carrying medicine and nothing else." He nodded slowly. "That's a good line. *Nothing else.* You should get it printed on the fuselage."

"Cole."

"No, I mean it." He turned, and for once there was no joke anywhere near his face. "Six weeks I've been lying in that clinic thinking about what I'd have done if Vossen had offered me the money. Straight offer, cash, buy my silence. And I want to tell you honestly, Felix — I don't know what I'd have said. I genuinely don't. I like money. Money bought this dock." He tapped the rail with his good hand. "But then I think about that girl with the bad lung, and Ilsa checking your crates, and Ansel paddling us through water that should've killed all of us because his grandmother told him to, and I think — okay. Okay. Some things aren't for sale, and the amazing part is how cheap it turns out to be one of them." He shrugged, wincing. "So. Medicine and nothing else. Print it on the plane. I'll letter it myself."

"You can't hold a brush," Sami said.

"I'll hold it with my *good* arm, Sami, I'm crippled, not useless."

June laughed, and the sound went out over the water and came back off the far shore, and for a moment the base felt like what it had always pretended to be: a family business. Which, she supposed, it finally was.

* * *

Dara's note arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope thick enough to contain more than paper, postmarked from a city none of them associated with her.

June found it in the mail crate and carried it up to the office, where Felix was doing the accounts — the accounts being a ledger that currently showed a healthy insurance settlement, a depleted operating fund, triple-pay invoices paid in full by Okonkwo Resources before Dara had learned what her money had actually funded, and a long column of red that represented, in June's professional opinion, the true cost of doing the right thing. They were poorer than they'd been in years. Felix had said, the night they tallied it, "We were poor before and we were haunted. I prefer poor and free." June had checked the math twice and decided he was right, which frightened her more than the numbers did.

The envelope sat on the desk between them. FELIX WHITLOCK, in handwriting June had last seen signing a contract in fluorescent hangar light — sharp, slanted, certain of itself. Except it wasn't certain anymore. The letters had slowed somewhere between then and now.

Felix opened it with a knife, carefully, the way you open something that might be either a wound or a gift.

Inside: a single sheet of paper, and a compass.

Not any compass. June knew it instantly, the way you know a face from a photograph you've studied too long. Brass case, dented bezel, a lanyard ring repaired with wire — Tom Reyes's compass, the one from the expedition photographs, the one that had gone into the mist with him ten years ago and come out of the high country twenty years after that, worn smooth by a lifetime of northern hands. Dara must have gotten it from Ilsa. From the cabin. From the grave.

The note was short. June read it over Felix's shoulder, because there was no version of events in which she wouldn't.

*Felix —*

*I'm writing this from a hotel where the ice machine hums all night and nobody has ever fired a rifle at me, and I find I hate it. The city fits worse than it used to. That's not your problem.*

*I stayed a week past the funeral rites. Ilsa let me sit with the cabin for a day. I read what was left to read. I'm not going to tell you what it said — some of it was mine alone, and I'm keeping it, the way you're keeping yours.*

*I sold the company. All of it. The consortium's retrieval contract is void, Vossen's employers are pretending he drowned in a boating accident, which is technically what happened to his equipment and close enough to what happened to his plans. There are men in suits very angry with me and I find I don't care, which is either growth or brain damage.*

*I took the compass because Ilsa gave it to me and I've carried it long enough. It points home for me and it isn't mine. It was never mine. It was his, and his people kept him, and now it should point at the people who kept his friend honest for ten years and finally, finally set him down.*

*Tell the fat one with the shoulder I owe him a beer. Tell the rookie she was right about everything and I hate her. Tell June — tell June she ran a river with a stranger's grief in her boat and never once made me feel like cargo. That's rarer than the mineral. Don't sell that either.*

*Some trails are meant to stay empty. I used to think that was a coward's proverb. I know now it's the hardest sentence in the language, and the only one worth learning twice.*

*Fly north for me. Look at the water for me. Don't land unless they wave you in.*

*— Dara*

P.S. The apology I owed you in the hangar, the one you wouldn't take? Consider it delivered to Ilsa, who accepted it on your behalf by making me gut fish for four days. We're even. Don't write back.

Felix read it twice. Then he picked up the compass and held it flat on his palm, and June watched the needle swing, hesitate, and settle — steady, unwavering, pointing north-northwest, toward the high country, toward a valley that appeared on no chart and a cabin that no longer had anyone in it and a grave that Dara had wept over and left flowers on and walked away from.

"She sold the company," June said quietly.

"She sold the company." Felix turned the compass over. On the back, scratched deep and old, worn nearly smooth: *T.R. — find your way back.* Someone had added a second line beneath it, newer, in a different hand: *He did.*

June felt her throat tighten and elected to ignore it. "What are you going to do with it?"

"What she said. Carry it until it's not mine either." He clipped it to the lanyard he wore under his shirt — the one that had held his father's dog tags, then nothing, then, for ten years, a folded photograph of a crew that came back one man short. The photograph was gone now. June had noticed its absence weeks ago and hadn't asked. The compass settled against his chest and he buttoned his shirt over it like a man closing a door gently.

* * *

Thursday came in clean and cold, the monsoon's late tail pulled back east like a curtain drawn by a considerate hand. By six a.m. the sky over the base was the hard washed blue that follows real rain, and the Beaver sat on the dock gleaming, freshly washed, freshly inspected, and — across the cowl, in Cole's careful block letters, three coats of white enamel — the legend he'd promised:

*MEDICINE AND NOTHING ELSE.*

Cole had painted it himself over three evenings, one-handed, swearing magnificently, refusing all help. Sami had added, in much smaller letters underneath, barely legible: *(mostly)*. Felix had allowed it.

June stood on the dock with Sami and Cole and watched Felix load. Penicillin in insulated crates. A rebuilt clinic chest. Blankets, boots, school supplies Ansel had requested with the gravity of a man ordering munitions. A bag of oranges, because Ilsa's granddaughter had tried one at the coastal clinic in the spring and talked about it for a week, according to the note. No survey gear. No satellite phone beyond the emergency unit sealed and registered. No cameras. No maps beyond the public chart. Nothing that could be photographed, weighed, drilled, or sold.

"Radio check at noon," Sami said, running the preflight with him out of habit and love in equal measure. "If you miss it, I file overdue at twelve-thirty, and if you miss the return window tomorrow I file SAR at dawn whether you like it or not."

"Yes, Navigator."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are. That's why you're Navigator." He shook her hand formally, which made her scowl and blush simultaneously, then gripped Cole's good hand. "Stay off the torque wrench."

"Differently is wrong with torque," Cole said solemnly. "Go on, old man. Fly gentle."

Last was June. He stood on the float beside her, engine ticking over behind him, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The lake lay flat as poured glass. Somewhere north, behind three ranges and a wall of cloud-country, a valley waited with its fires lit and its people watching the sky, and a woman named Ilsa who remembered everything, and a boy named Ansel who had carried them out of hell on a paddle and a promise.

"You could wait a week," June said. "Let the passes clear properly."

"They've cleared. And there's a girl who needs the penicillin before frost." He paused. "Also I made a promise to a dead man's river. You don't break those. You just... go back and keep them, quietly, forever."

She put her hand on his shoulder — the uninjured one, though his injuries were all healed now, all except the ones that weren't. "Come home with nothing," she said. "That's the whole mission. Come home having given everything away and carrying nothing back. Can you do that?"

Felix Whitlock smiled, and it reached all the way up, and June thought: *there he is. Ten years late, but there he is.*

"June," he said, "that's the only cargo manifest I've ever trusted."

He stepped onto the float, swung into the cockpit, and ran the checks with Sami calling them alongside him, her voice crisp, his calm, the two of them finishing each other's checklist like a liturgy. The engine caught. The prop wash flattened the water in a silver fan. He taxied out to the middle of the lake, turned into the wind, and paused — one moment, one breath, the way he always did, and June realized he was touching the compass through his shirt.

Then the Beaver lifted, clean and easy, climbing into the washed blue morning, banking once over the base — the old salute, but slower now, held longer, a salute and something else, a thank-you or a goodbye or a promise — and turned north.

They watched it until it shrank to a speck, and the speck to a rumor, and the rumor to nothing, and the lake closed over the sound of the engine like water over a stone.

Cole broke the silence first. "MED-4 next week. Two Rivers. I want it."

"You're on medical leave."

"I'm on *boredom*, June. There's a difference and it's killing me."

Sami was already walking back toward the hangar, chart tube under her arm, and she called over her shoulder without turning: "Filed. Monday, oh-six-hundred. And Cole — you're loadmaster, not copilot. Touch the yoke and I log you."

June lingered on the dock a moment longer. Far to the north, above the tree line, the high country stood blue and silent under its clouds, keeping its valley, keeping its dead, keeping its secret the way it had always kept everything — completely, and without gratitude, and forever.

She picked up the coffee mug Felix had left on the piling, still half full, still warm.

"Some trails are meant to stay empty," she said aloud, to no one, to the water, to the north.

Then she carried it back to the hangar, poured it out, and went to work.
