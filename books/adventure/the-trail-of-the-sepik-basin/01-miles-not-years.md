## 1. Miles, Not Years

The river at dawn was the color of old pewter, and Cyrus Sterling loved it the way you love a thing that has already taken something from you — carefully, with your hands open and your eyes on its mouth.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Fortune* while the mist lifted off the Sepik delta in slow rags, and she did what she did every morning of the last three years: she counted. Not days. Days were for people who believed time healed anything, and Cyrus had stopped believing that somewhere around the second year, when the grief stopped being a wound and became instead a kind of weather — always present, sometimes rain, occasionally a storm that flattened her without warning. No, she counted miles. Nautical miles logged, nautical miles dived, nautical miles between where she stood and the last known coordinates of her brother's ship.

Eleven thousand four hundred and six.

It was not a comforting number. It was not meant to be. It was a ledger, and ledgers do not comfort; they only keep faith.

"You're up early." Salim's voice came from the galley hatch, warm as the coffee smell trailing after it. He appeared with two enamel mugs, gray-haired and deliberate, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had spent forty years learning that haste was just another way of breaking things. "Or you never went down."

"Never went down," Cyrus admitted, taking the mug. The coffee was terrible and perfect, cut with condensed milk the way he made it when he thought no one was watching him be kind.

"Third night this week."

"Fourth."

Salim said nothing to that. That was one of the reasons she kept him — he understood that some silences were a form of company rather than absence. He leaned on the rail beside her and looked out at the water with the expression of a man reading a familiar letter.

"Bo says the wreck's sitting in eighteen meters," he offered after a while. "Visibility maybe two. Current's slack until nine."

"I know."

"He also says the navy chart marks this grid restricted. Red ink. Bo doesn't like red ink."

Cyrus turned the mug in her hands. Below them, thirty feet down, lay the bones of a patrol boat that had gone down eleven years ago with a cargo manifest the government had never released and a hull number that matched nothing in any registry Bo could find. It was exactly the kind of wreck that paid well and asked too many questions, and Cyrus had learned — expensively, repeatedly — that the two qualities traveled together like current and tide.

"Red ink means someone wanted us to stay away," she said. "Which means there's something worth finding."

"Or it means someone wanted people who went looking to disappear." Salim sipped his coffee. "You have a brother-shaped blind spot, girl. I say that with love."

The words landed the way they always did — not as an insult but as an accurate description of a fact she carried like ballast. Tomas Sterling, lieutenant, naval salvage and recovery, declared missing along with fourteen others during what the official record called *a routine hydrographic survey* in the upper Sepik Basin. No wreckage recovered. No bodies. A folded flag delivered to an empty house because their mother had died eight months before and the navy hadn't updated its paperwork. Cyrus had signed for him herself, standing in a room that smelled of floor wax, holding a flag heavier than any anchor she'd ever raised.

Three years. Eleven thousand four hundred and six miles. And still, every morning, the first thing she did was count the distance between herself and the place they said he wasn't.

"I'll dive it before the current turns," she said. "Nia can run the line."

"Nia is sixteen."

"Nia is better with the tender than half the deckhands I've employed, and she reads water like Bo reads engines." Cyrus drained the coffee. "And she wants to learn. You remember wanting to learn?"

Salim looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him sigh and take her empty mug. "I remember being young enough to think the river owed me answers," he said. "Go wake the others. I'll make breakfast worth diving back for."

* * *

Bo was already in the engine room, of course. Bo was always already in the engine room; Cyrus suspected he slept sitting upright against the main housing, arms crossed, daring the machinery to misbehave. He was sixty-one years old and built like something dredged from a harbor — squat, rust-streaked, immovable — with forearms that had more scar tissue than skin and a face that suggested all expression had been removed at the factory as unnecessary weight.

He looked up when she dropped into the compartment, wiped his hands on a rag that was itself mostly grease, and said, "No."

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were going to ask me about the red-ink grid, and the answer's no." He went back to the fuel pump he was rebuilding, though his eyes stayed on her. "Restricted waters, Cyrus. Navy-restricted. We've got a legal claim filed on three wrecks in safe grids, we've got a contract coming through Port Moresby next month, and we've got a crew of four, which is three fewer than I'd want going into water somebody's already told us to stay out of."

"We've done restricted grids before."

"We've *snuck into* restricted grids before, which is different, and each time I aged a decade and gained a ulcer." He set down a part with exaggerated care, the way he did when he was angry enough to want to throw it. "Ask me why the navy restricts a stretch of empty river delta."

"Because there's something under it."

"Because there's something under it they don't want found, or something under it they don't want *explained*, and both of those mean guns, Cyrus. Guns and paperwork and men in pressed uniforms asking questions we can't answer." He finally looked at her straight on, and beneath the gruffness she saw the thing that was actually there, the thing that had been there since the day she'd hired him off a broken-down tug in Lae and he'd decided, silently and permanently, that she was his to look after: fear. Not for himself. "I'm not losing this crew over your brother's ghost."

The words should have stung. They didn't, because Bo never wasted cruelty and never used it accidentally; he said *ghost* because he knew that was the truest word, and true words were the only ones he respected.

"He's not a ghost," Cyrus said quietly. "Ghosts are dead. Nobody ever gave me proof of dead."

"No. They didn't give you proof of alive, either. That's the trap, isn't it? The not-knowing." Bo picked the part back up. "Eighteen meters, you said. Two-meter vis. Croc water above and silt below." He worked in silence for a moment, then, without looking up: "Slack water's at nine. If you're diving it, you dive it by the book. Full checks. Nia on the tender with the knife and the radio, and if the current turns early, you come up whether you've found God himself down there or not."

Cyrus felt the grin arrive on her face before she authorized it. "You're a soft touch, Bo."

"I'm a fool with a good boat," he corrected. "Get out of my engine room."

* * *

Nia met her at the tender with the gear already staged, and Cyrus watched her out of the corner of her eye while pretending to check her own kit, because watching Nia directly made the girl self-conscious and self-conscious deckhands made mistakes.

Marisela Niamh Okoro — *Nia*, because the full name was too much cargo for a small boat — had come aboard two years ago in Wewak, thirteen years old, orphaned twice over, stowing away among the spare tanks with the specific ferocity of a child who had decided the sea was safer than land. Cyrus had intended to put her ashore at the next port. Instead she'd found the girl at 0500 re-organizing the dive locker with a precision that shamed the adults, and by Wewak-plus-three-days the arrangement had simply become permanent, the way the best things on the *Fortune* always did — not decided but accumulated, like barnacles, like family.

Now Nia ran the tender lines like she'd been born to them, checked Cyrus's gauges twice without being asked, and sat in the little boat with her hand on the knife and her eyes on the current, solemn as a churchwarden.

"Bottom's soft," she said. "Salim says the silt shifts with the tide, so if you stir it, you'll be diving blind in ten minutes."

"Then I won't stir it."

"And Bo says—"

"I know what Bo says."

Nia almost smiled. It was a rare currency with her these days; at thirteen she'd smiled easily, and at sixteen the smiles had started costing something, and Cyrus hated the world a little for that tax. "Regulator check," she said instead, businesslike, and Cyrus took the mouthpiece, cleared it, breathed.

The Sepik closed over her head like a door.

* * *

Down was a country with its own laws. At two meters the pewter light turned green-brown and warm; at ten it went to a brown so complete it felt tactile, like descending through amber; at fifteen her torch became the only sun in a private universe, and the beam carved tunnels through suspended sediment that drifted around her like snow falling upward. Eighteen meters. Her gauge read it, her depth sense confirmed it, and then the wreck rose out of the dark to meet her.

Patrol boats were common enough in these waters — coastal authorities, customs cutters, the occasional navy surplus sold and resold until provenance dissolved. But this one was wrong in ways that made the diver-brain behind her eyes go quiet and attentive. The hull lines were military, yes, but military from a navy nobody flew flags for anymore. The superstructure had been stripped — not salvaged, *stripped*, methodically, everything removable taken and everything identifying burned or ground away. Except.

Except the number.

Someone had missed it, or someone had left it, and Cyrus's torch found it low on the stern quarter where the silt had spared it: a hull designation in faded stencil, six characters, and the first three of them were a prefix she knew. She knew it the way you know a voice on a bad phone line. She knew it because she'd spent three years memorizing every prefix, every registry convention, every bureaucratic fingerprint of the service that had swallowed her brother and issued her a flag.

Her heart rate climbed. She watched it climb on her computer and made herself breathe it back down, because panic at eighteen meters in two-vis silt was how divers joined wrecks instead of raising them.

*Routine hydrographic survey,* the record said. Fourteen men. No wreckage recovered.

And here was wreckage. Here, in a red-ink grid, was a hull wearing the prefix of the very command that had declared Tomas Sterling lost, stripped clean of identity except for one stencil someone had failed to burn.

Cyrus hung in the green dark beside the dead ship and felt the counting start again, unbidden — not miles now but years, months, days, hours, the whole arithmetic of absence she kept locked below decks — and she put her gloved hand flat against the cold steel of the hull as if she could take a pulse from it.

Nothing. Steel has no pulse. But it has memory, of a kind, and she'd spent her whole adult life learning to read it.

She worked the wreck for forty minutes, by the book, methodical, photographing the stencil, mapping the stripped compartments, noting the odd detail that the engine room showed scorch patterns consistent with fire *before* sinking — deliberate fire, contained fire, the kind you set when you want a story to end. Her slate filled with numbers and sketches. And near the end, in the aft hold, her torch caught a shape the silt had half-swallowed: a chest. Iron-strapped, lead-lined by its weight when she tested it, sealed with a latch mechanism far too fine for a patrol boat's stores.

Stamped into the lid, beneath a film of river mud she wiped away with her thumb, was a seal. A double-headed bird inside a bordered roundel, worn but legible, and beneath it letters in an alphabet she recognized only because she'd once been a girl who collected atlases:

VASKARIA.

A nation that had ceased to exist before Cyrus was born. A name from the dead pages of history, riding in the hold of a navy ghost in a forbidden grid, three hundred miles from anywhere it had any right to be.

Her computer began its slow ascent alarm. The current was turning. Somewhere above, Nia would be watching the line go taut, and Bo would be timing her, and Salim would be burning breakfast on purpose because burnt breakfast brought divers up faster than any alarm.

Cyrus gave the seal one last pass of her thumb, committing it to memory the way she committed everything — as distance, as bearing, as a fixed point in the dark — and kicked for the surface.

* * *

She came up into sunlight and Nia's anxious face, and she let them haul her aboard, and she said "nothing much, an old hull, stripped" because saying *Vaskaria* out loud on an open river with fishing skiffs within eyeshot was a calculation she hadn't finished making. But her hands weren't quite steady coiling the line, and Nia noticed — Nia noticed everything — and said nothing, which was its own kind of message.

That evening the four of them ate on deck because the heat below was unbearable, plates balanced on knees, the delta turning copper and then violet around them. Salim had made rice and smoked fish and a chili relish that made Bo swear happily, and for a while the boat was just a boat and the crew was just tired people eating, and Cyrus let herself have that, let herself sit inside the ordinary like a diver resting in a shallow cave with the storm passing overhead.

These three. This was what she had. Bo, arguing with Nia about whether a winch could be persuaded or merely obeyed; Salim, refilling plates without being asked, humming something in a language none of the rest of them spoke; Nia, laughing — actually laughing, the expensive laugh, spending it freely for once. Found family. Salvaged, like everything else aboard: pulled from wrecks, patched, kept running past all reasonable expectations.

Eleven thousand four hundred and six miles, and this was what the miles had bought her.

She'd tell them tomorrow, she decided. About the hull number. About the seal. She'd spread the photographs on the chart table and watch Bo's face close and Salim's face open and Nia lean in hungry, and together they'd decide — because that was how the *Fortune* worked; Cyrus might hold the claim and the wheel, but decisions belonged to all of them, and she'd sunk a partnership once by forgetting that.

Tomorrow.

But the river, which had been listening, apparently had other ideas, because it was barely full dark when Nia — on bow watch, as always, because the girl trusted the water less than anyone aboard and therefore watched it most — called out, low and careful:

"Cyrus. Boat coming up the channel. Running dark."

Cyrus rose and shaded her eyes against the last light. Far down the channel, a shape moved against the dying copper of the water — long, low, military-clean, showing no lights where any honest boat would show lights.

Behind her, she heard Bo stop mid-sentence in the engine room hatch, and Salim's humming go quiet, and the whole river seemed to hold its breath around them.

"Nobody honest runs dark on the Sepik," Salim murmured.

"No," Cyrus said, watching the shape grow. "They don't."
