## 2. The Chest with No Country

The river at dawn was the color of old pewter, and it breathed.

Cyrus Sterling had learned its breathing the way other people learned a lover's — the slow swell of the tide pushing up from the delta, the exhale of silt-laden current sliding back toward the sea, the small sighs of eddies turning over drowned logs. Three years on this water and she still woke before her alarm to listen to it, as if some morning it might say something new. It never did. That was the comfort of it. The Sepik did not lie to you, and it did not surprise you, and if you respected its moods it would let you take what it had swallowed and give nothing back in return.

She stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Fortune* with a mug of Salim's ginger tea going cold in her hands and watched the mist lift off the wreck site. Forty meters down, maybe forty-five where the channel had scoured itself deep against the old shipping lane, lay the hull they'd been working for six days: a coastal freighter, navy registry stripped but the weld patterns unmistakable, sitting half-buried in the mud like something that had tried to hide and failed. Restricted waters. The charts said NO ENTRY in letters that had faded to gray, but charts were paper and Cyrus was a woman who had run out of patience for paper three years ago, the day the navy sent a man in a dress uniform to her door to say that Lieutenant Tomas Sterling was lost with all hands and no further information would be made available.

No further information. She had turned those four words over so many times they'd worn smooth as river stones.

"You're up early." Bo's voice came from behind her, gravel over gravel. He lowered himself onto the bench by the compressor with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions about weather, which at fifty-eight was all weather. "Or you never went down."

"Never went down."

He grunted. In Bo's language, that was a whole conversation. He didn't ask about the dreams anymore; he'd stopped asking after the first year, when she'd told him once, flatly, that she dreamed of a door underwater and her brother on the far side of it, mouthing words she couldn't hear through the glass, and Bo had gone quiet for a long time and then said only, *Some doors you shouldn't open twice.* He'd been right about most things mechanical and wrong about that one. She opened the door every night.

Nia came up the companionway in a burst of energy that should have been illegal before sunrise, seventeen years old and all elbows and questions, hair already tied back under her cap. "Tide turns at oh-six-forty," she said, consulting the battered notebook she kept everything in — depths, currents, the moods of the river written out in her careful hand. "Visibility might hold till eight if the rain upstream doesn't come down. Salim says eggs."

"Salim always says eggs," Cyrus said.

"Salim is wise." Nia grinned, then sobered the way she did when she remembered she was supposed to be professional. "Last dive today, right? After this we're done with this hole?"

"After this we're done." Cyrus drained the cold tea. The ginger sat bitter on her tongue. Six days of salvage on a restricted wreck, cutting free whatever the freighter's hold still held — brass fittings, a generator winch worth real money, copper ingots stamped with a smelter's mark that Bo swore he could sell in Port Moresby without anyone asking questions. Enough to keep the *Fortune* fueled and fed for another season. Enough to keep the family afloat. That was all any dive ever was, now. Not treasure. Just keeping the lights on.

But today was the last dive, and last dives had their own gravity. Today she would go down into the dark one more time, and when she came up, she'd have to start being a person again instead of a function.

She began to kit up.

* * *

The water took her the way it always did, first cold as a slap, then warm as an embrace, then nothing at all — just pressure and the green-gold murk of the river, silt drifting past her mask like snow falling upward. Her torch beam carved a cone through the gloom. Below her, the wreck waited.

Diving was the only time her mind went quiet. Down here there was no past and no future, only the next breath, the next kick, the next meter of hull. She checked her gauges without thinking, ran her line along the rail she'd rigged on day two, and dropped through the torn-open cargo hatch into the freighter's belly.

The hold was a cathedral of rust. Light didn't reach here; her beam swung across collapsed shelving, a fallen crane arm furred with silt, the pale ghosts of fish hanging motionless at the edge of the light. She worked her way aft, toward the section they hadn't cleared yet — a walled-off compartment behind a buckled bulkhead, where the sonar had shown a void.

The bulkhead door was jammed. It had been jammed for six days, and she'd been telling herself she'd get to it, and every day something more profitable had come first. But it was the last dive, and last dives made liars honest.

She set her pry bar into the seam and leaned.

Metal shrieked — a sound like a scream compressed into steel, rolling away down corridors that hadn't heard a voice in decades. The door gave a centimeter, then another, then swung inward with a groan, and the water inside the compartment came out to meet her.

It came out black.

Not silt-black. Ink-black, oil-black, a darkness so complete it seemed to have weight, billowing around her like smoke, and for one irrational moment Cyrus's heart slammed against her ribs because it felt less like water than like something being released — something held shut for years behind that jammed door, kept in the dark, and now loose. Her beam vanished into it. She hung in the blackness, blind, breathing too fast, counting her breaths the way she'd taught herself, *four in, six out*, until the cloud began to thin and the torch found shapes again.

The compartment was small. An officer's secure store, maybe — the kind of space a ship keeps locked, the kind of space that holds things someone thought mattered more than the ship itself. Shelving along one wall, mostly rotted. And in the center of the deck, half-sunk into the mud that had crept in through the seams, a chest.

Lead-lined. She knew lead when she saw it even crusted in river filth — the dull, dense, unwilling shine of it, the way the metal seemed to drink light rather than return it. Someone had wanted the contents shielded. From water, from salt, from detection. From instruments, a voice in her head supplied, the voice that sounded like Bo. Lead-lined boxes are how you carry things you don't want found.

And stamped into the lid, beneath the crust of sixty years of river, a seal.

Cyrus brushed the silt away with her glove, gently, the way she'd touch a face.

An eagle with spread wings, standing on a broken chain. Beneath it, a motto in a script she recognized from history books and museum plaques and nowhere living: *EX UMBRA IN LUCEM.* Out of shadow, into light.

Vaskaria.

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Vaskaria, the nation that had existed for barely thirty years between the wars and dissolved in blood and border-redrawing before her parents were born, a country that survived now only in stamps and coins and the collections of men who mourned lost causes. There was no Vaskaria. There had been no Vaskaria since before her grandfather could walk. And yet here its seal was, pressed into lead, sunk in a navy-restricted wreck in the Sepik Basin, in a compartment that had been sealed from the inside.

Her air gauge read fine. Her hands were not steady anyway.

She clipped the lifting sling to the chest's forged rings and tested the weight — heavy, heavier than it looked, lead plus whatever slept inside it. Too heavy to swim up alone. She signaled the surface with three pulls on the line, and above her, faint through the water, she heard the *Fortune*'s winch wake up.

As the chest rose, turning slowly in the brown light, trailing mud like a shroud, Cyrus watched it go and felt something she couldn't name. It wasn't greed. Salvage fever she knew, and this wasn't it. It was closer to vertigo — the sensation she'd gotten once standing at the edge of a sinkhole in the karst country, looking down into water that had no bottom, understanding that the ground she trusted was thinner than she'd believed. The past was supposed to stay down. That was the arrangement. You buried things, and the river kept them, and you got to keep moving.

The chest broke the surface behind her like a body rising.

* * *

They got it onto the deck aft, away from the winch gear, and stood around it dripping in the sun while the river went on pretending nothing had happened.

"Lead-lined," Bo said, crouched over it, running a thumb along the seal. His face had gone the color of porridge. "You don't line a box in lead unless—"

"I know what you line a box in lead for."

"It's not ours." Nia, arms crossed tight, looking at the eagle like it might look back. "Cyrus. Whatever's in there, it's not ours. We cut brass and copper off dead ships. This is—" she gestured vaguely at the whole horizon, "—this is *other*."

"Vaskaria," Salim said quietly.

They all turned. The old cook stood at the edge of the group with a dish towel over his shoulder as if he'd wandered up from the galley mid-thought, and maybe he had, but his eyes were fixed on the seal with an expression Cyrus had never seen on him — not curiosity, something older and sadder. Recognition.

"You know it?" she asked.

"My father traded the coast, long ago. Before the war." Salim touched the towel, folded it, refolded it. "There were men who came upriver in those days with that bird on their flags. They paid well." A pause. "They took more than they paid for. My father would not speak of them except after dark." He looked up at her. "That nation has been dead longer than I have been alive, Miss Cyrus. Dead things do not stamp their names on boxes and sink them in rivers. Living things do that. Things that want to be forgotten."

Nobody spoke. The compressor ticked as it cooled. Somewhere across the water a hornbill laughed its harsh laugh and fell silent, embarrassed.

"Well," Bo said at last, straightening with a wince. "We open it or we don't. But we decide now, because I'm not sleeping ten feet from a sealed mystery for another night. I'm too old for mysteries. Mysteries keep me up."

"We sell it sealed," Nia said quickly. "Unopened. To a collector, a museum, whoever. Sealed is worth more, and sealed means none of it is our problem—"

"Nia." Cyrus knelt beside the chest. The padlock was bronze, corroded, but the hasp was the weak point; she could see it already. "Look at the lock. Look at the hinges. This box was sealed to survive. Somebody spent real money making sure whatever's inside would wait." She looked up at her crew — her family, the only word for them, though none of them used it. "Things built to wait are built for someone. I want to know who."

Bo sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to a battle fought many times before and always lost. "Cutting tool's in the locker."

The hasp parted on the third strike. The padlock, stubborn to the end, held until Bo worked it with a screwdriver and a curse, and then the lid came up on hinges that had been oiled — *oiled*, within the last decade, someone had maintained this box in the dark — and the smell of wax and paper and long-sealed air rose out of it like breath from a mouth.

Inside, packed in waxed cloth and tarred canvas:

Logs. Dozens of them, bound in oilskin, spines labeled in a precise hand. *Expedition Records — Basin Survey — Confidential.* Dates spanning years. More recent than the war. Far more recent.

Beneath them, wrapped separately, a bundle of photographs, edges soft with handling.

And at the bottom, a chart case, and inside the chart case, coordinates inked onto waterproof film, pointing upriver, deep into the basin, to a place with no name that Cyrus had ever heard — just a grid reference and a single word written beside it in the same precise hand: *SITE.*

Bo lifted out one of the logbooks and opened it, and his eyebrows climbed, and he closed it again slowly, like a man shutting a door on something. "These aren't old," he said. "Cyrus. These aren't old at all. These dates are—"

"Give me the photographs," Cyrus said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. She didn't recognize it as hers.

She unwrapped the bundle with hands that felt like they belonged to someone else, and the top photograph showed a river landing, wooden crates, a dozen men in field clothes, and standing third from the left with his cap pushed back and that crooked grin he'd never been able to kill even in uniform—

Tomas.

Her brother. Alive. Sunburned, thinner, older than the man who had walked out of her life into a classified operation and become a sentence in a letter, but alive, undeniably, unbearably alive, caught mid-laugh at something outside the frame, his hand raised in a wave or a salute or just the gesture he made when he was happy, that open-palmed thing, *hey, sis, look at this.*

The world tilted. The deck, the river, the sun — all of it slid sideways, and Cyrus had the sudden vertiginous certainty that she was still underwater, that she had never come up, that she was forty meters down in the dark and drowning slowly and this was what drowning looked like from the inside: your brother's face smiling at you out of the past like a body surfacing, bloated with years, impossible, undeniable.

"Cyrus." Nia's voice, very far away. "Cyrus, sit down. Sit *down*—"

She was sitting. She didn't remember sitting. The photograph trembled in her hands. Behind it, the rest of the bundle — dozens of images, months of them, dates on the backs in that same precise hand, Tomas in every fifth or sixth frame, growing thinner, the grin fading frame by frame like a lamp running down, until the last photograph showed him standing at the edge of jungle so dense it looked like a wall, looking directly into the camera with an expression she had never seen on him in her entire life.

Not fear. Warning.

*Don't follow,* his eyes said. Or possibly: *Come alone.* With Tomas it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

The river breathed against the hull. The mist was burning off the water, and somewhere upstream the day was beginning for people who had not just watched their dead brother smile at them out of a lead-lined coffin of secrets.

"He was here," Cyrus heard herself say. Her voice sounded scraped, hollowed, like a channel after flood. "Three years they told me he was lost with all hands, and he was *here*. On this river. Taking photographs. Writing reports." She looked up at Bo, and saw her own vertigo mirrored in his face, and beyond him Nia's fear, and beyond her Salim standing very still with the dish towel forgotten in his hands. "Someone sent him upriver and then erased him. And someone sealed this box, weighted it with lead, and sank it with a ship full of evidence — and oiled the hinges."

"Meaning what?" Bo asked, though she could see he already knew.

"Meaning somebody planned to come back for this." Cyrus closed her hand around the last photograph, Tomas's warning eyes pressing into her palm. "And somebody else made sure they couldn't. And either way—" she looked at the coordinates gleaming on the film in the bottom of the chest, pointing upriver like a finger, "—either way, we're not the only ones who know this exists."

The tide was turning. She could feel it in the way the *Fortune* shifted at her moorings, the river changing its mind beneath them, the long slow pull that dragged everything — silt, logs, boats, sisters — back toward the sea.

Except this time, Cyrus thought, staring at the seal of a dead nation, everything was being pulled the other way. Upriver. Into the basin. Toward the place with no name.

Behind her, unnoticed by any of them, low and distant, an engine sound crossed the water. Then another.

Neither boat turned toward shore. Both turned toward them.
