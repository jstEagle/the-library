## 3. Visitors at Low Tide

Bo Kettering had spent thirty-one years listening to engines, and in that time he had learned that everything honest made a sound. A healthy diesel spoke in a low, even voice. A cracked manifold whispered. A bearing about to fail sang a thin note only the patient could hear. Lies, he'd come to believe, worked the same way — they had a sound, and his own lies sounded like a rusted-out generator coughing to life on a cold morning: ugly, obvious, and going nowhere.

Which was why, when the gray patrol boat came up the channel at low tide on a Tuesday morning with no markings but a hull number and a man at the rail wearing civilian clothes cut too clean for a river town, Bo's stomach dropped through the deck plates before anyone said a word.

He was under the *Magpie's* engine housing when Nia's bare feet slapped across the deck above him, quick as rain.

"Bo. Boat coming in. Not local."

"Whose, then?"

"That's the thing." Her face appeared upside down in the hatch, framed by the engine room's heat-shimmer. "It's got government lines. Military, maybe. Nobody's waving. They're just... coming."

Bo wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since the previous administration and climbed out into the light. The morning was doing what Sepik mornings did — laying a skin of gold on the water so thick you could believe the river was kind. The patrol boat sat low in it, gray as a knife blade, its wake rolling out long and deliberate toward their mooring. At the bow rail stood a woman in khaki trousers and a pressed shirt, hands folded behind her back like she was posing for a portrait of someone who gave orders for a living.

Behind her, two men who were not sailors stood very still in the way that hired muscle stands still.

Bo felt the old fear come up his spine, the one from his navy years, the one that lived below thought: *inspection*. He'd been through inspections where careers ended over a stenciled crate out of place. But this wasn't an inspection. Inspections announced themselves. This came quiet, at slack water, when the *Magpie* sat fat and slow and unable to run even if she wanted to.

Somebody had chosen this tide on purpose.

"Nia," he said, keeping his voice level the way you kept your hand level near a live wire. "Where's Cyrus?"

"Down at the chest."

Of course she was. Three days now, every spare hour, down in the hold with that lead-lined box open like a wound, reading papers that smelled of river mud and older rot. Salim had stopped asking her to eat. That was how Bo knew it was bad. Salim never stopped asking anyone to eat; feeding people was his whole grammar of love.

"And Salim?"

"Cooking. I told him they were coming. He said—" She hesitated, and Bo saw her decide whether to repeat it. "He said, 'Then we find out which of us can act.'"

Bo almost laughed. Almost. It caught somewhere in his chest and turned to ash. Thirty years ago he might have found that funny. Now it just sounded like a eulogy being rehearsed.

The patrol boat swung alongside with a competence that hurt to watch — no wasted fenders, no shouted instructions, the crew moving like parts of one machine. A gangway came across. The woman in khaki stepped onto the *Magpie*'s deck without waiting to be invited, which told Bo everything about how the next hour would go.

She was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with a face built for official photographs — pleasant, symmetrical, and completely closed. Her eyes did a full sweep of the vessel in four seconds flat: the crane, the dive gear drying on the rail, the compressor, Nia, Bo, the galley door where Salim stood with a dish towel over his shoulder like a man in a play about domesticity.

"Good morning," she said. Her accent was northern-coastal, clipped, educated. "Commander Ilse Rennick, Naval Intelligence. And you are?"

"Bo Kettering. Engineer. This is my boat — well, half mine. Co-owned." Why he added that last part he couldn't say. It was the first lie, and it was a stupid one, offered up for free, and he watched her file it away with the small stillness of a cat watching a bird land.

"Co-owned," Rennick repeated pleasantly. "With?"

"Cyrus Sterling."

"Sterling." The name went into whatever ledger she carried behind her eyes. "May I see the boat? I'm given to understand you've been conducting salvage operations in restricted waters."

"We do salvage," Bo said. "Mostly. There's permits—"

"I've read your permits, Mr. Kettering. All of them." She smiled, and it reached her mouth and stopped there, like a tide that couldn't be bothered to climb the beach. "May I?"

She was already moving aft. Bo fell into step beside her because the alternative was standing still while she walked his home alone, and there were things on this boat — there was *one thing* on this boat — that could not be seen by anyone whose boots shined like hers.

* * *

The tour took eleven minutes and cost Bo roughly a decade of his life.

Rennick looked at everything the way a surgeon looks at an X-ray: nothing personal in it, everything diagnostic. She noted the dive logs clipped by the wheel. She noted the fuel manifests. She paused at the compressor and asked its hours, and Bo answered honestly because lying about a compressor was the one lie he could tell well, and he needed to save his bad lies for the ones that mattered.

"You ran a lot of bottom time last month," she observed.

"Contract work. Bridge survey, downriver."

"Mm." She touched the rail, rubbed thumb and forefinger together, and studied the residue. River silt, iron stain, the particular orange bloom of metal that has sat in anaerobic mud a long time. Lead does that. Lead-lined chests do that. Bo watched her fingertip and felt sweat start between his shoulder blades.

"Good diving ground out here?" she asked.

"River's river. Mud and wrecks."

"Rich mud, though. Around the delta. Lots of... material from the war years. From other years." She turned those closed gray eyes on him fully for the first time. "Recovered anything interesting lately?"

And there it was. The question arrived wearing plain clothes, just like her, and Bo heard his own answer coming out sounding exactly like a rusted generator.

"Interesting? No. It's salvage. Anchor chains, propellers, copper wire. You'd be amazed what people lose in a river." He laughed. It came out wrong — one syllable too long, pitched a half-step high. Somewhere in the galley, Bo heard Salim set down a pot with exaggerated care, the universal sound of a cook trying not to listen and failing.

"Anchor chains," Rennick said. "Propellers. Copper wire." She nodded slowly, as if tasting each item. "No documents? No sealed containers? Nothing stamped?"

"Stamped with what?"

It was the wrong move and he knew it the instant the words left him — a counterpunch thrown by a man who'd already been knocked down. Only someone who knew what stamp she meant would ask *stamped with what*. Rennick's expression didn't change at all, which was itself a change; something behind her face shifted weight, like cargo sliding in a hold.

"With anything," she said mildly. "Official markings. Provenance marks. We track recovered materials of certain origins. It's routine."

"Nothing stamped," Bo said. "Like I said. Chains and props."

She let the silence sit. Bo had known interrogators in the service, and the good ones all used silence the same way — poured it out and waited for you to fill it, because people cannot bear a silence they know is a trap, and they pour words into it like water into a hole, and some of those words always drown them.

Bo filled it. "We're a small operation. Family outfit, mostly. We don't get into anything official."

"Family," Rennick said again, softly, and this time the word had teeth in it somewhere, hidden. "Yes. Ms. Sterling. I believe she had a brother in the service."

The river made a small sound against the hull. A kingfisher went off downstream like a struck match. Bo stood very still and understood, with total clarity, that this woman had not come to ask questions. She had come to deliver them, pre-answered, and watch which of them flinched.

"Tomas Sterling," Rennick continued, walking slowly along the rail, trailing one finger on it like a teacher along a student's desk. "Lost three years ago. Declared dead. A tragedy. The family — what remained of it — was notified with full honors." She stopped. Turned. "You can imagine our interest, then, when a salvage crew operating out of exactly this moorage pulls up materials connected to an expedition that officially never existed, in waters that are officially closed, and the co-owner of the vessel is the sister of a man from that expedition who is officially dead."

Every word landed like a diver's boot on a rotten plank. Bo felt the structure of the last three days — the chest, the photographs, Cyrus's face when she'd come up the ladder holding that picture of Tomas alive and grinning, sunburned, *dated after the loss* — groan and shift beneath him.

"We didn't know what it was," he said. Which was true. "A chest off a wreck. We log everything. It's logged. You can see the log."

"Oh, I intend to." She smiled again, that tide-that-won't-climb smile. "I'll need to see the chest as well, Mr. Kettering. Its contents. Everything recovered from the site."

"It's—" Bo's mind went white. He thought of the hold, the tarp, the padlock Cyrus had put on it yesterday with a look on her face he'd never seen before, a look like a door closing. "It's not aboard."

The lie came out and lay there between them, huge and steaming, and Bo knew it was dead on arrival. Rennick looked at him for a long moment with something that was almost gentleness — the gentleness of a vet deciding where to place the shot.

"Not aboard," she repeated.

"We sold it. To a collector. Downriver. That's the business, you know. Find, sell, move on."

"Sold it." She nodded. "To a collector."

"A private buyer."

"Named?"

"...We deal through brokers."

"Of course. Brokers." She took out a small notebook — actual paper, actual pen, an affectation or a threat — and wrote something in it. Bo watched the pen move and felt each stroke like a stitch going into him. "Mr. Kettering, I'm going to be direct with you, because I suspect indirection wastes both our time. Materials from that site are matters of national security. If any such materials pass through this vessel — have passed, will pass — the legal exposure for you and your partners is considerable. Salvage of restricted military property. Unauthorized recovery of classified effects. There are statutes." She closed the notebook. "There are also, however, arrangements. Cooperation is viewed favorably. Very favorably. Compensation, permits regularized, certain... past irregularities overlooked."

"We haven't done anything needs overlooking."

"No," Rennick agreed. "Almost certainly not." She looked past him then, deliberately, at the hold hatch — at the padlock, gleaming new brass, which Bo realized with a lurch he should have covered. New brass on an old boat. A flag planted in the deck. "I'll return tomorrow with a survey team. Standard procedure. In the meantime—" she produced a card, held it out; Bo took it because his hand moved before his brain could stop it, "—if you remember anything else. Any detail. Brokers' names. Buyers. Contents. My number is on there. Day or night."

She stepped back to the gangway, paused, and turned halfway.

"One more thing, Mr. Kettering." Her voice stayed pleasant; the pleasantness was the worst part. "Tell Ms. Sterling that grief makes people careless, and carelessness around state property makes people into felons. Tell her I'd rather be her ally than her problem. Tell her—" and here, for one unguarded half-second, something moved behind the official face, something tired and old and drowned-looking, "—tell her I knew her brother's commanding officer. Tell her that. She'll want to think about what that means."

Then she was gone, down the gangway, across the gray deck, and the patrol boat pulled away from the *Magpie* with that same terrible competence, its wake slapping their hull like slow applause.

* * *

They gathered in the galley because the galley was where the *Magpie* did its thinking. Salim put coffee in front of everyone without being asked, which was his way of calling a meeting. Nia sat curled on the bench with her knees up, looking younger than her seventeen years and older than Bo, both at once. Cyrus came up from the hold last, and Bo watched her take in their faces and arrive at the truth before anyone spoke, because she'd grown up in a house with a naval officer and knew what these visits meant.

"Well," she said. "How badly did you lie?"

"Badly," Bo admitted. "Generator-coughing badly. Sold the chest to a brokered collector. Put a padlock on the hold like a sign saying TREASURE THIS WAY."

Cyrus closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, they were calm in the way deep water is calm — pressure, not peace.

"She asked about Tomas."

Bo told them all of it, word for word as best he could, because Salim insisted — *exact words matter with that breed* — and when he got to the end, to *I knew her brother's commanding officer*, the galley went quiet enough to hear the river breathing against the hull.

"What does that mean?" Nia asked.

"It means she knows things the files don't say," Cyrus said quietly. "It means this isn't a fishing expedition. She came with a map." She wrapped her hands around her coffee mug without drinking. "It means they've known where that wreck was for three years and left it sitting there. And now a salvage boat pulls up a chest they either lost track of or wrote off, and suddenly there's a commander on our deck offering to be our *ally*."

"People like that don't have allies," Salim said. "Only instruments. Some they play, some they discard." He said it without drama, wiping down the counter, and Bo thought: *he's already decided something. Look at him. He's already packing something away in his head.*

"So what do we do?" Nia's voice cracked slightly on *do*, and none of them pretended not to notice, because pretending would have been its own cruelty. "If they come back tomorrow with a survey team, they'll find it. They'll find everything. And then—"

"Then we're witnesses," Bo said. "Or accomplices. Or loose ends." He looked around the galley — at the kid, at the old cook, at Cyrus with her brother's photograph folded in her shirt pocket like a second heartbeat. "I've been on boats where people became loose ends. It starts with a visit like that one. Polite. At low tide. When nobody can run."

Cyrus set down the mug. "Nobody's running."

"I didn't say run. I said nobody *can*. There's a difference, and she chose the difference on purpose." Bo rubbed his face with both hands. Thirty-one years of listening to machines, and no machine had ever frightened him like a polite woman with a notebook. "Tomorrow she comes back with a team and finds that chest, or we make sure she doesn't. Those are the roads. There's no third one. I've been sitting here hoping for a third road my whole life and it's never once shown up."

Salim stopped wiping the counter. He looked at Cyrus, and something passed between them that Bo couldn't read — some agreement already forming, some plan already taking on cargo.

"There's a third road," Salim said mildly. "There's always a third road. It's just never the easy one." He untied the dish towel from his shoulder, folded it, set it down with the precision of a man laying a table for a meal he intended to survive. "But first — before tomorrow, before her survey team, before any of it — we need to talk about what's actually in that chest. All of it. Because I've been cooking for this crew six years, and I know when people are hiding something from me to protect me, and I am telling you now—" his voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it carry like a shout, "—that ends today."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. Then at Nia. Then at Bo.

Outside, the tide had turned, and the *Magpie* began, very slowly, to swing on her mooring — facing downriver, toward the delta, toward everything that was coming up it.
