## 4. Terms of Silence

They came at dawn, when the river was still wearing its grey silk and the mist hung so low it seemed the water was breathing through cloth.

Cyrus saw the launch first — a navy-grey hull moving without lights, without wake, the way a knife moves through bread. It came around the bend of the delta channel with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry, and it did not slow until it was fifty meters off the *Marrow's* stern, and then it simply stopped, the way a verdict stops.

She was already on deck. She had not slept. The chest sat below in the hold, wrapped now in a tarp she'd tied and retied three times in the night, as if knots could keep the past sealed. Her hands still smelled of the river's bottom — silt and old iron and something faintly chemical, like the memory of a photograph developing.

Bo came up behind her, barefoot, moving quietly for a man built like a dredge. He didn't say anything. Neither did she. There was nothing to say that the grey launch hadn't already said.

"They waited for light," Bo said finally. "Navy doesn't do that. Navy comes when it comes."

"Someone wanted us to see them coming." Cyrus watched the launch. A figure stood at its bow rail, small against the paling sky, hands folded at the small of her back like a woman waiting for an elevator. "Someone wanted us to have time to be afraid."

"Is it working?"

Cyrus thought about the chest. About the photographs. About the face she had not let herself look at again after the first time, because the first time had put her on the deck of the *Marrow* at three in the morning with her knees buckled against the rail and the river running black and indifferent beneath her.

"It's working," she said.

* * *

Commander Ilse Rennick came aboard alone.

That was the first thing Cyrus noted, and she noted it the way she noted current and depth and the angle of a submerged hull — professionally, because her survival had always depended on reading what the water gave her. A woman who came alone was either very confident or very expendable, and Rennick did not move like an expendable woman. She moved like a woman who had already written the ending of this conversation and was merely waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

She was perhaps fifty, compact, grey at the temples in the precise way of someone who had decided grey at the temples was useful. Her uniform was correct without being immaculate. Her eyes went over the *Marrow* the way a surveyor's eyes go over a coastline — taking measurements, filing them, discarding what didn't matter. When they reached Cyrus, they stayed.

"Cyrus Sterling." Not a question. "Commander Ilse Rennick, Naval Intelligence, retired designation active. I'd like to talk about what your crew pulled out of the delta wreck."

"We pulled out ballast and bilge sludge," Cyrus said. "The wreck's a civilian freighter. Sank in the nineties. There's nothing on her but rust."

"May I sit?"

"It's your navy," Cyrus said. "Sit anywhere."

Rennick sat at the deck table where Salim served breakfast, where Nia had carved her initials at fourteen and Bo had pretended not to see, and Cyrus felt something in her chest tighten at the sight of the woman's hands — pale, unscarred, the hands of someone who had never pulled rope until her palms split — resting on the wood her family ate off of.

Salim appeared with coffee. He did it without being asked, the way he did everything, and Cyrus caught the look that passed between him and Bo — a look that said *we're listening, and we're counting everything she says.* Rennick accepted the cup, thanked him with a nod that was almost gracious, and waited until the galley hatch closed before she spoke again.

"Three years ago," Rennick said, "an expedition entered the Sepik Basin under a commercial charter with government observation. Seven foreign personnel, eleven contracted local laborers. The charter vessel reported mechanical failure on the descent and turned back. The expedition was declared lost. Flood season, crocodile water, the usual. There was a memorial service. There were letters to families. There was a settlement, quiet, handled by lawyers in three jurisdictions." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, precise as a clock hand. "The chest in your hold comes from that expedition."

Cyrus kept her face still. It was a skill her father had taught her without meaning to — the flat calm of a woman who had learned young that showing the water where it hurt only showed it where to strike.

"I don't know anything about a chest."

"Miss Sterling." Rennick's voice didn't harden. That was the frightening thing about it. It stayed level the way deep water stays level. "I have spent eleven days tracing that wreck's dive history. I know you found her on the fourth of this month. I know your dive profile says forty-one minutes on the bottom, and your air consumption for that profile says you spent at least twelve of those minutes doing something that wasn't salvage. And I know that yesterday morning, a woman stood on this deck at three a.m. and cried into the river." She took a sip of coffee. "The navy watches its restricted waters. I watch the watchers."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere across the channel a fishing boat was putting out, its engine a small stubborn heartbeat in the mist.

"Say what you came to say," Cyrus said.

* * *

What Rennick came to say, she said in pieces, and Cyrus understood that the pieces were the point — that the woman was laying them out like a hand of cards she had already counted, letting Cyrus see enough of each to want the whole.

"The expedition's designation was Kestrel. Its sponsor was a consortium — cultural salvage, archaeological recovery, the usual respectable language. Its government liaison reported directly to an office that no longer exists, which is a very convenient kind of not existing." Rennick set down the cup. "Kestrel went into the basin to recover artifacts from a pre-colonial site. The artifacts were real. So was the second cargo manifest, the one that was never filed. Kestrel was moving more than artifacts, Miss Sterling. And when the charter vessel turned back, Kestrel didn't turn back with it."

"Then where's the wreck? Where's the crew?"

"Scattered," Rennick said. "The basin is eleven thousand square kilometers of swamp and river and forest that eats satellites for breakfast. The search lasted nine days. It found a life raft, a fuel drum, and a shoe." She paused, and for one unguarded half-second something moved behind her eyes — something old and tired, a door opening on a dark room and shutting again. "It found a shoe."

Cyrus's hands were flat on the table. She made herself look at them. She made herself breathe the way Bo had taught her to breathe before a deep dive — slow, mechanical, the body kept running on its own settings while the mind did its work.

"My brother was on that expedition."

It was not a question, and Rennick didn't treat it as one.

"Thomas Sterling. Dive specialist and salvage engineer, contracted through a firm in Port Moresby. Yes." Rennick's gaze didn't waver, and Cyrus hated her for that — hated the professionalism of it, the terrible courtesy. "You didn't know he'd taken the contract."

"He told me he was doing a wreck survey off Rabaul." The words came out of her like water through a cracked hull, slow and pressurized. "He called me two days before he left. He sounded — " She stopped. She had replayed that call perhaps four hundred times in three years and she was not going to replay it for this woman. "He sounded fine."

"They always sound fine." Something in Rennick's voice shifted, a hairline fracture in the level tone, and was gone. "The chest you recovered contains Kestrel's field logs. Photographs. Coordinates. It was sealed and weighted for sinking — deliberately, by someone who wanted it to stay lost, or by someone who wanted it to be found by the right person at the right time. We don't know which. That ambiguity is a problem. You are a problem. This boat is a problem."

"And the solution?"

Rennick leaned back. Out on the water, the mist was burning off, and the light came through the deck canopy in long pale blades, and Cyrus saw the commander's face clearly for the first time: the fine lines around the mouth, the small scar through one eyebrow, the eyes that were not cruel but were not kind either — the eyes of someone who had made a long practice of standing in rooms where terrible things were discussed in reasonable tones.

"Three days from now, a sanctioned hydrographic survey enters the Sepik Basin. Officially, it's charting channels for a development corridor. Unofficially, it's recovering what remains of Kestrel's materials before anyone else does. I'm commanding it. I need a salvage diver with delta experience who knows how to read a wreck." Rennick's eyes went, deliberately, to the tarp-wrapped shape of the hold hatch. "I need you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I charge your crew with theft of naval property, obstruction of a restricted-site investigation, and trafficking in unregistered artifacts. The *Marrow* is impounded tonight. Your engineer has a conviction from twenty years ago that makes him a repeat offender. Your deckhand is seventeen, which means child welfare services takes a very close interest in a minor living aboard a vessel under criminal seizure." Rennick said it all in the same level voice, the voice of a weather report. "Your cook's immigration status is, I'm told, complicated. I haven't needed to look closely. I'd prefer not to."

The rage came up in Cyrus like silt disturbed off a bottom — sudden, total, blinding. She felt it in her teeth, in her wrists, in the old scar on her shoulder where a cable had caught her at nineteen. She wanted to put her hands across that calm face and the table between them and the whole grey machinery of a country that had lost her brother and then lost interest in him in the space of nine days and one shoe.

She did not move. She breathed. She let the silt settle, because a diver who kicks up the bottom sees nothing.

"You're not offering me a choice," Cyrus said. "You're offering me a leash."

"I'm offering you the only version of this story where your people stay free and you get answers." Rennick rose, smoothed her uniform with two efficient motions. "You want to know what happened to your brother. So do I. That's the truth, Miss Sterling, whatever you think of the rest. There are things in those logs that I have read twice and not slept after either time." She walked to the rail, then paused, her back to Cyrus, the river light silvering her hair. "The expedition included men your brother's age. The village reports from that year mention outsiders taken into the interior who never came out. The chest's coordinates are forty kilometers up a channel no chart has ever named." She turned her head, just enough. "Your brother's knife was recovered from a trading post two years after Kestrel was declared lost. Someone was alive to trade it."

The words went into Cyrus like a hook going into a fish — clean, deep, behind the gill plate. She heard the river. She heard her own blood. She heard, very far away, Nia laughing at something with Salim in the galley, the sound of the only family she had left, and she understood that Rennick had already counted them too, had weighed them, had priced them.

"I'll need to talk to my crew," Cyrus said.

"Of course." Rennick stepped to the rail's ladder. "The survey launches in three days. If I don't see the *Marrow* at the staging anchorage by then, the alternative begins. And Miss Sterling — " she looked back, and for a moment the commander's mask thinned, and Cyrus saw beneath it a woman standing in a doorway of her own, looking into her own dark room — "the chest stays sealed until we're under way. If it's opened, or copied, or moved, the alternative begins that much sooner. I will know. I always know."

She went down the ladder, across the grey deck of the launch, and the launch pulled away without wake, without hurry, back into the morning.

* * *

Cyrus stood at the rail for a long time after the channel was empty.

The mist was gone now. The river lay open and brown and glittering, running the way it always ran, toward a sea that kept everything and gave back almost nothing. Three days. Forty kilometers up a channel no chart had ever named. A knife in a trading post two years after the world had decided her brother was dead.

Bo came and stood beside her. He didn't ask. He was a man who understood that some readings you had to take yourself.

"She gave us three days," Cyrus said.

"Then we've got three days." Bo spat thoughtfully over the rail. "And a chest we can't open, a river we can't refuse, and a woman who smells like a trap with the cheese still on it."

"She knew about the three a.m.," Cyrus said. Her voice came out strange, distant, like a transmission from a diver on the far side of the safe limit. "She knew before she ever boarded. This was decided before we were offered anything. We were never being asked, Bo. We were being told the shape of it."

Bo was quiet a while. Down the deck, Nia was coiling line with the fierce concentration of a girl who felt the weather changing and didn't yet have words for it. Salim stood in the galley hatch, arms folded, watching the empty channel where the launch had gone.

"So what do we do?" Bo asked.

Cyrus looked at the hold hatch. Beneath the tarp, beneath the knots she had retied three times in the dark, the chest sat with its dead nation's seal and its living photographs, and somewhere upriver, past the last charted bend, a man had traded a knife two years after drowning.

She had spent three years measuring her life in miles — miles of river, miles of depth, miles between herself and the day the navy letter came. Rennick had just offered her the longest mile of all.

"We provision the boat," Cyrus said. "And Salim — Salim starts cooking for a long trip."

She turned from the rail. Behind her, the river ran on, patient as a witness, carrying everything toward the sea.
