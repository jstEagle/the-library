## 5. Provisions and Lies

Salim had cooked for liars before.

He had fed them rice and chili and the small kindness of a hot meal at the end of a hard watch, back in the years when he ran a galley on a coastal freighter out of Surabaya, and the men who owned her moved cargo that never appeared on any manifest. He had learned then that the best thing to do with a liar was to feed him well and watch his hands. Hands told you everything a mouth would not. A man who was planning to betray you ate too carefully, or not at all. A man who was merely frightened ate everything you put in front of him and asked for more.

Commander Ilse Rennick ate like a frightened woman. Salim noted it the first morning, when he served her coffee on the aft deck and she wrapped both hands around the mug as though the river wind had gotten inside her and would not leave. She drank it black, without sugar, and her eyes never rested on anything longer than three seconds — the water, the treeline, Bo's hands on the winch, Nia coiling rope with the exaggerated competence of the young. Only once did her gaze settle, and that was on the hold hatch, and it settled there the way a heron settles on a fish.

Salim poured himself a cup and stood beside her, uninvited, because an old cook is furniture and furniture hears everything.

"Good coffee," Rennick said. It was not a compliment. It was a test of whether he would speak.

"Sumatran beans," Salim said. "My own roast. I could make it better, but the boat rolls."

"The boat rolls," Rennick agreed, and looked at the hatch again, and Salim understood that she was not frightened of the river at all. She was frightened of what the river had already given up, and of what it might give up next.

He went back to his galley and began, quietly, to plan a betrayal of his own.

* * *

The market at the delta town smelled of diesel and durian and the green rot of the riverbank, and Salim walked it with a list in his head and a second list in his pocket, and only one of them was for Rennick's expedition.

The official list was generous. The navy had money, or something that looked like money, and the quartermaster's man — a soft-bellied lieutenant named Okafor who smiled too easily — had told Salim to spare no expense. Rice by the hundredweight. Dried fish. Tinned meat, tinned milk, tinned tomatoes. Fuel filters, lamp oil, medical stores. Enough for six weeks upriver, which was four weeks more than any honest survey required, and Salim did the arithmetic in the shadow of the awning while Okafor haggled over the price of rope he did not intend to inspect.

Six weeks. Either Rennick expected to go deep, or she expected the journey to be complicated, or she wanted them provisioned so thoroughly that no one aboard would have reason to turn back. All three, probably. Salim had known men who planned like that — men who bought loyalty by the crate and were astonished when it spoiled like everything else.

The second list was shorter, and he bought for it in three different stalls so that no single shopkeeper could describe the whole of what the old cook had carried away. A ream of good paper. Two blocks of pencil carbon, the soft kind that took an impression like skin takes a bruise. Sewing needles. A spool of waxed thread. And spices — this was the genius of it, and he permitted himself a thin pleasure at the thought — spices by the kilo, because no one in the history of boats had ever questioned a cook's spice tins. Turmeric and coriander and dried galangal, cumin and clove and the little cinnamon bark that came down from the highlands, all of it destined for the galley shelf where it would sit in plain sight, doing the one job no one ever suspects a spice of doing.

Holding the truth. Keeping it dry.

He carried his purchases back through the heat in two trips, and on the second trip he passed the navy launch moored at the town jetty, and he counted. Two sailors on deck playing cards. One at the gangway, bored. One below, by the sound of a radio turned too loud. And Rennick's own silhouette in the wheelhouse, bent over papers, motionless as a heron again. Five, plus the woman. He had fed crews of forty; he could count a crew of five in his sleep, and he did, that night, lying in his bunk with the river slapping the hull like a slow hand, counting them over and over until the counting became a kind of prayer.

* * *

The chest was kept in the hold, under the tarp, under a new padlock that Rennick's people had fitted themselves — a navy lock, brass-bodied, the kind that yields to a determined man with a crowbar and to a patient man with nothing but time. Salim had neither a crowbar nor time. What he had was a galley directly above the hold, a floor hatch for passing provisions down, and thirty years of knowing how ships breathe.

Ships breathe at night. The hull ticks as it cools. The timbers shrink a hair and the joints open a hair, and every old boat develops a language of small sounds that a crew stops hearing because they hear it every night. Salim had been listening to this boat breathe for four years. He knew which planks sang and which stayed silent. He knew that the hold hatch cover, if lifted with both hands and no hurry, made a sound no louder than a man turning over in his bunk — and that Bo, who slept light as a cat, slept deepest in the hour before dawn, and that Nia slept like the young everywhere, completely and without apology, and that Cyrus — Cyrus he could not predict. Cyrus slept like a diver, in short dives and long surfaces, and Salim did not intend to test her.

So he waited for the right night, and the right night came on the fourth day, when the navy sailors drank the town's warm beer and the rain came down in the fat, drumming way that covers all sounds, and Rennick sat up in the wheelhouse with her charts and her lamp burning past midnight.

Salim made his preparations the way he made a sauce: everything in its place before the fire was ever lit. The galley table cleared and wiped dry. The carbon paper cut to the size of a logbook page. A pencil sharpened to a needle. The lamp trimmed low. And on the shelf, waiting with the patience of the dead, six spice tins with their lids loosened, their contents decanted just shallow enough to leave a hollow beneath — because paper laid flat on top of turmeric looks like nothing at all, but paper rolled tight and buried at the bottom of a tin looks like a secret, and secrets buried shallow get found. He would decide which method suited which document when he saw them. He had learned long ago that you do not plan the cooking before you see the fish.

He waited until the rain thickened. Then he knelt at the provisions hatch, lifted the cover with both hands and no hurry, and went down into the hold like a man descending into his own chest.

The dark down there was total and wet and smelled of bilgewater and old rope, and beneath it, faintly, something else — the dry mineral smell of lead, the way a sealed thing smells even when it is shut. He found the chest by touch. The padlock rattled once against the hasp and he held it still with his palm, heart going like a deckhand's boots, and waited through a full minute of rain before he tried the hasp itself.

It was the work of a patient man and nothing but time. The hasp screws were new and the wood was old, and old river wood gives. He did not force anything. He persuaded. He worked the hasp plate loose from the rotten lid timber the way you work a splinter from a child's foot — slowly, apologizing to the wood with every movement — and in eleven minutes that felt like the whole of his life, the lid came up on its hinges with the lock still swinging intact, and the chest breathed its cold lead breath into his face.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: paper. He had known it would be paper. But knowing and seeing are different, the way hearing a man is drowned and finding his knife are different.

He did not read. That was the discipline of it, the part he was proudest of later. He was not a reader; he was a cook, and a cook's gift is that he knows the difference between looking and taking. He lifted the oilcloth bundle into the lamplight he'd left burning above the hatch — a thin blade of it, just enough — and he saw enough. A logbook with a stamped cover, the seal he had glimpsed once on the chest itself, the double-headed bird of a country that had stopped existing before Nia was born. Photographs in a separate envelope, their edges soft with handling. Loose pages in at least two hands, one of them cramped and furious, one of them careful as a surveyor's. And a folded chart, heavy paper, river-lines drawn in ink gone brown.

He took the logbook, the envelope, the loose pages, and the chart, and he left everything else — the brass instruments, the sealed tubes, the small lacquered box he did not open — because a thief takes everything and a preparer takes what cannot be replaced.

Then he climbed, and closed the hatch, and sat in his galley with his heart still running and began to work.

* * *

Carbon paper is an old man's magic. You lay the black sheet face-down against clean paper, lay the original face-down against the carbon, and draw the pencil across the back of the original with long, even strokes, and the original confesses itself in reverse, ghost-gray and perfect, every word, every signature, every smudge of the writer's hurry. Salim had learned it in a galley in Surabaya from a second engineer who copied fuel manifests for reasons he never explained and was eventually paid for in a way that ended badly. The engineer had taught him two things: how to do it fast, and when to stop.

Salim worked until the sky went from black to the color of wet ash. He copied the logbook entire — every date, every coordinate, every entry in the furious hand, including the last one, which stopped mid-sentence, which stopped the way a man stops when something comes out of the trees. He copied the loose pages, both hands. He copied the chart by eye, kneeling at the galley table with the original weighted flat under the flour tin, tracing the river's brown ink line onto clean paper and marking the coordinates in his own careful numerals, because a chart copied by carbon loses its fine lines and a man navigating on a lost line drowns with company.

The photographs he could not copy, and this hurt him more than he expected. He held them a long time in the low light. Men in expedition khaki, squinting at the camera. A camp on a riverbank, canvas and crates, the seal stenciled on a box in the foreground. And one photograph — he stopped on it, and his hands went still, and the rain filled the silence — one photograph of a younger man standing at the rail of some vessel, laughing at something outside the frame, and Salim knew the face because he had been cooking for the man's sister for four years and grief leaves a family resemblance that is almost unbearable to look at directly. Tomas Sterling. Alive, in this picture, in a way that made the word *alive* into an accusation.

He put the photographs back. He copied nothing of them but their number — nine, plus the one of the laughing man — and wrote the number in the margin of his own notes, because a preparer keeps an inventory.

By the time the rain thinned, he had forty-one pages of ghost-gray confession, a hand-drawn chart, and a page of his own observations written in the shorthand of old galley men — times, sounds, the number of sailors, the fact that Rennick's lamp had burned until three, the fact that the navy lock had not been disturbed because he had not disturbed it, the fact that the chest's hasp would need mending before anyone noticed and he would mend it today with waxed thread and glue and no one would ever know the wood had been persuaded.

He rolled the copies tight. He buried the logbook copy and the furious pages deep in the turmeric tin, beneath a false floor of spice pressed hard and leveled smooth. The chart he cut into three sections and laid flat under the shallow layers of three other tins — coriander, cumin, clove — because a chart in one tin is a treasure and a chart in three tins is a kitchen. The last pages, his own notes, went into the hollow handle of the knife he had carried since Surabaya, wrapped in waxed cloth. And the cinnamon tin he left untouched on the top shelf, full and honest, because a shelf with nothing hidden on it looks like a shelf with nothing hidden on it, and that is the deepest hiding place there is.

Then he cleaned his galley. He scrubbed the table, scoured the pot he had not used, ground the morning's coffee, and when Bo came stumbling in at dawn smelling of rain and bad sleep, the galley smelled of cardamom and frying bread and nothing else in the world.

"You're up early," Bo said, lowering himself onto the bench like a man surrendering to a chair.

"Old men sleep like cats," Salim said. "Little bits, all night." He set the bread down and the eggs and the strong coffee, and watched Bo's hands, and Bo's hands were a good man's hands — scarred, blunt, honest — and Salim felt the love he felt for all of them rise in his chest like heat from a stove, and he thought: *this is why*. Not the chest, not the seal, not the drowned nation. This. The hands at my table. You prepare for the people you cannot bear to lose twice.

* * *

The mending took him an hour, and he did it in plain sight, on the aft deck, with the chest's lid open beside him — because the boldest thing is to be seen doing what no one thinks to question. Rennick's sailors watched him glue and stitch the split hasp timber with waxed thread, and one of them said, "What happened to it?" and Salim shrugged and said, "Old wood. River wood. Everything old gives a little," and the sailor laughed and went back to his cards, and the lock hung intact and unopened, and the chest's contents — all but the paper in Salim's tins — lay exactly where the navy believed it lay.

Rennick came to the galley that evening. She stood in the doorway with her hands behind her back, and she looked at the spice shelf a long moment — he watched her in the reflection of the copper pot, watched her eyes move along the row of tins, turmeric, coriander, cumin, clove, cinnamon — and something in his chest went very quiet and very still, the way the river goes still before a storm.

"You cook for the whole boat," she said. "Even the sailors on the launch. That's generous."

"Hungry men are quiet men," Salim said. "I like a quiet boat."

"Hm." She picked up the cinnamon tin, weighed it in her hand, set it down. "My mother kept a spice cabinet like this. She said you could tell a household's whole history by what it hoarded." She looked at him then, directly, heron-still. "What does this household hoard, Mr. Salim?"

"Rice," Salim said. "Patience. And a very good recipe for fish." He smiled the smile of an old cook, warm and empty as a cleaned pot, and after a moment she smiled back the smile of an officer, and between the two smiles lay the whole truth of the next six weeks, and neither of them touched it.

She left. He stood in his galley with his heart going slow and hard, and he took down the turmeric tin, and he pressed his thumb flat into the spice until he felt the paper beneath, and he held it there, feeling the truth under the yellow dust like a pulse under skin.

That night the navy sealed the hold with a second lock and a lead strip stamped with an official number, and the lieutenant Okafor made a ceremony of it, and Cyrus watched from the rail with a face like the river in fog. Salim served dinner to fourteen people and told no one, and the not-telling sat in him like a swallowed coal — warm, and burning, and his.

It was Nia who came to him last, in the dark, after the plates were done. She stood in the galley doorway with her arms crossed, seventeen years old and trying to be older, and she said, "Salim. What did you take out of that chest?"

He looked at her for a long time. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upriver, past the last charted bend, a dead man was walking, and the proof of it lay under the turmeric, and the girl in the doorway had the eyes of someone who counted sailors without being taught.

"I took enough," Salim said, "so that whatever they do to that chest, the truth stays on this boat." He held her gaze. "And now you know. And knowing is a kind of cargo, Nia. It doesn't come off the boat."

Nia looked at the spice shelf. Then at him. And in the morning, when the expedition cast off the lines and turned its bow upriver, the old cook stood at his galley window with his tins riding quiet above the hold, and watched the first mist of the basin come down to meet them like a held breath.
