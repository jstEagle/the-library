## 6. Upriver

The river changed its mind three times before breakfast.

Nia had learned to read it the way other girls her age read faces — the small telltale shifts that meant mood, meaning, warning. The Sepik at dawn was brown silk, slow and heavy with silt from a hundred tributaries she would never see, and by mid-morning it could turn sullen, choppy, full of argument where two currents met and refused to make peace. By afternoon it might go glass-still again, so calm you could see your own face looking back at you from three fathoms down, distorted and strange, like someone else wearing your expression.

She kept her map book open on her knees in the wheelhouse, pencil stub behind her ear, and recorded all of it. *0610 — current slack, mist to masthead. 0730 — wind from northeast, smell of smoke, village burning fields somewhere east. 0845 — water color changes, tea to coffee, side channel entering on left bank.* Salim had taught her this before he'd ever let her touch the helm: *A river is not a road, little one. A road stays where you put it. A river is an animal. You do not drive it. You ask.*

She'd written that down too, on the first page of the map book, under the date she'd started keeping it — eleven months ago now, when she was still the girl who scrubbed decks and hauled lines and got called *sprat* by Bo in his gravel voice that never quite meant unkindness. Eleven months of asking the river's permission, and she was starting to think the river had begun, grudgingly, to give it.

Today it wasn't giving anything.

* * *

They passed the first stilt village at noon.

Nia saw it before anyone else — she always did, her eyes younger than everyone else's aboard except maybe the river's own — a dark fretwork of poles rising out of the water like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and above them the houses, thatch and woven bamboo, forty feet up on legs of ironwood black as tar. Smoke stood straight up from cooking fires. Dogs barked, thin and far away. And people came out onto the platforms and walkways and simply watched.

That was the part that made the skin crawl along her arms, though she'd never have said so aloud. Not hostility. Not welcome either. Just watching — dozens of faces, old and young, following the boat with the flat patience of herons standing in shallows. Nobody waved. Nobody shouted. In the delta villages, where they'd taken on fuel and water, people had waved. Had traded fruit for fishhooks, laughed at Bo's terrible attempts at Tok Pisin, pressed green coconuts into Nia's hands because she was the youngest and children were everyone's business.

Here there were no coconuts. Here there was only the watching, and the long silence after they passed, which Nia felt against her back like a hand between her shoulder blades.

"They know something," Cyrus said.

Nia hadn't heard her come into the wheelhouse. She never did; Cyrus moved like that, quiet as ballast shifting. She stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the village shrink astern, and her face had the look it got when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Know what?"

"Something about us." Cyrus's eyes tracked the receding stilts. "Or about who else has come up this stretch lately." She didn't say more than that. She never did. That was the thing about Cyrus Sterling that Nia had spent eleven months learning: she gave you exactly as much truth as she thought you could carry, and not one gram more, and she decided how much you could carry without ever asking you.

Nia bent over her map book and wrote: *1204 — village, left bank, no trade offered. Watching.* Then, smaller, underneath, in letters she told herself were just practice for neatness: *Something is wrong.*

* * *

The crocodiles began that afternoon.

Not that they'd ever been absent — Salim said they were in the water the way God was in the sky, everywhere and mostly minding their own business — but past the second bend the banks grew low and reedy and the big ones started showing themselves, laid out on the mud like driftwood that breathed. The first one Nia saw properly was longer than the dinghy. It slid off the bank without hurry, without even a splash worth the name, and was gone, and the river closed over it and looked exactly as it had before, which was somehow worse than if the water had boiled.

"Crocodile water," Bo said, coming up beside her at the rail. He spat over the side, ceremonially, the way he did whenever the subject came up. "My uncle worked the Mamberamo, years back. Said there were two kinds of men who swam in rivers like this. The quick and the dead."

"Which are you?"

"Neither. I stay in the boat." He knocked his knuckles on the rail twice, wood on wood, an old habit she'd seen him perform a hundred times without explanation. "You keep to the boat too, sprat. No swimming off the stern because it's hot. No leaning over to wash your hair. The river here doesn't care that you're young. Young is just easier to chew."

He said it gruffly, but his hand rested a moment on her shoulder before he went below, heavy and warm through her shirt, and Nia understood that the speech was love, delivered in the only dialect Bo spoke.

She wrote in the map book: *1500 — crocs on banks, large. B says stay aboard.* And then, because the page felt too plain, too ordinary for the cold weight the afternoon had taken on, she added: *The river is an animal. Today it is hunting.*

* * *

Mist came up with the evening, rising off the water in slow white ropes until the world shrank to the length of the boat and nothing beyond it existed — not the banks, not the sky, not the dark shapes of hills that the charts promised were out there somewhere. They anchored in a wide reach, engines off, generator humming, and the silence that settled in when the propeller stopped was so complete Nia could hear fish rising, the soft *bloop* of their mouths breaking the surface, and once, farther off, something enormous moving through the shallows with a sound like a mattress being dragged through wet leaves.

Dinner was rice and tinned beef and Salim's chili sauce, which he made from a recipe he claimed came from his grandmother and which Bo claimed could strip paint. They ate in the mess because Rennick's orders said lights low after dark, no music, minimal noise — *river discipline*, the naval man assigned to their expedition called it, a Lieutenant Vasquez with careful eyes and a sidearm he wore even at meals. Nia hated the sound of that phrase, *river discipline*. Rivers didn't have discipline. Rivers had moods, and you learned them or you drowned.

Salim sat across from her, spooning rice with his bad hand steadying the bowl, and caught her looking at him.

"You count things when you're worried," he said quietly. "Your eyes. They go side to side, like you're counting something on the horizon."

"I'm not worried."

"No," he agreed mildly. "Of course not." He pushed the chili sauce toward her. "Eat. Worry keeps better on a full stomach. Everything does."

Under the table, where Vasquez at the end of the bench couldn't see, Salim's good foot touched hers — a tap, tap-tap, pause, tap — and Nia realized with a jolt that it was deliberate, that it was a rhythm, that the old cook was teaching her something in the only classroom available. She tapped it back. His eyes crinkled above his beard and he went on eating as if nothing had happened, and Nia spent the rest of dinner trying to work out what the pattern meant while her heart did something complicated in her chest.

Later, in the narrow bunk she shared with the chart table's edge digging into her back, she wrote the rhythm out in the map book's margin: *tap, tap-tap, pause, tap.* She stared at it a long time. Tap, tap-tap. Pause. Tap. Like a sentence with a word missing. Like a door held very slightly ajar.

She fell asleep with the pencil still in her hand.

* * *

On the third day upriver, Nia found the shadow.

It happened the way most important things happen — accidentally, while she was looking at something else. She'd gone forward to check the anchor light at dawn, mist lying thick again, and she'd been idly watching the water slide past the hull when she noticed that the reflections were wrong.

The mist on the river didn't move evenly. Wind moved it, or current, or the heat of the coming day — but it moved in patterns, broad and slow, like cloth settling. Except for one patch, off the port quarter, maybe four hundred meters back and holding station. That patch moved *with* them. Exactly with them. When the boat slowed around a bend, the patch slowed. When they sped up on a straight, it kept pace, patient as a shark.

Nia stood at the bow rail for ten minutes and watched it, and the more she watched the more certain she became, and the more certain she became the colder her hands got, because a patch of mist that holds station doesn't mean weather. It means an engine. An engine means a hull. A hull means people, and people who follow a navy survey boat up the Sepik Basin at dawn, hiding in fog, are not sightseeing.

She went aft and found Cyrus already awake, sitting on the ammo box she used as a chair, drinking tea, watching the same quarter of the river with an expression Nia couldn't read at all.

"There's a boat behind us," Nia said. "In the mist. Port quarter. It's been matching our speed since before dawn."

Cyrus didn't startle. Didn't even lower her cup. But Nia saw her swallow, saw the tendons shift in her throat, and understood that Cyrus had known. Had probably known since yesterday.

"How long have you known?" Nia asked, and hated how young her voice sounded, how it cracked on *known*.

"Since the village yesterday," Cyrus said. "The one that wouldn't trade. They weren't watching us, Nia. They were watching past us. At whatever was coming upriver behind." She set the cup down. Her hands were steady. Everything about her was steady, always, and Nia had begun to suspect that the steadiness cost her something enormous, paid out daily, like ballast pumped overboard. "Vasquez says it's a patrol launch out of Angoram. Says we shouldn't worry."

"Do you believe him?"

Cyrus looked at her then — really looked, the full weight of those grey eyes, and Nia felt herself being weighed, measured, assessed for cargo capacity. How much truth could she carry?

"No," Cyrus said. "I don't believe him. And I think you should keep what you saw to yourself — write it in your book, in your own shorthand, and tell no one but me and Salim. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"It's not a game anymore. I need you to understand that. Whatever this expedition is supposed to be, there are people on this river who want what we're carrying, and they don't care how old you are or whether you've finished growing. From today, you keep your eyes open and your mouth shut, and you stay inside the rails. Understood?"

*Understood.* Nia nodded, and the word sat in her chest like a stone dropped down a well — she heard it falling and falling and never heard it land. Because she *did* understand, that was the terrible thing. Somewhere between the stilt village and the mist and the patch that held station, childhood had quietly slipped its mooring and drifted off downstream, and she was still standing on deck, and the river went on ahead of them, brown and patient and vast, winding up into a country that remembered things the rest of the world had agreed to forget.

She opened the map book and wrote, in her own shorthand, the private cipher she'd invented for exactly this kind of entry:

*Dawn — shadow boat, port quarter, holds station. V lies. C knows. Tell S.*

Then she drew, beneath it, small and precise, the shape of the river ahead as the chart showed it: a long wandering line climbing northwest into blankness, into country the maps abandoned, toward coordinates that lived in a dead nation's chest.

And beside the line, where the blankness began, she wrote one more word, the first honest entry in eleven months of careful observation:

*Fear.*

* * *

That night the mist came again, thicker, and with it the sound — faint, far upstream, carried down the current the way sound travels over water at night. Drums. Not the festival kind. Slow, spaced, deliberate, each beat landing like a footstep in an empty house.

Bo came up on deck and stood beside her at the rail, and neither of them spoke, and the drums went on.

"That'll be a signal," Bo said finally. "Village to village. Somebody's telling somebody something."

"Telling them what?"

Bo was quiet a moment. Out in the dark, the patch of mist that held station had drifted closer — close enough now that Nia could make out, for half a heartbeat when the fog thinned, the low silhouette of a hull running without lights, and the suggestion of figures standing motionless along its rail.

"Telling them we're coming," Bo said.
