## 7. The Village That Remembers

The village announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and river mud and something green and crushed, like the basin exhaling after holding its breath for a hundred years. Then the stilts rose out of the mist, forty feet of black ironwood driven into the riverbed, and the houses perched on top of them like herons standing in shallow water, patient, unhurried, watching us come.

Nia cut the engine a half mile out. We drifted the last stretch in near silence, the boat's wake dying behind us, and the only sound was the paddle-strokes of two men in a dugout canoe who had come out to meet us — not to greet, I noticed. To meet. There was a difference, and the difference was a rifle barrel's width.

"Easy," Salim murmured from the wheelhouse, though nobody had moved. He had a way of talking to all of us at once, the way a man talks to a fire he's trying to keep from spreading. "We're guests. Act like it."

"We're not guests," Bo said. "Guests get invited."

"We're guests the moment they decide we are," Salim said. "So let's help them decide."

The mist was burning off in rags, and through the gaps I could see the full architecture of the place: houses on stilts, walkways on stilts, a church on stilts with a corrugated roof gone orange with rust, a schoolhouse whose walls were papered with decades of mission handbills faded to the color of skin. Everything lifted above the water on those black legs. Everything up out of reach of the flood. I understood the instinct. I had built my whole life the same way — everything important stowed high and dry, above the waterline, above the reach of anything that could take it.

And then I'd gone and opened a chest at the bottom of a river, and now here we were, floating into a village that had every reason to fear us, carrying a seal that belonged to a country that had stopped existing before Nia was born.

* * *

The elder's name was Mambu, and we learned it the way you learn anything in the basin — sideways, slowly, through the patient translation of a schoolteacher named Ruth who had come upriver from the coast eleven years ago for a two-year posting and never left. She met us at the foot of the central ladder with a boy of maybe ten beside her, and she looked at our boat for a long moment before she looked at us.

"You came from downriver," she said. It wasn't a question.

"We did," Cyrus — I — said. "We're a salvage crew. We're doing a survey." The word *sanctioned* sat in my mouth like a stone. I'd been saying it for three days, ever since Rennick put it there, and every time it came out I could feel it losing weight, becoming the lie it had always been.

"Survey," Ruth repeated, tasting it. Her English was precise, faintly formal, the English of textbooks and church services. "The navy boats came through in the dry season. They also said survey."

Bo shifted behind me, a creak of the deck like a joint complaining. "We're not navy."

"No," she agreed. "You're not." And something in the way she said it — not warm, exactly, but less cold than the rest of the sentence — told me that not being navy was, in this village, a kind of credential. "Mambu will see you. Not all of you. Two."

Salim and I went up the ladder. Bo and Nia stayed with the boat, and I felt Nia's eyes on my back the whole climb, young and fierce and scared, and I hated leaving her there, and I went anyway, because that is what the river asks of you: it asks you to leave things behind, over and over, and to keep climbing.

* * *

Mambu received us on a veranda above the water, in a house that smelled of smoke and dried fish and old timber. He was small and immensely old, or perhaps not old at all — in the basin, the river ages men in its own currency, and I have met forty-year-olds who moved like driftwood and sixty-year-olds who moved like current. His skin was mapped with scars in patterns too regular to be accident, and his hands rested on a walking stick of ironwood polished to a shine by decades of palms.

Ruth sat to one side and translated, but it became clear within minutes that Mambu understood more English than he let on. He listened to my careful explanation — salvage, survey, documents, a seal — with the stillness of a heron, and when I reached into my shirt and brought out the rubbing I'd made of the Vaskarian crest, the wax and graphite impression of the double-headed bird and the crossed anchors, the air in the room changed.

I felt it before I saw it. The way a diver feels a pressure change before the gauge confirms it.

Mambu took the rubbing in both hands. He did not look at it so much as receive it, the way you'd receive a letter from the dead. And then he began to speak, low and continuous, in a language Ruth rendered into English a phrase at a time, her voice growing quieter as she went, as though the words themselves had weight and she was carrying them up from depth.

"He says this bird had two heads because it watched two directions," she said. "The past and the future. He says the men who wore it claimed to do the same. He says they only ever watched what they wanted to take."

"When?" I asked. My voice came out wrong. Too thin.

Ruth asked. Mambu answered, and his answer was a long time coming, and when Ruth translated it she had to stop once and start again.

"Before the war that ended the old country. He was a young man. The bird-men came up from the delta in a steel boat with a flat bottom, and they brought gifts — machetes, salt, medicine, a radio that played music. They asked for guides. They asked for laborers. They paid in the same machetes they gave as gifts, so the village gave, because a village cannot refuse men who carry guns beneath their gifts."

"How many men?"

"Eleven outsiders. Forty laborers from the villages along this stretch, his own cousin among them." Ruth's voice had gone flat, the flatness of someone carrying something carefully so as not to spill it. "They went up into the interior, following the old salt trails toward the mountains. The expedition was gone for one season. Then two."

The river slapped against the stilts below us, a slow patient rhythm, like something knocking.

"Then the steel boat came back down," Ruth continued. "But it came back with eight of the eleven outsiders, and none of the laborers. And the eight did not stop to trade or speak. Mambu was on the bank. He says they looked like men who had seen the inside of their own heads and did not like the furniture." She faltered on the idiom, then let it stand. "The village asked after its men. The bird-men said the laborers had been released at their villages downriver. But the villages downriver said the same about us. And the steel boat went away and never came back, and after some years the country with the two-headed bird stopped appearing on the radio, and the world moved on, and the river kept what the river keeps."

"Forty men," I said. "Gone. Just — gone."

"Not gone," Mambu said then, in English. His first words in our language, and they came out rough and deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a crossing. "Taken. There is a difference. The river does not take men. Men take men."

The floorboards seemed to tilt. I put my hand flat on the rail and held on.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in me in that moment, because honesty is the only salvage I have left to offer. What happened was arithmetic. Forty laborers, eleven outsiders, eight returned. Three unaccounted for. And somewhere in my chest a number I had been carrying for three years — the number *one*, the number *Tomas* — began, for the first time, to move. To shift its weight. To become not a stone but a seed.

I had spent three years performing a mathematics of grief. Three years of diving wrecks and measuring my life in nautical miles because miles could be logged and logged things could be totaled and totaled things could, in principle, be finished. Tomas was a closed account. I had held the funeral myself, on the aft deck, with a borrowed priest's words and Salim's cooking and Bo standing at attention like a man at a military burial, and I had told the river *keep him* the way you tell the sea *keep him*, and the river had said nothing, the way it always says nothing, and I had called that an answer.

Now an old man on a veranda was telling me the river does not take men.

"Ask him," I said to Ruth, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I steadied it the way you steady a load on a swaying deck. "Ask him about the outsiders. The three who didn't come back. Did anyone — did the village ever hear —"

Ruth translated. Mambu listened. And then he did something I have thought about every day since: he set the wax rubbing down on the rail, weighted it with a stone, and turned his whole body toward me, and took my hand in both of his. His palms were like bark. He looked at me the way you look at a diver coming up from deep water, checking for the signs.

"The bird-men had names," he said, still in English, still placing each word. "The village does not forget names. A name is a rope. You hold the rope, and the man on the other end is not lost." He closed his eyes. He was not remembering. I understood that with a diver's certainty. He was *retrieving* — hauling something up from a depth where it had lain wrapped and weighted for thirty years, and the hauling hurt him, and he did it anyway.

"Voss," he said. "The leader. The one who smiled with his teeth only. Keller, the one who counted everything. And a young one the laborers liked. He carried a knife with a bone handle and he cut fish for the cook and he learned eleven words of our language and laughed when he said them wrong." Mambu opened his eyes. "The young one was called Sterling."

The veranda went very far away and very close at the same time. I heard the river. I heard a chicken somewhere below, indignant about something. I heard my own blood, which is a sound you only hear at the moments when everything else stops mattering.

"That's my name," I said. Or I think I said it. It may have only happened inside me. "Sterling. That's — he's my brother. Tomas Sterling."

Mambu did not look surprised. That was the thing that undid me — not the name, but the lack of surprise, the terrible calm of a man who has known for thirty years that a rope was tied to a man at the other end, and has held it, and has waited for someone to come asking about the other end.

"The young one did not come back with the eight," Mambu said. "But he did not die with the laborers. The laborers died in the second season, in the mountains. The village knows this because one man came back — not downriver, but overland, a year later, half-dead, mad with what he had seen. He lived in the church house until the fever took him. Before the fever, he talked." Mambu's grip on my hand tightened until it hurt, and I let it hurt. "He talked about a bird-cage in a clearing. He talked about crates carried at night. And he talked about the young one, Sterling, who argued with Voss over the laborers, who tried to walk men out, who was last seen at the treeline with Voss's pistol against his neck." A pause, long as a sounding line paying out. "The madman said he saw him fall. But the madman also said the river spoke to him, and the village learned which of his words to trust."

"And the rest?" My mouth was full of river water. I was drowning on a dry veranda in the morning sun. "The rest of what he said — did you trust it?"

"Some." Mambu released my hand at last, and I felt the print of his grip stay on my skin like a band of iron, like a manacle, like a lifeline, all three at once. "We trusted the part about where. The place with the bird-cage. The village has never gone there. The place is *tambaran* — forbidden, spirit-ground. But the river does not honor what men forbid, and neither do the young." He turned and spoke rapidly to Ruth, and something in his tone shifted, and she stiffened, and translated with visible reluctance:

"He says there is a man in the next village up — a hunter who trades in crocodile and birds of paradise — who goes where he likes and fears nothing but debt. Two seasons ago the hunter came back from the high trails with a knife he would not speak about. A knife with a bone handle. He said he found it in a streambed near the old camp of the bird-men. He said the streambed was disturbed, recently, by human feet. Many feet. Coming and going." Ruth's voice dropped. "He said the village up there feeds a ghost. He said the ghost buys things from them with river gold and never shows his face in daylight."

The mist had burned off entirely by then. The basin stood revealed in its full green enormity, the river running silver toward mountains that were still only a rumor of blue at the world's edge, and somewhere in all that enormity, if an old man's memory and a madman's testimony and a hunter's silence could be believed, my brother was buying rice with river gold and hiding his face from the sun.

Hope is not a gentle thing. I had always imagined it would be, if it ever came — imagined it as warmth, as light, as the thing you'd been starving for finally set in front of you. But it came the way the chest had come, up from the dark all at once, and it hit me the way pressure hits a diver who ascends too fast: it got into my blood and fizzed and burned, and I gripped the rail and breathed and breathed, because the alternative was to come apart on a stranger's veranda in a language I didn't speak.

Three years I had measured my life in miles. And every one of those miles, it turned out, had been a circle, drawing slowly tighter around a clearing in the mountains where a man with my father's hands was still alive.

"Take me to the hunter," I said.

Mambu shook his head slowly, and for one black second I thought the whole thing was closing, the way a dream closes, the way a wreck closes over a diver who has stayed too long. Then he spoke, and Ruth translated, and her voice was strange with something that might have been awe.

"He says the hunter comes to us, not the other way — it is the season of the trade canoes, and he is due within days." She hesitated. "He says also that we are not the first to come asking about the bird-men. A navy boat stopped here three weeks ago. A woman with grey in her hair and a voice like a hatch cover asked the same questions. Mambu told her nothing." His eyes, when I met them, held no apology and no warmth — only the flat arithmetic of a man who had decided, thirty years too late, where the rope's other end belonged. "He says he will tell the hunter about you. And he says you should decide quickly what your woman in the wheelhouse is to be told. Because a rope has two ends, daughter of Sterling, and the bird-men always held one of them."

Behind us, down the ladder, across the silver water, our boat sat at anchor with Rennick's satellite phone sealed in its locker and Rennick's orders folded in its chart table, and I stood on the veranda with my brother's name still ringing in the air like a struck bell, and understood that the hardest dive of my life was not behind me.

It was the one I'd have to make without telling anyone how deep I was going.
