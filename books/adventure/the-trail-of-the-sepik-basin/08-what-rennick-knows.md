## 8. What Rennick Knows

The cabin lamp threw a yellow circle on the desk, and inside that circle lay the folder that could end Ilse Rennick's career — or the one that had already ended her soul, depending on which night you asked her.

Tonight was a bad night.

She had locked the cabin door twice, checked it twice more, and still sat with her back to the bulkhead as though the river itself might come through the planking to read over her shoulder. Outside, the Sepik moved in the dark with its endless wet whispering, and somewhere down the deck one of the sailors laughed at something, quick and human, and the sound made her feel obscene.

She opened the folder again.

It was thin for what it contained. Fourteen pages, most of them redacted into gray ladders of black bar, the classification stamp bleeding slightly where someone's thumb had been damp. TOP SECRET — SPECIAL HANDLING — NOFORN. Below that, in the flat bureaucratic language that always reminded her of autopsy reports: *Operation LANTERN REED. Recovery of sensitive materials. Confirmation of status, all listed personnel. Total information denial.*

Confirmation of status. She had read those three words perhaps two hundred times since the orders reached her in Port Moresby, and each time they shed another layer of their official skin until what remained underneath was naked and simple: *make sure no one is alive who shouldn't be.*

Rennick poured two fingers of gin from the bottle she kept wrapped in a chart sleeve and did not drink it. She held the glass the way she'd once held her father's hand at the funeral — for the weight of it, for something to grip.

*You knew what this was when you took it,* she told herself. *Don't perform surprise now. You're not an audience. You're staff.*

That was the discipline she had built her whole life on: never be an audience to your own orders. Be the instrument. Instruments didn't flinch. Instruments got promoted.

She turned to page six, the annex she had memorized against her own will, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

LANTERN REED had not begun as a crime. That was the thing nobody understood from the outside, the thing she herself hadn't understood until the file came apart in her hands like wet paper. It had begun eleven years ago as a heritage-recovery operation — legitimate on its face, sanctioned by a government that technically no longer existed but whose debts and secrets had been quietly inherited by three successor states and one very interested naval intelligence directorate. The Vaskarian expedition into the Sepik Basin had been mounted by private contractors under diplomatic cover, tasked with recovering cultural artifacts from the flooded mission stations and pre-colonial sites along the middle river.

Artifacts. She read the word and heard the word beneath it, the way a diver hears the hull-tone under the engine noise.

The expedition had found artifacts, yes. Masks, bronzes, ceremonial gold, crates of manuscripts. But the annex made clear — in the passive voice that governments used like anesthesia — that *local labor arrangements had proven difficult to formalize*, that *compensation disputes had arisen*, that *certain personnel had exceeded authorized parameters in securing cooperation.*

Securing cooperation.

Rennick set the glass down because her hand had begun to shake, and she refused — she absolutely refused — to watch it happen.

There were photographs in the annex. She had looked at them exactly once, on the transport plane, with the shade pulled down and the engines drowning everything, and once had been sufficient for a lifetime. Villagers standing in lines. Men with wrists bound with the same orange polypropylene twine the expedition used for cargo netting. A pit. She would not describe the pit even to herself. Her mind skated off it every time, the way a boot skates on river mud, and she let it skate, because the alternative was to look directly at the fact that her own service — her navy, her flag, her oath — had inherited the paperwork of a massacre the way you inherit a house with something buried in the garden.

And then there were the taken men.

Page nine. The list. Eleven names of expedition personnel unaccounted for after the incident — the *incident*, singular, as if the whole catastrophe had been a weather event, a squall, a bridge collapse. Nine of the eleven were presumed dead; the annex said so with the confidence of men writing about other men. Two were marked *status unknown — recovery priority.*

One of the two was Tomas Sterling.

The other was Daniel Rennick.

Her brother's name sat on the page in twelve-point type, and it looked wrong there, the way your own name looks wrong on a headstone mock-up. Danny. Who had joined the survey corps at nineteen because he wanted maps, only maps, who used to draw coastlines on napkins at dinner until their mother confiscated them, who had written her one letter from the basin — one — full of censored bars and a strange sentence near the end that she had thought about every day for eleven years:

*If anything happens to me, Ilsie, don't let them tell you it was the river.*

She had spent years deciding what he meant. Drowning? Crocodiles? The river was the obvious killer up here; it took boats and men and whole villages without malice or memory. *It was the river* was the sentence every bereaved family in the delta received, stamped and notarized, the all-purpose absolution. Don't let them tell you it was the river.

Danny had known, even then, at nineteen, writing through censorship bars, that whatever killed him would wear the river's name like a mask.

Rennick drank the gin now, all of it, fast, because her throat needed doing something else besides closing.

* * *

The lamp buzzed. Moths ticked against the screen outside. On the desk, the folder lay open to the operations summary, and she forced herself through it line by line, the way she'd once forced herself through her father's medical charts, because understanding was a kind of penance and she owed penance tonight.

Her orders, distilled:

**Recover the chest.** The lead-lined chest recovered by the salvage vessel out of Port Daru — the Sterling woman's boat, the one with the engineer built like a dock crane and the girl who watched everything and the old cook whose eyes missed nothing. The chest contained the expedition's true logs, the ones that had never been surrendered, the ones that documented in the expedition commander's own handwriting what had been done at the labor camps and where the artifacts had gone and who had signed for them. Names. Signatures. Men who were now directors and deputy ministers and holders of defense contracts. The chest was not evidence of a crime. It was evidence of a *network*, and networks, once exposed, dragged down everyone touching them.

**Confirm no survivors.** The phrase again, patient as a tap dripping. If Tomas Sterling lived — if any of the taken men lived — then testimony existed, and testimony could not be redacted. A dead man's logbook could be disputed, discredited, dismissed as forgery. A living witness sitting in a courtroom, breathing, pointing — that was different. That was a fire that couldn't be smothered with classification stamps.

**Bury the crimes.** Not conceal — bury. There was a difference and the difference was the whole shape of her complicity. Concealment left seams. Burying meant building over: a sanctioned survey expedition, a tidy official narrative, a salvage crew bound into silence by charges hanging over their heads, a recovered chest sealed in an archive that no journalist would ever see, and — if necessary — a grave or two in the interior that the river would be blamed for.

She had read the orders and accepted them in Port Moresby in eleven minutes. She remembered the exact duration because she had timed herself, some grim professional pride demanding efficiency even at her own damnation. Eleven minutes to read fourteen pages and say *understood, sir.* Eleven minutes. Danny had been gone longer than that times thirty thousand.

What frightened her now — lying awake in the wet heat while the boat's engines idled against the current — was not the orders themselves. She had long ago stopped pretending she was the kind of officer who would refuse. Refusal was a luxury of people with clean hands and somewhere else to go. What frightened her was how *good* she was at it. How smoothly the cover story had assembled itself in her mouth when she faced the Sterling woman — *a matter of recovered materials of historical significance, Ms. Sterling, nothing more* — how naturally the threat had worn the clothing of opportunity. Join us or drown. She had delivered it almost kindly. That was the part that made her skin crawl at 0300. Not the cruelty. The kindness. The professional warmth of a woman handing another woman a leash and calling it a lifeline.

The Sterling woman had seen it. Rennick knew she'd seen it. Cyrus Sterling had the eyes of someone who had spent years looking down into green water for a body, and such eyes did not miss much on the surface.

* * *

She rose, crossed the three steps to the porthole, and opened the cover.

The river lay black and muscular under a sky bruised purple at the edges where the storm cells stacked up over the interior. Mist stood on the water in columns, slow as smoke. Somewhere upstream a village dog barked twice and quit. The navigation lights of their escort launch floated far downstream, small and patient as bait.

Her reflection hovered in the glass, superimposed on the water, and she studied it with the detachment she'd learned in interrogation training — study the subject, note the tells. Forty-four years old. Gray coming in at the temples faster than she'd admit. Eyes that her mother had once called *your grandfather's eyes, Ilsie, he saw too much and said too little,* and her mother had meant it as a compliment, God help them both.

A careerist. That was what she was, that was the honest word, and she made herself hold it. Not a believer — she had stopped believing in the institution around the same year she stopped believing in marriage, and for similar reasons: proximity reveals architecture, and the architecture here was rot. Not a zealot. Not even, strictly, ambitious anymore; ambition had curdled somewhere around the rank of commander, when she'd realized the next rung belonged to whoever was willing to carry the most uncarryable things. No — she was a woman who had traded her integrity in installments so small that no single transaction ever felt like the one that mattered, and now she stood at the end of that long ledger holding a folder full of massacre and a brother's name on a kill-adjacent list, and the installments had compounded into something she could no longer lift.

*Confirm no survivors.*

She thought about the Sterling woman's face when the name Tomas Sterling had passed between them — carefully, professionally, watching. Nothing in the official record connected him to her; the file said *sister, deceased presumed, no next-of-kin notification required.* But Rennick had watched the woman's hands when she said it. Hands told you what faces had been trained not to. Those hands had gone still — completely still, the stillness of a diver hanging neutral in the water column, expending nothing, revealing nothing — and stillness like that was louder than screaming.

Cyrus Sterling believed her brother might be alive. And Rennick, reading the annex by lamplight, was beginning to believe it too.

Because here was the detail that the redactions had failed to fully devour, the splinter working under her skin for three days now: the recovery priority designation. You did not assign *recovery priority* to men you believed dead. Dead men got memorial citations and closed files. *Recovery priority* meant the directorate had received — from some source, at some time, redacted — indication of continued life. Which meant someone up the chain knew. Someone had known for years that at least one of the taken men might have survived in the interior, sheltered by villagers who asked no questions, and had chosen to leave him there — a living witness preserved like a body in cold storage — until such time as his existence became inconvenient enough to require *confirmation of status.*

They hadn't lost track of Danny and Sterling. They had been *keeping* them. And now the chest had surfaced, and the keeping was over.

Rennick pressed her forehead against the cool brass rim of the porthole and breathed the river smell — silt, vegetation, woodsmoke from villages she'd never see, and under it all the faint sweet-rotten breath of the drowned forest. Danny was somewhere up that river. Or his bones were. Either way, she was sailing toward him at eight knots carrying orders to make certain that whatever he knew died with him.

*If anything happens to me, Ilsie, don't let them tell you it was the river.*

"I know, Danny," she said aloud, to the glass, to the mist, to the boy who drew coastlines on napkins. "I know what they'll say."

The moths beat against the screen. Down the corridor, boots passed and faded. She went back to the desk, squared the folder, and did the thing she had decided three nights ago to do and had postponed every night since with the coward's arithmetic of *tomorrow I'll be stronger.*

She took a sheet of the boat's plain signal paper, uncapped her pen, and wrote at the top, in her own hand, unclassified, deniable, deniable, deniable:

*Notes on discrepancies, Operation Lantern Reed — personal file, Cdr. I. Rennick.*

Then she began to write what the redactions had hidden. The dates that didn't match. The casualty figures that contradicted the annex. The source code — half-visible through a careless strip of black — that indicated the directorate had received a sighting report of a survivor eighteen months ago and buried it. Page by page, discrepancy by discrepancy, she built a shadow file, a paper ghost of the truth, hiding it inside the false bottom of her chart drawer where she kept the letters Danny had sent her, all four of them, the whole of what remained.

It was not conscience, she told the lamp, the moths, the dark. Conscience was for people who still believed their souls were negotiable currency. This was simpler. This was a woman discovering that the ladder she had climbed for twenty-two years had been leaning against the wrong wall the entire time — and that her brother's name was carved somewhere near the top of it.

Outside, the mist thickened until the river and the sky became one gray breathing thing, and far upstream — though she could not have said how she knew, except that grief is a kind of sonar — something waited in the interior that would force her hand sooner than she was ready for.

She wrote until the lamp oil burned low, and when she finally slept, she dreamed of a napkin coastline, and a boy's pencil pausing at a place where the river bent, and a voice saying: *mark this spot, Ilsie. Whatever they tell you — mark this spot.*
