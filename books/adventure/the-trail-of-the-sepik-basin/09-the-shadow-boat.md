## 9. The Shadow Boat

Bo Kessler had spent thirty-one years listening to engines the way other men listened to music. He could tell a Detroit Diesel from a Caterpillar blindfolded, could hear a fouled propeller three decks down through steel and shouting. So when the sound came — low, muffled, wrong for the hour — he was awake before his mind finished naming it.

Not navy. That was the first thing. Navy boats announced themselves; they ran their lights and their generators and their self-importance like flags. This was something quieter. Something that didn't want to be heard until it wanted to be.

He lay in his bunk in the engine room's heat and counted. The *Marrow's Grace* sat at anchor off a bend of the Sepik where the river widened into a brown lake studded with drowned trees, their dead branches rising out of the water like the fingers of men reaching up from below. Mist lay on the surface in long gray ropes. Frogs sang in the reeds. And underneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: an engine idling at low revs, holding position somewhere upstream, just past the point.

Bo swung his feet to the deck plates and did not put on his boots. Boots made noise. He'd learned that in another life, another river, though he never talked about that one and never would.

* * *

He found Cyrus already on deck. Of course he did. She stood at the rail with her arms folded against the damp, watching the upstream dark, and she didn't turn when his weight came down on the boards behind her because she'd heard him coming the way she always did — or maybe she'd been waiting for him specifically, because it was always the two of them who took the night watches nobody asked for.

"You hear it," she said. Not a question.

"Idling past the point. Maybe four hundred meters." Bo came up beside her and squinted into the murk. "Diesel, but she's running quiet. Exhaust baffled."

"Navy doesn't baffle exhausts."

"No," Bo said. "Navy wants you to know they're there. That's half of what navy is."

Cyrus said nothing for a while. The mist moved. Somewhere across the water a night bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, over and over, patient as grief.

"Rennick's people?" Cyrus asked finally.

"Rennick would've hailed us by now. Announced herself. Made it official." Bo rubbed the stubble on his jaw. "This is somebody who doesn't want to be official."

"Contractors," Cyrus said, and the word came out flat and cold, like a coin dropped on a table.

Bo looked at her. In the dark her face was mostly shadow and jawline, but he'd known her three years now, since the day she'd hired him off a dock in Madang with nothing but a toolbox and a bad reputation, and he could read her the way he read gauges. She wasn't afraid. That was the thing about Cyrus Sterling that frightened him more than anything else — fear seemed to have been cut out of her somewhere along the way, replaced with something harder and less useful. Anger, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"Whoever they are," Bo said slowly, "they're not here to ask questions."

"No."

"And Rennick either doesn't know about them or she does, and if she does—"

"Then we're not passengers on this expedition," Cyrus finished. "We're cargo."

The word hung between them in the wet air. Cargo. Bo thought about Salim asleep in the galley with his spice tins lined up like soldiers, and Nia curled in the bow compartment with her charts and her pencil stubs, seventeen years old and mapping a river that was starting to map her back. He thought about the chest locked in the hold, lead-lined and countryless, full of photographs of a dead man who apparently wasn't dead, and how every day since they'd pulled it from the river felt like standing in a room where someone had left a lit match on a table and everyone was pretending not to see it.

"I'll wake Salim," Bo said. "Get him to move us. We can run downriver in the dark if we have to — I know these shallows better than they ever will."

"They're not chasing us, Bo." Cyrus still hadn't turned. "They're herding us. There'll be another boat downstream. There always is."

And there was. They found it twenty minutes later when Salim, woken and grim and moving with the unhurried competence of a man who had survived worse than this in three other countries, brought the *Grace* around slow and quiet toward the downstream channel — and Nia, who should have been asleep, appeared at the bow rail with her binoculars and went very still.

"Bo," she called softly. "Bo, there's a light."

* * *

It wasn't a running light. It was a cigarette.

That was what made Bo's stomach drop, later, when he understood it. The glow bobbed at the rail of a boat sitting dark in the mouth of the channel, maybe six hundred meters off, close enough that the ember showed and vanished, showed and vanished, patient as the bird's cry. Somebody over there was smoking and waiting and not worried about being seen at all.

Salim killed their own anchor light and they drifted, engine off, current nudging them gently toward the drowned trees, and the four of them stood on the deck in the mist and watched the cigarette burn its small red hole in the night.

"Two boats," Nia whispered. She'd come aft without being told, her binoculars hanging from her neck, her face pale under the starlight. "One up, one down. We're in the middle."

"We're in the net," Salim corrected quietly. He had his hands folded in front of him, the way he did when he was thinking, thumbs pressing against each other. "The question is what kind of net. Fisherman's or butcher's."

"Don't say things like that in front of her," Bo said, before he could stop himself.

"I'm right here," Nia said. "And I'm not a child."

"You're seventeen."

"I'm the one who spotted them." Her chin came up, and Bo saw — not for the first time — the woman she was becoming inside the girl she still was, and hated how fast it was happening, hated that this river and this chest and this whole rotten expedition were doing the carving. "Both boats are maybe twelve meters. Aluminum hulls, twin outboards, no markings. No radar arrays visible. That's not military. Military has antennas. These are—" she hesitated, working the word through her head, "—commercial. Private."

"Contractors," Cyrus said again.

"How do you know that word keeps being right?" Bo asked her.

"Because I've been diving wrecks for eleven years, and you learn to tell the difference between ships that sink and ships that were sunk." Her voice stayed level. "Governments leave paperwork. Contractors leave nothing. If those were navy boats we'd have been boarded and lectured by now. Nobody's boarding us. They're measuring us."

"Measuring us for what?"

Nobody answered. The frog song filled the silence, and the bird with the oiled hinge, and beneath everything the river itself, that vast brown breathing thing, carrying its silt and its secrets down toward a delta that had already given up one impossible chest and could surely give up more.

Then the upstream boat switched on a single white floodlight and swept it across the water toward them, once, deliberately, like a man clearing his throat.

* * *

They came alongside within the hour.

Bo stood at the amidships rail with his hands visible and empty — he'd made sure of that, had made sure all of them kept their hands visible, because he'd seen what happened to crews whose hands weren't — and watched the aluminum boat slide in against the *Grace*'s hull with fenders thumping, gentle as a handshake. Four men aboard. Five. Hard to count in the floodlight glare that they kept trained on the *Grace*'s wheelhouse so that everyone on Bo's side was silhouetted and everyone on theirs was shadow.

The one in front wore a polo shirt. That detail lodged in Bo's memory and refused to leave: a gray polo shirt, clean, with a little embroidered logo over the breast that might have been a bird or a spearhead or nothing at all. Khaki trousers. A pistol in a holster worn openly, the way a carpenter wears a hammer. Behind him, the others carried themselves like men who worked out and slept well and had done this before, whatever *this* was, in places with worse mosquitoes and fewer witnesses.

"Evening," the polo-shirted man said. His accent was hard to place — South African, maybe, or Australian flattened by years elsewhere. Friendly. Terribly friendly. "Lovely river you've got here."

"It's not ours," Salim said from beside Bo. "We're just traveling it."

"Aren't we all." The man smiled. His teeth were very white in the glare. "Mind if we come aboard? Just routine. We're doing an environmental survey for a mining concern. Checking sediment loads, water quality. You know how it is."

"An environmental survey," Bo repeated. "At two in the morning. With pistols."

The man's smile didn't change, but something behind it did — a small recalculation, the flicker of a machine adjusting. "Safety protocols," he said. "River's got crocs big enough to eat a dog. You people dive in this?"

"We people mind our own business," Cyrus said.

She stepped forward out of the shadow of the wheelhouse and into the edge of the light, and Bo watched the polo-shirted man look at her — really look, cataloguing, the way a buyer looks at livestock — and Bo's hands curled into fists at his sides and then, deliberately, uncurled again.

"Cyrus Sterling," the man said.

The name dropped into the night like a stone into deep water. Nobody moved. On the downstream boat, Bo noticed now, a second figure had risen at the rail, and the cigarette was gone.

"That's interesting," the man went on pleasantly, "because the Sterling name comes up in some documents we've been asked to review. Salvage claims, permits, that sort of thing. Paperwork's a nightmare out here. Half these claims are filed twice, half the rest are fraudulent. You'd be amazed what people pull out of this river and think nobody will notice." He let that sit. "You'd be amazed what gets noticed."

"We pulled scrap," Bo said. "Chain, copper, a compass housing. Filed it all. Every gram."

"I'm sure you did." The man's eyes never left Cyrus. "And the other item?"

"What other item?" Cyrus asked.

And there it was — the moment, stretched thin as wire. Bo had lived long enough to know that most violence happened in moments like this one, in the half-second where a sentence could go one way or the other, and he braced himself with his weight forward on the balls of his feet, ready to step in front of Nia, ready to grab the gaff hook, ready for anything except what actually happened, which was that the polo-shirted man simply smiled wider, raised both hands in mock surrender, and stepped back onto his own boat.

"No matter," he said. "Survey continues. We'll see you upriver, I expect. It's a long river. Lots of bends." He paused, one foot on his gunwale, and added, almost kindly: "Lots of shallow water, too. Be careful where you draft. Boats ground out here all the time. People too."

The floodlight snapped off. The aluminum boat backed away into the mist, engines barely whispering, and inside ninety seconds it was gone — no lights, no wake worth the name, just the river closing over it like skin over a swallowed thing.

The downstream cigarette glowed twice more, then went out.

* * *

They didn't sleep. None of them pretended to. Salim brewed coffee strong enough to strip paint and they sat around the galley table with the single lamp burning low, and for a long time nobody spoke, because there was a new shape in the room with them and it took a while to learn how to breathe around it.

"They knew her name," Nia said at last. Her mug sat untouched in front of her, steam curling. "How did they know your name?"

"Rennick," Cyrus said.

"Or whoever sent Rennick," Salim said. "Or whoever sent whoever sent Rennick." He turned his mug slowly on the table, a quarter-turn at a time, precise as a man setting a bearing. "A chain, Miss Nia. And we are somewhere near the bottom of it, holding something the top of the chain wants."

"The chest," Bo said.

"The chest," Cyrus agreed. "Or what's in it. Or what it proves. Take your pick — to them it's all the same thing. It's evidence, and evidence is the only cargo in the world that gets people killed."

Bo looked around the table. Salim, sixty-two years old, who had fled one country and buried a wife in another and still hummed while he cooked, still saved the best cuts for Nia's plate. Nia, who had come aboard two years ago with salt sores on her feet and a stolen chart book under her arm, and who was now learning words like *contractor* and *evidence* in the same season she should have been learning nothing worse than how to read weather. Cyrus, who had dragged a nationless chest out of the mud and with it the whole drowning weight of her brother's ghost.

And himself. Bo Kessler, wrench-hand, engine-whisperer, a man who had spent three decades keeping boats alive and had somehow never once asked himself what the boats were carrying, or whether he'd have cared.

"They're going to try to take it," he said. "Upstream or downstream, tonight or next week. They're going to try, and when they do, they won't care much whether we're attached to it."

"So we give it to them," Nia said. "Give them the chest and go home."

"And Tomas?" Cyrus said quietly.

The name landed on the table like a knife. Nia looked down. Even now, even after the village elder's story, after the name that matched, after everything — the idea of Tomas Sterling was still too large for the boat to hold easily. A dead man alive. A witness in the interior. The one thing in the world that made the chest matter more than gold.

"If we hand over the chest," Cyrus said, "we hand over the coordinates. We hand over him. And then we go home rich in nothing and alive, and every night for the rest of my life I get to lie in my bunk knowing exactly what my silence bought." She shook her head once, a short violent motion. "No."

"Then we need a plan," Salim said. "Because courage without a plan is just a slower way of dying."

Bo grunted. It was as close to agreement as his throat would allow. He got up, refilled the mugs, and when he sat back down he found Nia watching him with an expression he couldn't quite read — part fear, part something steadier underneath it, something that hadn't been there a month ago.

"Bo," she said. "Back there. When they came alongside. You stepped between me and the rail. You didn't even think about it."

"You're crew," Bo said, embarrassed, staring into his coffee. "Crew looks after crew. That's the whole religion. Everything else is rigging."

Nia nodded slowly, and around the table the silence changed quality — from the silence of people who are afraid alone into the silence of people who are afraid together, which is a different animal entirely, warmer-blooded, harder to kill. Bo felt it settle over the four of them like a tarp lashed down before a storm. Whatever came up that river after them, it would find them lashed together.

It was Salim who broke it, rising to check the anchor line, pausing at the companionway with the lamplight catching the gray in his beard.

"One thing I don't understand yet," he said. "Those contractors knew Cyrus's name. They knew about the chest. But they didn't board us, didn't search us, didn't take it tonight when taking it would have been easy." He frowned at the dark beyond the doorway. "Which means they don't want the chest badly enough to spill blood over it. Yet. Which means they think they don't have to."

"Why wouldn't they have to?"

"Because," Salim said, "I think they're expecting someone else to do the dirty work for them. Someone official. Someone with authority to stop us, seal the evidence, and hand everything over nice and legal." He looked back at them, and his old eyes were sorrowful and clear. "I think, my friends, that Commander Rennick is going to receive new orders very soon. And I think we should decide, tonight, whose orders we intend to follow. Because come morning, I suspect the choice will no longer be offered."

Above them, through the deck plates, the river breathed. And far upstream, past the point where the mist thickened into darkness, an engine that had been idling all night quietly changed pitch — and began, unhurried, to follow.
