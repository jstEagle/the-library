## 10. Into the Interior

The guide's name was Mekel, and he walked the way water moved — without hurry, without hesitation, as if the ground had already agreed to hold him. He was perhaps sixty, though it was impossible to say; his skin carried the texture of old rope, and his eyes held that particular patience of men who have outlived everyone who ever doubted them. He wore a woven cap and carried nothing but a machete and a length of vine coiled over one shoulder like a sleeping snake.

Cyrus followed him into the green.

Behind her came Bo, breathing hard but refusing to say so, his engineer's hands wrapped around a walking staff he'd cut himself and refused to put down. Behind Bo came Nia, who had insisted on coming despite every argument Cyrus could mount, and behind Nia came two of Mekel's kinsmen, silent as herons, and Rennick at the rear with a navy-issue sidearm she pretended not to be touching. The rest stayed with the boat — Salim among them, waving them off from the rail with a spatula in one hand like a scepter, because Salim did not believe in goodbyes, only in meals waiting when you returned.

The Sepik Basin swallowed them within the first hour.

It was not forest the way Cyrus had known forest — not the salt-scoured scrub of coastlines, not the managed timber stands of her childhood. This was jungle as an argument, as a position the earth had taken and would defend. Trees rose sixty, eighty meters on buttressed roots wide enough to shelter a family, their canopies laced so tight overhead that daylight came down in coins and splinters. Everything dripped. Everything breathed. Fungi glowed faintly on rotting logs like lamps left burning in an empty house. Somewhere above, birds argued in voices that sounded almost human, and somewhere below, things moved through the leaf litter with the unhurried confidence of creatures who had never once been prey.

Cyrus measured the distance in her head the way she always did — not hours but meters, not meters but fathoms, the habit of a woman who thought in depths even when walking uphill. They had come four kilometers from the river. The logbook coordinates put the camp at eleven. Seven more kilometers of this.

She touched the folded pages in her breast pocket again. She had been touching them all morning, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it still hurts. The Vaskarian logbook, water-stained but legible where Salim's careful drying had saved it, gave the camp's position to within thirty paces: a rise above a creek junction, marked by a surveyor's notation in a hand that slanted left. *Camp established. Laborers quartered downslope.* And then, three entries later, in the same hand: *Discipline administered.*

She had read those words forty times since the village. *Discipline administered.* Four words, and beneath them, in the margin, a small tick mark, as if someone were keeping score.

"You keep counting," Bo said beside her, low, not quite a question.

"I keep measuring." She stepped over a root system like the ribs of something enormous. "It's what I do."

"It's what you hide behind," Bo said, and said nothing else for a kilometer.

* * *

They stopped at noon in a grove of sago palms while Mekel's kinsmen cut stems and tapped the pith for starch. Mekel himself crouched at the edge of the clearing, studying the ground, and after a long moment he beckoned Cyrus over without looking up.

"Look," he said. His English was sparse and precise, each word set down like a stone in a crossing.

The ground showed marks — not footprints exactly, but disturbances. A snapped sapling, its break browned and healing at the edges. A strip of bark gouged high on a trunk, shoulder height or higher. A patch of leaf litter pressed flat in a pattern too large and too regular for animal traffic.

"Men came through here," Cyrus said.

Mekel nodded slowly. "Many feet. Carrying heavy. Going up." He pointed northeast, deeper into the basin, toward the coordinates. "Not soldiers' walk. Soldiers walk small steps, many stops. These walk big, fast, afraid."

"Running?"

"Carrying," Mekel corrected. "Afraid and carrying. Different thing." He rose, knees cracking like green wood. "We go quiet now."

They went quiet. The jungle absorbed their silence and made it its own, and Cyrus found herself listening the way she listened underwater — not with her ears but with her whole skin, reading pressure changes, reading the absence of sound where sound should be. Because that was the thing about the deep: it wasn't silence. It was attention. The whole weight of the water paying attention to you.

Something in this forest was paying attention now.

* * *

They smelled the camp before they saw it.

Not decay — or not only decay. Woodsmoke gone cold and sour, soaked into the ground. Tarpaulin rotting. And beneath it all, threaded through everything like wire through wax, the sweet-heavy smell that Cyrus knew from wrecks, from sealed compartments that had been sealed too long, from the places where the river kept what it took. The smell of human time ending.

Mekel raised a fist and they stopped. He gestured for Cyrus to come forward alone — a tilt of the chin, no words — and she went, machete drawn, moving the way she'd been taught to move through a flooded corridor: slow, deliberate, leaving nothing disturbed behind her that she might need to read later.

The camp sat on the rise exactly where the logbook promised, above a creek junction that still ran clear and quick over red gravel. But the logbook had described a working camp — tents, a cook shelter, a surveyor's bench, a flagpole flying the black-and-gold of a nation that no longer existed. What remained was a skeleton picked clean by twenty years of rain.

Three tent platforms stood on pilings, the canvas long collapsed into black sheets of rot. The cook shelter's frame leaned at a drunk angle, its iron stove rusted into a sculpture of orange lace. A surveyor's bench remained, astonishingly intact under a shelf of tin roofing, its surface furred with moss, a brass instrument still bolted to it, green with age. And at the center of the clearing, driven into the earth and listing like a dying man, the flagpole — bare, its halyard rotted away, but the cleat still there, and the paint at its base still showing, when Cyrus knelt and scraped it with her thumbnail, a single flake of gold.

Vaskarian gold. The color of the seal on the chest. The color of the letterhead on the orders Rennick carried.

"Nobody's been here in years," Bo said behind her, having ignored Mekel's instruction entirely, because Bo ignored all instructions that involved leaving Cyrus alone in strange places. "This is old news. Whatever happened here—"

"Bo." Her voice came out wrong. Flat. "Look downslope."

He looked. Then he went very still, the way he went still around live electrical lines.

Downslope, where the laborers had been quartered according to the logbook, the jungle opened into a small natural clearing — and the clearing was full of graves.

Not old ones. Not twenty years of rain-washed mounds sinking back into the earth. These were fresh. Days old, some of them. One of them, nearest the tree line, was so new the cut ferns laid over it hadn't finished wilting. Wooden markers stood at the head of each mound, and though most bore only carved signs in a script Cyrus couldn't read, several bore names in rough Latin letters, cut deep and filled with ash.

And at the edge of the graves, arranged with terrible care on a flat stone, someone had placed objects. A rusted entrenching tool. A broken identification bracelet. A child's sandal, sun-bleached white.

"They've been burying people here," Nia whispered. She had come forward too; Cyrus hadn't the heart or the authority to stop her. "Recently. Weeks. Maybe days."

"The villagers," Cyrus said slowly, working it through. "Mekel's people. They know about this place. They've been—" She stopped. Started again. "These aren't expedition graves. The expedition died twenty years ago. These are new deaths. Someone is still dying out here, and someone else is still carrying them here to bury."

Mekel appeared at the clearing's edge with his kinsmen, and whatever he saw in the arrangement of the graves made him close his eyes briefly, the way a man closes his eyes before bad news he has already guessed. He spoke softly to his kinsmen in their own tongue, and one of them answered, and the answer was short and grim.

"What is it?" Cyrus asked. "What did he say?"

Mekel considered her for a long moment. Whatever calculation he performed, he performed it visibly, weighing her the way she weighed a wreck before committing to the descent.

"My cousin says the graves are dug by the Kandari people, from the far side of the basin," he said finally. "They bring their dead here because this place is cursed and the dead must stay where they fell. Or near it." His eyes moved across the collapsed tents, the leaning flagpole. "Your countrymen made this place cursed. Now others pay the cost."

"How many graves?" Bo asked quietly.

Mekel counted without pointing, without moving his eyes obviously, the way a man counts crocodiles. "Thirty-one old ones, from long ago. Nine new."

Nine. In weeks. Cyrus felt the number settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom. Something was killing people in the interior of the Sepik Basin, right now, while she stood in a dead man's camp reading dead men's handwriting — and the villagers were responding not with investigation but with ritual, with burial, with the management of a curse.

Or with the management of something that looked like a curse to those who didn't know what they were looking at.

"We should go," Rennick said from the treeline, and her voice had lost its command cadence; it was just a voice now, tight and thin. "This site isn't sanctioned. If anyone finds us here—"

"Someone buried nine people here recently, Commander." Cyrus didn't turn around. "I'd like to know who found *them* first."

* * *

She found the knife an hour later, and she would remember for the rest of her life exactly how it happened — not because the moment was dramatic, but because it wasn't. It was small. It was almost nothing. That was the cruelty of it.

She had been working through the collapsed cook shelter, alone, turning over the debris with the methodical patience of a salvage diver cataloguing a wreck. Most of it was junk: rusted cans split like blooms, a boot sole, the green bones of a radio. Under the collapsed corner of the shelter's canvas, wedged between two floorboards where someone had hidden it rather than simply lost it, she found a oilcloth bundle tied with wire.

Her hands knew before her mind did. Hands are honest that way. They know the weight of things.

She unwired the bundle with fingers that would not quite obey, and the oilcloth fell open, and there it was.

A diving knife. Navy pattern, blunt-tipped, the handle wrapped in fresh cord — cord that had been replaced, rewrapped, maintained, cared for, sometime in the last few years. The blade was clean and oiled. And stamped into the brass guard, worn nearly smooth but still legible if you tilted it against the light, were the initials:

**T.S.**

Tomas Sterling. Her brother. The knife she had watched him buckle on at eighteen, grinning, the day he shipped out. The knife their father had given him, the knife he'd sworn he'd never lose because losing it meant losing the argument they'd had about it, the stupid loving argument, the last real conversation they'd ever—

Cyrus sat down in the wreckage of the cook shelter and held her brother's knife and made no sound at all, because the sound was too large to fit through her throat.

He had been here. Not twenty years ago — the cord proved that, the oil proved that, the care proved that. He had been here recently. Recently enough to leave a knife hidden under floorboards in a camp that had been abandoned for two decades, which meant he had come back, or been brought back, or had never in any way that mattered left.

And he had hidden it. Hidden it deliberately, wired it down, concealed it — which meant he expected to return for it, or he expected someone to find it, or he expected not to be able to come back for it at all.

*The dead leave instructions,* her grandmother used to say. *You just have to read them.*

She turned the knife over. And there, scratched into the oilcloth wrapping itself, in a hurried hand she recognized with a pain like decompression sickness — Tomas's hand, the same left-slanting hand as the surveyor's notes in the logbook, because of course it was, of course he had been the one keeping the records, even then, even at the end — were scratched six characters:

**K-7-4-RIVER**

Coordinates. River kilometers, the navy salvage convention, the shorthand they'd grown up with at their father's knee. Kilometer seventy-four, on the river. Upstream. Deeper.

"Tomas," she whispered, and the name came out steady, which surprised her, because nothing inside her was steady; everything inside her was fire moving through wet wood, hissing and spreading and refusing to die. "You brilliant, stubborn bastard. You're alive. You're alive and you're leaving me a map."

Bo found her there — of course he did, he always did — and he crouched beside her in the ruin and looked at the knife and the scratched cloth, and Cyrus watched him understand it, watched the grief and the fury arrive in his face in that order, and then watched the fury win, because Bo had never in his life let grief finish a sentence if fury could interrupt.

"He was here," Bo said. "Recent."

"Yes."

"And he left directions."

"Yes."

Bo stood, and his knuckles cracked around his walking staff, and he looked out over the graves below — the old ones and the nine new ones — and when he spoke again his voice was gravel dragged over steel.

"Then we're done mourning in this hole. We're going to get your brother." He turned toward the treeline, toward the dark rising interior, toward kilometer seventy-four. "And God help whatever's out there thought hiding him from us was going to work."

Cyrus buckled the knife onto her belt, her brother's weight settling against her hip like a hand.

Above the clearing, unseen, the canopy went suddenly, completely silent — every bird at once — the way the water goes silent when something very large turns toward you in the dark.
