## 11. The Man Who Didn't Drown

The interior did not want us.

That was the first thing I understood about the land beyond the river's edge — it resisted the way deep water resists a diver who fights it. Every step was negotiation. Vines took back the trail within hours of the guide's machete; mud swallowed boots to the ankle and held them like hands; the air itself was thick enough to chew, humid and green-smelling, alive with the small industrious sounds of things eating other things. After three days of walking I had stopped thinking of the Sepik Basin as a place. It was an element. We were drowning slowly, on dry land.

Maret led us. He was the elder's grandson, nineteen or twenty, built lean as a whipstock, and he moved through the forest the way Nia moved through rigging — without looking, without doubting, his body answering questions before his mind asked them. He had said perhaps forty words since we left the village. Most of them had been *stop*. Once he'd crouched at a bend in the game trail, touched a print in the mud with two fingers, and looked back at me with an expression I couldn't read until later, when I understood it had been pity.

*Soldier,* the print said. *Recent.*

Bo walked behind me with the rifle slung across his back and his jaw set in that way that meant he was cataloguing everything wrong with our situation and finding the list too long to recite aloud. Behind him came Nia, carrying the water and the logbook wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child, and behind her Rennick — because she had refused to stay with the boat, and because Bo and I had decided, in the wordless conference of long partners, that a leashed enemy was safer than one left loose among our things.

She kept up well, I'd give her that. Naval boots, naval spine, sweat cutting clean channels through the red dust on her face. She hadn't complained once, which somehow made me trust her less.

"Close now," Maret said, near noon of the third day.

I felt the words before I parsed them. My body had been walking toward this for three years — through the navy's form letters, through the folded flag they'd given my mother before she died not knowing, through every mile of black water I'd logged since — and now that the distance was collapsing to hours, to minutes, some animal part of me wanted to turn around. Grief is a house you furnish. However terrible the rooms, you know where everything is. Hope is a flood through all the doors at once.

We climbed a ridge of old coral limestone, porous and knife-edged, and the forest opened into a hanging valley where mist stood in the hollows like something poured. Below us, half-hidden by breadfruit trees, was a settlement — smaller than the river village, a scatter of houses on stilts above gardens, smoke rising thin from two cooking fires. No church. No airstrip. Nothing that would show on any map made by any government. A village that had learned, apparently, the value of not existing.

Maret raised a hand and we stopped. He spoke then, quietly, in his own language first, and then — because Salim had taught him the phrase during those first evenings aboard, trading words across the galley table the way men trade tobacco — in broken English:

"He does not know you are coming. This is important. You will be patient."

"Patient," Bo repeated flatly.

"A man who has been hidden three years," Maret said, "is hidden because hiding keeps him alive. You do not shout at a thing that is keeping someone alive."

He went down alone. We waited among the trees while the mist moved and the birds argued about something in the canopy, and I discovered that my hands were shaking — not from fear, exactly, and not from cold, though the sweat had cooled on my skin. They shook the way they shake at ten meters down, when the light goes strange and your mind offers you, unasked, the image of everything that could go wrong. I pressed them flat against my thighs and watched the village and did not breathe properly for what might have been ten minutes.

Then Maret emerged from between the houses with someone beside him.

Even at that distance, even through heat-shimmer and fifty meters of garden, I knew the walk.

You spend your childhood learning a person's body alongside your own — the way Tomas swung his arms slightly wide, the roll in his shoulders from years of hauling lines, the hitch in his left knee from the motorcycle accident when he was seventeen that he'd never let the navy doctors fully fix. I had watched that walk disappear down a gangway in Darwin six years ago. I had dreamed it walking away more times than I could count. And here it was, coming toward me up a trail in the middle of nowhere, changed — thinner, slower, the hitch worse, the arms held close now instead of wide, a man conserving himself — but *his*. Unmistakably, impossibly his.

My legs stopped working. That's the truth of it. I got maybe four steps down the ridge and then my body simply declined to continue, as if some part of me refused to approach the moment until it was certain the moment was real, because if I reached him and he wasn't — if this was another of the dreams, the ones I'd trained myself out of having —

"Cy."

His voice. Roughened, rusted, a voice that hadn't been used much in three years, but underneath the rust, his.

"Tomas." It came out as almost nothing. "Tomas."

He broke into a run — a bad run, lurching, the bad knee betraying him on the slope — and I broke too, and we met in the mud of the garden path with a collision that knocked whatever breath I had left clean out of me. He was real. He was appallingly real: ribs under my hands where there should have been muscle, hair gone gray at thirty-four, a beard matted with something sticky, scars — God, the scars, ridged burn tissue climbing his right forearm and disappearing up under a shirt made of bark cloth. He smelled of woodsmoke and river and illness. He was shaking as hard as I was.

"You're dead," I heard myself say, stupidly, into his shoulder. "They told us you were dead. There was a — they sent a letter, there was a ceremony, Mom—"

"I know." His hand came up and gripped the back of my head like when we were kids, like when I was nine and he was thirteen and the world was only weather and water. "Cy. I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I couldn't — they think I'm dead, everyone has to think I'm dead, that's the whole—" His voice cracked apart. He tried again. "I watched the letter get written. That's how I knew. I saw the list."

I pulled back far enough to see his face and the joy in it — the raw, disbelieving joy — and I thought, in the last clear second before the floor gave way beneath everything: *he looks like a man standing in a burning house trying to smile.*

"What list?" I asked. "Tomas. What list?"

Behind him, at the edge of the village, people had begun to gather. Women with children on their hips. Old men with spears they held loosely, the way you hold a tool rather than a weapon. They watched us embrace with expressions I would spend a long time understanding — not warmth, exactly. Something more careful than warmth. The look of people deciding whether hope was safe to bring outside.

And then I saw the others.

There were four of them, sitting in the shade of the largest house, apart from the rest. Three were local men, laborers by their builds, one missing an arm at the elbow, one with his eyes bandaged. The fourth was a skeleton of a white man in a Vaskarian field jacket, his scalp bald where it wasn't scarred, his hands resting in his lap with the terrible stillness of the very ill. None of them rose. All of them watched.

"Tomas," I said again, and my voice had changed and I felt it change, the joy draining out through some hole I hadn't known was there. "Who are they?"

"Come inside," he said. "Please. Not out here. Please, Cy."

"No." I stepped back. My hands found his arms and held on, and I hated myself for how hard I gripped, but I didn't let go. "Three years. You don't get to give me three years in installments. Who are these men? Why are they here? Why are *you* here?"

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the boy I grew up with was gone and something older stood in his place, something that had done arithmetic no one should ever have to do.

"Because I'm the only one left who saw it happen," he said. "All of it. And because if I die anywhere they can find me, so does the truth." He glanced past me — at Bo, at Nia, and last, longest, at Rennick, whose face had gone carefully, professionally blank. His jaw tightened. "Who is she?"

"Commander Ilse Rennick," Rennick said, before I could answer. Her voice was level. Too level. "Naval intelligence. Mr. Sterling, I've read your service record. The whole service record. I'm—"

"I know what you are." Tomas's voice didn't rise. It went quieter, which was worse. "You're the reason the chest went in the water. You're the reason the expedition was declared lost with all hands." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a sound with nothing in it. "'Lost with all hands.' I've had three years to think about that phrase. Do you know what it means, Commander? It means someone signed a document saying we never existed, so that no one would ever come looking, so that no one would ever find out what we—" He stopped. His breath was going ragged. Maret appeared at his elbow with a steadying hand, unhurried, practiced, and I understood that this happened often. That Maret had caught this man through storms of this exact kind before.

"We should sit," Maret said mildly. "All of you. Before the sun turns."

* * *

They fed us first. I will always be grateful for that — that they made us eat before they made us know. Taro and smoked fish and something bitter and green that cut through the heat, eaten in the long low house with its woven walls breathing with the wind, and the whole time Tomas sat across from me and watched me eat with an expression I couldn't name until I realized it was the expression of a man memorizing me. Filing me somewhere safe. As if he still half-believed I might be taken away and he wanted evidence.

Rennick ate nothing. She sat with her hands around a cup of tea she never drank and watched Tomas with an intensity she probably believed was invisible.

When the bowls were cleared, Tomas put both hands flat on the floorboards, the way a diver checks the bottom before letting go of the ladder, and began.

"The expedition wasn't salvage," he said. "It wasn't survey. It wasn't anything they told us. Vaskaria was finished as a country, but it wasn't finished as an idea — there were men who'd spent decades pulling that country apart piece by piece and selling it, and when the country itself collapsed, they kept the networks. The buyers. The routes. The expedition was a liquidation. We were there to recover assets — artifacts, documents, gold from the old reserves, things that had been buried or hidden when the government fell. And labor." He paused. His hands pressed harder against the boards. "Local labor. Contracted through intermediaries. Paid in promises."

"How many men?" Bo asked quietly.

"Sixty-one porters and boatmen over the course of the operation. Plus eleven of us from the naval detachment." Tomas looked at the men in the corner of the house. "Twenty-three of us survived the work itself. The rest—" He stopped. Started again, in a flatter voice, the voice of a man reading a cargo manifest. "When the recovery was complete, the decision was made that the laborers had seen the loading sites. The routes. The caches. Some of the items were sensitive enough that knowledge of them was considered a security risk. So the risk was managed."

Nobody spoke. Outside, a bird called once and fell silent, as if it too were listening.

"They marched them into the interior in groups," Tomas said. "Told them they were being taken to a new camp. Better pay. And then—" His voice failed entirely, once, twice. Maret's grandfather, who had sat silent through all of this, spoke softly in his own language, and Tomas nodded and drew a breath and continued, and I understood that the old man had just given him permission, or strength, or both. "I found out on the fourth group. I was supposed to be part of the escort. I refused. I told the detachment commander I'd report it the moment we reached radio range. He agreed with me. That's what I thought, anyway. Two nights later our camp was hit — not by locals, by our own side, dressed as locals. Eleven of us in the detachment. Nine died that night. The other one, Okafor, lasted six weeks in the bush before they found him." He touched the burn scars on his arm, absently, the way you touch an old injury while talking about a new one. "I went into the river with a machete wound and a bullet graze and the current took me twelve kilometers downstream. A fishing crew from this village pulled me out. They've kept me ever since. Kept all of us they could reach. Moved us three times when patrols came through. Fed us when there wasn't enough. Hid us like—" His voice broke and reformed. "Like family."

The old man spoke again, briefly. Maret translated, his voice careful: "My grandfather says: you do not return a man to the current after you have pulled him out. Whatever else is true."

"And the list," I said. My own voice sounded foreign to me. "You said you saw the list."

"The manifest of the recovered assets. I memorized what I could before they burned the originals — sites, contents, the names of the buyers' representatives. And the second list." He looked at me then, directly, and I watched him decide to stop protecting me, because that was always Tomas's flaw and his grace: he could not lie to family, even to spare them. "The list of names they submitted for the lost-with-all-hands declaration. Our names. Except it wasn't a casualty list, Cy. It was a *witness* list. Everyone on it was either dead already or meant to be. Twenty-two names. Mine was last." He smiled, and there was nothing in it. "Someone made an error. Or the river made it for them. Either way, when your navy wrote to your mother, they were reading from a document written by the men who did the killing."

The floor seemed to tilt. I put my hand against the boards. Across the house, Rennick had gone very still, and when I caught her eye there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the professional blankness, not the calculation. Something rawer. She looked, for one unguarded instant, like a woman watching her own funeral procession go by.

"That's why the chest matters," Nia said. She was hugging her knees, young and pale and absolutely steady. "The logs. The photographs. It's proof. It's the proof that the list was a lie."

"It's proof," Tomas agreed. "Which is why it can never reach a government office. Any government office. The rot goes up too high — I don't know how high, but high enough that the cover-up has already survived three years, two changes of administration, and one very thorough search of this basin." He leaned forward. "Whoever sent your Commander Rennick isn't trying to find survivors, Cyrus. Survivors were confirmed dead on paper before the ink dried. They're trying to confirm the paper worked."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside a child laughed and was hushed.

"So what do we do?" Bo asked. Practical Bo. Anchor Bo. "We can't go downriver. Can't go to the authorities. Can't sit here waiting for the next patrol."

"There is a journalist," Rennick said quietly.

Every head turned. She had spoken almost against her will, I thought — the words dragged out of her like splinters.

"Elias Vance," she went on, staring at the fire. "He filed the first story eight years ago, before the dissolution. Artifact trafficking through the old Vaskarian reserve network. Nobody ran it big; it got buried. But he kept his notes. He kept sources. If anyone alive knows where the bodies of this story are buried — literally — it's him." She paused, and her throat moved. "My brother was on that expedition too," she said, to the fire, to no one. "Petty Officer Daniel Rennick. I signed the condolence letters myself. I read the same fiction you did." She looked up at Tomas, and her eyes were wet and furious and utterly controlled. "So understand me, Mr. Sterling. I am not here for my career anymore. My career ended the day I read the true orders. I am here because somebody owes twenty-two families a debt, and I intend to collect it."

Tomas studied her for a long time. Then he looked at me, and asked the question with his eyes before he asked it with his mouth:

"Do you trust her?"

I thought about the wreck. About the chest coming up out of the green dark. About Rennick's boot on my deck and her offer that was a gun and her eyes when she'd said *my brother*, just now, like a confession pried loose from a locked drawer.

"Not yet," I said. "But I believe her."

"That'll do," Tomas said. "For now."

It was Maret who ended it — Maret, who had been listening at the doorway, who straightened suddenly and went out, and came back in less than a minute moving fast, all his earlier calm gone.

"Smoke," he said. "North bank. Two signals, close together." He looked at his grandfather, then at us. "Not villagers. Villagers signal three times."

Bo was already reaching for the rifle. Rennick was already on her feet.

"How long," I asked, "to the river?"

Tomas pushed himself upright using the wall, and I saw him wince and master it, saw three years of hiding harden into something like purpose.

"If they're good," he said, "we're already late."
