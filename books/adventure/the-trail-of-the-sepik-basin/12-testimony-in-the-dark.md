## 12. Testimony in the Dark

The fire was small, and that was deliberate. Tomas Sterling had learned in three years that a small fire could be explained — a fisherman's cook fire, a villager's warmth — while a large one was a signal, an accusation, a beacon for men with rifles. So they built it low in the hollow beneath the stilt house, behind a screen of woven palm, and the flames threw their light upward in a narrow cone, and everyone who mattered sat inside that cone as if the light were a roof.

He sat with his back against a post, because sitting against a post meant he could see the whole circle and the dark beyond it, and seeing the dark beyond it was the only way he could speak at all.

His sister was directly across from him.

Three years. He kept trying to make the number mean something. Three years was a thousand days, was thirty-six moons, was the time it took for a boy to become a man in the village upriver, was the age of Nia's whole remembered life. But the number refused to hold still. Some moments it was a lifetime, and he was a stranger wearing a dead man's face, and Cyrus was looking at a ghost. Other moments it was nothing at all — a blink, a held breath — and he could see the girl who used to race him down the pier still living inside the woman's eyes, and that was worse, because it meant all the time had passed over her like weather while he had been underground, literally underground, in a root cellar, learning to be small.

"You don't have to," Cyrus said. "Not tonight. Not all of it."

"I do, though." His voice came out rusted. He hadn't spoken this much English in months; the village tongue had colonized his mouth, and his own language now felt like a coat that no longer fit. "That's the whole point. I have to say all of it, or none of it counts."

He looked at the fire. He made himself look at the fire, because he had learned that too — that the story lived in the fire, and if you watched the fire you could tell it, and if you looked at the people while you told it you would stop, the way he had stopped the first four times, in the first weeks, when the village elders had asked him gently what had happened and his throat had closed like a drowned man's fist.

"The expedition wasn't lost," he said. "That's the first thing. Everyone says the Vaskarian survey was lost with all hands, a tragedy, a river that ate them. The river didn't eat them. The river is the only honest thing in this story."

* * *

He told it the way he remembered it, which was not in order. Memory, he had learned, was not a logbook. It was a wreck field. You salvaged what you could, piece by piece, and you never knew what the next dive would bring up.

He told them about the beginning, because the beginning was the part that still felt safe, the part he could hold without bleeding. The Vaskarian Cultural Recovery Expedition, nineteen strong, chartered under a flag that hadn't existed in forty years — a nation dissolved, its seal still stamped on crates because the seal meant *no one is watching*. That was the genius of it. A dead country's paperwork raised no flags in any living port. The chest they'd pulled from the delta, the chest that had started all of this — it had been sent ahead, downriver, a dead drop for a buyer who never came, because the buyer was arrested in another hemisphere and the crate had sat in the mud for three years like a letter never delivered.

"What was in it?" Salim asked quietly. "The rest of it."

"Gold work. Funerary pieces from the interior sites. And ledgers." Tomas's hands had begun to move without his permission, sketching shapes in the firelight. "Ledgers of everything they took. Every artifact, weighed and priced. Every laborer, listed by village, by name, by what they were *worth*." He made himself say the next part plainly. "They weren't hiring the men from the river villages. They were taking them. Men who came to work a season and never came home, and the villages were told the river got them. The river got them. The river is the only honest thing in this story."

He heard Nia's breath catch. He didn't look at her. He couldn't look at anyone yet.

* * *

The middle of the story was the part that lived in his body now, the part that came back at night with its own weather.

He had been the expedition's survey diver — that was how a navy man with a conscience and a talent for rivers ended up on a dead nation's payroll. The pay was obscene and the questions were few, and he had told himself the same lie every mercenary tells: *I'll see what's really going on, and then I'll decide.* He told them this and watched Cyrus's face and saw that she recognized the lie, because she had told it to herself her whole life. *I'll take one more job. I'll save a little more. Then I'll stop.*

The artifacts were only half the cargo. He had found out in the sixth week, diving a flooded mortuary chamber two days' walk from the river, when he surfaced early and came through the camp at dusk and saw the second ledger open on the expedition leader's table — Dr. Aurel Voss, a name Tomas said the way you spit out a fishbone. Not artifacts. Men. Listed with the same columns: origin, condition, price. Shipped downriver in the sealed holds of the *Vaskaria Star*, a ship that officially sank with all hands, a ship whose hold had been converted in a dry dock in a port that officially had no records.

"They were selling people," Tomas said. "Not labor. People. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole horror of it. Laborers get wages, even bad ones. These men were cargo. Voss had a buyer network — collectors, mostly, in countries that like to think of themselves as civilized. A man from the middle Sepik villages, strong, young, could bring more than a case of goldwork. And the villages along the route had been paid to look away, or frightened into looking away, and the ones who couldn't be bought or frightened—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a bend upriver where the current runs slow and deep. The expedition called it the Larder. I didn't know why until the ninth week."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark a night bird called, three notes, falling.

"I went to Voss. I was stupid. I was still a navy officer then, still believed in the chain of command, still believed that if you put the evidence in front of the right man, the machine would work. I told him I was reporting it. He didn't argue. He didn't threaten me. He thanked me." Tomas laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, a dry bark that scared him a little. "He thanked me for my service. And that night the expedition moved camp, and my dive gear was 'damaged,' and my radio was 'lost,' and when I tried to walk out along the survey line, two of Voss's men walked me back. And on the eleventh week, at the Larder bend, they put me in the water."

He held out his hands, palms up, into the firelight. The scars there were old and silver now, rope burns and worse.

"Weighted. Not heavily. Voss was careful — a body that surfaces is a body that gets found. They wanted me to go down and stay down, and they wanted it to look like a diving accident to anyone who ever asked, which was going to be no one, because no one was ever going to ask. The navy had already written me off. I know that now. There's a letter in that chest, or there was — a condolence letter, drafted before I was even in the water. They'd decided I was dead three days before they killed me. That's how efficient it all was. The paperwork moved faster than the knife."

He watched Cyrus. She had gone very still, the stillness of a woman holding something heavy underwater, and he knew — he knew with a diver's certainty — that she was thinking about the chest, about the photographs, about the coordinates, about how long the truth had been sitting in the mud while she measured her life in miles around it.

"The rope caught," he said simply. "A root, a snag, whatever the river wanted. I got the weights off. I came up two miles downstream, in the reeds, with a punctured lung and a broken wrist, and a man from the village found me. Old Yawa. He carried me — a hundred and forty pounds of drowned officer — eleven kilometers through the swamp on his back. And the village hid me. For three years. They hid me knowing what it would cost them if anyone found out, because two of their own sons were in Voss's ledger, and hiding me was the only revenge they could afford."

* * *

"And the institutions." Cyrus's voice, when it came, was flat in a way he recognized — she was keeping herself level the way you keep a load level on a deck, because if it tips, everything goes. "You said every institution. Say it plainly."

Tomas nodded. This was the part he had rehearsed in the dark of the root cellar for three years, the part that was less a memory than a ledger of his own.

"Vaskaria's gone, but its officers weren't. Voss's network had buyers on three continents and protectors in two navies. The operation was flagged in naval intelligence — I've seen the file, or part of it, in the chest materials, and I've pieced together the rest. They knew. Not everything, maybe, but enough. And instead of stopping it, they sat on it, because the expedition's buyers included people whose governments didn't want their names in a file. So the navy declared the expedition lost, declared me lost, and buried the file. The chest in your hold isn't evidence of a crime that was hidden. It's evidence of a crime that was *authorized to stay hidden*."

He let that sit. The firelight moved on all their faces — Bo's jaw working, Salim's eyes closed as if in prayer, Nia's knuckles white around her knees.

"And the men in the ledger," Tomas went on, quieter. "Some died in transit. Some were sold. And in the last weeks — I learned this from the villagers, who learned it from a laborer who escaped — when Voss understood the expedition was being wound down, when the buyers went quiet and the money stopped, he didn't release the men he couldn't sell. There are graves up there. I've seen them. I helped dig two of them, before—" He stopped. His hands had begun to shake, and he let them. "Before I understood what I was digging."

He looked up, finally, at the circle of faces.

"Here is what surviving means. It means I'm the only living witness who can put Voss in a room with the ledger. The villagers can testify to what was taken from them. The escaped laborer, if he lives, can testify to the graves. But the connection — the chain from the dead nation's seal to the living navies that buried it — that chain runs through me. I'm not a man anymore. I'm a link. And everyone in this circle is now standing on the same link."

* * *

Silence, then. The good kind and the bad kind mixed together, the way they always were.

It was Salim who spoke first, the old cook, in the voice he used for saying hard things gently. "The officer woman. Rennick. She said the expedition was declared lost. She said confirm no survivors." He opened his eyes. "You understand what her orders are, boy. You're not a witness to her. You're a line item."

"I know," Tomas said.

"And you told us anyway."

"I told you because you're the only ones who were going to hear it." He leaned forward, into the cone of firelight, and for a moment the three years fell away and he was just a man talking to people, which was all testimony had ever been. "A court might never see this. A government will try to bury it — that's what governments do with things like this, they don't burn them, they *file* them, and a filed truth is a drowned truth. But you're here. You're real. And a truth spoken aloud to real people, in firelight, with names attached—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—that truth exists now. It can't be un-said. Whatever happens tomorrow, whatever they send upriver after us, this has been said. I have testified."

Cyrus moved. She crossed the circle and sat down beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they used to sit on the pier, and she didn't say anything, and that was the right thing, because there was nothing to say that the shoulder didn't say better.

It was Bo who broke the silence, the old engineer, gruff as ballast. "Voss. Is he alive?"

The question dropped into the fire like a stone into deep water.

Tomas was quiet for a long time. He looked at the flames, and beyond the flames at the dark, and in the dark he saw the survey camp, and the second ledger, and the Larder bend, and the rope in his hands.

"I used to hope not," he said. "But the villagers traded at the upriver stations all through the wet season. And in the dry season, a trader came through wearing a coat that no trader earns." He turned to look at Cyrus, and his voice, when it came, was steady and cold as deep water.

"He's alive, Cyrus. He's upriver. And I think he knows we're coming."
