## 13. Rennick's Choice

The order came at 0400, which told Ilse Rennick everything she needed to know about it.

Orders that could bear daylight arrived with the morning traffic, typed on the boat's own teletype, copied into the log in her own hand. Orders that came at four in the morning came by encrypted burst transmission, addressed to her alone, and began — as this one did — with a classification string so long it wrapped twice around the page like a snake.

*In event of contact with expedition survivors or material witnesses, no recovery is authorized.*

She read it three times by the blue light of the code lamp, sitting on the edge of her bunk with her boots off, because some animal part of her had refused to put them on. As if boots meant readiness, and readiness meant consent.

*No recovery is authorized.* The phrase had a grammar to it, a bureaucratic elegance. It did not say *kill them*. It said *no recovery*, and left the verb hanging in the air between the words, the way a noose leaves a body.

Rennick set the flimsy down on her knee and looked at the bulkhead for a long time. The river slapped against the hull below her, patient as a clock. Somewhere aft, one of the sailors was snoring through his watch-off, and somewhere upriver — twelve kilometers, maybe fourteen, past the second bend where the mist sat all day like something drowned and risen — Cyrus Sterling was sitting at a fire with a man who was supposed to be dead.

*Confirm no survivors.* That had been the original instruction, back in Port Moresby, when the word *survivors* had still been hypothetical, an abstraction, a line item. She had signed the acknowledgment without reading it closely. That was the trick of it, she understood now. You were never asked to do the unforgivable thing all at once. You were asked to initial a page. Then another. The unforgivable thing assembled itself around you out of paperwork, the way coral builds a reef, grain by grain, until one day you were standing on it and it was an island and there was no swimming back.

She had told herself, in Port Moresby, that she would draw lines when the lines needed drawing. That she was the kind of officer who kept her hands clean enough to act when acting mattered. Every careerist tells herself this story. It is the bedtime prayer of the ambitious: *I am only climbing so that I can, someday, from the top, do good.*

The lamp's blue glow made the bulkhead look like water. Her brother had loved the water. That was the thought that came, unbidden, the way it always came at this hour, between the last watch of the night and the first light — the hour when the mind, unguarded, went walking through the rooms it kept locked in daylight.

Mikkel. Lieutenant Mikkel Rennick, hydrographic survey, seconded — *seconded*, that lovely innocent word — to a joint scientific expedition, Vaskarian-flagged, nineteen years old in his dress whites in the photograph their mother kept on the piano until the day she died. He had written her letters from the basin. She still had two of them, in a box she had not opened in eleven years, because opening it required deciding what she would do about what was in it, and she had spent eleven years declining to decide.

*Ilse — you wouldn't believe this river. It doesn't flow so much as breathe. The locals say it has moods. I say it has opinions. We're going up past the Larder bend next week, further than any survey has gone. There are men on this expedition who don't carry instruments, Ils. Nobody will tell me what they're for. Probably nothing. Everything is probably nothing until it isn't.*

Until it wasn't. The expedition had been declared lost with all hands — a storm, the official finding said, a squall on the middle basin, regrettable, the sea keeps its own counsel. Twenty-two names read into the record. A memorial wall in a naval chapel that smelled of wax and brass polish. Their mother had touched the engraved M every Sunday for nine years until her fingers wore the groove deeper than the engraver had.

And then, three years ago, a chest had surfaced in the Sepik delta with a dead nation's seal on it, and a disgraced intelligence officer with a disciplinary file as thick as a Bible had been handed a boat and a mission and told, in essence: *go and make sure the sea keeps its counsel.*

They had chosen her precisely because she was compromised. That was the elegance of it. A woman with nothing left to lose does not ask questions; a woman with nothing left to lose is grateful for the rope they throw her, even when she can see the knot in the end of it.

Rennick stood, put on her boots after all — the animal part of her had lost the argument — and went up to the wheelhouse.

* * *

Dawn on the middle basin came the way it always did here: not arriving but seeping, the mist thinning by degrees until the river appeared beneath it like a developing photograph. Grey water, grey sky, the far bank a charcoal suggestion of itself. A heron stood on a snag with the absolute patience of something that had never once doubted the world.

Ensign Prewitt had the watch. He straightened when she came in, and she saw him glance at the code lamp's log, and she saw him decide not to have seen it. Good boy. That instinct would keep him alive, or it would keep him obedient, and at his age those were still the same thing.

"Anything on the surface picture?" she asked.

"Nothing, ma'am. Fishing boats below the second bend, that's all. Local craft." He hesitated. "There was a canoe at first light, though. Well upstream of where canoes usually are. Heading downriver fast."

"Fast how?"

"Fast like someone's being paid," Prewitt said, and then looked embarrassed at his own joke, and then, because he was not stupid, stopped looking embarrassed entirely. "Or running."

Rennick took the glasses and swept the upper reach. Mist, water, the green wall of the bank. Nothing. But the river here was a liar; it held its secrets under its skin the way the chest had held its papers under lead. Somewhere up there was a fire with four people around it — five, if you counted the guide — and one of them was a ghost the navy had buried with full honors eleven years ago, and one of them was a diver who had pulled a nation out of the mud, and both of them were now, according to a burst transmission sent at four in the morning, *not to be recovered.*

"Ma'am?" Prewitt said. "Do we have orders to make contact?"

The question hung there. She could feel him waiting behind it, wanting to be told what shape the world was today.

"We have orders to maintain station," she said. "Log it as such."

"Aye, ma'am."

She stayed at the window long after he'd turned back to his instruments. Downriver, behind her, the contractors' boat lay at anchor off a stilt village, flying no flag, answering no hails, its crew visible on deck in the dawn light doing nothing at all with the studied idleness of men being paid to wait. They had been shadowing the expedition since the second night. They reported to no one she could name, and everyone she could name. And the burst transmission had been copied to them — she had checked, at 0415, with hands that were almost steady — which meant that whatever *no recovery* meant, it meant it in their language too.

They would go upriver tonight. Or tomorrow. They would move when the mist was thick and the village dogs were quiet, and they would do the thing that the transmission had declined to name, and afterward there would be a report, and the report would say *contact with hostile elements, regrettable loss of life,* and the reef would grow by another grain.

Unless someone moved first.

Rennick became aware that she was gripping the rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go, finger by finger, the way you surrender anything.

* * *

She spent the day performing command. It was a skill like any other; she had a talent for it, God help her. She inspected the magazine. She corrected the coxswain's course-keeping with a remark sharp enough to remind the crew she was iron. She ate lunch in the wardroom and discussed fuel margins with the engineer and laughed, at the correct moment, at the steward's joke about the tinned sausages. Every hour of it was a kind of drowning performed in reverse — staying above the surface by moving constantly, by giving the water nothing to hold.

But underneath, in the flooded lower compartments of herself, the decision was already making itself, the way a wreck makes its way to the bottom: slowly, then all at once, with a sound like tearing.

It was not conscience. She wanted to be honest with herself about that, at least; honesty was the one currency she had left. Conscience was a luxury of people whose brothers came home. What she had was older and less admirable and far more reliable. It was the arithmetic of the grave.

Because here was the thing she had understood at 0400, staring at the bulkhead while the river breathed against the hull: Mikkel was on that list. Twenty-two names, *lost with all hands,* and the official finding said storm, and the official finding was signed by the same directorate that had sent her upriver to bury the evidence of what the storm actually was. Which meant that either her brother had died in the massacre Tomas Sterling had survived — or he had been one of the men holding the rope.

She had turned that over all day, turning it like a stone, looking for the side that hurt less. There wasn't one. If he had died trying to stop it, then the navy had erased him twice — once in the water, once in the record — and the only person alive who might know which way it went was a half-feral witness hiding in the interior, currently scheduled for erasure number three. And if he hadn't died trying to stop it —

If he hadn't, then the truth was the only grave marker he would ever get, and she would dig it herself.

Either way, the truth was the only thing left of him that anyone could stand on. Not the chapel wall with its worn engraving. Not the folded flag in their mother's box. The truth — ugly, unsanctioned, career-ending — was the whole of her inheritance, and there were men in this river basin right now whose job was to foreclose on it before she could collect.

That was not conscience. That was grief with a work order.

* * *

Evening came down purple and hot. In the galley alleyway, where the ventilation hum covered voices, Rennick found what she needed: the signalman's spare key to the stores locker, and forty seconds of privacy. From the locker she took a waterproof message pad, the oilskin kind used for bridge-to-boat notes, and a pencil stub, and she wrote in block capitals, small and even, the hand of a woman signing forms:

*STERLING. CONTRACTORS MOVING TONIGHT. MIST THICKENS AT MOONSET. THEY WILL COME TO THE CAMP FIRST, THEN SWEEP THE RIVER. DO NOT BE AT THE FIRE. GO NOW, DOWNSTREAM, PAST THE FISHING SETTLEMENT, AND TURN OFF BEFORE THE CONFLUENCE. TAKE EVERY PAGE. TRUST NO ONE IN UNIFORM. — A FRIEND OF MIKKEL'S*

She read it over twice. *A friend of Mikkel's* — it would mean nothing to Sterling except that it meant everything; a stranger who knew that name was either a trap or the only ally in the world, and Sterling struck her, from the file, from the interviews, from the flat unflinching way the woman had looked at her across the table in Chapter Four of this disaster, as someone who had already decided the navy was a trap and therefore had nothing to lose by reading the note twice.

Getting it to her was the harder problem. Rennick considered and rejected four methods in the space of an hour: a runner (Prewitt would ask questions), a radio burst (intercepted, traced, fatal), going herself (witnessed, fatal, and worse — it would confirm to the contractors that the navy's officer was a loose thread). What remained was the oldest method on any river: pay a local to carry a thing and ask nothing.

The fishing settlement downstream had a dozen stilt houses and one man who traded petrol for fish and asked no questions because questions cost money. Rennick went ashore at dusk with two sailors in plain sight — cover, legitimacy, the performance of routine — dismissed them to buy fruit from the women at the landing, and walked alone to the end of the walkway where the trader's dugout was tied.

He was old, with a face like driftwood and eyes that had priced everything on this river for fifty years. She put the oilskin packet in his palm along with paper money folded small.

"The camp upriver," she said. "Past the second bend. There's a woman there, dark hair, a scar on her forearm. This goes to her tonight. Tonight — not tomorrow."

He weighed the packet in his hand, and her in his eyes, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once, without a smile, the way men nod at funerals.

"Moonset," he said, in trade English, meaning: *I know when the mist comes. I know what moves at moonset. I have known longer than your uniform has existed.* He pushed off into the darkening water, and the river took him the way it took everything, without comment.

* * *

She stood on the walkway a moment longer than she should have. Behind her, her sailors were laughing with the fruit sellers; ahead of her, the mist was already gathering over the channel like a congregation. Somewhere upriver, a fire was burning down to embers around which sat a brother she had mourned for eleven years and a sister-in-grief she had been sent to destroy.

*Trust no one in uniform.* She had written it and meant it, and the meaning of it included her, and there was a strange cleanliness in that — the first clean sentence she had composed in years.

Her hands were shaking now, finally, now that no one could see. She pressed them flat against the rail and watched the trader's dugout diminish to a fleck, to a suggestion, to nothing, and she understood that she had just done the one thing that could not be initialed away. The reef had cracked. Whatever happened next — the trial, the cell, the disgrace, the bullet in the dark at moonset — she was, for the first time since Port Moresby, an actor and not an instrument.

From upriver, faint across the water, came a sound she had been listening for all day without admitting it: engines. Two of them. Unmuffled, heavy, coming down the channel with the mist, moving fast like someone's being paid.

Rennick turned and walked back toward her ship, unhurried, iron-faced, already composing the log entry that would buy the divers their head start — and praying to a god she'd stopped believing in at a funeral wall that the old man's paddle was faster than the mist.
