## 14. Betrayal at the Confluence

Nia had learned to read the river the way other girls learned to read faces. The Sepik spoke in moods — a brown sulk after rain, a green whisper when it ran shallow over gravel, a flat glassy patience that meant nothing good. And on the morning they reached the confluence, where the dark water of the Karawari slid into the wide brown shoulder of the main river, the water was doing something she had never seen it do.

It was holding its breath.

The mist lay on the surface like a sheet drawn up to the chin. No birds worked the reeds. Even the crocodiles, which usually sunned themselves on the mud banks like old men arguing, were gone. Nia stood at the bow with her notebook open and her pencil still, and felt the hair rise along her arms.

"Something's wrong," she said.

Behind her, Salim laughed softly, the way he did when he was trying not to worry anyone. "The river is always wrong, little one. That is why we love it."

But Cyrus came forward anyway, barefoot, her eyes already moving across the treeline the way they moved across a wreck site — methodical, hungry for the detail out of place. Bo killed the engine and let them drift, and in the sudden quiet Nia could hear everything: water worrying the hull, the creak of rope, her own pulse.

"The confluence makes an eddy here," Cyrus said quietly. "Current comes off the main channel and curls back. Anything floating downstream from the side river gets caught and held." She turned. "Including boats."

Nia understood then what the flatness meant. It wasn't that the river was empty. It was that everything alive had made itself small.

"We should go back," she said.

"We can't," Cyrus said. "Not without passing them." She looked at Bo. "How much fuel?"

"Enough. Barely."

"Then we take the side channel. Wide turns, no wake if we can manage it. Nia—" and Cyrus's eyes found hers, and there was something in them Nia hadn't seen before, something that made her feel suddenly older and terribly young at once, "—you've been watching the shadow boat for three days. You know its habits better than any of us. Where would you be, if you were them?"

Nia looked at the mist. At the place where the two rivers braided together and lost themselves in each other. At the long green throat of the side channel, vanishing into wall-to-wall jungle.

"At the point," she said. "Where the eddy ends. Because every boat coming down either channel has to pass through it, slow, whether they want to or not. It's the only door."

Cyrus nodded slowly. "And if you wanted to wait for someone?"

"I'd cut my engine a mile upstream and drift. Let the current do the work. Then I'd sit in the eddy, inside the mist, and let them come to me."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere far off, a hornbill called twice and went silent, as if embarrassed.

"So we don't use the door," Bo said finally. He spat over the rail. "We go through the shallows on the far side. Scrapes the keel, but this old girl's taken worse."

"It's not the keel I'm worried about," Cyrus said. "It's being seen crossing open water. We'll be visible for ten minutes. Maybe twelve."

"Then we make them count," Bo said, and went aft to the engine.

* * *

They crossed at midday, when the light was flattest and the mist had burned off to nothing, because Cyrus said hunters watch dawn and dusk and grow lazy at noon. Nia steered while Bo nursed the throttle, the hull groaning over the sandbar shallows, brown water boiling around them so thick with silt it looked like liquid earth. Her hands were steady on the wheel. Her heart was not.

She counted the seconds aloud under her breath, a habit she'd developed from Salim, who said fear was easier to carry when you gave it numbers. Two hundred seconds of open water. Three hundred. At four hundred and ten the keel kissed bottom with a sound like a fingernail dragged down a blackboard, and then they were through, sliding into the green shade of the side channel, and Nia let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the delta.

Salim pressed a cup of tea into her hands. "You steered like your grandmother," he said.

"You never met my grandmother."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But I am certain she was exactly like that."

For an hour the channel narrowed and the world closed in — trees leaning overhead until their branches wove into a roof, orchids hanging like pale lanterns, the water going tea-dark and mirror-still beneath them. Nia began, despite everything, to believe they might have slipped the net. That was the thing about hope, she was learning. It didn't announce itself. It just crept aboard like a stowaway and sat down in your chest and made itself comfortable right up until the moment it didn't.

Tomas sat near the stern with the oilcloth bundle of documents on his lap, refusing to let it out of his reach even to sleep. He had barely spoken since the camp with the graves. But as they drifted through the green cathedral of the channel, he said, without looking up: "They won't quit. Men who do what they did don't stop. Stopping means admitting."

"Admitting what?" Nia asked.

"That it was all for nothing," Tomas said. "That the river kept everything they came to steal."

* * *

The confluence caught them at dusk after all, though not the way anyone planned.

The side channel bent south, then west, and opened without warning onto the main river — and there was no avoiding the door, because the sandbar they'd scraped over at noon had shifted with the day's current, and Bo said the boat drew too much water now to risk it twice. So they came down the last stretch with the light dying behind the trees, gold going to copper going to ash, and Nia at the bow again with her notebook forgotten in her pocket, watching the place where the two rivers met.

The eddy was there, exactly as she'd described it, a slow revolving lens of water maybe eighty meters across, holding leaves and driftwood in a lazy orbit. The mist was rising off it in ropes.

"There," she whispered.

At first she saw nothing. Then the mist moved wrong — a shape inside it, darker than the dusk, long and low and engineless, drifting with the terrible patience of something that had been waiting all day.

"Boat," she said. "In the eddy. Port side, forty meters."

She heard Bo reach the engine. Heard Cyrus say, low and fast: "Don't start it. Not yet. If we're quiet they may—"

The searchlight hit them like a fist.

White light swallowed the world. Nia threw up her arm and heard voices through a loudhailer, distorted, barking words she couldn't parse, and then a single shot cracked across the water and struck the rail a hand's width from her head, splintering wood into her cheek.

"Down!" Cyrus's arm was around her, hauling her behind the wheel housing. "Everyone down!"

Bo started the engine anyway, because there was nothing left to lose, and the boat leapt forward into the dark toward the far bank, toward the black seam of jungle, anywhere but the light. Shots followed them — three, five, seven — punching holes in the night, and Nia, curled on the deck with her arms over her head, understood with a strange floating clarity that these were not warning shots anymore.

Then the engine coughed.

Once. Twice. A grinding, drowning sound — and she knew before Bo swore what had happened, knew it the way she knew weather now: they'd taken a round low on the hull, or sucked in debris, or both, and the engine was dying, and the current was carrying them sideways into the eddy whether they willed it or not. The door was closing with them inside it.

"They'll board us," Cyrus shouted over the noise. "Bo, get us to the bank — any bank —"

"The bank's got teeth," Bo snarled, wrestling the wheel, "but it's better than those bastards—"

The second boat came out of the mist ahead of them, cutting the eddy's circle, and in its bow light Nia saw them clearly for the first time: four men, maybe five, in civilian clothes gone grey with river grime, rifles slung, faces wrapped against insects. Not navy. Nothing about them was navy. They were the kind of men whose job left no paperwork.

A voice carried across the water, calm and unhurried, and somehow that was the worst part — the calm. "Sterling. Send the chest across and we let your people swim home."

Cyrus rose halfway, keeping herself behind the wheel housing, and Nia saw her look at Tomas, at the oilcloth bundle, at the water between the two boats narrowing meter by meter as the dead engine surrendered them to the current.

"Nia." Cyrus's voice dropped to almost nothing. "The documents. Can you swim?"

Nia could. She'd grown up in water; it was the first language she'd ever spoken. But she looked at the brown flood moving at walking pace, thick with silt and logs and things she preferred not to name, and her mouth went dry.

"Yes," she said.

"Not yet." Cyrus's eyes were working, calculating, and Nia watched her spend the seconds like coins. "Wait for the turn. When we swing close to the bank — the near bank — you go. Take the bundle. Swim for the roots, get into the mangroves, stay under the surface until your lungs scream. They'll be watching the boat, not the water."

"And the rest of you?"

Cyrus didn't answer, which was an answer.

The boats converged. Twenty meters. Fifteen. On the contractor boat, a man raised a rifle to his shoulder, unhurried, sighting along the barrel at whoever presented the best target, and time went syrup-slow for Nia — she could see the man's finger, see the rain of insects around the bow light, see Salim standing up.

Standing up, calmly, deliberately, at the rail, in full view, holding his spice tin in one hand and his other hand raised high, palm out, like a man greeting a neighbor across the water.

"Good evening!" Salim called, in his warmest voice, the voice he used for frightened passengers and stubborn engines alike. "Gentlemen! Before anything unfortunate — perhaps we discuss this over tea? I have cardamom. Real cardamom, from home—"

The rifleman swung toward him.

It happened the way Nia later realized such things always happen: too fast to stop and too slow to forget. The shot took Salim high in the chest and spun him half around, and the spice tin flew from his hand, burst against the rail, and scattered cardamom pods across the deck like small dark tears. Cyrus screamed his name — Nia heard nothing, actually, her ears filled with white roaring — but she saw Cyrus move, saw Bo abandon the wheel to catch the old man as he folded, and in that suspended instant, in the gap the universe tore open for exactly as long as it took an old cook to fall, Nia went over the side.

The river took her like a hand closing.

Cold. Brown blindness. The current seized her immediately and began deciding things. She had the oilcloth bundle strapped across her chest the way Cyrus had lashed it to her an hour ago — *if anything happens, it goes on Nia, Nia swims strongest* — and she kicked for what she prayed was the bank, lungs burning, eyes useless in the murk. Something enormous brushed past her leg and she forced herself not to think about it, forced herself to think instead of Salim's voice saying *fear is easier to carry when you give it numbers*, and so she counted her kicks. One. Two. Three. At sixty her fingers found root. At seventy she broke the surface inside a cage of mangrove trunks, gasping, mud to her chin, and looked back at the river.

The contractor boat had come alongside theirs. Figures were leaping the gap. She heard shouting, a scuffle, Bo's roar like a wounded animal — and then, cutting through everything, impossibly, the loudhailer voice again, but different now, sharper, female, furious:

*"Break off! Break off, that's a direct order — Rennick, naval intelligence, this vessel is under navy authority—"*

Rennick. Nia's mind snagged on the name even as her body shook. The commander who'd trapped them into this voyage, arriving out of the dark like a storm front, buying them — what? Seconds? Minutes?

She didn't wait to find out. She hugged the document bundle tight, sank to her eyes in the tannin-black water among the roots, and watched.

Watched the two boats locked together in the eddy. Watched muzzle flashes stitch the mist. Watched Cyrus haul Bo and Tomas over the gunwale into the water on the far side, away from the contractors, away from her — the three of them vanishing into the current toward some plan Nia couldn't see and had to trust. Watched the contractor boat tear free and swing its searchlight across the banks, hunting, while somewhere in the mangroves a voice — Bo's voice, hoarse and broken — called a name into the dark:

*"Salim! SALIM!"*

And Nia, fourteen years old and no longer anything like a child, pressed the oilcloth bundle against her heart and answered the only way she could, a thin thread of sound across the black water:

*"Here! I'm here! I have it — I have the documents — I'm HERE!"*

The searchlight swung toward her voice and stopped, pinned her in white, and Nia did not look away. Behind the light, silhouetted at the wheel of the contractor boat, a man raised a rifle.

From the water beside the contractor boat, drenched and rising like something the river itself had sent, Cyrus Sterling's voice came out level and cold and absolutely certain:

"Touch her, and the world sees every page of what's in that bundle. Your bosses included. Kill her and you kill the only copy — and then you get to explain to the men who hired you why the story dies with a child."

The rifle did not fire.

The light held on Nia for three heartbeats, five, ten — long enough for her to understand that she was, at this moment, worth more alive than dead, and that this was both her salvation and her cage. Then the light snapped off, and the contractor boat backed water, turned, and slid away upriver into the mist, leaving the confluence to the current and the drifting cardamom pods and the silence where Salim used to be.

Nia swam for the bank. Cyrus reached her first, hauling her up by the arm, and for a moment neither of them spoke; they just held each other upright in the shallows, shaking, soaked, alive. Bo and Tomas stumbled down the mud to meet them, and Bo's face was grey and wet and ruined.

"He pushed me down," Bo said, in a voice Nia had never heard from him, small and cracked. "The old fool. He saw the barrel swing and he stood up on purpose. He stood up so the rest of us wouldn't."

Nia looked out at the eddy, turning slowly in the dark, holding its leaves and its driftwood in their patient orbit. Somewhere downstream, she knew, the river was carrying things it had taken tonight — blood, brass, a tin's worth of cardamom, a man's last warm breath given to the water. She thought about how she had spent months mapping the river's moods, believing that to know a thing was to be safe from it.

She knew now that some doors you map your whole life and walk through them anyway.

Cyrus touched her cheek where the splinter had cut it, gentle as bandaging. "You saved it," she said. "All of it. He'd want you to know that — the first thing, the only thing: you saved it."

Nia nodded, because her throat wouldn't work. Then, over Cyrus's shoulder, she saw Bo straighten suddenly in the dark, staring back the way they'd come, toward the boat they'd abandoned in the eddy — the boat still anchored there, riding empty in the searchlight's afterimage, untouched.

"Wait," Bo said. "The engine. They never boarded our boat — Rennick drove them off before—" His voice changed, sharpened through the grief like a blade through fog. "Nia. The hull. What did Salim do to the hull?"

Nia blinked through the rain beginning to fall. "What?"

Bo was already wading toward the derelict boat, laughing and sobbing at once, a wild broken sound. "That crafty old devil. That beautiful, stubborn old devil. Come on — all of you — Salim's not done protecting us yet."

The rain thickened. And out on the black water, their boat waited at the confluence like a sealed letter, holding whatever Salim had hidden inside it.
