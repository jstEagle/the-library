## 15. Salim's Tins

Bo had not slept. He'd told the others he would take first watch, and he had, and then he'd told himself he would lie down after, and he hadn't. He sat in the engine room instead, in the narrow space between the main housing and the port bulkhead where a man could wedge himself like a tool in a rack, and he listened to the river go by in the dark.

The *Marrow* was old enough that her bones had opinions. Every plank spoke at its own pitch. The hull timbers ticked as they cooled, the way they always did when the night came off the water and the temperature dropped, and Bo knew each voice by heart — the groan near the sternpost that meant nothing, the hairline complaint from the chine that meant nothing worse. He had spent eleven years learning this boat the way other men learned wives or psalms. She was the last thing Salim had touched with his whole attention, and now she was also the last thing of him that remained above water.

That was what Bo could not get past. Not the shot itself — he'd heard it, one flat crack out of the dark at the confluence, and then another, and then Nia screaming, and then nothing worth hearing for a long time. It was not even the manner of it, though that was bad enough: an old man standing in the open on the aft deck, holding a lantern high so the rest of them could see to run, making himself the brightest thing on the river because bright things draw fire away from dim ones. Bo kept replaying it. The lantern had not gone out when Salim fell. It had burned on the planks beside him, guttering in the spray, lighting the water where the documents were scattering like startled birds, and Bo understood now — he understood it in his chest, in the place where understanding is just pain wearing a better coat — that Salim had known exactly what he was doing. An old navigator who spent his life reading currents had read this current too. He had stood where he stood, held what he held, and fallen where he fell, all of it arranged so that Cyrus and Tomas and the girl could reach the canoes on the shadow side while every muzzle on that dark water pointed at the light.

He had made himself a decoy. He had spent himself like ballast thrown overboard to keep a boat from capsizing, and he had done it without hesitation, without ceremony, without even — Bo was almost sure of this, and it was the part that undid him — without telling anyone. Because if he had told anyone, someone would have argued. Someone would have said there had to be another way. And there wasn't another way, and Salim had known that too.

So Bo sat in the engine room with his back against the bulkhead and his hands around a cold mug of coffee nobody had asked for, and he grieved the way he did everything: slowly, structurally, with no talent for it whatsoever. Grief, in his experience, was a load-bearing problem. You didn't get over it. You figured out where the weight went.

* * *

The trouble with engines is that they don't let you think about anything else, which is exactly why Bo thought about everything else down here. His hands needed work the way other men's hearts needed talking. There was plenty to do. The *Marrow* had taken a round through the wheelhouse coaming during the ambush — a gouge in the timber the size of a man's thumb, splintered deep but through nothing vital — and two more holes low in the starboard topsides where the contractors' boat had walked its fire across them as they ran. Cyrus had patched the worst of it with canvas and tallow before they'd fled upriver, good enough to float, not good enough to trust. Bo trusted nothing anymore except his own hands and the sound of wood under a blade.

So he worked. He pried out the splintered section of the coaming, planed a fresh piece of ironwood to fit, drove it home with glue and pegs, and sanded it until you couldn't find the seam unless you knew where grief had put it. Then he went below and started on the hull patches, which meant pulling back the bilge boards along the starboard side, which meant lying half inside the frame of the boat with a work lamp hooked on a knee, which meant — and this was the thing, this was the thing he had been circling all night without knowing it — seeing parts of the *Marrow* that only got seen in crisis.

The bilge was dry. That surprised him; he checked twice. After the running fight, with the boat loaded heavy and pushed hard against the current, he'd expected water in the strakes, seepage somewhere, the ordinary dishonesty of an old hull doing honest damage. But the patches had held, and the limbers were clear, and the pump had barely cycled. The *Marrow* had carried them out of that killing ground the way she'd carried them through everything: complaining, faithful, unbroken.

And then, forward of the midships frame, tucked up under the stringer where the hull curved in toward the keel, Bo saw something that did not belong.

A tin. A spice tin — one of Salim's, the square ones with the hinged lids and the faded labels, turmeric and cardamom and the clove tin whose label had worn off years ago so that everyone just called it "the mystery." There were six of them in the galley rack, and Bo had counted them a hundred times without counting, the way you know the number of steps to your own door. Six tins in the rack. This was a seventh.

It was wedged into a gap between frame and planking that Salim must have measured with a fingernail, packed around with waxed cloth so it wouldn't rattle, positioned precisely where a hand could reach it if the hand knew it was there and no one else ever would. The lid was sealed with tape. On the tape, in Salim's small, upright, patient handwriting — the handwriting of a man who had written tide tables by lamp oil and never once smudged a page — was a single line:

**FOR BO. WHEN IT IS TIME.**

Bo did not move for a long moment. He lay there in the bilge of his own boat with a dead man's message six inches from his face, and the work lamp threw his shadow huge and crooked across the frames, and the river slid past outside the hull, indifferent, enormous, going about its business the way rivers do, which is the business of forgetting.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice weather. He put the lamp down, put the tin down — carefully, gently, the way you handle ordnance, because that is what it was, a dead man's last instruction packed in metal — and he climbed out of the bilge and sat on the deck plates and looked at the thing for a while before he touched it again.

Then he peeled the tape. Slowly. Along the seam, the way Salim would have wanted it opened, the seal intact enough to be resealed if it had to be.

Inside: oilcloth, folded twice. Inside the oilcloth: documents. Pages copied in Salim's hand — not photostats, not traceries, but true copies, laborious, letter-perfect, the expedition logs transcribed word for word including the marginalia, including the columns of figures, including the pages where the original writer's hand had gone ragged with fear and Salim had reproduced even the raggedness, noting in tiny script at the bottom of one leaf: *original shows tremor here — copy faithful.* Photographs, wrapped separately in tissue. And beneath everything, folded into a rectangle the size of a playing card, a single sheet in Salim's own words, dated three days before the confluence.

Bo read it four times. He will read it a hundred more. It said:

> If you are finding this, then I am not, and the plan has gone the shape I feared. Do not blame yourself for the shape. I have been copying these papers since the night Rennick came aboard, because a man who has navigated rivers knows that the map you trust is the one you drew yourself. One set went into my spice tins in the galley — you will have found those scattered, or lost, or wet. This set does not travel. This set waits.
>
> I hid it here because the hull is the one place no searcher looks twice. Everyone searches cabins, lockers, the strongbox, the fuel tank. Nobody searches the bones. I have watched men tear a boat apart looking for contraband and leave her skeleton untouched, because they do not love her, and only love finds the bones.
>
> Give these to Cyrus when the time is right, and the time is right when she believes she has lost everything. She will want to burn them. Tell her not to. Tell her the fire is not hers to light yet. These papers are not evidence for governments — we both know what governments do with evidence. These papers are a promise, and promises are carried, not consumed.
>
> Bo, you were the first to call me family and the last to say it aloud. I am sorry for the lantern. I am sorry for choosing the way I chose without asking. But you taught me that a boat is kept afloat by what you put in her lowest places, and I have put my faith in her lowest place, and I believe she will hold it until you need it.
>
> The river keeps what the river keeps. We keep what we choose to keep. Choose well, old friend. Feed the girl. Mind the engine. She runs rich on the idle — turn her down a quarter turn and she'll forgive you.
>
> — S.

The last line was the worst one. Bo read it and laughed — actually laughed, alone, in the dark, a broken sound that was mostly sob — because of course. Of course the old man's final note contained engine advice. Of course he had tuned the idle before he died. Bo had been fighting a rough idle for two days, cursing the carburetor, blaming the fuel, the humidity, the altitude of his own temper, and the whole time the answer had been sitting in a dead man's handwriting in the belly of the boat, waiting. A quarter turn. He went to the carburetor that same hour and turned it down a quarter turn, and the engine settled like a horse being told the day's work is done, and Bo stood there listening to it run smooth and clean and felt Salim in the machinery, felt the old man's hands still on the boat, still adjusting, still keeping her, and he cried then, properly, the way he hadn't managed at the burial — the little one they'd held at dawn off a gravel bar, a prayer in three languages and a wreath of palm that the current took south — because this was different. At the burial he had mourned a man. In the bilge he had found him still working.

* * *

He did not tell anyone right away. That felt wrong to explain even to himself, but he knew the reason, and the reason was timing. Salim's letter had been specific: give these to Cyrus when she believes she has lost everything. Bo turned that sentence over for hours, walking the deck in the grey of the morning, testing it against what he knew of her.

Cyrus had not lost everything. Not yet. She had Tomas back — alive, damaged, speaking in fragments, flinching at raised voices, but alive, and Bo had watched the reunion crack her open in a way that was joy and horror in equal measure, a wound and a gift at once. She had Nia, who had come through the ambush with a graze along her forearm and a silence in her that Bo recognized from his own mirror, the silence of someone who has seen blood come out of someone they love and has not yet decided what that makes them. She had the duplicate documents — some of them, the ones Nia had snatched from the current, dried now and curling, ink feathered at the edges but legible. She had a mission. She had fury. A woman with all that has not lost everything. A woman with all that is still measuring what she has left.

But Bo had watched her these two days since the confluence, poling the canoes up the side channels, hauling the *Marrow* through the shallows where the contractors' wake couldn't follow, and he had seen the arithmetic running behind her eyes. He knew that look. It was the look of a woman totaling accounts. What they had was not enough — not enough firepower, not enough speed, not enough proof, not enough protection for a witness who could barely speak and a girl who should be in school and a crew already down one soul. The contractors were behind them. Rennick's command, whatever Rennick herself had become, was behind them. The river ahead was unknown water, and the papers they carried were partial, water-damaged, incomplete — a case with holes in it, a testimony with gaps where Salim's careful copies used to be.

She believed she was going to lose. Bo could see it. She hadn't said it — Cyrus Sterling did not say things like that aloud, she said *we push on* and *we paddle* and *dawn in three hours* — but Bo had built engines beside her for eleven years and he knew the sound of a woman doing sums she expected to fail. And when she finally admitted it, when the arithmetic finished and the answer came up short, that would be the moment. That would be the moment she believed she had lost everything, or nearly everything, or everything except the people — and Salim's letter said that was when she'd want to burn the papers, and that was when she must not.

So Bo waited. He resealing the tin — new tape, pressed tight, and this time he wrote on it himself, in his own blunt carpenter's letters, because the message deserved updating: **STILL HERE. HOLDING.** He put it back in the gap between frame and stringer, packed the waxed cloth around it exactly as he'd found it, and buttoned the bilge boards down over it, and then he sat on top of the boards and drank his cold coffee and felt something shift in his chest that he did not have a name for and did not need one.

Because here was the thing he understood now, lying in the bones of the boat with a dead man's faith in his hands: Salim had not hidden documents. Salim had hidden *continuity*. The old man had looked at the shape of their situation — the trap, the cover-up, the contractors, the odds — and he had planned past his own death with the calm of a navigator plotting a course through water he would never sail. He had assumed the betrayal. He had assumed the loss. He had assumed that the first copies might scatter on the current, and he had built a second archive into the hull of the boat itself, into the one structure that would survive anything short of scuttling, and he had addressed it to the one man aboard who loved the boat's bones as much as he did. The dead man was still protecting the living. The dead man was still navigating. Somewhere downstream of his own death, Salim had plotted their position, estimated their drift, and left a fix — *when she believes she has lost everything* — and Bo was now the keeper of that fix, responsible for calling it at the right moment, the way a navigator calls the turn at the right mark.

It was the hardest watch Bo had ever stood. Harder than any storm. Because the mark had no buoy, and the moment had no clock, and the only instrument was his own judgment about the interior weather of a woman who showed him nothing she didn't choose to show.

* * *

On the third evening they made camp in a blind channel off the main stem, screened by fallen bamboo, engines cold, no fire — Cyrus's orders, and good ones, because smoke travels and the contractors had boats faster than theirs and no patience. They ate cold rice and the last of the smoked fish, and Nia cleaned her graze without being told, and Tomas sat apart with his back against a tree, present and absent at once, and Cyrus took first watch because she always took first watch now, as if sleep were a debt she refused to owe.

Bo took second watch. During the changeover, in the blue hour when the channel went from black to pewter, he stood beside her at the bow and said nothing for a long time, because saying things was not his gift, and then he said the smallest true thing he could find.

"He tuned your idle," Bo said. "Before he went. Whole boat's running sweeter than she has in years."

Cyrus looked at him sideways. "You've been in the bilge."

"I've been in the bilge."

"And?"

"And she holds," Bo said. "Everything holds. Better than we deserve."

He watched her take that in. Watched her not ask what else was in the bilge, because she was smart, smarter than all of them, and she had heard the weight in his voice and knew there was more, and she chose — deliberately, visibly, the way she chose everything — not to press. Not yet. She filed it. He watched her file it, behind her eyes, next to the arithmetic. And Bo understood that he had just made a deposit in the only bank that mattered between the two of them, the bank of *she knows I know*, and that when the moment came — when the arithmetic failed, when the loss finished totaling — she would come to him, or he would go to her, and the tin would come out of the bones, and Salim's fix would be called.

Maybe soon. Maybe not. The river narrowed ahead; the mist came down thick off the water at dusk now, and the channels braided and forgot themselves, and the pursuit behind them had gone quiet, which Bo did not trust for a minute, because quiet on a river is never absence, it is only distance. Somewhere back there the contractors were regrouping, or bribing, or buying information from someone who'd seen a canoe, and somewhere beyond them Rennick was making whatever choice she was making in whatever cabin she had, and none of it was in Bo's hands, and none of it had ever been in Bo's hands, and that was fine. His hands had one job. Keep the boat alive. Keep the tin hidden. Call the mark when the mark comes.

He went below before full dark and lay in his bunk listening to the engine tick as it cooled, the familiar voices, the groan at the sternpost that meant nothing, and he added a new sound to his catalogue: the faint, dense silence from forward of the midships frame, where a seventh spice tin sat in the dark between the bones of the boat, holding a dead man's patience, a dead man's handwriting, a dead man's quarter-turn of faith.

Sleep came for him finally, late, and it brought the dream it always brought now: the aft deck at the confluence, the lantern burning on the planks, and Salim standing upright in the light, unhurried, unafraid, checking his watch — checking his *watch*, in the middle of it, as if the whole thing were a tide table and he was simply confirming the hour — and then turning, in the dream, and looking straight at Bo across the water, and saying, clear as a bell over the noise of gunfire and river:

"Not yet, old friend. Not yet."

Bo woke before dawn with the dream still on him, and went and checked the bilge, and the tin was there, and the seal was tight, and the boat was dry, and the mist on the channel was lifting, and far off downstream — too far to see, close enough to feel — something was coming up the river toward them, patient and certain, and Bo stood at the rail in the grey light and listened to the water and waited for the mark to arrive.
