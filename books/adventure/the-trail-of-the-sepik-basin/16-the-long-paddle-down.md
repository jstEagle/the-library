## 16. The Long Paddle Down

We left the village before the mist had finished deciding whether to be fog or cloud, and we left it in a way I had never left anything in my life: quietly, without counting.

That was the strange part. Not the fear — fear I knew like my own hands. Not the grief for Salim, which sat in my chest like ballast that would never be pumped out. It was the absence of arithmetic. All my adult life I had measured everything — fathoms, miles, air remaining, days since Tomas went into the water and did not come back up. Numbers were how I kept the world from drowning me. And now here I was in a leaking canoe with my dead-alive brother's breath on the back of my neck, fugitive from two kinds of men with guns, and I had stopped counting entirely.

Tomas paddled in the bow. He had insisted, and Bo had let him, because there are some gifts you give a man who has been carried for three years — the gift of pulling weight, of being useful, of his arms remembering what they were for. His shoulders were wrong under his shirt, too sharp, the musculature of survival rather than work, but he caught the river's rhythm fast, the way you catch a language you were born speaking. Every few strokes he'd glance back at me, quick, as if checking I was still real. I did the same to him. We must have looked like two people afraid of waking up.

Nia sat amidships with the documents wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, her paddle working in the efficient, unschooled stroke she'd taught herself off Salim over four years of watching. She hadn't spoken since the confluence. None of us had said much. There is a silence that comes after blood that isn't shock exactly — it's more like the water after a rock goes in. The ripples move outward through everything, and you just have to wait until they reach the edges of what you are.

Bo took the stern. He was the heaviest and steadiest, and steering was the thing he could do that didn't require him to talk, because Bo talking right now would have been either tears or fury and the river didn't need to hear either. His hands on the paddle were engineer's hands, scarred and certain, correcting our line against the current with small unconscious movements, the way he corrected anything that drifted.

Behind us, maybe six kilometers, maybe less, the contractors' boat. Ahead of us, somewhere downriver, Rennick's command — because whatever Rennick had become in that last hour aboard, whatever war had opened behind her eyes when she read her own orders, her radio still worked both ways, and her superiors' voice still came out of it, and a woman can choose sides and still be shot by the side she chose if the choosing looks like mutiny. We were between them like a seed between millstones.

And we had nothing. That was the other part of the peace, the shameful peace. No claim papers, no boat — the *Maren Lee* was scuttled or captured or burned, depending on which version of the last twelve hours you believed, and I had chosen not to believe any of them yet. No government. No law. No plan beyond the next bend. When you have nothing left to lose, you discover that losing was the thing that had been weighing on you all along, and without it you float strangely high, like a diver at the end of a long decompression, rising through the thermoclines one by one toward light that hurts.

* * *

The Sepik in flood season is not a river so much as a slow brown sea wearing a river's name. It braids itself around islands that are only sandbars grown ambitious, splits into channels that die in swamps, reunites, forgets where it was going. The villages along its banks sit on stilts ten feet above the waterline, and the crocodiles lie beneath them like dogs under a porch, and the children watch you pass with the flat, ancient patience of people whose grandparents watched strangers pass too.

We paddled through the morning in shifts. Tomas and Nia, then me and Nia, then Bo alone while the rest of us bailed with a cut-down jerrican. My brother's hands blistered inside an hour and he didn't complain, and watching him not complain was worse than hearing it. At the second rest stop, drifting in the shade of a fallen rain tree, I took his wrists without asking and turned them over and looked at the blisters and the older scars beneath them — rope scars, burn scars, one long pale seam along his forearm that no crocodile had made — and he let me look, and neither of us said anything, because what was there? *I'm sorry I wasn't there*? *I'm sorry the world did this to you while I was measuring miles*?

"Your stroke's gone lazy," he said instead. "Starboard side. You're pulling past your hip."

It was such a Tomas thing to say that I laughed, and the laugh came out broken, and he reached across the gunwale and gripped my shoulder once, hard, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

Nia watched us with an expression I couldn't read — not jealousy, nothing so simple. Recognition, maybe. She had lost Salim three days ago and was carrying him the way I carried Tomas: as a weight that had become part of the body. Once, when she thought I wasn't looking, she unwrapped the corner of the oilcloth packet and touched the documents inside the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and then wrapped them again and put her paddle back in the water.

* * *

Midday, the channel narrowed and the canopy closed overhead and the light came down green as aquarium glass. That was where we heard the engine.

Not close. A kilometer back, maybe more, idling low — someone moving the way hunters move, throttling up in bursts and then cutting to drift, listening. Bo raised a fist and we pulled into a side slough so narrow the canoe's gunwales brushed fern on both sides, and we lay there among the buttress roots with the mosquitoes stitching our necks and waited.

The sound grew. Passed. Hung somewhere downstream of us, uncertain, and then faded toward the main channel.

"They'll do that all afternoon," Bo whispered. "Leapfrogging. They know this stretch better than we do."

"We know it better than they think," I said. Because I did. Three years of salvage charts, of diving wrecks in these waters, of learning every snag and sandbar the way you learn the face of someone you love. The contractors had sonar and engines and numbers. I had something else. I had spent half a decade learning what this river hid, and a man who knows where the bodies are buried also knows where the boats run aground.

"There," I said, pointing to a place where the current wrinkled oddly over a submerged log, invisible unless you'd seen it from below, from underneath, from the diver's side of the world. "Ferry channel runs wide of that. Anyone running a chart will hug the far bank to avoid the bar at Kilometer Forty-One." I smiled, and felt my own smile like something borrowed. "Their chart is three years old. The bar has moved."

Bo looked at me for a long moment. "You're going to navigate us by memory of shipwrecks."

"I'm going to navigate us by memory of everything this river has ever taken," I said. "It's the most detailed map in existence."

Even Nia almost smiled.

* * *

We passed a village near dusk — stilt houses, smoke rising straight in the windless air, a dugout crossing ahead of us with two fishermen who saw us and turned, unhurried, back toward their own bank. Word travels faster than motors on this river. By tomorrow every village between here and the delta would know that five strangers in a canoe were running from boats with armed men, and each village would decide for itself what that meant. Some would sell us. Some would hide us. Some would simply watch, which on the Sepik is its own kind of judgment.

Tomas pulled us toward the bank as the light failed. We made camp on a mud spit under a stand of sago palms, no fire, sharing cold rice and tinned fish from the provisions Salim had packed — and there it was again, Salim, in every tin, in the careful way the fuel was rationed, in the note in his handwriting on the inside of the first-aid kit lid that read *Blistex in the yellow pouch, stop losing it, Bo.* Grief doesn't arrive all at once. It arrives in installments, and each one finds you in a different place.

Tomas ate sitting slightly apart, facing the water, the way he'd probably eaten for three years — never with his back to open ground. I moved beside him, deliberately, and put my back to the river, and after a moment he shifted too, angling himself so that between the two of us we covered the whole dark. Old habit offered up like a gift.

"The villagers," he said quietly. "The ones who kept me. They lost people. In the camps, on the labor lines. They kept me alive because I saw it, and because—" his voice caught, steadied— "because Amara decided I was worth feeding. Her husband was one of the ones who didn't come back from the second dig site." He turned the tin plate in his hands. "When you take the testimony to the families — hers first. Whatever order you've planned, hers first."

"It's not my order to set anymore," I said. "That's the whole point. We carry it. They decide."

He looked at me then, really looked, and in the last grey light I saw my brother's face under the beard and the hollows and the three missing years, and I understood that he was trying to find *me* under my face the same way. "You've changed," he said.

"You haven't," I said. "That's the miracle."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "I've changed too. I just changed in the dark, where nobody could see it." He was quiet a while. "Cyrus. If they catch us—"

"They won't."

"If they catch us, the documents go in the water. Swear it. Better drowned than delivered to whichever uniform comes first. Promise me you'll be the one to let go."

I thought about the chest going down into the delta deep, weeks ago now, or years ago, time had gone strange — the lead-lined box sinking out of my hands into the green dark, the nation-that-was-no-nation returning to the country that keeps everything. I had already learned this lesson once. The river keeps what it keeps.

"I promise," I said.

* * *

Night on the Sepik is a sound. Frogs in orchestras, nightjars, the wet breathing of the swamp, and beneath it all the river itself moving in its sleep. We took watches in pairs. I sat the middle hours with Nia, both of us listening to the downstream dark.

"Do you think Salim knew?" she said at last. Her voice was very small in the big dark. "About the second set. In the hull. Do you think he planned it all — the tins, the hull — or did he just... love us enough to prepare for everything?"

"He planned for everything," I said. "That's what love looked like on him. He salted the rice extra because Bo likes it salty. He copied documents he hoped we'd never need. Same thing, different scale."

She nodded. Then: "Cyrus. When this is over. If there's an over. What happens to us?"

I considered lying to her, the way adults lie to the young, and decided she'd earned better. "I don't know. The boat's gone. The claim's burned. Everything we owned fit in a canoe, and half of that is evidence." I looked at her silhouette, sixteen years old and holding a family together with her paddle. "But families aren't boats, Nia. Boats sink. Families regroup."

She was quiet a long time. "Salim used to say the river always gives back what matters," she said finally. "He said it about salvage. But I think he meant himself, too. I think he meant us."

Downstream, faint as a heartbeat, an engine coughed and went silent. Listening. Moving. Patient and certain.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old brass, and with it came the sound we had been dreading and expecting: not one engine but two, separated, sweeping — the contractors behind us and, ahead, around the great bend at Kilometer Fifty-Two, a second motor running steady and official, the navy's flat diesel drone. A pincer closing along fifty kilometers of brown water.

Bo looked at me across the canoe. Tomas looked at me. Nia's hand found the oilcloth packet.

I looked at the water — at the wrinkled current over the sunken bar, at the drowned forest standing hip-deep off the eastern bank, at the narrow black mouth of the old flood channel that appeared on no chart because it hadn't existed three years ago, because the river had cut it new in the last wet season, through country only I had dived since.

"Everyone paddle," I said, and for the first time in three years, I wasn't measuring anything at all.
