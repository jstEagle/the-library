## 17. Dead Reckoning

The satellite phone weighed more than it should have. Ilse Rennick had carried a great many things in her career that weighed more than they should have — classified folders, other people's secrets, the particular gravity of orders she'd signed without reading twice — but the phone in her cabin aboard the *Vigilant* was the heaviest yet, because she knew who would be on the other end of it when it rang, and she knew what he would ask of her.

It rang at 0400 local time. The river outside her porthole was black and breathing, mist rising off the Sepik like the exhalation of something vast and patient that had been waiting for her all her life.

"Commander." Director Halloway's voice came through flattened by distance and encryption, stripped of everything human except intent. "Status."

"The survey party is intact. We're holding position near the confluence while the contractors regroup."

"That's not what I asked."

She closed her eyes. In the dark behind them she saw the manifest of her own life: twenty-two years of service, commendations filed in triplicate, a promotion board's worth of quiet competence. And beneath all of it, like ballast, the thing she had never spoken aloud — Marcus. Her brother. Flight lieutenant, seconded to a joint reconnaissance program, listed as lost during a training exercise over the Coral Sea in the same season the Vaskarian expedition went dark upriver. She had been twenty-six. She had received the folded flag and the letter with its careful, committee-approved language, and she had believed it, because believing it was easier than becoming the kind of officer who asked questions at her brother's funeral.

"I know why you're really here, Director," she said. "I've known since Port Moresby."

A pause long enough to hear the hum of a room somewhere in Canberra, air-conditioned, windowless, very far from any river.

"Then you'll understand the importance of precision," Halloway said. "Recovery of the chest contents. Confirmation regarding any surviving expedition personnel. And full containment of documentation pertaining to labor force casualties. Those are your objectives. They have not changed."

*Labor force casualties.* She turned the phrase over the way she'd once turned over intelligence assessments, looking for the seam, the tell, the small dishonesty that revealed the larger one. There it was. Not deaths. Casualties. As if the men had been equipment damaged in transit. As if the Vaskarians had mislaid them the way one mislays a crate of instruments.

"And if there are survivors?" she asked. "If one of those 'casualties' is standing in front of me?"

"Then you will report an identification failure and proceed with containment." His voice did not rise or fall. It was the voice of a man reading a shopping list. "Rennick. You were selected for this tasking because you don't flinch. Don't make me reconsider."

The call ended. She sat for a long time with the phone in her lap, listening to the river.

Her cabin was four feet wide and smelled of diesel and mildew and, faintly, of the cardamom Salim had cooked with before he died — the smell had gotten into the bulkheads, into the fabric of the chair, and she could not open a porthole without it finding her. On the desk lay the folder she'd been ordered to destroy: intercepts, payment ledgers, a photograph of a mass grave taken from a drone three years ago and then buried so deep in classification that even she had needed two clearances to see it. Eleven bodies. Local laborers who had helped the Vaskarian expedition move artifacts out of the interior and who had, at the end, known too much about where those artifacts came from.

And a twelfth grave, unmarked, dug shallow and abandoned. The one Tomas Sterling had crawled out of.

She thought about Cyrus Sterling's face when Rennick had first shown her the photographs — the way grief and hope had fought each other openly, no discipline at all, and how obscene and how magnificent that lack of discipline had seemed. Rennick had spent her whole career learning to keep her face still. It had never once kept her heart still. She had simply gotten better at pretending the two were unrelated.

Marcus had a laugh like a door coming off its hinges. He'd called her Ice every summer of their childhood because she could float in the coldest water without shivering. *You're not cold, Ilse,* he'd told her once, aged eleven, dripping at the edge of Lake Burley Griffin. *You're just waiting.*

She hadn't understood him then. She was beginning to understand him now, thirty years too late and eight hundred miles up a river that wanted her dead.

* * *

At dawn she went topside. The contractors' boat sat anchored fifty meters off the starboard beam — the *Kestrel*, six men, ex-military, armed under a charter agreement that existed on paper only. Their leader, a thick-necked man named Voss, met her at the rail with coffee he didn't offer to share.

"We're resuming at 0600," he said. "Your people stay aboard. This last leg is ours."

"Your tasking is recovery of materials. Mine is the survey. We go together or we don't go."

Voss smiled the way a crocodile smiles, which is to say not at all, only with the arrangement of teeth. "Director Halloway spoke to us directly this morning, Commander. You've been — what's the term — decoupled. You're logistics now. Boats and fuel. The rest is above your clearance."

Above her clearance. The oldest sentence in the language of complicity, and she had spoken versions of it herself a hundred times, to a hundred subordinates, watching their faces close like doors. Now she stood on the receiving end of it and felt how it worked: not as information but as erosion. Each use wore away a little more of the person hearing it until nothing remained but function.

"Understood," she said.

She turned back toward her own boat and let her face do what it had been trained to do, which was nothing. But underneath the stillness, something had finished settling, the way sediment settles in flooded water, leaving everything suddenly, terribly clear.

They were going to kill Tomas Sterling. They were going to kill Cyrus Sterling and whatever remained of her crew, and they were going to do it quietly, somewhere in the interior, and the report would read *expedition mishap*, and Ilse Rennick would sign it, and in fifteen years she would be a director herself, giving orders in a windowless room, and some younger officer would stand where she stood and feel the exact shape of this moment and swallow it, because she had swallowed it first, because everyone always swallowed it first, all the way down the line, generation after generation, the whole machinery running on swallowed moments.

No.

The word arrived without drama. That was the strange part. She had expected — if she had ever let herself expect this — some crisis of conscience, some storm. Instead there was only a door swinging shut on a room she'd lived in for thirty years, and the quiet after it.

*You're not cold, Ilse. You're just waiting.*

* * *

She made her preparations the way she'd been taught to make all her best work: slowly, invisibly, inside ordinary movements.

She copied the false coordinates first. The interior camp site was real; the logbook coordinates were real; but the secondary cache site — the one the contractors believed held the remaining artifacts — existed only in a document Rennick herself had drafted eighteen months ago as part of the cover-up's contingency planning. A fiction. A location chosen precisely because it was reachable, remote, and bordered on ground where the *Kestrel*'s draft would strand them. She wrote it onto a chart with hands that did not shake, because shaking was a luxury for people whose hearts were still undecided, and hers, she discovered, had decided sometime in the night without consulting her.

Then she took the satellite phone to the stern, waited until the deck watch turned, and dialed the number she had memorized three nights ago from a page in Salim's spice tins — because of course the old man had copied everything, and of course Rennick had found his duplicates before anyone else did, and of course she had said nothing, because even then, even before she knew what she would do, some part of her had been keeping options open the way a drowning sailor keeps air.

Cyrus answered on the fourth ring. Her voice was raw, sleepless, sharpened by grief and pursuit.

"It's Rennick. Don't hang up. Listen fast."

"You have thirty seconds before I decide you're bait."

"Voss has new orders. Not recovery — elimination. You, your brother, anyone who can testify. They're moving at 0600 on the coordinates I'm about to give you, except I've fed them a cache site that doesn't exist, northeast fork, past the narrows where the channel splits around the drowned island. They'll ground out on the shoal there if the river's doing what it does this time of year. It buys you hours, maybe a day. Maybe less."

Silence on the line. Then: "Why?"

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. Rennick looked downriver, where the mist was burning off the water in long gold rags.

"Because my brother flew for a program connected to yours," she said. "Marcus Rennick. 1998. They told my family it was a training accident. I signed the condolence letter's follow-up file myself, five years later, as part of a records review. I read what actually happened and I filed it back in the dark and I told myself service required it." She stopped. Her voice, when it resumed, was quieter and steadier than it had been in years. "I've been waiting a long time, Sterling. I didn't know what for. Turns out it was this."

"There's a journalist," Cyrus said. "Mara Quist. She broke the first story about the Vaskarian artifacts before anyone silenced it. Do you know where she is?"

"Port Moresby. She never left. She runs a print shop on Lawes Road, keeps her head down, keeps her files. Address is 14 Lawes Road, above the shop, blue door. Tell her the name Halloway. She'll understand." Rennick hesitated. "Tell her Rennick sent you. She'll want to know why she should trust anything from me. Tell her Marcus's sister finally read the file."

"How much time do we have?"

"Hours. And Sterling — when they realize the coordinates are false, they'll come back for me. I intend to be very slow about realizing anything."

"What are you going to do?"

What was she going to do. She looked at the folder on her desk, the drone photograph of the graves, the ledger with its neat columns of payments to men who dug holes for other men. She looked at the river.

"I'm going to surrender," she said. "Loudly. To the wrong people. There's a patrol post at Angoram — real navy, real record-keeping, officers who haven't been read in. I'm going to walk in this afternoon and request protection and file a statement. Everything I have. It won't hold them long — Halloway will have it sealed within a day — but sealing takes signatures, signatures take hours, and every hour someone in Canberra spends burying my statement is an hour you're on the water." She paused. "And when they come to collect me, I'll be the most documented prisoner in the basin. Hard to make a commander disappear quietly once she's on the books. Even for them. Especially for them."

"They'll court-martial you. What you're doing —"

"I know exactly what it is. I've spent thirty years knowing what things are and doing them anyway. Consider this the first exception." Something in her chest loosened as she said it, some knot tied so long ago she'd mistaken it for anatomy. "Sterling. Your brother. When you find him — and you will find him — tell him the navy sent an apology. It's thirty years late and it isn't enough and it's the only thing I have."

She hung up before Cyrus could answer. Some conversations should end on the speaker's terms, and she had earned, at least, the right to choose her own last word.

* * *

Angoram materialized out of the heat haze at midday — a straggle of tin roofs and trade stores along the riverbank, a government post flying a flag gone pink from sun, a church, a football field worn down to red earth. Ordinary. Stubbornly, beautifully ordinary. Rennick chose it for exactly that reason: places this ordinary kept records. Places this ordinary had clerks.

She dressed in full uniform. Medals. Cover. The whole architecture of rank, deployed one final time, not as armor but as evidence — because a woman in full dress uniform walking into a patrol post and asking, formally, on the record, for protection, created paper. Paper was the one weapon Halloway's people couldn't recover from a river bottom.

Voss watched her lower the skiff. "Where are you going, Commander?"

"Resupply," she said. "Fuel and stores. Back by 1800."

He studied her a moment too long. She met his eyes with thirty years of practiced stillness and gave him nothing, and he nodded, and turned away, and she understood that she had perhaps four hours before his suspicion matured into a radio call, and that everything now depended on the difference between those two numbers: four hours, and however long it took a clerk in Angoram to type.

The skiff crossed the brown water and grounded on the mud ramp and she stepped out into heat that hit her like a hand. Children paused their game to stare. A woman selling betel nut under an awning watched her walk past with the unhurried assessment of someone who had seen many uniforms arrive and knew, better than any intelligence briefing, what they usually meant.

The patrol post was a raised timber building with a duty officer named Sergeant Waim, young, serious, a notebook already open.

"Commander Ilse Rennick, Royal Australian Navy, attached to a joint survey operation currently on the river." She laid her identification on the desk, and beside it, the folder. "Sergeant, I am requesting formal protection and making a voluntary statement concerning multiple homicides committed during a foreign expedition in the Sepik Basin between 2019 and 2021. I am also requesting that copies of this statement be transmitted immediately to the national police command in Port Moresby and to the office of the public solicitor. Before you seal anything. Before anyone calls anyone. Type it first."

Waim looked at the folder, then at her, and she watched him perform the calculation every junior officer performs when a senior officer asks for something unprecedented: *is this a test?*

"It's not a test, Sergeant," she said. "It's the opposite of a test. For the first time in my career, nobody is grading me. Which means I'd like to say everything, please, while I still can."

He began to type. The keys sounded like rain on a tin roof, and Rennick sat in the hard chair across the desk and talked — the chest, the expedition, the laborers, the graves, the payments, the orders, the names, all of it, top to bottom, in the flat precise voice of a woman delivering a cargo she had carried too far. Outside, the light moved across the red earth. Somewhere downriver the *Kestrel* was discovering that the northeast fork did not contain a cache, only a shoal with their name on it, and the clock she had bought with a fiction was ticking.

It took ninety minutes. Waim printed three copies. She signed each one, and her signature had never felt less like surrender or more like arrival.

At 1712, the radio crackled. A voice asking for Commander Rennick, priority traffic, authorization codes she recognized from a lifetime ago. Waim looked up at her.

"That'll be for me," Rennick said. "Sergeant — whatever they tell you, remember one thing. Copies exist. Three of them. In three directions. That's the whole trick. That's all any of this ever was — making sure the truth had somewhere to be besides a filing cabinet in Canberra."

She picked up the handset. On the other end, a voice she knew well began speaking words she had spent thirty years waiting to stop obeying.

Downriver, in the last of the light, a canoe moved fast through the narrows past the drowned island — two paddles, a brother and a sister, and a satchel dry-bagged against the spray, carrying testimony toward a blue door on Lawes Road.
