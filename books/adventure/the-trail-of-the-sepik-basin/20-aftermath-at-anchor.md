## 20. Aftermath at Anchor

The story broke on a Tuesday, though we didn't know it was a Tuesday until Salim's old radio — the one Bo had sworn was dead and then spent three nights resurrecting with solder and stubbornness — crackled to life with a voice reading names.

Our names.

I was in the galley when it happened, scrubbing a pot that didn't need scrubbing. It was something Cyrus did too, I'd noticed — cleaning things that were already clean, keeping hands busy so the mind couldn't drift toward dark water. I'd learned it from her without meaning to. That's what living on a boat does; it passes habits down like heirlooms nobody asked for.

The voice on the radio was clipped and official, some news service out of Port Moresby or maybe further, and it said: *...testimony delivered by sources connected to the Sterling salvage operation has prompted investigations in three countries...*

Three countries. I set down the pot. My hands were shaking, and I watched them shake the way you watch weather come in over open water — from a slight distance, unable to do anything but observe.

Bo came through the doorway before I even called him. He always knew. He'd say it was the sound of my footsteps changing, but I think he just had a second sense for when the world tilted under any of us. He stood there wiping grease off his knuckles with a rag that was more grease than rag, head cocked at the radio like it was a snake he wasn't sure about.

"Turn it up," he said.

"I can't. It's already—"

"Then don't touch it." He lowered himself onto the bench across from me, slow, joints cracking like rigging in wind. "Let's hear what they've got to say about us."

They said a lot. The Vaskarian expedition. The massacre at the laborers' camp. The artifacts trafficked downriver in diplomatic pouches. A naval intelligence officer named Ilse Rennick, *in custody*, cooperating with investigators. And then — this was the part where Bo went very still, the way he goes still around anything fragile — they read the crew manifest of the salvage vessel *Kestrel's Wake*. Our boat. Our names, spoken into the airwaves like blessings or indictments, I couldn't tell which.

*Nia Okonkwo-Reyes, deckhand.*

My name. Out loud. In connection with words like *witness* and *whistleblower* and *survivor*. Words I hadn't chosen for myself but which were now stitched onto me whether they fit or not.

"They cleared us," Bo said quietly. "You hear that? 'No charges pending against the civilian crew.' Cleared."

"I heard."

"You're crying, girl."

"I'm not."

He passed me the greasy rag anyway, and I pressed it to my face, and it smelled like engine oil and diesel and the inside of our boat, our whole life, and I cried harder for that smell than I had for anything since Salim died. Because he would have loved this. Not the fame part — Salim never cared who was watching — but the *being believed* part. The part where the truth finally weighed more than the lie, where all those spice tins full of copied documents turned out to be worth more than any cargo we ever hauled.

"He planned for this," I said into the rag. "Before any of it happened. He knew."

"Course he knew," Bo said. "Old man planned his own funeral twice. Only thing he didn't plan for was dying before he could enjoy being right."

That got a wet laugh out of me, which I think was the point.

* * *

Cyrus heard the broadcast from up on deck. I know because she didn't come below for an hour afterward, and when she did, her eyes had that far-off river look, like she was seeing water that wasn't there.

She sat down across from me at the table without asking, which was its own kind of permission. Things between us had changed since the delta — since the scuttling, since the contractors' boat ground itself open on the wreck she knew better than her own heartbeat. She talked to me differently now. Less like a kid to be kept below decks in bad weather. More like someone who'd been in the same storm and come out the other side.

"Tomas is sleeping," she said. "First real sleep in days. The radio woke him and he just... listened. All of it. Then he said, 'So that's what my testimony sounds like in other people's mouths,' and went to his bunk."

"How is he?"

"Better than he was. Worse than he'll be." She turned her cup in her hands, not drinking. "There's no map for coming back from three years in the interior, Nia. We're making it up as we go. Some days he eats everything on the plate. Some days he can't be in a room with a closed door."

"And you?"

She looked at me then, really looked, and I understood she was deciding how honest to be. That was new too. Cyrus used to decide instantly — lock it down, keep it moving, grief worn as routine. Now she took her time.

"I keep waiting to feel finished," she said. "Like finding him should have closed something. But it didn't. It opened things. Every day there's another thing Tomas remembers, another name, another family waiting for word. Justice isn't a moment, turns out. It's a chore. Like bailing. You never actually finish."

"That sounds terrible."

"It sounds like being alive." Almost a smile. "Salim would've said it plainer. He'd have said, *the work is the prayer, girl.*"

We sat with that. Outside, the river moved the way it always moves — indifferent, enormous, carrying everything downstream eventually. Through the hull I could feel the current the way you feel a pulse under your hand when you're checking if someone's still with you.

"The trial starts next month," I said. "Rennick's trial. They're going to put her in a room in Port Moresby and ask her questions in front of cameras."

"Yes."

"Do you hate her?"

Cyrus considered this longer than I expected. "Her brother died on that expedition. Same as mine disappeared. She spent years helping bury the truth because she thought it was the only way to survive knowing. I understand that math, Nia. I've done versions of it myself." She paused. "But understanding isn't forgiveness, and forgiveness isn't mine to give anyway. That belongs to the families. To the village. We just carried the mail."

*Carried the mail.* I wrote that down later in the logbook margin, because it seemed like the truest sentence anyone had said all season.

* * *

The weeks after the broadcast blurred into a strange rhythm — reporters at anchorages wanting interviews, officials wanting statements, strangers sending letters to harbormasters addressed to *the Kestrel's Wake crew*. Bo answered none of them. Cyrus answered two, both from families of the laborers, both beginning *We received your package* and ending *Thank you is too small a word.*

Tomas answered none, but he read every one, sitting in the bow with the letters spread around him like cards dealt from a strange deck, and sometimes I'd find him holding one particular envelope — the one from the widow of a man named Anselm Kere, who had worked the expedition's supply line and whose bones, Tomas told us once, lay under a specific tree he could walk to blindfolded. The widow's letter said only: *Tell me how he lived, not how he died.*

So Tomas wrote back. It took him eleven days. When he finished, he read it aloud to Cyrus first, and whatever was in it made her hold his hand the whole time without saying a word.

Me, I kept working the deck. That was my answer to everything now — work. Lines coiled, hull scraped, charts corrected. Bo noticed, of course. Bo notices everything.

"You're running from something or toward something," he said one evening, watching me splice a line I'd already spliced twice. "Which is it?"

"Neither. I'm just... doing."

"Mm." He took the line from my hands gently, which from Bo was practically a ceremony. "Your father did that after your mother passed. Worked himself hollow. Took me years to learn the difference between grief that heals you and grief that just keeps you busy." He handed it back. "Slow down. The river's not going anywhere."

"The river's literally going somewhere constantly."

"Don't get clever with me, girl."

But I did slow down, a little. Enough to notice things. Enough to notice that Cyrus had started measuring distance differently — not in miles anymore but in tides, in meals, in Tomas's good days versus hard ones. Enough to notice that Bo had fixed the starboard engine properly, finally, and had painted Salim's name small and neat inside the engine housing where no one would ever see it but where the old man's hands had touched everything.

Enough to notice that I wasn't the youngest-who-didn't-know-anything anymore. Somewhere between the confluence and the delta, between Salim falling and the chest going into deep water, I had crossed some line on a chart that doesn't get printed. I was seventeen and I had seen men die and I had saved documents from a burning current and I had helped deliver justice outside any law, and there was no going back to being the girl who thought adventure meant stories.

* * *

It happened on a morning so ordinary it felt almost disrespectful. Mist on the water, birds working the shallows, Tomas teaching himself to fish again with a patience that surprised everyone including him. Cyrus below, sleeping in for once. Bo tinkering.

And the helm empty.

I'd stood at the wheel before — holding course while adults slept, that kind of thing. But standing at the wheel and *taking* the wheel are different animals, and I felt the difference in my palms before I even touched the spokes. This was the wheel that Cyrus gripped through storms. That Bo had lashed down in the ambush at the confluence. That Salim had steered by dead reckoning through fog on the run downriver, humming, calm as Sunday.

Nobody was going to hand it to me. I understood that suddenly, completely. On boats, like everywhere else, the important things are taken, not given. You step up and you hold on and you prove you won't let go.

I climbed into the helm chair. My hands found the wood — worn smooth in the exact places three sets of hands before mine had worn it — and I brought the engine up from idle, feeling the vibration climb through the deck plates into my spine like the boat clearing its throat.

"Where are we going?" Bo asked, not looking up from his work, which was his way of saying he saw everything.

"Nowhere special. Just out."

"Just out," he repeated, and there was something in his voice — approval wearing gruffness like a coat.

I eased the throttle forward. The *Kestrel's Wake* answered, swung her nose toward the channel, and I felt her settle under me the way a horse settles under a rider it has decided to accept. The mist was burning off. The channel markers ticked past. And I realized my eyes weren't on the instruments or the markers or the depth sounder — they were on the water ahead, reading it, the way Salim taught me without ever formally teaching me. Current seams. Drift lines. The places where the river changed its mind.

"You're steering by eye," Bo said behind me. There was wonder in it, badly hidden. "Dead reckoning."

"Is that what it's called?"

"Don't get clever."

But he was smiling. I could hear it.

I held the wheel through the morning. Through the narrows where the current pushed sideways and you had to crab the boat against it. Through the stretch where a crocodile slid off a mudbank and watched us pass with ancient indifference. Through the moment when Cyrus came up on deck, saw me at the helm, and stopped dead — and then, instead of taking the wheel back the way the old Cyrus would have, she leaned against the rail and just watched, arms folded, and after a long time she nodded once, small, like a door opening somewhere.

At noon I brought us back to anchor, easy, backing the power off so the chain didn't rattle. Bo checked the anchor set, pronounced it "adequate," which from Bo was a parade with brass bands.

Cyrus stayed at the rail as I stepped down from the helm. For a moment neither of us spoke. Then:

"Salim would have said something impossible and wise right now," she said. "Something about rivers and daughters."

"He'd have said the wheel fits my hands."

Cyrus looked at my hands on the last spoke before letting go. "It does," she said. "It always did. We were just waiting for you to believe it."

* * *

That evening, the radio brought one more thing before the static swallowed the band. A bulletin, short and strange: investigators in Port Moresby announcing that Commander Ilse Rennick, before her trial, had requested to testify *in person before the families of the massacred laborers* — not in a courtroom, but at the village. Her lawyers were fighting it. The government was horrified. She had refused to withdraw the request.

Bo turned it off. Nobody spoke for a while.

"She wants to kneel," Tomas said finally, from the doorway. He'd been listening from the shadows. "In front of them. Where it happened. That's not in any courtroom manual."

"Will they let her?" I asked.

"The families will decide," Tomas said. "That was always the point of everything we did. They decide." He looked out at the dark water. "But I know the elder. And I know what he'll say."

"What?"

Tomas smiled — the first real smile I'd seen on him that wasn't for Cyrus. "He'll say the river brings even the guilty home eventually. And then he'll make her sit through four hours of introductions before anyone lets her speak a word."

And we laughed — all of us, even Cyrus, even Tomas — the kind of laughter that hurts a little, the kind that carries grief inside it like a seed inside a fruit, and the *Kestrel's Wake* rocked gently at her anchor under the first stars, and I stood at the empty helm a while longer with my hands resting on the wheel, not steering, just holding on, learning the shape of what came next.
