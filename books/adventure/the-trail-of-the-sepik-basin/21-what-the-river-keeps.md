## 21. What the River Keeps

The morning of the last dive came in the color of pewter, and Cyrus Sterling woke before her alarm for the first time in three years.

She lay in her bunk listening to the river breathe against the hull — that slow tidal inhale and exhale she had once timed her whole existence by, the way other people timed theirs by church bells or school years. The *Marrow* rocked gently at anchor off the delta, where the Sepik finally gave up its silt to the sea and the water turned from tea-brown to a bruised green-gray. Somewhere above her, Bo was already moving around the deck, his boots making their familiar arrhythmic percussion on the plating. Nia's voice carried down through the open hatch, bright and unguarded, arguing with Salim's ghost about the proper way to fold an omelet — because that was how they kept him now, folded into ordinary mornings, present in the shape of every absence.

Cyrus dressed without turning on the light. She knew the boat the way she knew her own hands: the cold spot on the ladder rail, the loose rivet near the compressor locker, the way the deck always held one shallow pool of yesterday's rain no matter how many times you swept it. She had spent eleven years measuring herself against vessels like this one — against depth gauges and bottom-time tables and the long arithmetic of nautical miles logged, recovered, survived.

Today she would stop counting.

* * *

The wreck sat in forty-one feet of water, three miles off the delta's southern channel, exactly where it had sat since the monsoon of 1979 dragged it off a sandbar and drowned it. A coastal freighter once, before the war ate it and the jungle reclaimed its bones — now a cathedral of rust and river silt, its superstructure furred with black weed, its holds open like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down here to die and never gotten back up.

It was the first wreck Cyrus had ever dived alone. Seventeen years old, illegal, breathing off a borrowed regulator with a gauge that read wrong, she had slipped under the surface while her brother Tomas stood watch on the skiff and lied to their father about where she'd gone. She remembered the way the light died as she descended — gold to green to a brown so complete it felt like being swallowed — and how, at the bottom, she had put her palm flat against the hull and felt the wreck's stillness enter her like a lesson. *This is what the river keeps,* she had thought, though she hadn't known yet that she was thinking it. *Everything it takes, it keeps.*

She had been diving for salvage ever since. For brass and bronze, for propeller shafts and portholes, for anything with weight enough to sell and history enough to matter. And when Tomas went into the water off some classified coordinate the navy wouldn't name, she had kept diving for something else entirely — for him, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it. Every descent had been a question. Every ascent, a smaller version of the answer she feared.

Now she knew. Now she had held her living brother's face between her hands in a village deep in the basin, had heard the massacre named aloud by firelight, had watched the testimony travel out of the dark country and into the world like a lantern passed hand to hand. The story had broken. Rennick was in custody, awaiting a tribunal that would be televised. The contractors' boat lay grounded and broken on the very wreck Cyrus knew better than anyone alive — and the chest itself, the lead-lined thing stamped with the seal of Vaskaria, rested somewhere below her keel right now, in the deep scour hole off the freighter's port side, where no government would ever reach it and no court could ever claim it.

She had scuttled it there herself, nine days ago, with the contractors' searchlights sweeping the surface above her head. It had been the hardest and easiest thing she'd ever done: opening her hands underwater and letting sixty pounds of sealed metal drop away into the dark, keeping only the documents wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, keeping only the truth.

The truth had done its work. The chest could rest.

That was why she was going down today. Not for salvage. Not for answers. Not even for Tomas, who was safe aboard the *Marrow*, sleeping in the bunk that had been hers, eating Salim's recipes from Nia's hands, learning again how to be a man among people instead of a ghost among strangers.

She was going down to say goodbye.

* * *

"You don't have to do this alone," Bo said, when she came up the ladder with her tank harness over her shoulder. He was pretending to inspect the winch, which needed no inspection; he had been pretending to inspect things around her for two days, waiting for her to say what she wanted, because Bo's love had always been a kind of patient machinery that ran on silence until called upon.

"I know," Cyrus said. "I want to."

He nodded slowly, chewing on the inside of his cheek the way he did when he was swallowing something larger than words. "Forty-one feet," he said. "Bottom temp's warm, viz is maybe eight feet with this tide. Current's slack for another hour." He paused. "I'll keep your line."

"Bo."

"I'll keep your line," he said again, gentler, "because that's what we do. That's all any of us ever did for each other. We keep each other's lines." His eyes were wet and he pretended they weren't, and Cyrus loved him so fiercely in that moment that she couldn't speak, so she didn't try. She just gripped his forearm, hard, the way divers grip — once, meaning everything — and stepped to the gunwale.

Nia was waiting there with her mask. The girl — not a girl anymore, not after the confluence, not after Salim — had cleaned the lens herself and fitted a new strap, and she held it out with both hands like something ceremonial, which, Cyrus understood suddenly, it was.

"Salim would've made you take food down," Nia said. Her voice cracked on his name and steadied itself, the way it had learned to. "He'd have said the dead get hungry too, and you should bring them something."

"He did say that once," Cyrus said. "About my mother. He packed me a tin of cardamom rice and told me to leave it on the seabed." She smiled, and found that the smile didn't hurt. "I did. I don't know if it helped her. But it helped me."

"So?" Nia said. "Bring them something."

So Cyrus went below and came back with a small cloth bundle: a handful of betel nut from the village elders who had sheltered Tomas, a strip of dried fish, and — because grief deserved sweetness wherever it could find it — one of the last of Salim's candied ginger pieces, saved in a jar Bo hadn't been able to bring himself to finish. She tucked the bundle into her buoyancy vest, against her heart, next to nothing else, because she wasn't carrying evidence anymore. She wasn't carrying anyone's secrets. Just breakfast for the dead.

Tomas appeared at the cabin door as she weighed herself with the belt. He looked better than he had a week ago — filled out slightly, hair trimmed by Nia's impatient scissors, eyes less like a man watching the treeline. But he still moved carefully, like someone relearning the dimensions of rooms, and when he looked at the water his jaw tightened in a way Cyrus recognized because her own jaw did the same thing.

"You're going down to it," he said. Not a question.

"To say goodbye," she said. "To the wreck. To the chest. To—" She stopped, and then, because she had promised herself honesty on this boat above all things, finished: "To the version of me who spent three years diving for your body. I owe her a burial too."

Tomas crossed the deck and took her face in both hands, the way she had taken his in the village, and pressed his forehead to hers. His breath smelled of river and woodsmoke and the antiseptic Nia insisted on for his healing scars.

"Tell her," he said quietly, "that she found me anyway. Tell her it counted."

Cyrus closed her eyes. "Yeah," she managed. "Okay."

Then she spat in her mask, rinsed it, set it in her teeth, and fell backward off the gunwale into the green-gray morning, and the river closed over her like a book.

* * *

Descent was a language she had spoken so long she'd forgotten it was a language. Pressure gathered along her ears and she equalized without thinking, swallow-and-pinch, swallow-and-pinch, the way her father had taught her before he taught her anything else. The light thinned from pewter to jade to a deep suspended olive, and the temperature dropped by degrees she could feel on her forearms like fingers, and the sounds of the world narrowed to the two great rhythms of her life: her own breath, roaring softly in and out of the regulator, and the river's vast silence underneath it.

Eight feet of visibility, Bo had said. It was generous. The water was a brown veil, and the wreck assembled itself out of it in pieces — first the shadow of the mast, leaning like a drunk against the sky she could no longer see; then the rail, furred white with freshwater mussel; then the great black flank of the hull rising out of the silt, and her own bubbles streaming up alongside it like a string of silver beads offered to the surface gods.

She leveled off at forty-one feet and hung there, weightless, and let the wreck come to her.

It had changed since the contractors' boat tore itself open on its bones. There was new wreckage now — a scatter of fiberglass shards, a snapped cleat, a length of synthetic line drifting like kelp — the fresh scar laid across the old one. Two disasters, forty-some years apart, sharing a grave. The river kept everything. That had always been the bargain, and she had hated it for most of her life: the river keeps everything, keeps the drowned and the sunk and the secret, keeps your brother's last known coordinates in its gray ledger, keeps the chest with no country, keeps the men who were taken into the interior and the laborers who were massacred and the names nobody wrote down. The river keeps everything, and gives back almost nothing, and you build a life on the almost.

But hanging there in the brown-green light, Cyrus felt the truth of it turn over, the way a coin turns, the way a body turns in current.

The river keeps everything — and that is not cruelty. That is custody. The river had kept Tomas for three years, hidden in the basin's green interior, held safe by villagers who understood better than any navy what witness meant. The river had kept the chest sealed in lead until the one diver in the world with a reason to find it came looking. The river had kept Salim's duplicates dry inside the hull, had kept the testimony moving hand to hand, had kept the story alive in the mouths of elders who refused to forget names. Everything she had fought for these past months, the river had been holding for her the whole time. She had never been fighting the water. She had only been fighting the men who feared what the water kept.

Her breath fogged the mask and cleared. Below her, at the edge of visibility, the scour hole waited — the deep pocket where the chest lay in its final anchorage, wrapped in chain she'd welded shut herself, settled into silt that would take decades to give it up, and might never.

She finned down to it. Slowly. Ceremonially.

At the lip of the hole she stopped and hovered, and reached into her vest, and brought out the bundle. Betel nut, dried fish, candied ginger. Offerings for the drowned of the Vaskarian expedition, for the laborers whose names the logs had recorded as cargo, for the sailors of the 1979 wreck, for her mother, for Salim — oh, especially for Salim, who had fed this crew for eleven years and died feeding its escape — for everyone the river kept.

She let the bundle go. It turned once in the faint current, opened like a flower, and sank into the hole, and the silt received it without a ripple worth naming.

"There," she said, and her voice was a strange animal sound in her own ears, huge and blurred through the regulator. "Eat. Rest. I'll carry the rest."

Because that was the other half of the truth she'd come down to learn: the river keeps everything, but it does not need everything. Some things are meant to be carried up. Testimony. Names. The memory of a man folding omelets badly and navigating brilliantly. The weight of a brother's forehead against yours. You dive, you grieve, you descend into the dark and touch the hull of what you've lost — and then you go up. That was the whole art of it. That was the thing she'd never learned in seventeen years of bottom-time tables: that surfacing was not abandonment. Surfacing was the point.

She put her palm flat against the freighter's hull, the same gesture she'd made at seventeen, and felt the wreck's stillness enter her again — but differently now. Not as a lesson in loss. As a blessing. *Go on,* the stillness said, in the voice of every wreck she'd ever touched. *We're kept. Go up.*

And Cyrus Sterling, who had measured her life in nautical miles — miles of cable, miles of river, miles of distance between herself and her brother's grave-that-wasn't — understood, with a clarity that felt like warmth spreading through cold water, that she was done measuring. Not because the distances had shrunk. Because she had stopped needing them to. A mile was just a way of saying *not yet*. She was in the *now* now, forty-one feet down in the river that had given her back everything, and the only number left in her was one: one breath, then the next, then the next.

She kicked for the surface.

The light came back in reverse — olive to jade to pewter to a sudden blinding silver as she broke through, and the sky was enormous, and Bo's face was there at the gunwale, and Nia's hand hauled her mask off, and Tomas was kneeling at the rail with tears running freely down his face and no attempt whatsoever to hide them.

"Welcome up," Bo said gruffly, gripping her arm as she climbed the ladder, and his voice said everything else.

Cyrus knelt on the deck, dripping, lighter than she had been since she was a child. Lighter than the day she'd found the chest. Lighter, maybe, than the day Tomas disappeared — because that day she had picked up a weight she'd mistaken for her own, and now she knew whose it had been, and she had set it down where it belonged, in the keeping of the river.

Nia wrapped a blanket around her shoulders. Tomas sat beside her and leaned his head against hers, and none of them spoke for a long time, because the speaking was done and the keeping was done and the mourning, for the first time in Cyrus Sterling's life, was finished rather than merely postponed.

Then Bo straightened up at the wheel, squinting south toward the delta channels, and a slow grin cracked through his beard.

"Tide's turning," he said. "Fair current all the way to the village reach. If we make way within the hour, we'll raise the stilt-houses by tomorrow dusk — and I promised the elder's granddaughter we'd bring the medicine chest and a spare bilge pump." He looked back at them all — Cyrus dripping and shining, Tomas steady on his own feet, Nia already moving to cast off with the sure, quick hands of someone born to a wheel she hadn't yet admitted she wanted. "Well? This boat doesn't sail itself."

Cyrus laughed — a real laugh, unarmored, startling her own ears — and got up, and went to take her place beside her family as the *Marrow* swung her bow upriver, into the current, toward home water.
