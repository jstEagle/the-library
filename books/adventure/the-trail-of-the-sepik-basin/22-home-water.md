## 22. Home Water

*Nia*

The wheel was warm under her hands.

That was the thing nobody warned you about — that a boat's wheel could feel like something alive, like the pulse of the river coming up through the shaft and into your palms, steady as a heartbeat. Nia had steered before. Little corrections, ten minutes here while Salim ate or Bo swore at the engine. But this was different. This was the helm, the whole of it, dawn to dusk if she wanted it, and Cyrus had said *take her up* like it was nothing, like she wasn't handing over three years of trust along with the wheel.

The Sepik opened ahead of them in the early light, wide as a brown sea, breathing mist. The river had moods — Nia had mapped them in her head for two years now, back when mapping them was just a game to keep her mind busy on long watches. The sullen water after rain in the highlands. The glassy lying calm that meant crocodiles below. The wind-chopped glitter of midday when the current ran fast and the banks blurred green. This morning the river was in its generous mood, broad and slow and golden at the edges where the mist burned off, and it carried them north the way a hand carries a paper boat.

Behind her, at the stern table, they were setting out breakfast. She could hear it without looking: the clink of the tin plates, the hiss of the burner, Bo grumbling about the coffee the way he grumbled about everything, which was how he said *I'm glad you're here.* And she could hear the space too. The space where Salim's voice would have been, telling some long winding story about cardamom markets in a port whose name changed three times in his lifetime. The space had its own sound now. They'd all learned to hear it.

"Course?" Tomas asked from beside her. He'd taken to standing near the wheel in the mornings, quiet, watching the water with eyes that still flinched at sudden noise. Three years in the interior had left him thin as a heron and just as watchful, but every day a little more of him came back downriver toward them, like cargo arriving in stages.

"North by the big bend," Nia said. "Then we cut through the channel behind Kandri Island when the tide turns. Salim showed me once." She stopped. Saying Salim's name didn't hurt the way it used to — or it hurt differently, the way an old scar hurts, familiar, almost private. "He drew it in my chart book. Said I'd need it someday."

Tomas looked at her, and there was something in his face that made her stand straighter. Not pity. Recognition. He knew what it was to be handed something by someone who hadn't lived to see it used.

"He'd have liked this," Tomas said. "You at the wheel."

"He'd have told me I was holding her too tight."

"Are you?"

Nia checked her hands. She loosened her grip one finger at a time. "Maybe."

"River doesn't like being gripped," Tomas said, and turned to go help with breakfast, and Nia laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone at the helm in the morning mist — because it was exactly what Salim would have said, and somehow the dead man and the half-dead man had met somewhere in the middle of her boat and agreed on the rules of the water.

She held the course easy and let the river carry them home.

* * *

*Bo*

Bo had rebuilt the engine twice in his life and buried one friend, and he'd tell you straight-faced that the second job was harder, though he'd never say it sober and never say it loud.

He sat at the stern table with a wrench in one hand and a plate of Salim's flatbread recipe — Nia had found it written on the back of a tide table, flour-smudged, annotated in three languages — trying to get the ratio right. Third batch. The first had come out like ballast, the second like roofing shingles. This one was close. Close enough that when he set it on the tin plate in the middle of the table, Cyrus looked at it a long moment and then looked away, jaw working, and Bo pretended not to see, because that was the arrangement between them and always had been.

They'd bolted a new plank onto the table rail last week. Oiled teak, sanded smooth, with letters burned into it in Nia's careful hand: **S.V.** No dates. Bo had argued about that — *put the years, people should know* — but Nia had shaken her head. "He hated dates," she said. "Said a man who counts days is counting down." So it was just the initials, and the plank, and the chair that nobody sat in, pulled up to the table every morning like Salim had just gone forward to check the lines and would be back any minute wanting his tea strong enough to strip paint.

Bo poured that tea anyway. Set it at the empty place. Felt foolish doing it and did it regardless, because grief was mostly a matter of doing foolish things on purpose until they turned into something else.

"You're burning it," Cyrus said, nodding at the pan.

"I'm *finishing* it. There's a difference."

"There is. One of them smells like fire."

He swatted her hand away when she reached to steal a piece, and she grinned, and something in his chest unclenched a notch. That was the other thing nobody told you about losing someone: the living kept needing things from you. Breakfast. Jokes. Someone to argue with about bread. Some mornings the needing felt like a weight and some mornings — more of them now, weeks on — it felt like a rope thrown to a man in water. You grabbed it. You hauled yourself toward shore one needed-thing at a time.

Tomas came aft with a crate of medical supplies they'd loaded at Wewak — bandages, antibiotics, antimalarials, a proper splint kit, all paid for out of the insurance money and Cyrus's salvage claim, what was left of it after she'd let the rest burn. Literally burn; Bo had stood on the dock and watched the claim papers go into a coffee can fire, watched Cyrus feed them in page by page with the same steady face she wore underwater, and he had understood then that she wasn't destroying anything. She was cutting herself loose.

"Where do you want these?" Tomas asked.

"Aft hold, dry side." Bo wiped his hands. "And the school supplies go in the bow locker — Nia packed 'em, don't let her hear you've mixed 'em up."

"School supplies," Tomas repeated, slow, like the words were in a language he was relearning.

"Crayons. Paper. A globe, don't ask me why, the girl found a globe." Bo shrugged, embarrassed suddenly by how much of it there was, how much they'd bought, how much they wanted to give. "Village has kids. Kids should have maps of somewhere bigger than the river."

Tomas stood there holding the crate, and Bo saw his throat move. "They had nothing," Tomas said quietly. "When I was there. The laborers' families — after the massacre, after everyone left — they had nothing but each other and the river."

"Well," Bo said, "now they've got us too. Load the crate."

Tomas went. Bo drank his terrible coffee at the table with four plates set and only three men eating, and found, to his own surprise, that the morning was good. Not whole. Nothing would make it whole again. But good the way a welded seam is good — scarred metal, holding water out, stronger along the break than it ever was before.

* * *

*Tomas*

He slept in the forward bunk now, the one with the hatch above it, because Cyrus had insisted and because — though he never said so — he still couldn't sleep anywhere he couldn't see sky.

Three years under the canopy of the interior, three years of green dark and whispered warnings and moving camp whenever strangers came upriver, and his body had learned things his mind was still unlearning. Loud voices made his hands shake. Closed rooms made his breath go short. But the hatch above this bunk framed a rectangle of river sky, and last night there had been stars in it, and he had lain there watching them turn and thought: *this is what the sky does when no one is hunting you. It just turns.*

Now it was midmorning and he sat on the foredeck with the village children's crayon box open beside him — Nia's idea, she'd said drawing would help his hands remember gentle work — sketching the riverbank as it slid past. He was bad at it. His lines wobbled. He drew anyway.

Cyrus came forward and sat across from him, cross-legged, the way she seemed to do everything: without ceremony, without wasted motion. His sister. The word still landed strangely. For three years he had been nobody's anything — a ghost, a witness, a secret kept by villagers who risked their lives to keep him. Now he was somebody's brother, and the word fit like a boot broken in by another man: usable, warm, not quite his yet.

"You're staring at the bank," Cyrus said.

"Looking for the marker tree." He nodded at a bend coming up. "There's a fig tree on the point with a scar cut into it — old trade mark, older than anyone living. When you see it, the village channel is two miles past. My guide, Maren — the elder's daughter — she used to joke that the tree was the only honest signpost on the river."

"And is it?"

"It'll be there." He looked at her. "Trees keep their promises. It was people who didn't."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. Out here on the bow, with the engine note steady beneath them and the mist long burned off, she looked younger than her command of the boat suggested, and older than her years, both at once. He was learning her the way you learn a river you're going to live on — slowly, by watching what she did rather than what she said.

"The journalist's piece runs tomorrow," she said. "Wire services picked it up. Rennick's testimony is in it — all of it, names, the artifacts, the laborers. Everything."

"I know. You told me."

"I'm telling you again." Her mouth twitched. "Habit. I used to brief myself out loud before dives. Bad ones."

"How many were bad ones?"

"All of them, near enough." She turned her face into the wind. "Tomas. When we get there — when we see them — the families. What do you want to say to them?"

He had thought about this every night since they'd left the delta. He had rehearsed speeches in the dark above his bunk, formal words, apology-shaped words. And every rehearsal had collapsed under the same weight: that sorry was a coin so small next to what was taken that offering it was almost an insult.

"I want to give them the documents," he said finally. "All of them. The originals, not copies — the ones Salim hid in the hull. Their husbands and fathers built that expedition's wealth and died for it and got a hole in the ground and silence. The evidence is theirs. Whatever they want done with it — courts, newspapers, burning it in the square — their choice. Not mine. Not any government's." He flexed his hands, the ones that had held a knife in the dark three years ago and dropped it and run and lived. "Justice that gets decided *for* people isn't justice. It's just management."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached over and took one of the crayons — the ochre one, river-colored — and made a small mark on the corner of his sketch, like a countersignature.

"Maren will like you," she said. "You think like a diver. We plan for what's down there, not what we wish was."

Ahead, around the long green bend, a point of land rose up — and on it, vast and scarred and keeping its promise, stood the fig tree.

* * *

*Cyrus*

The village came to meet them before the anchor was even down.

They came in dugouts, six boats, eight, paddles flashing, and the sound reached across the water first — singing, low and layered, a song Cyrus didn't know and understood completely. Word had traveled downriver ahead of them the way it always did, faster than any boat: *the ghost of the interior is coming home, and he brings his family, and they bring medicine.*

Nia brought them alongside with a touch so soft the hull kissed the pilings. Bo threw lines. And Tomas stood at the rail, gripping it with both hands, and Cyrus stood beside him and did not touch him, because some thresholds a man crosses alone.

Maren stood at the head of the landing stairs, older than Cyrus remembered, straighter than she should have been. Behind her the whole village had come out — elders on the gallery, children hanging off the rails, women with babies on hips, and off to one side, apart, a cluster of families Cyrus recognized from the photographs in the chest: the widows and orphans of the laborers. The ones the expedition had worked to death and shot and buried in unmarked ground and reported as *lost equipment.*

Tomas went down the stairs slowly. At the bottom, Maren took his face in her two hands the way you'd hold something rescued from water, and said something in her own language, soft, and Tomas answered, and whatever passed between them made the old woman laugh and cry at once.

Then the families came forward, and Tomas knelt — knelt, in the mud of the landing, in front of the widows — and began to speak, and Cyrus turned away to give him the privacy of it, and found Bo at her shoulder, and Nia on her other side, and the three of them stood on the deck of the boat like a crew standing watch over something larger than a vessel.

"What happens now?" Nia asked quietly.

"Now?" Cyrus watched Tomas rise, watched the eldest widow take the oilcloth packet of original documents from his hands and press it against her chest like a child. "Now they decide. Courts, papers, fires in the square — their call. We're just the delivery service."

"And us? After?"

Cyrus looked at her — at the girl who'd become a helmsman somewhere between the delta and here, salt-stained and steady-eyed, seventeen going on captain — and felt the answer arrive whole, the way truth sometimes does, without calculation.

"We take on water and provisions tomorrow. Day after, we run medicine up to the mission station at Ambunti — they sent word they're short. Then there's a school in Pagwi that needs a roof, and Bo says he can roof anything, and I believe him because I've seen him weld underwater." She smiled. "Then wherever the river says."

"That's not a plan. That's just — going."

"That's what it is." Cyrus put her arm around the girl's shoulders, rough and brief, the way affection was done aboard. "I spent my whole life measuring in miles. Miles to the wreck, miles to the payout, miles between me and my brother's ghost. You know what I figured out at the bottom of the delta, with the chest in my hands?" She shook her head before Nia could guess. "The river doesn't measure itself. It just goes. Carries what it carries, gives what it gives, ends where it ends. I'm done measuring."

Below them, the singing rose again, and someone started beating a drum, and children swarmed the gangplank demanding to see the boat, and Bo bellowed happily that they could look but not touch the engine, and the evening filled up with noise and cooking smoke and the smell of Maren's stew drifting out over the water.

At full dark, they ate at the stern table — all of them, plus Maren and two of the elders, the boat groaning cheerfully at the crowd. The empty chair stood in its place. The tea sat steaming at Salim's setting, strong enough to strip paint. Before they ate, Nia stood, lifted the cup, and poured it slowly over the rail into the dark river, and nobody explained it to the guests, and nobody needed to.

"For the road," Nia said.

"For the road," they echoed.

Later — much later, when the village lights had gone to embers and the guests had gone ashore and only the night sounds of the basin remained — Cyrus stood alone at the rail where the tea had gone into the water. Tomas joined her, silent, his presence beside hers as easy now as breathing.

"Strange," he said at last. "For three years I dreamed of getting off this river. Anywhere else. Anywhere at all."

"And now?"

He was quiet a while. A fish broke the surface downstream, a soft silver kiss of sound. Up in the hills, invisible, something sang.

"Now I dream of going further up it," he said.

Cyrus laughed, low, and leaned her arms on the rail, and looked north into the dark where the river ran on toward its source, carrying them, carrying everything, asking nothing measured of anyone.

"First light, then," she said. "We'll want the tide."

And the river, in its generous mood, agreed.
