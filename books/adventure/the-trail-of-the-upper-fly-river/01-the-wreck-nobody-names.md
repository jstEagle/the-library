## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The tide turned at four, and Orsen Quintana went down with it.

She slipped off the gunwale of her boat without a splash, the way her brother had taught her when she was nine and afraid of the brown water. *Don't fight the river,* Tomas had said, treading beside her in the shallows off Kiwai Island, his hand light between her shoulder blades. *The river always wins. Be something it can carry.* Twenty years later she still entered the water like a thing being carried — boneless, patient, letting the current take her down along the anchor line into the green murk where the wreck waited.

Visibility in the delta was a rumor. Three feet on a good day, less now with the tide hauling silt off the mudflats. She wore no tank — just hookah line, sixty meters of it running back to the little compressor aboard the *Magpie*, chattering to itself like an idiot bird. The hose was a leash and she knew it. Every diver on the delta knew it. You dove tethered or you didn't come back, and the river had already taken enough from her family to fill a small cemetery.

The wreck lay on its port side in eleven meters of tea-colored water, a coastal freighter that had gone down sometime before the war nobody on the delta would name out loud. The charts called it nothing. The navy charts called it nothing louder. Salvage rights had been contested for years by men in Port Moresby who had never gotten their shoes wet, and then eighteen months ago the navy had strung a buoy line around it and posted a patrol schedule, and after that even the boldest of the delta divers gave it a wide berth.

Orsen did not give things wide berths. It was not courage, exactly. It was more that grief had burned away whatever part of a person calculates risk against reward. When your brother drowned doing exactly what you do for a living, the arithmetic stops meaning anything. You either stop diving entirely — and she had tried that, eight months in Port Moresby fixing outboard motors and drinking too much — or you dive harder, deeper, closer to the thing that killed him, as if the water might eventually give him back if you just kept asking.

She asked every day.

Her gloved hands found the hull plating and she moved along it the way other people move through their own houses in the dark. She knew this ship. Not its name — nobody knew its name, which was itself a kind of obscenity, a vessel dying unnamed — but its body. The tear in the plating aft of the funnel where the river had begun eating it. The cargo hold full of silt and rusted drums. The wheelhouse with its brass fittings gone green as pond weed, the telegraph still frozen at FULL AHEAD, which she found unbearable and visited anyway, the way you visit a grave.

Today she was working the officers' country amidships. There was brass there, and brass meant money: porthole rings, lamp housings, a door plate if she was lucky. The navy cordon made salvage technically illegal, which meant she worked short windows between patrols and sold what she brought up quietly, through Ila Mero's buying shed on Daru, and never asked how Ila moved it onward. Asking questions was how people on the delta disappeared into the general silence.

The compressor chattered above. Her air hissed. Somewhere far off, muffled by water, she heard the deep diesel throb of a patrol launch and went still against the hull, one hand flat on cold steel, listening the way her father had taught her to listen for crocodiles — not for the sound itself but for the change in the sound, the way it bent around the bends of the channels. This one was heading up the middle passage. Not toward her. Yet.

She gave it ten minutes anyway, hanging in the brown water like a leaf, watching silt drift past her mask in slow galaxies. Patience was the whole trade. Tomas used to say she had none, and he'd been wrong; he'd been the impatient one, the brilliant reckless one, and look where patience had gotten each of them.

*Be something the river can carry.*

She worked the seam of a bulkhead door with her pry bar. The metal shrieked — a small shriek, swallowed by water, but her heart slammed anyway. Sound carried strangely down here. Everything sounded like it was aimed at you.

The door came free and behind it was a cabin, half-full of silt, and in the silt the ordinary archaeology of drowned lives: a shoe. A typewriter fused into a single red-orange stone of rust. A shaving mirror, silvered backing flaked away so it reflected nothing, which seemed right. And bolted to the bulkhead, a fitting — a brass escutcheon plate, the kind that framed a ship's name board, except the name board was long gone and only the frame remained, and around the frame ran a border of stamped relief.

Orsen brushed the silt away with her thumb, gently, the way she'd once brushed ash off her mother's photograph.

The stamping showed a seal. A crest: a bird with spread wings over crossed tools — a pick and an oar, she thought, though the corrosion made everything provisional — ringed by lettering in an alphabet she didn't recognize. Not English. Not Dutch. Not the Japanese characters she knew from other wrecks, or the German gothic she'd seen once on a submarine bell. The letters were angular, deliberate, beautiful in the way of things designed by someone who believed they would last forever.

Nations were like that too, once.

She turned the plate in the water. It came free of the corroded screws with a twist, and she stowed it in her mesh bag without letting herself think about why her pulse had picked up. It was brass. Brass was money. That was all. But her hands, which never shook in the water, were not quite steady, and she noticed that about herself the way you notice weather changing — distantly, with a sense of consequence arriving from far off.

* * *

She surfaced into late gold light with the bag heavy against her hip and hauled herself aboard the *Magpie* with the economy of long practice. The delta at dusk was the most beautiful place she knew and the loneliest: mangrove islands going black against a sky the color of flame-touched copper, egrets crossing in lines like handwriting, the water turning from brown to bronze to something almost holy. She had grown up believing beauty was a thing you could hold. Then Tomas drowned, and she learned beauty was mostly a thing that happened near you while you were looking elsewhere.

She ran the compressor's fuel check, coiled the hose, and opened the mesh bag on the deck boards.

Porthole rings, three. Lamp housing. And the plate.

In the dying light the seal looked worse than it had underwater — more corroded, the lettering nearly smooth — but the crest was clear enough. Winged bird. Crossed pick and oar. She sat cross-legged over it for a long time while the mosquitoes found her and began their evening argument with her skin.

Here was the thing she kept circling: she knew wrecks. Not all of them, but the delta's dead ships passed through her hands year after year, and she knew their nationalities the way a nurse knows faces. British tramp steamers. Australian luggers. Dutch coasters. Japanese barge-carriers from the war years, and American landing craft, and one sad Portuguese trader. Every flag that ever worked these waters, she could read from a valve wheel or a builder's plate.

This seal matched none of them.

A nation she couldn't name had put a ship in the Fly River delta, and the ship had died unnamed, and the navy had cordoned it off as if grief required security, and none of it made any sense unless the answer was a kind she wasn't supposed to want.

Tomas would have wanted it. That was the thought that arrived last, quiet as the tide, and it settled over her like a wet shirt. He'd have been up all night with a magnifying glass and his grandfather's atlas, hunting through pages of defunct nations, extinct colonies, protectorates that had lasted a decade and dissolved. He'd have laughed at her for hesitating. *You're the careful one,* he'd said once, and it hadn't been a compliment, and she'd carried it for twenty years like a splinter.

The patrol launch came back down the middle passage at full dark, searchlight sweeping the mangroves in slow arcs. Orsen killed her riding lamp and sat in the dark with the plate in her lap until the light passed over the water a hundred meters off and moved on, indifferent, the way large powers are indifferent to small boats.

When it was gone she wrapped the plate in an oilcloth and stowed it under the floorboards, beneath the false bottom where she kept the things that mattered: her mother's photograph, Tomas's last letter, a knife, and forty-one thousand kina in small bills.

Then she made coffee on the single burner and drank it scalding, and let herself feel the shape of the evening, which was this: she had spent six years diving toward her brother, asking the river to give him back, and tonight, for the first time, the river had given her something else instead. Something with a dead nation's face on it. Something the navy didn't want found.

Curiosity, she thought, was just loneliness that had learned to point somewhere.

She finished the coffee, weighed anchor, and ran the *Magpie* down the black channel toward Daru, running lights off, engine at a murmur, a woman carrying a small piece of a large secret through the dark like a coal cupped in both hands.

By morning she would be at Ila Mero's buying shed. By tomorrow night, she suspected, she would know whether the plate was worth money or worth dying for.

She was already afraid she knew which.
