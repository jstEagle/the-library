## 2. Chest of a Dead Flag

The silt gave it up grudgingly, the way the river gave up anything at all.

Orsen Quintana hung forty feet down in the green-brown murk of the Fly's channel, her lamp beam cutting a cone through water the color of steeped tea, and watched the shape resolve out of the sediment she'd been fanning away for three days. It was not a pretty dive. The current here ran lazy on the surface and treacherous below, and the visibility was measured in arm-lengths, and every hour a patrol boat passed overhead trailing a propeller-wash that came down through the water column like the fist of God knocking on a door. She had learned to feel those knocks in her teeth.

But the chest was real.

It sat half-buried in the clay of the old channel bed, listing against a fallen rib of the wreck above it — some nameless coastal trader or government lighter that had gone down decades ago and been forgotten by everyone except the navy, who had cordoned off the whole reach with buoys and patrol schedules and a silence so complete it rang. Nobody salvaged a wreck nobody would name. Nobody paid Orsen Quintana's rates to raise ballast stones. Whatever lay under that cordon was worth lying for, and Orsen had decided long ago that she could live with a great many things if the money was clean enough.

She worked the probe around the chest's base, clearing mud in careful strokes, and her lamp found the first detail that made her stop breathing through her regulator for a long, disciplined moment.

Iron. Not wood, not brass-bound cedar like the tea chests traders had carried upriver in the old days — iron, riveted, the kind of box built to survive fire and flood and the specific violence of men who wanted what was inside to stay inside. The surface was furred with corrosion, orange blooms spreading across black metal, but along one edge, where a lifting lug stood proud of the silt, the metal had been scoured clean by the current. Stamped into it, small and precise as a signature:

A seal. A crest she did not know — a bird with spread wings over crossed lines, ringed by lettering worn nearly smooth. She rubbed her gloved thumb across it and felt the grooves, deep and deliberate, cut by a die pressed hard into soft brass before the fitting was ever bolted home.

She knew the flags of this coast. She knew the colonial crests and the republic seals and the mining consortium marks and the naval ensigns, because knowing them was part of knowing which wrecks were worth diving and which were traps. This seal belonged to none of them. It belonged to a country that, as far as she could reckon from the little history she'd absorbed in delta taverns, no longer existed — had existed only briefly, in wartime, on paper, its borders drawn by men in distant rooms and erased again before most of its own citizens ever saw the flag fly.

*The wreck nobody names,* she thought, *and the flag nobody flies.*

Tomas would have loved this. The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts of Tomas always did — sideways, sudden, a hook behind the ribs. Her brother had been the one who read everything, who kept a shelf of water-swollen paperbacks about lost expeditions and sunken treasuries, who used to say the river kept better secrets than any government because the river never needed to lie. He'd been dead four years now, drowned in water not thirty miles from where she knelt in the clay, and she still caught herself turning toward him when something extraordinary happened, still expecting to see his grin, his hand outstretched: *Show me, Orse. Show me what you found.*

She showed him nothing now. She showed him the bottom of rivers, sometimes, when the work took her near the place he died, and once a year she brought him a beer poured out on moving water, which was the only church she attended.

Her air gauge said she had time. Her nerve said otherwise; the last patrol boat pass had been close, closer than the schedule allowed, and someone aboard was looking at sonar instead of the channel chart. She rigged the lift bag without hurry — hurry killed divers faster than currents did — filled it in slow stages, and rode the chest up through forty feet of brown silence while the river closed over the hole she'd left like a mouth.

* * *

By dusk the chest sat in the hold of her boat, the *Magpie*, under a tarp weighted with chain, and Orsen sat on the deck above it drinking warm beer and telling herself she wasn't nervous.

The delta at night was a country of sounds. Water lapping hulls. Frogs in the nipa stands, a sound like gravel poured endlessly into a tin. Somewhere across the channel a generator thudding in a village that never quite slept. And beneath it all, when the wind dropped, the river itself — a vast low breathing, patient as digestion, carrying everything downstream eventually. Silt. Logs. Boats. Men.

She'd moored in a backwater she trusted, a slough behind a mangrove island where the patrol boats didn't bother and the only other traffic was fishermen too poor to ask questions. She'd cleaned the worst of the corrosion off the seal by lamplight and confirmed what she'd felt underwater: the crest matched the stamp on the brass fitting she'd recovered days ago, the find that had started all this, the small wrongness that had made her take a contract she should have refused. Someone had wanted this chest identifiable. Someone had wanted whoever found it to know exactly whose it was — even if the someone finding it couldn't read the name.

Triple its weight in gold. That was the number rattling around her skull, though no one had offered it yet. She was getting ahead of herself, pricing things that weren't hers to price. But she knew how this went. She'd raised enough strange cargo to know that an iron chest sealed with a dead nation's crest, pulled from a navy-cordoned wreck, was not going to sit quietly in her hold. Sooner or later someone would come asking, and the question was whether they'd come with money or with guns, and whether she'd be smart enough to tell the difference before the difference mattered.

She heard the outboard at half past nine.

Small engine, muffled, running quiet the way engines run when their owners don't want to be counted. Orsen killed her deck lamp without hurrying — never hurry, hurry tells people you're afraid — and stood in the dark listening as the sound approached the slough, slowed, and died. Oars after that. Good. A man who switched to oars was being careful, and careful men were usually either professionals or thieves, and the line between the two was thinner than a dive line.

"Miss Quintana." A voice from the dark water, low and pleasant, accented from somewhere upcountry. "I can see your cigarette. I'll assume the rifle beside you is decorative."

It was. Mostly. "You have me at a disadvantage," she called back.

"Ila Mero." A pause, and then a shape resolved against the starlight on the water — a barge pole, and a man poling it, standing easy, broad-shouldered, unhurried. "I buy salvage. I sell salvage. I move cargo that prefers not to be listed on manifests. You may have heard of me."

She hadn't, which meant he was either new or very good, and his calm suggested very good. "I haven't."

"That's the idea." The pole touched her gunwale and held there, gentle as a handshake. "May I come aboard? I'll keep my hands where you can see them. I'm told you're a reasonable woman, and I've come a long way to make you a reasonable offer."

He came up the ladder with his palms out, a big man gone soft at the middle, maybe fifty, with a face like a river stone — smooth, gray, impossible to read — and eyes that were not soft at all. He looked at the tarp over the hold exactly once, briefly, the way a man looks at a thing he has already seen in his mind a hundred times.

"You raised something today," he said. "From the cordon. Iron chest, this big—" he sketched it in the air, and the dimensions were right to the inch, "—with a seal on the corner. A bird, wings open, over crossed lines."

Orsen drank her beer and let the silence do her talking.

"I'll pay you," Ila Mero said, "three times its weight in gold. For the chest. Unopened, undamaged, tonight, and you never speak of it again. Not to me, not to anyone. There are men who will pay more than I will, Miss Quintana, and I want you to understand clearly that this is not a reason to deal with them. The men who pay more are the men who put it down there. Selling to them is selling to the grave."

"And selling to you?"

"Selling to me is selling to a man who forgets things professionally." He smiled, and it reached his eyes without warming them. "I'm offering sight unseen because I don't need to see it. I need it to disappear, and you need to be rich, and both of those needs can be met before midnight."

Three times its weight in gold. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. The chest weighed perhaps four hundred pounds with its contents. Gold was gold. It was more money than ten years of honest salvage, more money than she'd ever pretended to want, and it came with a single condition: ignorance. Buy the blindness, pocket the gold, motor back to port and never wonder again what a dead flag was doing on a living river.

Tomas would have opened it. That was the trouble with having a brother who read everything — he'd infected her. Curiosity was the family disease, and grief hadn't cured it; if anything, losing him had made it worse, because every unanswered question in the world now seemed to stand next to the biggest one, the one about him, the one she'd never get to open.

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course." Ila Mero stepped back to the rail, entirely unruffled, a man who had expected nothing else. "I'll be at the fuel dock at Kiunga Bend tomorrow evening. If the chest is aboard your boat when you arrive, we conclude our business. If it isn't, we conclude it anyway, just differently, and I'll wish you luck with genuine feeling, because you'll need it." He paused, one foot on the ladder. "One thing, since you're thinking. Whatever's in there — and I genuinely don't know, that's the truth — it was important enough to sink twice. Once in the river, and once in the records. Things sunk twice don't stay sunk by accident. Somebody swims down regularly to push them back under. Think about who's been swimming."

Then he was gone, oars whispering, and the dark took him back the way the river takes everything.

* * *

She lasted until two in the morning, which she considered a personal triumph.

The *Magpie*'s cabin was hot and close, and the kerosene lamp threw her shadow huge against the bulkhead as she knelt over the chest with a pry bar and a cold chisel and the particular shame of a woman doing precisely what she'd been paid handsomely not to do. The lock was old, older than she'd guessed — a wartime pattern, heavy, corroded shut — but corrosion was her oldest trade partner. She soaked the mechanism in penetrating oil, worked it with a wire brush by inches, and at last the shackle gave with a crack that sounded obscene in the night quiet.

Inside, packed in tarred canvas gone stiff as bark, were papers.

Not gold. Not coin, not jewels, none of the cargo her imagination had been quietly pricing all evening. Papers — hundreds of them, bundled in twine, wrapped in oilcloth, layered like sediment in the chest's iron dark. She lifted a bundle into the lamplight and turned pages that crackled like insect wings. Typewritten sheets, dense blocks of text in a language she didn't recognize — angular script, formal, bureaucratic, the prose of forms filed and reports submitted. Carbon copies, some of them, the ink faded to ghost-gray. Photographs, too, when she dug deeper: black-and-white prints curling with age, and she looked at the first one for a long moment before understanding tilted under her feet.

Villages. The photographs showed villages — river villages, stilt houses, the same architecture she poled past every week of her life — and they showed them burning. They showed lines of people on riverbanks, and pits, and men in uniforms whose insignia matched the crest stamped on the chest's brass. They showed faces. She made herself look at the faces, because she could already feel her eyes trying to slide away, and sliding away was how things got sunk twice.

At the bottom of the chest, wrapped separately in waxed cloth, was a map.

It was beautiful and terrible, the way old maps are — hand-drawn, inked with the confidence of a surveyor who loved his work, showing the whole length of the Fly from delta to headwaters in fine hatched relief. The gorges. The bends. Mission stations marked with small crosses, villages marked with dots, and upstream, far upstream, past Kiunga, past anywhere she'd ever had business, the river fraying into a hundred threads of blue among mountains drawn in increasingly speculative lines. At the source — the true source, the forbidden one, the place the maps in the port offices simply stopped — someone had drawn a small symbol. A cave mouth. And beside it, in the same dead language as the documents, a single line of text, and beneath that, pressed into the paper and hardened by sixty years, a seal in red wax.

The bird, wings open, over crossed lines.

Orsen sat back on her heels in the swaying cabin and listened to the river breathe, and felt greed drain out of her body like blood from a wound, leaving something colder behind.

This wasn't treasure. Treasure you could spend. This was testimony — testimony that someone had thought worth sealing in iron and drowning, testimony that someone else had thought worth paying triple weight in gold to keep drowned, testimony with photographs of faces and a map leading to more of it hidden at the roof of the world. And somewhere in the bundles, she knew — knew the way you know a current shift under your keel — there would be names. Names of witnesses. Names of the dead. Names of the men who'd done it and the men who'd signed it away.

And one other thing, one smaller thing, that snagged her as she rewrapped the map with hands that were not quite steady.

The handwriting on the map's border — annotations, depth soundings, notes on seasonal water levels, the marginalia of a working diver checking a route — was in English. Familiar English. A hand she'd known since childhood, since he'd written her name in the dust of their mother's porch and taught her to swim in water exactly like this.

She knew Tomas's handwriting the way she knew her own heartbeat.

The lamp hissed. The river breathed. Orsen Quintana knelt over a dead nation's secrets with her brother's pen-strokes under her fingers, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head, that whatever had drowned him four years ago, it had not been the river alone.

Tomorrow evening, at Kiunga Bend, Ila Mero would be waiting at the fuel dock.

She began, slowly, to repack the chest.
