## 3. The Officer on the Pier

The launch came up the channel at half past six, white hull, grey superstructure, the pennant of the Coastal Survey Directorate snapping at its mast — a flag chosen, Dario Voss knew, precisely because nobody looked twice at it. He stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind his back and watched the delta resolve out of the morning haze: the brown fan of the river spreading into the sea like spilled tea, the mangrove keys standing dark and patient in the shallows, and beyond them the salvage camp, a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets on a spit of coral sand that appeared on no chart worth trusting.

He had been to this delta before. Not here, exactly — but to places shaped like it, in another life, when he had been a younger man with salt in his hair and a woman's laugh in his ear, and the navy had still seemed like a thing you served rather than a thing that served you. That was eleven years ago. The woman had been Orsen Quintana, and she had taught him that a river was not a place but an argument — that it was always trying to tell you something, and that most people went their whole lives without learning to listen.

He had learned to listen. That was the trouble.

"Making our approach, sir," said Ensign Prewitt at the helm, a round-faced boy who pronounced *sir* as though it were a small religious observance.

"Slow to bare steerageway. We'll come alongside the pier, not the beach. I don't want us beached like a whale in front of these people."

"Aye, sir."

Voss turned back to the rail. In his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, lay his orders — three pages, typed, unsigned by any name he recognized, bearing a classification stamp he had never before seen in nine years of service. *Recovery of materiel of interest. Discretion paramount. No written reports. Verbal debrief only.* The paper felt wrong in his hands, the way a knife feels wrong when you pick it up without meaning to. He had read it eleven times since leaving the anchorage at Port Moresby, and each reading had peeled away another layer of what was *not* being said.

Nobody had told him what the materiel was. Nobody had told him why a hydrographic survey vessel suddenly needed a naval lieutenant, two armed petty officers, and a sealed courier case. Nobody had told him why the Director of Naval Intelligence had received him personally — a man Voss had met exactly once before, across a table, for ninety seconds — or why that man had kept his eyes on the window while saying, in a voice as flat as a tide chart: *"Whatever it is, Lieutenant, it predates the current government, and it embarrasses three governments, and if it surfaces, men very much senior to me will be ruined, and men considerably less senior than you will be dead. Bring it back quietly. Quietly, do you understand? If there is noise — if there is so much as a rumor — the noise will be attributed to you."*

*Predates the current government.* Voss had turned that phrase over all night in his bunk while the launch drummed north through the swell. A wreck, then. Something old. But old things did not frighten directors of intelligence. Old things were dust and ballast and fish habitat. What frightened men like that was not age but *provenance* — a name, a seal, a signature; proof that somebody had done something, and that somebody else had signed off on it, and that the ink had dried and been filed away and forgotten by everyone except the men whose careers rested on the forgetting.

And now the trail led here, to a salvage diver named Quintana, who had pulled something out of the silt four days ago and had not reported it, because salvage divers never reported anything until they were good and ready, and sometimes not even then.

Orsen Quintana. He said the name to himself the way a man touches a scar to check whether it still hurts. It still hurt.

* * *

The pier at the salvage camp was a joke of a structure — ironbark piles gone soft as cheese, decking patched with planks from three different wrecks — but it held the launch's fenders, and Voss stepped ashore onto it at 0651 with Prewitt trailing him like an anxious shadow. The camp smelled of diesel, tar, and drying fish. A dog regarded them from atop an upturned dinghy with the professional indifference of a customs officer.

Three men were working on a barge fifty meters down the beach, and they stopped working when the naval party arrived, which told Voss everything about how news traveled here. But the person he had come to see was not among them. She was at the far end of the pier, kneeling over an air compressor with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, her forearms black to the elbow with grease, and she did not look up when their boots rang on the planks. She knew. Of course she knew. She had probably heard the launch ten minutes ago and decided, with characteristic Quintana logic, that finishing the compressor job was more important than acknowledging the arrival of His Majesty's representative — or whatever this country's equivalent phrase happened to be this decade.

"Lieutenant Quintana," he said, and immediately regretted it. She wasn't a lieutenant. She had never been anything so tidy.

She sat back on her heels and looked up at him, and for one unguarded second her face did something complicated — surprise, yes, but underneath it something older and more dangerous, a kind of recognition that had nothing to do with the present moment. Then it closed, like a hatch. "Voss." She wiped her hands on a rag that made them dirtier rather than cleaner. "They give you a boat now. Congratulations."

"They gave me a boat with a crew of twelve and a fuel allocation I have to justify in triplicate. It's less fun than it sounds."

"I'd imagine." She stood. Up close she was thinner than he remembered, harder, the way driftwood is harder than living wood, and there was a new line between her brows that hadn't been there at twenty-six. Her eyes went past him to Prewitt, to the two petty officers loitering at the pier's landward end pretending to admire the view, and back. "You've brought friends."

"Crew. They're staying with the launch." He let that sit for a moment. "I'm alone. More or less."

"More or less is your national anthem, isn't it?" She tossed the rag onto the compressor. "What does the Coastal Survey want in the Fly delta? You lost a buoy?"

"Something like that." He smiled, and hated himself for how easily the smile came, how it still fit his face around her. Courtesy was the uniform he wore best, and both of them knew it was a uniform. "Actually, I'm here about salvage. A recovery. Four days ago, maybe five, something came up out of the river near the old wreck site — the one the charts mark as a hazard. Iron. Heavy. Sealed."

The line between her brows deepened by a millimeter. "Delta's full of iron. Half of it fell off barges, the other half fell off the war. You'll need to be more specific."

"A chest," Voss said. "Iron-strapped, brass-bound, roughly a meter long. And on the brass, a stamp. A seal."

The morning went very quiet around them. Out on the water a kingfisher hit the surface and came up with a silver flicker in its beak. Orsen Quintana did not move, did not blink, and Voss understood that he had just made a serious error, because he had shown her exactly how much he knew, and she was already filing that knowledge away in whatever relentless ledger she kept behind those grey-green eyes.

"What seal?" she said.

"That's rather the question, isn't it." He kept his voice light, conversational, two colleagues discussing weather. "The one on the chest you raised Tuesday night. I know it was Tuesday night because the tide tables say the site was only workable after midnight, and because the *Kestrel*'s skipper logged an unfamiliar dive light out at the hazard buoy, and because a man in Port Moresby who buys scrap brass got a phone call asking what such a fitting might fetch." He paused. "You see my problem, Orsen. Everyone saw the light. Nobody saw the chest. Which means either it doesn't exist, or it exists and someone is hiding it, and my orders require me to determine which."

"Your orders." She said it flatly, tasting it. "From the Coastal Survey Directorate. Which surveys coasts. With pencils."

"The coast has gotten interesting lately."

"Has it." She crossed her arms — grease-streaked forearms, deliberate, a working woman's posture, and also, he suspected, a barrier. "Dario. Why would a survey launch carry armed petty officers?"

He had prepared for this conversation on the flight north, rehearsed versions of it in the head of the launch's tiny shower, and every version had assumed she would be evasive, defensive, hostile. He had not prepared for her to be *direct*, because directness from Orsen had always meant one thing: she had already decided what she was going to do, and the conversation was merely the corridor she was walking down to get there.

"Prewitt," he called, without turning. "Check the mooring lines."

"Aye, sir." Boots receding down the pier.

When they were alone — truly alone, within the small acoustic island the river made around two voices — Voss took a breath and abandoned the script entirely. "There's a seal on that chest belonging to a country that hasn't existed since before you were born. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Not a defunct company. Not a colonial administration. A *nation*. Its archives were supposed to have been destroyed — officially destroyed, ceremonially destroyed, with photographers — at the end of the war. If a sealed diplomatic chest bearing its arms has come out of that river intact, then the destruction was a performance, and somewhere upstream there may be more, and there are people in three capitals who cannot sleep at night because of that possibility. People who have given me a document I'm not permitted to photograph, ordering me to recover this quietly, and telling me — explicitly, Orsen — that if there is noise, the noise becomes mine." He heard his own voice drop. "That's what I can tell you. What I can't tell you is what happens to whoever's holding it if I report that it's gone missing. And I genuinely don't know, which should frighten you more than anything I've said."

She was silent for a long moment. A barge horn sounded far down the channel, mournful and absurd.

"You've changed," she said finally. "You used to lie better. Or worse. One of the two."

"I used to be lying about smaller things."

"Were you?" Something moved through her face then, quick as a fish under ice — hurt, maybe, or its fossil record. "Eleven years, Dario. You could have sent a letter."

"I wrote forty." The truth came out of him before he could weigh it, and he watched it land, watched her take a half-step backward on the greasy planks as though he'd shoved her. "None of them were honest enough to send. That's not an excuse. It's just the inventory."

The silence between them filled up with water sounds, with the creak of the pier, with eleven years of unsent correspondence. Then Orsen shook her head slowly, like a swimmer clearing water from her ears.

"I don't have your chest," she said.

"But you know where it is."

"I know what I know." Her chin came up. "And here's what I know about you, Lieutenant: you came up that gangway hating this errand. Whatever's in your pocket — orders, that folded-oilcloth shape you keep touching like a rosary — you think it stinks. So ask yourself why they sent a man who thinks it stinks. Ask yourself what happens to *you* after the recovery. Because I've read enough drowned men's logs to know how stories like yours end, and the ending isn't a commendation."

It was his turn to be silent. She had found the wound in under a minute — the thing he had admitted to no one, not even himself in daylight: that he was expendable, that the directorate had chosen him not despite his history with Quintana but *because* of it, because a lieutenant with a personal connection could be disavowed more cheaply than a stranger. *If there is noise, the noise becomes yours.* And afterward, when the chest was safe in a vault and the noise needed burying, the burier became the buried.

"I'll find it," he said quietly. "With you or without you. That's not a threat, Orsen. It's arithmetic. This delta has maybe forty people in it, and by Friday every one of them will have been asked, and some of them will answer, because the next launch won't come with me on it. It'll come with men who were hired rather than commissioned, and they won't wipe their boots."

Something flickered in her expression — not fear, he realized. Calculation. She was weighing him, measuring the distance between what he was ordered to do and what he might actually do, and finding the measurement uncertain. That uncertainty was the only gift he had to offer her, and they both knew it.

"Then I hope you find your chest," she said, stepping back toward the compressor, dismissal in every line of her body. "I hope it's full of brass fittings and pre-war invoices. I hope you file your verbal debrief and go home and write one of those forty letters."

"Orsen—"

"The tide's turning, Lieutenant. Your launch draws too much water to linger." She knelt, picked up her wrench, and did not look at him again. "Mind the third pile coming off. It's rotten."

* * *

He watched her shrink in the launch's wake, a small figure on a rotten pier, and felt the old argument of the river begin again in his chest — *it is always trying to tell you something* — and understood, with the cold certainty of a man reading his own chart position against a rising storm, that she had the chest, that she would run, and that he had perhaps thirty-six hours before the hired men arrived and turned this delta into the noise his orders feared.

He also understood something else, something that curdled slowly in him through the afternoon as the launch beat south toward the patrol anchorage to file his first false-in-advance report: that his superiors had not sent him to recover a chest.

They had sent him to establish who else knew it existed.

And Orsen Quintana, kneeling at her compressor, had known it too — had seen it in his face before he'd seen it himself — which meant that by now she was already moving, and every hour he bought her with paperwork and plausible courses was an hour stolen outright from the service he had sworn to, spent gladly, on a woman who would never send him a letter either.

At 1900 he radioed the anchorage: *Site investigated. Salvage activity routine. Nothing of interest.*

Then he opened the chart drawer, found the pencil he used for positions he did not believe, and began, very carefully, to draw the wrong river.
