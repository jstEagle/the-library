## 4. What Tomas Knew

The chest sat on the floor of her shack like a sleeping animal.

Orsen had carried it inside before dawn, wrapped in her spare tarpaulin, hunched under its weight along the mud path from the jetty while the delta still smelled of night and diesel. It was iron, green with river rot at the corners, and heavy in a way that had nothing to do with metal — heavy the way a body is heavy, she thought, and then hated herself for thinking it. She set it down on the boards where she ate, where she mended nets, where she did everything a person does in a room barely larger than the chest itself, and she looked at it for a long time without touching it.

Outside, the Fly moved past in its brown, patient way, carrying silt from half a country toward the sea. Somewhere out there, patrol boats were cutting their white wakes through the channels, looking for what she already had. She could hear them sometimes at night — engines held low, searchlights sweeping the water like slow fingers.

She made coffee she didn't drink. Then she knelt, took out her pry bar, and went to work on the lock.

It fought her. Of course it did. The seal was wax over lead, stamped with that impossible crest — the crossed fern and the seven-pointed star of a nation that had existed for eleven years and then been erased so thoroughly that Orsen, who had grown up within sight of these waters, could not have said what its flag had looked like until she saw it pressed into the wax. The lead beneath was soft with age. She worked the bar with the patience she'd learned from ten thousand hours underwater, where haste kills and force breaks things you can't replace, and somewhere near dawn the hasp gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside: paper. Nothing but paper.

Bundles of it, oil-wrapped, tied with cord gone brittle as insect legs. She lifted one bundle into the grey light and unwrapped it with hands that were not quite steady. The pages were dense with typing — an old typewriter's uneven strike, some letters heavier than others, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood — and above the typing, in places, handwriting. Marginal notes in a script she didn't recognize. The language was not English, not anything she knew, though the structure of it was familiar enough that she understood she was looking at reports. Numbers. Dates. Lists.

Lists of names.

She turned page after page, and the names went on and on, arranged in columns, each column headed by a date, and after certain names came small marks — a dash, a cross, a symbol like a hook — that repeated with a regularity no accident produces. She counted forty names on the first page alone. There were hundreds of pages.

The light strengthened. The river birds started up outside, that raucous morning argument they always had, and Orsen realized she had been kneeling on the floorboards for two hours without moving, and that her knees ached, and that she was cold in a way the tropical morning could not explain.

At the bottom of the chest, beneath the bundles, lay something flat and stiff. A map, folded into eighths, sealed inside a second skin of wax. She almost broke it open then. Her thumbnail was actually against the fold when she stopped herself — because the map was addressed to someone who could read it, and she could not, and because a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like Lieutenant Dario Voss said, *You've opened the box, Quintana. Now everything that happens is yours.*

She put the map back. She rewrapped the documents. And then she sat down at her table, pushed the coffee aside, and began to work the way she worked best: slowly, methodically, alone.

* * *

She had three things to work with, and by midday she had laid them all out on the table like instruments on a tray.

First, the documents themselves. The typed language was foreign, but the reports were bureaucratic, and bureaucracy rhymes across every border on earth. She found the numbers first — tonnages, dates rendered in a familiar calendar, coordinates written in figures she could read plainly. One coordinate appeared again and again, always in the same context, always followed by the same cluster of symbols: 7° south, 141° east, give or take. She knew those waters. Every diver on the delta knew them. It was the stretch below the old mission bend where the channel scoured deep between clay banks — where the wreck lay, the wreck nobody named, the wreck the navy had cordoned off last month with buoys and silence.

Second, Tomas's letters. She kept them in a biscuit tin under her bunk, and she had not opened the tin in fourteen months, because opening it meant reading his last letter, and reading his last letter meant hearing his voice, and hearing his voice meant — well. She had been managing very well by not doing any of it.

She got the tin down now because the alternative was worse. The alternative was sitting in this room with four hundred pages of dead men's names and admitting that she did not know why her brother had died, and that she might be holding the answer in her hands and too much of a coward to look.

His letters were few. Tomas wrote the way he dived — sparingly, checking everything twice. He'd gone upstream in the spring of the year he died, working salvage runs out of Kiunga, sending back money and short cheerful notes full of fish and engine trouble and jokes about the missionaries' cooking. She read them all in order, fast, the way you rip off a bandage, saving the last one.

*Orse —* it began, in his cramped leftward slant, *found something big below the mission bend. Old wreck, pre-war iron, sits in thirty meters of soup. Navy's got it marked now so I'll go quiet about it, but there's brass on her with a stamp I don't know. Flag I've never seen. Ila Mero says leave it, and Ila Mero never says leave anything.*

*I'm going back down Thursday when the vis improves. If I find what I think is down there, we won't have to dive for anyone else's lost cargo again. We'll be square. Don't worry. You know me — I check everything twice.*

*— T.*

He had checked everything twice his whole life. That was the joke of it, the unbearable joke: Tomas Fairweather was the most careful man on the river, the one who tested his own air twice and his buddy's once more, the one who taught his little sister to count her kicks going down and coming up so she'd never lose her place in the dark. They had pulled him out of the water off the delta flats with his gear intact, his gauges fine, his weights on, drowned in six meters of calm water on a day with no current and no wind, and the coroner had written *misadventure* in a tired hand, and everyone had accepted it because accepting it was easier than the other thing.

Orsen had accepted it. For fourteen months she had accepted it, and told herself the river takes even the careful ones, especially the careful ones, because the river doesn't care.

Now she laid his letter beside the documents and read the phrases again. *Below the mission bend.* The coordinates in the reports pointed to the same reach of water. *A flag I've never seen.* The crest on the wax seal. *What I think is down there.* And the documents — she was surer of it now, working through the recurring shapes of the words — the documents were records of something done at that place, or hidden at that place, or sunk at that place, in the last year of a war whose name had been scrubbed from the maps along with the nation that fought it.

Her brother had found the wreck months before she had. He had found the brass fitting with the seal — or one like it; hers was sitting on her shelf right now, green with river rot, stamped with the fern and the star. He had written to her about it in a letter that passed through three post offices and two censors' desks, because everything on the river did. And five days after that letter was posted, Tomas Fairweather drowned in six meters of flat water with all his gear working.

Orsen sat very still. A gecko ticked somewhere in the wall. Down at the jetty someone was winching a boat, the cable singing its one note over and over.

*Don't worry,* his letter said. *You know me — I check everything twice.*

"I know," she said aloud, to the empty room, in a voice she didn't recognize. "I know you do."

* * *

The third thing arrived at dusk, and it nearly broke her.

She had spent the afternoon teaching herself the documents the only way she could — by pattern, by repetition, by the diver's trick of memorizing a wreck piece by piece until the pieces assemble into a shape you can navigate blind. The typed language yielded slowly. But the handwritten marginalia was different: fewer words, more urgent, a pen pressed hard enough to emboss the page. Someone had annotated these reports with feeling. And certain annotations recurred beside the marked names — beside the hooked symbol, the cross, the dash — a short phrase, four or five words, written again and again in that hard-pressed hand until it became as familiar to her as her own signature.

She copied it out phonetically on the back of a fuel chit. She sounded it aloud, syllable by syllable, the way Tomas had once taught her to sound out the markers on the charts before she could read them. It meant nothing to her. It was just a shape of sound, a knot of consonants her mouth kept fumbling.

Then, near dark, lighting her lamp and turning back through the bundles one last time, she found it in a different hand entirely — a looser hand, a younger one, written on a slip of paper tucked inside the last bundle like a bookmark. Not the annotation language. English. Six words in pencil, in handwriting she would have known anywhere on earth, in the dark, underwater, dead.

*They kept the lists. God forgive us.*

She knew the handwriting because she had learned her letters tracing it. Because every birthday card, every chart margin, every note pinned to the icebox of her childhood was in that same leftward slant with the looped capitals. Tomas had been here. Tomas had held these papers. His pencil had touched this page, and he had written those six words, and then he had folded the slip back into the bundle and closed the chest and — what? Carried it out? No. He'd gone back down Thursday, his letter said. He'd planned to raise it. Something had stopped him. Something had reached him between the writing of that letter and the Thursday dive, something that made him hide the chest instead of lifting it, tuck it deeper into the silt where the navy's sonar wouldn't sing against it, and write *Ila Mero says leave it* in a hand that shook if you looked closely enough at the downstrokes.

And then he had died.

Not misadventure. Not the river being the river. Tomas had found the lists, and the lists had found him, and six meters of calm water on a windless day had become the most convenient grave in the delta.

Orsen stood up so fast her chair went over backward. She stood in the middle of her shack with the lamp throwing her shadow huge against the tin wall, and she let it come — the grief, fourteen months of it, all the tears she hadn't shed at the funeral because someone had to sign the forms, all the nights she'd lain awake listening to the river and hating it, all the mornings she'd suited up and gone down and searched the silt around the wreck site for nothing, for nothing, for nothing, telling herself she was looking for his lost watch when she was really looking for a reason.

The reason was on her table. Four hundred pages of it. Drowned men in columns, and a hooked mark beside the ones who'd tried to speak, and her brother's pencil-slip tucked among them like a seed.

She thought of the stranger, Ila Mero, offering gold for the chest sight unseen. She thought of the navy buoys going in last month, unannounced, unexplained. She thought of Voss standing on the pier with his officer's courtesy stretched thin over something frightened. Three separate powers, all converging on a box of old paper — and none of them grieving, none of them angry, all of them afraid.

They were afraid of it. Good, she thought, and the thought had teeth. Let them be afraid. Tomas had checked everything twice, and what he'd found was worth killing for, which meant it was worth carrying, which meant somebody owed him a debt that interest alone would drown a nation.

She righted the chair. She repacked the chest, all but one bundle — the one with his slip of paper — which she read again, and again, until the six words were carved into her. *They kept the lists. God forgive us.* Then she blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the river, and for the first time since they'd pulled him from the shallows, the sound of it didn't feel like an accusation. It felt like a road.

There was a knock at her door.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, unhurried — the knock of someone who expected to be answered and had never in his life been refused. Through the tin wall she could see the shape of a launch riding low at her mooring, navy-grey, its running lights burning steady and illegal this far up the back channel.

Orsen slid Tomas's slip of paper into her shirt, put her palm flat against the cold iron of the chest, and opened the door onto the lamplight and the face of the last man on earth she wanted to see.

"Lieutenant Voss," she said. "You're a long way from your buoys."
