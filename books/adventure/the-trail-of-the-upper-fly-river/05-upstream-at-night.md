## 5. Upstream at Night

The chest sat in the bottom of Ila Mero's barge like a sleeping animal, and Orsen Quintana could not stop looking at it.

She had wrapped it in a tarpaulin against the spray, weighted the corners with sandbags, and then — because she could not help herself — lifted one corner of the canvas to look again at the wax seal on the map inside, that pressed emblem of a country that had stopped existing before she was born. The seal was a bird with its wings folded, standing on water. A heron, maybe. Or a cormorant after a dive. She didn't know. Nobody she'd asked knew, and the ones who might have known had gone quiet in ways that told her everything.

"You'll wear out the tarp," Ila said from the wheelhouse.

She let the canvas fall. "You'll wear out your patience."

"I have none to begin with." He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a face like river clay — features that seemed always about to be reshaped by whatever current passed over them. Right now the current was impatience. "Tide turns in forty minutes. The navy launch has been circling the fuel depot since sundown. You want to explain to me why we're not moving?"

"Because they're watching the channel markers, not the depot. If we leave now we leave into their searchlight." She climbed into the wheelhouse beside him and took the binoculars off the hook. Downriver, past the black humps of moored barges and the sodium glare of the cannery, a white shape moved against the darker water. Running lights off. That alone told her what kind of search it was. Honest patrols ran lit; this one wanted to be found only when it chose to be.

Voss's launch. She would have known its silhouette anywhere, even unlit, even blind. She had helped him paint that hull once, three years and another life ago, both of them stripped to the waist in the delta heat, arguing about whether blue or green hid better against the Fly's brown water. Green, he'd insisted. She'd said it didn't matter, that the river gave up nothing it didn't want to give, not boats, not bodies, not secrets. He'd laughed and said that was the most romantic thing she'd ever said to him, which said more about their romance than she liked to remember.

Now he was hunting her, politely, with paperwork and questions and that voice like warm oil, and somewhere behind him were men who did not bother being polite at all.

"They know you have it," Ila said.

"They know someone has it. They don't know it's me yet."

"The diver who pulled the fitting with the dead flag on it? The one salvage office logged under her own name because she's too honest to file false reports?" He shook his head slowly. "Orsen. They've known for days."

She lowered the binoculars. The launch had turned, unhurried, toward the cannery light. Toward them, or near enough.

"All right," she said. "Now we move."

* * *

They went out on the ebb, engine idled down to a mutter, running dark between the moored hulks where the navy's bigger boats couldn't follow without scraping paint. Ila's barge was called the *Kindly Tern*, which was a lie of a name for a flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted cargo scow with an engine older than either of them, but it drew less than a meter loaded, and that was the whole reason Orsen had come to him. The upper Fly beyond the last government post wasn't a river so much as a rumor with rapids. Anything with a keel died there. The *Tern* would survive it, barely, the way Ila survived most things — by having no pride and no depth.

Orsen stood at the bow with a pole while Ila steered by feel, calling depths to each other in low voices, sliding past the cannery, past the fuel depot, past the chapel with its crooked cross silhouetted against the town's few lights. The launch was a hundred meters off their starboard quarter, close enough that she could hear the idle of its engines under the night sounds, close enough that if anyone aboard swept a torch across the water at the wrong moment —

A torch swept across the water.

Orsen dropped flat behind the chest without deciding to. Her body made the choice the way it made all its best choices, fast and below thought, and she lay with her cheek against the tarpaulin smelling of rust and old iron and something faintly sweet, like wax, like the seal itself, while the beam crawled along the surface toward them, touched the stern of a moored lighter ten meters away, held there, and moved on.

Her heart was going like a pump with a bad valve. She stayed down anyway, counting breaths, feeling through the deck plates the tremor of the engine, Ila's bare feet shifting at the wheel. Above her the sky was overcast and moonless, the clouds hanging low enough to touch, and the river breathed its usual smell of silt and rot and green things growing too fast.

When she finally rose, Ila was watching her with an expression she couldn't read in the dark.

"You dove on that wreck four times before you found the chest," he said quietly. "Four times, in water full of crocs and patrol traffic. Why?"

Because Tomas had dived it first. Because his last letter had named the coordinates in a joke he thought she wouldn't understand, and she had understood, and she had needed to know what he'd found down there in the dark before the river closed over him. But she said none of that.

"Curiosity," she said.

"Curiosity is what gets people buried." He turned back to the wheel. "Hold on. We're coming to the cut."

* * *

The cut was where the delta braided into a dozen channels and came back together, a maze of mudbanks and drowned forest that no chart bothered mapping properly because no honest traffic went there. This was the part of the escape Orsen had planned for three sleepless nights, and it went the way such plans go — mostly right, immediately wrong in one detail that nearly killed them.

The detail was a log boom, half-sunk, strung across the second channel by some timber company years dead. Ila saw it late, a black line where black should have been water, and threw the wheel over, and the *Tern* slewed sideways with a groan of displaced load, and Orsen felt the whole barge shudder as something scraped along its belly — mud, thank God, mud and not timber — and then they were through, listing slightly, engine coughing, in a channel narrow enough that the overhanging trees brushed both rails at once.

For a long time afterward neither of them spoke. The barge crept upstream between walls of mangrove and nipa palm, and the town's glow fell away behind them, and the darkness ahead became total, the kind of darkness that has weight.

"There's a lantern under the chart drawer," Ila said at last. "Light it if you want. We're past the patrols now. Nothing official comes up here."

She found the lantern but didn't light it. "Nothing official comes up here. Then what does?"

"Fever boats. Smugglers. Mission supplies, sometimes." A pause. "Ghosts, if you believe the people along the banks. They say the drowned walk the shallows above the gorge. Villages went under when they raised the dam water, whole villages, and the people who got out say their dead never left."

"Do you believe them?"

Ila was quiet long enough that she thought he hadn't heard. Then: "I believe the river keeps what it's given. Same as you do." He glanced at her. "That's why you're really going upstream, isn't it? Not the gold. Not even the papers. You're going to find what the river kept of your brother."

She turned away and looked at the black water sliding past, and did not answer, because the answer was yes, and yes was a wound she didn't want pressed.

* * *

They ran the first rapids two hours before dawn, and it was nothing like she had imagined and worse than she had feared.

She had imagined noise. What she got instead was a rising hum in the hull, a vibration in the deck plates that climbed through her boots into her teeth, and then the channel bent and the darkness ahead turned a different shade of black — a moving black, a boiling black — and Ila said, "Sit down and hold the chest," in a voice stripped of everything except function, and the river took them.

The *Tern* did not ride the rapids so much as get argued through them. Water came over the bow in cold slaps. The pole tore out of Orsen's hands. She fell onto the chest and wrapped her arms around it and felt it shift under her like something trying to wake, and Ila fought the wheel with both fists, talking to the engine, talking to the river, a low continuous stream of words she couldn't make out — prayer or profanity, probably both, probably the same thing out here. Once the barge struck something soft and huge and yielding, a submerged bank or a floating island of vegetation, and lurched, and for one endless second Orsen felt the whole world tilt toward lying on its side, and then the current changed its mind and they slid free.

Then it was over. The hum faded. The water smoothed. Somewhere ahead a bird cried out once, a raw descending note, and was silent.

Orsen realized she was laughing. Small, involuntary, hysterical laughter, muffled against the tarpaulin. She pressed her mouth shut and made herself stop.

"You all right?" Ila called.

"No," she said honestly.

"Good. Means you're paying attention." He eased the throttle forward. "Two more sets like that before the gorge. Then the water's deep and slow all the way to Kiunga Bend, if we live."

She sat up. Her hands were shaking, and she watched them shake with a kind of detached interest, the way she'd once watched her own hands shake in a decompression chamber after a dive that had gone forty minutes too long. Tomas had been outside the chamber glass then too, making faces at her, mouthing jokes, staying until she smiled. He had always stayed until she smiled.

He was not here now. He was under the delta somewhere, or upriver, wherever the wreck had finally given him up, and the chest under her arms was the closest thing to him she had left, and three governments wanted it, and one of those governments had sent the man whose face she used to see every time she closed her eyes.

"Dario will figure out the cut by morning," she said. "He'll know I went upstream. He grew up on this river too."

"He did," Ila agreed. "Which means he also knows what the upper Fly does to boats. He can't take a naval launch past the gorge. He'll have to charter, hire, beg. That buys us days."

"And after the days?"

"After the days," Ila said, "we'd better be ghosts ourselves."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and dripping, the mist so thick on the water that the banks dissolved and the barge seemed to hang in a white void, engine muttering, the wet rope fenders creaking. Orsen brewed tea on a single burner and carried a cup to Ila at the wheel, and for a while they just drank it and watched the void drift past.

It should have felt like freedom. That was the strange thing she kept turning over as the mist thinned and the country began to assemble itself around them — the steep green shoulders of hills, the sudden verticality of the gorge walls ahead, birds lifting in shrieking flocks off the water. She had spent six years since Tomas's death keeping herself in motion, dive after dive, contract after contract, never unpacking more than one bag's worth of her life into any room, and now she had finally done the thing the restlessness had always been pointing at: she had run. Cut every mooring. Left the delta, the salvage office, the rented room with its one bag still packed, left the whole small tolerable architecture of her days.

And it felt nothing like freedom. It felt like exile. It felt like the moment in a dive when you leave the lighted water and drop over the edge of the shelf and the blue goes black beneath you and there is no bottom and no wall and only your own discipline holding you to the line home. She had loved that moment once. She had built a life on chasing it. Now she understood that she had never once chosen it — that the choosing had always been done for her, by grief, by the need to be somewhere the silence was different from the silence at home.

Upstream was just more silence. Deeper silence. And somewhere in it, answers, or the end of needing them.

"Kiunga Bend by tomorrow night," Ila said, reading her mind or her face. "There's a mission station there. Old sister, German or Swiss or something, been up there fifty years. If anyone on this river knows what that seal means, it'll be her." He drained his tea. "Fair warning — stopping anywhere costs somebody. That's how it works when powerful men are frightened. The boat that stops becomes the village's problem. You sure you want to stop?"

Orsen looked at the chest under its tarpaulin, at the mist burning off the water, at the gorge walls rising ahead of them like the sides of a wound in the earth. She thought of Tomas's letter, of the phrases in it that matched phrases in the documents, of the word he'd written that she had not been able to translate and had copied out and carried against her skin for two years: *witnesses*. There were witnesses upstream. Living men who had seen what the papers described. Which meant that up there, in the high country where the river was born, there were people who had been carrying this secret longer than she had been alive — and every day she delayed, every mile she traveled toward them, she dragged the fear of three governments behind her like a net.

"No," she said. "But I'm going to anyway."

Ila nodded, unsurprised, and reached for the throttle.

That was when they both heard it — faint, downstream, threading up through the gorge behind them like smoke: the sound of an engine. Not the *Tern*'s. Not one engine. Several, running hard, and under them, thin and unmistakable across the water, the crackle of a radio transmission.

Ila cut the throttle. The barge drifted. Orsen picked up the binoculars and looked back down the long gray corridor of the river they had climbed all night.

Far off, at the limit of vision, where the mist still hung, something was coming up the gorge. Something that had no business being able to make this stretch of water move that fast.

"Orsen," Ila said quietly, "tell me that's not the navy."

She kept the glasses steady. The shape resolved, lost itself in mist, resolved again — shallow, low-slung, twin engines, running lights burning openly now, boldly, a vessel that wanted to be seen.

It wasn't the navy. The navy painted green.

"That," she said, lowering the binoculars, "is a consortium boat. And it's between us and Kiunga."
