## 6. The Drowned Village

The river told them they were close before they saw anything at all.

Orsen felt it through the deck plates first — a change in the water's voice, a hollowness beneath the usual murmur, as though the Fly were speaking over its own drowned dead. Then came the smell: rot and river-weed and something older underneath, the sweetish stink of timber that had been dying slowly for years in water that never let it rest.

Ila cut the engine and let the barge drift.

"Koromai," he said quietly, and the name in his mouth was not a place so much as a wound. "They flooded it in 'sixty-one. Dam project, up near the gorge. The government said the water would rise slow, that people would have time." He spat over the rail. "Water doesn't rise slow. Water rises the way grief rises. All at once, and then it never goes down."

Orsen stood at the rail and looked at what remained.

The village was there and not there. The tops of trees broke the surface in ragged clusters, their crowns bleached bone-white by years of drowning, branches stripped and skeletal, reaching up like the arms of men who had gone down waving. Between them, at intervals, the roofs of houses showed — or rather, the suggestion of roofs, green with algae, sagging, some collapsed into the brown water like the ribcages of enormous animals. A church spire leaned at an angle that hurt to look at, its cross long since rotted away, and around it the water lay flat and patient and utterly without conscience.

And above it all — above the floodline, on the ridge where the water would never reach — the living village stood on stilts. Dozens of houses on ironwood legs, thatched and weathered, connected by walkways of split bamboo that creaked in the current's breath. Laundry hung between posts. Smoke rose from cooking fires. Children ran the walkways on bare feet, and their noise — their bright, ordinary, unafraid noise — carried across the water and made the drowned village below seem, somehow, even more silent.

"They moved up the hill," Ila said. "But they didn't leave. You don't leave your dead, and their dead are all down there. Every garden, every grave, every house where somebody was born. All of it under." He nodded at the iron chest lashed beneath the tarp amidships. "You're carrying a dead nation's papers through the middle of a living graveyard, Orsen. Best you understand that."

She said nothing. There was nothing to say that the water hadn't already said.

They moored at the ridge landing as the light went long and copper, tying off to a post worn smooth by decades of hands, and Orsen climbed the ladder to the walkway with the feeling of walking into a place that had been waiting for her. Not for her specifically — she had no such vanity left — but waiting the way a wound waits for a hand.

An old man met her at the first house. He was small and straight-backed, with a face like river stone and eyes that had the particular flatness she had seen only in people who had watched something irreversible happen. He looked at her, then at the barge below, then back at her, and said in trade English:

"You came up from the delta. From the wreck."

It was not a question. Orsen's hand moved without her willing it, an inch toward her belt, and stopped.

"How do you know the wreck?"

"Because we watched them sink her." He said it the way a man reports weather. "The war was ending. They came down the river at night, burning their own papers in the galley, and in the morning the hull was on the bottom and the flag they flew was a flag nobody flies anymore. My father fished the bodies out for three days. He would not let us eat fish from that stretch of water for the rest of his life." He extended a hand, gnarled and dry as pandanus root. "I am Wariu. You will eat with us, because you are thin and the river is long. And then you will show me what is in that box, because I already know, and I need to know if I am right."

* * *

They fed her fish and sago and greens bitter as regret, on a veranda whose floorboards were polished by generations of feet, and the whole time the children watched her from the shadows of the doorway with the frank, unblinking stare of children who had learned early that strangers bring change and change brings water.

Wariu's granddaughter brought a lamp. And when the plates were cleared, Orsen went down the ladder and up again with the chest in her arms, and set it on the veranda boards between them, and the whole living village seemed to lean in around the light.

She had not opened it since the night Ila made his offer. She had told herself it was caution. Standing there, with the old man's eyes on her and the drowned houses black against the last light below, she admitted the truth: she had not opened it because she was afraid of what it would make her. Some cargo changes the carrier. She had felt it from the first moment her gloves closed on the iron, cold through the neoprene, colder than the river.

The lock was old work but honest work, and her picks were good, and it opened with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: documents. Bundles of them. Typed pages in a language she could not read, though she recognized the letterforms now from the delta, from her long nights with the dictionary Ila had produced from a locked drawer as though he'd been waiting years for someone to ask. Photographs. And beneath everything, a ledger with a cover stamped in gold leaf that had not yet entirely flaked away — a crest, a wreath, a star.

Wariu looked at the crest for a long time.

Then he did something Orsen had not expected. He took off his hat — a soft felt thing, sweat-dark, older than she was — and held it against his chest, and bowed his head, and the old man's mouth moved in silence, and around the veranda, one by one, the others who had gathered did the same. Even the children, who could not have known why, understood that they should.

"That is the flag of the Republic of Vessania," Wariu said at last, replacing his hat. "Dead twenty years. You know what happened to Vessania?"

"It's not on any map."

"No. It is not on any map because it was removed from all of them at once, by agreement, by men who signed the agreement in a room with air conditioning." His voice did not rise. It went flatter, which was worse. "My mother's people traded with Vessania up the tributaries for a generation. Then the war came, and Vessania was on the wrong side of a line drawn by other people, and at the end of the war the soldiers came down this river, and what they did at the mission stations and the villages that sheltered them —" he stopped, and his jaw worked, and he started again — "what they did is in that box. I cannot read their letters. But I know what my father saw. I know what he carried in him until the day he died, and he carried it because there was nowhere in the world to put it down."

Orsen looked down at the photographs. She had seen only the top one, and the top one was enough: a line of people on a riverbank, and soldiers, and the composition of the image told her everything the caption — in that dead language — might have said. She closed the ledger.

"Where did the soldiers go afterward?" she asked, and heard her own voice as if from underwater.

"Scattered. Some died. Some are old men in towns with new names, living under new flags." Wariu's eyes came up and fixed on hers, and they were not angry eyes, and that was the terrible thing. They were tired eyes. "And some are upriver. At the source country. Where your box wants to go."

"How do you know where my box wants to go?"

The old man almost smiled. "Because everything that goes up this river at night, running, with a navy launch behind it, is going to the source. The source is where people take things they cannot put down." He leaned forward and laid one finger on the chest's open lid, gently, the way a man touches a coffin. "So now I will ask you the thing I have to ask, and you will hear me, because I am old and I buried my father with his secret and I know the weight of it.

"Burn it."

The word went through her like cold water through cloth.

"Everything in it is already true," Wariu said. "Burning it will not un-kill anyone. But that paper is a reason, Orsen Quintana. Paper is a reason men with helicopters give themselves. Right now the people who made that atrocity are old and scattered and the world has agreed to forget them, and my people are alive, fishing, planting, marrying, on this ridge above their drowned homes. The moment that box moves upriver, the forgetting ends. The moment someone powerful learns the rest of the archive exists at the source, every man, woman and child between here and there becomes a door someone will kick open to get at it. You are not carrying the truth, girl. You are carrying the fuse."

Orsen sat with it. The lamp hissed. Below, the river moved over the rooftops without a sound, without a conscience, and somewhere under that brown water were gardens where these people's grandmothers had grown taro, and graves where their dead lay in the dark, and a church where they had been married, and none of it — none of it — had ever been asked its permission.

She thought of Tomas.

She thought of him the way she always did now, not as a whole man but in pieces: his laugh, the scar on his knuckles from a winch cable, the last letter she had read forty times until the words had worn grooves in her. *They're hiding something at the head of this river, Orse. Something that would crack the country open if it got out. I'm going back down for one more look.* And then the water. And then the silence. And then the navy man at her door with his kind, terrible courtesy, telling her the river had taken him, as if the river were a thing that acted alone.

If she burned the chest, Tomas's last dive became a boy's foolish errand. If she burned it, whatever he had found, whatever had killed him, stayed buried under the same water that had drowned this village — and the water kept everything, and gave back nothing, and no one would ever have to answer for anything ever again.

"I can't," she said.

"I know," Wariu said. "I could see it in you from the ladder. You have the same face my father had." He sighed, and it was the sigh of a man setting down something heavy on the far side of a river he cannot cross. "Then hear the rest, and take it as payment for the meal. There is a woman up at the bend — Kiunga Bend, the mission there, an old sister, Anselm, who has been in this country fifty years and reads dead languages the way you read water. If anyone can tell you what your box truly says, it is her. Go to her. And go knowing this—" he gripped her wrist, and his hand was astonishingly strong — "every house you stop at from here to the source, someone in it will pay for your stopping. We have chosen to pay. That is our right. But do not pretend, afterward, that you did not know."

* * *

They cast off in the grey hour before dawn, when the river was all mist and the drowned trees rose through it like the masts of a fleet sunk in some ancient war.

Orsen stood at the rail and watched the stilt village recede, the cooking fires going out one by one, the walkways emptying, and at the very end, small in the fog, she saw Wariu standing at the landing post, hat in hand, watching her go. He did not wave. He simply watched, the way you watch a thing you have released and cannot recall.

She looked down at the water sliding past the hull and thought: *somewhere under there is a house with a cradle in it.* She thought: *somewhere under there is a grave with flowers on it, planted by a hand that is itself under the water now.* She thought: *I am carrying a fuse through a house full of sleeping people, and I have decided that what's at the end of the fuse matters more than the house.*

She did not know if that was courage. She suspected it was something colder, something that wore courage's clothes.

Behind them, far downstream, faint as a heartbeat, came the sound she had been listening for since the delta and dreading every hour since: an engine. Deep-throated, purposeful, running against the current with the particular patience of a thing that has been sent.

Ila heard it too. He appeared beside her at the rail, chewing on the unlit end of a dead cigar, and looked back into the fog with the professional calm of a man appraising a salvage job gone bad.

"Navy launch," he said. "Two, maybe three hours behind us. They've been running dark until now." He glanced sideways at her. "Wariu give you the speech?"

Orsen nodded.

"And you're still going."

"I'm still going."

He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd already entered in his ledger. "Then we'd best make the bend before light's full up," he said, and turned for the wheel, "because the sister at the mission keeps the only radio on this stretch of the river — and I'd very much like to know who she's been talking to."
