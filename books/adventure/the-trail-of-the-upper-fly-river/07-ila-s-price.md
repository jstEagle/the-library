## 7. Ila's Price

Ila Mero had learned long ago that the river forgave nothing and forgot less, and that a man who thought otherwise was either very young or already dead. He stood at the wheel of the *Kanawi Rose* with one hand on the brass and the other around a tin mug of cold tea, watching the delta fall away behind them in the dark, and he counted — as he always counted — the things he had sold.

The list was short. That was the cruelty of it. A man could ruin a hundred lives with a single sentence, if the sentence was good enough.

*Wreck at the second bend below Sadowa Point. Navy-cordoned. Something heavy in the silt.*

He had spoken those words eleven days ago to a man with a mining consortium's card and a consortium's smile, in the back room of a tavern that smelled of diesel and dried fish, and he had taken payment in gold weight because paper could be traced and gold only weighed. Three days later two divers who had been working the same stretch of river — brothers, locals, men who waved when his barge passed — went down on a routine salvage and did not come up. Their air lines had been cut clean. The river gave back one of them two tides later, face-down among the reeds, and Ila had stood on his deck and looked at the swollen shape of him and understood exactly what his sentence had purchased.

He had told himself it might have been anything. Divers died. The Fly killed careless men every season, took them into its brown throat and kept them without apology. But the air lines had been cut, and cut lines do not happen by current or chance, and Ila Mero was many things but he had never been a liar — not to others, and, after that night, never again to himself.

So now there was a woman asleep in the wheelhouse behind him, curled on the bench under his spare coat with the iron chest locked in the hold beneath her feet, and the patrol boats of Lieutenant Dario Voss somewhere astern, and the whole dark river ahead of him running fast toward the gorges, and Ila stood at his own wheel like a man steering toward a debt he could not pay and would not stop trying to.

* * *

She woke before dawn, the way river people did, coming up out of sleep all at once like something surfacing. He heard her move behind him, heard her stop when she saw him standing watch, and then she came forward and leaned on the rail beside the wheel without speaking, looking back the way they'd come.

The delta at first light was a country of smoke and silver. Mist lay on the water in long unbroken sheets, and the mangroves stood out of it black as burnt matches, and here and there a fishing light still burned, small and patient, afloat on nothing.

"You didn't sleep," Orsen said.

"Old men don't need much."

"You're not old."

"I'm old enough to know what I am." He said it lightly, the way he said most true things, and she turned to look at him, and he felt the look settle on him like a hand.

Orsen Quintana had a face that did not waste anything. He'd watched her work the wreck for two days before he ever spoke to her — watched her dive alone where three men wouldn't, watched her come up with her eyes already reading the water for the next thing — and he had thought, with the professional detachment he applied to everything: *there is a woman who has already survived the worst day of her life, and is waiting to see if there are worse ones.* Then he'd learned about the brother, and understood that the worst day had already happened, and that she carried it the way some people carried ballast — low, permanent, the thing that kept her upright in rough water.

"The chest," she said now. "You offered me triple its weight in gold. Sight unseen. Nobody does that."

"No."

"So either you knew what was in it, or you were paid to make sure nobody else found out." Her voice was level. Not accusing — worse than accusing. Weighing. "Which is it, Ila?"

He drank his cold tea. The river ran. Somewhere ahead, hidden in the mist, the channel began its long climb toward the highlands, and every mile of it was water that had drowned somebody.

"Both," he said. "Neither. Sit down, Orsen. It's a long story and you'll want to be sitting for the parts that are your business."

* * *

He told it plainly, because plainness was all the honesty he had left.

Two years ago, before the navy cordons, before the seal on the brass fitting meant anything to anyone, Ila had run cargo and rumor both, the way every salvager on the Fly did. Information moved up and down this river like silt — who was diving what, which company had bought which concession, where the government survey boats anchored at night. He'd sold it all his life, small pieces, harmless pieces, the grease that kept the whole economy turning. Everyone did it. He had never once believed it mattered.

Then a man came asking about a wreck at the second bend below Sadowa Point. Not treasure — the man made that clear, made it almost boring, the way professionals do. Records. Paper. A wartime archive, maybe, sunk with a transport nobody had ever bothered to name. The man paid triple what the information was worth, and Ila took it, and told himself the man was a crank chasing ghosts.

"And then?" Orsen said.

"Then the brothers Toma and Esekiel went diving at the second bend." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he was carrying something too heavy to let it shift. "They were good boys. Better divers than me at that age. They'd found something down there — I don't know what, a fitting, a plate, something with a stamp on it — and they were talking about it at the landing, the way young men talk, loud, proud. Two nights later their lines were cut."

Orsen had gone very still. Stillness, he had noticed, was her first response to everything — the diver's instinct, conserve, assess, descend.

"You think whoever paid me followed the information downstream until it reached them," Ila said. "I think so too. I've had two years to think so. And I will tell you the truth I've never told anyone, Orsen, which is that I sat in that tavern with the gold in my pocket and I heard myself say the words *second bend below Sadowa Point*, and some part of me — some old, tired, honest part — knew. Knew the words were heavier than they sounded. And I said them anyway, because the gold was real and the danger was a story, and I chose the thing I could hold over the thing I could imagine."

The mist was burning off now. The sun came up the color of brass, and the water turned from silver to the brown-green of strong tea, and the banks began to slide past — reed beds, a crocodile log that wasn't a log, the far hills blue and getting bluer.

"Tomas," Orsen said.

It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway, because she deserved the whole weight of it.

"He dove that wreck. Months before he died. Whatever he pulled off it, whatever he figured out — it's why he's dead. And the same sentence that bought his death came out of my mouth twice, Orsen. Once for the consortium, once for you, the night I offered you gold for that chest. I've been running this river ever since wondering which time the river would collect for."

* * *

She didn't say anything for a long while. She stood at the rail with her hands wrapped around it, knuckles pale, looking at the water as though it might give the answer up the way it gave up wrecks — slowly, in silt and pieces, requiring a person to go down into the cold and dark after it.

When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet and terrible.

"Why tell me now? Why not the night you made the offer?"

"Because that night you'd have thrown the chest in the river and gone home, and home is where they'd have found you eventually, and I'd have watched it happen knowing I steered you onto the reef." He set down the mug. "And because you deserved to choose with open eyes. That's the only thing I have left to give anyone — open eyes. So here they are."

He turned to face her fully, and made himself say the rest, the part that mattered, the part that had kept him awake at the wheel all night.

"Listen to me. Everyone who helps you becomes a target. Not might become — becomes. The brothers helped no one; they just talked near the wrong water, and they're dead. Sister Anselm at Kiunga Bend will feed us and hide us because that's what she's done for forty years, and if Voss or the consortium trace us to her door, they will burn that mission down with her in it, and it will be my sentence and your chest that lit the match. The cartographer's family upstream, whoever they are — same. Every stilt village we pass, every kid who waves at this barge, every man who sells us fuel. You are carrying something worth killing for, and killing spreads outward from a thing like that the way oil spreads on water. It doesn't ask permission. It just finds everyone touching you."

"Then why—" she started.

"Because I'm going to keep going anyway." He said it before she could, because it needed saying by him, owed by him. "That's the price and I'm paying it. Not because I'm brave — I'm the farthest thing from brave, Orsen, I'm a fat old trader who sold a death sentence for gold weight. I keep going because the debt's already mine. Those brothers are mine. Your Tomas is mine, near enough. If I turn this barge around now, the men who cut those air lines don't stop existing; they just stop having to chase us. There's no safe harbor back there. There's only the place where I get to watch it happen instead of being near it." He looked out at the river, at the hills climbing blue against the sky. "A man can drown in shallow water too. I learned that late. But I learned it."

* * *

Orsen crossed the deck and put her hand flat on the wheel beside his, over the brass, the way divers sealed a pact — palm to metal, no shaking, nothing ceremonial.

"They cut the lines," she said. "Tomas's too?"

"If he dove that wreck, and he did — yes. I'd bet this boat on it."

"Then we're past choosing, aren't we." It wasn't bitter. It was almost gentle, which frightened him more than anger would have. "You sold the information. Someone else held the knife. But the knife came down the river, Ila, and it's still coming, and the only direction left is upstream — toward whatever Tomas found, toward whoever wrote what's in that chest. Either we carry it to the source or we feed it to the current and spend the rest of our lives listening for engines."

"That's the arithmetic," he agreed. "Ugly sums."

"My brother used to say the river keeps what's true and gives back the rest." She smiled, briefly, and it was a wound of a smile. "Let's find out if it's true."

He laughed — short, surprised, real — the first laugh he'd had in two years that didn't taste like coin. Then he rang the engine bell twice and brought the *Kanawi Rose* around the bend into the rising sun, and the gorge country opened ahead of them like a book whose first page was already bleeding.

It was mid-morning, the mist long burned away, when he raised the binoculars out of habit and swept the water behind them — and stopped.

Far back, where the delta narrowed to a single braided channel, a white hull sat motionless against the green. Too far to read a name. Close enough to read an intention.

It wasn't moving.

It was waiting.

Ila lowered the glasses slowly and did not say anything yet, because some news kept better in silence — and because he wanted one more hour of the river being just a river, before he had to tell Orsen Quintana that the price had come due early, and that it was sailing up behind them wearing the navy's white and Dario Voss's face.
