## 8. Gorge of Whispers

The gorge announced itself before they entered it, the way grief announces itself before you admit it's there.

Lieutenant Dario Voss stood on the flying bridge of *Kestrel* and watched the walls close in. The Fly had been a kilometer wide at the delta, brown and lazy as a sleeping animal, but here it narrowed to a stone throat barely sixty meters across, and the water ran green-black and fast beneath walls of limestone so sheer that the jungle could not hold them. Orchids clung to the crevices like small pale hands. Somewhere above, hidden in the canopy, birds were making sounds he had no names for — long descending cries, like something being lowered on a rope into a well.

"Contact bearing zero-four-zero," Ensign Rau said from the helm. "Small craft, shallow draft, moving upstream at speed."

Voss lifted his binoculars even though he didn't need them. He'd known for two days where Ila Mero's barge was; he could have taken her at any of three bends below Kiunga. He had chosen instead to log fuel stops, to file position reports, to let the river do what rivers do, which is make every decision for you until only one remains.

The barge sat low in the current, riding light, its deck stacked with salvage drums and tarpaulin. And there — at the rail, one hand shading her eyes against the morning glare — Orsen Quintana.

Even at four hundred meters, even through heat shimmer and spray, he knew the set of her shoulders. He had watched those shoulders dive off a pier in Darwin at nineteen, watched them shake once, only once, at Tomas's funeral, watched them turn away from him in a doorway in Port Moresby with a finality he had spent three years pretending was relief.

"Range closing," Rau said. "They'll be in the narrows in six minutes."

"I see it."

"Sir?" Rau hesitated. "Do we want the loudhailer now or—"

"When we're inside." Voss lowered the binoculars. "Not before. Sound carries strangely in gorges. I don't want them panicking in the rapids and putting themselves on the rocks. Dead salvagers can't tell us where the chest went."

It was a reasonable lie. It was even mostly true. But he heard his own voice say it the way you hear a recording of yourself — someone with your timbre saying words your mouth chose without consulting you.

* * *

Three years ago, in another life, Dario Voss had known exactly who he was.

He had been a junior hydrographic officer then, seconded to a survey run up the Fly, and she had been the salvage contractor whose charts were better than the navy's own. The first week he'd corrected her soundings and she'd laughed at him — not cruelly, but with a delight that made correction feel like a gift he hadn't known he wanted. *"Your instruments are honest, Lieutenant. Your maps aren't. The river moves faster than your bureaucracy."*

She'd been right. She was always right about the water. That was the thing people missed about Orsen Quintana, he thought now, watching her barge thread the first bend: they saw the restlessness, the salt-cured skin, the way she never unpacked anything, and they mistook it for flightiness. It wasn't. She read moving water the way priests read scripture — fluently, reverently, and with the constant awareness that it would kill you if you got it wrong.

They had spent one monsoon season together, moored side by side at the Kiunga staging dock, trading coffee and silence and eventually everything else. He remembered nights when the rain came down so hard the river seemed to rise around their voices, and they'd sit under her awning while she talked about Tomas — how her brother had taught her to swim by throwing her off the pier and diving after her, how he'd said *the water is only scary until you stop fighting it, then it holds you*. She spoke of Tomas the way other people spoke of saints. Voss had understood, even then, that he was courting a woman who kept a grave at the center of her chest, and that he would always be moored beside it, never inside the harbor.

He'd been transferred. She'd stayed. The letters thinned. The last time he saw her she'd said, standing very straight on the dock with her duffel over her shoulder: *"You belong to the flag, Dario. I've buried enough things that belong to flags."*

He should have let it end there. Instead he'd spent three years telling himself the story naval officers tell — that duty is a kind of love, that service is a kind of faithfulness — until the orders came through last month with the chest's description attached, and the word *quietly* underlined twice, and he'd understood that the flag wanted something from this river that had nothing to do with navigation.

* * *

"Entering the narrows, sir."

The walls rose on either side of *Kestrel*, close enough that the wake slapped back at them from the rock. The air changed — cooler, wetter, smelling of stone and old water. Ahead, the barge's engine note climbed as Mero pushed her harder, gambling on the rapids rather than stopping.

Voss raised the loudhailer. His thumb found the trigger.

And through the binoculars, Orsen turned.

She looked directly at him. Four hundred meters of river between them, and he felt the look land on his skin like cold spray. Her hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth — he recognized it, absurdly, as the band she'd used to tie off mooring lines that season at Kiunga — and her face held no surprise at all. Of course it didn't. She'd known he was behind her since the delta. She had been running *toward* this moment, the way you run toward a wave you can't outrun, timing it.

His thumb stayed on the trigger and did nothing.

*"Kestrel,"* he thought. *"I have you. One burst across your bow and Mero has to heave to or die on the rocks. I take the chest, I file my report, I go home a decorated man, and she goes to prison or worse, because the men who wrote these orders do not write merciful ones."*

*"Orsen,"* he thought, in the older alphabet, the one from the rainy season. *"You're heading toward the source. You know what's up there. God help me, you know, and you're going anyway, alone, with a smuggler and a dead nation's papers."*

The gorge whispered. That was the thing the charts never captured — the sound the rock made here, wind funneled through the narrows, a long exhalation that rose and fell like breathing. The local crews refused to run this stretch after dark. They called it the place where the river talks to itself, where the drowned ask questions. Voss had always filed that under superstition. Standing at the rail now, he was less sure. The whisper sounded like every conversation he'd ever failed to finish.

"Sir." Rau again, careful. "Shall I sound the siren? They're coming up on the fog bank."

Fog bank. Voss swung the glasses forward and saw it: a wall of white lying in the gorge ahead of the barge, where a cold tributary spilled from a side canyon and met the warm main channel. It happened on this river sometimes — steam fog, sudden and total, a blindness that lasted minutes or an hour depending on the mood of the water. The barge was thirty seconds from vanishing into it.

Thirty seconds to fire a warning shot. Thirty seconds to end this cleanly, the way his orders demanded, the way his career required.

In the binoculars, Orsen's hand left the rail. She pressed it flat against her sternum — a small gesture, private, unconscious, the same one she'd made at Tomas's funeral when the honor guard fired and everyone else flinched and she did not. He had watched her from the back of that crowd, in uniform, unable to approach her, and he had told himself the distance was protocol. It hadn't been. It had been cowardice dressed in white.

*Tomas.* The name arrived uninvited, the way it always did. Tomas Quintana, certified diver, had drowned in eight meters of calm water eleven months ago, and the incident report Voss had quietly pulled from the archive read like a story someone had stopped telling halfway through. Equipment checked. Conditions nominal. Witness statements contradictory. A navy tender logged in the vicinity that day that had no business being there. Voss had requested the tender's movement records and received, nine days later, a page with a section blacked out so heavily the paper had gone soft.

He had told himself that meant nothing. He had told himself a great many things.

Now he watched the woman who loved that drowned man steer straight into the fog with a nation's secrets in her hold, pursued by his boat, and the two stories — the official one and the true one — tore along the seam inside him like wet canvas.

"Sir, the fog bank—"

"Stand down from firing stations," Voss said.

Rau turned. "Sir?"

"You heard me. All stations stand down. Helm, reduce to one-third. We'll hold position at the gorge entrance until visibility improves." He heard his voice go flat and official, the voice of a man reading from a card. "No point risking the hull on rocks we can't see. We'll take them at the next clear stretch."

It was a plausible order. It was even, in narrow technical terms, prudent. Every officer aboard would log it as caution. Only Voss would know — only Voss would ever know — that he had just looked at a woman across four hundred meters of stolen river and chosen her over the flag, and that the choice had taken less time than a heartbeat.

The barge's stern disappeared into the white. Then her midships. Then, for one last instant, the red cloth at her hair, a fleck of color burning in all that grey — and then nothing. The fog closed like a door.

* * *

For eleven minutes *Kestrel* drifted at the gorge mouth while the whisper of the rock filled the silence, and Voss stood at the rail and did the arithmetic of treason.

It wasn't dramatic, the arithmetic. That was what nobody told you. Treason, in the moment, felt like paperwork. It felt like falsifying a position report, which he had already done twice since the delta, logging *Kestrel* forty kilometers east of where she actually was, buying Orsen hours with ink. It felt like knowing that Commander Sefu at headquarters expected results by Thursday, and that Thursday would come, and that the next order after *retrieve quietly* was the one underlined three times: *if compromise is imminent, deny the material by any means.*

Deny the material. He'd read that phrase four times before he understood it meant the chest, the papers, and anyone carrying them.

The fog began to thin, unraveling in rags. Through the gaps the gorge showed itself empty — green water, white stone, orchids, the long descending cries of unseen birds. No barge. Mero had used the blindness well; there were a dozen side channels in the upper narrows, and a shallow-draft barge could slip up any of them while *Kestrel*, drawing too much water, ground herself on the bar within minutes.

"Visual search pattern, sir?" Rau asked.

"No." Voss folded his binoculars. "Set course downstream. We'll refuel at Kiunga Bend and re-sweep the lower gorge. They doubled back — that's the smart play, and Mero's a smart operator."

Another lie, smooth as ballast. Rau nodded and rang for the helm, and *Kestrel* turned her bow away from the source of the Fly, away from the misted headwaters where a cave full of dead men's testimony waited, and pointed herself at the mission station where, if the river was kind, Sister Anselm would already be translating the documents Orsen carried — and where, in ninety-six hours, helicopters with consortium markings would come asking the same questions Voss had been ordered to stop asking.

He stood at the stern rail as the gorge fell away behind them, and he took out his radio handset, and he held it for a long time without keying it.

There was a frequency he shouldn't have known. An old one, from the survey season, that salvage boats used among themselves — unencrypted, unmonitored, a working channel from a cheaper era. If Orsen still listened to it, out of habit, out of nostalgia, out of the superstition of people who live on water — then one transmission, thirty seconds, no call sign, might reach her.

If she didn't listen, it reached whoever else was scanning, and it ended his career tonight instead of Thursday.

The gorge whispered behind them. *Ask,* it seemed to say. *The drowned are asking. What did you choose?*

Voss keyed the transmit switch.

"They're coming by air," he said, in the plain unaccented voice he'd used once, years ago, across a rain-drummed awning, to tell her something truer than any report he'd ever filed. "Kiunga Bend. Get them out. Please."

He released the switch. The channel hissed static, indifferent as weather. Somewhere upstream, lost in the white, a barge engine faded toward the highlands, carrying a dead flag's secrets and the only person alive whose opinion of him he still cared about.

Voss put the handset down and gave the order to make best speed for Kiunga Bend — officially, to refuel. Unofficially, to arrive before the helicopters did, and to stand on a mission station dock with his sidearm unbuckled, waiting to learn whether love, having finally committed its treason, would be rewarded with survival or simply with company in the ruin.

Behind him the gorge went on whispering, and for the first time in three years, Dario Voss did not feel alone inside the sound.
