## 9. The Mission at Kiunga Bend

The mission announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and frangipani and the green rot of the riverbank — a thin grey spire of smoke rising through the rain-forest canopy where the Kiunga Bend widened into a slow brown lake of water. Orsen saw it first from the bow of Ila's barge, one hand shading her eyes, the other resting on the iron chest as if it were a sleeping animal that might bolt.

"You're sure about this," she said. It was not really a question. She had asked it three times since dawn, in three different tones, and Ila had answered it three different ways, none of them reassuring.

"I'm sure about Sister Anselm," he said, working the tiller with his hip, his wounded arm bound tight against his ribs. The bandage had gone brown at the edges. He would not let Orsen change it on the water. "She's been up here longer than the river's been angry. Longer than the dam, longer than the war, maybe longer than the church." He squinted at the bend. "She buried two bishops and half a village's dead, and she never once asked anyone what they'd done to earn it."

"And she won't ask us?"

Ila laughed, then winced. "Oh, she'll ask. She'll ask everything. That's not the same as caring about the answers."

The barge slid in against a landing of blackened pilings, and Orsen stepped off onto mud that sucked at her boots like something reluctant to let go. Behind her the Fly ran on, wide and indifferent, carrying its silt and its secrets toward a delta that wanted her dead. She looked back once — habit now, the habit of the hunted — and saw nothing but water and mist and the far green wall of the gorge they'd come through. Voss could be anywhere behind that mist. Or nowhere. She no longer knew which possibility frightened her more.

The path to the mission climbed through cassava gardens gone half-wild, past a chapel whose tin roof had been patched with corrugated advertising signs for a fertilizer company that had pulled out of the country before Orsen was born. A bell hung in an open frame at the top of the rise, green with verdigris, silent. Everything here had the look of a place that had decided, long ago, to outlast whatever came for it.

Sister Anselm was waiting on the veranda.

Orsen had imagined, from Ila's descriptions, some shrunken relic of a woman, all knuckles and prayer. What stood there instead was tall and straight-backed, seventy if she was a day, with white hair cropped short as a soldier's and forearms brown and corded from sixty years of hauling water and burying the dead. Her habit had been traded decades ago, apparently, for a faded man's shirt rolled to the elbows and trousers stiff with river mud. Only a small silver cross at her throat marked what she was.

"So," the old woman said, before either of them could speak. Her eyes went to Ila first, cataloguing the wound with a single glance, then to Orsen, then — inevitably, hungrily — to the chest under Orsen's arm. "Ila Mero brings me a bleeding stranger and a box. It's been eleven years since anything interesting came up this river. Put him on the cot inside. And you—" her gaze settled on Orsen with a weight like a hand on the shoulder, "—you'll tell me what's in the box while I work. I find people lie less when their hands are busy."

* * *

Inside, the mission house smelled of camphor and old paper and the ghost of ten thousand meals. Anselm cleaned Ila's wound without ceremony — a mercenary's bullet, through the meat below the shoulder, ugly but clean — while Orsen held the lamp and told her everything. Not because she trusted the woman. Because after the drowned village, after the stilts rising out of the reservoir water and the old man who had wept at the sight of the seal, Orsen had learned that carrying the thing alone was heavier than any risk of telling it.

Anselm listened the way she worked: steadily, without flinching, asking only surgical questions. *Where exactly did you raise the chest? What depth? Who else has seen the documents? Did the officer give you a name?*

When Orsen reached the part about Tomas — the wreck dived months before his death, the phrases in his last letter matching phrases in the sealed papers — the old nun's hands went still over the wound for the first time.

"Say that again," she said.

Orsen said it again. The letter. The matching words. The drowning ruled accidental by men who had never seen the water.

Anselm tied off the bandage, washed her hands in a basin, and dried them slowly, looking at nothing. When she turned around her face had changed — not softer, but older somehow, as though a weight she'd been carrying alone for fifty years had just been offered a second pair of shoulders.

"There's a room behind the chapel," she said. "It used to be a classroom. Now it's where I keep the things nobody wants. Bring your box."

* * *

The room behind the chapel had no windows facing the river. Its walls were lined with steel cabinets salvaged from some flooded government office, and its shelves sagged under ledgers, registers, bundles of letters tied with string, photograph albums swollen with damp. A single kerosene lamp burned on the central table beside a stack of dictionaries in four languages, two of them dead.

"This is the archive of everything this country would prefer to forget," Anselm said, laying her palm flat on the nearest ledger. "Baptismal records. Death records. The massacre at Wabo Creek. The resettlement lists from the dam — names of villages that officially never existed, so the compensation could go nowhere. And this." She drew out a slim folder, paper gone amber. "Correspondence from the last year of the war. I was younger than you are now. I transcribed some of it myself, from men who stumbled upriver with things in their memory they couldn't carry."

She nodded at the chest. "Open it."

Orsen set the iron box on the table. The lock had been forced days ago, in the delta, by lamplight and dread; the lid came up with a groan of rust. Inside lay the documents as she'd left them — oilcloth-wrapped bundles, typed pages in a language neither she nor Ila could read, official letterheads crowned with the seal of the nation that no longer existed: the crossed torch and anchor beneath a crown that had been melted down before Orsen learned to swim.

And the map. Wax-sealed, folded eight times, showing the upper river in the fine confident hand of a cartographer who had drawn it to hide something rather than reveal it.

Anselm put on spectacles, took up the first bundle, and began to read.

For a long time the only sounds were the lamp hissing, rain starting somewhere far off on the tin roof, and the whisper of old paper turning. Orsen watched the old woman's face and tried to read it the way she read water — the surface for what moved beneath. Anselm's expression did not change so much as deepen, line by line, like a channel being cut.

Finally the nun set down the page and removed her spectacles and pressed the bridge of her nose.

"Well?" Orsen said.

"It's a report," Anselm said. "Dated nine days before the armistice. From a garrison commander to his ministry. You understand, the war was already lost. Everyone knew it. And this man —" she tapped the signature, a hard black scrawl, "— this man sat down with a clear head and wrote out, in perfect bureaucratic language, what his unit did at a place called Tereva Landing."

"Tereva Landing," Orsen repeated. The name meant nothing. Upstream, somewhere. Everything upstream meant nothing to her yet.

"Two hundred and six people," Anselm said. "Villagers, mostly. Some laborers conscripted from the coast who'd escaped north. They were gathered on the riverbank because the garrison had orders to leave no witnesses to the withdrawal — witnesses who could say where stores were buried, where ammunition dumps were sunk, where officers had taken what from whom. So they shot them at the water's edge and weighted the bodies and sank them in the deep channel above the bend." She said it flatly, the way she'd cleaned the wound. "Then the ministry wrote back. Not ordering an investigation. Ordering the destruction of the report's distribution list. The original was filed under a false designation — agricultural survey, I think — which is why it survived. Nobody burns what they can't find."

Orsen heard her own pulse. "That's it? A massacre, covered up. That's what three governments will kill for?"

"That's the skeleton." Anselm's eyes came up, sharp. "Keep listening. The report includes the distribution list itself — the names of the officers present. Forty-one names. And appended to it, in a different hand, a witness statement. A conscripted laborer who survived by swimming the channel at night. He gave names too. Local names. He named the men of Tereva Landing who died, one by one, because he'd grown up with them, because someone should know." She laid two pages side by side on the table. "This is why it cannot surface, do you see? Not the crime. Crimes are forgiven, forgotten, rebranded. But forty-one officers went home after the war and became administrators, ministers, directors of companies. Their children run this country's ministries and its mines. One of them chairs the consortium that owns every dredge lease on the lower Fly." Her finger stopped on a name near the top of the list. "If this page is authentic, half the board of that consortium is descended from or married to the men who stood on that bank."

The lamp hissed. Rain arrived properly now, drumming the roof, running loud in the gutters.

"And the second page?" Orsen asked, though she already knew. She could feel it coming the way you feel a storm front on your skin.

"The witness," Anselm said gently. "He didn't die. He walked upriver, and he kept walking, and eventually he reached a mission station much like this one, and a much younger sister sat across a table from him and wrote down every name while he wept." She turned the page so Orsen could see the transcription header, the date, the mission's old stamp. "He lived another thirty years. He's buried in the cemetery behind you. But his testimony was copied, and copies traveled, and men upstream — sons, grandsons, elders of the families he named — have kept the knowledge alive the way you keep a fire in wet weather. There are people at the source country, girl. Living people, who have waited their whole lives for someone to carry this the last hundred miles."

Orsen looked down at the two pages. The dead language had become, in the space of an hour, a riverbank, a line of villagers, a night swimmer, a young nun's careful handwriting. The abstract crime had acquired faces. Worse — it had acquired *descendants*, living and breathing, scattered through the very ministries and boardrooms that would burn the world to keep these pages dark.

Her hand found the edge of the table and gripped it.

"My brother," she said. "Tomas. He found this. Months before he died. His letter — the phrasing —" She pulled the worn envelope from inside her jacket, unfolded the soft paper, and laid it beside the witness statement. "Read it. Tell me I'm wrong."

Anselm read. Her lips moved slightly, translating in her head, comparing. The rain filled the silence.

"'They sank them standing,'" the old nun said at last, quietly. "Your brother wrote that phrase. The witness said it — *they made them stand at the edge so the water would take them the rest of the way.* It's a detail that appears nowhere in the official report. Only in the testimony." She looked up. "You're not wrong. Your brother saw this document, or one copied from it. He knew what it said. And then he went diving on that wreck, alone, and drowned in water shallow enough to stand up in."

Outside, the rain hammered. Somewhere in the mission a door swung on its hinge, knocked softly against a frame, knocked again.

"They killed him," Orsen said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — level, almost calm, the calm of deep water. "For reading. For knowing. They put him in the river the way his grandfather's generation put those people in the river, and everyone called it an accident, and I believed them for six years." She stared at the two pages side by side — the bureaucrat's list, the survivor's names — and felt something in her chest close like a fist around a rope. "Six years I thought I was diving to get away from losing him. I was diving to get away from believing I'd failed him."

Anselm said nothing for a moment. Then she reached across the table and put her brown corded hand over Orsen's white-knuckled one.

"Grief that finds a job becomes a dangerous animal," she said. "Feed it carefully."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the dark, translating by lamplight while Ila slept off the fever the bullet had left in him. Anselm read aloud; Orsen wrote, filling a school exercise book with English in her diver's blunt hand. Page by page the dead nation spoke: requisition orders, fuel manifests, the garrison's withdrawal timetable, and threaded through it all the quiet monstrous arithmetic of Tereva Landing — two hundred and six souls reduced to a column of figures, then restored, name by name, by a swimmer who refused to let the water finish the job.

Near midnight Anselm paused over a final sheet — smaller, older, folded separately — and went very still.

"What is it?" Orsen asked.

"A covering letter," Anselm said slowly. "From the archivist who hid this collection. He was the ministry clerk ordered to destroy the distribution list. Instead he copied everything and sent it upriver in pieces, to missions, to headmen, to anyone he judged might keep it. This letter is addressed to the source country. To the keeper of the archive there." She looked up over her spectacles, and in the lamplight her eyes were bright and terrible. "It says the collection is incomplete. It says the photographs and the master register went ahead of it, years ago, carried by a river pilot. And it says — listen — *the pilot's daughter knows the way, if the way is ever needed.*"

Orsen's blood slowed. "The cartographer."

"His daughter," Anselm confirmed. "Still living, if she lives. In the highlands, past the confluence." She folded the letter with great care. "Which means, my dear, that everything you need is within reach — and everything you need is upstream, through the narrows, past the mining leases, in country where strangers are noticed and remembered."

Orsen stood at the windowless wall a long moment, hearing the rain, feeling the current of it all pulling her onward the way the Fly pulled everything, patiently, toward the sea.

Then Ila appeared in the doorway, fever-bright but upright, and behind him, out in the compound, one of Anselm's few remaining parishioners was hurrying through the rain toward the chapel, waving his arms.

"Boat lights," Ila said. "Downstream. Two boats, running dark, coming up the bend fast — and they're not navy. Navy runs honest lights." He looked at Orsen, and the fever in his eyes was almost like apology. "Consortium colors. They're not here for the church."

Anselm was already snuffing lamps, moving with the unhurried speed of a woman who had buried too many people to waste motion on panic. "Back door, through the cassava," she said. "There's a track to the confluence. You can make the jungle before they land."

Orsen swept the documents into the oilcloth, hugged the exercise book of translated names to her chest, and paused at the door only long enough to look back at the old woman standing straight in the dark.

"Come with us."

Anselm smiled — the first true smile Orsen had seen from her, brief and fierce as a fish breaking water. "Child, I've been the last witness on this river for fifty years. Someone has to stay and be found innocent." She pushed Orsen gently out into the rain. "Run. And when you meet the daughter — tell her her father's debt is nearly paid."

Behind them, downstream, the first dark boat rounded the bend into the lamplight of the mission, and the rain swallowed everything else.
