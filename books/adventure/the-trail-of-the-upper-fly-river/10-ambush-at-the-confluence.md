## 10. Ambush at the Confluence

The confluence announced itself before we saw it. The Fly ran brown and heavy with highland silt, but a mile below the junction we began to hear the other river — a lighter, quicker voice threading through the gorge walls, the sound of water that had come a different way and was in a hurry. Ila stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the brass rail, the way he did when he was thinking instead of steering, and I watched the back of his neck where the sweat had drawn a map of its own.

"Strickland side," he said, without turning. "You can smell it. Different rain up there. Different trees."

I couldn't smell any difference. But I trusted his nose the way I trusted my ears underwater — as an instrument older than argument. We had been three days out of Kiunga Bend, three days since Sister Anselm had pressed the translated pages into my hands with ink-stained fingers and said, *Now you know what it weighs. Carry it like a woman who knows.* Three days of running the barge through water that narrowed and quickened, of sleeping in shifts, of Ila teaching me the sound his engine made when it was happy and the sound it made when it was lying.

I had begun, against every instinct I owned, to feel almost safe. That was the mistake. Safety on this river was a fish's belly-flash in dark water — a warning dressed as a gift.

The barge rounded the last bluff before the junction and the two rivers met below us in a long, braided fan of gravel bars and deadfall, channels splitting and rejoining like the veins of a leaf. It was beautiful the way wounds are beautiful when they're fresh and clean. And moored against the far bank, tucked into the shade of a fallen rain tree with its engine idling low, was a boat.

Not a patrol boat. Not navy gray. It was a workboat, the kind a survey crew might run, with a canvas awning and a depth sounder's transom mount and nothing at all on it that said what it actually was — which was exactly the problem. Everything about it said *nothing to see here*, and on this river, in this season, with the chest's ghost trailing me from the delta to the gorge to the mission steps, nothing to see here meant everything.

Ila saw it the same instant I did. His hand came off the rail.

"Survey boat," he said quietly. "Nobody surveys the Strickland junction in the wet. Current eats their instruments. Current eats *them*."

"Can we turn?"

He was already answering with the wheel, swinging the barge's blunt bow toward the near bank, toward a side channel that would take us back the way we'd come — but the channel was narrow, choked with floating islands of grass, and a loaded barge turns like a rumor. It takes distance. It takes time. And the other boat's engine note changed as we watched, rising from idle to something purposeful, and it came out from under the rain tree with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had been waiting and was pleased the waiting was over.

"Orsen." Ila's voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reading a bill of lading. "Under the tarp. The fuel drum with the false bottom. Take the papers and go over the side on the port quarter, and swim for the gravel bar, and don't look back at me."

"No."

"It wasn't a request."

"It wasn't an answer either." I was already moving — but toward the wheel, not the tarp. I put my hand over his on the spokes. His knuckles were scarred from thirty years of rope and river, and under my palm they were steady as bedrock. "Two engines are better than one. You know this water. I know how to be small and quiet. We run it together or not at all."

For a moment he looked at me with something I hadn't seen on his face before — not anger, not fear. Recognition. The look of a man who has finally understood the shape of the debt he's carrying and finds, to his own surprise, that he doesn't resent it.

"Together, then," he said. "Hold on to something that loves you back."

The first shot came off the water like a whipcrack against a drumskin, and the sound of it — flat, small, almost polite — was the most obscene thing about it. The bullet hit the wheelhouse roof and the roof rang like a church bell with something to say. Then the polite part was over and the noise became what it actually was: a rifle, worked by someone who knew his business, walking its fire across our deck in short, patient sentences.

Ila threw the wheel hard over and the barge heeled, cargo shifting below with a groan like a waking animal. We were running the braids now, threading gravel bars at a speed the old girl had never been asked for, and Ila was doing it by eye and by the lift of water on the bow, shouting channel calls over the gunfire — "left, LEFT, gravel, hold — right —" — and I was hauling on the fenders, throwing them to the offside to keep our silhouette broken, and the papers were in a waterproof bag strapped to my chest like a second heart.

The survey boat was fast. That was the whole arithmetic of it, and the arithmetic was bad. It cut across the confluence in a long white curve, closing the angle, and I could see them now — four men, maybe five, in the dull olive that isn't a uniform precisely so that no one can be blamed for wearing it. One at the bow with the rifle. One at the wheel. Two amidships doing something methodical with a long black case.

"Orsen," Ila said, and there was no flatness in his voice now. There was something worse. Tenderness. "The side channel. Fifty meters. When I say, you go, and you don't stop swimming until the jungle closes over your head. Promise me."

"Ila—"

"*Promise.*"

The long black case opened. What came out of it wasn't a rifle. It was the kind of weapon that doesn't shoot sentences, it shoots whole paragraphs, and I understood in one cold flash that these men had not been sent to capture the papers. They had been sent to end the question of the papers, and everyone who had ever touched them, and the boat they touched them on.

"They're going to sink us," I said.

"They're going to *try*," Ila said, and grinned, and it was the bravest and most terrible thing I had ever seen a man do. "Now, Orsen. NOW."

I went over the port quarter with the bag cinched to my ribs and the river took me like a hand closing — brown, cold at depth, loud as a crowd. I'd been diving since I was nine years old. I knew how to enter water, how to go deep and stay small, how to let the current carry me while spending nothing. I swam down and downstream in the blind brown noise, counting strokes, and above me — muffled, enormous, like weather happening to someone else — the world came apart. The big gun opened up. Wood shrieked. The engine note that Ila had taught me to hear, the happy sound and the lying sound, climbed into a third register I hadn't been taught and understood completely.

I came up in the lee of a gravel bar with reeds in my teeth, and I looked back. Of course I looked back. I had promised nothing.

The barge was still moving — listing, smoking from a wound near the waterline, but moving, and Ila had done the only thing left to do with a dying boat: he'd aimed it. He had put the wheel over and pointed forty tons of salvaged timber and stubbornness directly at the survey boat, and the survey boat's crew, professionals to the last, were breaking off their attack run to avoid the collision, and in the breaking-off, in that half-second of hesitation that no amount of money can train out of a man who doesn't want to die, Ila swung for the side channel.

A rifleman on the bow made the shot that mattered.

I saw it from a hundred meters — saw the small, precise jolt of Ila's body at the wheel, saw him sag and then, impossibly, straighten, one hand clamped to his side, the other still on the spokes, still steering, still putting the boat and himself between me and the men in the olive clothes. The barge lurched into the side channel and the jungle swallowed it, engine hammering, smoke trailing, and the survey boat came about to follow and then — this I will wonder about for the rest of my life — did not. It held at the confluence, turning slow circles, as if its orders had a boundary drawn on a map, as if even the men who paid for murder were afraid of what lived upstream.

I swam for the near bank with my arms tearing and the bag banging my ribs like a second pulse, and I dragged myself up through the mud and the kunai grass into the green dark, and I lay there with my heart going like a compressor and the river noise in my ears, and I did the thing I had trained myself never to do in twenty years of diving.

I panicked about someone else.

*He's hit. He's hit and he's alone on a burning boat and it's because of you, because you came up his river with your dead flag and your dead brother's ghost, because everyone who ties their line to yours starts to drown—*

Tomas. The thought arrived with the smell of the mud, sudden and total: *Tomas, who dove this same wreck, who wrote to me in phrases I'd found in a dead man's files, who went into the water one last time and did not come out.* I had spent years telling myself the river took him, because the river takes everyone, because the river is nobody's fault. But the river had help now. I'd seen the help. It wore olive clothes and shot paragraphs, and it worked for men who had never gotten their boots wet.

I got up. I didn't decide to get up; my body decided, the way it decides in black water when the air gauge reads low — pure animal bookkeeping. I went upstream along the bank, parallel to the side channel, crashing through wet undergrowth with no thought at all for the noise I was making, because Ila Mero had taken a bullet standing at a wheel he could have abandoned, and I was going to find that channel if I had to dig it out of the earth with my hands.

I found the barge before I found the channel — found it grounded on a gravel spit a quarter mile in, listing hard, smoke still rising off the deck in greasy ribbons. And I found Ila sitting against the wheelhouse door with his legs out straight and his hand pressed flat below his ribs, and when he saw me his face did something I will carry longer than the scars this river is going to leave on me.

It relaxed.

"Told you," he said. His voice was thin and pleased with itself. "Told you to swim and not look back."

"You told me to promise. I never promised." I was on my knees beside him, tearing open the bag, tearing open his shirt. The wound was a red, ugly mouth low on his left side, well below the ribs — I made myself look at it the way I look at a gauge, coldly, reading numbers. Not pumping. Not bubbling. Bad, but not the worst thing the river had shown me. "You steered a sinking boat through a gunfight with a hole in you."

"Somebody had to." He hissed as I packed the wound with a strip of his own shirt. "The boat's done, by the way. She's taking water. I'd appreciate it if you didn't mention that I loved her."

"The boat or the cargo?"

"The boat." His grin came and went, gray at the edges. "The cargo I only liked."

I got him upright by sheer arithmetic — my shoulder under his arm, his weight distributed, the bag of papers redistributed to my hip where it swung like a counterweight. The jungle came down to the water's edge in a green wall stitched with vines, and somewhere behind us, faint but not faint enough, the survey boat's engine was idling at the confluence, patient as a creditor.

"Where does this channel go?" I asked.

"Up." He said it like a man describing his own funeral and finding the arrangements acceptable. "Into the swamps. Leech country, the highland people call it. Nobody goes in. Nobody comes out quick. The consortium's boats won't follow — too shallow, too many snags." He coughed, and looked at the blood on his palm with mild academic interest. "Which is the good news."

"And the bad news?"

"The bad news," Ila said, "is that it's three days on foot, it's the wet season, and you're going to be carrying me half of it."

I got my grip tighter under his arm and looked at the green wall that was going to try to kill us both, and I thought of Tomas in the black water with his lungs full of somebody else's secret, and I thought of Sister Anselm saying *carry it like a woman who knows*, and I understood, with the clarity that only comes when the blood has started and cannot be unstarted, that the river had finally asked me its real price.

Not the chest. Not the map. Not even the truth.

The price was this: that I would not be the only one who bled for it. That from here to the source, survival would come to me secondhand — paid for in other people's blood, drawn on other people's courage, and I would have to live long enough to be worth the exchange rate.

"Lean on me," I said to Ila Mero. "We're going in."

And the jungle, which had been listening the whole time, closed its green hand around us both.
