## 11. Leech Country

The jungle did not end. That was the first thing Orsen understood about it, somewhere in the second hour or the fourth — time had gone soft and unreliable as river mud — the jungle simply did not end, and neither would she.

She had Ila's arm across her shoulders, his weight distributed as best she could manage, which was badly. He was a big man built like the barges he loved, all beam and ballast, and every step she took sank her deeper into ground that pretended to be solid and then laughed about it. The wound in his side had soaked through two dressings already. She had used her shirt for the third, tying the sleeves around his waist, so now she moved through the leech country half-naked and burning under a sun that came through the canopy in blades.

"Put me down," Ila said. It was not the first time he'd said it. "Orsen. Girl. Put me down and run."

"I don't run," she said. "I swim. Ask anyone."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughing didn't hurt, which she could tell by the way his hand pressed flat against his flank. Blood seeped between his fingers, dark and slow, the color of the water they were wading through when there was water to wade through, which was most of the time. The mercenaries' bullets had gone into him at the confluence and out again somewhere near his spine, missing everything that mattered by what the old divers called a diver's inch — the margin you lived your whole life inside without ever seeing it.

She had not looked back at the barge. She had heard it burn. That was enough. That was more than enough.

The swamp took them in the way the delta took wrecks: gradually, then completely. There was no trail anymore, only a direction — northeast, toward the highlands, toward the blue wall of mountains that Sister Anselm had pointed to on her last morning alive, saying *the cartographer's people live where the river is born, child, and they remember*. Orsen held that sentence in front of her like a lamp. She held the map in her head too, though the paper copy was wrapped in oilcloth against her belly, because maps on paper could burn and maps in the skull burned slower.

Water to the knee. Water to the thigh. A fallen strangler fig to walk along, its buttress roots rising out of the black water like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. She got Ila up onto it and he lay there gasping while she scouted ahead, and when she came back he was watching her with an expression she'd seen on drowning men.

"You're limping," he said.

"I'm walking with style."

"Your leg's bleeding."

"It's a scratch."

"It's three leeches and a scratch." He tried to sit up and failed, and she saw how much that cost him, this man who had hauled engines single-handed, who had once lifted her clean off a jetty by the collar of her shirt like she weighed nothing. "Let me see."

"No time."

"There's always time for leeches. You leave the heads in and they fester, and then you lose the leg, and then you're carrying *me* on one leg, and honestly, girl, your technique needs work already—"

She sat down on the root beside him and pulled them off — salt from the little tin in her pocket, the last of the ship's stores, each grain rationed like gold — and the leeches dropped writhing into the black water, and the bites welled up thin lines of red that the mosquitoes found within seconds. Three on one calf. Seven on the other. She counted them the way she counted everything now, breaths and strokes and hours, because counting was a rope and as long as she held it she would not slide off into the water at the bottom of herself.

Ila watched her do it. "You're talking to yourself," he said.

"I'm counting."

"That's worse."

* * *

The fever came for her in the late afternoon, or what she guessed was the afternoon; the canopy had closed overhead into a green dusk that never changed, and her own body had become unreliable terrain. First her skin went hot, then cold, then both at once, heat radiating off her chest while her hands went numb and white. She knew what it was. Malaria, or something wearing malaria's clothes. She'd had it twice before, in the Bismarck Sea, in the straits south of here, and both times it had come on like weather and broken like weather, and both times someone had been there to put a wet cloth on her forehead.

There was no one now. There was Ila, who needed carrying, and there was the rope of counting, and there was Tomas.

Tomas came back the way he always did in fever — not as a ghost, not as grief, but as a fact, present tense, six years old and standing in the brown shallows of the delta behind their mother's stilt house with his hand out.

*"Come on, Rennie. The water's fine."*

"The water has crocodiles in it," she said, out loud, to the swamp, and Ila, half-conscious on the fig root, said, "What?"

"Nothing. Drink." She held the canteen to his lips and gave him most of what was left, keeping only a swallow for herself, and the swallow tasted of tin and river and iron.

*"It doesn't have crocodiles HERE,"* Tomas said, patient, the way he was always patient, seven years older and certain the world was fundamentally survivable. *"This is the shallow. Crocs are lazy in the shallows. Everything dangerous lives deep. That's the first rule."*

"What's the second rule?" she had asked, nine years old, toes in the mud, terrified and wanting desperately not to be.

*"Never dive alone. Even if you're only going down ten feet. Even if you can touch bottom. Because the day you need help is the day you decided you wouldn't."*

She had learned to swim that afternoon. He had stood in the water with his hands under her ribs — she could still feel them, those small strong hands, the exact pressure of them, thirty years gone and her body had kept the receipt — and he had said, *the water holds you, Rennie, that's the whole secret, everyone thinks you have to fight it, but the water wants to hold you up, you just have to let it.*

She let the fever hold her up. She walked. The swamp breathed around her, frogs and insects and the far-off complaint of birds, and somewhere behind them, fainter with every hour, the sound of engines on the river they had left.

* * *

Night fell like a lid. She could not stop — stopping meant cold, meant the wound going bad, meant pursuit catching up — but Ila's weight had doubled, tripled, become the weight of a sunken engine block, and she understood with a diver's cold clarity that she could not carry him and walk all night. So she found the one dry place the swamp offered, a termite mound hard as fired clay, and propped him against it, and built no fire because fire was a beacon, and sat with her back against his back so they could share warmth and so she would feel it if he stopped breathing.

"Tell me something true," Ila murmured. His voice had gone thin. "Divers know things. Tell me one."

"When the current takes you," she said, "don't fight it sideways. Swim with it until you find slack water. Fighting the current is how divers die. Not the depth. Not the pressure. Pride against current."

"And what are we doing right now?"

"Fighting the current."

"So we're dying."

"We're swimming with it," she said. "Sideways is the mine. Sideways is going back to the delta and letting Voss's people put us in a room with bright lights. We're going with the flow, Ila. Northeast. Toward the source."

He was quiet a long time. When he spoke again his voice had changed, flattened out, the way voices go when a man decides to spend his last honest words. "I sold information once. About the wreck. Before you. Before any of this." She stiffened against his back and he felt it. "A man came to Kiunga asking after salvage. Old wreck, navy-cordoned, nobody names it. I told him the coordinates. I told him a diver named Quintana had been asking questions too." A breath, ragged. "They killed the family that ferried him upriver. All four. I read about it in a newspaper I trust less than the tide tables. And then you walked onto my barge with his seal on a brass fitting, and I thought — here it comes. Here's the bill."

"Ila—"

"Let me finish. I'm spending." The swamp dripped. Something large moved through the water forty meters out and decided they weren't worth the trouble. "Everyone who helps you dies. You know that. I told you that on the barge and I was right, wasn't I? The nuns. The ferryman's kids, probably, by now. Me." His hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, and his grip was still strong, which she held onto like a rail. "So here's my price, and it's the same price as before. Keep going. Don't you dare stop moving to feel sorry for me. You carry the papers to the source and you let the dead nation testify, and maybe then the ones who died for it will have died *for* something instead of just *of* it. That's the price. Promise me."

"I promise," she said, and the fever took the words and shook them, and she wasn't sure afterward whether she'd said them out loud or only in the water where Tomas could hear.

* * *

She dreamed the delta. She dreamed it whole and lit from beneath, the way it looked at dawn before the patrol boats woke, and she was diving the unnamed wreck with Tomas beside her, and he was not drowned, he was *down there*, working, his bubbles rising silver past her mask, and he turned and looked at her through two faceplates of glass and said:

*"You found my letter."*

"I found your letter," she said, and her voice worked underwater, which dreams allow.

*"Then you know it wasn't the river that killed me."*

"I know."

*"Rennie."* His eyes behind the glass were calm and terrible. *"The water holds you. But men let go. Remember which one let go of whom."*

She woke with her cheek in mud and Ila shaking her shoulder, and the sky above the canopy had gone the gray of old zinc, and her hands were shaking so hard she had to press them flat against her thighs to make them stop. The fever hadn't broken. It had only stepped back to watch her.

"Talked all night," Ila said. His color was bad — gray-yellow, waxy — but his eyes were clear, and clear eyes in a man that sick meant either a strong heart or a short road. "About your brother. About swimming lessons. About a man named Voss, and I won't ask, but I'll say this: whoever he was, you said his name like it was a coin you'd bitten to check."

"Get up," she said. "We're close. I can smell the altitude."

"You can't smell altitude."

"Slate. Cold stone. Rain that hasn't touched lowland air." She hauled him upright, took his arm across her shoulders, and stood, and the world tilted and steadied, tilted and steadied. "My brother taught me to swim in water like this. Brown, warm, full of things with teeth. He said the water holds you. He was wrong about a lot of things but he was right about that." She took the first step. Then the next. "So hold on to me, Ila. I'm the water today."

* * *

By midday the swamp began to give up its argument. The water thinned, then vanished into spongy ground, then into ground that merely squelched, and the trees changed — fewer of the great buttressed giants, more of the moss-hung midstory, and the air itself cooled by degrees she felt like mercy falling on her neck. Ila could no longer walk. She had known since dawn; he had known longer, and neither of them said it, because saying it would have required deciding what to do about it, and the only options were leaving him or building a litter, and she was not built for either.

So she built the litter anyway. Two poles of light hardwood, cut with the machete she'd taken from the barge, cross-braced with vine, and she dragged it rather than carried it, hauling it over roots and hummocks while Ila lay on it giving directions in a voice growing fainter and more amused by the hour.

"Left. Left, girl, the ground drops. No — your other left. Are all divers this stubborn or is it a family trait?"

"Family trait. My brother once dove a wreck with a dislocated shoulder because he didn't want to miss the tide."

"How'd that go?"

"He popped it back in at forty feet and finished the job." She hauled the litter over a fallen log, breathing in gasps, the sweat pouring off her despite the cool. "That's the family trait. Finishing the job."

The land rose. By late afternoon they were climbing in earnest, the swamp behind them and below them, spread out like a dark stain to the horizon, and the air smelled of slate and cold stone and rain that hadn't touched lowland ground, and she allowed herself, for exactly four seconds, to believe they had made it.

Then she crested the ridge and saw the smoke.

Not forest fire — too thin, too domestic, a thread of cooking smoke rising from a fold in the hills ahead, and below it, terraced into the slope, gardens. And among the gardens, figures. Small, distant, motionless. Watching the two ragged shapes come up out of the leech country — a woman hauling a litter, a dying man aboard — and not running away, which after everything was the most astonishing thing Orsen had seen in weeks.

One figure detached from the others and started down the slope toward them. Walking, not hurrying. An old woman, straight-backed, in a dress of faded trade-cloth, and even at that distance Orsen saw her stop, tilt her head, and look — not at Orsen, not at Ila — at the oilcloth bundle strapped against Orsen's belly, as if she could see straight through canvas and skin to the wax-sealed map and the dead flag's documents underneath.

The old woman smiled. It was not entirely a comforting smile.

"You'll be wanting my father," she called up the slope, in accented but fluent English, "and I'm afraid I have news about that."
