## 12. The Cartographer's Daughter

They came down out of the leech country on the ninth day, when the mist lifted just long enough to show them the hamlet — a scatter of thatched roofs stitched to the shoulder of the mountain like buttons on a coat too big for it. Orsen saw the woodsmoke first, thin gray threads rising straight up in the windless cold, and then the terraced gardens falling away below in green steps, and then she stopped walking altogether.

Ila leaned against her, his weight more burden than support now. The wound in his side had gone from angry red to a bruised purple-black, and the fever came and went like weather. "Is it real?" he asked, squinting through eyes gummed half-shut. "Or is it another one of your mirages? You've shown me three rivers and a wedding party so far."

"This one's real." She shifted under his arm, taking more of him. "Smoke goes straight up. Mirages don't smoke."

"Mirages don't carry you, either," he said, and laughed, a dry rattle that worried her more than any fever chart could have. "That's how you know you've finally lost your mind, Fairweather. When the hallucinations start doing the work."

The path narrowed to a foot-track worn into the clay, switchbacking between casuarina trees whose needles carpeted the ground in rust-red drifts. Pigs grunted somewhere behind a fence of sharpened stakes. A dog announced them long before they were seen — a lean yellow thing with a torn ear that circled them at a distance, barking in bursts, as though filing a report with each one.

By the time they reached the first house, half the hamlet had come out to look. Children peered from behind their mothers' skirts. Men stood with their hands resting on the heads of stone axes that were, Orsen noted, mostly ceremonial now, the steel bush-knives tucked in their bark belts doing the real work. She raised her empty hands, palms out, the way Tomas had taught her years ago in a different country entirely: *show them you're not holding anything, and most people will decide you're not carrying anything either.*

An old woman separated herself from the group.

She was small and straight-backed, wrapped in a tapa cloth gone soft as paper with age, her white hair cropped close to her skull. Her face was a topography of its own — ridges and valleys, everything mapped by eighty-odd years of sun and weather. She looked at Ila first, cataloguing the wound with a glance that missed nothing, then at Orsen's salt-crusted clothes, the river mud dried in layers on her boots like geological strata, and last at the oilcloth bundle strapped across Orsen's back.

At the bundle, her eyes stopped.

"You came up the Fly," she said. Her English was precise, accented, learned somewhere formal and kept like pressed flowers. "Through the gorge."

"We did." Orsen's voice came out rougher than she intended. Nine days of swamp water and fever-speech had sanded it down. "Part of it."

"The gorge has a voice," the old woman said. "My father said if you listen long enough, it tells you every secret the river ever swallowed." She turned, unhurried, and spoke a stream of words to the others in her own tongue. Two younger men came forward without being asked and took Ila's weight from Orsen's shoulders, gently, the way you handle something already broken but not yet beyond mending.

"I am Rupeni's daughter," she said, facing Orsen again. "You may call me Maren. And you will tell me what my father drew for you, or you will fall down, because I can see you are held upright by nothing but the question in your eyes."

* * *

They put Ila in a house set slightly apart, warm with a fire, and an ancient man with hands like tree roots began working over the wound with boiled water and a paste of crushed leaves that smelled sharp enough to make Orsen's own eyes water. Ila gripped her wrist before she left him.

"If she asks what's in the bundle," he whispered, "tell her the truth. This whole river, everyone we've met — they all knew pieces of this before we did. We're the ones playing catch-up."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be comforting. It's supposed to be true." His hand tightened. "Go on. I'm in better hands than yours anyway. Yours shake."

Maren's house stood at the hamlet's edge, closest to the drop-off where the mountain fell away toward the river far below — a silver thread now, barely visible through the haze. Inside it smelled of smoke and dried herbs and old paper. There was a chest against one wall, iron-banded, older than anything else in the room, and on the walls hung drawings.

Not photographs. Drawings. Ink on paper gone amber, some of them cracked along fold-lines worn soft from decades of handling. Rivers seen from above, rendered in a draftsman's hand — precise, patient, obsessive. The Fly and its tributaries, the gorges, the bends, each annotated in a small careful script in three languages, one of which Orsen recognized from the documents in her bundle: the dead language of the dead nation, the flag that no longer flew anywhere on earth.

"My father surveyed these waters for thirty years," Maren said, following her gaze. "First for the colonial administration, then for himself, after they no longer wanted his kind of accuracy. He married my mother here. He died here. And in all those years, he drew maps that told two stories at once — the one the government wanted, and the one that was true." She touched one drawing, a gorge rendered in fine cross-hatching, with a series of small symbols inked above the headwaters. "A mapmaker who is honest learns quickly that honesty is a form of hiding. You put the truth where only the patient will find it."

Orsen unslung the oilcloth bundle with hands that trembled — from exhaustion, she told herself, though she knew better. She unwrapped it on Maren's table: the wax-sealed map she'd pulled from the iron chest, the documents Sister Anselm had translated before Kiunga Bend burned, the copies made in Anselm's own careful hand on mission station paper. Copies that had cost Anselm her life eleven days ago, when the helicopters came.

Maren looked at the map for a long time. Then she took it in both hands, tilted it toward the firelight, and turned it slowly — ninety degrees, then ninety more.

"There," she said quietly. "He always did that. Drew north to the left, so that anyone reading it casually would read it wrong." Her finger traced a line through the cross-hatched mountains above the river's source. "This is not a route. It is a confession. My father hid the archive, and then he spent the rest of his life drawing the way to it into every map he made, disguised as survey marks. Waiting for someone patient enough to ask."

"Were there others?"

"One, before you. A diver." Maren's eyes came up and held hers, and Orsen felt the floor tilt under the question before it was even asked. "Years ago now. He came with a letter of introduction from a priest downriver. He stayed two nights. He asked about the source, about the cave above the falls. He signed his name in my father's visitor book."

The room went very still around Orsen. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the dog barked twice and quit.

"Tomas Quintana," Maren said. Not a question.

"Yes." Orsen's voice cracked on the single syllable. She sat down without meaning to, onto a stool that seemed to have been placed exactly where her legs would give out. "He drowned. Eighteen months ago. They told me it was an accident. A current, a cramp, deep water."

"He did not drown here," Maren said. "But he asked my father what the river keeps at its source, and my father told him — because Tomas had already read enough to be dangerous, and my father believed dangerous knowledge should travel with a guide or not at all. Tomas went back downriver saying he would return with proof of his own, testimony of his own, that the world would have to listen to a living witness." She paused. "We heard, later, that he was dead. My father stopped drawing after that. His hands, he said, had failed him. His hands had not failed him. His hope had."

Orsen looked down at the map, at the careful hidden route threading through the highlands to the cave above the falls, and understood suddenly, completely, the shape of the last eighteen months. Her brother hadn't stumbled into this. He had walked into it with his eyes open, carrying the same terrible inheritance she carried now, and someone had closed his mouth for it, and the river had been given the blame.

"They'll kill me too," she said. It wasn't self-pity. It was arithmetic. "There's a naval officer hunting me who loves me, and men with helicopters who don't need to love or hate anything. Every person who helps me dies. A missionary. Probably others I don't know about. Ila took a bullet—"

"And yet you are sitting in my house," Maren said, "drinking my tea, holding my father's map. Do you know why?"

Orsen didn't answer.

"Because stopping anywhere costs a life, yes. But running everywhere costs all of them." Maren folded the map along its original creases, precisely, reverently, and pressed it back into Orsen's hands. "I am eighty-one years old. I buried my father in that garden. I will bury myself near him. My debt is paid by staying — someone must remain who remembers what these papers mean, who can read the third language, who can tell the children here, in twenty years or fifty, when the newspapers are done shouting, what actually happened on that river. Witness is not only carried forward, child. Sometimes witness is standing still."

* * *

That evening the whole hamlet fed them — taro and pitpit and river fish smoked over coals, and a broth Maren's neighbor swore would pull the fever out of a man through his skin. Ila ate sitting up, color back in his face for the first time since the confluence, and told lies about how much pain he was in until the old healer threatened to re-break something just to prove a point.

Afterward, by firelight, Maren gave Orsen the final route. Not from the map alone — from memory, walking her through it turn by turn while Orsen committed it to the same place she kept the feel of water moving against a hull.

"A day's climb past the saddle. You'll see the falls before you hear them — do not trust your ears in that valley, the rock throws sound like a liar throws blame. The cave sits above the second fall, behind a curtain of roots. My father's mark is cut into the stone at its mouth — a circle with a line through it, the way he drew north-to-the-left. Everything inside is wrapped in waxed canvas. It has waited thirty years. It can wait one more day for you to sleep."

"And after?" Orsen asked. "After I take it out — what then?"

"After," Maren said, "is not mine to draw." She banked the fire, and the light shrank to embers, and her face became all shadow and ridge-line. "But I will tell you what my father wrote in the margin of his last map, the one he never finished. He wrote: *The river gives up everything eventually. The question is whether anyone is still standing on the bank to receive it.*"

Orsen slept that night in Maren's house, on a mat that smelled of herbs, and dreamed of nothing at all — the first true nothing in months, a black still water with no brother in it, no wreck, no chest. She woke before dawn feeling robbed and rested in equal measure.

Ila could walk. Barely. The healer fitted him with a staff and pronounced, through Maren's translation, that the bullet had missed everything that mattered and grazed everything that didn't, and that if he tore the stitching climbing mountains it would serve him right for arguing.

They left at first light, when the mist still filled the valleys like milk poured into the world's cracks. Half the hamlet walked them to the edge of the gardens. Maren came as far as the casuarina tree where the track began to climb.

She pressed something into Orsen's hand — a small square of ambered paper, folded once. "His visitor book page," she said. "Tomas's signature, and the date, and one line beneath it in his handwriting. My father let him write it. Read it when you reach the cave. Not before."

Then she stepped back, straight-backed in the gray light, and said the last thing she would ever say to Orsen Quintana:

"Some debts are paid by going. Mine is paid by staying. Go and collect yours."

Orsen climbed. The track rose through the casuarinas, and the hamlet fell away below, and Maren grew smaller and smaller until she was a dark figure at a bright tree, and then only the tree, and then only mountain.

Behind them, far downstream, the mist over the valley broke apart — and through it, glinting, Orsen caught one glimpse of the river, and on the river, moving fast against the current, a boat with an engine note she knew by heart.

Voss had found the trail.
