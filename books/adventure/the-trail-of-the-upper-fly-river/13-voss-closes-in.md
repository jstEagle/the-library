## 13. Voss Closes In

The order came in at 0400, typed on flimsy paper and hand-carried to the patrol boat *Kestrel* by a courier who would not look Dario Voss in the eye. That was the first sign. The second was the signature block — not his squadron commander's name but a stamp from the Ministry of Interior, an office that had no business issuing orders on the river at all.

*Engage and neutralize all persons aboard the vessel designated BARGE 7. Lethal force authorized. No survivors required for debriefing.*

No survivors required. He read the line three times, then read it aloud to himself in the dark of the wardroom, because there was something about hearing a sentence that made its teeth show. In eleven years of service he had been ordered to board vessels, seize cargo, escort convoys through pirate water. He had never once been told that the people he was sent to find were better off dead.

He burned the flimsy in the galley stove and watched the ink curl black.

Outside, the Upper Fly moved past the anchorage in the slow brown way it had, carrying silt and rotted logs and things nobody wanted to name toward the delta. Somewhere upstream — two days, maybe three, if the fog held and Ila Mero's pilot knew the channels the way everyone said he did — Orsen Quintana was running his river with a chest of dead men's secrets lashed under her tarpaulin.

His river. He had thought of it that way for eleven years, the way a man thinks of a woman he married out of duty and came to love out of habit. And now she was on it, and the ministry wanted her drowned in it, and they had handed him the gun because they believed — correctly, once — that he was the kind of man who did what he was told.

Voss stood at the rail until the sky went grey, then the color of old pearl, then the color of nothing at all, and made his decision the way he made most decisions: slowly, and then all at once, like a knot coming loose.

He would lie.

* * *

Lying, it turned out, was harder than diving. He had always assumed treachery required imagination; what it actually required was bookkeeping.

The *Kestrel*'s position reports went out twice daily on the scheduled frequency, encrypted, terse, professional: *Patrol proceeding upstream, grid reference, visibility poor, no contact.* He composed them himself now, refusing to let his signalsman touch the cipher wheel, and each one was a small masterpiece of fiction. He reported himself in the lower gorge when he was still at the mission anchorage. He reported engine trouble he did not have, buying six hours of official patience. He reported weather that existed only in his own invented forecast — *heavy rain, ceiling nil, air operations suspended* — because he had learned by the second day that the real threat was not the patrol boats chasing Orsen upriver.

It was the helicopters.

There were three of them based at the consortium strip above Kiunga Bend, gun-grey, unmarked except for a registration number painted over with paint that didn't quite match. They belonged, officially, to a mining survey operation. Voss had watched one of them lift off at dawn two days ago and circle the confluence in slow widening spirals, and he had understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading his own x-ray, that the navy was no longer the sharpest instrument being pointed at Barge 7. The consortium had stopped asking the government for permission somewhere around the time the chest came out of the delta mud, and the government — embarrassed, frightened, complicit — had decided to let the mining company do its drowning for it while maintaining deniability down every chain of command.

Which meant his falsified reports were not just treason. They were a race.

Every hour he bought with a fictional engine fault or an invented fog bank was an hour Orsen gained on the helicopters, which did not file position reports at all, which answered to no ministry, and whose pilots — he'd met two of them at the strip bar in another life, back when he wore his dress whites and she wore that green dress and they'd danced badly to a bad band — were paid by results.

He sat in the cramped chart room with the river spread out before him on paper so old the channels had shifted since it was surveyed, and he traced her likely route with one finger. Up past the drowned village — she would have stopped there, she was constitutionally incapable of passing suffering without looking at it — then the gorge, then Kiunga Bend where the old missionary lived, then the long climb of water toward the source. She was following the map. Of course she was. Orsen Quintana had never in her life left a locked thing unlocked, a sunk thing unsunk, a question unanswered. It was the first thing he had loved about her and the thing that was going to kill her, and he could no longer tell whether he wanted to save her from it or simply be near it when it happened.

That was the honest thought, the one he turned over at night like a stone found in a shoe. Not *I want to protect her.* Something less clean. He wanted to be present. He wanted, if the river was going to take her the way it had taken her brother, to be standing on the bank. Eleven years ago he had chosen the service over her with the smooth certainty of a young man who believes both doors will stay open, and the door had closed, and he had told himself for a decade that the ache behind his ribs was regret when it was actually just unfinished business wearing regret's clothes.

Now the ministry had given him orders to finish it permanently, and he found that the only way he could live inside his own skin was to sabotage the finishing.

* * *

On the fourth day his executive officer began to ask questions.

Petty Officer Renn was a good man, which was the problem. Good men noticed things. He noticed that the captain took the morning cipher watch personally. He noticed the position plots in the log didn't match the fuel consumption — the *Kestrel* was burning diesel like a boat covering ground while reporting itself parked. He came to the wardroom after evening mess, hat under his arm, and stood in the doorway with the particular stiffness of a sailor about to say something insubordinate.

"Sir. The fuel figures."

"I know," Voss said.

Renn waited. He was owed waiting; he had served under Voss for six years and had never once seen him falsify so much as a leave chit.

"There's a barge ahead of us," Voss said finally, "carrying a woman I used to know, and papers that three governments have decided are worth more than her life. My orders are to make sure she doesn't reach port alive. I'm not going to do that." He let the sentence sit in the room like spilled oil. "The reports I've been sending are false. From this point, everything you see me do, you have a choice about. You can report me to the district commander, and I'll stand on that bridge and admit all of it. Or you can help me buy her enough river to reach the highlands before the aircraft find her."

Renn looked at him for a long moment. Outside, rain had started again, drumming on the deck plates, filling the silence the way it fills every silence on the river — completely, indifferently.

"The aircraft, sir," Renn said. "They overflew us yesterday. Low. One of them waggled its wings."

"I saw."

"They weren't surveying."

"No," Voss said. "They weren't."

Renn put his hat back on, which in the grammar of that ship meant the conversation was over and a decision had been made. "Then I'll take the midnight watch, sir. You should sleep. You look terrible."

It was, Voss thought later, the strangest act of loyalty he had ever received: not agreement but logistics. Renn hadn't said he approved. He had simply begun helping, the way sailors do, without ceremony, as though falsifying a nation's position reports were a pump that needed manning.

* * *

The message from district command came on the fifth day, and it was short, and it ended his ability to pretend.

*Intel indicates target vessel may attempt airstrip resupply near upper reaches. Air asset coordination requested. Confirm your position and readiness to support.*

Confirm your position. They wanted coordinates. They wanted him to stop pretending to be lost in fog and hand the helicopters a fix on the last known location of Barge 7, which — if his estimates held — was somewhere within a day's run of Sister Anselm's mission at Kiunga Bend, where an old woman translated dead languages and grew tomatoes against the advice of everyone.

If he confirmed honestly, he condemned her. If he falsified, the falsification would now be detectable — the request was too specific, the timeline too tight. Someone at the ministry was already comparing his reports against consortium flight logs. He could feel the net drawing in around himself even as he tried to hold it open around her.

He sat with the message form in front of him for forty minutes. Renn stood watch outside the chart room door and turned away anyone who approached, reporting apologetically that the captain was "working navigation."

Navigation. Yes. Voss worked navigation. He worked the whole river in his head, every bend, every sandbar, every drowned tree that marked a channel like a grave marker. And at the end of forty minutes he wrote his reply, and it was the last lie he had in him, and he spent it all:

*Position: lower gorge, holding against weather. Engine cooling failure, repair underway, estimate 18 hours. Unable to support air ops. Recommend assets proceed independently per original tasking. Will resume patrol upon repair.*

Eighteen hours. It was nothing — a rounding error in the life of a river, a sneeze in the timeline of a war. But eighteen hours was the difference between the helicopters lifting at first light with a fresh fix and the helicopters lifting at first light guessing. Eighteen hours was Sister Anselm's tomato garden. Eighteen hours was maybe enough.

He sent it. Then he sat very still and listened to the rain and understood that he had just, in the eyes of his government, committed the only sin it truly recognized: he had been useless on purpose.

* * *

It was Renn who suggested the radio, in his flat practical way, two nights later. "You're going to want to talk to her eventually, sir. Better you choose the channel than the ministry intercepts yours."

Voss knew the barge's working frequency — every salvage operator on the river ran on the old delta bands, unencrypted, the way small boats always do, because encryption costs money and the river doesn't respect it anyway. He waited until the *Kestrel* lay anchored in a side slough where the terrain shielded the transmission from the relay station at the delta, and he waited until full dark, and then he keyed the handset and spoke into the static the way a man speaks into a well.

"Barge 7, Barge 7, this is Kestrel actual. Do you copy?"

Static. The river hissed. Somewhere in the black water a fish slapped the surface, once, like a hand letting go.

He tried again. "Barge 7, Kestrel actual. Orsen. If you're listening, copy this."

More static. Then — faint, broken, half-swallowed by distance and weather — a voice. Not hers. A man's voice, rough, careful, the voice of someone who has learned that radios kill people. "Copy Kestrel. Go ahead. Keep it short."

"This is Voss." He paused, because saying the name felt like opening a vein. "Tell her—" He stopped. Started again. There were a hundred things he could have said and almost none of them were survivable to transmit. *I'm sorry. I chose wrong eleven years ago. I've been lying to my own navy for five days to buy you river.* All true. None of it useful at four knots against the current with killers overhead.

What was useful was this:

"Tell her they're coming by air. Three aircraft, gun-mounted, operating off the strip above Kiunga Bend. They don't file flight plans and they don't answer to the navy anymore. Tell her to get off the water at dawn and dusk — that's when they fly. Tell her the mission at the bend is already known to them. Tell her—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "Tell her Tomas was right. About all of it. And that some of us are done pretending otherwise."

Silence on the band. Long enough that he thought the transmission had failed, that the words had gone out into the wet dark and died there unheard.

Then the voice again, quieter: "Copy all, Kestrel. Short and sweet." A pause. "She says she knows your handwriting even on the radio."

The channel went dead. Voss lowered the handset and found that his hands were shaking, not from fear — he had been afraid for days and had stopped noticing — but from the strange lightness of a man who has set down something heavy he has carried so long he'd forgotten its weight.

Treason, complete. Five falsified reports, one obstructed air operation, one unauthorized transmission to a fugitive vessel. If they caught him, they would court-martial him in a room without windows and take eleven years and fold them into a paragraph. He found that he could not make himself care. The paragraph would say what it said. The river would say what it said. Only one of those things would still be saying anything in a hundred years.

* * *

At dawn the helicopters flew.

He heard them before he saw them — the flat chopping thunder rolling down the gorge, wrong for this stretch of river, wrong for any legitimate work — and then they came around the bend in echelon, low and fast, gun-grey, registration numbers smeared, rotors bending the mist flat. They passed directly over the *Kestrel*. One of the pilots looked down. Voss looked up. For half a second their eyes might have met across sixty meters of morning air, and Voss understood that the pilot knew exactly what ship this was, and exactly what its captain had been doing, and did not care, because the pilot answered to a payroll now and not to a flag.

They continued upstream. Toward the bend. Toward the mission. Toward the hamlets and the stilt houses and whatever thin thread of shelter Orsen had managed to weave between herself and the sky.

Voss rang up full ahead. Renn was already at the helm, having asked no questions, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the current with her engines howling, chasing the sound of the rotors upriver into the rain.

He did not know yet what the guns would find at Kiunga Bend. He would not know for two more days, and by then there would be a dead nun and a burning airstrip and a wound in the world that no amount of testimony would close cleanly. All he knew, driving his ship north through the grey water with his career burning behind him like a bridge cut loose, was that the helicopters had reached the bend first —

— and that somewhere ahead, in the smoke, Orsen Quintana was still moving.
