## 14. The Airstrip Ambush

The sound came before the light did.

Orsen was in the mission kitchen, hands wrapped around a tin mug of Sister Anselm's bitter tea, when the morning went wrong. It began as a tremor she felt through the floorboards — a low, rhythmic pulse, like a heart beating somewhere above the clouds — and for a moment, half-asleep still, she mistook it for thunder rolling up the valley from the coast.

Then Sister Anselm set down her cup with a small, deliberate click, and said, "That is not weather."

Orsen had spent enough years listening to water to know what engines sounded like when they were coming to kill you. She was moving before the thought finished forming, crossing the kitchen, throwing open the shutters onto a courtyard grey with early mist. The airstrip lay beyond the mission's eastern fence — a scar of crushed coral and grass that some forgotten administration had cut into the valley floor decades ago, long enough unused that the jungle had begun to take it back at both ends. Children played on it sometimes. This morning there were no children on it. There were only the villagers from the hamlet, two dozen families who had come down from the highland trails over the past three days because word traveled faster than fear, and word had said: *the nun has copies, the nun will read them aloud, come and be counted.*

They were gathered near the mission's bell tower, some of them holding the laminated pages Orsen had helped copy through two sleepless nights — photographs, manifests, lists of names in a dead nation's careful bureaucratic hand. Names of villages. Names of men shot at the river's edge in the last weeks of a war most of the world had already stopped watching. Names that matched names the old people here still carried like stones in their chests.

The rotors grew louder. The mist above the airstrip began to move against the wind.

"Get them off the strip," Orsen said. Her voice came out flat and strange, the voice she used underwater. "Get them into the tree line. Now."

Sister Anselm was already gone, moving through the courtyard with a speed that belied her seventy years, calling out in three languages, her arms wide, herding. Ila Mero appeared at Orsen's shoulder, his wounded arm bound tight against his chest, his face grey but his eyes clear. "How many can you carry?" he asked.

"Carry where?" Orsen said. "There is nowhere." And even as she said it she knew it was true — the trail north was four days' climb, the river south was watched, the gorge was watched, everything was watched. The helicopters had not come to search. They had come to erase.

The first one came out of the cloud deck sideways, nose-down, a dark insect shape shedding rain, and behind it came a second, and then the world tore open.

* * *

Later, Orsen would try to remember it in order, and fail. Memory gave her fragments instead, each one lit too brightly, each one lasting too long.

The door gunner opening fire on the empty airstrip first — walking the rounds across the coral in a long, almost leisurely line, as if testing, as if announcing. The bell tower taking a burst and shedding its roof in pieces, the old bronze bell dropping through the wreckage and striking the ground with a note so pure and enormous that for one second everyone stopped running, even Orsen, even the gunners, as if the valley itself had spoken.

Then the second helicopter swinging wide over the village houses and the hammering beginning in earnest, and the world becoming noise.

She remembered carrying a child. She would never learn whose. A girl, maybe six, barefoot, frozen in the open between the mission wall and the tree line, standing with her hands over her ears and her eyes shut, and Orsen simply picked her up mid-stride without breaking stride, the way you lift a heavy coil of rope, and ran. The girl weighed nothing. That was the detail that would haunt her — how little a child weighs, how easily the whole future of a family fits against your chest, and how the bullets came anyway, stitching the mud ten meters behind them, closer, closer, until the tree line swallowed them both and Orsen fell to her knees among the ferns and the girl opened her eyes and looked at her with an expression of such perfect, uncomprehending calm that Orsen had to turn away so the child would not see her face.

She remembered Ila dragging a wounded man uphill by the collar, one-armed, swearing steadily in river pidgin, refusing all help.

She remembered the copies. God help her, she remembered thinking about the copies.

Sister Anselm had insisted they be kept in the mission chapel, in the cedar chest under the altar, because — she had said, with the mild obstinacy of a woman who had buried three generations of this valley — *a church is the one place even soldiers hesitate.* Orsen had argued. She had wanted them dispersed, split into bundles, carried by runners in five directions. The sister had smiled and said that dispersal was doubt, and doubt was how these things died quietly, and that if the papers were worth dying for then they were worth keeping together where anyone who came could see them plainly.

Now the helicopters were circling back toward the chapel.

"Anselm!" Orsen screamed it into the noise, scrambling up the slope through the wet ferns, Ila shouting behind her. "ANSELM — THE CHAPEL —"

The sister was already there. Of course she was already there. Through the smoke Orsen saw her small, straight-backed figure in the chapel doorway, and saw her go inside, and saw the door close behind her with the same deliberate click as the teacup, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water filling her lungs, exactly what the old woman intended to do.

* * *

The gunners held fire for ninety seconds. Orsen counted them. She crouched at the edge of the clearing with mud to her chin and her heart going like a dive watch counting down, watching the lead helicopter hover twenty meters off the chapel roof, its door gunner visible in silhouette, weapon traversing, waiting — waiting for the old woman to come out, perhaps. Waiting for orders. Waiting for someone else to make the decision that history would have to carry.

Sister Anselm came out. She carried the cedar chest in both arms, held before her like an offering, like a burden, like a child. She walked down the chapel steps into the open ground between the burning bell tower and the hovering guns, and she stood there, small and straight and utterly unhidden, and she looked up at the helicopter.

From the tree line, Orsen heard her voice — thin with distance, thin with age, but carrying across the clearing the way a bell carries, the way truth carries when it has been rehearsed by seventy years of saying difficult things out loud:

"You are too late! It is copied! It is sent! Kill me and you kill no one who matters!"

Whether that was even true, Orsen never learned. Perhaps the runners had gone out at dawn; perhaps the sister was buying their escape with a bluff. It didn't matter. What mattered was the door gunner's silhouette shifting, the muzzle dipping, the terrible mechanical patience of it —

— and Orsen was up and running before she knew she'd decided anything, screaming, waving her arms, offering herself, *here, here, look here* — and the gunner swung toward her, and in that half-second of transferred attention Sister Anselm turned and hurled the cedar chest with all the strength left in her old arms, high and far, into the deep fern brake below the chapel wall —

— and the world went white and loud and endless.

* * *

The helicopters left eleven minutes after they arrived. They strafed the airstrip once more on the way out, methodically, and dropped something incendiary onto the mission roofs, and rose back into the cloud deck as casually as herons leaving a flooded field. The noise faded upriver, north, toward the source, toward the cave, toward everything Orsen had not yet reached. They knew where she was going. They had always known.

She found the chest first. It had landed thirty meters downslope, cracked along one seam, its contents scattered in the ferns — photographs slick with rain, pages stamped with the seal of a dead flag, ink already beginning to run. She gathered them with shaking hands, stuffing them inside her shirt against her skin, and only then did she let herself walk to where the sister lay.

She knelt. There was very little blood, which seemed obscene. The old woman's eyes were open, turned toward the sky, and her face held an expression Orsen recognized from the drowned village, from the stilt houses, from every person along this river who had lost something unspeakable and refused to lie down under it: not peace, exactly. Defiance, finished.

Ila crouched beside her, breathing hard, and after a moment he took off his hat and held it against his chest. Around them the survivors were coming down out of the trees, silent, and beginning — without being told, without weeping yet — to dig.

"They knew," Orsen said. Her voice didn't sound like hers. "They knew exactly where the copies were. Someone told them. Someone—" She stopped. She thought of Voss, falsifying position reports somewhere downstream, buying her hours with his career. She thought of the radio traffic she'd never hear. She thought of how many people along this river had known about Kiunga Bend, and how few of those names she'd ever learn.

"It doesn't matter who," Ila said quietly. "It matters that she was right." He nodded at the papers against Orsen's ribs. "Copied. Sent. She made sure of it before you ever got here, woman. Nuns are sneakier than salvagers."

Orsen looked down at the grave the villagers were digging, at the smoke still rising off the mission roofs, at the child from the tree line who stood at the edge of the clearing staring at the burned bell tower with her hands at her sides now, no longer covering her ears, as if she had decided she would hear everything the world did from now on and flinch at none of it.

Fury came into Orsen then, and it did not burn hot. It settled into her like ballast, cold and permanent, shifting her center of gravity forever. Helplessness sat beside it, its twin. She had run up this river telling herself that moving fast meant hurting fewer people, and every stop had cost a life, and now the cost had a face and a name and a teacup set down with a deliberate click, and there was nothing left to do with any of it except carry it higher.

"The cave," she said. "I leave tonight. Alone."

Ila started to object. She looked at him, and he closed his mouth, and after a moment he said only: "The cartographer's daughter's route goes past a falls. You'll need rope. I'll get you rope."

Above the valley, unseen in the cloud deck, the helicopters droned north, and Orsen Quintana stood in the rain among strangers digging a grave, holding a dead nation's secrets against her heartbeat, and began — finally, irrevocably — to climb.
