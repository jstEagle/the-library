## 15. Source of the Fly

The mist did not lift so much as it thinned, the way grief thins — never gone, only spread wider until you could move through it.

Orsen climbed out of the hamlet before dawn, while the old woman was still banking her fire and the dogs were still asleep under the stilt houses. She had told no one she was leaving that morning. The cartographer's daughter had drawn the final route on a strip of bark with a charred stick: follow the stream that ran cold instead of warm, past the falls where the water fell in three threads, up through the saddle of grey stone, and there, where the river was born from rock, look for the cave behind the curtain of roots. *My father walked it once a year,* the old woman had said. *He came back thinner every time. He said the mountain ate a little of him on each visit.* Then she had pressed the bark into Orsen's hands and folded her own arms, refusing the journey, refusing even to watch her go. Some debts are paid by staying. Orsen had understood it then the way you understand things at night, half-heard, half-believed. By morning she understood it completely.

She carried what she had carried since the confluence: the wax-sealed map, Tomas's last letter softened soft as cloth from handling, a machete, a tin of matches, two days of rice wrapped in banana leaf, and Ila's knife, which he had given her before she left him recovering in the hamlet — *so you have something sharp that isn't for cutting people*, he'd said, though they both knew better. Her boots were ruined. The leech country had eaten them from the inside, stitching rot into the seams, and each step now announced itself with a squelch like a heartbeat in the wrong place. But her legs were good. Her lungs were good. Ten years of diving had taught her body to work in places where air was rationed, where panic was a luxury billed by the minute, and the mountain, whatever else it meant to do to her, could not drown her. That was the one thing it could not do.

The stream that ran cold led her true.

By midmorning she was above the tree line's first thinning, walking through forest so old and wet that moss grew on all four sides of every trunk, hanging in beards, swallowing sound. There were no birdsong here above a certain altitude — or there was, but it came from somewhere else, distant, filtered, like music played in another room. The silence had texture. It pressed against her ears the way deep water did, and she found herself breathing the way she breathed at thirty meters: slow, deliberate, spending nothing.

She thought about Tomas the whole way up, but differently than she had in the swamps. Down in the leech country, feverish, she had needed him — his voice, his patience, the summer he taught her to swim in the delta shallows by making her float on her back and look at the sky until floating stopped being frightening and became simply another way of standing. That memory had been a rope. Now, climbing, she didn't need him. She was going toward him, or toward whatever remained of him, and need had curdled into something colder and more precise. A question with a shape. *Did you know? Did you find it first? Is that why the river took you — or why someone did?*

His last letter had matched phrases in the documents. Sister Anselm had confirmed it before the helicopters came, before the strafing runs, before Orsen had knelt in the mission garden beside a woman who had spent forty years translating other people's suffering and died holding copies of this same truth. *He dived the wreck months before he died,* Anselm had said, in the calm voice she used for unbearable things. *And what he wrote home — these phrases, here and here — they are not similar, child. They are quotations.*

Orsen climbed faster after that. Not because speed helped. Because stillness hurt more.

* * *

The falls appeared in early afternoon, three white threads falling perhaps sixty meters down a face of black stone, and the sound of them arrived before the sight — a humming in the rocks themselves, felt through her boots. She drank without stopping, filled her canteen, and began the traverse across the saddle. The grey stone was slick with condensation, ribbed like the inside of a shell, and she moved along it the way she moved along a wreck's hull underwater, testing each foothold twice, committing only when commitment couldn't be undone. Below her the valley opened green and enormous, the river visible now as a bright thread stitching its way down toward everything she had fled: the gorge, the drowned village, the mission, the delta, the pier where Dario Voss had stood asking questions with such terrible gentleness.

From up here none of it looked real. From up here it looked like a map — which was, she supposed, exactly what it was. The cartographer's father had stood somewhere on this slope with his instruments and his fear, drawing the world he was about to help hide something inside of. Orsen touched the bark strip in her pocket and felt the charred lines through the fabric. Inheritance of witness. The phrase kept returning to her, unbidden, wearing the old woman's voice.

She found the cave an hour before dusk.

It was exactly where the bark promised: behind a curtain of aerial roots that poured down the cliff face like brown water frozen mid-fall. She pushed through them and the roots closed behind her, and the sound of the outside world — the wind, the falls, the vast echoing valley — cut off all at once, replaced by a hollow dripping quiet. Her eyes took a long time to adjust. When they did, her breath stopped.

The cave opened inward into a chamber tall as a church, and it was full.

Not rubble. Not bones. Crates. Dozens of them, stacked with a care that spoke of ritual rather than storage, each one wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff and dark with age, each one marked with the seal she had first seen stamped on a brass fitting in the silt of a navy-cordoned wreck — the eagle-and-star of a nation that had ceased to exist before Orsen was old enough to remember it existing. The seal was everywhere. On the crates. On the wall, painted large in faded pigment, like a flag hung in a tomb. On small tin boxes arranged on a stone shelf like votive offerings.

Orsen stood in the entrance a long time without moving. The awe was physical; it sat on her chest like a pressure change. Somewhere below her, men with helicopters and mining money and government telephones were willing to burn villages to keep this room empty-handed. And here it all was, patient as silt, waiting longer than any of them had been alive.

She lit one match, then a candle stub from her pack, and went in.

* * *

The first crate held paper. Reams of it, typed and handwritten both, bundled in oilcloth packets tied with string, each packet labeled in that dead language whose characters she had learned to recognize if not read — the same script that covered the documents in the iron chest, the script Sister Anselm had begun translating in the weeks before the airstrip. Testimony. That was the word Anselm had used, and it was the right word. Orsen untied one bundle and the pages whispered open like something exhaling.

Names. Dates. Places she knew — some of them villages she had passed on the barge, some of them names of drowned places, flooded by the dam, erased from maps that had already been redrawn twice. Statements signed with thumbprints in faded brown ink, because the men who made them could not write but could not stay silent either. One page bore a long account in a careful schoolroom hand, describing a week in the last month of the war, describing soldiers, describing a bend in the river, describing what was done there to people who had offered nothing but their proximity. Anselm had translated fragments of this. Orsen had heard the words spoken aloud in a mission kitchen over kerosene light, and hearing them had been bad enough. Reading them, alone, in the cave where they had been hidden by hands that hoped someone would someday come — that was different. That was the difference between hearing a drowning described and watching the water close over someone's head.

There were photographs. Hundreds, sleeved in oiled silk. Orsen made herself look at eleven of them and then made herself stop, because she understood that looking was a duty and that duties, unlike appetites, must be rationed to be survived. She would look at all of them eventually. Not tonight. Tonight she had one thing to find, and she was afraid of finding it, and she was more afraid of leaving without finding it, and between those two fears she moved through the crates with a diver's discipline: systematic, unhurried, checking each marker against the list in her mind that Anselm had recited — *the cartographer's crates are marked with a blue thread; the military ones with red; the civilian testimonies with plain string.*

Blue thread. Fourth crate from the wall, second layer.

She cut the string with Ila's knife.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, were the cartographer's own papers: survey notes, the original drawings — ancestors of the map burning a hole in her pocket — correspondence in three languages, and beneath all of it, flat and separate, a folder of statements collected from outsiders. Men who had not belonged to the dead nation. Men who had seen, and been positioned to survive seeing, and chosen to write it down anyway and smuggle it here, up the river, into the mist, into the keeping of a mountain that asked a little piece of each visitor in payment.

Orsen lifted the folder. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and was grateful, because inside the steadiness something was already screaming.

The third statement in the folder was signed in a hand she knew.

* * *

She knew it the way you know a voice in the dark — instantly, totally, before thought has any say in the matter. The forward slant of it. The way the crossbar of the *t* slashed through the neighboring letters. The little hook he put on his *y*s that their mother had tried for years to correct and finally surrendered to, saying, *let him have one thing that's only his.*

*Tomas Quintana.*

Her brother's name, written in her brother's hand, dated fourteen months before the river gave him back to her facedown in the delta current.

Orsen sat down on the stone floor. She did not decide to sit; her body simply ended its argument with gravity. The candle flame leaned in some draft from the tunnel mouth and the shadows of the crates swung across the walls like slow water, and she read.

*I, Tomas Quintana, salvage diver, working the wreck locally known as the Old Consul, do make the following statement freely and without payment...*

He had found the chest first. Months before Ila Mero ever heard of it, months before the stranger with gold came to the pier, months before Dario Voss arrived with his courtesy and his orders — Tomas had gone down into the silt of the Fly River with his own hands and his own lamp and brought up the truth. He had opened it. He had read it. And then, instead of selling it, instead of running, he had copied what he could, carried what he could, and made the climb she was making now — the cold stream, the three-thread falls, the grey saddle, the root curtain — and deposited his statement here among strangers' testimony, filed under blue thread, in the keeping of the cartographer's tradition.

*...I understand that carrying this knowledge may cost me my life. I accept this. What I do not accept is that it should cost me my silence. My sister will tell you I have never been able to keep my mouth shut. Let this be the one exception that proves her right: I am writing instead of speaking, and I am hiding instead of shouting, because shouting gets people killed faster than writing does. If you are reading this, then either I got out, or I didn't. Either way, the words got out. That was the whole plan. That was always the whole plan.*

Orsen read it four times.

The first time she read it as evidence. The second time she read it as history. The third time she read it as a letter, and realized it was one — addressed to whoever came, which was always, secretly, going to be her. He had known she would come. Not hoped. Known. Because he had written the line about her mouth, the joke that was theirs alone, the joke from a hundred kitchen tables, and jokes like that are not written for archives. They are written for sisters.

The fourth time she did not read it at all. She held it, and the candle burned lower, and the devastation that had been building behind her steadiness all evening came through the levee all at once.

He had found the truth first. He had carried it alone. He had hidden it where only someone relentless enough — someone like him, someone like her — would ever follow. And then he had gone back down to the delta, back to the wrecks and the patrol boats and the men who had killed a nation and would kill again to keep it buried, and he had drowned. Facedown. Alone. Officially an accident, officially a cramp, officially the river doing what rivers do.

But the river did not write official findings. Men did.

Orsen knelt on the stone floor of the archive and wept the way she had not wept at his funeral, where she had been too numb, or in the years since, where she had been too controlled, or in the swamps, where she had been too busy staying alive. She wept for the boy who taught her to float by making her look at the sky. She wept for the man who had solved the mystery of his own death fourteen months before it happened and had walked into it anyway with his eyes open, because the words mattered more than the body carrying them. She wept because he had trusted her — not with a note, not with instructions, but with a trail, a trail only she could read, laid through wrecks and chests and seals and strangers, winding up a river into a mountain, ending here, at a candle, at a folder, at a signature.

*That was always the whole plan.*

She laughed once, brokenly, in the middle of it, because of course it was. Of course her brother — restless, clever, incapable of leaving anything sunk — had planned his own posthumous rescue operation and staffed it with the only diver in the family stubborn enough to complete it. He had hired her from beyond the water. He had sent her upstream. And she had come, hauling guilt and curiosity and a dead flag's secrets, believing she was chasing a mystery, when in fact she had been answering a summons the entire time.

When the crying finished, the candle was nearly gone. Orsen wiped her face with a sleeve that had no dry part left, folded Tomas's statement with the care owed to a relic, and placed it inside her shirt against her skin, where the map also lived, where the letter lived, where everything that could not be allowed to sink now rode above her heart.

Then she sat in the dark and took inventory.

Crates of testimony. Photographs beyond counting. Names — hundreds of names, witnesses and victims both, preserved against every effort to unname them. Enough paper to fill a courtroom. Enough truth to fracture three governments and a consortium, if it could be gotten out, if anyone survived getting it out, if the world beyond the mist still wanted truth badly enough to pay what it cost. She thought of the drowned village on its stilts, begging her to burn it all. She thought of Sister Anselm dying over photocopies. She thought of Ila bleeding in the jungle because information, in his experience, was just another word for bloodshed with a delay built in. Every person who had touched this secret had paid. The mountain took its tithe. The river took its due.

And yet here she was, kneeling in the treasury of the dead, holding her brother's living voice against her sternum.

Outside, the mist was turning the color of pewter. Night came fast at this altitude, pouring down the valley like water into a hull. Orsen relit the candle, chose one crate — the smallest, the one packed by hands that had labeled it, in that dead language, with a word Anselm had once translated for her over kerosene light: *beginnings* — and began, methodically, with a diver's patience, to figure out how a woman carries a mountain's worth of truth down a mountain alone.

She was halfway through repacking the crate when she heard it.

A sound from the tunnel mouth. Not wind. Not water. Not the drip and settle of the cave breathing. Footsteps — deliberate, spaced, climbing — and then a light, moving through the root curtain, throwing long shadows ahead of it into the chamber where she knelt among the crates of a murdered nation with her brother's confession against her heart.

Someone had followed her up the cold stream. Someone was standing at the threshold of the archive, silhouetted against the last grey light, and the shape of the shoulders, the set of the head, was one she knew better than her own handwriting.

Dario Voss stepped out of the roots, unarmed, his uniform jacket gone, and looked at her across the crates with an expression she had never once seen him wear in all the years she had loved him and all the years she had hated him:

He looked afraid.
