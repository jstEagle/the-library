## 16. The Choice at the Cave Mouth

The cave breathed cold. It came up out of the limestone in slow exhalations, carrying the mineral smell of deep water and old stone, and it moved the flame of Orsen's lamp so that the shadows of the crates leaned and swayed like a congregation deciding whether to rise.

She did not lower the lamp. She held it between herself and Dario Voss the way she would have held a knife, and she watched him take another step into the light.

He looked wrong without the jacket. She had known him in dress whites at the Kiunga regatta, known him in oilskins on the delta piers, known him once, memorably, in nothing at all, standing hip-deep in the warm black water off D'Albertis Point with his hands full of her hair — but never like this: shirt sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms bare and pale where the sun had never reached them, no insignia anywhere on him. A man unmade. He had walked out of his own life and left the uniform hanging in it like a shed skin.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. "Or arrested. One of the two."

"Arrested." His voice was rough, as if he'd been shouting at altitude or not speaking for days. "I filed my last position report from the mission station at Yulai. I reported my patrol boat aground on a sandbar with engine failure. By tomorrow morning there will be three search parties looking for me in places I am not." He almost smiled. "It's the first lie I've ever told them that felt like telling the truth."

"How did you find me?"

"The cartographer's daughter drew me a map." He said it simply, and something in her chest turned over, because she had carried that woman's directions folded against her heart for six days through leech country, and had believed them hers alone. "She said you'd need someone to walk you down."

"I didn't need anyone walked down."

"No," he agreed. "You never have."

He came closer, slowly, the way a man approaches a dog he has kicked before, and stopped an arm's length from the nearest crate. Up close she could see what the climb had cost him — the raw scrape along his jaw where he'd fallen on the scree, the tremor in his right hand that he kept trying to still by closing it. He had not slept. He had maybe not eaten. And yet his eyes went past her, past the lamp, to the crates themselves, and she watched him read the stenciled markings on the nearest one the way she had watched him read tide tables: completely, hungrily, all at once.

"So it's real," he said quietly. "All of it."

"Real enough to kill for. You should know. You've been killing for it since the delta."

"I haven't killed anyone, Orsen." He said it flatly, without defensiveness, which somehow made it worse. "Not for lack of orders."

* * *

They sat among the crates because there was nowhere else to sit, two people who had once shared a bed in a room above a chandlery now sharing a cave with the dead. She built a small fire at the mouth — small, screened, smokeless fuel from Sister Anselm's stores, because she had learned what smoke meant now — and they ate hard biscuit and tinned fish and drank water from the stream in cupped hands, and for a while neither of them spoke, because some silences are arguments already underway.

It was Voss who broke it, and he broke it the way he broke everything: carefully, having thought about exactly where the pieces would fall.

"I've read the reports," he said. "Not these ones. The other kind. The ones that circulate at headquarters with the classification stamps blacked out and the names removed. I've spent eleven years reading around the edges of this thing, Orsen. The massacre at Wawoi Falls. The convoy that went upriver in the last month of the war and came back with its records sealed. The witnesses who signed statements and then—" he gestured vaguely upstream, at everything, "—stopped existing. Fishing accidents. Fevers. Men who drowned in their own villages in two feet of water."

"Tomas didn't drown in two feet of water."

"No," Voss said. "He didn't."

The words landed in her like ballast dropped. She had known it — had forged the knowing herself out of documents and grief — but hearing it from him, from the voice of the institution itself, made it structural. Made it load-bearing.

"He found this place first," she said. "Fifteen years ago. There's a statement in there with his signature on it. He interviewed survivors when he was barely older than I am now. He wrote down names, Dario. Forty-one names. He wrote down what was done to them, in his own hand, and he signed it, and he sent copies—" her voice caught and she hated it, "—he sent copies somewhere, and then he went diving alone off a wreck everyone refuses to name, and the river closed over him, and every government on this river spent the next fifteen years making sure nobody asked why."

Voss fed a twig to the fire. Watched it catch.

"If you carry those crates out," he said, "you will bury him a second time."

"Don't you dare—"

"Listen to me." He turned to face her fully, and she saw that his eyes were wet and that he was letting them be, not hiding it, not blinking it away, and that frightened her more than any weapon could have. "I know how it sounds. I know what it costs me to say it. But I'm saying it because I love you and because I have read the same intelligence summaries your brother's killers read, and I am telling you what they will do." He counted it off on his fingers, quiet as a litany. "One: the consortium's aircraft find this cave within the week — they have infrared and helicopters and we have a fire the size of a dinner plate. Two: whatever is in these crates goes into a furnace in Port Moresby or Canberra or wherever men with clean hands do their burning. Three: every person who helped you — Ila Mero, the sisters at the mission, the hamlet that sheltered you, the old woman who gave you the map — every single name enters a file, and files like that get settled. Four: the story dies. Not loudly. Quietly. The way Tomas died. The way the village at Wawoi died. Nobody hears a shot. The river keeps it. The river always keeps it."

"And if I burn it?"

"Then they win by accident instead of design, and the people who helped you live." He spread his hands. "That's the arithmetic, Orsen. That's all it is. I've done the sums a hundred times on the way up here and they never come out any other way. Some truths are too heavy to carry out alive. You don't smuggle a verdict out of a country. You need a court, a press, a government willing to hang itself — and there isn't one. Not on this river. Not for this."

"There's always one," she said. "There's always one reporter, one archivist, one priest. Sister Anselm translated half of this with her own hands and she said—" Her throat closed on the memory: the old woman's ink-stained fingers on the brittle paper, the lamplight, the names being spoken aloud one by one like a rosary. "She said a witness only dies when the last person who heard them stops repeating it. She's dead now, Dario. They killed her at the airstrip. So I'm the last person who heard them. I'm what's left of forty-one voices and my brother's handwriting, and you are asking me to be quiet."

"I'm asking you to live."

"That's not the same thing!" She was on her feet without remembering standing, the firelight jumping on the walls, on the crates, on his upturned face. "You think I haven't done the sums? You think I haven't lain awake in Ila's hold doing exactly your arithmetic, weighing Ila's blood against Tomas's truth? I have. Every night. And here's what your sums leave out, Lieutenant: silence doesn't protect anyone. It just decides in advance who's allowed to die next. The consortium burned a hamlet to reach me. They'll burn the next one whether I'm carrying papers or not, because the papers exist, because *I* exist, because Tomas existed — the secret is already loose, it's been loose for fifteen years, and the only question left is whether it dies in a cave or comes down the river with my fingerprints on it."

"They'll intercept it."

"Maybe."

"They'll bury it and bury you with it."

"Maybe." She crouched, took his face in both hands — his jaw rough under her palms, his skin cold from the climb — and made him look at her. "But listen to me now. My brother dove that wreck knowing what it was. He wrote the truth down knowing what it would cost. He didn't do it because he thought he'd win. He did it because a thing witnessed is a thing that happened, and a thing written down can't be unhappened, no matter how many furnaces they feed. If I burn this, they don't just kill him twice — they make it so he never mattered. And I will not. I will not do that to him. Even if it buys the whole river's safety, I won't trade his name for it, because that's the one currency they haven't figured out how to counterfeit."

For a long moment he said nothing. The fire ticked. Somewhere far below, the young Fly ran over stones, indifferent, eternal, carrying everything toward the sea.

"You sound like him," Voss said finally, and his voice cracked on it. "Your letters. The ones you used to write me from the salvage camps. You sound like him."

"I am all that's left of him," she said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

He didn't agree. That was the thing she would remember afterward, in all the years that followed — that he never actually agreed. What he did was worse and better: he reached into the breast pocket of his ruined shirt and took out a folded sheet of paper, soft as cloth from handling, and laid it on the crate between them.

"What's that?"

"My resignation. Unsigned. I wrote it at the mission station, before I started climbing." He laughed, short and joyless. "I told myself I'd deliver it either way. If I talked you into burning it, I'd resign and go home and try to forget your face a third time. If I couldn't talk you out of it—" he looked at the crates, at the stenciled seals of a nation that had ceased to exist before either of them was born, "—then I resign anyway, and I carry crates. Because you're right about one thing, Orsen. Silence doesn't protect anyone. I've been silent for eleven years and it hasn't protected a single soul, least of all me."

"So which is it?"

"I don't know yet." He said it honestly, terribly honestly, and she loved him for it even as fear climbed her spine. "Ask me again when they get here."

"Who says they're—"

The sound came up the gorge then, thin and wrong, a sound that did not belong to the highlands at all: the flat mechanical stutter of rotors, still distant, still behind the western ridge, but coming. Both of them went still, the way prey goes still, and the fire seemed suddenly enormous, a beacon, a confession.

Voss was on his feet, and when he turned back to her his face had changed — the hesitation burned away, the arithmetic finished, whatever sum he'd been doing all night come at last to its total.

"They're early," he said. "Or I'm late. Either way—" he kicked earth over the fire, fast, methodical, a man dismantling his own hesitation piece by piece, "—get the crates to the back wall. Now. And Orsen—" he caught her arm, and his grip was steady at last, the tremor gone, decided, "—whatever happens in the next hour, I need you to know I've already chosen. I chose on the scree, halfway up, when I realized I'd rather be treasonous with you than correct without you. Ask me again anytime you like. The answer's not going to change."

The rotors swelled in the gorge below, and the mist at the cave mouth began, very faintly, to shiver.
