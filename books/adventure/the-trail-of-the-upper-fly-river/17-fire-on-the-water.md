## 17. Fire on the Water

The mist came up the valley at dusk the way it always did, pouring over the lip of the headwall like slow milk, and Orsen watched it swallow the lower trail and thought: *now*.

They would come in the mist. She had known it since noon, when the birds went quiet all at once above the gorge — not gradually, not settling into evening song, but cut off, the way sound is cut off when a door closes. The highland men who had guided her up from the hamlet had heard it too. Old Kowa had touched her elbow without looking at her and said only, "They walk below," and turned back down the mountain to bring word to Ila.

Now she stood at the cave mouth with the whole of the dead nation's testimony at her back — crates of it, forty years of names and photographs and signed confessions rotting gently in the cold dry dark — and she counted what she had.

One naval officer turned traitor, crouched at her left with a service pistol he'd kept against every regulation that mattered. One fuel drum, half full of aviation gasoline, hauled up from the abandoned strip on a sledge made of crate lids. One coil of fuse wire, one box of matches gone soft with damp, one machete, and herself.

Below, in the blue dark of the valley, something moved that was not mist.

"You should be downstream by now." Voss didn't look at her when he said it. He'd stopped looking at her somewhere past the second switchback of the climb, as if the sight of her had become a thing his discipline couldn't afford. "Ila's people could have carried the crates out through the karst caves. You know that. Kowa told you the passages go all the way to the Mubi."

"I know it." The mist beaded on her forearms. "And I know what happens to passages once the consortium learns they exist. They don't seal them, Dario. They flood them. They flood everything." She nodded at the cave behind them, at the crates stacked like a small city of the dead. "This is the last dry place those names will ever have. If they're going to burn it, they're going to burn it with witnesses."

"That's not courage, Orsen. That's just arithmetic with your life as the remainder."

"Then you understand me perfectly," she said, "because you've been doing the same sum since Kiunga Bend."

He almost smiled. In the failing light she saw it move across his mouth and die there, and she thought about the pier at the delta, three lifetimes ago, when his courtesy had been a wall built brick by brick between them, and how she had hated him for it, and how she understood now that the wall had been load-bearing — that some part of him had been holding up the weight of his orders every hour they'd spoken, and if he'd let any of it show, the whole structure would have come down on both of them.

"Listen to me," he said, and now he did look at her, and his eyes were the flat grey-green of deep water, the color she had drowned in once, gladly, years ago, in another country, in another life where neither of them had owed anything to anyone. "When they come up the trail, they'll send two ahead. Scouts. Consortium contractors work in fives — you saw that at the confluence. The two ahead will be good. The three behind will be better, because they'll be the ones whose job is killing, not looking."

"And the drum?"

"The drum goes over when the scouts reach the bend. Not before." He traced the trail's line in the air with one finger. "It rolls there" — the finger stopped at a shelf of rock where the path narrowed between a fallen slab and the drop — "and the gasoline spreads across the slab, and we give it fire, and the slab gives it back down the mountain. Everything behind the flame is blind. Everything in front of it is ours."

"Ours," she repeated.

"For about four minutes." He checked the pistol's magazine with a motion so practiced it looked like nothing at all, a man smoothing a cuff. "After that we're two people with a machete and six rounds against three professionals on their own terrain. So when I say run, Orsen—"

"I know. You've said it eleven times since the airstrip."

"Twelve," he said. "You weren't listening the first time."

She laughed — actually laughed, a short helpless sound that the mist took away immediately — and he looked at her then with an expression she hadn't seen on him since before Tomas died, since a night on a boat deck in another sea when they'd been young and unburdened and stupid with happiness, and she had to turn away from it, because that expression was more dangerous than the mercenaries. That expression could make her want things. Wanting things was how people died on this river. Everyone who helped her became a target; everyone she loved became fuel.

Below them, at the tree line, a flashlight blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again.

"They're signaling the top," Voss breathed. "Positions."

Orsen put her hand on the drum. The metal was cold and sweating under her palm, and through it she could feel, faintly, absurdly, the heartbeat of the whole valley — water moving somewhere far beneath the limestone, the river that began here as a seep and ended a thousand miles away as a brown god carrying half a nation's silt to the sea. Tomas had loved this river. Tomas had found its secret first and been killed for it, and now she stood at its source with his confession in a waterproof pouch against her ribs, and she was done running.

"Light on my mark," she said. "Not yours. Mine."

Voss opened his mouth — she could feel the objection forming, the whole twelve-step argument — and then closed it. He nodded once.

"*Yes,*" he said, and it sounded almost like surrender, and almost like something else entirely.

* * *

They came up through the mist the way she'd imagined they would: two shapes first, low and fluid, moving between the rocks with the unhurried economy of men who had done this in worse places than a Papuan headwall. Then, further down, three more, spread wide, rifles slung forward, faces blacked. The lead scout paused at the bend below the slab, knelt, and swept his light along the ground — and found, exactly where Voss had predicted, the scuffed drag-marks of a sledge leading toward the cave.

He signaled. The others moved up.

Orsen waited until the second scout's boot came down on the slab itself, until the three behind had committed to the narrows below, until the whole five of them were strung out along the trail like beads on a wire — and then she put her shoulder to the drum and pushed.

The drum went over the edge with a sound like a struck bell, hit the slab, and rolled. It gathered speed with a horrible grace, bounding once, twice, shedding a silver rope of gasoline behind it, and the lead scout looked up into its path with just enough time to throw himself sideways — too late, too slow, the drum caught his rifle arm and spun him off the trail into the dark, and the gasoline sheeted out across the wet stone in a fan that reached the narrows just as the second scout turned to run back down.

Voss fired the flare pistol he'd taken off a dead man at the confluence.

The world turned orange.

Flame ran down the slab like something alive, like a creature released rather than ignited, and the roar of it filled the gorge and came back off the walls doubled. The narrows became a furnace door. Orsen heard screaming — one voice, then muffled, then nothing — and felt the scream pass through her own body without stopping, because there was no time to feel it in, because the two scouts above the fireline were still alive and coming, one of them already firing blind through the smoke, rounds cracking off the rock above her head in bright stone-shrapnel stutters.

"Move!" Voss hauled her by the collar into the cave mouth, and they dropped behind the first crate stack as the smoke rolled in after them, acrid and thick, laced with the sweet reek of burning gasoline and, underneath it, the older smell of the cave itself — paper, wax, iron, time.

A shape lunged out of the smoke at the cave mouth. Voss shot it twice without standing up, without seeming to aim, the way a man swats a fly in the dark, and the shape folded and slid back down into the orange glow and did not reappear.

Four rounds. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four rounds, one machete, and somewhere below, through the fire, the last mercenary — the professional, the patient one, the kind who waits.

Silence, except for the fire eating the mountainside and the river whispering under everything.

"He'll circle," Voss whispered. "Up and around through the rocks. He'll come from above the cave. They always—" 

Gravel ticked. Above them. At the lip of the cliff overhanging the cave mouth, where the mist poured over like milk.

Voss moved faster than she had ever seen a man move. He didn't shout a warning, didn't waste breath; he simply stepped out from cover into the open, into the exact line between the cliff and where she crouched, and raised the pistol at the darkness overhead —

— and the rifle spoke from above, once, flat and final as a book closing.

Voss sat down. There was no other way to describe it; he simply sat, mid-stride, as if he'd decided to rest, and then he tipped slowly sideways against the crates, and Orsen caught him, and her hands came away red.

"No," she said. It wasn't a word so much as a reflex, the body's dumb refusal. "No no no — Dario—"

"Above," he said. His voice was already thinning, going distant, a radio losing signal. "He's reloading. Orsen. *Above.*"

She laid him down among the crates of the dead — among the testimony, among the names, among the photographs of men who had also bled on this ground for the same reason — and she took the machete, and she went up.

* * *

Later, she would never be able to assemble those ninety seconds into an order she could live inside. The climbing, yes: hands finding holds in the wet rock, the firelight painting her shadow huge and wavering on the cliff face, blood on her palms that was not hers. The ledge, yes: the mercenary kneeling with his back to her, working the bolt of his rifle with calm fingers, a professional finishing a task. Even the moment itself she could hold: the machete taken in both hands, the swing, the impact, the rifle clattering away over the edge into the fire-glow, the man turning with a knife already out and her meeting him chest to chest on a ledge barely wider than a coffin, the two of them locked together above a burning mountain, his breath hot against her ear, her arms shaking, the machete gone, her fingers finding the knife hand and bending it back and back and back until something gave that was not hers to give.

What she could not assemble was the falling. His weight leaving hers. The long silence of it. And then the sound, far below, swallowed by fire.

She stayed on the ledge. Her legs would not negotiate. Below her the mountainside burned in a great slow ring, and beyond the fire the mist was returning, closing over the valley like a bandage over a wound, and somewhere in that whiteness — she heard it before she believed it — drums.

Not drums. Feet. Many feet, descending fast, and voices in the highland tongue, and one voice cutting through all of them, cracked and hoarse and unmistakable, a voice that had spent three weeks teaching her that everyone who helped her died:

*"ORSEN!"*

Ila Mero came up the trail through the smoke with twenty men behind him and a bandage wrapped around the shoulder wound that should have killed him at the confluence, and when he reached her he did not ask what had happened, did not ask about the bodies or the fire or the blood on her; he simply took her face in both hands and looked at her the way you look at someone you had already begun to mourn, and said, "We heard the drum from two valleys away. We thought — we thought they'd got you."

"They nearly did." Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Dario's in the cave. He's shot. Through the—" She couldn't finish it. "Ila, he stepped in front of me. He just *stepped*—"

"Go to him." Ila was already turning, shouting orders, sending men to the ledges and the trailhead, setting watchers against whatever might follow. "Go. We'll hold the mountain tonight. Go to him, salvager."

She went.

* * *

Voss was conscious. That was the first mercy and possibly the cruelest thing about it, because a man who is conscious can talk, and Voss talked.

Kowa's people had carried him into the deepest chamber, among the crates, and done what could be done with cloth and boiled water and the missionary's old medical kit that Sister Anselm had pressed into Orsen's hands at Kiunga Bend, back when Sister Anselm was alive to press things into anyone's hands. The bullet had gone in below the collarbone and out the back, missing everything that kills quickly and hitting enough that kills slowly. Everyone in the chamber knew it. Only Voss pretended otherwise, and he did his pretending in a low, even voice, giving instructions.

"The copies," he said, when Orsen knelt beside him. "In the double hull. You have to get them to the coast. The consortium vessel — the *Karawari Star* — she puts in at the delta Thursday tides. If the archive surfaces with the newspapers before the consortium can buy the story, it's over. Truly over. Not buried-quietly over. Over over."

"I'm not leaving you here."

"Yes, you are." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she had ever seen, that smile, because it was the real one, the boat-deck one, the one from before. "That's the whole point of me, Orsen. Did you never work that out? Every wall I ever built, every courtesy, every order I followed — it was all so that at the end of it I could stand in front of you exactly once, at the correct moment, and be useful." His breath caught. He rode through it with his eyes open, refusing it. "Falsified position reports. Treason. Court-martial. I was finished the day I keyed that radio. This is just the paperwork arriving early."

"Dario—"

"Tomas found the truth first," he said. "Carry it out for him. Both of them. The statement and the man." His hand found hers, slick and weak and certain. "And Orsen — when it's done — stop moving. Promise me. You've been running since the delta. Stop. Somewhere green. Somewhere with clean water. Promise me you'll let yourself stop."

Above them, faintly, the fire crackled its way down the mountainside, and the mist came in through the cave mouth, and the river whispered under everything, the way it had whispered for ten thousand years, keeping what it keeps.

She bent close, so that only he could hear, and made the promise.

And then, from the dark of the outer chamber, Ila's voice, urgent and strange: "Orsen. You need to see this. There's a boat coming up the river — and it's flying no flag at all."
