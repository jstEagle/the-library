## 18. Downstream in a Rival's Hull

The *Meridian Star* came upriver on the third morning after the fire at the cave mouth, riding low in the water with her holds full of consortium equipment and her hull painted the flat gray of official indifference. Orsen watched her from the high bank above the confluence, lying flat in the fern cover with Ila's binoculars pressed to her eyes, and she felt something turn over inside her that was not quite hope and not quite despair — a third thing, colder and more patient than either.

The ship was beautiful in the way that instruments are beautiful. Twin cranes folded along her deck like sleeping insects. Her superstructure sat aft, white-painted, air-conditioned, absurd this far into the green throat of the world. And along her waterline, just visible where the current peeled away from her bow in a long V of brown foam, ran the seam of a double hull — the salvage configuration, the void space between skins that kept a working vessel alive when the river tried to bite through her.

Orsen lowered the binoculars and lay still for a long time, listening to the river move beneath her.

"They'll run her down to the delta," Ila said beside her. He was sitting up now, finally, his wound bound in fresh cloth Sister Anselm had boiled and boiled again before the helicopters came. His color was better. His hands still shook when he thought no one was watching. "They always do. Equipment, ore samples, whatever they pulled out of the ground — it all goes down to Kiunga, then Port Moresby, then wherever the money lives."

"That's what I'm counting on."

Ila looked at her. He had that expression he got sometimes, the one like a man watching weather come in over water he'd sworn was calm. "You want to put the archive *inside* them."

"I want to put it where no one will look for it." She turned the binoculars over in her hands, feeling their weight, the grit of the jungle already worked into every seam. "Voss is right about one thing, Ila. If we carry it out ourselves, we die carrying it. Every checkpoint, every patrol boat, every man with a radio between here and the coast. They'll take it off us or they'll take our bodies, and either way the papers go into a furnace and the names go into the ground twice."

"And if you give it to the river—"

"The river doesn't stop. The river doesn't get searched. The river delivers."

* * *

She spent that day and half the night building the plan out of nothing but what the mountain gave her, which was almost enough.

The problem was simple and it was impossible: the archive filled four crates. Oilcloth-wrapped testimony, tin boxes of photographs, ledgers with names written in three dead languages and one living one. Four crates, dry-weather weight maybe two hundred kilos all told, and somewhere below her a ship with a crew of eleven and a captain who answered to men who had already strafed a mission station to keep those pages from seeing daylight.

"You can't swim them out," Ila said. "Not you, not me, not both of us and the whole hamlet besides. The current down there—" he gestured at the water, which moved with the slow muscular indifference of something that had been moving since before there were names for it, "—that current takes dugout canoes and gives back splinters."

"I'm not going to swim them out."

"Then how—"

"I'm going to let the river carry *me*," Orsen said, "and I'm going to be carrying nothing at all when they search me. Which they will."

She laid it out for him in the dark of the lean-to, drawing lines in the dirt with a stick: the bend below the confluence where the *Meridian Star* would have to slow for the shallows; the gravel bar on the far side where the current slackened into an eddy wide enough to float a canoe; the hour before dawn, when the watch changed and even careful men grew careless. She drew the ship. She drew the seam of the double hull, and the access plate she remembered from the salvage manuals Tomas had kept stacked by his bunk like scripture — *inspection port, void space, wing tanks, fore and aft* — because everything Tomas had ever learned, he had taught her, and everything he had taught her had been waiting all these years like tools in a dark drawer.

"He'd have hated this plan," she said quietly. "He'd have said I was trusting my life to a machine built by the people who killed him."

"But?"

"But he'd have done it anyway. Because it might work."

* * *

The old woman from the hamlet — Wariya, the cartographer's daughter, who had given her the route to the source and refused, with terrible gentleness, to make the journey herself — came to her on the second evening with a bundle wrapped in tapa cloth.

"The young men cut bamboo today," she said. "For the houses we are rebuilding. There is left over." She set the bundle down and unwrapped it: lengths of bamboo, hollow, sealed at the joints with wax and resin, each one the width of a strong forearm. Inside the longest of them, rolled tight as a scroll, went the photographs. Inside the others, the ledgers, the testimony, page after page after page, rolled around thin splints of cane so they would not crush themselves.

"We waterproofed them with tree sap," Wariya said. "My father used the same. His maps crossed this river forty years and were still legible." She paused, and her old hands smoothed the tapa cloth flat, and she did not look up when she spoke next. "You understand what you are doing. You are giving them to strangers. To the enemy's own boat. To luck."

"To the river," Orsen said. "Luck is just the river's other name."

Wariya nodded slowly, the way old women nod when a younger person has finally said the true thing after saying many false ones. Then she took Orsen's hand and pressed it flat against the bundle, and held it there, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of blessing.

* * *

They went down to the water in the last black hour before dawn, six of them: Orsen, Ila, two of the highland men whose names she had learned and would not forget — Kepi and Danu — and two more whose faces she knew only by firelight. They carried the bamboo bundles lashed into a raft frame so light it seemed wrong to call it a craft. A raft of reeds and sealed bamboo, barely wider than a man, built to look like debris because debris was what the river expected.

The eddy took them exactly as she had drawn it, and for twenty minutes they drifted in the slack water behind the gravel bar while the sky went from black to the color of wet slate, and the *Meridian Star* came down the channel with her running lights burning, and Orsen lay on the raft with her cheek against wet bamboo and listened to the engines grow from a hum into a presence, into a thing she could feel in her sternum.

"There," Ila breathed. "The bend. She'll slow now."

The ship slowed. The great gray flank swung toward them across the water, close enough that Orsen could see the weld seams in the plating, could see a crewman leaning on the forward rail smoking, the coal of his cigarette the only warm color in the whole gray world. The raft drifted toward the hull like a leaf toward a drain.

And then the thing happened that she had not planned for, because you cannot plan for everything, only for most things: a rope came over the side.

A heaving line, thrown casually, expertly, by a deckhand who had spotted what looked like flotsam snagging against his ship and wanted it clear of his propellers before it fouled anything. The line dropped across the raft frame. Before Orsen could move, before any of them could move, the deckhand began hauling — easy, rhythmic pulls, drawing the raft and its six passengers toward the hull like a fish on a handline.

Kepi's hand went to his machete. Ila grabbed his wrist. Orsen put one finger to her lips and then, slowly, deliberately, raised both empty hands above her head, palms out, the universal language of *I am not what you fear.*

The deckhand saw her. He was young, broad-faced, chewing betel nut, and when the raft bumped against the hull plates he looked down at six soaked people clinging to a raft of garbage and his face went through surprise, confusion, and then the particular blankness of a man deciding this was not his problem.

"Refugees," he called back over his shoulder, in Tok Pisin, disgusted. "More bloody refugees."

And then, because the world is stranger than any plan: he tied off the line.

* * *

They came aboard as salvage, which was the only way anyone came aboard the *Meridian Star*. The bosun — a heavy, sour-mouthed man named Okuk who had worked consortium ships for nineteen years and trusted them exactly as far as he could throw one — looked at Orsen's hands first, the way experienced men do, and saw the diver's calluses, and made his decision in about four seconds.

"Diver," he grunted. "Good. We lost our inspection diver at Kiunga — drank himself off the ship. You want work, you work. You don't, you swim." He jerked his chin at the others. "Your people stay on deck. They eat if they pull their weight. Nobody goes aft of the engine room. Nobody asks about cargo."

Orsen looked at him and lied with her whole face, which was a skill grief had taught her. "I want work."

What she wanted was the void space.

It took her two days to earn her way down to it. Two days of honest labor, because dishonest labor would have gotten her caught: scraping barnacles off the intake gratings in water cold enough to ache, crawling the bilges with a lamp, sounding tanks. The crew watched her the way crews watch new hands — with suspicion that curdled gradually into the grudging tolerance of men who needed divers and had one. She learned their names. She learned who gambled, who prayed, who wrote letters home. She learned that the youngest of them, a boy called Sipa who had never seen the sea until the consortium hired him, was afraid of the dark water under the hull and said so openly, and that Okuk mocked him for it nightly, and that Orsen liked Sipa immediately and dangerously.

On the third night, anchored in the pool below the Kiunga rapids, she went into the double hull.

She went down the inspection trunk at slack water, alone, with the bamboo bundles strapped to her chest under her diving vest, and the plate came open easier than she had feared — the bolts greased, the gasket sound, because consortium ships were maintained to protect cargo, not people. Inside, the void space ran away from her in absolute blackness, a corridor between steel skins two meters high and forty long, dry, echoing, smelling of rust and diesel and the memory of the sea. She swam it by feel, by the thin light of a chemical stick cracked between her teeth, and in the section abaft the number two wing tank she found what the manuals had promised: a frame of angle iron, a maintenance shelf, a place where nothing had ever been stored because nothing was ever supposed to be there.

She worked by touch. Lashed the bundles to the frame with wire she'd brought for the purpose. Sealed the seams with resin from a tube in her kit. Checked each joint twice, three times, the way Tomas had checked her gear when she was fifteen and reckless and he was the only person in the world who loved her enough to be thorough. When it was done she hung in the black water between the hulls and rested her forehead against the cold steel of the outer skin, and through that steel, faintly, she could hear the river going by — the whole enormous patient weight of it, moving downstream toward the delta, toward the sea, toward whatever waited there.

"It's yours now," she whispered, to the river, to Tomas, to no one. "Take it where I can't."

* * *

Getting off the ship was harder than getting on, because by then they knew her face and liked her, and being liked is a complication.

She chose the night before the *Star* was scheduled to run the lower gorge — the stretch where the channel narrowed and the current ran eight knots and no sane captain carried passengers on deck. She told Okuk she was done, contract finished, and asked for her pay in trade goods, and he counted out tinned meat and rope and a coil of copper wire with the sour generosity of a man who believed he was cheating her and wanted her to know it.

"You're going overland?" he said.

"Downstream," she said. "I have a canoe."

"In this water? Woman, the gorge will kill you."

"The gorge has tried," Orsen said. "We've met before."

He stared at her for a long moment, and something in her face must have answered a question he hadn't asked, because he looked away first and spat over the rail and said, "Then go, before I change my mind and keep you. Good divers are rarer than good luck."

She went over the side at midnight, at slack water, with Ila waiting in the shallows of the far bank where Kepi and Danu had carried him on a stretcher of woven cane, walking him down out of the mountains by stages, refusing every time she told them to stop, refusing every time she told them the risk. The current took her the moment she cleared the hull shadow. It took her like a hand closing, and she rode it — fins, floodlight off, body flat, reading the water the way Tomas had taught her to read it in another river in another life — and shot the first riffle of the gorge in darkness with the ship's anchor light shrinking behind her like a star someone had let go of.

Behind her, in the double hull of the enemy's own vessel, the archive rode too. Four crates of the dead nation's testimony, wired to a maintenance shelf in a space no manifest listed, in a ship owned by men who had burned a mission station to keep those pages dark, bound now for the very port where the newspapers lived.

Let them search her, she thought, gasping in an eddy below the gorge, Ila's arms hauling her onto the stones. Let them search every boat, every barge, every bag, every body. Let them turn the country upside down.

The truth was already downstream, traveling first class, in the enemy's own hull, and nobody aboard knew it but the river.

* * *

They camped that night on a shingle spit below the gorge, the five of them around a fire small enough to cup in two hands, eating tinned meat from Okuk's stingy payment. Ila had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when he was thinking hard, which was different from the way he went quiet when he was afraid, and Orsen had learned to tell the difference.

"You know what happens when they find it," he said at last. Not a question.

"When they find it, it'll be too late to unfind it. It'll be in port. It'll be in the hands of stevedores and customs men and whoever opens that hull next, and some of them will talk, and one of them will talk to a journalist, because somebody always does." She fed a twig to the fire. "That's the whole design, Ila. I'm not delivering the truth. I'm making it *findable.* What happens after that belongs to everyone else."

"And Voss?"

The name landed in her chest the way stones land in still water. "Court-martialed by now. Or worse. He falsified his position reports for us. He warned us by radio with his own voice." She looked at the fire. "Whatever they do to him, he bought us the days we needed. I won't pretend I don't owe him a debt I can't pay."

"You could testify."

"No." She said it fast, and then heard how fast, and made herself say the rest slowly. "If I stand up in some hearing room, I become the story. The diver, the drama, the woman who ran the river. And stories get managed. Names get redacted. Witnesses get relocated, or buried." She shook her head. "Tomas signed his statement. His name is in those crates, in his own handwriting, telling what he found. Let *him* testify. He's beyond what they can do to him, and he earned the right to speak more than I ever did. I'm just the courier. The best courier is one nobody remembers."

Ila was silent a long time. Then he laughed — a short, rough, genuine laugh, the first since the ambush — and said, "You know what you are? You're the most stubborn coward I ever met. You'll dive a wreck everyone fears and hide from a courtroom everyone survives."

"Something like that," Orsen said, and lay back on the shingle, and let the river's voice fill her ears.

Above them the stars came out through the breaking cloud, and far downstream, invisible, unstoppable, the *Meridian Star* rode the current through the dark with her secret sleeping in her bones, carrying the dead nation's testimony toward the light of the coast at eight knots, right on schedule, exactly as greedy and exactly as blind as Orsen had wagered everything on her being.

Two hundred kilometers downstream, the truth was winning.

All Orsen had to do now was survive long enough to watch it arrive — and decide, once it did, what she would burn, what she would keep, and whether she would ever, after all these years of running, allow herself to stop.
