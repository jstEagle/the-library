## 20. Testimony

The tavern had no name that anyone could agree on. The fishermen who drank there called it the Old Woman's, after the proprietor, who was neither old nor a woman but had once been caught wearing his mother's dress during a cyclone and never lived the reputation down. The dredge crews called it the Wet Anchor, though there was no anchor and little that was dry. On the government charts it appeared as nothing at all — a smudge of roof tin on the delta's southern finger, where the river gave up its last argument with the sea and the water turned the color of weak tea and old apologies.

Orsen Quintana sat at the corner table with her back to the wall, because some habits outlive their reasons, and read the newspaper for the third time.

It had come up from Port Moresby on the weekly supply boat, wrapped in oilcloth with a crate of condensed milk and someone's mail-order boots, and by the time it reached the delta it had been folded and refolded so many times that the front page wore soft as cloth. But the words held. The words were holding very well indeed.

**MASSACRE ARCHIVE SURFACES: WARTIME RECORDS IMPLICATE THREE GOVERNMENTS**, said the headline, in type large enough to read across a room. Below it, a photograph of a hearing room in Geneva — or The Hague; the papers kept disagreeing about which city was doing the accusing — rows of men in dark suits behind microphones, and on the witness table before them, spread out with white-gloved care, documents she knew intimately. She had packed those documents herself, in the dark, inside the double hull of a consortium salvage vessel called the *Meridian Star*, sealing each bundle in wax and plastic while Ila held the flashlight between his teeth and swore creatively at the bilge water rising around his knees.

She had not signed her name to any of it. Neither had Ila. Neither had Voss, though Voss's name appeared anyway, three paragraphs down, in the way names appear when a career ends badly enough to become public property: *Lieutenant Dario Voss, whose falsified patrol reports are currently under investigation by naval authorities, could not be reached for comment.*

Could not be reached. Orsen set the paper down and looked out through the tavern's open shutters at the river, brown and patient, carrying its silt toward the sea the way it had carried everything else — the chest, the map, the dead men's testimony, the bullet that had gone through Voss and into the country of whatever came after. Could not be reached. She knew exactly where he wasn't. That was different from knowing where he was, and she had learned, over these weeks, to live inside the difference.

"You're doing the thing again," Ila said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you read the same paragraph like it owes you money." He reached over without asking and turned the newspaper face-down on the table, which was the kind of liberty only a man who had taken a machete slash across the shoulder for you was permitted to take. "It hasn't changed since breakfast. It won't have changed by supper. The consortium is still bleeding. The hearings are still happening. Nobody in Geneva knows your name." He grinned, wide and gold-toothed and unrepentant. "Drink your beer."

The beer was warm and tasted faintly of the barrel it had been decanted from, which itself tasted faintly of fish, which was the delta's way of putting its signature on everything. Orsen drank it anyway. Six weeks ago she would have found it undrinkable. But six weeks ago she had been a woman who moved — always moving, always upstream or downstream or under, restless as the current, because stillness meant thinking and thinking meant Tomas, and Tomas meant the wreck, and the wreck meant the iron chest with the seal of a nation that had stopped existing before she was born.

Now she was sitting still. It was remarkable how much practice it took.

* * *

The story had broken eleven days after the *Meridian Star* made port in Daru.

Orsen knew the exact interval because she had spent it in a state she could only describe as suspended drowning — the peculiar agony of a plan launched and beyond recall, when all you can do is tread water above your own decision and wait to see if it floats. She and Ila had watched the consortium vessel steam off downriver with its hold full of what its owners believed was routine salvage, and then they had done the hardest thing either of them had ever attempted, harder than the gorge, harder than the swamps, harder than the cave mouth with the mercenaries coming up through the mist: they had waited.

Ila had wanted to follow the ship. Of course he had. Ila Mero did not trust currents; he trusted engines, and engines could be steamed alongside, and steaming alongside meant control. "We put it in their hull," he'd argued, pacing the dock at Kiunga Bend with his arm still bound against his chest, "we should be there when it opens. What if they find it? What if they burn it?"

"Then we've lost nothing we wouldn't have lost carrying it ourselves," Orsen had said. "They won't burn it. They don't even know it's there. And if they did know—" she'd shrugged, and felt the shrug cost her something, because the truth underneath it was that she had run out of ways to protect the archive by force. Every road she could walk beside it was a road someone had already died on. Sister Anselm, shot in the doorway of her own mission with copies fanned around her like shed feathers. The two highland boys at the airstrip whose names Orsen never learned. Voss, folding sideways into the ferns with a rifle round through him, saying *go, go, go* with blood on his teeth.

No. The archive had to travel without her. That was the whole design. A secret dies in the hands of the people who carry it openly; it survives by hiding inside the machinery of the people who want it dead. Let the consortium haul its own condemnation to market. Let the double hull of the *Meridian Star* do what she could not: arrive.

And it had arrived. Eleven days later, a maritime surveyor doing a routine inspection in Daru harbor had noticed wax-sealed bundles wedged in the void space between hulls, pried one open, and found himself looking at photographs of a massacre that three governments had spent forty years pretending was a weather event. By the end of the week the story was in Sydney, in Singapore, in London. By the second week there were hearings. By the third week two consortium directors had resigned, a third had boarded a plane that was met by police, and the mining concessions along the upper Fly — the ones that had paid for helicopters and hired guns and, though no reporter had proven it yet, the ambush at the confluence — were frozen pending investigation.

Justice, Orsen thought, turning her glass slowly on the scarred wood. It hadn't come the way she'd once imagined it, back when the chest was still sealed and the world was simpler: no dramatic confrontation, no confession wrung from anyone, no moment where the guilty stood revealed before the people they'd wronged. It had come sideways, the way driftwood comes ashore — riding an enemy's own hull, delivered by a stranger's curiosity, unowned by anyone. The drowned village would get its dam inquiry. The survivors on their stilts might see compensation, or might not; inquiries moved like glaciers and melted just as easily. But the names were out. The photographs were out. Forty years of official silence had cracked open like river ice, and somewhere in the highlands, old men who had carried witness in their bodies since boyhood were being asked, at last, to speak.

She thought of the cartographer's daughter, who had refused to come, saying some debts are paid by staying. Orsen wondered if anyone had told her. She hoped someone had climbed up to the hamlet with a newspaper. Some debts, maybe, were also paid by knowing.

* * *

"Another?" The proprietor — the Old Woman himself, a broad man of fifty with kind eyes and a voice like a landslide — nodded at her empty glass.

"One more. And whatever he's having." She tipped her chin at Ila, who was three beers past the point of pretending otherwise and telling a story to the dredge crew at the next table — the story, inevitably, of the fuel drum at the cave mouth, except that in Ila's version there were forty mercenaries instead of nine, and Ila himself arrived over the ridge on foot ahead of schedule rather than limping in half a day late with a bandaged shoulder, and the drum exploded with such magnificence that the sound was reportedly heard in Kiunga.

"—and that," Ila concluded, to appreciative groans and laughter, "is why you never buy fuel drums from a church mission. Consecrated. Every one of them. Goes up like the wrath of God." He collected a round of drinks bought for him on the strength of the tale, wove back to Orsen's table with the practiced navigation of a man who had been drunk on this particular floor before, and dropped into his chair with a sigh of deep contentment.

"They love me here," he announced.

"They love the story. You're just the packaging."

"I am magnificent packaging." He peeled the label off his new bottle in one long strip, a habit of his she'd never seen him manage sober. Then, quieter, without the performance falling away so much as settling: "You've read it four times today. The paper."

"I've read it twice."

"I've watched you read it four times." He looked at her with the shrewdness that lived underneath all his bluster, the salvager's eye that could price a wreck at a glance and read a client's desperation through a hull. "Say it out loud. Whatever it is. It rots faster in the dark."

Orsen turned the newspaper over again. The photograph of the hearing room. The white gloves. The documents laid out like relics.

"Tomas should be sitting there," she said.

Ila didn't answer. He was one of the few people left alive who understood that sometimes the correct response to a sentence like that was silence, and he gave it generously.

"He found it first. He signed his statement in that cave six months before I ever heard of the wreck. He did the diving, he did the finding, he did the dying for it — and now some surveyor in Daru gets to be the man who discovered the archive, and some minister gets to call for accountability, and my brother is a footnote if he's anything at all." She laughed, short and bitter, and hated the sound. "Unnamed in every report. Both of us, I suppose. Him and me. The river keeps what the river keeps."

"The river keeps plenty," Ila said. "But it doesn't keep credit. Credit's a shore thing." He tapped the table. "You know what I keep thinking about? The night you opened the chest. First night, at the delta, before any of this. You had a buyer offering triple weight in gold, sight unseen, and instead you cut the seal. Everyone thought you were crazy. I thought you were crazy." He grinned. "But here's what I know now. Gold spends. Truth compounds. You chose the thing that grows." He raised his bottle an inch off the table. "Your brother chose it too. Footnotes sink ships, Orsen. Ask any navy."

It was, from Ila, practically scripture. Orsen touched her bottle to his and drank, and let the warmth of it loosen something in her chest that had been clenched since the cave.

Outside, the light was going long and amber across the water. A dredge barge crawled upriver trailing its wake, and somewhere downstream a village dog was making its evening complaint, and the tide was beginning its slow reversal, the great river hesitating, breathing in, before giving itself to the sea. Orsen watched it and felt the old restlessness stir and — for the first time she could remember — not take hold. There was nowhere she needed to run. Nothing was chasing her. The strangest part of these weeks had not been the headlines or the hearings; it had been waking each morning without a route to memorize, without counting the exits, without listening for engines. Fear, it turned out, had been furnishing her life for years, and evicting it left rooms she didn't yet know how to live in.

"Ila," she said. "What happened to the *Kestrel*?"

His barge, impounded at Kiunga Bend after the ambush, riddled and listing, the boat that had carried them through rapids and fog and gunfire and grief.

"Bought at auction last week." He said it carefully, watching her. "By a consortium shell company, if you can believe the irony. They're scrapping her."

Something in Orsen's chest went tight and cold. Not grief, exactly. Something adjacent. The *Kestrel* had taken a bullet meant for both of them; boats did that, if you loved them right.

"And the drowned village," she said. "The stilt people. Anyone been up since the inquiry started?"

"Relief boat went Tuesday. Rice, tarpaulin, a man with a clipboard taking statements." Ila turned his bottle slowly. "They're rebuilding. Higher stilts, they say. Farther from the channel." He glanced at her sidelong. "Why?"

Orsen looked out at the river a long moment. The amber light lay on it like varnish, and beneath the varnish the current ran steady and sure toward the sea, carrying everything, keeping everything, needing nothing from her.

"No reason," she said, which was a lie, and Ila, who had known her through leech country and gun smoke and the worst night of both their lives, let it lie, because he could already see the shape of what she wasn't saying, and he was wise enough to let her arrive at it in her own time.

He ordered another round. The Old Woman brought it. Somewhere down the delta, in a place she couldn't name and didn't need to, a man named Voss or not-Voss was living under a new sky, and a hearing room on another continent was filling each morning with witnesses, and the river ran on.

Orsen finished her beer, folded the newspaper into her jacket pocket — she would read it a fifth time tomorrow, and knew it, and didn't mind — and stepped out onto the tavern's sagging porch to watch the last light go off the water.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would ask about boats for sale.
