## 21. Anchored

The boat was called *Kestrel*, though the man who sold her had never seen a kestrel and had named her after a beer he liked. She was eleven meters of salt-bleached teak with a diesel engine that coughed like an old smoker and a hull Orsen spent four days inspecting before she paid — running her palm along every seam the way she'd once run it along Tomas's ribs when they were children, checking for cracks, for weakness, for the places water would find.

She bought her in Kiunga from a Chinese trader who was going home, who wanted cash and no questions and got both. The trader asked her once where she planned to take her, and Orsen said: nowhere. He laughed, thinking she was joking. She wasn't.

The trip downriver took nine days. She ran the gorges alone, by daylight this time, moving at the speed of caution instead of fear, and there were stretches — the long brown sweep below the confluence, the drowned forest standing gray and patient in six meters of water — where she cut the engine entirely and let the current carry her, hands off the wheel, listening to the river work. It sounded different now. It had always sounded to her like hunger, like something swallowing. Now it sounded more like breathing. She didn't trust the change. But she noticed it.

The village appeared around the last bend in the late afternoon, exactly as she remembered it and nothing like it at all. The stilt houses stood above the waterline again — new stilts, pale-cut ironwood still weeping sap, the houses themselves rebuilt from salvaged timber and corrugated tin and woven bamboo, some walls patched with tarpaulin gone milky in the sun. There were more of them than before. Families had come back. Word traveled down the river the way silt did, slowly and without being asked, and word had said that the woman who carried the papers had stopped carrying them, and that the helicopters had stopped coming, and that the men in the mining company's shirts had packed their drills onto barges and gone away to argue with lawyers in cities none of them would ever see.

Orsen brought the *Kestrel* in slow, past the old church foundation that still showed at low water like a row of broken teeth, past the schoolhouse roof that rose and fell with the tide, and moored against the largest platform, where a dozen faces turned toward her with the unhurried attention of people who had learned not to be surprised by anything the river delivered.

Old Maren was the first to speak. He'd lost his left arm below the elbow to a crocodile decades ago and had never once let anyone forget it. He looked at her boat, then at her, then back at the boat, and said in his cracked pidgin:

"You staying?"

It was not a greeting. It was a question with weight behind it, the same question the river itself seemed to ask everything that floated on it — the logs, the bodies, the boats, the secrets. Everything that came down the Fly either passed through or stayed. Nothing stayed by accident.

"I'm staying," Orsen said.

Maren considered this for a long moment, chewing on the inside of his cheek. Then he nodded once, the way men nodded at weather they'd decided to accept, and turned back to the plank he was planing, and that was that. No welcome feast. No ceremony. Just a place made for her among them, the way a gap opens in a current and closes again around whatever settles into it.

* * *

She unpacked on the fourth day.

This requires explanation, because Orsen Quintana had not truly unpacked in nineteen years. She owned two bags — one canvas duffel her father had given her at sixteen, one waterproof roller case bought in Darwin with her first salvage money — and between them they contained everything: three shirts, two pairs of trousers, dive mask and fins, a tool roll, a first aid kit worn soft at the corners, a coil of good rope, a knife, a Bible she never read but couldn't throw away because Tomas's name was written inside the cover in their mother's hand, and a photograph of two children squinting into delta sunlight on a pier that no longer existed. Everything else she had ever owned had been abandoned, sold, sunk, or burned. She could pack her entire life in eleven minutes. She had timed it once, drunk, in a room in Port Moresby, and had sat afterward on the bare floor feeling like a woman who had just proved something terrible about herself.

On the *Kestrel* she built shelves. This took most of a day, because she built them the way she did everything — overbuilt, doubled-bracketed, able to survive a capsizing — and Maren watched her from the neighboring platform and offered commentary that was mostly silence shaped like advice. When the shelves were done she stood in front of her open bags for a long time, feeling something absurdly close to stage fright.

Because unpacking meant deciding where things lived. And deciding where things lived meant admitting she did too.

She hung the shirts on a nail. She set the Bible on the shelf beside the photograph, face out, so the two children on the pier faced the cabin door. She put the tool roll under the bunk where her hand could find it in the dark. The fins went in the bilge locker with the spare mask. Each object got its place, and each place held, and by evening the cabin looked like the inside of someone's life rather than the inside of a waiting room, and Orsen sat on her bunk and looked at it and felt a grief so quiet she almost mistook it for peace.

There was a space on the middle shelf, roughly book-sized, that she had left empty without meaning to. She knew what belonged there. She knew it the way you know a missing tooth. But that was a story for another day, or maybe for no day, and she closed her eyes and slept eight hours without dreaming of water, which had not happened since she was a child.

* * *

The stilt houses went up faster than she expected, because everyone worked and no one supervised. Orsen discovered that salvage diving translated strangely but usefully into carpentry: she understood load, shear, the way water attacked a joint, the way a structure breathed. She dove the flooded foundations with the village boys — Kalo, who was fourteen and fearless in the way that frightened adults; his cousin Beni, who talked constantly underwater through some private language of gestures he'd invented himself — and they raised old house posts, door frames, a church bell that came up crusted white and rang, when Kalo struck it, with a sound so clean and sudden that half the village came down to the water to stand and listen.

They rebuilt the house that had belonged to the teacher, Mrs. Ani, who had drowned in the flooding and whose daughter now taught school on the platform where the house had been. They rebuilt the cookhouse. They drove new pilings with a borrowed hammer rig and Orsen learned to swing it properly, the whole body in the stroke, and woke every morning with her shoulders burning and found that she liked the burning, liked that it came from building instead of fleeing.

Some evenings the old women sat with her while she mended netting she didn't need mended, and told her stories about the village before the water. They spoke of the dead nation's seal without fear now — one of them, old Sera, even had it tattooed on her forearm from her grandmother's time, a faded blue spiral-and-star that the soldiers had once made men hide. "It was ours before it was theirs," Sera said, tapping the ink. "Before it was anybody's flag, it was a river mark. My grandmother said the spiral is the gorge and the star is the source. You've been to the source."

"Yes," Orsen said.

"And?" Sera's eyes were shrewd and kind at once, which was a combination Orsen had never learned to withstand.

"And it's smaller than you'd think," Orsen said. "Just water coming out of a mountain. All that noise upstream, all that blood, and at the top it's just — quiet. Water doing what water does."

Sera nodded as if this confirmed something she'd known all along. "Everything important starts small and quiet," she said. "Only the endings are loud."

* * *

Ila came downstream in the sixth week, his barge riding low and fat with legitimate cargo for once — fuel drums, rice, roofing tin — and tied up alongside the *Kestrel* with the graceless confidence of a man parking a truck he'd crashed many times. His shoulder wound had healed into a knot of scar tissue he showed to everyone whether they asked or not.

"You look terrible," he said, looking at her. "You look rested. It's disturbing. Where's the woman who used to sleep with a knife under her pillow?"

"She unpacked the knife," Orsen said. "It's in a drawer now."

Ila stared at her. "In a *drawer*." He said the word the way other men said *heresy*. Then he laughed, that big broken-horn laugh of his, and clapped his good hand on her shoulder hard enough to stagger her, and that was the whole conversation, because Ila Mero had never in his life needed more than five words to say what mattered.

He stayed three days. He helped drive pilings badly and complained magnificently. On the second night he sat on her deck drinking warm beer and told her, unprompted, that the hearings had entered their second month, that two consortium directors had resigned, that a naval board of inquiry had convened in the capital and that names were being read aloud in rooms with microphones. He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but he watched her face while he said it.

"Any word," Orsen said carefully, "from anyone."

"From the navy?" Ila shook his head. "Nothing official. There's talk of an officer court-martialed for falsified position reports. No name released. Some say he was transferred. Some say worse." He shrugged elaborately. "You know how the service keeps its own."

Orsen looked out at the dark water and said nothing, and Ila, who understood everything about cargo and most things about people, let the silence stay unbroken, and finished his beer, and went to his bunk.

On the third morning he stood at his barge rail and pointed at her mooring lines with theatrical disgust. "Look at this. Double lines. Spring line. Fenders fore and aft. You've moored like a woman who intends to still be here next year."

"Maybe I do."

"Disgusting," Ila said, and threw her a sack of oranges, and was gone downstream, his wake rolling the stilt houses gently in his passing like the village waving goodbye.

* * *

The rains came early that year, and with them the high water, and Orsen learned what the village had always known: that the flood was not a disaster but a season. The water rose two meters and the platforms became piers and the children went everywhere by canoe, racing between the houses, and the fish came into the spaces beneath the floors to spawn, and the whole village adjusted around the river the way a body adjusts around a heartbeat.

One evening in the height of the wet, Kalo came paddling out to the *Kestrel* holding something wrapped in oilcloth, grinning the grin of a boy bearing treasure. Inside was a brass fitting, green with age, stamped with a spiral and a star — the dead nation's seal, dredged from the mud near the old church foundation by one of the kids playing at salvage, the way Orsen herself had once played at it, the way Tomas had taught her.

"We found it," Kalo said. "Beni says it's worth money. I say we should give it to you."

"Why me?"

Kalo shrugged with the magnificent indifference of adolescence. "Because you're the one who carries things," he said. "That's your job here now. Everybody knows."

Orsen held the fitting a long time. The rain ticked on the cabin roof. Somewhere under her keel the river moved in the dark, carrying everything it carried — silt, memory, the small unquiet bones of history — toward a sea that kept all of it and told none of it.

"Keep it," she said finally, closing the boy's fingers back around the brass. "Hang it on the schoolhouse. Let it be a river mark again. That's all it ever wanted to be."

Kalo paddled away through the rain, and Orsen climbed into her bunk, and lay listening to the water rise against the hull, and found — not easily, not completely, but genuinely — that she was not afraid of it. The water rose and the boat rose with it, held by her double lines, her spring line, her fenders fore and aft, moored like a woman who intended to still be here next year.

She slept. In the morning there was mail.

It came, improbably, by canoe — a mission launch had passed through the week before and left a bundle at Mrs. Ani's daughter's platform, and among the circulars and a church newsletter was a single envelope, heavy paper, no return address, postmarked from a coastal town Orsen had never heard of. Her name was written on it in a hand she knew better than her own — the careful, slightly formal script of a man trained to make reports legible, softened now, as if the hand had learned patience somewhere in the years between.

She sat on her deck with the envelope unopened in her lap for a long time while the village woke around her — smoke rising from cookhouses, canoes sliding out, Sera's voice lifted in some morning song — and the river ran gold with first light beneath her anchored keel, going somewhere, going nowhere, holding everything, keeping nothing, home.

She broke the seal.
