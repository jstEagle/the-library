## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The rain came into Bracken's Reach sideways, the way it always did in the last week of the herring season, flung off the harbor like a cat shaking water from its coat. It hissed on the tin roofs of the fishmongers' row and turned the cobbles of Lampblack Street into a river of dull mercury. Coral Dunmore stood at the window of the Salt & Cinder tavern with a cup of something that pretended to be tea and watched a gull fight the wind above the breakwater, losing gracefully, the way she intended to lose nothing today.

She had dressed for the meeting the way other people dressed for funerals: carefully, and with private contempt. Her good coat — good meaning it had only two burns and one mended seam — hung heavy on her shoulders, wool dark enough to hide the salt stains. Beneath it, her storm-chasing leathers, because you never knew when the sky would turn on you, and because wearing them reminded her who she was supposed to be. Coral Dunmore, chaser of the Serrano squalls, taker of impossible wagers, woman who had once ridden a lightning front down the Kestrel Cliffs and lived to sell the story twice.

The stories sold better than the storms did. That was the trouble.

"Your tea's gone cold," said Maren behind the bar, not unkindly. Maren had a face like a beached dory — wide, weathered, permanently listing — and eyes that missed nothing on her street. "You want me to warm it?"

"It's fine." Coral turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "He'll want me sharp."

"He?" Maren's rag stopped mid-wipe on the counter. "Oh. *Him.*"

"Him," Coral confirmed.

"You could go out the back. Through the smokehouse. Old Piet wouldn't say a word."

"And then he'd come tomorrow. And the day after." Coral smiled, and felt the smile sit on her face like paint on wet wood. "No. I made the appointment. I keep my appointments. It's practically a virtue."

Maren looked at her the way sailors look at weather they can't do anything about. "You want anything stronger first?"

"What I want," Coral said, "isn't on your shelf, Mare."

The bell over the door rang at half past the hour exactly, which told Coral everything she needed to know about Juno Pike before she even turned around. Men like him were never early — early meant eagerness, and eagerness was weakness — and never late, because late meant the world could move without them. He arrived precisely when he said he would, dripping just enough rain to look human, shaking his umbrella with a small efficient snap and leaning it against the doorframe as though he were a regular, as though he had ever in his life been a regular anywhere.

Juno Pike wore gray. Not the gray of poverty or the gray of mourning but the gray of expensive cloth cut by someone who understood that the most frightening thing a man could wear was nothing memorable at all. He was handsome in an unfinished way, like a portrait where the painter had gotten everything right except the eyes, and he smiled the way other men breathed — constantly, automatically, and without any evidence of thought.

"Coral Dunmore." His voice carried across the empty tavern like warm oil poured over cold stone. "You look wonderful. The coast agrees with you."

"The coast is trying to kill me, Juno. Same as everyone else in this town."

"See, that's what I like about you." He crossed the room, took her hand before she could decide whether to offer it, and pressed it between both of his — dry, warm, unnervingly gentle hands. "Most people complain about the weather. You make it sound like a rivalry. Like it's personal."

"It is personal."

"And how is the rivalry going?" He released her hand and settled onto the stool across from her table as though the stool had been reserved. He ordered nothing. He never ordered anything; that was part of the performance, the way he made every room accommodate him rather than the reverse. "I hear you lost the *Merrow's Daughter* to the storm off Cape Verity. And the salvage contract on her went to the Corvus brothers. And the Corvus brothers, I happen to know, are financed by—" he touched his own chest lightly, modestly, "—well. Friends of mine."

"They got lucky."

"They got *insured*, Coral. There's a difference, and the difference is why I'm here." He folded his hands on the table. Rain ticked against the window behind him. Somewhere out in the street a cart groaned past, wheels screaming on wet stone. "But you know why I'm here. Let's not insult each other by pretending otherwise. Insulting each other is for people who don't respect one another, and I have always respected you enormously."

"Six weeks," Coral said.

"Six weeks," Juno agreed, delighted, as if she'd answered a riddle correctly. "Forty-two days from today. The principal stands at nine hundred crowns. The interest—" he winced sympathetically, a man pained by arithmetic, "—well. You know how our interest works. It isn't measured in crowns."

"I know how it works."

"Do you? Because I think sometimes people hear it and nod and don't truly *hear* it." He leaned forward, confiding, intimate, the smile softening into something almost tender. "It's measured in fingers, Coral. Yours specifically. We start with the left hand, because you're right-handed — see, we're thoughtful, we're considerate, the Syndicate has always been considerate — and we work our way across. A finger per fortnight of delinquency, beginning the forty-third day. At that rate you'd run out of fingers before you ran out of winter, and I'd hate that. Genuinely. I'd hate it the way you'd hate a bad ending to a good story."

"You've given this speech before."

"I give it to everyone." He spread his hands, rueful. "That's the tragedy of my profession. Everyone gets the same speech, and no one believes it applies to them until the forty-third day. You'd think I'd vary it. But there's comfort in ritual, don't you find?"

Coral lifted the cold tea and drank it anyway, because her throat had gone dry and pride was a poor substitute for swallowing. Outside, the gull had given up and the rain had thickened into that lowland murk that made the whole town feel submerged, a drowned place still pretending to conduct business.

Nine hundred crowns. She turned the number over in her mind the way she'd turn a stone on the beach, looking for what lived underneath. Nine hundred crowns was three storm seasons of honest chasing, if the storms cooperated, if the insurers didn't blackball her again, if the *Merrow's Daughter* fiasco didn't follow her up and down the coast whispering her name into every broker's ear. Nine hundred crowns was a fortune to her and a rounding error to the Meridian Syndicate, which was precisely how they liked their debts: large enough to own you, small enough that you kept believing you could climb out.

And interest besides. Interest measured in fingers.

"So," Juno said, and now his voice changed — brightened, actually brightened, the way a man's does when he brings up the real subject he came for, "let's talk about solutions. I love solutions. I wake up thinking about solutions."

"I bet you do."

"A woman with your talents shouldn't be drinking cold tea in Bracken's Reach waiting for the axe. You read weather like scripture. You've stood on decks where grown sailors lay down and prayed. There are people — serious people, well-funded people — who would pay handsomely for that kind of instinct pointed in useful directions." He paused, savoring. "In fact, that's partly why I'm here. Word reaches us, Coral. Word reaches us from everywhere. And the word lately is that you've been asking questions."

Coral kept her face still. It cost her something. "What kind of questions?"

"Oh, the interesting kind." He produced, from inside his gray coat, nothing at all — just gestured with an empty hand, a conjurer showing you the space where the coin should be. "Questions about the Serrano Range. About the high passes before the snows close them. About a prospector." He let the word sit there. "*Elias Vane.*"

The name went through her like a hook through bait. She hadn't even met Elias Vane yet — wouldn't meet him until tomorrow, until the squall drove her into the roadside waystation at Miller's Bend with her collar up and her luck down — but the town had been humming with him for a week, the way towns hum when something rare passes through. An old man come down out of the mountains for the last time, they said. Sick, they said. Rich, they whispered, and rich men coming down out of the Serranos were either liars or miracles, and both kinds drew crowds.

"I ask questions for a living," Coral said. "It's called research."

"It's called *shopping*," Juno said pleasantly, "and the Syndicate would very much like to know what you intend to buy." He rose, unhurried, retrieving his umbrella. At the door he paused, half-turned, rain-light silvering the edge of him. "Six weeks, Coral. Forty-two days. But here's a secret between friends: I'm not really counting the days. I'm counting the *weather*. When the first snow closes the high passes, the season ends, and everything that hasn't been settled by then gets... simplified." The smile widened by one tooth. "Settle it before the snow. Do yourself that kindness. And Coral — if you happen to come across anything worth more than nine hundred crowns up there in the white — you know where to find me. I'm very easy to find. It's one of my best qualities."

The bell rang. The door shut. The rain filled the space he'd occupied as though he'd never existed, and Coral sat alone with her cold tea and her hammering heart and understood, with the particular clarity that dread provides, that the trap had not been set today.

The trap had been set long ago. Today he had merely shown her the shape of it.

* * *

She walked home through the drowned streets with her collar up and her mind working like a bilge pump, throwing the same water over the side again and again. Nine hundred crowns. Forty-two days. Fingers. The Syndicate wanted Elias Vane, or whatever Vane was carrying down out of the Serranos, and they had somehow guessed that she might be the one to reach him first — or they were simply seeding the ground, letting her know they were watching, so that whatever she found would feel less like discovery and more like delivery.

Her lodgings were a rented room above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, one window, one bed, one stove that smoked unless you swore at it in the correct dialect. She climbed the stairs, lit the lamp, and sat on the bed with her mother's compass in her cupped hands.

It was a strange instrument and she had never quite explained it, even to herself. Brass-cased, older than she was, with a needle that ignored north entirely. Point it anywhere — at the sea, at the hills, at a man's chest — and the needle swung lazily around and settled toward the same quadrant of the Serrano Range, every time, patient as grief. Her father had claimed it was broken. Her father had claimed a lot of things before he walked out. Coral had privately decided, somewhere in the years after her mother vanished into those same mountains, that the needle pointed at whatever the holder had lost. It was a child's theory. She had never managed to grow out of it.

The needle trembled in the lamplight, pointing northeast, into the white silence of the range.

"Everyone wants something out of those mountains," she said to it quietly.

The needle did not answer. It never did. It just held its bearing, faithful to a loss she couldn't name, while downstairs the chandler's dog barked at the rain and somewhere across the drowned town a clock counted out another hour of her forty-two.

She wrapped the compass in oilcloth and put it away, and lay down without undressing, and stared at the ceiling beams, and did the arithmetic again. Nine hundred crowns. No ship, no contract, no credit, six weeks, and a debt collector who smiled like a friend and counted in fingers.

There was one thread left dangling, and it was thin as spider silk: a sick old prospector holed up at the waystation at Miller's Bend, half a day's ride inland, rumored to be carrying the kind of secret that made men like Juno Pike leave their offices and come out into the rain.

If he was a liar, she'd wasted a day.

If he was a miracle —

Coral closed her eyes. Tomorrow she would ride to Miller's Bend. Tomorrow she would look a dying man in the eye and decide how much of her soul she could afford to spend.

Outside, the first flakes of the season's earliest snow drifted past her window, melting before they touched the glass, like promises nobody intended to keep.
