## 2. A Stranger in the Rain

The storm came off the Serrano foothills like something with a grudge.

Coral Dunmore saw it building all afternoon — that bruised-green smear along the western ridgeline, the kind of sky she'd spent half her life reading and the other half running from. She'd been walking the coast road out of Bracken's Reach with nothing but a knapsack, a bad mood, and forty-one days left on Juno Pike's cheerful arithmetic, and when the first fat drops hit the dust like thrown stones, she did what any sensible person does: she ran for the waystation.

It was one of the old toll-house shelters the road wardens had built back when the salt trade still mattered — a squat stone box with a slate roof and a door that groaned like a dying man. Inside: a cold hearth, two benches, a table scarred by a century of knives and boredom, and a smell of wet stone and old smoke that settled over her like a coat.

She got a fire going from the driftwood stacked under the eaves — someone's courtesy, or someone's trap; either way she was grateful — and hung her soaked jacket over a bench to steam. Outside, the rain turned from noise to wall. The world beyond the doorway simply stopped existing, erased in gray.

That was when she heard him coughing.

* * *

He'd wedged himself into the far corner behind the woodpile, as if he'd been trying to make himself small enough to be forgotten. An old man, sixty if he was a day, wrapped in a canvas slicker gone green with mold, his pack beside him like a faithful dog that had followed him too long. His beard was white and untrimmed, his hands were the hands of a man who'd spent fifty years breaking rock — knuckles like river stones, nails blackened to the quick — and he was burning up. Even across the room, even through the rain-damp air, she could feel the heat coming off him like a stove door left open.

"Hey," she said. "Old man. You alive?"

His eyes opened. They were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a fish or a ghost, and they took a long time finding her face.

"Mostly," he rasped. "Ask me again at dawn."

"You picked a fine place to die."

"Picked worse." He tried a smile. It didn't have much practice. "You're the storm chaser."

Coral went still. It wasn't a famous name, hers. It was a name known in betting parlors and insurance offices and the back rooms where men wagered on weather the way other men wagered on horses — Coral Dunmore, who could read a squall line three hours before it read her, who'd ridden out the Graycell Funnel on the Saltmarsh flats when every other chaser had run for high ground, who'd lost everything anyway because weather doesn't pay its debts.

"Who told you that?"

"The rain." He coughed again, deep and wet, and she heard something in it she didn't like at all. "And your boots. Storm chasers walk like the ground owes them money."

"The ground and everyone else," she said, and came closer despite herself.

Up close he was worse. His lips were cracked and gray. Sweat stood on his forehead in beads that wouldn't stop coming. When she put the back of her hand to his brow — an instinct she hadn't known she still carried, some leftover tenderness from a life before debts — he flinched, then leaned into it like a cat.

"Fever," she said, stating the obvious, because obvious things were safe.

"Three days." He said it the way another man might say *three miles*. "Started at the ford. I was making for Coldharbor."

"That's nine days' walk in good weather. You won't make it in this."

"No," he agreed, pleasantly enough. "I don't suppose I will."

There was something about the way he said it — no fear in it, no self-pity, just the flat inventory of a man counting his own stock — that made her want to shake him. Instead she went back to the fire, found a dented tin kettle hanging on a nail, and filled it from the rain barrel outside while the storm tried to take the door off its hinges. There was willow bark in her kit — she carried it for the headaches that came after long nights doing sums she couldn't win — and she shaved a curl of it into the water and set the kettle in the coals.

"Why Coldharbor?" she asked, not looking at him.

"I'm expected there."

"By who?"

"A person who'll be better off never seeing me." Another of those unpracticed smiles. "Which is most people I've ever loved, come to think of it."

The kettle began to mutter. Coral crouched by the hearth and let the warmth work into her bones, and studied him sidelong the way she studied weather systems — looking for the shape underneath, the thing the surface was hiding. He was dying. She'd seen enough death on the flats and in the fens to know the look: the body running hot because it had nothing left to lose by spending. Three days, maybe four. Less if the fever found something in him worth eating.

She should leave at first light. That was the sensible arithmetic. Every hour she spent playing nursemaid was an hour Juno Pike's interest compounded, an hour closer to the passes closing, an hour closer to whatever the Syndicate did to debtors who ran out of road. She owed nobody anything. Kindness was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago, because it weighed too much and spent badly.

But the rain kept falling, and the old man kept breathing in that ragged, laboring way, and the waystation felt less like a shelter than a held breath between two worlds — the lowlands with their smiling creditors behind her, the mountains with their indifferent snow ahead — and Coral Dunmore, who had run from every soft feeling since she was nineteen years old, sat down on the floor beside him and waited for the tea.

* * *

His name was Elias Vane, though it took him until the second cup to say so, and longer to say much else. The willow bark brought the fever down from its worst register but not out of it, and he drifted — surfacing to talk, sinking again into dreams that made his face do terrible things.

She learned fragments. That he'd been a prospector in the Serrano Range for most of a life. That he'd gone up looking for something called storm-glass — she'd heard the word before, in the same back rooms where her name was known; a mineral the mountain folk swore could hold lightning, worth more by weight than gold to the right buyer — and that he'd found it, or found enough of it, once, long ago, and lost it, or been robbed of it, or given it away; the story changed each time the fever let him tell it. That there was a daughter. That the daughter was in Coldharbor. That he hadn't seen her in eleven years, and that the number came out of him like a tooth.

"I keep a ledger too," he murmured at one point, staring at the rafters. "Everyone keeps a ledger. Mine's just... mostly red."

"Mine's all red," Coral said. "Every page."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on so long she had to hold him upright, one hand flat against a chest that rattled like a wagon on washboard road. When it passed he sagged against her, lighter than a man his size had any right to be, and for a moment neither of them moved. The fire popped. The rain hammered. Somewhere in the walls, wind found a gap and played it like a flute.

"You've got kind hands," he said. "For a woman who walks like the ground owes her money."

"Don't mistake me, old man. I'm not kind. I'm just stuck here till the weather breaks."

"Mm." His eyes closed. "That's how kindness always explains itself. *I'm only here because of the rain.*"

She wanted to argue. She found she didn't have the material.

* * *

It was near midnight, the storm at full throat, when he saw the compass.

She'd taken it out without thinking — an old habit, checking it the way other people checked a watch or crossed themselves. It lived in an inner pocket of her vest, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was the only thing she owned that Juno Pike would never get his hands on, because it was the only thing she owned that couldn't be priced.

Her mother's compass. Brass case gone dark with handling, a hairline crack across the crystal, and a needle that did not point north. It never had. Her mother had told her, once, sitting on a fence rail above the Saltmarsh with the whole gray world steaming after a storm: *It points to what you've lost, little bird. Whatever you've lost worst, that's where it swings.* Coral had thought it a game then, a mother's pretty lie. Then her mother had walked into the Serrano Range one spring morning and never walked out, and Coral had opened the compass expecting grief to have broken it, and found the needle steady as scripture — pointing north-northwest, into the high country, unwavering, for nineteen years.

She'd shown it to exactly no one. Not to lovers, not to crewmates, not to the doctors who'd suggested, gently, that a woman who talked to a broken compass might want to talk to them instead.

But the fever had sharpened the old man's eyes instead of dulling them, apparently, because when she lifted it from the oilcloth his gaze fixed on it like a hawk on a vole.

"What is that?" he whispered.

"Nothing. A trinket."

"It's pointing wrong." He struggled up onto one elbow, and she was too surprised to stop him. "North's that way—" a trembling finger toward the door, toward the range wall invisible in the rain— "and that needle's not quite on it. Off by a few degrees. Deliberately off." His breath caught. "Loss-iron. My God. I haven't seen loss-iron since— Where did you get that?"

"My mother made it. Or remade it. The case is older than she was." Coral turned it in the firelight, watching the needle hold its stubborn angle. "It doesn't point north. It points to whatever you've lost. Worst."

"And yours has pointed the same direction for how long?"

The question landed harder than it should have. "Nineteen years," she heard herself say. "Give or take."

Elias Vane looked at her for a long moment, and whatever the fever had left of his judgment, it was enough to see what that number meant. Nineteen years, one direction. A wound with a bearing.

"Your mother," he said softly. "She went into the range."

It wasn't a question, and Coral didn't answer it, which was answer enough. She closed the compass and wrapped it away, and the click of the clasp sounded very loud in the storm-dark.

"They say there's a valley up there," the old man said, settling back against the wall, his voice going dreamy at the edges. "Hollow Vale. Place where the storm-glass grows like frost on a window. My partner and I found the mouth of it thirty years ago. He died getting us out. I sold what we carried and told myself the mountain could keep the rest." His hand crept to his breast, to some flat shape inside his shirt. "Told myself a lot of things."

"You should sleep."

"I will. Soon." His colorless eyes found hers across the firelight, and there was something in them now that hadn't been there before — an appraisal, a weighing, the look of a man testing ice before putting his weight on it. "You're going up there anyway. Aren't you. Into the range. That's where your needle points, and that's where you're walking, debt or no debt."

"How do you know about my—"

"You talk in your sleep, Coral Dunmore. Numbers, mostly. Bad ones." Almost a smile. "And you've got the look. Everyone who goes into those mountains has the look. We're all looking for something the lowlands took from us."

The rain eased, just slightly, as if the storm were leaning in to listen.

"Sleep," she said again, and this time he obeyed.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater. The storm had blown itself east overnight, leaving the road a brown river and the hills dripping, and Coral stood in the doorway with her jacket steaming faintly on her shoulders, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since midnight.

Nine days to Coldharbor in good weather. Six weeks on Pike's clock. A dying man behind her who knew her name and her debts and the direction of her compass, and who had something flat and secret against his chest that he touched in his sleep like a man touching a wound.

She should walk away. Every sensible voice she owned said so, and she owned a great many sensible voices; they'd kept her alive this long. Walk south, find a crew, chase a paying storm, scrape together enough to slow the interest if not the principal. Let the old man keep his appointment with the mountain. Grief had a bearing; so did survival, and hers pointed the other way.

Behind her, Elias Vane coughed — a long, tearing cough that ended in a silence a beat too long — and then breathed again, thin and stubborn, a man negotiating terms with the dark and refusing to sign.

Coral watched the last clouds tear off the ridgeline. Watched the Serrano Range stand up out of the morning haze, white-crowned and indifferent, holding its valley and its storm-glass and whatever was left of her mother somewhere in its folded gray distances.

Then she went back inside, knelt by the corner behind the woodpile, and put her kind hands — her stuck-here-till-the-weather-breaks hands — on the old man's burning forehead.

"All right, Vane," she said. "Tell me about this valley."

His eyes opened, and this time the smile had practice in it.
