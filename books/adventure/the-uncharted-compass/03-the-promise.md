## 3. The Promise

The rain had stopped sometime in the small hours, but Coral woke to the sound of water anyway — dripping through the waystation's ruined roof into a tin basin someone had left beneath a crack in the beams, each drop landing with the patience of a metronome. *Tink. Tink. Tink.* She lay on the bench by the cold hearth and watched the grey light gather at the window like something cautious, something that wasn't sure it wanted to come in.

Elias Vane was still breathing.

She'd checked three times in the night, telling herself she was checking for other reasons — that she wanted his boots if he didn't last till morning, that his coat looked warm, that a man dying alone was a purse waiting to be picked and she'd be a fool not to. All lies, and she'd known they were lies even as she told them, because her hands kept doing things her mind hadn't authorized: wringing out the cloth in melted snow, pressing it to his forehead, propping him higher on the rolled blanket so the wet rattle in his chest could find an easier road.

He was a ruin of a man. Seventy, maybe older — the mountains aged people dishonestly, burning years off them in weather rather than time. His beard was white and uncombed, his hands were maps of their own, every crease a route walked, and when the fever took him he spoke in fragments to people who weren't there. A woman named Maren. Someone called Little Wren. Once, very clearly, he said *I'm sorry* eleven times in a row, and Coral had turned away from him and stared hard at the wall until the tightness in her throat went somewhere it couldn't embarrass her.

Now the light came up properly, thin and pearl-grey, and she built a fire from the split logs stacked by the hearth — whoever ran this waystation had abandoned it kindly, everything a traveler might need left in neat piles, as if they'd only stepped out and meant to return. The kettle went on. The room filled with the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone and the medicinal reek of the willow bark tea she'd been steeping.

Elias's eyes opened.

They were pale eyes, almost colorless, and for a long moment they fixed on the ceiling beams as though reading something written there. Then they found her, and sharpened, and Coral had the uncomfortable sensation of being seen all the way through — of being, for once in her life, the transparent one.

"You stayed," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"Don't make it strange." She poured the tea, blew across it, held the cup while he fumbled it to his lips. His hands shook badly. "You'd have done the same."

"No." He drank, wincing. "No, I don't believe I would. I've been forty years learning what I'd do, girl, and none of it's kind." He said it without self-pity, the way a man reports the weather. "But you sat up all night with a stranger in a dead waystation, and you want me to believe you're nobody."

"I'm nobody with a kettle. Drink your tea."

He drank. Outside, the wind moved through the pines below the station, a long exhale that seemed to go on longer than any breath should. Somewhere up in the Serrano Range, invisible behind the low clouds, snow was already falling on passes that wouldn't stay open much longer. Coral felt the season turning inside her own chest, a cold weight settling. Six weeks. Less, maybe. Juno Pike's smile floated up in her memory, cheerful as a festival lantern, and she pushed it down where she kept all the things she couldn't afford to look at.

"Your compass," Elias said.

She glanced at her belt, where the brass instrument hung in its worn leather case. She didn't remember taking it off. She didn't remember him seeing it either, but feverish men noticed strange things; fevers stripped the world down to its bright objects.

"What about it?"

"May I?"

Coral hesitated. The compass was the one thing she owned that she'd have refused to sell, even in the worst months — worse than these months, even the winter she'd eaten boiled leather. It had been her mother's. Its needle pointed wherever it pleased, which was rarely north, and Coral had long ago stopped asking it questions. But there was something in the old man's face — not greed, nothing so simple — that made her unclip the case and lay it in his palms.

He held it the way a priest holds a relic. His cracked thumb traced the engraved rim, the worn initials — *M.V.* — and something moved through his expression like weather.

"Maren's work," he murmured. "Or her mother's. This engraving — the mountain rose with nine peaks — that's Serrano craft. Old Serrano craft." He looked up. "Where did you get it?"

"My mother." Coral took it back before he could ask more, clipping it to her belt with fingers that were steadier than she felt. "It's broken. The needle doesn't point north."

"It wouldn't," Elias said, and said nothing else about it, which was somehow more unsettling than an explanation.

The fire popped. The basin caught another drop. *Tink.*

"I'm going to die here," Elias said conversationally, as though remarking that the rain had stopped.

"People say that. They're usually wrong."

"Not this time." He pushed himself up against the wall, and Coral saw the effort cost him something permanent — saw the color leave his lips, saw how carefully he arranged his breathing afterward, like a man counting coins he knew were almost spent. "I've been dying since the lowlands, Miss Dunmore. The wet lung. I heard you say my name last night, so I'll return the courtesy — you asked it of me twice, and a dying man pays his debts of information. Coral Dunmore. Storm chaser. Out of Bracken's Reach." A ghost of a smile. "And afraid of something. You check the road every hour. You sit facing the door. You sleep like a cat in a dog yard."

Coral said nothing. The fire needed tending. She tended it.

"So I'll make you an offer," Elias went on, "and you can pretend you're not interested, and we'll both save some dignity. Or you can listen, and lose it."

"The dignity ship sailed," Coral said, sitting back down. "Talk."

From inside his coat — the labor of it took nearly a minute, and she watched sweat stand out on his forehead and did not help him, because she understood instinctively that helping would be an insult — he drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord. It was smaller than she expected. Flat. Heavy in the hand when he passed it to her, the way certain paper is heavy, dense with ink and intention.

"Open it."

She untied the cord. Inside the oilcloth was a map — no, more than a map. A map drawn on good rag paper, hand-inked, dense with notation in a cramped, patient hand. Ridges she half-recognized from her storm-chasing years in the range's southern skirts. Elevations marked in a surveyor's careful fractions. And threading through it all, a route — a line of small confident strokes climbing north and west into country where Coral's own maps simply gave up, dissolving into blank space labeled only *UNCHARTED — GLACIER FIELD* in some cartographer's defeated print.

At the center of the blank country, in Elias's hand, two words: **Hollow Vale**.

Coral knew the name the way everyone who worked the Serrano Range knew it — as a story, a drink-raising legend, a thing guides mentioned to fleece rich clients. A valley sealed behind icefalls and unnamed peaks, where the storms of centuries had deposited something the old miners called storm-glass: fulgurite, lightning-fused stone, but in quantities and qualities found nowhere else on earth. Veins of it, they said. Fields of it. Glass that held light like a held breath, that sold in the lowland cities for more per ounce than gold, that had made and destroyed a dozen fortunes in rumor alone. No expedition had ever confirmed it. Most hadn't returned. The ones that had returned brought frostbite and nonsense.

"This is a joke," Coral said. "A dying man's joke. It's cruel, actually."

"It's a survey," Elias said. "Thirty-one years of it. I found the Vale in my thirty-ninth year, and I've spent every season since mapping the safe approaches, because the first approach I tried killed both my partners and I decided the valley deserved better than corpses at its door." His voice was steady now, the fever burned back by whatever iron thing was holding him upright. "There are four routes in. Two are death. One is closed — a rockfall six years ago, I watched it come down. One works. It works until the snows close the high pass, and after that it works for no one until spring, and I will not see spring."

Coral looked at the map. Then at the man. Then at the map again, doing arithmetic she hated herself for. Storm-glass fields. A fortune. Enough to pay Juno Pike ten times over. Enough to buy back her boat, her rigging, her name. Enough to never again sit facing a door.

"And you're showing me this why?" she said, too fast, too hungry, hating the sound of her own voice. "Because I changed your bandages? Old man, there are easier marks than—"

"Because I need you to take it somewhere," Elias said, "and you're the only person in a hundred miles who isn't already looking for it."

The words landed strangely. Coral frowned. "What do you mean, looking for it?"

"I mean," Elias said, "that I haven't been quiet enough, these thirty-one years. I talked when I was young. I sold copies of partial surveys to men who sold them onward. There are people in Bracken's Reach right now — and in Coldharbor, and in every river town between — who know the Hollow Vale exists, who know a map exists, and who would open a stranger's belly on a bet to get it." His colorless eyes held hers. "You've been checking the road every hour, Miss Dunmore. Who is it you're expecting?"

The fire cracked. Coral said nothing, and her silence was answer enough, and she watched him understand it — watched him file it away with the unhurried precision of a man cataloging his last possessions.

"Here is my offer," he said. "The map is yours to carry. Not yours to keep — yours to *carry*. One promise, and the promise is the whole price. Take it to Coldharbor, over the Kestrel Pass, before the snows close it. Find my daughter. Her name is Wren Vane. She keeps the lighthouse records at the harbor office — or she did, when I last had word, which was seven years ago, which is its own confession." Something flickered across his face, old and sore. "Give it into her hands. Tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her it's from her father, and that the route marked in red is the one her mother drew, and that I'm sorry. That's the message. That's all of it. *I'm sorry.*"

"That's it?" Coral said. "That's the whole errand? Walk a map over a mountain pass and hand it to a woman who hates you?"

"She doesn't hate me. Hate requires thinking about a person, and I've trained her very well not to." Elias almost smiled. "Yes. That's it. That's everything."

"And what do I get?"

"Nothing," Elias said promptly. "That's the offer. Nothing."

Coral laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Old man, I've been robbed by professionals and amateurs, and this is the worst robbery I've ever heard proposed. You want me to haul your map over a killing pass, through the first snows, past whatever's chasing *you*, to deliver an apology to a woman who'll probably throw it in the harbor — and my payment is *nothing*?"

"Your payment," Elias said, "is that you'll have done a thing worth having done. I know that sounds like nothing. I promise you it isn't. It's the only currency I have left, and I've been saving it a long time, and I can tell you from the far side of a wasted life that it spends better than gold."

"Well, I'm broke in every currency," Coral said, standing, "so I'll pass."

She got three steps toward the door.

"Ask yourself one question first," Elias said to her back, and his voice had changed — gone quieter, gone intimate, gone straight through her guard like a pick through rotten timber. "When you hesitated just now. When you looked at that map and did your sums. What were you adding up? What number were you trying to reach?"

Coral stood with her hand on the door latch. Rain dripped in the basin. *Tink.*

"A debt," she said finally, not turning around. "A big one. To people who smile a lot."

"Ah," Elias said softly. "Then you're exactly the person I hoped you'd be. Because a woman running from creditors won't stop in a waystation to nurse a dying prospector unless there's something in her that hasn't been sold yet. Something the debt hasn't reached." A pause, and the wet rattle of his breathing. "I'm betting my daughter's inheritance on that something, Miss Dunmore. Don't make me lose."

Coral turned around. She meant to refuse him again — she had the refusal loaded, polished, ready — but what came out instead was:

"Why not just pay someone? A courier. A guide. Anyone."

"Anyone can be bought off me," Elias said simply. "I have nothing. But a debt — a real debt, a crushing one — that buys a person absolutely, and everyone knows it. Except." He raised one crooked finger. "Except it doesn't, does it? Because you're still here. You could have taken this map and my boots and my coat in the night and been twenty miles gone, and instead you fed me willow bark and worried about my breathing. So either you're the worst businesswoman on the Serrano frontier, or there's a part of you that doesn't do sums." His eyes glinted. "I'm wagering on the second. I've been wrong about most things, but I was right about Maren, and I know the type."

"My mother," Coral said, before she could stop herself, "died in those mountains. Looking for something. So no, I don't put much stock in—"

"In worth beyond price?" Elias finished gently. "No. I imagine you don't. And yet here you are."

The wind came down off the range and pressed against the waystation, and the whole structure groaned like a ship, and Coral thought absurdly of her storm-chasing rig moored in Bracken's Reach harbor, probably already under lien, probably already flying the Syndicate's cheerful pennant. Six weeks. Interest measured in fingers. She had run the numbers a thousand ways and there was no honest way out; there was only the Vale, or the knife, or the long disgrace of running until the range itself closed behind her.

And here was a dying man offering her the one thing no creditor could touch: an errand. A promise. A debt owed in the only coin that couldn't be seized, levied, or collected at knifepoint.

It was, she told herself later, the most cynical bargain she ever struck. She took the map because it was her only exit. She took the promise because promises cost nothing to make. She told herself the old man was a fool and she was a fool for humoring him, and that the moment Wren Vane had the map in her hands, Coral Dunmore's obligations ended and her real life — the life of debts and escapes and looking over her shoulder — could resume.

That is what she told herself. She believed it for perhaps another week.

"One condition," Coral said. "If I do this — if I get this map to Coldharbor — you tell me the route. The working route. All of it. Every waypoint, every camp, every place the ice rots in summer. If I'm carrying your treasure over a mountain, I'm not carrying it blind."

Elias studied her for a long moment, and then he laughed — a dry, rusted sound that turned into coughing, and the coughing went on too long, and Coral crossed the room and held the cup to his lips and hated how naturally her hands did it.

"Done," he rasped, when he could speak. "Every waypoint. Though I warn you, the route is worthless without the map's notations. The pass I mean — the Kestrel — it moves. The glacier under it shifts every season, and the safe line shifts with it. Memory won't hold it. Only the paper." He tapped the oilcloth packet in her hands with one trembling finger. "Guard it with your life, Miss Dunmore. Others will try to take it. Some of them will smile while they try."

"I know all about smiling men," Coral said.

"I imagine you do." He sank back against the blanket roll, spent, and for a while the only sound was the wind and the drip and his labored breathing. Then, just as she turned to bank the fire:

"Coral."

She paused. He'd never used her first name. It sounded strange in his mouth — not familiar, exactly, but *tested*, like a rope taking weight.

"When you give it to her — when you put it in Wren's hands — you'll feel something. Right about then. A moment where you understand what you're carrying." His eyes were closing. "Don't fight it. It's the only part of this whole business that's worth anything, and it's free."

"Go to sleep, old man," Coral said gruffly, and wrapped the map in its oilcloth, and tied the cord, and sat facing the door with the packet heavy in her lap, heavier than paper had any right to be.

Outside, far to the north, the clouds over the Serrano Range had begun, very quietly, to snow.

* * *

He lasted two more days.

Coral spent them learning things she hadn't asked to learn: how to keep a dying man comfortable, how to fake cheerfulness at a bedside, how to listen to thirty-one years of a life poured out in fragments between fevers. Maren, his wife, dead of the same wet lung, drawing routes in her final winter with fingers too weak to hold a pen. Little Wren, born in a basecamp tent at eight thousand feet, who learned to walk on glacier moraine and read contour lines before she could read letters. The quarrel — he never explained it fully, and Coral never pressed, but she gathered it had been about the Vale itself, about whether some doors should be opened, and that Elias had chosen the door and lost everything that mattered choosing it.

On the second evening, lucid in the way the dying sometimes are, terribly clear, he made her recite the promise back to him three times. Word for word. *Coldharbor. Before the passes close. Into Wren Vane's hands. From her father. The route in red is her mother's. I'm sorry.*

"Again," he said, the third time, though she'd gotten it perfect.

"You're stalling," Coral said.

"I'm savoring," Elias corrected. "There's a difference. Go on. Again."

She said it again. He closed his eyes, satisfied, and slept, and near dawn — she was almost sure it was near dawn, though in that grey season the hours blurred — his breathing changed its rhythm, slowed, spaced itself out like footsteps growing further apart on a dark road, and then stopped altogether, without drama, the way a storm ends when no one's watching.

Coral sat with him until the light came up properly. Then she went outside into the frozen mud of the yard and dug his grave herself with the waystation's abandoned spade, because no one else would, and because she discovered she couldn't not. She laid him deep, above the frost line as best she could manage, and marked the cairn with a flat stone, and stood over it in the wind for a long time with her hat in her hands, feeling like a fraud at a funeral she'd only attended for the inheritance.

"He paid you nothing," she told the cairn. "You understand that, right? Whatever you think you bought with that speech — I'm doing this for me. For the debt. For the way out." The wind stirred the pines. The cairn said nothing. "Good talk."

She shouldered her pack, clipped the oilcloth packet inside her coat, against her chest, where she told herself it was safest, and set her boots south toward Bracken's Reach — because no one crosses the Kestrel Pass alone in the closing season, and because there were people in that town she needed, and because she was enough of a fool to believe she could slip in, recruit, and slip out before anyone smiling noticed.

She was two miles down the road, the waystation smokeless behind her, when she saw the rider coming up the valley trail at an easy canter, dressed in dove-grey, waving like an old friend.

Juno Pike was smiling before he was even in shouting distance.
