## 4. Juno Knows

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps, polite as a dinner invitation.

Coral knew before she opened the door. There was only one person in Bracken's Reach who knocked like that — like the world owed him good manners and he intended to collect on every one of them. She looked down from the window of her rented room above the Salt & Anchor, watching him stand in the muddy lane with his hands clasped behind his back, his coat brushed clean of rain, his face tilted up toward her window with an expression of pleasant anticipation. Juno Pike. The Meridian Syndicate's smiling knife.

She could pretend not to be home. She'd done it before, back when pretending still worked on men like Juno. But he'd already seen the candlelight move behind the curtain — she could tell by the way his smile widened a fraction, a man pleased to confirm what he'd suspected. And besides, Juno Pike did not travel all the way to a salt-stained dockside rooming house by accident. He'd come for a reason, and reasons like Juno's had a way of climbing through windows if doors stayed shut.

Coral blew out a slow breath, watched it fog the glass, and went down to meet him.

* * *

"You look well," Juno said, stepping past her into the room without waiting to be asked, the way a tide steps past a sandcastle. "Restful. I like what you've done with the place." He gestured at the water stain spreading across the ceiling like a continent, the single chair, the bed with its blanket worn thin as old paper. "Very honest. Very you."

"Juno." Coral leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and made herself smile the way he smiled — wide enough to show teeth, empty enough to mean nothing. "To what do I owe the pleasure? Come to admire my view of the fish guts?"

"I came because I heard a rumor." He removed his hat — a fine grey thing, felted wool, worth more than everything Coral owned stacked together — and set it carefully on the bedpost, as though he were settling in for a long and friendly evening. "And you know how I feel about rumors, Coral. They're like weather. Most of them blow through and amount to nothing. But some of them—" he tapped the side of his nose, "—some of them are storms."

"And which is this one?"

"The kind I drove two days through rain to check on." He lowered himself into the chair, crossing one leg over the other, and laced his fingers over his knee. Up close, Coral could see the details she always tried not to notice: the pale seam of an old scar along his jaw, the wedding band he wore despite no wife anyone had ever seen, the eyes that were warm the way a hearth is warm when someone's being held over it. "A dying prospector. An old man, feverish, half out of his mind, staying at the waystation on the Lowmarsh road. And a map."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Coral kept her face still. She'd spent years reading storm fronts, learning to see the turn in the wind before the sky showed it, and she turned that same attention inward now, watching for the flicker, the flinch, the tell. She thought about Elias Vane — his cracked lips, his burning hands clutching hers, the map sewn into oilcloth and pressed against her chest like a heartbeat. She thought about the promise she'd made, kneeling at a stranger's bedside while rain hammered the waystation roof.

*I need you to take it to my daughter. Wren. In Coldharbor. Before the snows.*

She thought about six weeks. About interest measured in fingers.

"A map," Coral said, and laughed — a short, ugly sound she'd practiced in front of cracked mirrors her whole life. "That's the rumor? An old man's treasure map? Juno, half the prospectors between here and the Serrano Range die clutching maps to valleys full of nothing but their own regrets. You drove two days through rain for *that*?"

"I did." He said it simply, without embarrassment, and somehow that was worse than any threat. "Because this particular prospector was named Elias Vane. Because twenty years ago, Elias Vane walked into the Serrano Range with a survey team and walked out alone. Because the last thing he told anyone at the trailhead was that he'd found something in the high country that 'made gold look like gravel.'" Juno tilted his head, birdlike, pleasant. "And because yesterday morning, a tinker came through the waystation looking to sell a kettle, and found the old man's bed empty and cold, and a young woman asking pointed questions about the road to Coldharbor."

The fire in the little stove had burned down to embers, and the room had gone the color of iron. Coral became very aware of the satchel under the loose floorboard beneath the bed — three planks, four nails, one secret. She could feel it the way you feel a bruise: constantly, whether or not you touched it.

"So," she said. "You think I have it."

"I think," Juno said gently, "that you owe the Meridian Syndicate a great deal of money. I think that debt has been... accruing. Interest upon interest, like snowfall, until now there's rather a lot of it. And I think that a woman in your position, holding a map to a fortune in storm-glass, might find herself facing a very simple arithmetic." He unfolded his hands, spread them wide — a man presenting a gift. "So here is what I've come to offer. Not to threaten. To *offer*. The Syndicate is generous, Coral. We're misunderstood that way."

He let the pause stretch. Outside, gulls screamed over the harbor, and somewhere below, the Salt & Anchor's kitchen clattered through the supper hour, ordinary life going on as though the world were a place where ordinary things happened.

"Bring us the map," Juno said. "Bring us the Vale. Every coin you owe us — forgiven. All of it. Wiped clean, first frost to last. You walk away owing nothing to anyone. Free." His smile deepened, and for one unguarded instant something moved underneath it, something older and hungrier, and then it was gone, smoothed away like a footprint in snow. "No more looking over your shoulder. No more six weeks. No more interest measured in anything but years, long and comfortable ones. That's the offer. That's the *only* offer, Coral, and I want you to understand — I say this with real affection — that the alternative doesn't bear describing. Not because we're cruel. Because we're thorough."

* * *

Coral crossed to the window. It wasn't that she needed to look at anything; the harbor at dusk was gray water and gray sky and the black humps of fishing boats, nothing worth seeing. But she needed her back to him, needed a moment where her face could do whatever it wanted without an audience, because right now her face wanted to do something desperate and animal, and Juno Pike collected tells the way other men collected coins.

Simple arithmetic, he'd said. Here was hers:

Take the deal, and she owed nothing. Walked free. Six weeks of dread dissolved like sugar in rain. All she had to do was hand a dead man's last wish to the people who broke legs with a smile, and spend whatever remained of her life knowing it.

Refuse, and keep the promise. Deliver the map to a daughter who didn't know her father was dead, in a town she couldn't reach without crossing a range that ate expeditions whole, with Syndicate trackers on her heels and winter coming down off the peaks like a closing fist. And even if she managed it — even if she stood in Coldharbor and put the oilcloth packet in Wren Vane's hands — then what? The debt would still be there. The six weeks would still be ticking. Juno would simply follow her up the mountain and take the map off her corpse, or off Wren's.

There was no version of this where she won. There was only the version where she chose what to lose.

"Coral," Juno said behind her, softly. "Talk to me. You've gone quiet, and quiet women make me nervous. Nervous men do rash things."

"I'm thinking about arithmetic," she said.

"Ah. My favorite subject." She heard the creak of the chair as he shifted, settling deeper, patient as rot. "Then let me help. Consider what the map costs you to keep. Fear, every hour. Sleepless nights. A lifetime of cheap rooms above fish-gut taverns, jumping at knocks on doors. Now consider what it costs you to give it up. One walk to our offices. One handshake. And freedom." A pause, warm as honey. "It's the easiest trade you'll ever make."

"That's the thing about easy trades," Coral said, still watching the harbor. "They're only easy for the man offering them."

Juno chuckled, delighted. "There she is. There's the wit. You know, I've always liked you, Coral Dunmore. In another life, we'd have been partners. Real ones. You chase storms; we fund them. You'd have made a fortune."

"In another life," Coral said, "I'd have better taste."

Another chuckle, and then the chair creaked again, and she heard him rise. When she turned, he was standing in the middle of her shabby room adjusting his cuffs, unhurried, a guest preparing to leave a dinner party he'd thoroughly enjoyed. His hat sat on the bedpost. He picked it up, turned it once in his hands.

"I'll take your silence as deliberation," he said. "Which is fair. These decisions shouldn't be rushed." He settled the hat onto his head at its precise, familiar angle. "But I'll want an answer soon. Say — by the time the first snows touch the low passes. After that..." He spread his hands, apologetic, and didn't finish the sentence. He never finished those sentences. That was the artistry of him. The horror was always left for you to supply yourself, in the dark, at three in the morning, in exhaustive detail.

"I'll find you," Coral said.

"Oh, I know you will." At the door, he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at her with an expression so genuinely fond it curdled her stomach. "One more thing. A courtesy, between friends. The old man — Vane. Did he suffer?"

The question was a hook dressed as kindness, and Coral felt it sink in deep. Because the truth was tangled and strange: yes, he'd suffered, fever-wracked and afraid, and no — at the end, holding her hand, he'd found something like peace, and she was the one who'd given it to him, and she would carry that like a coal in her chest for the rest of her days.

"He was scared," Coral said. "And then he wasn't. That's more than most of us get."

Something flickered across Juno's face — surprise, maybe, or the ghost of a memory of his own — and then the smile reassembled itself, plank by plank. "Yes," he said quietly. "It is." And he tipped his hat and stepped out into the dark.

* * *

Coral waited until his footsteps faded on the stairs, until the front door of the Salt & Anchor opened and closed, until she counted to five hundred with her heart slamming like surf. Then she knelt, pried up the floorboard, and drew out the oilcloth packet.

It was lighter than it should have been, for something weighing so much. She unwrapped it there on the floor, knees aching against the boards, and looked at Elias Vane's careful ink lines: the range, the passes, the valley tucked into the high country like a cupped hand. Somewhere in that cupped hand lay a fortune in storm-glass — and a daughter who thought her father had abandoned her, and a mother-shaped hole in Coral's own chest that the compass on her neck seemed to ache toward whenever she held it.

Salvation and death sentence, wrapped in the same oilcloth. Juno hadn't lied about that part. The arithmetic really was simple. It just didn't add up to anything she could live with.

She thought about lying to Juno's face — realized, with a cold drop in her stomach, that she already had. *A map? Half the prospectors die clutching maps.* He'd believed her. Or he'd pretended to believe her, which amounted to the same thing and was infinitely more dangerous, because it meant the next knock wouldn't come with an offer. It would come with men.

Six weeks. He'd given her six weeks, and now, with rumors already crawling through the port like rats, she'd be lucky to get two.

Coral wrapped the map, slid it back beneath the floorboard, and sat on the edge of the bed in the dark, doing sums she hated. She couldn't cross the Serrano Range alone — not with Syndicate eyes on every road out of Bracken's Reach, not with the passes already breathing frost. She needed a guide who knew the high country and had reason to hate the Syndicate enough to risk it. She needed pack animals, provisions, muscle, luck.

She needed a crew, and she had coin for exactly none of it.

Down in the harbor, a bell rang the change of watch, and the first flakes of the season's first frost began to sift past her window, fat and slow, melting on the glass like tears that hadn't decided yet. Coral watched them come down out of a sky the color of a bruise, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment, that the race had already started — that somewhere out in that darkening city, Juno Pike was probably sitting in some warm office, smiling, writing her name in a ledger, and setting a clock beside it.

Two weeks, then. Maybe less.

She lay back on the thin mattress and stared at the water-stained ceiling, and began, methodically, to make a list of everyone in Bracken's Reach she could blackmail, bribe, beg, or borrow from — and at the top of the list, written in the ink of pure necessity, she put a name she'd sworn never to say again:

*Odile Marchetti.*

The disgraced guide. The woman whose business the Syndicate had burned. The one person in this town with a grudge sharp enough to cut rope and skills sharp enough to use it.

Coral closed her eyes. Somewhere north, under the first frost, the passes were waiting.
