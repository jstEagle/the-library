## 5. The Crew Assembles

Coral woke before dawn with Juno Pike's smile still hanging over her like weather.

She lay in her rented room above the Salt & Anchor, watching the ceiling beams go from black to grey, and did the arithmetic again because it was the only prayer she knew. Six weeks minus two days since the waystation. Elias Vane's map sewn into the lining of her coat, warm as a second heartbeat. The Serrano Range rising behind Coldharbor like a wall the gods had built and then abandoned halfway through the job. And between her and that wall: three hundred miles of autumn country, two passes that ate expeditions for breakfast, and a body count of solo climbers that the mountain registries kept in a ledger thick as a bible.

She could not do it alone. That was the plain truth of it, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She'd crossed ranges alone before — smaller ones, kinder ones, ones where the worst thing waiting on the far side was a long walk to a hot meal. But the Serrano in late season was a different animal. It wanted rope partners. It wanted someone to hold the other end when the world tilted. It wanted, more than anything, more than one pair of eyes reading weather, because the weather up there didn't warn you so much as it ambushed you and then apologized by burying you.

So she needed people. And the trouble with needing people, Coral had learned by thirty-one, was that they came with needs of their own.

She dressed in the dark, checked the map's hiding place twice — once out of caution, once out of something closer to superstition — and went down into Bracken's Reach to buy herself a crew she couldn't afford with money she didn't have, using promises she wasn't sure she could keep.

* * *

Odile Marchetti drank her coffee like it had wronged her.

Coral found her at the Gull's Rest, the dockside tavern where the fishing crews went to argue and the guides went to be ignored. Odile had the corner table by the window — the one with sightlines to both doors, which told Coral everything about how the last few years had treated her — and she was a head shorter than Coral remembered, though that might have been the sitting. Grey shot through her black braid now. Her hands, wrapped around the cup, were guide's hands: broad-palmed, scarred across every knuckle, the nails cut short as if even they might need to grip rock at short notice.

"Marchetti," Coral said.

"Coral Dunmore." Odile didn't look up. "Heard you were in town. Heard you were in debt." She took a sip. "Heard those two facts were related."

News traveled fast in Bracken's Reach, faster than the tide. Coral pulled out the opposite chair and sat without being invited, because Odile respected trespassers who committed fully. "Who told you about the debt?"

"The Syndicate tells everyone about the debt. That's the point of the debt." Now Odile looked up, and her eyes were the flat grey-green of glacial meltwater, the kind of color that looked beautiful and meant cold. "Juno Pike stood at the end of this bar three nights ago buying rounds and telling anyone who'd listen that Coral Dunmore owed Meridian four hundred crowns and change, and that he was a patient man, but patience has a season, ha ha, isn't that funny, another round for the lady."

"He does love an audience."

"He loves making examples." Odile set the cup down. Its ceramic rang against the wood, a small clear note in all the tavern noise. "You're not here for coffee and reminiscence, Dunmore. You're here because you need a guide and I'm the only one left in this port who isn't on Meridian's payroll or too scared to say no to you. So let's skip the part where you charm me. I've watched you charm rooms full of people. It doesn't work on me. I taught half your tricks to a younger generation, and you weren't paying attention when I did."

That stung, mostly because it was true. Coral had learned route-reading at Odile's knee back when Odile still ran the Marchetti Line and its name meant something on the Serrano — twelve years of safe passages, before the avalanche season that killed two clients and the inquiry that decided, wrongly, that Odile had pushed them past weather she should have turned back from. Before the Syndicate bought her debts the way they'd later buy Coral's, and squeezed until the guiding license went to cover them, and the name Marchetti became a warning instead of a brand.

"I need to cross the range," Coral said. "High passes. Before first snow locks them."

"Nobody crosses before first snow. First snow is already falling up there. You cross *ahead* of the killing snows, and you pray the killing snows are running late." Odile turned the cup a quarter-turn, a fidget disguised as ceremony. "Which pass?"

Coral hesitated. The map's weight pressed against her ribs like a hand. "I can't tell you that yet."

"Then we're done." Odile started to rise.

"Wait." Coral leaned forward, lowered her voice. "It's the high route. North of Coldharbor. You know what I'm saying."

Odile stopped. Sat back down slowly, the way you sit down when the floor has shifted under you. "The old mining country," she said. "The Ghost Stair."

"You've been up there."

"Once. Twenty years ago, with my father, before the mines closed. He called it the Stairway God Forgot." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's not a route, Dunmore. It's a rumor with switchbacks. Half of it's collapsed, the other half's never been mapped proper, and the middle third is exactly the kind of terrain where disgraced guides go to die and have their deaths blamed on their own poor judgment."

"That's why I need you and not some licensed puppet from the Guild. They won't touch it. You don't care anymore what the Guild thinks."

"No," Odile agreed. "That's the one luxury ruin buys you." She studied Coral for a long moment, and Coral felt the look like a lamp being shone into corners she kept dark. "You're lying to me about something. Maybe small, maybe not. I can see it sitting behind your eyes."

"I'm lying to everyone right now," Coral said. "It's temporary."

"It always is." Odile drained her coffee and grimaced as if confirming it had, indeed, wronged her. "Here are my terms. Not negotiable. One: I lead. On the mountain, my word is the last word, and if I say turn around, we turn around, and no map, no promise, no debt changes that. Two: I get paid half up front and half on the far side, and if you can't pay, you sign over your storm-chasing rig, the balloon and the wagon both. Three—" and here her voice dropped into something older and harder, "—if the Syndicate's people come at us on the ice, we fight. I'm done running from Pike and his smiling friends. If crossing with you puts them in front of me, I want that understood going in. I'm not cargo, Dunmore. I'm a weapon you're pointing north."

Coral thought about Juno Pike's easy laugh, his fingers drumming on tables, his interest measured in joints and tendons. She thought about saying yes quickly, before honesty could catch up with her.

"All three terms," she said. "Half up front costs me most of what I have left."

"I know," said Odile. "That's why it's half up front. When did you ever trust a cheap guide?" She stood, dropped a coin beside her cup, and looked down at Coral with the glacial eyes. "We leave when you're ready. Don't make me wait long. Snow doesn't negotiate either."

* * *

She caught the pickpocket by accident, three streets from the Gull's Rest, and lost the compass by design of somebody else entirely.

It happened the way these things happen in port towns: a knot of bodies around a fishmonger's cart, a shouting match about the price of herring, a child-shaped gap in the crowd brushing past her left side — and then twenty steps later, Coral reached for her compass out of habit, the way some men reach for a cigarette or a saint's medal, and found her pocket lighter by one heirloom.

She stopped dead in the middle of the street. A cart swerved around her, the driver cursing. Coral didn't hear him. The compass had belonged to her mother. It was the single object Coral owned that she would burn a city to recover, and it was currently receding down Fishgut Alley on the hip of somebody young enough to still find theft funny.

She ran.

The kid was good — better than good, weaving through the market crush like water finding cracks, doubling back through a tannery yard, vaulting a fence that Coral went through rather than over. But kids made one mistake: they ran home. And home, for this particular kid, was the loft above the cooperage, reachable by a drainpipe that groaned under Coral's weight but held.

Coral came through the loft window low and fast, and found the thief sitting cross-legged on a pile of sacking, holding the compass open in both hands, utterly transfixed.

"Don't," the kid said, without looking up. "Just — don't. Look at it."

"I've seen it."

"You haven't." The kid turned it toward her. In the dusty light from the window, the compass needle was swinging, settling, swinging again — not north, not anywhere near north, but dragging itself around in slow arcs, like a dog circling before lying down. "It's broken. Or it's not. Which is it? Because I've robbed forty-some pockets this year and nothing's ever done *that* before."

Coral held out her hand. "Give it back."

"Make me." But the kid said it automatically, the way you'd recite a lesson, and then ruined the effect by asking, quieter, "Where's it point? Really. I held it steady for ten minutes while you were climbing the pipe. It keeps wanting to go northeast. Up. Toward the mountains. What's up there?"

For a moment Coral almost answered honestly, which frightened her more than the theft had. She snatched the compass out of the kid's hands instead — and the kid let her take it, which was its own strange confession.

Up close, the thief resolved into specifics: fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and sharp angles, hair chopped raggedly short with what looked like knife-work, a face scrubbed of anything soft. Boy or girl, Coral genuinely couldn't tell, and suspected the kid worked hard at keeping it that way. Street kids in Bracken's Reach came in two varieties: the ones who belonged to gangs, and the ones who belonged to themselves. This one had the self-owned look, the hungry careful look, the look of someone who slept in shifts and trusted nothing that breathed.

"Tam," the kid said, offering no surname. "Before you call the watch. Though you won't. Watch asks questions, and you've got answers you don't want to give."

"You're quick."

"I'm alive. Same thing, mostly." Tam unfolded off the sacking, joints cracking, and looked Coral over with frank appraisal. "You climb angry. Bad form. You'll tear a shoulder doing that someday." A pause. "You're going up the range, aren't you. Everyone's talking. The guide woman, the debt, the map rumor."

Coral went very still. "What map rumor?"

"Oh, come on." Tam rolled their eyes with the full theatrical contempt available only to teenagers. "Half the docks knows Juno Pike wants a map. The other half knows because the first half wouldn't shut up about it. You think you're subtle? You're a storm chaser walking around with a dead man's coat and a face like a funeral. Subtlety is a rich person's hobby." They stuck out a grubby hand. "Take me with you."

"No."

"I'm the best climber you'll find under sixty pounds. I've been up every roof, crane, and church spire in this port. I don't eat much. I don't sleep much. I don't talk—"

"You're talking right now."

"—much *on the mountain*," Tam finished, unbothered. "And you need someone small. Someone who can go places ropes can't. Chimneys, crevasse mouths, ice caves. You're going to hit terrain where big people die and small people fit. You know I'm right. Your face says I'm right."

"My face says a lot of things." Coral pocketed the compass, checking it was truly there, feeling its weight settle against her ribs next to the map. Two secrets now, stacked. "Why? Why the range? Nobody runs *to* the mountains, Tam. People run from them."

Something flickered across the sharp face — there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Because nobody looks up," Tam said simply. "Down here, I'm a stray the watch sweeps along eventually. Up there, I'm just another body in a crew. Invisible in a crowd of five." A shrug, elaborately careless. "Also I heard there's treasure. Everyone hears there's treasure. I'd like to steal some legally for once. Is that legal? Stealing from a mountain?"

Coral should have said no. Every sensible instinct said no: the kid was a liability, a mouth to feed, a loose thread that Juno Pike could pull. But she remembered the needle dragging northeast, and the question — *what's up there?* — asked with a hunger that Coral recognized from her own mirror, and she heard herself say:

"Dawn after next. The east road gate. You're late, you're left. You steal from me again, you're left somewhere worse than a roadside." She turned for the window.

"What's your mother's name?" Tam called after her.

Coral froze, one leg over the sill.

"The compass," Tam said. "People engrave things. I read it upside down while you were climbing. *M. Dunmore.* So — mother, or wife, and you don't wear a ring, so." The kid's voice had gone quiet, stripped of performance. "Mine engraved mine on a tin whistle. I lost the whistle. That's why I grabbed yours. Habit of the heart, the old woman at the temple calls it. Stealing what reminds you of what you lost."

Coral looked at this feral, brilliant, insufferable child for a long moment.

"Maren," she said. "Her name was Maren."

Then she dropped through the window and was gone, before the conversation could become a feeling.

* * *

Bramble found *her*, which was the first warning sign.

She was provisioning at the dry-goods exchange — rope, hardtack, tallow candles, the unglamorous grammar of survival — when a shadow fell across her list, broad as a barn door. She looked up. And up. The man attached to the shadow was built like the hull of a retired ship: vast, weathered, slightly listing to starboard. A beard the color of wet sand poured off his chin in two braids. Beside him, chewing philosophically on a bale of hay it had apparently purchased for itself, stood a mule.

"Coral Dunmore," the man rumbled, in a voice like a keel dragging gravel. "Heard you're hiring."

"I'm not hiring you. I haven't even seen you before."

"Bramble," he said, as if that settled it. "Ferryman, thirty years, Lower Serran crossings. Retired." He gestured at the mule with evident pride. "This is Duchess."

"Congratulations to you both. I still don't need—"

"You need a pack animal and you need a cook and you need someone who knows rivers, and I'm all three, and Duchess is the fourth thing, which is the best thing, because there's no crossing the Serrano foothills in late season without a mule that knows ice. Horses panic on ice. Mules think about it first." Bramble said all this with the serene confidence of a man reciting scripture. "Also you'll be wanting hot food above the treeline, because cold food breaks morale, and morale, girl, is the actual cargo. I've ferried enough weeping expeditions across the Sorrow to know."

"The Sorrow?"

"The river. Lower gorge. Thirty years." He shook his head at her ignorance, gently, the way you'd shake your head at weather. "Everyone thinks the mountain kills you. The mountain just stands there being a mountain. It's the river and the cold and your own stupid heart that kill you. I've pulled forty-one bodies out of the Sorrow. I remember every face. I'd rather not learn yours."

Coral lowered her pencil and looked at him properly. He was perhaps fifty-five, sun-cured, with forearms like mooring line and the particular calm of a man who had spent decades alone with moving water and had therefore made peace with the fact that the universe owes nobody anything. There was a faded tattoo on his wrist — a ferryman's mark, the oar and the circle.

"Why?" she asked. "Retired ferrymen sit by fires. They don't hire onto suicide marches up the Ghost Stair."

Bramble was quiet for a moment. He scratched Duchess's nose, and the mule tolerated this with the weary magnanimity of royalty.

"Wife died in spring," he said at last. "Thirty years on the water and I never once took her to see the high country. Always *next season*. Always the crossing needed minding." His voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep, like current under ice. "Now there's nothing needs minding, and I find I can't sit still in a house that quiet. So. I'll carry your packs, cook your food, mind your mule — well, *my* mule, you're renting her — and I'll talk too much, because that's the toll, girl, everybody pays a toll, and talking is mine." He fixed her with eyes the pale blue of very old ice. "One condition of mine. We don't leave anybody behind. Not the living, not the dead. Whatever happens up there, whatever it costs us — nobody gets left on the mountain. I've seen what leaving does. It follows you down."

Coral thought of Elias Vane dying alone in a waystation while strangers hurried past his door. She thought of a skeleton she hadn't found yet, in a valley she hadn't reached, and felt the future press against the present like a thumb against a bruise.

"Nobody gets left," she said. "Agreed."

"Then we're five," Bramble said cheerfully, producing — from somewhere in the depths of his enormous coat — a contract he had apparently drafted in advance, in beautiful copperplate, with a clause about Duchess's feed costs itemized to the copper.

"We're four," Coral corrected. "Duchess makes five."

"Duchess makes six," said Bramble. "She counts double. Ask anyone."

* * *

They gathered at the east road gate two dawns later, in the blue hour before sunrise, breath steaming, the port behind them still dark and smelling of tar and fish. Frost had come in the night — the first frost, early, weeks early — and it silvered the fields beyond the walls all the way to the foothills, so that the whole world looked dusted with sugar, or ash.

Coral counted heads and felt something in her chest she refused to name.

Odile arrived first, of course, standing at the gate with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a mountaineer's axe at her hip, checking the sky with the narrowed eyes of a woman reading a letter she expected to contain bad news. Tam materialized out of the shadows ten minutes late — deliberately, Coral was sure, to test whether *late means left* was a real rule — carrying a battered rucksack that clinked in suspicious ways. Bramble came last, leading Duchess, who wore loaded panniers and an expression of profound resignation, while Bramble himself wore a frying pan strapped to his back like a shield and was already talking, describing to Odile the correct way to brew tea at altitude, a subject on which Odile appeared to be composing her rebuttal.

"So," Odile said, cutting across him, turning to Coral. "A disgraced guide, a street thief, a ferryman, and a mule. That's your expedition. That's what you're taking into the Ghost Stair."

"And you," Coral said.

"And me." Odile looked east, where the Serrano Range was beginning to define itself against the lightening sky — a jagged white-and-grey sentence written on the edge of the world, the peaks already wearing their winter caps. "God help the mountain."

Tam swung up onto the gatepost, perched there like a gargoyle with opinions, and grinned at all of them with the uncomplicated joy of someone who had never once been included in anything. "Last chance to vote me off," they said. "Going once."

"Nobody's voting," Coral said. She tightened the strap of her pack, felt the map shift against her spine, felt the compass knock gently against her sternum with each heartbeat — two secrets, riding together, keeping time. "We move. Coldharbor's nine days if the weather holds and six if it doesn't, and either way we're racing the snow."

They passed through the gate as the sun cleared the hills, five shadows stretching long and thin across the frosted fields, walking toward the mountains while the whole port still slept.

None of them looked back. Later, each of them would privately believe this was because they were focused, or brave, or done with the lowlands forever. The truth was simpler and none of them would ever say it aloud: looking back at Bracken's Reach meant admitting how much they were afraid, and it was far easier, that first morning, to simply walk — together, awkwardly, arguing already about tea — into the white silence waiting for them in the east.

Behind them, at the gate, a boy in a Syndicate courier's coat watched them go, counted them twice, and turned to run for the telegraph office.
