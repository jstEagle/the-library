## 6. Departure at First Frost

Odile Marchand had watched a hundred dawns come up over Bracken's Reach, and she had hated every one of them.

Not because they were ugly. The opposite, if anything — the light came off the harbor in long copper bands, gilding the fishing masts, turning the wet cobbles to hammered bronze, laying a softness over the town that it did not deserve and never kept past noon. That was the problem with dawn in Bracken's Reach: it lied. It made the place look like something worth waking for. Odile had spent eleven years guiding clients up the Serrano Range on the strength of that kind of lie — *the mountain will be beautiful, madame, you'll see* — and she had learned to distrust beauty that arrived on schedule.

This morning's lie was frost. The first of the season, early by two weeks at least, silvering the paddocks behind the tannery and furring the fence rails white as bone. It lay on the fields like a held breath. Somewhere up in those blue-shadowed heights, the same cold was already settling into the high passes, thickening, waiting. By the time they reached them, the passes would be thinking about closing. By the time they needed to come home — if they came home — the thought would have become a decision.

Odile stood in the yard of the Drowned Gull stable with her breath smoking and her pack already buckled, and she counted her crew the way she'd once counted her ropes: not because she doubted their existence, but because she wanted to know exactly what she was trusting her life to.

One mule. Bramble's mule, a gray, long-eared creature named Duchess who looked at Odile with the flat contempt of an animal that had carried worse men than this through better country. Two: Bramble himself, down at the trough, arguing with the stable boy about oats as though the price of grain were a matter of theology. Three: Tam, folded into the shadow of the tack room doorway, small and watchful, hands buried in a coat three sizes too big, eyes moving over everything in the yard the way a magpie's eye moves over a table set for dinner. Four:

Four was Coral Dunmore, coming out of the Gull's back door with a satchel slung across her chest and a smile on her face that Odile did not believe for one second.

"Morning, guide," Coral said. "Beautiful day for it."

"It is," Odile said. "That should worry you."

Coral laughed — a real laugh, quick and low, the first honest thing she'd produced all morning — and went to check the mule's load straps herself, which Odile noted and filed away. A liar who checked her own knots was at least a careful liar.

Odile had taken this job for three reasons, and none of them were the money, though the money had been insultingly good. She had taken it because Coral Dunmore was going into the Serrano Range in the teeth of winter with no guide worth the name, and someone had to keep her corpse from being an inconvenience to others. She had taken it because the route Coral had bought — or been given; the story shifted depending on how many drinks were in the telling — supposedly crossed country Odile had failed to reach once, years ago, when the Syndicate took her license and her livelihood in the same afternoon. And she had taken it, third and least admittably, because when Coral Dunmore lied, she lied with her whole face, and Odile had spent enough years reading weather to be curious about a storm that worked that hard to look like sunshine.

The woman owed somebody. That much was written on her the way debt is written on everyone in Bracken's Reach — in the way she counted coins twice, in the way her eyes went to the door whenever it opened, in the smile she wore like a coat buttoned against inspection. Odile had guided bankers and smugglers and one archbishop, and she knew the smell of a person running from something. Coral ran toward things instead, which was rarer and more dangerous, but the smell was the same underneath.

"You're staring," Tam said, materializing at her elbow without having crossed any of the intervening ground. The kid had a gift for that. "You do it a lot. Staring."

"I'm assessing my client."

"She's not your client. She's your boss. There's a difference." Tam turned a stolen apple over in their fingers — Odile genuinely could not say where it had come from; there were no apple sellers within a quarter mile — and took a bite. "Bosses pay you. Clients just die on you."

"That's grimly accurate."

"I'm a grimly accurate person." Tam chewed, watching Coral across the yard. "You don't trust her."

"No."

"But you came anyway."

"Yes."

Tam nodded slowly, as if this confirmed some private theory about the fundamental stupidity of adults. "Me too," they said, and wandered off toward the mule, leaving Odile alone with the frost and the question of what, precisely, she had signed on for.

* * *

They left by the north road while the town was still deciding whether to be awake, hooves wrapped in sacking so the sound wouldn't carry, and Odile felt the old rhythm take her the moment the last roof fell behind — the counting stride, the reading of sky, the constant low arithmetic of distance and daylight and water that a guide runs whether she wants to or not. Eleven months without it, and her body picked it up again like a dropped tool.

The road climbed away from the harbor through orchard country gone over to bramble, and the frost held on longer here, in the shade of the walls, so that they walked through patches of white that steamed faintly where Duchess's breath touched it. The world was very quiet. Even Bramble had run out of opinions for the moment, which Odile suspected would not survive the first hill.

Coral set the pace, and it was a good pace — faster than Odile would have chosen for civilians, slower than panic, steady as a metronome. She didn't look back at the town. Not once. Odile watched for it, actually watched for the glance over the shoulder that most people give the place they're fleeing, and Coral never gave it, and somehow that told Odile more than a confession would have. You only refuse to look back at a thing when looking back might change your mind.

By midmorning the road had become a track and the track had begun to climb in earnest, switchbacking up through pine toward the shoulder of the first ridge, and Odile called the first halt at a spring she remembered from years ago — still running, barely, skinned over at the edges with ice thin as window glass. They drank. Duchess drank with a noise like a bellows failing. Bramble distributed bread and cheese and commentary in equal portions.

"You know what I said to my wife when I retired?" Bramble said, lowering himself onto a rock with the groan of a man whose knees had their own weather system. "I said, Marta, I am done with mountains. Done. I said I will spend my remaining years on flat land, near a kitchen, within walking distance of a bed. And now look at me."

"Nobody made you come," Coral said.

"No, that's true, nobody made me." He pointed the cheese knife at her. "But you asked, and you asked in that voice you have, the one that makes a man feel like refusing you would be a small personal failure, like letting down his own mother. That voice ought to be illegal."

"He's right," Tam said. "She used it on me too. I had plans that day."

"What plans?" Odile asked.

"Plans," Tam said, with dignity, and ate their bread.

Coral laughed again, and Odile watched the crew gather around the sound of it the way cold hands gather around a small fire — not because it was warm enough, but because it was warmer than nothing. And she thought, not for the first time since the Drowned Gull: *she's buying us.* Not with coin. With whatever that laugh costs her. And the question Odile couldn't put down was what she intended to do with us once she had us, because nobody crosses the Serrano Range in the death weeks of autumn for a delivery errand. Nobody pays guide's rates and silence-money and buys a mule's feed for a round trip they describe as simple. Simple things don't make a woman check her own knots twice.

"You've done this crossing before?" Coral asked, settling beside Odile at the spring's edge. Not looking at her. Looking up-valley, at the range, which had appeared between the trees as they climbed — white-shouldered, remote, utterly uninterested.

"The Ghost Stair? Twice. Once guiding, once—" Odile stopped. Recalibrated. "Once."

"Once running?"

Odile glanced at her. Coral's face was open, guileless, interested. It was very well done.

"Once not guiding," Odile said.

Coral accepted that with a nod and didn't push, which Odile also noted. A careless liar pushes. A good one leaves doors closed so you'll want to open them yourself. Coral Dunmore was, Odile was beginning to suspect, a very good one.

"The route Elias sold you," Odile said. "I'd like to see it tonight. Before we commit to the Stair."

"Sure." Too smooth. Half a beat too fast. "It's rough notes. Old man's hand. You probably won't be able to read it."

"I read dead men's handwriting professionally," Odile said. "It's most of the job."

For a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — something moved behind Coral's eyes like a fish under ice. Then the smile came back down over it, warm and easy, and she said, "Tonight, then," and got up to help Bramble argue with the mule, and Odile sat alone at the freezing spring and admitted the thing she'd been circling since dawn:

Coral Dunmore was not showing her the whole map.

Whether there was more map than she'd said, or less, or something else entirely stitched into the pages — Odile didn't know yet. But the woman had flinched, and flinches don't lie, and Odile Marchand had been ruined once already by a client with a secret and a schedule. She finished her water, cracked the skin of ice with one finger, and watched the hole she'd made fill slowly back in, sealing itself as though she'd never been there at all.

*Like that, is it,* she thought. *We'll see.*

* * *

They made the ridge line by late afternoon, and the range took its first payment.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing about the Serrano that no lowlander ever believed until they stood inside it: the mountain didn't attack. It deducted. A wind that hadn't existed in the valley arrived at the top of the ridge with the suddenness of a slammed door, carrying ice grains that stung like flung sand, and the temperature fell through ten degrees in as many minutes, and Duchess planted all four feet and expressed, at length and at volume, her position on the matter of continuing north.

Bramble argued with her. Tam, to everyone's surprise including their own, ended the argument — walked up to the mule, murmured something into the gray ear, and led her forward as docile as a churchgoer. When Odile raised an eyebrow, Tam shrugged. "Mules like me. Horses think I'm going to rob them. Mules figure I already did and don't care."

From the ridge they could see the whole lower world laid out behind them — the road unspooling back toward Bracken's Reach, a brown thread through silvered fields, the harbor a dull pewter smudge beyond — and Odile, scanning it out of habit rather than fear, found what she found.

Riders. Two of them, maybe three, far back along the thread, moving at a pace that was steady in the specific way that paid men move. Not chasing. Following. Keeping the distance generous, keeping themselves a rumor.

She said nothing for the length of a full breath. Then she said, quietly, beside Coral, without pointing: "Don't turn around. Back along the road. Half a mile, maybe less."

Coral didn't turn around. Odile watched her hands instead, and watched them go very still on the mule's strap, and watched the stillness get deliberately replaced by motion — tightening a knot that didn't need tightening.

"How long have they been there?"

"Since the orchards, I think. Maybe since town."

"They're Syndicate?"

"If they're not, they're the politest bandits I've ever seen. Bandits close distance. These are pacing us." Odile finally let herself look, a casual sweep of the horizon like a woman checking weather. The riders had stopped, tiny as punctuation marks on the long brown sentence of the road. Waiting for the light to fail before they made camp, most likely. Professional. "We're ahead of them by hours. Not days. Hours."

Coral was silent for a moment. The wind pulled at her collar. When she spoke, her voice had changed — the performance stripped off it, down to something bare and tired and young. "Hours isn't enough."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't. But it's what we have, and hours can grow. We cut off the road at the ford tomorrow and take the quarry track. Rougher, slower on the legs, invisible from below. If your Elias knew his business, his route joins ours above the tree line anyway."

"It does." No flinch this time. Just exhaustion, and under it something hard and bright that Odile recognized from mirrors. "Odile. Why are you really doing this? The truth. I'll know if it isn't."

*Will you,* Odile thought. *Probably.*

"Because the Syndicate took eleven years of my work and called it regulation," she said. "And because you're going to get someone killed lying to me, and I'd rather it happened later, at altitude, when I've had time to decide whether I mind." She swung her pack up. "Show me the map tonight, Coral. All of it. Or I walk back down this hill and let you explain the Ghost Stair to your creditors yourself."

For a long moment Coral looked at her, and the wind blew, and somewhere below them the riders made camp in the failing light, patient as interest accruing.

Then Coral smiled — and this one, at last, Odile believed, because it was crooked and unwilling and cost her something to give.

"Tonight," Coral said. "All of it. Or most of it, anyway."

"*Coral.*"

"Most of it," Coral said again, and turned up the trail into the darkening pines, toward the high passes and the closing snows, and Odile stood a moment longer watching her go — this stubborn, smiling, half-honest woman leading four strangers and a mule into the teeth of the Serrano Range — before shouldering her pack and following, because someone had to, and because God help her, she wanted to see what was on the rest of that map.

Behind them, far down the darkening road, the riders lit a fire. Small. Patient. Burning.
