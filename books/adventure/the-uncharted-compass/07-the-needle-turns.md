## 7. The Needle Turns

Tam had stolen a hundred things before they stole Coral Dunmore's compass, and not one of them had ever felt like this.

That was the trouble with it. Tam knew theft the way other children knew their prayers — the quick fingers, the soft soles, the way you learned to breathe through your mouth so the sound of your own heart didn't give you away. A lifted purse was a purse. A lifted watch was a watch. You took it, you felt its weight in your palm for exactly as long as it took to feel clever, and then it became someone else's problem or someone else's fortune.

But the compass had been sitting against Tam's ribs for three days now, sewn into the lining of their coat where no pickpocket would ever think to look — because who searches a thief? — and it did not behave like an object. It behaved like a *creature*. It was warm when the nights were cold. It seemed to shift its weight when Tam shifted theirs, like a bird settling on a branch. And once, on the second night, half-dreaming by the fire while Odile kept first watch, Tam could have sworn it *ticked* — not like a clock, but like a small knuckle rapping politely from inside a box, asking to be let out.

So on the third night, when the camp at the edge of the pine country had gone quiet — Bramble snoring in a rhythm like a saw working through wet wood, the mule Hesperia stamping and sighing, Odile rolled in her bedroll with her back to the coals and her knife, Tam noticed, never more than a hand's length from her sleeping fist — Tam slid out of their blanket and carried the compass to the far side of the firelight, where the dark came down between the pines like a curtain.

They'd meant only to look at it. Truly. That was the story Tam told themselves, and Tam was a very good liar, which made them the easiest person in the world to lie to.

* * *

The compass was older than it looked, which was saying something, because it looked old enough to have survived a flood. Its case was brass gone green at the seams, dented along one side as though it had been dropped from a height and caught by someone who loved it too much to mind the scar. The glass over the face was cracked in a fine starburst, like frost on a window. Someone had scratched initials into the underside of the lid — *M.D.* — in a hand that slanted hard to the left, the letters pressed deep, the work of someone who wanted the marking to last.

And the needle — the needle was wrong.

Tam had seen compasses before. Every gutter-rat in Bracken's Reach had, sooner or later; half the smugglers who worked the harbor paid in them, and Tam had learned early that a compass was worth stealing precisely because it looked valuable and wasn't, which made it perfect bait for a trade, or perfect payment for a job you never intended to finish. Compasses pointed north. That was the whole of their personality. They were honest in a way nothing else in Tam's life had ever been honest.

This needle did not point north.

Tam knew the direction of north the way they knew the smell of rain coming — instinctively, in the body, a sense beaten into them by years of navigating the crooked streets of the Reach, where knowing which way the harbor lay could be the difference between supper and a beating. North was behind them now, back toward the lowlands, back toward Bracken's Reach and its debt collectors and its smiling men with soft voices.

The needle pointed up-range. Into the Serranos. Into the white wall of peaks that even now, in the last blue hour before true dark, held the day's last light like a cupped hand holds water.

Tam turned slowly in place, watching the needle. It swung — lazily, reluctantly, like something being woken — and settled again. Up-range. The same quarter of the sky. Not wobbling the way a cheap needle wobbles, not hunting for magnetic iron in Bramble's buckles or the fire's buried stones. *Choosing.* That was the word that came, unbidden and unwelcome, and Tam stood very still in the cold with the little brass thing trembling in their palm like a held breath.

They walked ten paces east. The needle dragged around, patient, certain. Ten paces west. Around again. Up-range. Always up-range.

"It doesn't want north," Tam whispered, and the words came out in a puff of silver, and the mountain dark swallowed them, and for the first time in longer than Tam could remember, they felt small. Not hungry-small, not hunted-small. Small the way you feel standing under a cathedral you've broken into, when the theft suddenly seems beside the point.

They sat down on a fallen log with the compass open on their knee and watched the needle hold its line, and above the black teeth of the pines the first stars came out, hard and cold, and somewhere up in all that darkness was whatever the needle wanted — and Tam found, to their considerable alarm, that they wanted it too. The pull wasn't just in the needle. It was in the air. Like a hook set gently under the skin.

"Found family," Tam muttered, mocking Coral's careful phrase from the road that afternoon, because mockery was easier than admitting that a piece of brass was making their chest ache. "Found family, found treasure, found —"

"You know," said Coral Dunmore's voice, from directly behind them, "most people ask before they handle another person's things."

* * *

Tam did not scream. Tam wanted the record to show, forever, that they did not scream. What they did was go absolutely rigid, close the compass with a snap that sounded in the silence like a rifle shot, and turn around with the bland, insolent expression they'd perfected by age nine — the one that said *I am a child and therefore harmless and also I dare you to prove anything.*

Coral stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed and her collar turned up against the cold, and she did not look angry. That was somehow worse. Tam had planned for angry. Angry had scripts. Angry meant running, or lying fast, or producing tears on demand — Tam could cry within four seconds, dry-eyed weeping, a genuine gift. But Coral just looked at them across the dying fire, and her face in the ember-light was tired in a way that went deeper than the climb, deeper than the debt, deeper than anything Tam had seen on her in six days of travel.

"How long," she said.

Tam considered the options. Denial: *What, this? Found it on the trail.* Too thin; she'd seen them lift it, or suspected, and besides, Tam's coat pocket hung open like a confession. Bargaining: *I was going to give it back.* True, actually, in the way that most of Tam's promises were true right up until they weren't convenient. Outrage: *How dare you accuse me.*

What came out instead, because Tam was thirteen going on ancient some days, was the truth:

"Since the waystation."

Something moved behind Coral's eyes. Not surprise. Something more like a door opening onto a room she already knew.

"I figured you'd notice eventually," Tam said, emboldened by her silence, the words tumbling out now the way they always did when Tam was scared, faster and faster, a spill rather than a speech. "You never checked your pockets. Who doesn't check their pockets? You slept like a dead woman our first night out and left your pack unzipped and I thought — well, I thought you were either stupid or you *wanted* me to take it, and stupid seemed likelier." They turned the compass over in their hands, and the star-cracked glass caught the ember light. "Then it started doing this. And I couldn't put it back, because —" They stopped. Swallowed. "Because I needed to know what it wants."

"And?" Coral said quietly. "What does it want?"

Tam looked up-range, into the dark where the peaks were, and Coral followed their gaze, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the fire ticked and settled, and somewhere off in the pines Hesperia blew out a long, disgruntled breath.

"That," Tam said.

* * *

Coral came and sat on the log. Not next to Tam — a body's length away, an arm's reach short of touching, the distance of a woman who had learned to keep a margin. She held out her hand, palm up, and Tam hesitated — every instinct screaming that handing it over was surrender, that the compass was leverage, that leverage was life — and then handed it over anyway, and was astonished to find that the handing-over hurt.

Coral opened it. The needle swung, steadied, pointed up-range. She watched it the way you'd watch a sleeping animal — with tenderness and wariness in equal measure.

"My mother's," she said.

Tam waited. Waiting was a skill too, though nobody had ever taught it to them; waiting was what you did when talking might break something.

"She was born Meridian Dunmore. M.D. — she scratched it inside the lid herself, with a nail, when she was younger than me. Said if the compass was going to be the only thing that stayed with her, it ought to at least know whose it was." Coral's thumb moved over the dented case, tracing the scar of the drop. "It belonged to her mother before her, and hers before that. Four generations of Dunmore women, and every single one of them swore the same thing about it: it doesn't point north. It points to what you've lost."

"That's not how compasses work," Tam said, because somebody had to say it, and the alternative was believing it, and believing it felt like stepping off a bridge.

"No," Coral agreed. "It isn't." She laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "My mother used to test it. We'd hide things — my hair ribbon, her wedding ring, once a whole loaf of bread, which she claimed proved the compass had opinions — and she'd spin in circles with it until the needle dragged her to the hiding spot. Every time. I thought everyone's mothers had compasses like that. I was nine before I understood ours was the strange one." Her voice did something complicated on the word *nine*, bent in the middle like a branch under snow. "It was in her pack when she went into the range. Eleven years ago. So either she lost it before she went — which she would never, never have done — or..."

She didn't finish. The needle finished for her, holding its patient line into the dark.

Tam sat with that. The cold crept up through the log and into their bones, and the fire burned lower, and the stars wheeled by degrees overhead, indifferent and enormous. Up-range. Whatever the compass wanted was up-range, in the same general country as the Hollow Vale, the same white wilderness where Elias Vane's map promised storm-glass and salvation, and Tam — who had spent their whole life learning to read what people didn't say — watched Coral look at that dark and understood, with a lurch, that the map and the compass might be pointing at the same thing. Or that one of them might be pointing at the other.

"You're not angry," Tam said at last. It came out almost accusatory. "I stole from you. On purpose. And you're sitting here telling me ghost stories."

"I'm furious," Coral said mildly. "I've been furious since the waystation. I've had whole speeches. There was one with gestures." She turned the compass so the needle trembled between them, a bright sliver of intent in the dark. "But here's the thing, Tam. I spent three days pretending I hadn't noticed, because noticing meant deciding what to do about it, and every option was ugly. Take it back and lose the only pair of hands in this crew that can get us through a locked door or a locked town. Turn you out and lose the only member of this crew light enough to cross the icefall at the Ghost Stair without cracking it. Or —" she paused — "or tell you the truth and see what you do with it."

"That's not trust," Tam said. "That's gambling."

"I'm a debtor," Coral said. "Gambling is the only language I'm fluent in." And then, quieter, in a voice that had none of the swagger left in it at all: "But yes. It's also trust. Don't make me regret it. I haven't got much left to spend."

* * *

They sat together until the fire was coals and the cold drove reason out of stubbornness. Somewhere in that hour, without quite deciding to, Tam told Coral things they had never told anyone — not the polished street version, the urchin-with-a-heart-of-gold routine that got coins dropped in cups, but the real inventory: the winter in the drainage culvert behind the tannery, the year of the fever, the man called Cutter who had taught Tam fingers and taken payment in worse. Tam didn't know why they told her. Maybe because she'd handed over the truth first, and truth, Tam was discovering, was a currency that demanded change. Maybe because the compass lay open on Coral's knee, pointing steadily at something lost in the high dark, and it seemed suddenly obscene to sit next to that kind of loss and hoard your own.

When Tam finally ran out, Coral didn't offer pity, which Tam would have had to bite. She just nodded, slow, like a ledger being balanced.

"So we're both orphans of one kind or another," she said. "Odile's an orphan of her own making. Bramble's an orphan of his wife's patience. Wren Vane's an orphan of her father's cowardice, though she doesn't know yet that she's walking back into his story." She closed the compass and held it out, and Tam took it, and this time it didn't hurt to take. "We're a crew of strays, is what we are. God help the mountain."

"What are you going to do about the needle?" Tam asked. "About where it points?"

Coral looked up-range for a long time. The clouds had come in off the west, erasing the stars one by one, and the peaks were gone entirely now, and still she looked, as though she could see through weather and night to whatever waited there.

"Elias Vane sold me a map," she said finally, "and I sold him a promise, and I've been telling myself the promise is the only thing that matters on this climb. Deliver the map. Save my skin. Pay my debts. Simple arithmetic." Her jaw tightened. "But I've had this compass since I was six years old, and I know its moods, and tonight is the first night it's ever pulled like this. Like it's not remembering something. Like it's *reaching*." She stood, and brushed the frost from her coat, and looked down at Tam with an expression that was half warning and half something warmer that she clearly hadn't authorized. "Keep it. Since you're so attached. But it stays in your coat, and you don't wind it near Odile, and if it starts pulling harder than it did tonight — you wake me. First. Before anyone. Understood?"

"Why me first?"

"Because you're the one it chose," Coral said, "and I'm starting to suspect this expedition listens to that sort of thing."

She walked back toward the fire, and Tam sat alone a moment longer in the dark, the compass warm against their palms, the needle hidden now but still pulling — Tam could feel it through the brass, faint and insistent, like a hand closing around theirs from very far away.

Up-range. Toward the Vale. Toward whatever else.

Toward morning, and the Ghost Stair, and the first real snow.

Tam buttoned the compass into its secret pocket, lay back on the frozen ground, and discovered they were smiling, and had absolutely no idea why.
