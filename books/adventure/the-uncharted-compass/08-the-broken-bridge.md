## 8. The Broken Bridge

The rain had been following them for two days.

Coral felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, an ache in the old break in her left wrist that always spoke to her when the weather turned mean. Storm-chasers learned to read their bodies the way sailors read the sky. Her wrist said: *something's coming, and it isn't going to be gentle.*

By midmorning the sky over the Serrano foothills had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had picked up that low, grinding moan that meant it intended to stay a while. They walked with their heads down, hunched into oilskins, the mule — Perpetua, because Bramble insisted on naming everything, including, apparently, his own opinions — plodding along with her ears flattened and her breath steaming out of her nostrils like a small, disgruntled locomotive.

"River's ahead," Odile called from the point, her voice half-swallowed by the wind. She'd climbed up onto a shelf of granite to get a look at the valley below, and now she stood there with water streaming off the brim of her hat, staring at something with the particular stillness she got when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Come see this."

Coral scrambled up beside her. Below, the river — the Vessel, the maps called it, though nobody local did; they just called it *the bad water* — came tearing down its gorge in a brown torrent, swollen three times past its summer width, carrying whole trees in its grip like a child dragging toys across a floor. And strung across it, sagging between two stone pylons like a dead thing, was the bridge.

It had been a rope bridge once. Properly built, by the look of the pylons — cut stone, mortared, older than anything else in the valley except the mountains themselves. The miners who'd carved the Ghost Stair must have thrown it across here eighty years ago, maybe more. But the storm that had come through last week — the one Odile had been muttering about since Bracken's Reach, the one that had closed two passes early — had gotten its teeth into the cables and shaken.

One of the main lines was simply gone. Snapped clean, the frayed end whipping in the current far below, wrapped around a boulder like a severed tendon. The deck planks hung in a long, drunken catenary, half of them missing, the rest rotted or sprung. What remained was less a bridge than a suggestion of one — a dare written in hemp and rot.

"That's not crossable," Bramble said flatly, from below. He'd come up the slope slower than the rest, leading Perpetua, and he stood at the base of the rocks with his arms crossed and his jaw set in the way that meant the debate had already begun inside him and he'd already lost it. "Not for us, and certainly not for a mule."

"Nothing on this route is *for* a mule," Odile said. "The mule is a luxury we've been indulging."

"She's not a luxury." Bramble's voice went up half an octave, which it did whenever he was about to be sentimental and wanted to pretend otherwise. "She's a member of this expedition with four legs and no vote, which makes her the only honest one among us."

Tam snorted. They were crouched under the granite shelf, wringing out their cap, looking — as always — like a stray cat that had followed the expedition home and hadn't yet been told it wasn't welcome. "We could go upstream," they said. "Odile said there was a ford."

"There *was* a ford," Odile said. "Two days ago. At this level, the ford is a rumor. We'd be crossing in chest-deep water moving faster than a galloping horse, and if anyone goes down, the river takes them to the gorge and keeps them." She turned to face the crew, and the wind pushed her words at them in ragged pieces. "Or we fix what's left of the bridge. One line's still sound. I've crossed worse. But it'll take all day, and it'll take nerve, and someone has to go first."

Everyone looked at Coral.

She hated that. She hated how quickly it happened now, how the eyes swung to her like compass needles — and then she caught herself thinking about compasses and made herself stop, because her mother's compass sat against her ribs in its leather case, and every time she thought of it she thought of Tam's face when she'd told them what it was, and the strange raw tenderness of that night in the tent, and how trust, once you'd handed it out, became a thing you could lose.

"Then I go first," she said. "Rig me a safety line off the good cable. If the deck goes under me, I dangle and you haul. Simple."

"Nothing about this is simple," Odile said, "but fine. You first. Then Tam — they're lightest, and if the planks hold for you they'll hold for them. Then me. Then Bramble and the mule last, and Bramble—" she paused, and Coral watched her choose her next words with the care of a woman laying stones across a stream, "—Bramble, I want you to think very hard about whether Perpetua crosses."

Bramble bristled immediately. "You're suggesting I leave her."

"I'm suggesting that a panicked animal on a broken rope bridge over floodwater kills people. That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic."

"Well," Bramble said, in the tone of a man opening a letter he already knows contains bad news, "you can keep your arithmetic. Where I go, she goes."

* * *

They spent three hours on the repair, and it was the worst three hours of labor Coral had done since her days hauling chain on the salvage barges, and she told herself that comparison deliberately, because the alternative was admitting she was scared.

The rain never stopped. It came in gusts now, slantwise, needling. The good cable was slick as eel-skin. Odile spliced a new hand-line along the surviving main rope, working with a speed and economy that made Coral feel clumsy just watching, her fingers reading the hemp like braille, finding the strong strands, discarding the ones that crumbled gray between her nails. Tam ferried tools and lashing cord back and forth along the bank, running with the sure-footedness of someone whose childhood had been spent on rooftops, and Coral tried not to think too hard about what kind of childhood taught a person to run like that.

Coral went first, as promised.

There is a specific quality to fear on a rope bridge over floodwater, and it is not the fear of falling. Falling is quick. It's the fear of *hanging* — of swinging thirty feet above brown water that wants you, over stones that will break you, with your arms burning and no one able to reach you and nothing to do but wait. Every plank groaned a different note under her boots, a rotten xylophone, and twice a board gave way entirely and her foot punched through into empty air and her weight swung onto the hand-line and the whole structure shuddered like a living thing in pain. Below her, the river roared. It didn't sound like water. It sounded like traffic — like a road full of heavy wagons moving very fast in the dark.

Halfway across, she made the mistake of looking down.

A pine tree, whole, root-ball and all, swept under the bridge. Its crown brushed the lowest sag of the cables and the entire span bucked, once, hard, like a fish on a line. Coral's stomach dropped through the soles of her boots. She froze, both hands clamped on the line, rain in her teeth, heart hammering somewhere up near her throat where it didn't belong.

And then — this was the part she would remember later, in the dark, when she couldn't sleep — she noticed what her hands were doing.

Her right hand held the line. Her left hand was pressed flat against the satchel strap across her chest. Elias's map, sewn into oilcloth, riding over her heart like a second heartbeat. Even hanging over a flooded river, some animal part of her had reached for it. Had checked on it. Was protecting it.

She didn't let herself finish the thought. Not then. There was a bridge to cross and a crew watching and no time for honesty. She finished the crossing on legs made of wet rope and stood on the far pylon breathing like a bellows while Tam came across behind her, quick and light as a squirrel, grinning the whole way, because Tam was fifteen and immortal and had never once in their life considered that the world might actually collect.

Odile crossed clean. Of course she did. She crossed like a woman walking down a hallway she'd built herself.

Then it was Bramble's turn, and everything went wrong.

* * *

He'd argued for another hour after Odile's warning, and Coral had let him win, mostly because arguing with Bramble was like arguing with weather — you expended enormous energy and changed nothing — and partly because she'd looked at the mule's big, patient, ridiculous face and found she didn't want to be the one who ordered a living creature abandoned on a riverbank either. Sentiment, she'd told herself. Fine. A little sentiment. Cheap enough.

They loaded Perpetua light — stripped pack, halter and lead only — and Bramble led her onto the deck while Coral and Odile took tension on a second safety line run through a carabiner on the mule's pack harness, a rig Odile improvised out of spare cord and stubbornness and, she admitted afterward, a certain professional pride in doing the impossible neatly.

For the first ten feet, it worked. The mule stepped carefully, picking her way over the broken planks like an old woman crossing ice, ears forward, trusting the man on the lead rope because Bramble had spent six years being trustworthy to this animal and animals don't know any other way to love.

At the midpoint, the river sent up a wave of spray, and Perpetua spooked.

It was nothing — a gust, a slap of cold water, a plank flexing under her hind hoof. But a mule doesn't parse causes. She felt the bridge move beneath her and the water roar beneath that and every instinct she had, every inherited million years of prey-animal terror, arrived at once and in agreement. She reared. The deck dropped away under her hind hooves. And then she was down, scrambling, screaming that awful hollow bugle-scream mules make, hooves punching through rotten boards, one leg hooked outside the side-rope, the whole span whipping and rolling like a whipcrack, Bramble dragged off his feet and holding the lead with both hands, his heels hooked under the one sound plank, his body horizontal over the void.

Coral watched it happen from the far pylon, and for three seconds — three seconds she would count and recount for the rest of her life — she did nothing.

Not because she was paralyzed. Because she was *choosing.*

That was the terrible discovery. In those three seconds, her mind — her fast, practical, debt-shaped mind — ran the numbers without asking her permission. *If I go out on that span while it's loading, the cable could go. If the cable goes, the map goes into the river. If the map goes into the river, the promise dies, the Syndicate deal dies, and my debt comes due in five weeks with interest measured in fingers.* Three seconds. Long enough to die in. Long enough to save someone in. She stood on solid stone with a coil of rope in her hands and she *calculated*, and the calculation said wait, and so she waited, and Bramble hung above the flood holding a mule, and the rain fell on all of them equally.

Then Odile moved, and Coral's paralysis broke like fever.

Odile went out onto the span with no safety line at all — she simply clipped her own line to the main cable and walked out into the chaos, calling to the mule in a low, flat, hypnotic voice, some guide's trick she'd never explained and Coral never asked about, a voice that seemed to come from underneath the wind rather than through it. Bramble, hanging by his hands, hauled the mule's head toward the deck. Between the two of them — the disgraced guide and the fat old ferryman — they got Perpetua's leg back inside the ropes and her feet under her, and the mule, having decided in that single mad instant that the bridge was trying to eat her and these humans were apparently on her side, went still. Utterly still. Standing swaying on a broken rope bridge over a flooded river, calm as a churchgoer.

They brought her across the last twenty feet in slow motion. When her hooves finally hit stone, Bramble sat down where he stood, in a puddle, in the rain, and put his arms around her neck and didn't say a word, and didn't have to.

Coral coiled the safety line. Her hands were steady. That was the worst part. Her hands were perfectly steady, and inside, somewhere under the breastbone, something had taken a wound it hadn't shown her yet.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the old mining pylons, under a tarp stretched between them, and the rain kept on, and everyone was too exhausted to talk much. Tam slept like a stone. Odile took first watch out of habit, sitting under the tarp's edge with her back to a pylon, cleaning her knife.

Coral lay awake.

This was the hour when the day came back for its payment. She'd crossed the bridge. She'd done her job. The crew was safe, the mule was safe, the map was dry. By any measure that mattered to the mission, the day was a success. And lying there listening to the river roaring in the dark — it never stopped, even at night, especially at night — she found herself replaying those three seconds on the pylon. Running them again. Watching herself stand there with the rope in her hands, weighing a man's life against a piece of oiled cloth with ink on it.

She knew what she'd been raised to say. Debt was debt. The Syndicate didn't accept excuses, and Juno Pike smiled exactly the same whether he was shaking your hand or measuring your finger for removal, and a dead prospector's map was the only coin she had. Practical. Necessary. Anyone in her position would have done the same.

But Bramble would not have done the same. That was the thing that kept circling back, patient as the river. Bramble — opinionated, impractical, soft-hearted Bramble, who named his mule and carried extra tea for people who hadn't asked for it and had once, Coral was fairly sure, given his coat to a stranger in Bracken's Reach and walked home wet — Bramble had gone out onto that span for an *animal*, without hesitating, without calculating, without a single thought in his head except *she's mine and she's frightened.* And Coral had stood on stone and done arithmetic while a man she'd started, without meaning to, without wanting to, without admitting to — while a man she cared about hung over black water.

She thought about Elias Vane, dying in that waystation, pressing the map into her hands. *Deliver it to Wren,* he'd said. *Swear it.* And she'd sworn, and she'd meant it the way you mean things when you're drowning and someone offers you a rope — fully, desperately, and with a private ledger in the back of your mind tallying what the promise might cost. She'd told herself the promise and the plan pointed the same direction: deliver the map, collect whatever the Vale might pay, clear the debt, walk away whole. Everyone wins. That was the story she'd been telling Juno Pike, and telling Odile, and telling herself.

But the bridge had asked a different question. Not *what will you do to save yourself?* The bridge had asked *what will you do when saving yourself and saving someone are standing on opposite banks?* And for three seconds, Coral Dunmore had stood in the middle of that question, and her feet had stayed dry, and her conscience had gotten soaked.

She got up quietly, past sleeping Tam, past Odile's watchful silhouette, and stood at the edge of the tarp looking at the river. It ran silver-black under the cloud-light, fast and blind, carrying its trees southward toward the gorge. Somewhere downstream, everything it took ended up in the deep water where the salmon runs died and the walls narrowed and nothing that went in came back out. She thought about the frayed end of the snapped cable, wrapped around its boulder, and how the river had already won that argument years ago and the bridge was only the news arriving late.

"You should sleep," Odile said, without turning her head. "Tomorrow's the Ghost Stair proper. It gets steep from here, and the scree section eats ankles."

"I'm sleeping," Coral said.

"No, you're not." Odile's voice was dry as old rope. "I've watched you lie still for an hour. That's not sleeping. That's prosecuting." She turned, finally, and looked at Coral across the fire-ring's cold ashes, and her eyes were the color of the river. "Whatever it is, say it or bury it. Carrying both is heavier than either."

Coral didn't answer right away. The rain ticked on the tarp. The mule shifted and sighed somewhere in the dark, and Bramble, sleeping beside her like a man who had made his peace with every choice he'd ever made, breathed slow and deep.

"I hesitated," Coral said finally. "On the pylon. Before I threw the line. Three seconds. Maybe four. I was deciding whether the map was worth more than he was."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Did you decide?"

"The river decided. Odile moved first." Coral heard her own voice come out flat and hard, and hated it, and couldn't soften it. "But I know what I was about to choose. That's the part I can't put down. I stood there and I *knew*, and knowing doesn't wash off."

"No," Odile said. "It doesn't. It's supposed to scar, not wash." She fed a twist of bark into the embers of her small shielded lantern, and the light jumped, and for a moment her face was all planes and shadow. "You want absolution, go find a priest. What you've got instead is information. Now you know what you are when the water rises. Some people never learn that about themselves until it's too late to use it." She glanced at the satchel strap still across Coral's chest, even here, even asleep-bound, even now. "Question is what you do with it tomorrow."

Coral looked down at the satchel. Elias's map, riding over her heart. Her ticket out of the Syndicate's ledger. A dead man's last request. A promise. A debt. A treasure map. All of it, one object, one strap, one weight — and tonight, for the first time, it didn't feel like salvation. It felt like a stone she'd picked up and couldn't identify the size of until she'd carried it a while.

Across the camp, half-buried in her bedroll, Tam stirred in their sleep and muttered something — a fragment, a snatch of dream-talk, the kind of thing teenagers said and never remembered saying. Coral leaned closer, curious despite herself, and caught two words before the wind took the rest:

"—the needle—"

Then silence, and rain, and the river going on and on in the dark like something that knew exactly where it was going and had never once doubted it.

Coral lay back down and stared at the underside of the tarp until morning, and when morning came it came gray and colder, with frost on the guy-lines and a thin white rime on the first shoulders of the high country to the north — the range, waking up, beginning to think about winter. Odile stood looking at that rime for a long time with her hands jammed in her pockets, and when she turned around she wore the expression of a woman who has just read a letter she didn't like.

"We've lost more time than I told you we had," she said quietly. "The high passes close early this year. If we're not on the Ghost Stair by tomorrow noon, we're not on it at all."

Coral shouldered the satchel. The needle in her mother's compass, she noticed, had begun — faintly, tremulously, in a way she'd never seen it do in the lowlands — to pull.
