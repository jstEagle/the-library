## 9. Smoke on the Ridge

Odile had spent nineteen years reading mountains the way other people read letters — slowly, twice, looking for what was hidden between the lines. So when she stopped on the switchback above the Kestrel ford and turned her face into the wind without saying anything, Tam noticed first. Then Bramble's mule, Hesper, who was smarter than any of them and had opinions about it. The mule planted her feet and swung her ears west.

"Smoke," Odile said.

Coral came up the trail three steps behind her, breathing hard from the climb but not complaining, which Odile had come to grudgingly respect. "Where?"

"There." She nodded at the far ridge, a gray-blue spine of granite running parallel to their route across the valley of the Little Serrano. To an untrained eye it was nothing — haze, maybe, or low cloud snagged on the scree. But cloud moved with the wind, and this did not. It rose in a thin, patient column from the lee side of the ridge, where a fire would be sheltered from view, and it bent east in long, deliberate ribbons.

"Somebody's camped warm," Bramble said, squinting under his hat brim. "That's a cook fire. Wet wood, green boughs over it — they're banking the smoke so it hangs."

"They're not trying to hide it," Odile said. "They're letting it be seen. By us."

The wind carried nothing up to them at this distance — no smell of pitch or tinned meat, no voices. Just the cold off the glacier fields above, tasting of iron and coming winter. Below them the river ran loud and white through its gorge, erasing whatever small sounds might have crossed the valley. A good place for two parties to watch each other and say nothing.

"How many?" Coral asked. Her voice was level. Her hand wasn't; Odile saw it flex once against the strap of the satchel, the one that never left her shoulder anymore.

"Can't tell. Three fires would look like one if they banked them right." Odile crouched, pulled her spyglass from the chest pocket of her coat — brass, dented, older than Tam — and steadied her elbow on her knee. The ridge swam close. Scree, snow patches in the shadowed couloirs, a stand of wind-bent pines near the saddle. And there, half-sheltered under the pines: canvas. Two tents, maybe three. Figures moving between them, small as ants, unhurried.

Unhurried. That was the part that made her stomach tighten. Men who'd found what they were looking for didn't hurry.

Then one of the figures stepped out onto an outcrop, and even at four miles the way he stood told her everything — weight on the uphill leg, hands loose at his sides, head tipped back to read the sky the way she read it herself. She knew that silhouette. She had followed that silhouette up eleven major routes on the Serrano Range before the Syndicate took everything, and she had hated him for six years, and she had never once managed to stop recognizing him.

"Silas Crane," she said.

Beside her, Coral went very still. Not the stillness of surprise. The stillness of someone who has been waiting for a sound in the dark and finally hears it.

"You're sure?"

"I taught him to stand like that." Odile lowered the glass. Her breath came out white and hung between them. "He was my second on the Corrie Traverse. Best ice man I ever trained. Better than me, on blue ice, though I never told him so." She paused. "I'm telling you so you understand what we're looking at. Juno Pike doesn't hire porters. He hires professionals. If Silas is on that ridge, Pike paid for the best crew money can buy, and Silas took it, which means he knows exactly whose map he's chasing and exactly who's carrying it."

Tam, perched on a rock with their knees drawn up, spat elegantly over the edge. "Ex of yours too, then? You and Coral collect them like buttons."

"He's not mine," Coral said, too fast, too flat.

"He's everyone's, one way or another," Odile said. "That's how he works. He was mine, then he was the Syndicate's, and now he's theirs wearing his own face so nobody asks questions." She stood, knees cracking, and looked down the trail ahead of them — the river gorge narrowing toward the ford, then the long gray shoulder of the Ghost Stair beyond, still a full day's hard walking away. "Here's what matters. We're on the east side of the water. They're on the west. There are two crossings between here and the Stair — the Kestrel ford, which is knee-deep and slow, and the falls bridge, which is fast and exposed and visible from that ridge for the whole length of it. They've got the high ground and the light. Whatever we do next, they'll see it before we do it."

"So we don't do anything next," Coral said.

Odile almost smiled. Almost. "Now you're thinking like a guide."

* * *

They made no fire that night.

It was Odile's order and she enforced it without apology, standing over Bramble's kindling bundle with her boot until he grumbled and stowed it away. They ate cold — hard bread, dried plums, a wedge of cheese gone sweaty in its cloth — huddled in the lee of a granite wall two miles past the ford, having crossed it in the last gray light with the water pulling at their thighs and Hesper complaining the entire way in a register only dogs and ferryman widows could hear. Above them the stars came out hard and pitiless, the way they do at altitude, and the temperature fell like something dropped from a height.

No fire meant no warmth, and no warmth meant no sleep worth the name, and no sleep meant everyone's temper sat just under the skin like a splinter. Odile counted on it. Fear made people stupid, but discomfort, properly rationed, made them honest.

She took first watch because she always took first watch, wrapped in her coat with her back to the stone, watching the far ridge where the smoke had gone out at dusk. In its place: no lights. A professional crew dark-camped, which meant they were moving at dawn or already moving, and either way the arithmetic was ugly. Four of them, one mule, a day behind on a route with no good alternatives. Five, counting Silas's party, if they were as good as she thought — and Silas never traveled with less than five.

Footsteps behind her, soft on the frost. Coral settled down beside her without asking, the way people do when they've decided silence is a form of company. For a while neither spoke. The mountain breathed around them — rock ticking as it cooled, somewhere far off a serac calving with a sound like a door closing.

"You knew him well," Coral said at last. Not a question.

"Well enough to know what he'll do." Odile kept her eyes on the ridge. "He won't rush. That's the thing people get wrong about Silas — they think because he's Syndicate now he's a thug. He's not. He's patient the way glaciers are patient. He'll let us do the work of finding the route, and he'll follow our boot prints, and he'll take the Vale off our backs at the moment it costs us most. He learned that from me." Her jaw tightened. "And I learned it from worse."

"What happened?" Coral asked. "Between you and the Syndicate. You've never said."

The wind shifted, carrying a thread of woodsmoke after all — faint, impossible, probably memory. Odile let the question sit while she decided how much truth the night could hold. Down in the valley the river ran on, indifferent, keeping its own counsel.

"I had a business," she said finally. "Not a grand one. A guide service out of Coldharbor, before your time there. Eleven years. My father's name over the door — Eastwick & Daughters, though it was mostly daughters by the end, since my sister Marta ran the accounts and my mother, God rest her, ran both of us." She heard her own voice go dry and flat and didn't try to soften it. "We walked pilgrims up to the Shrine of the Nine Winds. We walked miners down when the season broke them. We walked rich idiots up Kestrel Peak every summer and charged them double for the privilege of vomiting at fourteen thousand feet. It was honest work. It was *ours*."

"And then?"

"And then the Meridian Syndicate opened a branch office in Coldharbor." Odile turned her hands over in the starlight — the knuckles, the old rope burns, the finger that had healed crooked after a crevasse fall in '89. "They don't start with violence, you know. Everyone thinks they do. They start with a loan. Marta borrowed against the business to replace the pack stock after a bad winter — reasonable terms, cheerful men, papers signed over tea. Then the terms changed. There was always another fee, another reassessment, another clause nobody remembered agreeing to. Within two years we owed more than the business was worth, and the debt was structured so it could never, ever be paid down. That's not lending. That's farming. We were the crop."

Coral said nothing. But Odile felt her go still in a new way — the stillness of recognition, of a woman hearing her own future described in another woman's voice.

"When we couldn't pay, they didn't break our legs. They were nicer than that. They bought our insurance company, quietly, and denied our liability claim after the rockfall that killed two clients on the Shrine route. One inquest later, Eastwick & Daughters couldn't get a permit to guide a goat across a road. Marta took the settlement offer because there was nothing left to protect. I stayed angry instead." She shrugged, a small motion inside the big coat. "Anger keeps poorly. It ferments. But it keeps."

"That's why you hate them."

"That's why I hate them *professionally*." She glanced sideways at Coral, and the starlight was enough to see the tight line of the woman's mouth, the satchel strap gripped in her fist like a rosary. "You owe them too. Don't bother lying — I watched your face at the ford when you looked back at that ridge. I know what a debtor looks like when the creditor finds her. I wore that face for two years."

For a long moment Coral stared out at the dark valley. When she spoke, her voice was low enough that the others, twenty feet away in their bedrolls, couldn't have heard it.

"Six weeks ago it was just numbers. Interest, fingers, the usual theater. Now it's a map and a dead man's promise and a girl in Coldharbor who hates her father, and I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "Juno offered to wipe it clean. All of it. For the map."

"I know."

"You know?"

"I've known since Bracken's Reach. Why do you think I said yes to this job for less than half my rate?" Odile let out a breath that steamed and unraveled. "I've been waiting for you to tell me yourself. I'd started to think you wouldn't."

"Does it matter? What I choose?"

"It matters to whether I tie you to Hesper at night," Odile said, and was rewarded — barely — by the ghost of a laugh. Then she sobered. "Listen to me, Coral Dunmore. I've guided forty-one expeditions on this range, and I've buried nine people, and the ones who died all died the same way: they wanted something on this mountain more than they wanted to go home. The mountain finds that want and it opens under it like a crevasse. So decide. Tonight, in the cold, before Silas makes the decision easy for you. Because tomorrow he's going to offer you something, or take something, and whichever it is, you'll need to already know who you are."

Coral was quiet a long time. The stars wheeled. Somewhere below, the river argued with its stones.

"My mother's compass points at what you've lost," she said at last. "Did you know that? Not north. What you lost." Her thumb moved over the brass case, a habit Odile had seen a hundred times now, unconscious as prayer. "Elias Vane saw it and knew what it was. He said his wife had one. He gave me his map because I promised him something no one else would promise: that I'd finish a delivery instead of starting a theft." She looked up, and her eyes in the starlight were fierce and exhausted and young. "I haven't decided yet. But I'm closer than I was."

"Good enough," Odile said. "Sleep. I'll wake Tam for second watch."

But Coral didn't move right away. "Odile. If it comes to it — if I turn out to be the kind of person who trades a dead man's promise to save her own fingers — will you stop me?"

"Yes," Odile said, without hesitation, because it was true, and because some truths are kinder spoken plainly. "I'll stop you the way I'd stop any client about to walk off a cornice. Firmly, and from behind."

This time Coral did laugh, quiet and cracked, and went to her bedroll, and Odile sat alone with the cold and the stars and the dark ridge across the valley, and let herself feel, for one unguarded minute, the thing she'd been refusing to feel since Bracken's Reach: that these ridiculous people — the debtor, the thief, the ferryman, the mule — had begun to arrange themselves inside her chest in a shape she recognized, and that shape had a name, and the name was *crew*, and crews could be buried.

She put the feeling away. There'd be time to grieve later, or time to keep them. One or the other.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old bone.

Odile woke them before light, as planned, and they struck camp in silence — boots laced, loads shouldered, Hesper saddled with the resigned air of a creature who had seen many bad ideas and expected this to be one of them. The plan was simple: gain the shoulder of the Ghost Stair before the sun cleared the eastern peaks, using the dawn shadow for cover, and put the Stair's broken switchbacks between themselves and the western ridge before midday.

They were halfway up the first slope, breath ragged, frost squeaking underfoot, when Tam — who had ranged ahead as point scout, being the only one of them light enough to cross the wind slab without waking it — came back down at a run, which was itself an answer.

"Tracks," Tam gasped, skidding to a halt in front of Coral and Odile. "On the trail ahead. Fresh. Boots, five sets, and crampon marks over the top of them. They crossed the Kestrel ford in the night — they must have moved the second we lost the light — and they cut upslope here, hours ago." Tam's eyes were wide, and under the fear there was something else, the thief's reluctant admiration. "They're not behind us, Odile. They're *ahead*. They're going to reach the foot of the Ghost Stair first."

Odile looked up the slope, at the pale line of the trail vanishing into the shadow of the peaks, and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading. High ground. The choke point. Time.

Behind her, she heard Coral unsling the spyglass from her own pack — Odile's glass, taken without asking, which told her everything about how far the woman's manners had decayed in eight days — and swing it toward the western ridge, where the morning sun had just touched the snow.

"Coral," Odile said sharply. "Don't—"

But it was too late. Sunlight flashed on the ridge, and answered.

A single glint, high on the rocks. Deliberate. Once, twice. Then a third time, longer.

"They see us," Bramble rumbled, coming up with Hesper. "That's a signal."

"No," Odile said quietly, taking the glass back and finding the spot where the light had been. A figure stood on the outcrop she'd marked the evening before, small and black against the snowfield, one arm raised. Not signaling his own party. Signaling *them*. Holding up something small and pale that might, at this distance, have been a folded paper. An envelope. An offer.

"That's an invitation," she said, and lowered the glass, and met Coral's eyes. "Silas Crane wants to talk."
