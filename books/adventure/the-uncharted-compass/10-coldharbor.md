## 10. Coldharbor

Coldharbor announced itself the way grief does — gradually, then all at once. First there was only the smell of woodsmoke threading down through the pines, sharp and resinous, and then the road bent around a shoulder of granite and the town was simply there, stacked up the mountainside like dishes in a cupboard that had survived an earthquake: slate roofs, crooked chimneys, lanterns already lit against the shortening day. Above it all, the Serrano Range stood with its first snows like a held breath.

Coral reined in at the crest of the road and let herself look at it for a long moment. Six weeks since Bracken's Reach. Six weeks since Juno Pike had smiled at her across a table and counted her fingers like coins. The passes above Coldharbor were still open — she could see the pale seam of the high trail cutting toward the saddle — but the light had changed, gone thin and metallic, and every local they'd passed on the last two days of road had said the same thing with the same shrug: *early winter this year. Get where you're going.*

"Town looks half-dead," Tam said, materializing at her stirrup the way they always did, silent as spilled water. The pickpocket had grown bolder on the road, walking openly beside the mule instead of trailing behind, though Coral noticed their eyes still did their constant circuit — hands, pockets, doors, windows. Old habits stitched into the skin.

"Half-dead is still alive," Bramble said from the cart bench. He'd been quiet all morning, which was unlike him; his opinions usually arrived before he did. His hands were wrapped in wool against the cold, and he flexed them now, wincing. "Half-dead means there's a fire, and a fire means stew, and stew means I'll forgive this road for existing."

Odile said nothing. She stood beside the cart with her arms crossed, looking at the town the way she looked at everything now — measuring it, reading its angles, checking exits. Since the ridge, since the smoke of Silas Crane's campfires on the parallel line of heights, she hadn't stopped scanning. Her ruin had taught her that the Syndicate never sent one knife when three would do.

Coral swung down and felt the altitude in her knees, in the thin bite of the air. Somewhere in this town was a woman named Wren Vane who didn't know her father was dead. Somewhere in this town was the end of a promise Coral had made to a feverish old man in a roadside waystation, a promise she'd taken for the price of a map and never once believed she'd have to keep.

She touched the satchel strap at her shoulder. Elias's map rode against her ribs, sewn into oilcloth, warm from her body. It had started as currency. Somewhere between the broken bridge and the whiteout-cold nights, it had become something heavier — not treasure but testimony, a dead man's hand pressed into hers saying *finish this.*

"Two rooms at whatever passes for an inn," she said. "We find Wren Vane tomorrow. Tonight we eat, we sleep under a roof, and nobody talks to strangers."

"You talk to strangers," Tam said.

"I'm a professional."

* * *

The inn was called the Anvil & Fir, and it smelled of tallow, pine pitch, and decades of spilled ale soaked so deep into the floorboards that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift them. The common room was low-ceilinged and warm, crowded with miners and teamsters who spoke in the clipped cadence of people who lived most of the year inside their own collars. A fire cracked in a stone hearth big enough to roast an ox.

Coral negotiated two rooms and a corner of the stable for the mule, paid in coin she counted twice, and then they ate. Bramble ordered enough stew for three men and got through most of it. Odile sat with her back to the wall, facing the door, and drank one small beer over the course of two hours without appearing to taste it. Tam ate fast, hunched over the bowl like a gull defending a catch, and kept glancing at the stairs.

It was Odile who saw her first.

"Coral." Barely a breath. Her chin tipped a fraction toward the far end of the room.

A woman had come in out of the dark, shaking snow from a canvas coat. She was perhaps twenty-five, tall, with her hair cut brutally short — the kind of cut you give yourself with a knife when you can't be bothered with mirrors. She moved like someone who worked with her body and trusted it: quick, economical, no wasted motion. She hung her coat, nodded to the innkeep, and took a bowl of stew without sitting, standing at the end of the bar like a woman who expected to leave again soon.

Coral knew before anyone said anything. It was in the set of the shoulders — the same stubborn architecture Elias had carried even feverish and dying. It was in the jaw, too, the way it set when the woman caught someone staring.

"Wren," Coral said quietly.

"Don't," Odile murmured. "Not here. Not tired. You get one chance at this, and you're about to do it exhausted, in public, with me watching. Think about what you'll say."

Coral thought about what she'd say. She had been thinking about it for six weeks, actually, turning it over on cold ridgelines and in the dark of camps: *Your father died. He loved you. He sent this.* She had rehearsed it until the words wore smooth. But every version ended the same way, with the same problem she'd been carrying since the waystation: Elias Vane had abandoned this woman, and no delivery of paper could un-abandon her.

"She's leaving," Tam said. And she was — draining the bowl, reaching for her coat.

Coral stood before she'd decided to. Her legs were stiff from the road and the room tilted briefly, altitude and fatigue, and then she was crossing the common room with the whole crew's silence pressing against her back.

"Miss Vane?"

The woman turned. Up close the resemblance was worse — or better. Her eyes were Elias's exact grey, the grey of weathered slate, and they went over Coral with a surveyor's speed: boots, satchel, the calluses, the road-worn quality of her gear. A working assessment. A mountain woman's assessment.

"Who's asking?"

"My name's Coral Dunmore. I'm — I came from the lowlands. From Bracken's Reach." She watched the name land and mean nothing, which was a mercy. "I knew your father. Elias."

Something happened in Wren Vane's face. It wasn't grief. It was faster than grief and colder — a door swinging shut on a room where a fire had once burned down to ash long ago. "He's alive, then. That's a surprise."

"He died. Eleven days ago. Fever, at a waystation on the lowland road. I was with him. I—" Coral's rehearsed speech dissolved. What came out instead was true and clumsy: "I buried him under a cairn by the trail. I couldn't carry him."

Wren set down her empty bowl with great care, as if the small precision of it was all that was holding her together. "Eleven days," she repeated. "And it took you eleven days' ride plus six weeks to tell me."

"The route wasn't—"

"No." Wren held up a hand. Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Don't explain. Don't apologize on his behalf either, because I've heard those apologies my whole life and none of them ever came with him attached. Did he send something? Money? Is that why you climbed all this way — he finally remembered he has a daughter, and it comes with a payment?"

"He sent a map."

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. Wren's face did not change. "A map."

"To a place called the Hollow Vale. Storm-glass country, high in the range. He wanted you to have it. He said—" Coral reached for Elias's actual words, the ones he'd gasped out between coughs, and found them inadequate and true at once. "He said it was the only thing he had left to give you. He said he'd spent his whole life walking away and this was him walking back."

For a moment — just a moment — the door in Wren's face trembled. Coral saw it: the flicker of a girl who had once waited at a window for a father who promised to come home before the snows. Then it sealed.

"Storm-glass," Wren said flatly. "He chased that story my entire childhood. Every spring he'd come back broke and frostbitten talking about the Vale, the fortune, how this time, *this time*, we'd have a house with glass windows. My mother sewed our coats with patches because he was out hunting a fairy tale." She picked up her coat. "You want to know what my father left me? A talent for recognizing that story. And you walked six weeks to deliver it."

"It's real," Coral said. "Or it might be. The map is detailed. It's his own work, years of it—"

"Then it's years of lies, carefully drawn." Wren shrugged into the canvas coat. Snowmelt still clung to the shoulders. "Keep it. Sell it. Whatever you were paid to bring it here, take double and go."

"Nobody paid me."

That stopped her, halfway to the door. She looked back, and for the first time her voice cracked along some hidden seam. "Then you're a fool. Or he was, and you caught it off him like the fever." She pulled the door open. Cold air knifed in, carrying the smell of snow. "Tell my father—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "Nothing. There's nothing to tell him. He's dead, and dead men don't hear it anyway. That was always his gift — being unreachable."

The door banged shut behind her.

Coral stood in the sudden quiet of the common room. Behind her, she heard Bramble mutter something to Odile, and Odile's low reply, and Tam's chair scrape as they pretended very hard not to have watched any of it.

The satchel strap pressed against her ribs. The map weighed nothing and everything.

* * *

She couldn't sleep.

The room she shared with Odile was narrow and cold, the window glazed with frost ferns, and Coral lay on the lumpy bed listening to the building tick and settle around her and replaying the conversation until the words wore grooves. *Dead men don't hear it anyway.* She had delivered the promise. She had done the thing, exactly the thing, and it had landed like a slap. Some promises, she was beginning to understand, could be kept and still fail.

Near midnight she gave up, dressed, and went down to the common room, thinking a cup of the innkeep's terrible mulled wine might burn a hole through the knot in her chest. The fire had been banked to embers. She was halfway to the hearth when she saw the light under the front door — lantern-light, moving — and heard the low murmur of voices outside.

Three voices. One of them laughing.

Coral knew that laugh. It had haunted six weeks of bad sleep — warm, unhurried, the laugh of a man who genuinely enjoyed his work. Juno Pike didn't travel to Coldharbor himself; she'd told herself that on the whole climb, told herself the Syndicate would send hired hands, nameless men. But the laugh said otherwise, and through the frost-glazed window she caught the glow of a lantern turning toward the inn's side entrance, and the silhouette of a broad-shouldered man in a fine coat, snow settling on his hat brim like it was glad to be there.

She moved. No decision in it, just motion — up the stairs, down the hall, hammering on doors. "Up. All of you. *Now.*"

They came awake the way road people do, fast and wordless. By the time the Syndicate men hit the front door — no subtlety now, the lock splintering like green wood — the crew was in the hall, and Coral had the map satchel in her fist and one heartbeat to decide what to do with it.

There was no time to run. Four of them, maybe five below, and the back stair creaked like a confession. So Coral did the only thing left: she shoved the satchel at Tam — "hide it, *now*, anywhere" — and stepped out onto the landing as the first boot hit the bottom step.

Juno Pike filled the doorway of the common room below, brushing snow from his sleeves, smiling up at her with genuine delight, as if they were old friends meeting at a wedding.

"Coral Dunmore." He spread his hands. "Do you know, I said to myself, *she'll make for Coldharbor* — and here you are. The mountains agree with you. You've got color in your cheeks."

"You're a long way from your countinghouse, Juno."

"I go where the assets are." Two of his men fanned out below, and a third began, methodically, to turn over furniture. "We can do this politely. Hand over the satchel, and everyone sleeps tonight. Or we can do it the other way, and I'll be honest with you, Coral — the other way is more fun for my boys, but it always makes such a mess of the carpets."

"There's no satchel."

"Coral." He said her name like a fond scolding. "Six weeks ago you lied to me in Bracken's Reach, and I let it slide, because I like you, and because I believed you'd come to your senses. Now here we are, at the top of the world, and the snows are coming, and my patience — well." He sighed, theatrical and warm. "My patience has closed for the season. Same as the passes."

Below, a man wrenched open a cabinet; dishes shattered. Another was coming up the stairs.

Tam slipped past Coral in the dark of the hallway, satchel swallowed somewhere into their ragged coat, gone like smoke — and Coral put her hand on the rail and smiled down at Juno Pike with all the bravado she had left, which was less than she needed and more than he deserved.

"Search the rooms," she said. "Search the whole damn inn. You'll find nothing but dirty laundry and a mule with opinions."

"Oh, I intend to." Juno's smile widened, and beneath it, for the first time, she saw the steel — the thing the smile was built to cover. "But maps are small things, Coral. Easy to hide. Easy to copy. And my men are *thorough*." He gestured, almost gently, and the ransacking rose around them like a tide — drawers, mattresses, floorboards, the shriek of the innkeep's wife waking and screaming about her property.

Coral stood on the landing and listened to her promise being hunted through the walls of a stranger's inn, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that delivering the map had not ended anything at all.

It had only shown everyone where to look.
