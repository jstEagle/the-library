## 11. What Tam Took

The inn at Coldharbor had a smell Tam knew from every town they'd ever slept in: tallow smoke and wet wool and the sour ghost of spilled ale, all of it soaked so deep into the timber that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out. They'd been in Coldharbor two days now, holed up in the common room of the Wayward Ewe while the weather outside turned from a suggestion of winter into a threat, and Tam had spent both of those days being very, very good at not looking guilty.

It was a skill. Tam had learned it young, the way other children learned their letters. You didn't avoid someone's eyes — that was the amateur mistake, the thing that got you caught. You looked at them *more*. You asked them questions about themselves. You made yourself small and helpful and forgettable in the specific way that people's eyes slid right over.

So when Coral Dunmore came down the stairs on the second morning with her jaw set like a cliff face and her eyes going around the room — the landlady, the fire, Bramble arguing with his mule through the window, Odile sharpening her ice axe by the hearth — Tam met her gaze square on and asked, cheerful as anything:

"More porridge?"

"I want to talk about the map."

The words landed in Tam's stomach like swallowed stones. But the training held. "What map?" And then, because the amateur mistake was to push it too far: "Oh. *That* map. The one the agents were tearing the inn apart for last night? I figured you had it."

Coral sat down across from them. Up close, in the grey morning light leaking through the frost-furred window, she looked older than she had in Bracken's Reach. The road did that to people. It was doing it to Tam too; they could feel it happening, some softness getting scraped off.

"The agents," Coral said quietly, "tore apart every room in this inn. Every mattress. Every floorboard. They found nothing." She turned her cup around on the table without drinking from it. "Which means either they're incompetent — and Juno Pike doesn't hire incompetent — or somebody moved it before they got here."

Tam's heart was doing something complicated against their ribs. "You think it was me."

"I think you lifted my compass off my belt inside an hour of meeting me. I think you've got hands like quicksilver and a conscience like a sieve. And I think—" Coral stopped. Rubbed her eyes. When she looked up again there was something in her face that wasn't anger at all, and somehow that was worse. "I think if it was you, you had a reason. So give me the reason before Odile gives you the axe."

From the hearth, without looking up from her whetstone: "I heard my name."

"You didn't," said Coral.

"I always hear my name."

* * *

They took it upstairs, because some things couldn't be said in front of a room full of strangers and one retired ferryman who gossiped like a woman half his age. Wren Vane was already there, sitting on the windowsill of the little room she'd refused to leave since refusing the map, arms wrapped around her knees, staring out at the mountains the way a person stares at a grave.

Coral shut the door. "Tam. Now."

And here was the thing Tam had rehearsed seventeen times in the dark, lying awake listening to Bramble snore like a landslide: the confession never came out the way it was supposed to. In rehearsal it was clean. In practice it came out sideways, tumbling, hands already moving — digging under the loose board beneath the mattress, pulling out the oilcloth bundle, holding it up between them like a shield.

"I didn't steal it," Tam said. "I *saved* it. There's a difference. Stealing is for profit. This was—"

"Open it," Coral said.

Tam opened it. Inside lay a sheet of paper covered edge to edge in Elias Vane's cramped, dying hand — the ridgelines, the river bends, the pass notations, the whole route to the Hollow Vale rendered in ink gone brown at the folds. Not the original. A copy. Tam had done it in the waystation at Coldharbor's outskirts, the night before they'd arrived, hunched over by candlelight with the original spread flat and their fingers black to the knuckle, working fast because fast was the only speed Tam had ever known.

"They came at midnight," Tam said. "The agents. I heard the horses first — I sleep light, you know that, I sleep light as a bird — and I heard Juno Pike's voice doing that thing where he's friendly, which is worse than when he shouts. So I took the map out of your satchel and I copied what I could and then I hid the original somewhere they'd never look, and I put the copy back in your satchel so you wouldn't notice, and I figured—" Their voice cracked, just slightly, and they hated it. "I figured you'd thank me."

Silence. Wren had come off the windowsill. She was staring at the copy with an expression Tam couldn't read at all.

"Where's the original?" Coral asked.

"That's the thing." Tam's mouth had gone dry. "That's the thing, and you have to understand, I copied it *fast*, because there were boots on the stairs, and most of it I got right, I got the rivers right, I got the ridge lines right, but the pass notation — the crucial one, the one that says which saddle gets you over the Serrano spine without walking into a cornice — he wrote it in shorthand. Little marks. Numbers. And I thought I remembered them, I *checked* them twice against my memory, but memory's a liar, everyone knows memory's a liar, and this morning I compared it against what I actually wrote down and—"

"TAM." Coral's voice cracked across the room like a whip. "Where. Is. The original."

Tam took a breath. Let it out. Held up the copy, and pointed at the notation in the corner — the little cluster of marks that should have described the true pass, and instead described something that would drop a climbing party into a crevasse field or worse.

"It's sewn into the shawl," they said. "Her mother's shawl. The one Wren's been carrying since Bracken's Reach. I watched Elias stitch it in myself, the night he gave me the map. He told me — he made me swear — that the map wasn't finished until it was home. That the shawl was the map's keeper. And I was going to tell you, I was, but then we got to Coldharbor and Wren said no, and it seemed like a bad time, and then the agents came, and now it seems like a worse time, and I know, I *know* how this looks—"

Wren had gone very still.

"My mother's shawl," she said.

"She carried it up the range twenty years ago," Tam said, quieter now. "Elias told me. He said she stitched the Vale's route into the lining herself, before he ever drew his own copy, because she was the one who found the valley first. He said the map was always hers. He was just the man who carried it after."

The silence that followed had weight to it, physical weight, like snow load on a roof. Outside, the wind had come up and was worrying at the eaves of the Wayward Ewe, and somewhere below them Bramble was still arguing with his mule, his voice rising and falling like weather.

Wren reached into her pack with hands that weren't quite steady. She pulled out the shawl — faded wool, dyed some colour that had once been blue and was now the grey-blue of glacial ice, frayed at every edge from years of use. Tam had seen it a hundred times. Had slept next to it. Had never once thought to look.

She turned it over. Found the seam along the border where the stitching ran too fine, too regular, too deliberate for mending. Her thumbnail found the thread's end and picked.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then a seam split, and from inside the lining Wren drew out a folded square of oilskin, thin as a wafer, and unfolded it on the table between them.

Elias Vane's map — no. *Not* Elias's map. The hand was different. Finer. A woman's hand, sure and unhesitating, the ridgelines drawn with the confidence of someone who had walked every one of them. Annotations in the margins in two different inks — hers first, then years later, Elias's additions crowding in around the edges like a man adding postscripts to a letter he was afraid to finish writing.

Wren made a sound. Small. Terrible. The kind of sound a person makes when something they've carried as a burden turns out to have been a gift all along.

"She drew this," Wren said. "My mother drew this."

"And Elias added to it," Coral said softly. "After."

"After she died." Wren's thumb traced the margin, where in tiny letters, in the second ink, someone had written: *for Wren, when she's ready.* "He kept it. All these years. He stitched it into her shawl because—" Her voice broke and reformed. "Because it was hers. Because he couldn't give it to me himself. Because he was a coward."

"He was a dying man," Coral said, "who kept a promise the only way he could."

"Don't." Wren folded the map closed with sudden violence. "Don't defend him to me. Not today."

But she didn't put the shawl away. She held it, bunched in her fists, pressed against her sternum like something that hurt to hold and hurt more to release.

* * *

They told the others at midday, gathered around the fire in the common room while the storm outside thickened from threat into fact. Odile took the news of Tam's deception with a single raised eyebrow and a long look that promised a conversation later, alone, involving vocabulary Tam did not want to hear. Bramble said, "Well, I'll be damned," and then, "does this mean we're going up the mountain, then?" with an eagerness he tried and failed to disguise.

Coral laid the original map on the table, weighted at the corners with four cups, and studied it the way she studied weather — reading patterns, reading intent, reading the story the lines told.

"The pass notation," she said finally. "Tam's copy would have killed us. This—" she tapped the corner, the cluster of fine marks in the dead woman's hand, "—this takes us over the spine by the Ghost Stair. Abandoned mining route. Steep, exposed, but it stays under the cornice line if the weather holds."

"If the weather holds," Odile repeated. She was looking at the map too, but her eyes kept flicking to Wren. "It won't hold. First snows are three weeks early. We go up in that, we go up in the teeth of it."

"Then we go up fast."

"We go up *dead*, more likely."

"Odile." Coral's voice was quiet. "Six weeks ago I owed the Meridian Syndicate more than I'll earn in ten years of chasing storms. As of last night, they think the map is lost or destroyed, which buys us days, not weeks. Silas Crane is on a parallel ridge with hired trackers. And every day we sit in this inn drinking bad ale, the passes close a little more." She looked around the table. "I'm not asking anyone to come. I'm telling you what I'm doing, and when, and why. Anyone who wants off, this is the stop."

Nobody moved. The fire popped. Wind worried the eaves.

It was Wren who broke the silence.

"I'm coming."

Every head turned. She was standing at the edge of the firelight with the shawl draped over her shoulders like a mantle, and her chin was up, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain, all at once.

"Not for the treasure," she said. "I don't care about your storm-glass. I don't care about debts or Syndicates or whatever's waiting in that valley. My mother walked into those mountains twenty years ago and never walked out, and my father spent the rest of his life carrying her map around his neck like a penance, and neither of them ever—" Her breath hitched. Steadied. "Neither of them ever came home. Somebody should. Somebody should finish the journey they started. That's mine to do. Mine alone."

"It's dangerous," Odile said. "The Ghost Stair in early winter is no place for—"

"For someone who's never climbed? I know." Wren's jaw set in a way that Tam suddenly, horribly recognized — the same jaw, the same set, the same stubborn angle they'd seen on a feverish old man in a roadside waystation, refusing to die until his bargain was struck. "Teach me fast."

Odile held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever she saw there seemed to satisfy something in her, because she nodded once, curt, and turned back to the fire.

"Then we leave tomorrow," Coral said. "First light. Before the weather closes the door for good."

* * *

That night, Tam couldn't sleep.

They lay in the loft above the stable, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of horse and hay, listening to the wind build itself into something with teeth. Below them, the mule stamped and muttered. Somewhere in the inn, Bramble was snoring like distant artillery. And across the yard, in the window of the room Wren had taken, a candle burned long past midnight — Tam could see the shadow of her moving behind the frost-furred glass, pacing, or packing, or simply standing with the shawl in her hands, learning its weight all over again.

Guilt was a strange animal. Tam had carried small ones all their life — the cutpurse's ordinary guilt, the kind that fit in a pocket and weighed nothing and meant nothing. This was different. This guilt had hooves and antlers and it stood on Tam's chest in the dark and stared at them with wet black eyes. Because the copy had been wrong. Because if Tam hadn't spoken up — if Coral had climbed with the forgery, if they'd trusted the wrong notation, if they'd taken the false pass into the high country—

They didn't finish the thought. Some thoughts were doors best left shut.

A floorboard creaked. Tam went rigid, hand sliding toward their knife out of pure reflex — and then stopped, because the figure climbing into the loft was small and hooded and moved with a familiar economy, and only one person in the world climbed like that.

Wren settled cross-legged on the straw a few feet away. For a while she said nothing. Neither did Tam. The wind filled the silence for them, pressing against the roof, testing the boards.

"You could have let it burn," Wren said at last. "The copy. You could have let them take it, or lose it, or destroy it, and then nobody would ever have gone looking, and the valley would have kept its secrets, and I'd never have known what my mother stitched into her own shawl." She was turning something over in her hands — the shawl itself, folded small. "Why didn't you?"

Tam considered several answers. The clever ones. The deflecting ones. What came out was none of them.

"Because it was hers," they said. "Your mum's. And things that belong to the dead shouldn't get lost just because the living are careless."

Wren looked at them for a long moment. In the dark, with the candlelight from the window catching the edge of her face, she looked suddenly very like the old man in the waystation — same bones, same stubbornness, same tired fire.

"Elias used to say something," she said. "When I was small. Before he left. He said: *the needle doesn't point north, girl. It points to what matters.* I thought it was nonsense. I thought everything he said was nonsense." She tucked the shawl under her chin, the way a child tucks a blanket. "Maybe he was only ever half a liar."

Below them, the mule stamped. The wind rose another register, moaning through the yard like something grieving.

"Wren," Tam said. "The pass notation. Your mother's marks. I need to ask you something, and I need you not to hate me."

"Ask."

"When I copied the map — the numbers, the shorthand — I got them wrong. I know I got them wrong. But I can't remember whether I misread them or misremembered them, and the difference matters, because if I misread them, then the copy isn't just wrong, it's wrong in a *direction*, and if the Syndicate ever got hold of it—"

"The Syndicate doesn't have the copy," Wren said. "You hid it. You said."

"I hid the *original*. The copy went back in Coral's satchel. And tonight, when we were packing—" Tam's throat tightened. "Tonight I checked the satchel. To make sure. And the copy's gone."

The words dropped into the dark between them and lay there, cold and heavy as stones.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Coldharbor, a lantern was moving through the falling snow. Small. Patient. Moving not toward the inn, but away from it — down the street, toward the low quarter, toward the stables where strangers' horses waited saddled in the night.

Toward the pass road.

Toward the mountains.
