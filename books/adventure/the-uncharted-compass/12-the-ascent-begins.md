## 12. The Ascent Begins

The snow came at them sideways, the way weather does in the Serrano Range — not falling so much as being thrown.

Coral pulled her collar up and squinted into the white. Three days out of Coldharbor, and the flurries that had flirted with them in the foothills had turned serious, thickening by the hour like a debt accruing interest. Behind her, the mule — Bramble's mule, a grey, judgmental creature named Duchess — planted her hooves in the fresh powder and refused, with the whole stubborn weight of her ancestry, to take another step.

"She'll move when she's ready," Bramble said, which was his answer to everything, including questions no one had asked.

"We don't have 'when she's ready,'" Coral said. "We have now. We have a window the width of a knife blade, and every hour we stand here arguing with a mule, that window narrows."

Bramble looked at her over Duchess's ears, his frost-reddened face arranged into an expression of profound, unhurried offense. "You can't rush a mule, girl. You can only negotiate."

"Everything in my life is a negotiation," Coral muttered. "I was hoping the mountain would be different."

Ahead, Odile had stopped where the trail bent around a granite shoulder, and she stood with her back to the wind in a way that made the wind seem like her idea. She was studying the cliff face above them — a wall of grey stone streaked with old rust stains, the ghosts of iron stakes driven into it a century ago by miners who'd wanted something in this mountain badly enough to die for it.

"There," Odile said, and pointed.

Coral followed her finger and saw it: a seam of cut steps ascending the rock like a ladder built by giants with bad tempers. Switchbacking. Narrow. Disappearing into the cloud.

"The Ghost Stair," Odile said. "The old Meridian mining route. They carved it in the boom years, before the mountain killed the last of them and the company wrote the valley off." She turned, and her eyes — pale as glacial melt — found Coral's. "It's steep. It's exposed. Half the steps are rotten. But it's the fastest way through the eastern wall, and the eastern wall is the only way to the high saddle before the snows close everything."

"And the other routes?"

"Closed. Or worse than closed." Odile smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "The Syndicate's scouts are watching the low passes. Silas Crane knows every trail I ever guided. The Ghost Stair is the one road in this range nobody wants, because everybody who took it left bones on it."

"Then it's ours," Coral said.

Behind her, Tam made a small sound — the sound of a teenager discovering that bravado has consequences. Coral didn't turn around. If she turned around, she'd see their face, and their face was seventeen years old under all that street-hardened scowl, and she'd start feeling things about it, and feeling things on a mountain was how people died.

"Everyone check your ropes," she said instead. "Odile leads. I'm second. Wren after me, then Tam, then Bramble and the negotiation specialist."

Duchess, as if understanding she'd won, finally moved.

* * *

The Ghost Stair was everything Odile had promised and less.

The steps had been cut for miners hauling ore in chains, not climbers with a mule, and a century of freeze and thaw had chewed them into suggestions. In places the stair simply stopped, and Odile would traverse along a ledge the width of a coffin, calling back instructions in her flat guide's cadence — *weight on the uphill foot, three points of contact, don't look down unless you've made peace with it* — and they would follow, one at a time, while the wind tried its best to peel them off the mountain like old paint.

Coral climbed with the map satchel strapped tight against her chest, under her coat, where it rode like a second heartbeat. Elias Vane's careful ink. The Hollow Vale. The promise she'd made to a dying man in a roadside waystation while rain hammered the roof and her debt to the Meridian Syndicate sat on her chest like a stone.

*Deliver it to my daughter.*

Well. She'd delivered it. Wren Vane had refused it, then hidden it, then — after Tam's confession and a night of fury and tears in a Coldharbor inn — sewn it into her dead mother's shawl and shown up at the trailhead at dawn with a pack twice too big and an expression that dared anyone to comment.

Now Wren climbed twenty feet below her, and Coral could feel her stare like sun through a magnifying glass.

She held out until the first rest ledge — a shelf of stone where the wind dropped just enough to let them hear each other — and then she turned around, and there it was: Wren's dark eyes, Elias's eyes, fixed on her with an accusation that hadn't cooled since Coldharbor.

"Say it," Coral said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying since the trailhead. You've been rehearsing it. I can hear you rehearsing it. Say it."

Wren pulled off one glove with her teeth and flexed her fingers, red with cold. "Fine." She said it the way you'd spit. "He sent a stranger. Not me. Not a letter in eleven years — a stranger. Some storm-chaser he met in a tavern, and he gives *her* the map, gives *her* the fortune, gives *her* his last words, and all I get is — what? A package. A delivery." Her voice cracked and she hated it, Coral could see her hating it, jaw tightening against the crack like someone bracing a door against wind. "He had my address. He knew where I was the whole time. He just didn't want to face me while he was dying, so he outsourced it. To you."

The wind gusted. Below them, Duchess made a low complaint, and Bramble murmured something soothing that was probably about oats.

"He was ashamed," Coral said.

"Don't." Wren's voice went flat and hard. "Don't you dare explain him to me. You knew him for three days. I knew him for sixteen years, and then I knew the absence for eleven more. You don't get to hand me a tidy little grief and call it understanding."

"Then what do you want me to do?"

"I want you to stop pretending this is a pilgrimage. You're not here for him. You're here because the Syndicate owns you and this map was your way out. Don't dress it up as a promise. I've seen how you look at the satchel. You look at it like money."

The words landed exactly where Wren wanted them to land, because Wren, Coral was learning, had her father's precision even if she'd never had his luck.

"That's true," Coral said.

Wren blinked. She'd been braced for a fight, and the absence of one knocked her off balance more thoroughly than any counterblow.

"It's true," Coral said again, quieter, over the wind. "I took the map because I'm drowning and it looked like a rope. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But here's what I've learned about drowning, Wren — you grab the rope, and then somewhere on the way up you notice there are other people holding it too, and you can either let go or you can pull together." She looked past Wren, down the stair, where Tam clung to the rock with the focused ferocity of someone who'd never had anything to hold before and wasn't going to waste it. Where Bramble coaxed Duchess up a rotten step with a patience Coral didn't have and maybe needed. Where Odile stood watch, scanning the ridgelines for trackers, guarding people she'd known for two weeks with a loyalty she'd never explained. "I made a promise to a dying man in bad faith. I'll give you that. But I'm keeping it in good faith now, and that has to count for something, because it's all I've got."

Wren stared at her for a long moment. The wind filled the silence with white noise.

"He was a coward," Wren said finally. But her voice had changed — the blade gone out of it, leaving only the wound.

"Maybe," Coral said. "Cowards and heroes look the same from the inside. It's only distance that tells you which one you were."

She turned and started up the stair, because the mountain didn't care about their grief and neither did the weather, and behind her, after a moment, Wren's boots followed.

* * *

They made the top of the Ghost Stair at what Odile's pocket watch insisted was noon but the sky insisted was dusk — a brown-grey murk pressing down through the cloud, the light coming from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless. The mining route crested onto a broad saddle of broken stone, and there, for the first time in three days, the cloud tore open for the length of a held breath.

The range opened beneath them like a book.

Peaks to the west, white and blue-shadowed, running in ranks to the horizon. Glaciers hanging in the high cirques like frozen waterfalls caught mid-fall. And ahead — north, into the heart of it — the passes they'd come to take, threaded with the thin grey lines of old trails, all of it utterly indifferent to them, to their debts and promises and griefs. The mountain had been here ten million years. It would be here ten million more. They were weather to it. They were a sneeze.

"God," Tam breathed. They'd come up last, hauling the rope coil, and now they stood at the crest with the wind pulling at their too-big coat, and for once there was nothing pickpocket-quick about them. Just a kid, staring.

"Yeah," Bramble said, coming up beside them with Duchess. "That's the trick of it. Makes you feel small as an ant, and then about a minute later it makes you feel like the only living thing that ever mattered. Both at once. That's mountains for you. They don't pick a feeling. They give you all of them and let you sort it out."

"Did you feel that, ferrying?"

"Ferrying's just a mountain made of water." Bramble patted Duchess's neck. "Same indifference. Wetter."

Odile spread the route across the saddle with her hands, pointing out the day's stages: the shoulder of the Grey Tooth, the moraine below it, the high camp at the tarn. Her voice was all business, but Coral watched her face while she talked, and saw what the guide let show only when she thought no one was looking — the way her eyes kept going to the peaks, hungry, like a woman visiting a house she'd been thrown out of.

They ate cold rations in the lee of a cairn the miners had built, stones stacked a century ago by hands that were now part of the mountain. Tam found a coin wedged between two rocks — an old mining scrip, green with corrosion — and held it up triumphantly, and Wren, to everyone's surprise, laughed. It was a short laugh, startled out of her, and Coral caught Odile's eye across the cairn, and something passed between them that had no words: *there she is. There's the person under the armor.*

The cloud closed again before they finished eating. Of course it did.

* * *

The snow turned serious an hour later.

It came in bands — a flurry, then a lull, then a flurry with teeth — and by mid-afternoon it was falling thick enough to erase the trail behind them as fast as they broke it. Odile navigated by dead reckoning and by the cairns, some of them miners', some of them her own from another life, and Coral watched her do it and understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, exactly how much of their survival was walking around in one disgraced guide's head.

"Odile." Coral drew level with her, pitching her voice under the wind. "If something happens to you—"

"Nothing's happening to me."

"If something happens to you. I need to know the route. At least the shape of it."

Odile was quiet for three steps. Four. "Grey Tooth shoulder, then down into the moraine valley, north-northeast, two hours to the tarn. High camp on the east shore. From the tarn, the route climbs the glacier tongue to the saddle — the one on your map, the one Tam copied wrong." She glanced sideways, and there was dry humor in it, thin as the air. "From the saddle, your father-in-law's valley is a day down the far side. Assuming the far side is climbable in a closing season, which is a bet I wouldn't take at any odds you could afford."

"He's not my father-in-law."

"You argue like family. Close enough."

Coral let that sit. The snow ticked against her hood. Ahead, Wren was helping Tam over a boulder field, her hand out, steady, no hesitation — the same hand that had refused a map and a father and Coral herself, extended now without being asked.

"Odile," Coral said. "Why are you here? Really. The Syndicate ruined you, fine. But revenge doesn't get a person up a mountain in October. Revenge keeps you in taverns, drinking, telling the story. This—" she gestured at the white void, the five of them, the mule, "—this is something else."

Odile walked six more steps before she answered.

"My father was a guide," she said. "His father before him. In my family, you didn't ask what you did for a living. You asked which mountain." The wind gusted and she leaned into it, unbothered, like a tree. "When the Syndicate burned my outfit — the permits, the contracts, the name my grandfather built — everyone told me to go south. Start over in the lowlands. Be someone else." Her mouth twisted. "But you can't be someone else. You can only be what you are, somewhere new, until the mountain finds you again. Mountains always find you. So when you walked into my camp with a map and a suicide schedule and asked if I could get five people over the range before the snows—" she shrugged, the motion barely visible under her pack, "—it wasn't a job offer, Coral. It was a resurrection. I'd have done it for free."

"You didn't."

"I have a mule to feed."

* * *

They reached the tarn at last light.

It was a small black eye of water in a bowl of stone, already rimmed with ice, and the snow was falling straight down now, heavy and silent, the wind having finally spent itself. They pitched the tents in the blue half-dark, Bramble's hands — Coral noticed, and filed the noticing away with a small cold dread — moving slower than they had that morning, clumsier, the cold already writing its first draft on his fingers.

Dinner was soup that tasted mostly of smoke and altitude. They ate in Odile's big tent, all five of them and the stove, because Odile said — correctly — that body heat was fuel and she didn't waste fuel.

It was Tam who broke the quiet, the way Tam always did, sideways and from an angle no one was guarding.

"Do you think he made it up here?" they asked. "Elias. Your dad," they added, nodding at Wren, who stiffened.

"He never came," Wren said. "He talked about it. The Vale, the storm-glass, the whole legend. He talked about it my whole childhood. Drawed maps on the kitchen table in flour." Her spoon circled the soup without lifting. "But he never went. Eleven years, and he never once came north."

"Maybe he was waiting," Tam said.

"Waiting for what?"

"Dunno. Maybe for it to be safe. Or for the right crew. Or—" Tam shrugged, embarrassed by their own sincerity, hiding it in a slurp of soup. "Maybe for you to be old enough to come with. People do that. Wait so long the waiting becomes the thing."

Wren didn't answer. But she looked at Tam for a long moment, and then at the tent wall, where the stove light made shadows of all of them, huge and flickering on the canvas — a family, if you squinted, if you were generous, if you were the kind of person who found families in impossible places.

Coral was that kind of person, apparently. She'd fought it the whole way from Bracken's Reach and lost.

She stepped outside before she said something true and dangerous. The snow had stopped. The cloud was shredding, and through the tears she could see stars — hard, close, brutal stars — and the bulk of the Grey Tooth against them, and beyond it, invisible in the dark, the glacier and the saddle and the valley where a dead man's fortune waited, and somewhere off the edge of every map, a needle's worth of something else.

Her compass hung around her neck under three layers. She didn't take it out. She was afraid of what it would do, and more afraid of what she'd do about it.

Behind her, the tent flap rustled. Wren came out, wrapped in the shawl — her mother's shawl, with its secret cargo — and stood beside her in the snow, not close, not far. The distance of two people sharing a fire they haven't admitted to building.

"He talked about the flour maps to a stranger in a waystation," Wren said at last, to the mountains, not to Coral. "You knew that much about him in three days."

"He talked about you the whole time," Coral said. "The three days I knew him, it was mostly you."

Wren was quiet for a long time. The stars burned. Somewhere up on the Grey Tooth, wind moved through rock and made a sound almost like singing, almost like mourning.

"He was still a coward," Wren said finally. But it was a truce. Coral took it.

They were turning in when Odile, who had taken the last watch and was standing at the tent line with her head cocked like a bird's, raised one hand.

Everyone froze.

"What?" Bramble whispered.

"Light," Odile said. "On the glacier tongue. Moving."

Coral followed her gaze, and there — far up the ice, a mile maybe, two — a pinprick of yellow swung once against the snowfield and vanished. A lantern. Or a torch. Carried by someone climbing through the night, at altitude, in a closing season.

Nobody climbed like that for honest reasons.

"That's not the Syndicate," Odile said quietly. "Syndicate scouts travel in daylight and complain the whole time. That's someone in a hurry." She turned, and the starlight showed Coral her face, and Odile's face was doing something Coral had never seen it do: doubting itself. "That's someone who knows this route."

The light swung again, far up the dark ice, and went out.
