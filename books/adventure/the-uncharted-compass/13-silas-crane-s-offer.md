## 13. Silas Crane's Offer

Wren Vane had spent her whole life learning the shape of other people's silences, and so she heard it before anyone else did: the way Coral went quiet at breakfast, the way Odile's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth, the way Bramble's mule — that shaggy barometer of trouble named Duchess — turned her ears toward the treeline and refused to turn them back.

They had made camp in the bones of an old waystation, a mining-era shelter whose roof had half collapsed into a lopsided wooden smile. Someone had built a fire ring out of river stones a hundred years ago and someone else — probably Tam, who could not leave anything unturned without turning it — had added to it last night. The wind came down off the Ghost Stair smelling of iron and old snow, and Wren sat with her back against the wall, her mother's shawl around her shoulders, watching Coral not-watch the ridge.

"You felt it too," Wren said.

Coral didn't pretend otherwise. That was one of the things Wren was still deciding how to feel about: Coral Dunmore did not lie to her face. She lied with her whole body, constantly, to everyone, but never to your face, which was somehow worse and better at once.

"Someone's been climbing behind us since yesterday," Coral said. "Keeping pace. Not closing." She turned her cup in her hands. "Syndicate scouts don't keep pace. They close."

"Then who—"

"I know who," Odile said from across the fire. She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "I've been knowing since the lantern on the ice. There's only one person alive who climbs like that — fast, alone, no camp smoke, no complaints." She looked at Coral. "You know who too. You've known for two days. You just haven't said it out loud yet, because saying it out loud makes it real."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow sloughed off a bough with a soft whump.

"Say it anyway," Wren said.

Odile looked into the flames. "Silas Crane."

* * *

Wren knew the name the way you know a scar on someone else's body — something mentioned once, quickly, and never explained. She'd caught it in fragments over the weeks of the climb: Silas this, Silas that, always dropped by Coral mid-sentence and abandoned like a hot coal. From Tam she'd gotten the fuller story, extracted the way Tam extracted everything, through patience and pickpocketing of conversation rather than pockets.

Silas Crane had been Coral's partner once. Not a business partner — Wren understood that distinction now, having watched Coral laugh at things that weren't funny and go still at things that were. A partner the way a keel is a partner to a boat. They had chased storms together for six years, shared tents and frostbite and one legendary season on the coast where they'd ridden out a hurricane tied back-to-back to a lightning rod because it was the only thing left standing. And then something had happened — Tam couldn't say what, only that Coral came back from it alone, and that afterward she stopped saying the word *we* about anything except debts.

Now Wren watched Coral stand up, brush off her trousers, and say, in a voice carefully sanded smooth of everything:

"He'll want to talk. So we talk. Nobody hands over anything, nobody agrees to anything, and nobody — *nobody* — wanders off to have private conversations." She said the last part looking directly at Tam, who put a hand over their chest with wounded innocence. "We do this in the open, together, or we don't do it at all."

"And if he brought friends?" Bramble asked. He was mending a harness strap, his thick fingers clumsy with cold. Since the river crossing he'd been quieter, and since Coldharbor quieter still, and Wren had noticed him checking the southern skyline more often than a man checking weather needed to. She filed that away. She was getting good at filing things away.

"Then we find out how many," Odile said, "and I remind myself why I stopped guiding for people I don't like."

They broke camp and climbed.

* * *

The waystation ruin sat in a saddle between two shoulders of the mountain, a stone-walled relic with a collapsed roof and a chimney that still stood like a raised finger. It had been a shelter once, then a storehouse, then nothing; now its empty windows framed views of the valley below and the pass above, and the wind moved through it making a low moan that Wren decided was the building complaining about its retirement.

He was already there when they arrived.

He stood at the far window with his back to them, and even from behind Wren could read the story of him: tall, lean in the way of people who burn themselves as fuel, grey shot through dark hair, a climbing coat worn at the elbows and patched with what looked like sailcloth. He didn't turn when they entered. He let them come all the way into the room, let Odile position herself by the door and Bramble by the wall and Tam slide into the shadows near the broken stair, before he spoke.

"Coral."

One word. Her name in his mouth sounded like an old key turning in a lock that hadn't been opened in years — rusty, reluctant, and unmistakably still fitting.

"Silas." Coral's voice was level. Her hands, Wren noticed, were not quite still. "You're a long way from the coast."

"The coast got boring." He turned then, and Wren saw his face, and understood immediately why Coral had gone silent for two days. He was handsome the way winter is handsome — clean-lined, severe, with eyes the pale grey of ice under strain. But it wasn't the looks. It was the recognition. The way he looked at Coral was the way you look at a house you grew up in: every detail catalogued, every change noted, every change mourned. "You look terrible, Dunmore."

"You should see the other guy."

"There is no other guy. There never is, with you. You just wear yourself down and call it endurance." His gaze moved past her, cataloguing: Odile, with a nod that held genuine respect — guides recognized each other the way wolves do. Bramble, with a flicker of something unreadable. Tam, half-hidden, with a small amused tilt of his head that suggested he'd already lifted something off someone. And then Wren. He stopped on her. Something moved behind his eyes. "You must be Vane's girl."

"Wren," she said. "Just Wren."

"Elias Vane's daughter," he said, as if she hadn't spoken. "The map-maker's daughter. Do you know what your father found?"

"I know what my father *lost*," Wren said. "Everything. Including me."

Something flickered across Silas's face — not sympathy, exactly. Recognition, maybe. He turned back to Coral.

"I'm going to make this simple, because I know how you feel about complicated things. The Syndicate hired me to bring them the Vale. Juno Pike, specifically. And I took the contract, because I take contracts, because that's what I do now instead of—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening, "—instead of other things. But here's what Juno doesn't know, and what you're going to figure out eventually anyway: the Syndicate doesn't want a valley. They want a monopoly. Storm-glass in Syndicate hands means storm-glass prices that no chaser, no town, no family can afford. They'll strip the Vale bare in five years and leave a hole in the mountain, and everyone who ever needed glass to survive a bad season will be paying for it until they die."

"So?" Coral said. But Wren heard the catch in it. The tiny hitch.

"So I'm offering you the same deal I gave myself." Silas stepped closer. The wind moaned through the broken roof. "Give me the map. I'll get to the Vale first, file the claim under a name the Syndicate can't touch, and cut them out entirely. No debt collectors. No fingers. No Juno Pike smiling at you across a table for the rest of your life. You walk away clean, Coral. Clean. When has anyone ever offered you that?"

The silence stretched.

And Wren — who had spent her whole life reading silences, who had learned silence in a house where her father's absences shouted louder than any words — watched Coral Dunmore almost say yes.

She saw it happen. Saw it in the way Coral's chin lifted, the way her eyes went distant and calculating, running numbers the way she ran storms: risk, reward, margin of survival. Saw it in the way her hand drifted toward the satchel strap at her shoulder — not touching it, but drifting, the way a compass needle drifts before it settles. Coral wanted it. God help her, Coral wanted the clean exit more than Wren had ever seen her want anything, and why wouldn't she? Six weeks of dread, a debt measured in fingers, a life spent outrunning creditors — and here stood the one man alive who knew exactly what that cost, offering her a door out.

"That's a lie," Wren said.

Everyone turned. Even Silas raised an eyebrow.

"It's a beautiful lie," Wren went on, her voice steadier than she felt, "and I think maybe you even believe it yourself, which is what makes it dangerous. But claims get bought. Names get bought. The Syndicate doesn't get cut out, Silas — it buys in. It always buys in. My father told me that about mining towns my whole childhood, and he was a coward about everything except that truth." She stepped forward, into the space between Coral and the man from her past. "You're not offering her freedom. You're offering her a different cage with a better view."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — a thin, tired, genuine thing. "Vane blood," he said. "Your father used to argue like that. Before he stopped arguing and started running."

"Don't," Wren said quietly. "Don't talk about him. You didn't know him."

"No," Silas agreed. "I didn't." He turned back to Coral, and his voice dropped, went quiet, went *private*, and that was the cruelest thing he'd done yet, because he made the whole room into an eavesdropper. "Coral. Six years. Back-to-back on a lightning rod. You know I'm not Juno Pike. You know what I am. One word. Just tell me where the Vale is, and by spring you'll be chasing storms again with nothing on your back but weather."

Wren held her breath.

Coral looked at him. Really looked at him, the way you look at a door you could walk through, and Wren saw the whole arithmetic of her life pass behind her eyes — the debt, the fear, the six weeks, the fingers, the years of running — and then Coral's gaze moved. Slid sideways. Past Silas. To the doorway, where Odile stood with her arms crossed. To the wall, where Bramble leaned, watching with those sad steady eyes. To the shadow by the stair, where Tam was very deliberately not fidgeting. To Wren herself, standing in the middle of the ruin in her dead mother's shawl, holding a promise she hadn't asked for and couldn't put down.

Coral's hand came up — and settled on the satchel strap, and stayed there, gripping it, not opening it.

"No," she said.

Silas exhaled slowly through his nose. "Coral—"

"No." Steadier now. "You want to know the funny thing, Silas? For about ten seconds I was doing the math. Ten good seconds of math." She laughed, short and joyless. "But here's what the math keeps leaving out. Every clean exit I've ever been offered had a toll, and the toll was always the same: everybody I was traveling with pays it, and I walk through alone. That's the deal. That's always been the deal. And I'm—" she stopped, and Wren watched her actually hear herself, watch the surprise move across her face like weather, "—I'm not doing that anymore. I'm done arriving places alone."

Silas studied her for a long time. The wind filled the pause, moaning through the chimney.

"You've changed," he said finally, and there was something in it that wasn't accusation. Something almost like grief.

"Yeah," Coral said. "Turns out that's possible."

He nodded slowly. Then he reached inside his coat, and the whole room tensed — Odile shifted her weight, Tam's hand disappeared into a pocket — but what Silas drew out was only a folded paper, which he tossed onto the stones between them. "Weather window," he said. "Three days, maybe four, before this system parks itself on the range for the winter. After that, nobody goes anywhere, including me." He looked at Coral one last time. "I'm going to beat you to the Vale, Dunmore. Not for them. For the argument. So that someday, when you're old and stubborn, you'll have to admit I was right about at least one thing."

"Get in line," Coral said.

He smiled — the tired smile again — and walked to the doorway. He paused beside Odile. "Your route up the Ghost Stair is wrong, by the way. The third switchback collapsed two seasons ago. Go wide east, along the moraine." And then he was gone, out into the wind, and within a minute the mountain had swallowed him whole, as if it had only lent him to them for an hour and wanted him back.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while.

Bramble put the kettle on, because Bramble believed every crisis could be improved by tea and was usually right. Tam emerged from the shadows and, without a word, produced Silas's own tin cup from somewhere inside their coat, examined it with professional appreciation, and set it down by the fire. "Souvenir," they said.

Odile crouched by the fire, feeding it a stick. "That was the hardest thing you've ever done," she said to Coral. It wasn't a question.

Coral didn't answer right away. She was looking at the doorway Silas had gone through, and her hand was still on the satchel strap, and Wren thought: she's not letting go of the map. She's letting go of him. There's a difference, and it costs more.

"Yeah," Coral said at last. "It was."

Wren crossed the ruin and sat down next to her, shoulder to shoulder, the way you sit with someone when you want them to know you're a fact in their life and not a variable. "Thank you," she said quietly. "For not giving him the map."

"Don't thank me yet." Coral's voice was rough. "I almost did. You saw."

"I saw you not do it. That's the part that counts."

Coral huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then her eyes drifted to the paper Silas had left on the stones — the weather window, three days, maybe four — and Wren watched the relief drain out of her face and get replaced by something colder and more urgent.

"Three days," Coral said. "Odile, how far to the high saddle?"

"Two days, if the weather holds and nothing breaks." Odile glanced up at the sky, which was beginning to bruise at the edges, clouds stacking themselves in the west like plates before a fall. "If it doesn't hold—"

"It'll hold," Coral said. She stood, and the tiredness fell off her like a coat, and underneath it was the woman who chased storms for a living. "Pack up. All of you. We climb tonight."

Tam groaned. Bramble sighed and began breaking down the kettle. But Wren stayed sitting a moment longer, looking at the western sky, at the bruise spreading there, and felt the first flakes land on the shawl — her mother's shawl — light as ash.

Behind them, far down the valley, a lantern swung once against the dark.

And did not go out.
