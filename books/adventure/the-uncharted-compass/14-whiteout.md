## 14. Whiteout

The mountain had been grumbling for two days before it finally spoke.

Odile felt it first in her teeth — a pressure behind the molars, the old ache that meant the air itself was turning against them. She'd grown up with that ache in the high valleys of the Serrano, where her grandmother taught her to read weather the way other folk read scripture: by the color of the light on the ice, by the way ravens flew low and silent, by the smell of the wind coming off the glacier fields. Her grandmother used to say the mountains only ever told you the truth once, and if you weren't listening, they'd tell you again with a shovel.

By midmorning on the third day above the Ghost Stair, Odile was listening hard.

The sky had gone the color of dirty wool. Not clouded — *sealed*, like a lid clamped down over the range, no horizon anywhere, sky and snowfield bleeding into each other until the world was one flat white nothing with five small figures crawling across it. The wind came up out of the southwest in long, low moans, then in gusts that hit like fists, driving needles of ice sideways. Coral stood at the head of the column with her collar up and her compass out, shouting something Odile couldn't hear over the wind, and Odile watched the girl's hair whip loose from its braid and thought: *she doesn't feel it yet. She's still looking at the needle instead of the sky.*

"Bramble!" she bellowed, cupping her hands. "How's the mule?"

Bramble turned, and even through the wind-scour she could see his grin — that great, unbothered grin of his, the one he wore like armor. "Hesper's fine! Better than fine! She's the only one of us with any sense, walking backward so the wind don't get in her teeth!"

"Get her close! Everyone rope up!"

They roped up. Tam went gray-faced and quiet, which Odile had learned to read as fear; the kid talked constantly when they were comfortable and went mute as a stone when they were not. Wren Vane moved well — better than Odile expected from a town girl, steady-footed, economical, as though her body remembered things her mind had never been told. And Coral — Coral walked like a woman being pulled, leaning into the wind at an angle that had nothing to do with balance and everything to do with want.

That was the thing about Coral Dunmore that Odile had never decided whether to admire or despise: she wanted things too much. You could see it in her stride. A good mountaineer wanted nothing. A good mountaineer walked empty, so the mountain had nothing to grab hold of.

The storm hit them an hour past noon.

It didn't build. It arrived — all at once, like a door slamming, the wind doubling and trebling in the space of ten breaths until the world dissolved entirely. Odile lost sight of Bramble six feet ahead of her. She lost sight of her own boots. She kept moving by counting steps and by the pull of the rope, and she shouted the count aloud because she knew the others would need to hear a human voice more than they'd need anything else.

"Fifty-one! Fifty-two! Keep the rope taut — Coral, call your count!"

Coral called it. Good. Then: "There — ahead left — is that rock or shadow—"

It was rock. A shoulder of granite leaning out of the slope, and beneath it, half-buried in the drift already building against its lee side, a hollow — not a cave proper, but a scooped-out pocket where the snow had never quite filled in, deep enough, maybe, if they dug. Odile made the decision the way her grandmother had taught her: fast, and without apology.

"We dig! Here! Now!"

They dug for forty minutes while the storm tried to bury them alive. Tam clawed snow like a terrier, silent, ferocious. Wren carved blocks with the shovel and passed them back to stack a windwall. Bramble hauled Hesper's lead and sang something low and tuneless under his breath, some ferryman's working song, and Odile understood later that he was singing for the same reason she was counting — because a voice in the white is a lantern.

The shelter, when it was done, was ugly and perfect. A chamber big enough for four bodies and a mule packed tight against the entrance wall, a sleeping platform of stacked blocks, a candle-nook cut into the side, and a tunnel out angled downward against drift. Odile crawled in last, sealed the door-block behind them, and lit the candle, and in its small trembling light she looked at the faces around her — white-rimed, exhausted, eyes too bright — and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her chest.

She'd buried this feeling years ago, along with her business, her name, and her reputation. It was the guide's oldest weight: *these people are mine now. Whatever happens on this mountain happens to me.*

* * *

The first day, they slept.

There was nothing else to do. The storm roared outside like a living thing, pressing against the walls until the whole shelter hummed, and inside there was only the candle, the mule's warm flank, and the sound of five people breathing. Odile rationed without discussion: a strip of dried meat each, a handful of oats shared between the humans because Hesper needed the rest herself, a spoonful of fat melted over the candle flame and drunk hot. Hunger was manageable. Cold was the enemy. Cold crept in through the floor and stole heat from the body's core, and the only defense was to stay dry, stay together, and keep the blood moving.

Bramble took the watch first, because he insisted, and because his hands were already wrong.

Odile noticed it when he passed her the fat-cup. His fingers wouldn't close properly around it — stiff, waxy-pale at the tips, the nails gone a bruised blue-gray. He saw her looking and grinned wider, which was how she knew it was bad. Bramble wore his grin the way other men wore bandages.

"Just cold," he said. "Been colder. Fished the winter run off the Kettle Narrows with bare hands once, caught near forty pounds of—"

"Bramble." She took his hands in hers without asking. They were cold as river stones, and the fingertips did not flush pink when she pressed them. "Boots off. Hands in your armpits. Next to your skin."

"Now hold on, I'm not—"

"That's not a request." She kept her voice level, guide-voice, the voice that brooked no argument on a rope. "You'll keep them warm tonight or you'll lose them. Those are the choices, and I've already made yours."

He grumbled, but he did it. Later, in the dark, she heard him breathing slow and deliberate through the pain of thawing — that deep, rhythmic breath men use when they're hurting badly and refuse to say so — and she lay awake listening to it and doing arithmetic she hated. Three days of storm, maybe four. Frostbite in the fingers by day one. If the tips went black, someone would have to decide what to do about it up here, with a candle and a knife and no surgeon within a hundred miles.

She decided, privately, that she would be the one who decided. That was what guides were for.

* * *

The second day, the stories started.

It began, as these things do, with boredom curdling toward dread. Tam cracked first — Tam always cracked first, being young and full of unspent motion. By midday the kid was fidgeting, picking at the wall, asking how long, asking what if, asking whether anyone had ever died in one of these shelters, which was the wrong question to ask a room full of tired climbers.

"They die in them all the time," Odile said, because lying to children made them stupid. "Usually of boredom. Sometimes of bad decisions. We'll avoid both."

"You're a terrible comfort," Wren said.

"I'm not a comfort. I'm a guide."

It was Coral who changed the weather inside the cave. She'd been quiet all day — quieter than usual, and Coral's usual was quiet enough — sitting with her back against Hesper's warm flank, turning the compass over and over in her gloved hands. Then, apropos of nothing, she said:

"My mother hated storms."

The candle guttered. Nobody spoke. Coral wasn't looking at any of them; she was looking at the compass, at the needle lying still beneath its glass.

"She grew up lowland, down in the marsh country. Said thunder sounded like the sky falling on you personally. But she married my father anyway, and he was a range man, and after he died she kept coming up here. Every season. People said she was chasing his ghost." Coral's thumb traced the compass bezel. "She wasn't. She was chasing the thing her compass pointed at. Same as mine does. This belonged to her."

"The needle," Tam said softly. "It points to what you lost."

"It points to whatever the holder has lost. That's the story, anyway. My father bought it off a marsh-witch before I was born, paid a price nobody would name twice. When my mother vanished — eleven years ago, up in this very range — I got it back, and the needle's been pulling me ever since. I spent three seasons following it before I ran out of money and started chasing storms for hire instead." She laughed, short and bitter. "Storms pay better than ghosts."

"And now?" Wren asked. "Where does it point now?"

Coral was quiet a long moment. "Northwest," she said finally. "Off every map I own. Toward something I haven't found yet."

Tam, curled against the mule, said nothing at all. But Odile saw the kid's eyes flick toward the satchel where the map lived, and saw the quick complicated guilt cross the young face, and filed it away. There was something Tam hadn't told them. There was something everyone in this cave hadn't told the others. That was how families worked, in Odile's experience — a pile of secrets pressed close enough together to make warmth.

"Your turn," Coral said, and nodded at Odile. "Fair's fair. You know everything about us. We know nothing about you except you hate the Syndicate worse than we do."

"I don't hate them," Odile said. "Hate takes energy. I *bill* them."

But the candle was low, and the storm was loud, and something about the dark makes liars of us all — or honest ones. She heard herself begin before she'd decided to.

"I had a business once. Eastwick Lines, we called it — no. Never mind the name. Thirty guides, four stations, contracts with every mining concern in the central range. Fifteen years I built it. I knew these mountains better than the Syndicate's own surveyors, and they knew it, and that was the problem." She fed the candle another inch of wax. "They wanted exclusive passage rights. All the high routes, all the waystations, everything north of the Kettle. I refused. Politely, at first. Then less politely. Then Juno Pike came to my station himself — smiling, the way he does — and offered to buy me out at triple value. I threw him out. Six weeks later, three of my guides died on a route I'd run two hundred times without incident. Fixed ropes cut. Anchors pulled. An 'accident' the Syndicate's surveyors helpfully confirmed, after which my insurance lapsed, my contracts evaporated, and my clients discovered a sudden preference for Syndicate-certified guides." She shrugged, as though the shrug could carry the weight. "I lost everything in a season. So when Coral came to me talking about crossing the range ahead of Pike's people, I didn't join for the pay."

"Why then?" Tam asked.

"Because I've been waiting eleven years for someone to point me at that man," Odile said, "and I intend to walk up to him on his own mountain and hand him the bill."

Silence. Outside, the wind screamed and screamed. Then Bramble, from his corner, hands tucked in his armpits, voice rough with pain and something gentler:

"My turn, seems like. Though I don't know why anyone'd want my story. It's not much. Ferryman thirty-eight years on the Graywater. Carried pilgrims, smugglers, brides, corpses. You learn things about people on a boat. You learn that everybody's running from something or toward something, and mostly it's the same thing wearing different clothes." He shifted. "Wife died eight years back. Daughter lives south in Millbrook, got three little ones. I send what I can. That's the whole of it. Man runs a ferry long enough, he stops needing adventures. He just needs the water to keep flowing and the family to keep eating."

"That's not nothing," Wren said quietly.

"No," Bramble agreed. "I suppose it isn't."

And then Wren told them about her mother — not Elias, but the woman before, dead a decade, who had come up this same range with Elias on their honeymoon and loved it so fiercely she'd made him promise to bring her back every year until the year she couldn't anymore. Wren had never seen the mountains before this trip. She'd spent her whole life angry at her father for leaving, and it was only up here, in the white roaring dark, that she'd begun to understand he might have been coming back to a place rather than running from one.

"He sewed her shawl into the lining of his coat," Wren said. "I found it after he died. I've been carrying it since Bracken's Reach. It smells like cedar and smoke. I sleep with it under my head." She paused. "I think that's why I came. Not for the treasure. For her."

Nobody spoke after that. The candle burned down. Tam, in the dark, in a voice smaller than Odile had ever heard from the kid, said: "I don't have a story. I got lifted off a stoop when I was six. Don't remember a mother's face. Remember doors, mostly. Which ones lock, which ones don't, which windows stick. Streets teach you buildings before they teach you people." A pause. "This is the first place I ever stayed where somebody noticed if I stopped breathing."

Odile reached across the dark and put her hand on the kid's shoulder, and left it there.

* * *

The third day, the food ran to crumbs and the storm did not care.

Bramble's fingers were worse. The left hand especially — index and middle finger gone from pink to mottled gray, the skin blistering at the nail line. He'd stopped joking about it, which frightened Odile more than the injury did. She thawed them against her own belly in shifts, wrapped them in dry cloth, fed him the largest share of what remained and lied about how much that was.

"How bad?" he asked her, once, quietly, while the others dozed.

"Ask me when we're down," she said.

"That bad."

"Maybe not. Maybe we get you to Coldharbor inside a week and a real surgeon saves most of it." She met his eyes. "But if the black comes up past the knuckle, I'm deciding, Bramble. Not you. You'll argue and I'll win, because I'm right and you'll be feverish."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he smiled — the real one, the one under the armor-grin — and said, "You're a hard woman, Odile."

"I'm a guide."

"Aye. Well." He settled back against the wall. "My daughter'd like you. She's hard too. Gets it from her mother, thank the gods."

Outside, the wind had begun to change — Odile could hear it, the pitch dropping, the scream flattening into a moan, the moan into a long exhausted sigh. Storms that die slowly are either finished or reloading, and a guide learns to tell which by the weight of the silence that follows. This silence felt finished. Light, almost. Spent.

Near dusk — if it was dusk; the candle said so, and the candle was the only clock they had — the sound changed again, and Tam, who had been pressed to the entrance block for an hour like a cat at a door, whispered:

"It's stopping. Odile. It's *stopping.*"

They broke the seal carefully, all of them, even Bramble, crowding toward the gap as Odile eased the block aside — and cold clean air poured in, thin and glittering, and beyond the mouth of the shelter the world had been remade. Snow to the depth of a man's chest in the drifts, the sky scoured to a hard blue-black clarity, stars already pricking through, and the whole range standing silent and enormous under the fresh white, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful.

"Three days," Odile said. "We lost three days. Food's gone, fuel's nearly gone, and Bramble needs a surgeon." She turned to Coral. "We move at first light. Hard march, no delays. And Coral—" she hesitated, because the next part mattered, "—your needle. It's been twitching since the storm broke. I've seen you checking it. Where's it pointing?"

Coral had the compass out already. In the starlight Odile watched her face change — watched the color leave it, watched her lips part around words that took two tries to find.

"West," Coral said. "Off the route. Off every route. Into the uncharted valley." She looked up, and her eyes were shining and terrified in equal measure. "Odile — it's pointing at something. Something real. And it's pointing *hard*."

Behind her, Tam went very still, and Odile — who had spent a lifetime reading mountains and only recently learned to read people — saw the kid's hand slide, casual as breathing, toward the satchel where the map slept.

And the needle, Odile would swear afterward, seemed to lean toward it too.
