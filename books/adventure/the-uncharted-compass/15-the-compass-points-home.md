## 15. The Compass Points Home

The storm died the way storms do in the high country — not with a retreat but with a surrender, the wind simply running out of argument. Coral woke to a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving, and for a long moment she lay in the snow cave's blue half-light and could not remember where she was, only that she was cold in a way that had become familiar, cold the way a stone is cold, all the way through and unbothered by it.

Then Tam sneezed, and the spell broke.

They crawled out into a world remade. Three days of storm had buried everything — the Ghost Stair, the cairns Odile had built, the whole argument of the mountain — under six feet of new snow, smooth and featureless as fresh paper. The sky above was that hard, rinsed blue that comes after weather, so clear it looked like it would ring if you struck it. The peaks around them burned white and gold in the early light, and the air was so thin and clean it hurt to breathe, like swallowing glass that had been dipped in honey.

"Beautiful," Bramble said from the cave mouth, his bandaged hands cradled against his chest like something rescued from a fire. "And completely useless. We don't know where we are."

"We know exactly where we are," Odile said. She stood hip-shot on the drift, squinting east, reading the ridgelines the way other people read letters from home. "That's the Serrated Tooth. That notch is Miller's Gap, or what's left of it. The storm took our trail, not our position." She turned, and her eyes went to Coral, and there was a question in them she didn't ask out loud. Not yet.

Coral dug the compass out of her inner pocket, where it had lived against her heart since Bracken's Reach, warming itself on her body heat like a small sleeping animal. Her mother's compass. The one with the needle that didn't point north — that pointed, Elias Vane had said with fever-bright eyes, toward whatever the holder had lost.

She'd never fully believed it. She'd told herself it was a curiosity, a tinker's trick, a story old men told to make their junk seem precious. But she'd carried it anyway. You carry things like that whether you believe in them or not, the way you carry a scar.

She opened the brass lid.

The needle was spinning. Not wavering, not hesitating between two truths — spinning, fast and frantic, a blur of silver, like something trapped and beating its wings against glass.

"That's new," Tam said, appearing at her elbow with the soundless way they had, all pickpocket's grace even after three days in a hole in the snow.

"It's broken."

"It's not broken." Tam tilted their head, watching the needle whirl. "It's *deciding*."

Coral held it out flat on her palm and waited. Around her the others gathered without seeming to gather — Odile turning from the ridge, Wren pulling her hood back, Bramble shuffling closer with his ruined hands. Even the mule, Hesper, stretched her neck over the snow wall and blew warm air across Coral's wrist, as if she too wanted to know what the little instrument would say.

The needle slowed. Wobbled. Swung wide in a great lazy arc, like a hawk circling before the stoop —

— and settled.

Not north. Not south. It pointed west-southwest, off the edge of everything, straight at a flank of rock and ice below the Serrated Tooth where no route existed, no pass, no mine, nothing but the blank space cartographers filled with hatching and the words *unexplored* or *inaccessible*, words that were really just polite ways of saying *we were afraid*.

"There's nothing there," Odile said quietly. "I've guided this range eleven years. There is no valley there. There's barely a *there* there — just the Teeth falling away into the White Waste. Nobody goes."

"The needle doesn't care about maps," Coral said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. She cleared her throat. "It never has."

Wren stepped closer. She'd been quiet since the cave — quieter than usual, which for Wren was saying something — and now she looked at the compass with an expression Coral couldn't read, grief and suspicion fighting for the same ground. "Elias told me about it once. When I was small. He said my grandmother's people made them, back in the coast towns. Compasses that pointed to what you'd lost." Her jaw tightened. "He said it like it was poetry. He said a lot of things like it was poetry."

"And what did it point to?" Tam asked. "For him?"

Wren's mouth opened. Closed. "I don't know. He left before I was old enough to ask."

The wind moved along the drift, lifting a veil of spindrift that caught the sun and burned briefly rainbow before it scattered. Coral stared at the needle. Steady now. Certain. Pointing at nothing, at blankness, at the one direction on the whole mountain that led away from Coldharbor, away from the Hollow Vale, away from the map sewn into a dead woman's shawl and the promise stitched into it.

Away from everything she'd sworn to do.

"It's pointing at the Vale," Bramble said hopefully. "Has to be. Old maps are wrong all the time. Maybe the Vale's further west than anyone thought."

"No," Odile said. "It's not pointing at the Vale. I've been watching her with that thing since the bridge." She met Coral's eyes, and there was no judgment in her face, which somehow made it worse. "Ask her what she lost."

Nobody asked. Nobody had to. The silence did the asking for them, and Coral stood in it with the compass open in her palm and felt the truth rise in her like water coming up through floorboards.

Her mother. Marisol Dunmore, who'd walked into the Serrano Range eleven years ago with a partner and a plan and a compass just like this one, and never walked out. Who'd left behind a daughter of nineteen who'd spent every year since pretending she wasn't waiting.

"She went into the range looking for the Vale," Coral said. The words came slowly, each one costing something. "Everyone knows that part. Storm-glass, fortune, the whole story. That's what she was chasing." She swallowed. "But she had a compass too. Hers pointed the same way these point. And I used to think — when I was younger and stupider — I used to think maybe hers was pointing at the treasure too. Maybe that's why she went. Maybe that's why she didn't come home."

Tam frowned. "But if it points at what you lost—"

"Then hers wasn't pointing at the Vale at all," Wren finished softly. She looked at Coral with something dawning and terrible in her face. "It was pointing at whatever *she'd* lost. Before she ever climbed. Something that pulled her up here. Or—" she stopped, and her voice dropped, "—or someone."

The wind gusted. Somewhere far below, a serac calved off a distant icefall with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"My mother's last camp," Coral heard herself say. "If the needle works the way Elias said — if it's honest — then it's pointing at the last place she was. Wherever she stopped. Whatever's left of her." She closed the lid with a snap that sounded obscenely loud. "Eleven years, and it's still pointing. Still holding."

Odile exhaled through her teeth. "That's not possible."

"You watched it spin."

"I watched a needle spin. Needles spin." But Odile's voice lacked conviction, and everyone heard it.

Bramble lowered himself carefully onto a snow block, wincing, his bandaged hands in his lap. "My grandmother had one of those stories too," he rumbled. "Back on the ferry. Sailors' tales — compasses that point home instead of north. She swore her own father's needle found his drowned brother's boat, forty years after. Everyone laughed at her." He shrugged his big shoulders. "She never laughed, though. Not about that."

"So we follow it," Tam said. They said it the way they said everything, quick and bright, already three steps ahead, eyes shining with the particular hunger Coral had learned to watch for — the hunger of someone who'd grown up with nothing and wanted *everything*, wanted it now, wanted it badly enough to climb into the blank spaces of maps. "We follow the needle. It's showing us the way. Maybe your mother found the Vale first. Maybe the real way in isn't on Elias's map at all. Maybe—"

"Stop." Coral's voice came out harder than she intended. Tam flinched, and she hated herself for it, and softened. "Just — stop. Think about what you're asking."

She turned and looked west, at the blankness the needle insisted on. The Serrated Tooth's western flank fell away in a chaos of ice cliffs and hanging glaciers, terrain that ate expeditions, terrain with no name because no one had come back to give it one. And beyond it, somewhere in that whiteness, either her mother's bones or her mother's ghost or nothing at all.

"Now think about what else we're carrying." She touched the satchel strap across her chest, where the shawl lay folded, the original map hidden in its seams. "A promise. To a dying man. Deliver the map to Wren before the passes close. Well — delivered. Done. Except the Syndicate has trackers on the mountain and Juno Pike has my contract, and the only thing standing between my crew and his smile is a route through the Vale that Silas Crane now has memorized. Every hour we spend chasing a needle is an hour they get closer to the extraction camp. Every mile west is a mile deeper into winter." She looked at each of them in turn. "Following that compass might mean none of us see Coldharbor again. It might mean the Syndicate takes the Vale while we're out here communing with ghosts. It might mean I lose the only family I've got left for the sake of one I already lost."

The words hung in the thin air. Tam looked down. Bramble studied his hands. Wren's face had gone very still, the stillness of someone holding something fragile and deciding whether to drop it.

"And yet," Odile said.

Coral turned on her. "And yet what?"

"And yet you haven't put the compass away." Odile nodded at Coral's hand. Sure enough, her thumb had found the brass lid again, worrying it open a crack, letting the needle show its stubborn silver line. "You've been holding it this whole speech. You've been holding it since the cave. So ask yourself the real question, Dunmore, and stop making speeches at us to avoid it."

"What's the real question?"

Odile's voice gentled, which from her was more frightening than shouting. "Whether you can walk past your mother's grave. Again. Knowing this time you'd be doing it on purpose."

* * *

They argued for an hour. Coral let them argue; she'd run out of arguments of her own.

Bramble was for the Vale — duty first, debt second, ghosts third, in that order, because order was how he'd survived sixty years on rivers. Tam was for the needle, passionately, recklessly, arguing that the map was compromised anyway, that Silas knew the route, that the compass might be the only advantage left. Wren said almost nothing, which frightened Coral more than any argument, until finally she spoke, standing apart with the wind pulling at her dark hair.

"Elias talked about you," she said. "In the end. When he was dying and thought I couldn't hear him through the wall." She didn't look at Coral. She looked west, at the blankness. "He said he'd found a woman with a compass that pointed wrong, and that she was going to die owing money to smiling men, and that he was sorry for what he was about to do to her. He said the map was a chain and he was putting it around her neck because he was a selfish old man and he couldn't carry it himself anymore." Now she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious. "So no. I'm not going to tell you what to do with your mother's grave, Coral Dunmore. My father spent his whole life telling people what to do with theirs. But I'll tell you what I know about promises." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You can keep them perfectly and still hate yourself. Or you can break them honestly and sleep at night. But you have to choose which kind of person you are *before* the choosing gets hard. After that, it's too late."

She walked away toward the mule, shoulders rigid, leaving Coral standing in the spindrift with the compass open in her hand and the needle steady as a heartbeat, pointing west, pointing home, pointing at whatever waited in the white.

* * *

They voted at midday, such as votes meant anything among five people and a mule. Bramble for the Vale. Tam for the needle. Odile abstained — *"I guide routes, not destinies"* — though Coral noticed she'd already re-packed her rope coil with the extra length climbers use for unknown ground. Wren wouldn't vote. Which left Coral, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, and the mountain waited with its terrible patience.

Coral climbed to the top of the drift alone and opened the compass one last time.

The needle held. West-southwest. Into the unnamed country. Toward a camp eleven years cold, where a woman who had once braided her daughter's hair by lamplight had lain down in the snow with a compass of her own, and Coral understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, what that compass must have been pointing at all along. Not the Vale. Never the Vale. A daughter, growing up in a port town, alive, unreachable, lost to her the moment she'd chosen the mountains over the road home.

Two needles. Two mothers' worth of losing. All of it pointing at the same patch of blank white.

"Oh, Mam," she said aloud, and the wind took the words and gave nothing back.

She closed the compass. Went down the drift. Found them all waiting — Tam bouncing on their heels, Bramble resigned, Odile already facing west, Wren with her arms crossed and her chin up, daring Coral to make it worse.

"We follow the needle," Coral said. "One day. Maybe two. If the ground turns impossible, we turn back and take Elias's route and I burn that decision out of my memory forever. If the Syndicate reaches the Vale because of this, that's on me, and I'll settle it with Juno Pike myself, and none of you will pay for my choice." She looked around at them, at their frostbitten faces and exhausted eyes, at the ragged improbable family she'd never asked for and could no longer imagine living without. "But if there's anything of her left up there — anything at all — I'm done walking past it."

Nobody cheered. That wasn't this crew's way. Tam just grinned and shouldered their pack. Bramble grunted and hauled himself up. Odile uncoiled her rope and started walking without a word, because guides lead with their feet.

Only Wren lingered. She fell into step beside Coral as they started down the western slope, boots crunching, and for a long time said nothing. Then:

"My father's compass," she said quietly. "When he died. Did it point anywhere? At the end?"

Coral thought of Elias in the waystation, feverish, clutching her hand, mistaking kindness for absolution. She thought of the map pressed into her palms like a confession.

"It pointed at you," she said. "I'd bet everything I have left. It always pointed at you."

Wren's breath caught — a small sound, quickly mastered. She walked on, head down, and after a hundred yards she said, so softly the wind nearly stole it: "Then let's hope yours is honest. Because if we're doing this, Coral Dunmore, we're doing it all the way."

Ahead of them the Serrated Tooth threw its long shadow across the unnamed snow, and beneath it, invisible, patient, the needle pointed on — toward a camp that had waited eleven years, and whatever else the mountain had kept safe for her, and whatever else it intended to take as the price of giving it back.
