## 16. Betrayal at the Saddle

Wren had learned to read her father's silences the way other children learned letters, and in the three days since the storm broke and they climbed out of the snow cave, she had been reading Bramble's.

He laughed too easily now. That was the first sign. The old ferryman had always been a man of opinions, grumbling like a river over stones, but lately his complaints had gone thin, decorative, a curtain hung over something. He checked the mule's packs too often. He walked at the rear of their line and let his eyes drift back along their trail the way a man checks whether he's been followed — or whether his message was received.

And every evening when they made camp, Bramble would wander off with some excuse — checking the mule's shoes, gathering wood that didn't need gathering — and come back smelling faintly of pipe smoke he hadn't smoked.

Wren told herself it was nothing. She had spent eleven years telling herself things were nothing: that her father's absence was nothing, that the shawl sewn with a dead man's map was nothing, that the ache she carried up this mountain for a mother whose journey she was only now finishing was nothing. She was an expert in nothing. Nothing was the family trade.

But on the morning they reached the saddle, she watched Bramble pause at the lip of the last rise, look down at the broad white shoulder of rock where Odile meant to cross, and mouth something to himself. Not a prayer. A count.

* * *

The saddle itself was beautiful, which Wren would remember afterward as one of the mountain's cruelest jokes. It curved between two peaks like the neck of a swan, wind-scoured to bare blue ice in places, drifted hip-deep in others, and above it the sky went up and up into a hard clean morning that made everything look possible. Even the cold felt honest here — not the wet treachery of the lowlands but a dry, ringing cold, the kind that made your blood feel like it mattered.

Odile led them across in the long diagonal she'd described the night before, planting each step with the deliberate grace of a woman writing her name in snow. "Stay in my track," she said, voice flattened by wind. "The cornice on the north side is rotten. If you hear it groan, you don't stop, you don't look, you *move*."

Tam, wrapped in Coral's spare coat until only their eyes showed, trotted obediently in Odile's prints. Coral came behind them with the map satchel strapped tight across her chest, and Wren brought up the rear beside Bramble and the mule, who had been named — absurdly, inevitably — Duchess, and who picked her way across the ice with more dignity than any of them.

It was Tam who heard it first. Wren saw the kid stop mid-stride, head cocking under the hood like a bird's, and then Tam turned and looked back down the way they'd come, and even at forty paces Wren could see the color drain from what little face was visible.

"Odile," Tam called, and the name cracked in the thin air. "Smoke."

There was no smoke. There was movement — figures in the rocks below the saddle's southern shoulder, where the slope dropped into a field of crevasses blue as veins. Six of them. Eight. Ropes snaking between them as they climbed out of the hollow where they must have waited through the night, patient as frost.

"Silas," Coral said. Wren heard it in her voice before she understood it — not surprise. Recognition. Something older and rawer than either.

"Down!" Odile was already moving, cutting diagonally toward a rib of rock that offered cover, her voice stripped to command. "Off the open ice — *now* —"

They ran. It wasn't running like the lowlands; it was a desperate, lunging scramble, boots skidding on wind-policed ice, breath tearing in throats. Wren clutched the shawl beneath her coat — her mother's shawl, Elias's map sewn into its weave — and felt it press against her heart like a second heartbeat. Behind her, Bramble shouted something about the mule, about Duchess, and his voice had a strange doubled quality, fear layered over something else, something almost like —

Like rehearsal.

They reached the rock rib and huddled behind it, chests heaving, steam pouring off them. Below and around, the crevasse field spread out like a shattered mirror, deep slots of glacial blue splitting the white. And coming up toward them, unhurried, roped and equipped and utterly certain of himself, came Silas Crane.

He stopped thirty feet away, close enough that Wren could see the frost in his beard and the tiredness around his eyes, and he did not draw a weapon. None of his people did. They simply arranged themselves across the slope with the easy competence of professionals, and that competence was worse than any blade.

"Coral." Silas's voice carried flat and clear across the ice. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," Coral said. Her hand had found the satchel strap. Wren watched her thumb move along it, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, like worrying a rosary.

"I don't want a fight on this slope," Silas said. "Nobody wants that. Give me the map, take my rope, walk down the east side with us. I'll get you to Coldharbor alive. The Syndicate gets their valley, you get your life. Everyone eats."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we take it," Silas said, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "Coral. I've known you ten years. Don't make me do this the ugly way."

Wren stepped forward before she knew she was moving. "It's not hers to give."

Silas glanced at her, mildly curious. "You're the daughter, then. Vane's girl." Something moved through his expression — pity, maybe, or its cheap cousin. "Your father sold that map twice, near enough. Once to her—" he tipped his chin at Coral "—and once, years ago, to men who paid better and asked fewer questions. You want to know what kind of man Elias Vane was? Ask the mountain. It remembers him."

"That's a lie," Wren said, but her voice wobbled on the ice of it, because she didn't know if it was a lie, because she had never known the man at all.

"Is it?" Silas shrugged. "Ask her. She's been lying to all of you since Bracken's Reach. Ask her why Juno Pike knows exactly where—"

"Bramble," Coral said quietly.

Not loudly. Just the name, spoken into the wind, and Wren felt the whole morning tilt.

She turned. Bramble stood apart from them, nearer to the mule, his big weathered hands resting on Duchess's pack straps, and he would not meet anyone's eyes. His jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice came out rough and low, the river-grumble gone, leaving only the stone underneath.

"Me family's in Meridian hands," he said. "Have been since Coldharbor. Since before." He swallowed. "Juniper Pike's people took my sister's deed. My nephew's apprenticeship papers. Said they'd burn 'em both if I didn't send word where you slept, what passes you aimed for." His eyes came up at last, wet and furious and ashamed, all at once. "I never thought it'd come to *this*. I thought they'd just... follow. I thought you'd lose 'em in the high country and no harm done. I thought—"

"You thought," Odile said, and her voice was quiet in a way Wren had never heard from her, quiet the way a glacier is quiet, "that you could sell us by inches and nobody would notice the bleeding."

"I sent word the cave would hold you through the storm," Bramble said desperately. "I told 'em to wait you out gentle. I never gave 'em the Vale. Never gave 'em the map. On my mother's grave, I never—"

"The map," Silas said, "is the only thing left to discuss."

And that was when the rocks started coming down.

Later, Wren could never reconstruct the order of it. One of Silas's climbers dislodged a serac or a stone, or someone fired a warning shot that struck the cornice above the southern shoulder — she heard the crack, sharp as a knuckle rapping a door — and then the cornice answered. It came off the ridge in a long white sigh, gathering weight, and the slope beneath their feet began to move with it, and the world became noise and tilt and the terrible soft roar of a mountain rearranging itself.

Wren remembered grabbing for the shawl. She remembered Tam screaming Coral's name. She remembered Odile's arm hooking her collar and hauling her sideways as the ground opened.

And she remembered — the last thing, the thing she would carry — Coral turning *toward* her, mouth shaping words the avalanche ate, satchel strap already half-unbuckled, as if she meant to throw the map clear, as if in the last instant she had chosen—

Then the white took everything.

* * *

Sound came back before sight: a high ringing tone, and under it, wind. Then cold, total and intimate, pressing on her eyelids like thumbs.

Wren dug. She didn't decide to dig; her body simply began, arms hauling in blind strokes, spitting snow, breath coming in ragged white bursts. Her left boot had lost its crampon. Somewhere below her, Duchess the mule was screaming, a sound Wren would hear for the rest of her life, and then — mercifully, horribly — not.

She broke surface into light so bright it hurt. The saddle was unrecognizable. Half the cornice was gone; the slope below had been scoured and repaved, and the crevasse field beyond had been churned into a wilderness of new bridges and broken blue jaws. Figures moved far upslope — Silas's people, digging, searching. Voices carried thin and distorted:

"—the satchel, find the satchel—"

"—man's alive, pinned, get him out—"

Bramble. Alive. Of course he was; the Syndicate protected its investments.

Wren got her bearings by terror alone. Tam — she spotted the kid fifty feet down-slope, half-buried, thralling free, hood torn back, black hair frosted, eyes huge and streaming. Odile was a dark shape higher up, rising out of the debris with blood sheeting from her scalp, moving with the grim economy of a woman cataloguing her own wounds for later.

And Coral — Coral was nowhere.

"Where is she?" Wren screamed, and Tam pointed, and Wren followed the finger down into the crevasse field, to a place where the snow had slumped into a long depression and, at its edge, a slot of blue had opened like a mouth.

She reached the lip and looked down into light that came up from below instead of above — glacial blue, luminous, forty feet of it falling away into darkness. And wedged on a shelf of ice fifteen feet down, sprawled against the wall, lay Coral Dunmore. The satchel was gone. Her right leg was bent wrong. Snow dust still sifted past her, settling on her like a shawl.

"Coral!" Her own voice sounded foreign. "Coral, can you hear me?"

No answer. Blood on the ice beneath her, shockingly red, a spill of color in all that blue.

Wren did not think about the rope. Later, Tam told her she'd used the coil off Duchess's wrecked pack, that she'd anchored it around a boulder of ice with a knot Odile would later call "a crime against the craft," and that none of it should have held. All Wren remembered was the burning slide of rope through her palms, the blue light rising around her, the cold reaching through her clothes like fingers made of meaning — *this, this is what you came for, this is the journey, not the valley, not the glass, this* — and then her boots hitting the shelf, and her hands finding Coral's shoulders, and Coral's eyes opening, unfocused, finding her face.

"Wren," Coral whispered. Blood at the corner of her mouth. "The map. Did I—"

"It's gone," Wren said. "Let it be gone."

"They have it." A sound came out of Coral that might have been a laugh, might have been a sob. "Eleven weeks of my life. My debt. My—" Her eyes tried to close. Wren shook her, hard.

"Look at me. Elias Vane sent me a father's promise sewn in wool, and I nearly burned it in Coldharbor because I hated him. You carried it up a mountain for a stranger. Whatever else happens—" Wren's voice broke, and she let it. "Whatever else happens, you don't get to die owing me a delivery. *Up.*"

Getting her up those fifteen feet took everything Wren had ever been given or stolen. Tam appeared at the lip like a small furious angel, anchoring the rope with their whole slight body, screaming encouragement in a voice that cracked on every word. Odile arrived at last, bloody and silent, and took Coral's weight without being asked, and the three of them hauled her onto the snow like fishermen landing something rare and half-dead.

Above them, Silas's expedition had stopped searching. Wren looked up the slope and met Silas Crane's eyes across two hundred feet of ruined ice. He had the satchel in one hand. He looked at Coral, laid out and bleeding on the snow, and something crossed his face that was not triumph.

He turned away. He gave some order Wren couldn't hear. And the Syndicate's climbers began to descend the far side of the saddle, taking the map, taking the route, taking eleven weeks of Coral Dunmore's life down into the Hollow Vale — and leaving the four of them alive on the snow, which was either mercy or arithmetic.

Bramble was gone with them. Wren saw him once, limping between two Syndicate men, looking back over his shoulder with a face like a man watching his own funeral from the outside. She found she could not hate him. That frightened her more than anything else that day.

Tam sat down heavily in the snow beside Coral and put one small mittened hand on her frozen one. "What do we do now?" the kid asked, and their voice was very young. "We've got nothing. No map. No food worth naming. No mule."

Wren looked at Coral. Coral's eyes had cleared somewhat, focused somewhere past all of them, on the crevasse field below — and then, slowly, on the coat pocket where she kept her compass. Her hand moved toward it, trembling, and stopped.

"No map," Wren agreed. She pulled the shawl from inside her coat, unwound it, and wrapped it around Coral's shoulders, tucking it close the way you'd wrap something living. "Just a needle that points to what's lost."

Coral's breath caught. For a long moment nobody spoke, and the wind moved over the saddle, erasing their tracks, erasing the morning, erasing everything except the five of them — four, now — and the mountain.

"Odile," Coral said hoarsely. "How far to the side valleys? The ones that aren't on any map?"

Odile stared at her. Then, slowly, she looked north, toward a confusion of unnamed ridgelines where the range went wild and unvisited, and Wren watched twenty years of a guide's discipline war openly with something older and hungrier in her face.

"Three days," Odile said. "If the weather holds. Which it won't."

"Then we'd better be quick," Coral said, and closed her hand around the compass through the wool, "before the needle changes its mind."

Far below them, half-buried in the fresh avalanche debris, Duchess's pack lay torn open, its contents scattered — and among them, catching the light, a single shard of something that glittered like frozen lightning, though none of them noticed it, and none of them would understand it until much later.

The wind rose. The mountain waited. And Wren Vane, kneeling in the snow with her hand pressed flat against Coral's bleeding side, felt the shawl's stitched seams shift against her fingers — and realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, that the stitching had been disturbed. Someone had unpicked it. Someone had read it. And someone, sometime between Coldharbor and this saddle, had sewn it back wrong.
