## 17. The Hollow Vale

The cold had stopped being an enemy somewhere in the last hour and become something more like a country — vast, indifferent, and slowly claiming her as a citizen.

Coral climbed out of the crevasse field the way she'd learned to climb everything: badly, stubbornly, one handhold at a time. Her left arm hung wrong at the shoulder where Wren had wrenched her out of the blue ice, and every breath came in like broken glass and went out like an apology. The sun sat low and white over the saddle behind her, and ahead the glacier spilled down between two black walls of rock, cracked into a thousand leaning teeth.

The compass lay open in her palm.

Its needle did not tremble anymore. All the wild spinning of the morning — the frantic, drunken circling that had made Tam shout and Odile swear and the whole sky seem to tilt — had settled into a single unwavering line, pointing off every map she'd ever seen, off the route, off the promise, off the edge of anything Elias Vane had drawn in his careful dying hand. It pointed down-glacier, into the uncharted throat of the range, toward a side valley that existed on no paper made by men.

It pointed at what she had lost.

Her mother had carried this compass. Her mother had vanished carrying this compass, twenty-two years ago, into these same mountains, and Coral had spent twenty-two years telling herself a story about it — that Maren Dunmore had been reckless, that she'd chased some prospector's rumor and died for it, that she'd chosen the mountain over her daughter. Coral had built her whole life on the foundation of that story. Storm-chasing, debt-collecting, promise-breaking Coral, who never stayed anywhere longer than a season and never loved anything she couldn't outrun.

The needle pointed at the truth, and the truth was still three miles of broken ice away.

She should have gone after the others. Wren was dragging her down-slope with a torn rope and blood on her knuckles; Bramble was weeping and confessing; Silas's crew was a day behind and Juno Pike was a day ahead, and somewhere below them all the Hollow Vale waited with its fortune in storm-glass, glowing like bottled lightning. Every practical voice in her head — and Coral's head was full of practical voices, they'd kept her alive this long — said *follow the map, follow the money, follow the plan.*

But the map was gone. Seized at the saddle, sewn now into the pack of a man who'd smiled at her across a hundred campfires while selling her position to the Syndicate. And the plan had died in the crevasse along with nearly everything else.

So there was only the needle.

"Damn you," she said to it, or to her mother, or to herself; the wind took the words before she could decide. Then she turned down-glacier and walked.

* * *

The crevasse field took the rest of the afternoon.

It was slow work, the kind of work that hollows a person out. She roped nothing — no one left to rope to — so instead she moved like the old guides moved, probing the snow bridges with her ice axe, listening with her whole body for the drum-hollow sound of thin ice over emptiness. Twice the bridge under her sagged and sighed, and twice she lay flat on her belly in the blue shadow and breathed until it stopped talking. The glacier groaned beneath her, deep and tectonic, a sound like a giant turning over in its sleep. Somewhere far below, meltwater ran through veins in the ice, and if she pressed her ear to the surface she could almost hear it speaking.

She thought about stopping. That was the honest truth of it. Around midday, in the white glare, her legs simply informed her that they were finished, and she sat down against a serac the size of a chapel and considered, quite calmly, lying there until the cold made the decision for her. Half-dead was not a metaphor. She could feel the exact border of it, sharp as a blade's edge, and she was standing on the wrong side of the line with frost in her lashes and her heartbeat going strange and slow, like a clock winding down.

Then she looked at the compass.

The needle hadn't moved. Not a hair's width, not a tremor, through all the hours and all the miles. It lay against its mark with the patience of something that had been waiting twenty-two years and could wait twenty-two more — though whether *she* could was another question entirely.

"You always were stubborn," she told it. Her voice came out cracked and small in the enormous silence. "I know where I get it from."

She got up. She walked.

* * *

Near dusk the glacier narrowed, pinched between walls of granite furred with rime, and the needle swung — gently, decisively — toward a cleft in the eastern wall that no map would ever have shown her because no mapper had ever come this far and lived to draw it.

The cleft was barely a body's width. She had to turn sideways, scraping her ruined shoulder against stone, pushing her pack ahead of her through a corridor of ice polished smooth by centuries of wind. And then the corridor opened, and Coral Dunmore stood at the edge of the Hollow Vale, and forgot, for one long moment, how to breathe.

It was a bowl of a valley, maybe half a mile across, cradled in peaks that leaned over it like elders around a cradle. The floor was a meadow gone silver with early snow, and rising out of the meadow, catching the last slanted light, were the storm-glass fields — thousands upon thousands of slender spires of translucent crystal, each one taller than a man, growing from the earth like frozen lightning. When the wind moved through them they rang. Not a clatter, not a chime — a *ring*, deep and resonant, a chord held so long it stopped being sound and became weather. The light entered them and broke apart and came out again in slow pulses of violet and gold, and the whole valley breathed with it, glowed and dimmed and glowed, like the chest of something sleeping.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it meant nothing to her at all.

That was the surprise. She'd expected to fall on it — the treasure, the debt-killer, the thing worth fingers and betrayal and Bramble's ruined honor. Instead she stood at the valley's mouth with tears freezing on her cheeks and felt only a vast, ringing emptiness, because the storm-glass was real but it wasn't the answer, and she had known since the saddle that it wouldn't be. The needle didn't point here. The needle pointed *through* here, deeper, toward the valley's far wall, where a dark seam of scree ran up toward a shelf of rock beneath an overhang of blue ice.

Toward a camp.

She saw the tent first — or what was left of it. Canvas gone to lace, ribs of bamboo poking up through the snow like the bones of some small beached creature. Then the fire ring, stones still stacked in a careful circle after two decades. Then, scattered across the shelf with terrible gentleness, the belongings: a tin cup. A coil of rope, rotted to red threads. A brass lantern, green with age. And a journal, bound in oilcloth, weighted under a stone.

And beside the fire ring, arranged with unmistakable deliberation — someone had done this, someone had laid her down and folded her hands and turned her face to the sky — a skeleton in the rotted remains of a blue climbing coat.

Coral did not remember crossing the meadow. She was simply there, on her knees in the snow, and the ring of storm-glass was tolling its endless chord above her, and her hands were shaking too hard to untie the oilcloth.

* * *

The journal's pages were soft as cloth with age, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood, but the handwriting was strong. She knew it. Of course she knew it. She'd grown up watching it march across grocery lists and birthday cards and margin notes in borrowed books — the confident forward slant, the way the letters leaned into the future.

*Maren Dunmore,* the first page said, in that hand. *Serrano Range, third expedition. If anyone finds this, I am looking for my daughter.*

Coral read it by the light of the low sun through the storm-glass, kneeling in the snow, and the valley rang around her, and she read.

The entries began formally — routes, elevations, weather — a professional's log, because her mother had been a professional before she'd been anything else. But the formality lasted four days. On the fifth, the entries changed.

*Dreamed of Coral again. She was seven, arguing with me about the proper way to tie a bowline, absolutely certain she was right, and she WAS right, God help me. I woke up laughing and then I woke up crying and then I got up and checked the compass and it hasn't moved in nine days. It points home. It has pointed home since the day I left.*

*I need to write down why I came, in case the cold takes my memory before it takes the rest. Everyone thinks I came for the glass. Even Elias thinks so — sweet fool, he drew me his maps and wished me luck and never asked the real question. But the compass doesn't point to treasure. It points to loss. Mine points to Coral. It has pointed at her since the hour she was born.*

*They tell me she's in Bracken's Reach. They tell me she chases storms now, of all the mad professions, and that she's good at it, and I don't know whether to be proud or terrified. Both. Always both.*

*I am going to find her. I know what everyone says — that the passes are closing, that a woman alone can't cross the Serranos in autumn, that I should wait for spring. But spring is five months and she is RIGHT THERE, at the end of a needle, and I have spent eleven years being patient. Patience is for people whose children are findable.*

The entries grew shorter as the pages turned. The dates grew later. The handwriting stayed strong — that was the thing that broke Coral, later, when she could feel things again: how strong the hand remained, right up to the end. No fading, no despair. Just shorter entries, because daylight was short, and cold fingers wrote slowly.

*Storm held us four days in the tents. Rations fine. Read Coral's last letter until I have it by heart. She writes angry. Good. Anger is fuel. I burned all mine getting here.*

*Crossed the high saddle today. The compass pulled east, off the route, into country no one maps. Followed it. Where else would I go?*

*Found the valley. The glass is everything they say — I could pay off a kingdom with one spire. I cut a single small piece, for Coral, because she'll laugh at me for bringing back a souvenir from the ends of the earth. It sits in my pocket and hums when I hold it near my heart.*

*But the needle doesn't stop here. It points through the vale, up the east wall. Whatever it wants me to find, it isn't money.*

The last entries were written in a sheltered crouch, the letters cramped, the ink thinning — she was melting snow for water to mix it, Coral realized. Melting snow with a dying fire to write to a daughter who might never come.

*Leg went through a snow bridge on the descent. Bad break. Set it as best I can. Fire is small. Food is small. The glass sings all night and it is the finest company I have ever kept, but I would trade every spire in this valley for one more argument about bowlines.*

*I am not afraid. I want that written plainly, in case the finding of this is hard on her. I am not afraid, and I do not regret one step, and if my girl is reading this then listen to me:*

*The compass was never broken, and neither are you. It doesn't point at what we've lost. It points at what we refuse to stop loving. I refused, all the way to the top of the world. Do you understand? Every mile, every storm, every frozen night — I was coming to YOU. The needle was pointing at you the whole time. I just ran out of mountain before I ran out of love, and there is no shame in that. There is no shame in any of it.*

*Be brave, little storm. Be kind, even to the ones who don't deserve it — especially to them. And when you find this place, take the glass if you want it, sell it, buy yourself a life with doors that lock. But keep the compass. And know that the last thing your mother saw was the needle pointing at you, steady as sunrise, and she went smiling.*

*— Maren Dunmore, who loved you past the edge of every map*

Beneath the signature, pressed flat between the final pages, was a small shard of storm-glass, violet-hearted, humming faintly against Coral's palm like a second pulse.

* * *

Coral did not know how long she knelt there. The sun went off the valley walls and the cold came down like a lid and she stayed, and the storm-glass rang its great slow chord, and somewhere in the middle of it she stopped being cold, which frightened her distantly, the way things frighten you in dreams.

She talked to the skeleton. She couldn't help it. Twenty-two years of words backed up behind her teeth and came out in a flood — about the storms she'd chased and the debts she'd owed and the people she'd collected along the way without meaning to, a pickpocket with quick hands and a guide with a grudge and an old ferryman who'd sold them out and wept doing it, and a girl named Wren who was walking through the dark right now carrying a dead man's promise because Coral had taught her, without meaning to, what promises were for.

"I thought you chose the mountain over me," she said, when the flood finally ebbed. Her voice was wrecked. "Twenty-two years, I thought that. And you were coming *home*. You were pointing at me the whole time." She laughed, and it came out as a sob, and the valley caught both sounds and rang them back transformed, made them part of the chord. "We're idiots, you and me. Stubborn as glaciers. Both of us."

She buried her mother properly. It took what strength she had left — scooping snow with bare hands, stacking stones from the fire ring, laying the blue coat straight and folding the skeleton's hands the way whoever had found her first, long ago, must once have done. Wait. No one had found her. The hands had been folded by Maren herself, in her last hours, arranging her own death with the same competence she'd brought to everything. Coral understood that too. She laid the journal inside the cairn, then took it back out. Some things weren't meant to stay buried.

The compass she held up to the last grey light.

The needle lay still. Not pointing north, not pointing at the vale, not pointing at anything at all — resting, at last, the way a dog rests at a grave, or a sentinel stands down after a war. Its work was finished. What Maren Dunmore had lost had been lost no longer.

"It's done," Coral whispered. "You can stop now. We're found."

And in the ringing silence of the Hollow Vale, with the storm-glass pulsing violet and gold around her like the heartbeat of the sleeping world, Coral Dunmore felt something let go in her chest — something she'd been carrying so long she'd mistaken it for a bone. Grief, maybe. Or the story she'd built her life on. Either way, it went, and what remained was lighter and stranger and harder to name.

She was still kneeling there when she heard the crunch of boots on snow behind her, and turned, too exhausted to reach for her axe, and saw a lantern-light bobbing down the meadow through the singing spires — and a voice she knew, hoarse with cold and fear, calling her name.

Wren. Coming for her, into the dark, off the map, following nothing but faith.

Coral started to laugh.
