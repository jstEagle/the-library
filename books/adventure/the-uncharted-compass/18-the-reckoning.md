## 18. The Reckoning

The compass led me down.

That was the strange mercy of it — after everything, after the crevasse field and the cold that had climbed into my bones like a tenant refusing to leave, the needle held steady. It didn't point to treasure. It had never pointed to treasure. It pointed to my mother's last camp, and now that I had stood in it, now that I had knelt in the snow beside a skeleton wrapped in a storm-worn coat and read a journal written in a hand I'd know anywhere, the needle simply rested. Pointing nowhere. Pointing at me, maybe. At whatever was left to find.

I left her there. I couldn't carry her out — no one could have carried anything out of that valley of ice and silence — but I built a cairn from the stones of her fire ring, and I took the journal, and I said the words I should have said eleven years ago on a dock in Bracken's Reach when she walked into the fog and I was too proud, too seventeen, too certain she'd come back, to say them.

*I'm sorry. Come home.*

The mountain kept her answer. But I felt lighter walking away than I had felt in years, and I knew what that lightness cost, and I paid it gladly.

* * *

I heard the Vale before I saw it.

Sound behaves strangely in high country. It travels along the ice the way rumor travels through a port town — finding every ear, changing shape as it goes. What reached me first was the ring of iron on stone: pitons, chisels, the industry of men who believed they had found something worth taking. Then voices, many voices, threaded with the thin whistle of wind off the glacier walls. And beneath it all, so low I might have imagined it, a hum — the storm-glass singing to itself in its fields, the way it does when weather is coming. The way it had sung, I realized, my whole life, from inside my mother's old sea chest, from inside the compass housing itself. I had grown up sleeping beside a lullaby I never learned to hear.

I came down the last shoulder of scree with the sun behind me, low and orange, throwing my shadow long across the blue ice of the valley floor. And there it was.

The Hollow Vale opened like a cupped hand. Sheer walls of granite and glacier rose on three sides, streaked with frozen waterfalls that caught the dying light and burned amber. And across the floor, in terraces where ancient floods had laid down their sediment in patient bands, grew the storm-glass.

I had heard about it my whole life the way sailors hear about mermaids — half proof, half wish. Nothing prepared me for the reality. The glass grew in clusters like frost-flowers, like coral, like the branching veins of lightning frozen mid-strike. Each shard was the size of a knife blade or a ship's spar, translucent and faintly violet, and each one hummed. Together they made a chord too low for human ears but not too low for human bones. I felt it in my teeth. In the scar tissue of my knuckles. In the place behind my sternum where dread usually lived and where, for one long breath, something else lived instead.

It was beautiful. It was worth more than my debt, more than the Syndicate, more than every ship and warehouse Juno Pike's cheerful masters owned between here and the sea. And it was going to destroy everyone standing in this valley if I didn't do something fast.

Because they were already here.

Two camps faced each other across the lowest terrace of glass, close enough to shout, separated by maybe two hundred yards of humming violet crystal. On the western side, under canvas and lantern-light: the Meridian Syndicate. I counted twenty men, maybe twenty-five, roped and equipped like a proper extraction crew, because that's what they were. The Syndicate didn't send collectors anymore; they sent miners. They had winches and sledges and crates stenciled with the Meridian sunburst, and they were already cutting. Even from the ridge I could see the shards stacked in their sledges, duller than the living glass, already dying once separated from the field. They didn't care. Dead glass still sold.

On the eastern side, smaller, meaner, camped among the rocks without tents: Silas Crane's expedition. Eight figures around a smokeless fire. Silas himself stood apart from his people, arms crossed, watching the Syndicate the way a man watches a dog he isn't sure is friendly. He'd been spared by me on the saddle, and he'd brought his crew down here anyway — whether following us or racing us, I couldn't tell. It didn't matter. Everyone in this valley wanted the same thing, and only one thing stood between them and getting it.

Me. And a dead man's map sewn into a dead woman's shawl, currently riding in Wren Vane's pack somewhere above me on the mountain, with Odile and Tam and Bramble — Bramble, who had sold us, who had wept while he did it, whose betrayal had put me in a crevasse and whose repentance had helped drag me out. My family, scattered across the high passes with a blizzard building in the west like a debt coming due.

And in the center of the valley floor, alone, unhurried, standing on the ice between the two camps as though he were waiting for a coach rather than presiding over a small war —

Juno Pike.

He saw me before I decided to show myself. Of course he did. He raised one arm in a wave so warm and unguarded you'd have thought we were old friends meeting by accident at a regatta, and he called out in that voice of his, bright as a brass bell:

"Coral Dunmore! There she is! Ladies and gentlemen — the woman of the hour!"

Every weapon in two camps came up. Every eye turned to the scree slope where I stood, backlit, exhausted, holding nothing but a compass that pointed nowhere and a journal full of a dead woman's love.

I walked down to meet him.

* * *

The ice sang under my boots. Up close, the storm-glass was even stranger — warm to the air somehow, breathing almost, each cluster flexing by fractions when the wind gusted, like sea anemones in a tide. I stepped carefully. Not because I feared breaking it. Because some animal part of me understood that this stuff was alive in a way I couldn't name, and I have never in my life been able to walk past a living thing without owing it something.

Juno met me halfway, hands spread wide, and I got my first good look at him in six weeks and saw that the mountain had been no kinder to him than to anyone. He was thinner. Frost-nipped at the cheeks. His immaculate coat was replaced by expedition wool, and there were new lines around his eyes that the smile couldn't quite plaster over. But the eyes themselves hadn't changed. That was the horror of Juno Pike — the eyes stayed cheerful no matter what happened behind them.

"Coral." He said my name like a toast. "You look terrible. You look fantastic. You look like a woman who has just come down from a place no one comes down from, which raises fascinating questions about what you found up there."

"You know what I found."

"I know what the rumors say you found." He tilted his head toward the eastern camp, where Silas watched us with an expression I couldn't read at this distance. "Silas says you refused his very generous offer. Silas is wounded, Coral. Silas talks about you the way other men talk about lost loves."

"Silas can go to hell."

"He's considering it. The rates are competitive." Juno laughed at his own joke, then let the laugh die cleanly, the way only he could — switching registers like a man turning a page. "Coral. Let's talk business."

"Let's."

He gestured, and we walked together along the edge of the glass field, our shadows stretching violet-tinted across the ice. Behind us, both camps pretended not to watch, which meant they watched every second.

"Here's where we are," Juno said pleasantly. "My employers dispatched an extraction team the moment your debt was attached to Elias Vane's map. They are thorough people. They don't gamble. When they invest in a rumor, they send enough men to make the rumor true." He nodded at the sledges of cut glass. "As you can see, the investment is performing."

"You're killing it," I said. "The glass. Cut from the field, it dies. Look at it. Your sledges are full of corpses."

Juno glanced at the nearest sledge, at the dulled shards, and his smile didn't flicker. "Dead pearls still sell, Coral. So do dead forests and dead whales. My employers are not sentimental about the mortality of inventory." He stopped walking and turned to face me fully, and for just a moment — a heartbeat, no more — the cheerfulness dropped and I saw the actual creature underneath: tired, cold, far from home, doing a job. Then the smile came back up like a shopkeeper's shutter. "But here's my problem. Here's why I'm delighted to see you. The field is bigger than the maps suggested. Much bigger. The main vein runs deep under the north wall, and my surveyors say the extraction will take a season. A season, Coral, at altitude, in Serrano weather, with a rival crew camped two hundred yards away and a blizzard coming that my own guides are whispering about. My employers want certainty. And you—" he tapped my compass where it hung at my side, and the needle, dead these hours, gave the faintest twitch against its housing, "—you are the only person in this valley who has been where this compass has been. So. Business."

"The crew," I said.

"Ah."

"The trade. You want the Vale's location — the true route, the deep vein, whatever my mother's notes add to Elias's map. Fine. Here's my price. Every debt erased. Mine, and any attached to my people. Odile Marchand's name cleared with the Meridian ledger — you destroyed her business, you'll restore it or pay it. Tam walks free, no collection orders, ever. Bramble's family protected — and yes, I know about Bramble, and yes, I'm including him anyway, because that's how this works. And Wren Vane is nobody's asset. Nobody follows her. Nobody watches her. She's finished with the Syndicate before she knows she started."

Juno listened with his head cocked, attentive as a priest at confession.

"And Silas?" he asked.

"Silas made his choices."

"That's remarkably cold, Coral. I'm proud of you." He looked out over the glass field, and the low sun turned his face to copper. "You understand what you're offering. The location of the richest strike in a century. For four names in a ledger."

"Five names," I said. "Yours."

His eyebrows rose — genuine surprise, the first I'd ever seen on him.

"You're done after this," I said. "Whatever you're running from, whatever makes a man smile like that in a job like yours — you're done. You walk out of this valley with us, and you don't come back. That's part of the price. I'm not leaving you behind me on this mountain with a grudge and a radio."

For a long moment Juno Pike said nothing. The glass hummed. Somewhere above us, thunder muttered in the building cloud, and the whole field answered it, a thousand crystal throats humming in sympathy, and I watched the enforcer of the Meridian Syndicate stand in a valley of singing glass and think about being free.

"You're serious," he said finally.

"I'm always serious. Ask anyone I've ruined."

He laughed — really laughed, helplessly, doubling over, and when he straightened there were tears freezing at the corners of his eyes. "Oh, Coral. Oh, you magnificent idiot. Yes. Yes, done, agreed, sold — you have yourself a deal." He stuck out his hand, and I took it, and his grip was warm and firm and utterly without malice, which was the most frightening thing about it. "Every debt erased. The crew freed. Odile restored. The boy released. The ferryman's family protected. The girl untouched. And Juno Pike retired to a life of honest obscurity." He shook my hand once, twice, briskly, sealing it. "In exchange for the location of the deep vein and your mother's notes. Delivered now."

"Now," I agreed. "After you call your camps off. Weapons down. Both sides stand down while I write out the route."

"Of course, of course." He turned toward his camp, lifting his voice into that carrying bell-tone. "Gentlemen! Stand down! We've reached an arrangement—"

And that was when the trap sprang shut.

I heard it before I understood it: the click of a mechanism, soft as a knuckle cracking, somewhere behind my right ear. Then Juno's hand — the warm, honest, friendly hand — closed on my wrist, and his voice dropped its brightness all at once, like a mask falling, and what was underneath was flat and tired and terribly sad.

"I'm sorry, Coral. I truly am. You have no idea how much I wanted to say yes."

* * *

They came out of the ice.

That's the only way I can describe it — they'd been there all along, dug into the glacier wall along the valley's edge, canvas-draped and invisible, a dozen of them, and they rose out of the white like the mountain itself deciding to grow men. Rifles first. Then faces. Two of them took my arms with professional gentleness, which was worse than violence, and someone relieved me of the compass with a delicacy that made my skin crawl, and someone else went through my coat and found my mother's journal and carried it to Juno like a deacon bearing a relic.

Across the field, Silas Crane's crew surged to their feet — and stopped. Because behind them, from the rocks on the eastern side, another dozen shapes rose, armed, wearing Meridian colors, and I understood with a sick lurch that the standoff I'd walked into had never been a standoff at all. It had been theater. Two companies, one employer, and the whole tableau staged to draw out whoever came down the mountain holding the true route. To draw out me.

Silas stared at the Syndicate men behind his own camp, and I watched him do the arithmetic, and I watched him arrive at the answer, and his face went gray. He'd been played too. Whatever deal he thought he had — beat the Syndicate to the Vale, keep the prize for himself — it had died the moment his usefulness ended. His usefulness had ended the moment I walked into the valley.

"Juno," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You gave your word."

"I know." He held my mother's journal in both hands, and he wouldn't look at it, and he wouldn't look at me. He looked at the glass field instead, at the humming violet forest of it, and his voice when it came was quiet enough that only I could hear it. "That's the trick of it, Coral. That's the whole trick. Everybody believes my word because I've never broken it. Twenty years. Not once. Do you know what that's worth to men like my employers? A man whose word is iron is a man you can build anything on top of." He finally turned to me, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling, and God help me it wasn't even a cruel smile. It was apologetic. "So when I break it, it has to be for something enormous. And you—" he lifted the journal slightly, "—you just made it enormous."

"My mother's notes mean nothing without me. The deep vein runs under the north wall — you need a guide who's read those pages, who understands them. Kill me and you've got a book in a language you can't speak."

"Oh, I'm not going to kill you." He sounded genuinely shocked. "Coral. Please. I told you — I wanted to say yes. I still want to say yes. We're going to wait out this blizzard together, you and I, and in the morning, warm and fed and reasonable, you're going to read those pages to my surveyors. And your crew—" he glanced up at the darkening sky, at the west, where the cloud bank had swallowed the last of the sun and was now swallowing the ridgelines one by one, "—your crew is going to freeze somewhere on the Ghost Stair, or they're going to come down here looking for you, in which case we'll have a lovely reunion. Either way, the Meridian closes its books."

The first flakes came down while he was still talking. Big, slow, deliberate, like the mountain thinking.

They bound my wrists in front of me — considerate, even now — and walked me toward the Syndicate camp, past the sledges of dying glass, past men who wouldn't meet my eyes. Juno walked beside me the whole way, chatting, actually chatting, about the weather and the quality of the camp's coffee and whether I'd eaten, and the awful thing, the thing I would turn over for years afterward, was that none of it was performance. He meant every word of kindness. He simply meant the betrayal more.

At the edge of camp he paused, and nodded up-valley, toward the north wall, toward the deep vein sleeping under a hundred feet of glacier.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope you decide to cooperate. I hope it sincerely. Because my surveyors flagged something today that I haven't reported yet." He leaned close, and the snow gathered in his smiling mouth as he spoke. "This whole valley is loaded. The slope above the north vein — the cornice, the wind-slab, thirty years of accumulation sitting on a forty-degree bed. One hard shake and the Hollow Vale becomes a grave. My people say it's stable. My people are being paid to say it's stable." He straightened up. "You're a storm chaser, Coral. You know mountains. Sleep on that."

They put me in a supply tent with a guard outside and a stove ticking warmth into the dark, and through the canvas I watched the blizzard arrive like a verdict.

Somewhere up on the Ghost Stair, my family was walking into it — Odile reading the weather the way she read rock, Tam's sharp eyes, Wren's stubborn heart, Bramble's mule and Bramble's guilt. They'd come for me. Of course they'd come for me. That was the whole disease of us: we kept choosing each other at exactly the moments it would cost the most.

And Juno Pike had just handed me the shape of the only way out, smiling as he did it, trusting me to be smart enough to hear it.

The slope above the north vein was a loaded avalanche.

The Syndicate was camped directly beneath it.

I sat in the ticking warmth of the stove light, wrists raw, mother's journal gone, compass gone, promise delivered and betrayed, and outside the canvas the storm began to sing — and beneath the wind, beneath everything, the storm-glass of the Hollow Vale hummed its low chord, louder now, answering the pressure drop, answering the coming weight of snow.

It didn't sound like treasure anymore.

It sounded like a fuse.
