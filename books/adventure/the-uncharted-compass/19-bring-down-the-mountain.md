## 19. Bring Down the Mountain

Odile had spent thirty years reading mountains, and this one was screaming.

She stood at the lip of the Hollow Vale with the wind pulling at her coat and felt the truth of it in her teeth, in her knees, in the old break in her ankle that ached before every disaster. The slope above the valley — a great white shoulder of corniced snow, eight hundred feet of it, hanging over the extraction camp like a held breath — was wrong. She'd known it since dawn. The snowpack had that dense, glassy look, layers upon layers of storm laid down without bonding, and the week's warmth had glazed the rock beneath it into something slick as river ice. Any guide worth her rope would have looked at that slope and refused the route.

The Syndicate's crews were not guides. They were miners, thugs, and hired hands, forty of them swarming the valley floor below her, and they were cutting the storm-glass out of the earth with picks and pry bars, loading sledges, hammering pitons into the very slope that would kill them.

*Loaded,* Odile thought. *The whole mountain is loaded.*

Below, the Vale spread out like something from a fever dream. Storm-glass grew here in veins — great translucent shards thrust up through the scree, blue-green and glowing faintly even under the grey sky, humming when the wind crossed them. She could hear it from here, a low singing note, like wet fingers on a wineglass rim. Men were prying those shards loose and stacking them on canvas sledges, and every shard they pulled left a wound in the mountainside, a dark socket weeping meltwater.

Coral crouched beside her behind a ridge of wind-carved snow, glass dust still crusted in her hair from the crevasse field, her face gaunt and frost-nipped and absolutely steady. Wren knelt at her other side. Tam pressed close against Odile's shoulder, small and shaking — not from cold, Odile thought, or not only. And down at the treeline, where he'd been made to wait with the mule, Bramble stood in the open, hands raised, shouting something the wind shredded to pieces.

"He's telling them we're coming," Tam said. "He's telling them everything."

"No," Coral said quietly. "Look at his feet."

Odile looked. Bramble was planted like a fence post, and though his arms waved and his mouth worked, he wasn't moving toward the camp. He was standing *between* the Syndicate men and the treeline — between them and the mule, and the escape route south, and the four of them watching from above. A fat old ferryman with frostbitten fingers, drawing a line in the snow with his body.

"He's buying us time," Coral said. "However much of it he can."

"And how much is that?" Wren asked.

Odile didn't answer. She was doing the arithmetic she'd been doing since Coldharbor, since the Ghost Stair, since she first saw this valley and felt her ankle sing its warning song. Forty men below. Juno Pike's voice carrying up the slope, bright and cheerful, directing the work like a man conducting a choir. Silas Crane's crew camped apart, near the eastern wall, their gear already half-packed — she'd watched them through Tam's stolen spyglass all morning, watched Silas stand at the edge of the glass fields and look up at the loaded slope with an expression she recognized, because she'd worn it herself for thirty years.

Silas knew. Silas was a mountaineer, whatever else he was. He could feel the mountain holding its breath same as she could.

Which meant there might be one person down there who would listen.

"Coral," she said. "I need you to hear me, and I need you to believe me, because I'm only going to say this once." She turned, took Coral by the collar of her coat, and made the younger woman look at her. "That slope is going to come down. Today, tomorrow, next week — but if they keep cutting, if they keep hammering pitons into it, it comes down *today*, and it takes everyone in this valley with it. I've seen slides like what this will be exactly twice in my life. There is nothing left afterward. Not camps. Not sledges. Not men."

Coral's eyes went to the glass fields, to the men swarming them, then back. "You're telling me we can just wait."

"I'm telling me we can just wait." Odile let her go. "But you're not going to want to wait. Are you."

Down in the valley, Juno Pike had stopped directing and started walking — up the slope toward the treeline, unhurried, cheerful, his hands in his pockets, two Syndicate men trailing him with ropes and knives. Toward Bramble.

Coral stood up.

"Coral—" Wren started.

"He'll talk him around," Coral said. "Juno talks everyone around. That's the whole point of Juno." But her voice cracked on the last word, and Odile saw it — saw the exact moment the plan she hadn't yet spoken aloud became the only plan there was.

"Then tell me," Odile said.

Coral told her.

* * *

It was a terrible plan. Odile wanted that recorded somewhere, carved into stone, read aloud at her funeral if anyone ever found enough of her to bury. It was a terrible plan and it was the only plan, and so they did what climbers have always done with terrible plans: they broke it into steps, and they climbed.

Step one was Silas.

They came down the eastern gully single file, Odile leading because no one else could read the wind slab, Wren second because she refused to be anywhere but where the fighting would start, Coral third, Tam last with the mule left hobbled above. The storm-glass sang louder as they descended, that eerie chord rising off the fields, and Odile felt it in her fillings, her bones, the scar tissue over her ribs. The whole valley hummed like a struck bell.

Silas Crane stood alone at the edge of his camp, and he was looking up at the slope. Of course he was. When Odile crunched to a stop ten feet from him, he didn't reach for a weapon. He just said, tiredly:

"You're going to tell me what I already know."

"The slope's a bomb," Odile said. "Your employer is lighting the fuse with a pickaxe. You've got maybe two hours before it goes on its own, and when it goes, it buries everything from the glass fields to the moraine. Everyone."

"I told Pike." Silas's jaw worked. "This morning. He laughed at me. Said the mountain wouldn't dare." His laugh was hollow. "Forty years I've climbed things, Odile. I know what a mountain dares."

"Then get your people out."

"And go back to Meridian with nothing? They don't forgive nothing. You know what they do to—"

"I know *exactly* what they do." Her voice came out flat and hard, thirty years of ruin in it. "They burned my business. They put my name on a list. I am standing in a valley full of their money telling you the mountain is about to erase their claim, and you are worried about *forgiveness*?"

Silas looked past her then, at Coral. Something passed between them that Odile deliberately did not watch — old history, old wounds, the kind of thing that doesn't heal so much as scar over into usefulness. Whatever Coral said to him, she said it low, and whatever it was, Silas closed his eyes for a long moment.

When he opened them, he said: "My crew walks east. Now. We take nothing."

"Now," Coral agreed.

"And Pike?"

Coral's silence answered for her.

Silas whistled up his people. To their credit — to whatever was left in them of the climbers they'd been before the Syndicate bought them — they moved fast. Within minutes the eastern camp was collapsing into packs, and Silas Crane's expedition filed out of the Hollow Vale without a single shard of storm-glass, leaving their sledges standing empty in the snow like abandoned coffins.

At the edge of the gully Silas paused. He looked back once, at Coral, and said something too quiet for Odile to catch. Then he was gone, and step one was done.

* * *

Step two was Juno Pike, and nobody had a plan for Juno Pike except Coral, and Coral's plan was to walk down into the valley and hand him the thing he wanted most.

Odile watched from the rocks above with Wren and Tam, and she would remember the shape of it forever: Coral Dunmore descending into the enemy camp with her hands open and empty, snow swirling, the storm-glass singing around her, and Juno Pike turning to greet her with that smile — that awful, warm, delighted smile, the smile of a man who has never once doubted that the world exists to please him.

"Coral Dunmore!" he called, spreading his arms. "There she is! We were *worried sick*. Well — some of us were. Some of us were mostly curious whether the crevasse field finished you." He clicked his tongue. "And here you are, walking right up to me. Do you know why I like you, Coral? Because you always, always make the sensible choice. Eventually."

"I brought you the Vale," Coral said, loud enough for every man in the camp to hear. "Every approach, every vein, the whole of it. It's yours. In exchange: my contract burns, my crew walks free, and these men stop cutting that slope before it kills us all."

Juno's smile widened. And Odile, watching, felt the trap spring shut in real time — because she saw the flicker in his eyes, the little gleam of appetite, and she knew what Coral knew and had hoped against hope she was wrong about: Juno Pike had never intended to honor any trade. He'd simply waited to see if Coral would offer, and now that she had, the map's location was his regardless of what happened next.

"Sensible," Juno said warmly. "Sensible to the last. Yes, Coral. Done. Every debt erased, your people free, and we'll all drink to it in Bracken's Reach come spring." He snapped his fingers at a man near the sledges. "Get her a slate. Let her draw it out properly—"

"She draws nothing," Odile said, stepping out of the rocks, "until your pickaxes leave that slope."

Juno turned his smile upward, unhurried. "Ah. The disgraced guide. Still lecturing, I see. No wonder the business failed."

Something in Odile went very quiet and very cold, the way it did before the worst decisions of her life.

"Mr. Pike," she said, "I have guided this range for thirty years. I am telling you, as a professional courtesy, that the slope above your head is unstable, that your pitons are acting as levers, that the temperature is dropping and the wind is backing around to the north, and that every hour you keep cutting, you are gambling forty lives against a fortune you have not yet dug out of the ground."

"And I am telling *you*," Juno said pleasantly, "that the fortune leaves this valley today, and the only question is whether you're standing under my slope when it does." He gestured, cheerfully, at the crews. "Back to work, gentlemen! Glass doesn't cut itself!"

The picks resumed. The singing of the storm-glass rose to a wail.

Above them, high on the shoulder of the slope, a cornice the size of a chapel shifted with a sound like a sighing giant, and shed a ribbon of powder that fell three hundred feet and stopped, harmlessly, a hundred yards above the nearest sledge.

Everyone in the valley heard it. Everyone in the valley saw it.

Juno Pike smiled and clapped his hands like a man applauding fireworks. "There's our mountain saying hello!" he called out. "Keep cutting!"

Coral backed away from him, slowly, up the slope, and Odile met her eyes across the distance, and gave her the smallest nod.

Step three.

* * *

They gathered at the base of the loaded slope itself — the four of them, plus one penitent ferryman who arrived at a run with tears frozen in his beard and no explanation anyone wanted to hear yet. Above them, eight hundred feet of wind slab sat on glassy ice, stitched through with Syndicate pitons, groaning under its own weight. Below them, forty men cut the heart out of the valley.

"There's a weakness," Odile said, pointing with her axe. "There. Where the cornice overhangs. One load, placed right, at the fracture line, and the whole shoulder lets go. It'll run the full fall line, take the camp, the sledges, the piton scars — everything. Nothing above the moraine survives."

Tam stared up at the slope. "And us?"

"We're standing at the flank. The slide runs center-left. We stay tight to the rock wall here, we're outside the track." Odile looked at each of them in turn. "Probably."

"*Probably*," Wren repeated.

"That's the honest number. I won't lie to you on a mountain. Anyone who wants to walk east with Silas's crew, walk now, and no one will think less of you."

Nobody moved. Bramble wiped his face with a mittened hand. "I've run from enough," he said hoarsely. "Not this one."

"Then here's how it happens." Odile laid it out with her hands, quick and precise, guide's shorthand. "We climb the flank to the release point. It's forty minutes' work, exposed, and if any of Pike's spotters see us, we're dead on the rock. At the fracture line we set one charge — Tam, you still have the blasting oil from the bridge cache?" Tam patted their coat and nodded. "One charge, tamped in deep, lit long. We light it and we *run*, down the flank, to the wall, and we pray to whatever gods run avalanche paths that the flank holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Wren asked.

"Then we find out together," Odile said, "what the Vale sounds like from underneath."

Coral looked up at the slope a long moment. Then she looked down at the camp — at Juno Pike, tiny now with distance, directing his choir — and Odile watched her arrive at the place the rest of them had already reached.

"He offered me a clean exit this morning," Coral said quietly. "Silas did. Walk away, debt gone, no one hurt. And I almost took it." She flexed her hands. "But there's no clean exit through that man. There never was. So we bring down the mountain."

They climbed.

* * *

Forty minutes on the flank of a bomb. Odile had done harder climbing, but never while listening to the mountain argue with itself beneath her axes. The snowpack ticked and settled. Somewhere deep below, a layer sheared with a sound like tearing linen. Twice the slope shuddered and shed small slides that hissed past below their boots, and twice Odile pressed them flat against the rock and counted heartbeats until it stilled.

Tam set the charge at the fracture line with hands that shook only a little, tamping the blasting oil into the cornice's root the way Odile talked them through it, word by word, breath by breath. Wren held the fuse. Coral watched the valley. Bramble prayed, loudly, to a frankly eclectic list of deities.

"Light it," Odile said.

Wren struck the match. Wind took the first; Wren cursed and struck the second, cupped it, touched it home. The fuse hissed to life, a thread of fire crawling into the snowpack.

"RUN."

They ran. Down the flank, axes useless, boots skidding, the world reduced to white and the roar of their own blood — and behind them, above them, a *crack* like the sky splitting, deep and enormous, the sound Odile had heard only twice before and would hear in her dreams for the rest of her life.

The mountain let go.

Eight hundred feet of snowpack detached as one living thing. The sound was not an explosion; it was a *voice* — a bass note so vast it wasn't heard so much as felt, in the sternum, in the bowels, in the roots of the teeth. The cornice folded, the fracture line ran, and the whole white shoulder of the mountain poured down into the Hollow Vale like a flood given teeth.

Odile hit the rock wall and flattened herself against it and held on to Tam, and the world went white and deafening. Snow blasted past them in a horizontal river, thick enough to choke on, cold enough to burn, and through it she heard — distantly, terribly — the singing of the storm-glass rise to a shriek as the slide buried the fields, the camp, the sledges, the pitons, the picks, the men.

It lasted perhaps ninety seconds. It felt like a year.

Then it was over, and the silence that followed was the deepest Odile had ever known. The valley floor had vanished. Where the extraction camp had been there was only smooth white, unbroken, sculptural, beautiful in the way that graves are beautiful. The storm-glass was silent. The wind, backing around from the north, began to sift fresh powder across the whole wound of it, softening the edges, erasing the evidence, patient as anything.

From the rocks, small and human, Tam began to cry.

* * *

They found no bodies. That was the mercy and the horror of it both — the slide had taken everything so completely that there was nothing to find, no wreckage, no camp, no claim. Just a valley wearing a new face. Bramble walked the moraine edge calling names until his voice gave out, and got back nothing but echo.

Except.

Near the eastern wall, half-buried but upright, they found a sledge — Silas's, abandoned, and beside it a figure digging with bare hands. Silas Crane had gotten his people out barely ahead of the fall, and he had come back anyway, back into the settling debris, digging for a man he owed nothing to.

He'd found Juno Pike.

Or rather, the slide had delivered Juno to him — spat out at the toe of the debris flow, half-buried, crushed in ways Odile didn't look at closely, and still, impossibly, alive. Still smiling. Blood on his teeth and that warm, delighted grin fixed on his face like it had been bolted there.

"Well," Juno said, when they gathered around him. His voice was a wet whisper, and he used it cheerfully. "Well, well. Look at this. All my debts... collected at once." He coughed, laughed, coughed again. "Coral Dunmore. You know what the funny thing is? I'd have paid you. In the end. I always pay. Ask anyone."

"No one's asking," Coral said.

"The mountain..." His eyes wandered up the slope, and there was something in them that wasn't fear, wasn't pain — something almost like admiration. "The mountain doesn't negotiate. Isn't that something. Thirty years in the business and I never..." The smile flickered, steadied. "Tell Meridian — tell them the Vale keeps its own books."

His hand found Coral's sleeve, gripped it with the last of his strength, and Odile braced herself for a threat, a curse, a name.

"Good climb," Juno Pike whispered, and died smiling, exactly as he had lived.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Then Silas Crane stood up, brushed the snow from his knees, and looked at Coral — at all of them — with a face stripped down to the bedrock of the man.

"I owe you," he said. "All of you. I don't know what I'm going to do with that."

"Winter's coming," Odile said. "Figure it out somewhere warm."

Silas nodded slowly. He shouldered his pack, took one last look at the buried Vale — at the storm-glass silent under its shroud of snow, keeping its secrets — and walked east, alone, a changed man heading into a changed life.

Coral watched him go. Then she turned to the rest of them — Wren with Elias's shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders, Tam with tear tracks frozen on their cheeks, Bramble with his ruined hands tucked under his arms — and Odile saw her count them, the way you count your party at the end of a route, making sure everyone who went up came down.

Four. All four. Against every honest odd.

"It's snowing for real now," Odile said, nodding at the sky, which had begun delivering the season in earnest, fat flakes falling straight and silent. "Passes close within days. Maybe hours. If we're going to walk out of this range, we walk out *now*."

"What about the map?" Tam asked. "What about the Vale?"

Coral looked at the buried valley a long moment. When she answered, her voice was quiet and certain, and Odile understood that whatever remained of the journey had already been decided in Coral's mind, somewhere between the crevasse field and the grave.

"The map's got one stop left," she said. "A fire."

Behind them, the mountain went on sifting snow over the Hollow Vale, gentle and thorough, tucking its treasure in for a sleep that would outlast them all.
