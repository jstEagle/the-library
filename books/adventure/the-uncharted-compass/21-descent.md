## 21. Descent

The snow came down like a decision already made.

Tam had never trusted weather that felt calm. In Bracken's Reach, the quiet hours were when the knives came out — when the pickpockets worked the fish market and the Syndicate's smiling men walked the docks with their ledgers and their pleasant voices. Quiet meant someone was about to take something from you.

But this quiet was different. This was the mountain's quiet, vast and empty and utterly without appetite, and Tam walked down into it with a mule's lead rope in one hand and Bramble's arm over their shoulders, and for the first time in their life they weren't watching the exits.

They noticed it somewhere past the second switchback of the Ghost Stair, on the morning after the burning. The map had gone into the fire at dusk — Coral striking the match, Wren holding her mother's shawl closed around nothing now but warmth, Odile standing witness with her arms folded like a woman burying a flag. The paper had caught fast, curled black, and the wind had taken the ashes up the valley in a long grey ribbon, and Tam had watched them go and felt — not grief, exactly. Something more like a door closing gently in a house they'd only just realized they lived in.

And then this morning, trudging down through the fresh fall with Bramble's weight dragging at their shoulder, it came to them all at once, so plainly they almost laughed: *I haven't checked my pockets.*

Not once since Coldharbor. Not once since the cave, since the crevasse field, since the Vale. Their fingers, which had spent eleven years learning every seam and lining they'd ever worn, which counted coins by touch in their sleep, which mapped exits the way other children learned prayers — those fingers had gone quiet. Somewhere between the whiteout and the avalanche, between stealing a compass and carrying a dying man's weight down a mountain, Tam had stopped being a thief.

Or maybe not stopped. Maybe just — retired. The way old ferryman retire. The way storms do.

"You're grinning," Bramble rasped beside them. His voice was a ruin these days, scraped thin by the cold and the cough he'd brought down off the mountain along with his frostbitten hands, wrapped now in strips of Wren's shawl-wool and Odile's spare scarf. "Nobody grins on the Ghost Stair. It's bad luck."

"Everything's bad luck," Tam said. "You told me that yourself, day one. You said mules can smell bad luck and I was carrying enough of it to draw every mule in the Serranos."

"And did I lie?" Bramble wheezed something that might have been a laugh. His boots dragged twin furrows in the snow. "Where's that beast got to?"

Ahead, Hesper the mule picked her way down the stair with the delicate outrage of a creature who had been asked to endure entirely too much. She'd carried Bramble's pack since the saddle, and half his blankets, and she'd complained about it continuously in the language of flattened ears and theatrical sighs, but she hadn't once refused. Tam had decided sometime during the third day of the storm that Hesper understood more about family than most people did: you carried what needed carrying, and you complained the whole time, and you didn't leave.

"She's ahead of us," Tam said. "She's always ahead of us. She's the only one of us with any sense."

"Sense is overrated." Bramble's breath plumed and tore away. "Look where mine got me."

"Alive," Tam said. "It got you alive, and down the mountain, and home." They heard their own voice go strange on the last word and covered it with a shrug. "More than Juno Pike can say."

Bramble was quiet for a few steps. The snow squeaked under their boots, that dry cold squeak that meant the killing frost was close behind them, riding their heels down the range like a debt collector.

"I need you to know," Bramble said finally, and his voice had lost its wheeze, gone low and level, "that I've been doing sums. All night, every night, since the saddle. And there's no sum that makes me square. Whatever I tell myself — my girls, my debts, the men who came to my door with their smiles — none of it changes what I did. I sold you. All of you. For a promise they were never going to keep."

"I know," Tam said.

"I sold *you*," Bramble said, harder, as if Tam hadn't understood, as if the word needed driving in like a piton. "A child alone on a mountain, and I handed the map of your footsteps to men with ropes."

"I know," Tam said again. And then, because it was true and because they were tired of carrying things they didn't have to carry: "My mother sold me too. When I was six. To a crew boss on the Reach waterfront, for two crowns and a bottle. So I know how it works, Bramble. I know you can love somebody and sell them in the same hour. I know the sums don't come out clean no matter how you run them." They adjusted the dead man's arm on their shoulder, feeling the fine tremor in it. "What matters is what you do after. You came back for us. On the mountain. With your hands ruined and your deal burned, you picked up a shovel and dug Coral out of the ice with your bare fingers until Odile made you stop."

"They were mostly frozen anyway," Bramble muttered.

"Yeah," Tam said. "That's kind of the point."

Behind them, boots crunched. Odile drew level, taking stock of Bramble with one glance the way she took stock of everything — slope, load, sky, margin. Her face was still carved from the same granite it had always been, but Tam had learned to read the small weather of it, and this morning there was something almost soft in the set of her mouth. Almost. Odile's softness came in millimeters, measured out like rope on a dangerous pitch.

"How is he?" she asked Tam, quietly, in the way people ask things in front of the person they're asking about.

"He says sense is overrated," Tam reported.

"Hm." Odile looked up the slope behind them, back the way they'd come, where the peaks stood white and silent under a lid of cloud. Somewhere up there was the Hollow Vale, sealed now under its new shroud of avalanche snow, keeping its storm-glass and its secrets and its graves. Somewhere up there, too, was Silas Crane, walking away on his own two legs because Coral had let him — had looked at him across the smoking snow and said something Tam hadn't been close enough to hear, and Silas had nodded once, like a man accepting a sentence, and gone. "He shouldn't be walking. None of us should be walking. We're three days ahead of the deep snow and losing ground every hour."

"Then we don't stop," Tam said.

"We don't stop," Odile agreed. Then her eyes flicked to Tam — quick, assessing, the look she gave a route before committing to it. "Walk with me a while. Bramble, lean on the rope line; there are stakes every fifty paces and you'll feel them before you need them."

* * *

They walked together in silence for a while, Tam leading Hesper, Odile ranging slightly above them on the slope with the easy diagonal stride that never seemed to cost her anything. Below, the valley opened in slow stages — pine darkening the white, a frozen creek stitching silver through the trees, and far off, faint as a rumor, the smoke of some highland homestead rising straight in the still air.

"You count your steps," Odile said at last. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone counts their steps."

"No," Odile said. "They don't. Most people look at where they're going. You look at where your feet are. You count the distance back to shelter. You note the sun's angle against the time we passed the last landmark. You've been doing it since the first day, badly, and for the last week you've been doing it well." She let that sit for ten steps, twenty. "Do you know why I lost my guiding license? Officially, it was the Syndicate — they bought the guild, they buried me in fines, they burned my outfitting shed with everything in it. That part's true. But the reason they could, the reason nobody fought for me, is that I broke the rule. I took a client up in weather I shouldn't have, because he'd paid double and I needed the money for my mother's care. He lived. Another man on the mountain that day didn't, and it was ruled my fault, and the Syndicate simply — collected. I have hated them for eight years for what they did to me." Her breath steamed. "But I have never once lied to myself about what gave them the opening."

Tam said nothing. The mule's ears swiveled back toward Odile, listening, which Tam privately considered the correct response to most wisdom.

"You have the best eye for terrain I've seen on someone who never trained," Odile went on. "You found the wind scoop that saved our lives in the whiteout. You spotted the cornice overhang above the saddle before I did — you shouted, and that shout is the only reason Wren isn't under forty feet of snow. You read ground the way you used to read pockets, I think. Like everything on it is something that can be taken or kept." She stopped walking. Tam stopped too, and turned, and found Odile looking at them with an expression Tam had never seen on her face before — open, unguarded, almost young. "Come to Coldharbor. After. Work for me. Let me train you."

Tam's heart did something complicated. "As what?"

"A guide."

The word landed strangely, like a coin dropped into a cup they hadn't known they were holding. Tam looked away, up the valley, at the smoke rising straight and thin. "I'm fourteen."

"Fifteen in the spring. Coral told me." Of course Coral had told her. Coral knew everything; it was her most annoying quality, right after her habit of running toward avalanches. "I started younger. My father put a rope on me at twelve. It's not too early. It's exactly early enough — the mountains get your body young or they don't get it at all." Odile's voice roughened slightly. "I'm not offering you a job, Tam. I'm offering you a trade. Everything I know, for everything you are. It's the only fair bargain I've ever heard of."

Tam stood very still. Snow ticked softly down through the branches of a stunted pine nearby. Somewhere below, faintly, Bramble was singing to the mule — an old ferry song, tuneless and cracked, about a river that forgives everyone eventually.

"Why?" Tam said at last. It came out smaller than intended. "Why me? You've got Wren. Wren's strong, Wren's smart, Wren doesn't steal things."

"Wren has her own road," Odile said. "And I'm not asking you to stop being what you are. Thieves make the best guides. A thief reads a room for what can hurt them and what can be used — that's ninety percent of mountaineering. The other ten percent is deciding what's worth carrying down." She held out her hand — bare, scarred, steady in the cold. "Well?"

Tam looked at the hand. Looked at the valley, at the smoke, at the long white staircase unspooling down toward a town where they knew nobody and owed nobody and had no exits marked. It terrified them, how much they wanted it. Wanting things was how you got robbed.

But wanting things was also, apparently, how you got found.

They took the hand. Odile's grip was dry and hard and warm as a hearthstone.

"One condition," Tam said, because a bargain wasn't a bargain without terms, and some habits deserved to survive. "Hesper comes too. She's crew."

Odile's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "The mule is crew. Obviously."

* * *

They made the treeline by late afternoon, and camped under the pines where the wind couldn't reach, and it was there, in the blue hour between daylight and firelight, that Tam finally understood what had changed in them — really understood it, all the way down.

Coral was boiling snow for water, moving with the economy of someone who had burned everything unnecessary off herself on the mountain and liked the shape of what remained. Wren sat against a trunk with the shawl around her shoulders, sewing — actually sewing, repairing Bramble's pack straps with small fierce stitches, her father's map gone to ash and her hands needing work the way lungs need air. Bramble lay propped against Hesper's warm flank, singing his terrible river songs, and Hesper endured it with the resignation of the long-suffering righteous. Odile was checking the rope line for morning.

And Tam sat among them, counting — not exits, not pockets, not steps to safety. Just counting. Five. One mule. One fire. One story, still being told, that they were somehow inside of now instead of outside of.

In Bracken's Reach, Tam had slept in doorways and haylofts and once, memorably, inside a piano crate awaiting shipment, and every single night they had slept facing the door. Feet toward the wall, eyes on the exit, knife in the left boot and coins sewn into the right cuff. Eleven years of sleeping like a held breath.

Tonight they spread their bedroll with their back to the dark of the trees and their face to the fire, to the five shapes in its light, and fell asleep mid-thought, mid-sentence almost, mid-bargain — and did not wake once, and did not dream of doors at all.

* * *

On the third morning they came down out of the high country for good, and the world changed around them the way it does at the edge of mountains — suddenly, completely, like stepping through a curtain. The pines thickened. The air softened and grew smells again: woodsmoke, wet earth, dung and hay, bread. A cart track appeared, then wheel ruts, then a fence, then a dog that barked at Hesper and thought better of it.

And there, at the bend of the track where the valley floor began, stood a figure waiting in the road.

Tam saw her first — of course they did, old habits surfacing like trout — and their whole body went tight before their mind caught up. Small woman. Traveling cloak, city-cut. Standing with the particular stillness of someone who has been waiting a long time and intends to be seen waiting.

"Not Syndicate," Odile said, reading Tam's freeze instantly. "Wrong coat. Wrong boots. No escort."

Coral had already stepped forward, hand drifting to the compass at her throat — an old reflex, though the needle hung dead and quiet now, pointing nowhere, pointing at everything. "Hello!" she called, easy and loud, the port-town voice she wore like armor. "You're a long way from anywhere, friend. Looking for someone?"

The woman pushed back her hood. She was perhaps fifty, grey-streaked, with a face like a locked drawer — and eyes that went straight past Coral, past Odile, past the mule and the singing wreck of Bramble, and fixed on Tam with an intensity that made the morning cold seem warm by comparison.

"I'm looking for the child who took my compass," the woman said. "Eleven years ago. From a house on Anchor Lane, in Bracken's Reach. The night the Meridian men came for my husband's debts." Her voice didn't shake. That was the frightening thing about it. "I've followed the rumor of that compass through four towns and two mountain passes, and every trail went cold — until a fisherman in Coldharbor swore he saw a girl wearing it, heading uprange with a guide and a mule." She took one step closer, and Tam saw that her hands, hanging at her sides, were trembling very slightly, like a needle finding north. "So. Before anyone says another word. Is my daughter's compass still in your pocket, child — or will I have to ask what else you've been carrying of hers?"

The road went very quiet. Snow ticked off the pines. And Tam, who had stopped watching the exits, discovered there were none.
