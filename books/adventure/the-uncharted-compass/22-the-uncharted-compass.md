## 22. The Uncharted Compass

Spring came to Coldharbor the way forgiveness comes to the guilty — slowly, grudgingly, and all at once.

Coral watched it happen from the window of a rented room above the tannery, where the smell of lye and wet hide had become so familiar she no longer noticed it. Below her, the town was shedding its winter skin. Icicles let go of the eaves in bright, ringing drops. The river that had been a frozen fist all through the killing months loosened its fingers and began to move again, carrying broken ice downstream like letters finally being delivered. Somewhere a smith's hammer rang out a rhythm too cheerful for the hour, and someone — probably Tam, probably up to something — laughed in the alley behind the chandlery.

Six weeks since they had walked down out of the Serrano Range carrying Bramble between them on a litter of rope and ski poles and Odile's spare coat. Six weeks since Coral had stood in the Hollow Vale with her mother's journal open in her hands while snow fell into the pages and did not melt, and understood at last what she had been chasing her whole life.

She still dreamed about the mountain. She suspected she always would. But the dreams had changed shape. In the early ones — the ones from Bracken's Reach, from the waystation, from the first hard miles — the range had been a wall, an obstacle, a thing to be beaten or bribed or outrun. Now, when she dreamed, the mountain simply stood there under the stars, vast and indifferent and beautiful, and Coral stood with it, and neither of them needed anything from the other.

That, she thought, watching a cartload of spring lambs rattle through the square, might be the closest thing to peace she would ever own. It was enough.

* * *

The building had been a cooperage once, then a chapel, then nothing much at all — a hollow on Cooper's Row with a sagging roof and windows fogged with forty years of dust. Wren found it. Wren, who had arrived in Coldharbor as a stranger to her own name and now walked the town like a woman taking inventory of an inheritance, had knocked on Coral's door one gray morning in late thaw with sawdust still in her hair and said, "There's a place on Cooper's Row. Roof needs half replacing, floor needs all of it. The owner will let it go cheap because he thinks it's haunted."

"Is it?"

"It's got drafts," Wren said. "Same thing."

They bought it with money that felt strange in Coral's hands — clean money, earned money, the wages Odile had insisted on splitting from the guiding work she'd already begun doing for the wool merchants who needed their passes scouted before the summer drives. No Syndicate coin. No treasure. Just honest pay for honest knowledge, and it had taken Coral three separate nights to stop checking the bottom of her purse for the catch, the trick, the smiling man waiting inside the arithmetic.

Juno Pike was dead. Buried, insofar as anything was buried up there, under forty feet of avalanche snow in a valley that no longer appeared on any map. The Meridian Syndicate's claim had died with him, unrecorded, unmourned. Silas Crane had walked out of the range alone, and when Coral had last seen him — a chance crossing in the Coldharbor market, both of them pretending not to look — he'd had a mended coat and a new quietness about him, like a man who had set down a heavy bag and kept reaching for it anyway. He'd nodded to her. She'd nodded back. That was the whole of it, and somehow the whole of it was enough.

The roof took eleven days. The floor took nine. Odile did most of the structural work herself, moving through the gutted building with a hammer and a carpenter's level and the same absolute authority she brought to a mountainside, barking measurements at Tam until the boy — the girl; Coral had learned, somewhere on the Ghost Stair, that Tam answered to either and preferred the question never be settled — learned to hold a chalk line straight.

Bramble couldn't hammer. Bramble sat by the stove in a chair Odile had dragged in from a junk pile and reupholstered with a ferocity that suggested personal insult, his bandaged hands resting in his lap like two sleeping animals, and he offered opinions. He had many. He gave them freely, on everything: the angle of the stairs (too steep), the color of the sign (too cheerful), the wisdom of going into business at all (dubious). And every evening, when the work stopped and the four of them sat around the stove with tea or the cheap red wine Wren favored, Bramble told stories about the rivers he'd ferried in his prime, and his voice went soft and far away, and nobody mentioned the fingers.

The surgeons in Coldharbor had saved six of ten. On cold mornings, the missing four announced themselves anyway, a phantom ache in hands that remembered being whole. Coral had learned not to look away when Bramble flexed them. Looking away was how shame survived. Starved of eye contact, it died.

"You're staring," Bramble said one evening, catching her at it.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit. Leads to business ventures." He wiggled the surviving fingers of his right hand, a slow, deliberate motion, like a man testing a rope bridge before trusting it with his weight. "I've been thinking too. About the river."

"What river?"

"The one I nearly drowned in. With your mule." He said *your mule* the way other men said *your dog*, with the particular tenderness of a man who had almost died for an animal and would do it again without hesitation. "I keep thinking about how you hesitated."

Coral's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Not the map satchel," Bramble said, before she could flinch. "I know what you think you chose against, and you're wrong. You looked at me. You looked at me first, girl, and then you looked at the water, and what you were weighing wasn't me against the map. It was whether you were allowed to care. Whether caring was something a woman with your debts could afford." He leaned back, satisfied, the way he always was after landing a blow wrapped in kindness. "You can afford it now. That's my opinion. Take it or leave it."

Coral drank her tea. Her eyes were burning, and she blamed the woodsmoke, and everyone at the table politely pretended to believe her.

* * *

They argued about the name for two full weeks.

Odile wanted something plain and true: *Eastwick & Vane Mountain Guides*, though she refused, on principle, to put her own name anywhere near it. "My name's ruined," she said. "Every syndicate clerk from here to the coast has it written next to the word *disgraced*. Let it stay buried."

Wren wanted *Vane & Company*, and Coral understood why — understood it the way you understand a scar, from the inside. Wren had spent the winter learning her father from secondhand sources: from the map sewn into her mother's shawl, from the stories the crew told over camp stoves, from the journal pages Elias had never known existed, which Coral had finally surrendered in the Vale and which Wren read so many times the paper went soft as cloth. Naming the business for him was a resurrection. It was also, Wren admitted on the third week of arguing, maybe a monument to a man who didn't deserve one, and she wasn't sure yet which, and she wanted to be sure.

It was Tam who ended the argument, in the way Tam ended most things: by doing something unauthorized and letting the consequences sort themselves out.

Coral came back from the timber yard one afternoon to find the sign already hung above the door of the old cooperage — a plank of silvered pine, the letters burned in with a soldering iron in Tam's careful, crooked hand:

**THE UNCHARTED COMPASS**
*Expeditions Outfitted · Passes Scouted · Honest Work*

Below the words, painted in white, was a compass rose. And below the rose, small enough that you'd miss it unless you were looking, a single line:

*We find what's worth finding.*

Coral stood in the street looking at it for a long time. The spring wind moved through Cooper's Row, carrying the smell of the river and cut lumber and someone's bread, and the sign creaked gently on its new hinges, and she could not have said, if a stranger had asked, why her throat was closed.

"Heard you arguing," Tam said, appearing at her elbow with the soundless suddenness that had once made them a thief and now just made them Tam. "Figured somebody should decide. You're all terrible at deciding. You'd have argued until the passes closed again."

"The paint's crooked."

"The paint is *rustic*."

"The last line's presumptuous."

Tam shrugged, hands in pockets, studying their own handiwork with a critical squint. "It's true, though. Isn't it?" And there it was — the question underneath the question, the kid asking without asking whether the thing they'd all been through meant what it felt like it meant, whether the family was real, whether it would hold.

Coral put her hand on Tam's shoulder. The kid didn't shrug it off anymore. Hadn't since the snow cave.

"Yeah," she said. "It's true."

* * *

The compass itself went up on the day they opened.

Coral had carried it through everything — through the debt and the promise and the passes, through the crevasse field where she'd crawled with cracked ribs toward a needle that knew her better than she knew herself, through the Vale, through the descent, through the long convalescent spring. Its brass case was dented now, scarred across the face where a rock in the Whiteout had struck it, and the glass over the dial had a hairline crack running from north to south like a meridian of its own.

But the needle. The needle was perfect.

All winter it had done nothing. Lay flat in her palm, in her pocket, on the table beside her mother's journal, pointing nowhere with the serene confidence of a thing that had finished its work. She had caught herself checking it anyway, the way you check a wound that's healed, half expecting the ache to come back. It never did. The needle rested. And some nights that rest had frightened her more than any spin ever had — because a compass that points nowhere is a compass with nothing left to say, and Coral had spent her whole life needing things to say something, even debts, even threats. Silence was the one language she'd never learned to live in.

On opening morning, Odile drilled a small brass hook into the beam above the door — the great old cooperage beam, black with a century of smoke, solid as the mountain itself — and Coral climbed the ladder and hung the compass from it on a loop of braided cord.

Wren held the ladder. Tam supervised from the floor with their arms crossed. Bramble, in his chair by the window where the sun came through, raised his damaged hands in something between a salute and a blessing.

"There," Odile said, when Coral came down. "Now it watches the door instead of the road. Better job. Less walking."

They opened at dawn, because Odile believed all honest enterprises should begin at dawn, and because Tam had already promised their first client — a nervous wool merchant named Hesk who wanted his summer pass scouted — that dawn was when the experts woke up. By midmorning they had three bookings on the slate Odile had mounted by the door: Hesk's pass scout, a party of botanists wanting the lower valley trails, and a widow who paid in preserves and only wanted company for a walk to the falls, which Odile booked without hesitation and charged nothing for, and which Coral privately considered the finest transaction the business would make all year.

By noon, Coral stepped out onto Cooper's Row to look at her own door from the outside, the way you have to, sometimes, to believe a thing is real.

The sign creaked. The compass turned gently on its cord above it, catching the light, and the needle lay still in its cracked glass cradle, pointing at nothing, pointing at everything, pointing — Coral realized, standing in the spring sun with sawdust on her sleeves and clients on her slate and a family arguing cheerfully about rope gauges behind her — home.

She had spent years believing the needle pointed to loss. Her mother's ghost, her mother's grave, the hole in the world shaped like a woman who'd walked into the mountains and never walked out. In the Vale she had found the truth of that: the journal, the camp, the record of Maren Eastwick spending her final season searching ridgelines for a daughter who was alive and far away and did not know she was being searched for. Loss, yes. All of it loss. Coral had wept there among the storm-glass spires until the grief ran clean, and had thought that was the end of the compass's story — a relic of sorrow, retired.

But standing here now, she understood it differently. The needle had never been a pointer to the lost. A compass doesn't tell you where you've been. It tells you where to go. Every pull Coral had ever felt from that trembling brass finger — toward the waystation where a dying stranger waited, toward the pass where a thief became a child became hers, toward the cave where strangers became a hearth, toward the saddle where betrayal broke her heart and rescue mended it, toward the valley where her mother's love was waiting all along — every pull had been toward the thing she hadn't found *yet*. The people. The promise. The point.

Her mother had died following the needle toward Coral. Coral had lived by following it toward everything else. The instrument had never once lied. It had simply always known, before she did, what was still worth finding.

"Coral!" Wren called from the doorway, wind-flushed, laughing at something Tam had said. "Hesk's here, and he's brought a friend, and the friend wants to hire us for the high country in July, and Odile says yes but she wants you to look him in the eye first because she doesn't trust his boots."

"Nobody trusts his boots," Tam said, appearing beside Wren. "I checked them while he was signing. They're wrong in a way I can't explain but definitely feel."

Coral smiled. Behind her the compass hung silent above the door, keeping its vigil over the comings and goings of ordinary people bound for ordinary mountains — and if it stirred, some mornings, when the wind came down off the Serrano Range smelling of snow and stone and distance, stirring on its cord like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice, well. Coral noticed. Coral always noticed.

But the needle never spun. It never pointed anywhere at all.

Because there was nothing left to find.

Everything worth finding was already here.
