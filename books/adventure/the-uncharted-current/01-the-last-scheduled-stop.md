## 1. The Last Scheduled Stop

The spur line out of Corben's Hollow hadn't carried a scheduled train in eleven years, which was exactly why Jonah Ravensworth liked it. Scheduled things had timetables, and timetables had witnesses, and witnesses asked questions like *where are you headed* and *how long will you be staying*, and those were questions she'd spent six years learning to answer wrong.

She rode the flatcar slow, one boot hooked over the coupler, the surveyor's map rolled tight against her spine inside her coat where it rode everywhere she went. The rails sang under her in that half-voice dead track makes when nobody's greased them — a low complaint, a note held too long. She knew every dialect of rail song there was: live mainline hum, branch-line chatter, the groan of a bridge deciding whether to hold. This one said *nobody's coming, nobody's looking, keep going.*

So she did.

Corben's Hollow announced itself by smell before sight — creosote and cold brine and diesel gone sour in rusted tanks. Then the town itself, folded into the crook of a gray estuary like something the sea had tried to swallow and spat back out. A cannery with its windows boarded in a pattern that almost looked deliberate. A grain elevator leaning two degrees past comfortable. And at the end of the long wooden pier, riding low and working loud, the salvage vessel *Meridian*, all crane arm and floodlight, tied up like a patient that had decided to stop pretending it would get better.

Jonah hopped the flatcar at the old freight apron, rolled her shoulders, and walked.

Three nights. That was the rule. She'd kept it so long it had worn smooth as a river stone: three nights anywhere, then move. Not because anyone was chasing her — though someone always might be; debt didn't die of neglect, it just accrued interest — but because of the other thing, the thing she didn't name even to herself on bad nights. Stillness was where the past caught up. Keep moving and you were a rumor. Stop, and you became an address, and addresses got letters, and letters got opened, and opened letters could say anything. Her father's last letter had said plenty.

She bought coffee from a kiosk run by a woman who looked at Jonah's boots, then her hands, then nodded once — the nod dock towns give to people who work for a living and don't waste words. Good. Word traveled fast in places like this, but only if you gave it something to carry.

The *Meridian*'s gangway had a man on it with a clipboard he wasn't reading. Big shoulders, small patience, a crew jacket with the collar turned up against the wind off the water.

"You're not harbor master," he said.

"Harbor master's been dead since March," Jonah said. "You're moored against pilings he should've inspected."

Something flickered across his face — the particular discomfort of a man who'd been told this already and hadn't wanted to hear it. "We're not staying long."

"Nobody is." She came up the gangway without asking, which was its own kind of credential. "That's why I'm here."

On deck, the *Meridian* resolved into organized chaos: dive gear racked under canvas, a winch being argued over by two people, crates stenciled with a name she didn't recognize yet but would soon enough. And at the center of it, a woman with gray threading her braids and a voice that cut through engine noise like it had been designed for exactly that — directing, correcting, never raising it twice.

Mara Okonkwo. Dive lead. Jonah had done her homework on the crossing over, the way she did homework on everything: names, histories, the incident off Sallow Point three years back where Okonkwo had brought up four divers from a flooded wreck when every protocol said leave them. The kind of person whose crews came home alive. The kind of person worth trusting, briefly, professionally, from a safe distance.

"You're the guide," Okonkwo said, turning. Not a question. "Ravensworth."

"I'm a guide," Jonah said. "Whether I'm *your* guide depends on what you're buying."

Okonkwo's mouth twitched. She gestured, and the clipboard man drifted off with the grace of someone relieved to be dismissed. "Word around the yard is you know the closed lines. All of them. Even the ones that aren't on charts."

"The ones that aren't on charts are the only ones worth knowing."

"That so." Okonkwo studied her the way divers study water — checking for what moved underneath. "We need passage north. Two hundred miles of coast, most of it dead rail, all of it closed. Officially."

"Officially," Jonah repeated, letting the word sit between them like a coin on a table.

"There's a site we're surveying. Tide-dependent. Narrow window." Okonkwo crossed her arms. "I'm told you can get equipment and eleven people along lines the railway company pretends don't exist, without the railway company finding out they exist."

"I can." Jonah let the pause stretch just long enough. "For a price."

"Name it."

"Triple day rate. Cash. And my name stays off whatever manifest you file with your insurer."

That earned her a longer look. "You don't like manifests."

"I don't like lists," Jonah said, and heard how it came out, too flat, too fast, and covered it with a shrug. "Lists have names on them. Names have histories attached. I travel light."

Okonkwo didn't smile, but something near her eyes suggested she'd filed the moment away for later examination. "Triple rate. Done. We load tomorrow, run at first light."

"Then I'll see you at first light." Jonah turned for the gangway.

"Ravensworth." She stopped. Didn't turn. "One more question. The chart work you'll be doing — the routes themselves. Where'd you learn dead track like that? Nobody learns it anymore. It has to be taught."

The wind came off the estuary and pushed at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades, urging her down the gangway, down the pier, out of this conversation and onto the next flatcar heading anywhere. Three nights. First light tomorrow made tonight night one. Night three she'd be gone before the gulls woke up, and whatever Okonkwo thought she saw in Jonah's face would stay aboard the *Meridian* and sink with the rest of it.

"My father," Jonah said. "He surveyed these lines. Before they were dead."

"And after?"

"After," Jonah said, "he stopped coming home."

She took the boarding house on Anchor Street because it was the only one, paid for three nights in advance because paying for four would have meant intending to stay, and sat by the window with the map unrolled across the bed while the storm light died over the water.

It was a beautiful thing, her map. Hand-inked on linen-backed stock, forty years of corrections layered over the original survey — her father's hand young and sure in the margins, then his hand older, tighter, the letters crowding each other like men in a lifeboat. Every closed spur, every sealed tunnel, every trestle condemned on paper and still standing in fact. He'd drawn routes the railway denied existed, and somewhere in the last year of his life he'd started marking things differently. Not routes. *Destinations.* Little annotations in the margins, tide tables copied out beside them, one word repeated in his smallest handwriting:

*tollgate.*

She'd never found it. Six years of riding his lines and she'd never once found the place his notes pointed to, and some nights — nights like this one, in rooms she'd leave in two days — she told herself that was fine, that the map was for work and the work was passage and passage was freedom, and the rest was just paper.

Other nights she knew she was lying.

Below the window, the *Meridian*'s floodlights burned against the dark water, and figures moved on deck loading gear that clinked and knocked. Salvage. Survey. Tide-dependent, narrow window. She smoothed the map flat and ran her finger north along the coast, past the dead stations, past the collapsed cuttings, to where her father's ink grew strange and crowded and the margin notes began.

Her finger stopped.

There was a mark there she'd seen a hundred times and never read properly — a small annotation beside a spur terminus, dated the spring of the year he died. Not a route correction. A tide table. Extreme low water, once a decade, with a date circled.

She checked it against today's date in the almanac she kept in her kit, the way she checked everything, twice.

The circled date was nine days out.

Jonah sat very still in the rented room in the town she'd sworn to leave in three days, and felt the old fear rise in her chest like water in a lock — the fear that stillness was where the past caught up, that if she stopped moving long enough, stopped running long enough, the thing her father had died carrying would finally reach her.

Nine days. The window she'd sold to Okonkwo's expedition ran straight through it.

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to anyone raised on rail song — she heard the *Meridian*'s crane begin to swing, loading for a run she was now contractually bound to make.

Night one of three. She had eight days to decide whether to keep her rule or break it, and both choices, she suspected, ended at the same place: wherever the tollgate was, and whatever her father had found there.

She rolled up the map. She did not sleep.
