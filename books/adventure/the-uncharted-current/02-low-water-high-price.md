## 2. Low Water, High Price

The Meridian's crew found me the way everyone finds me — through a rumor with a price tag attached.

I was three days into my stay at Port Calloway, which meant I had one night left by my own law, and I'd spent it in the back booth of the Salt & Cinder watching the tide charts on the wall like they were racing forms. The barman had stopped asking what I wanted an hour ago and just kept the coffee coming. Outside, the harbor was doing something it wasn't supposed to do: draining. Not the ordinary pull of a normal ebb, but a long, slow exhale, like the sea had decided to hold its breath for a week.

Old-timers stood on the seawall pointing at mudflats that hadn't seen sun since before most of them were born. A kid was out there already, ankle-deep in exposed silt, holding up something that might have been a boot or might have been a rock. His mother screamed at him from the wall until he came back.

I knew what was happening. I'd been carrying the knowledge around for six years in a waterproof tube along with everything else my father left me, and I'd never once expected to be standing in the town where it would matter.

That's when the woman in the oilskin jacket sat down across from me without asking.

"You're Ravensworth," she said. "The one who knows the closed lines."

"I'm someone having coffee."

"Finch told me you sold passage to the Kestrel crew last spring. Down the abandoned spur through the Cut." She set a card on the table between us. Heavy stock, embossed, the kind of card that costs more than my monthly food budget. MERIDIAN EXPEDITION — SALVAGE & RECOVERY. Underneath, a name: M. OKONKWO, DIVE LEAD.

"The Kestrel crew got where they were going," I said. "That's not the same as me selling them anything."

"They said you charged them double because the line was 'technically haunted.'" Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "We'll pay triple whatever you quoted them."

I looked at her properly then. Mara Okonkwo. Broad hands, rope-scarred knuckles, the calm of someone who has pulled bodies out of water and intends to keep not doing that. Dive leads don't waste words. I respected that immediately and trusted it not at all.

"What's the job?"

"A cave."

"No."

"You haven't heard the number yet."

"I've heard the word *cave*," I said. "Caves under coastal headlands open twice a day and kill people four times a year. Find another guide."

"It's not a twice-a-day cave." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the salt dried white on her collar. "It opens once. Maybe twice, if the decade is generous. There's a tidal window starting nine days from now — extreme low water series, the kind they print warnings about. For roughly forty minutes a day, maybe less, there's a passage into the base of Greywacke Point that doesn't exist any other time. My backer wants it surveyed and salvaged before the window closes. After that, the cave seals up for years. Possibly forever, if the winter storms rearrange the scree like they usually do."

Greywacke Point. I kept my face still, but inside my chest something turned over, slow and heavy, like a rail car shifting on a bad trestle.

Because I knew Greywacke Point. Every child of Elias Ravensworth knew Greywacke Point, though none of us were supposed to know why. In his notes — the ones I'd been hauling up and down the coast since I was nineteen, the ones I'd half convinced myself were the obsessive scribblings of a man who'd spent too many nights alone with survey instruments — there was a page headed with two words and nothing else:

*The tollgate.*

And beneath those words, a sketch of a headland I could have drawn blindfolded, because I'd grown up looking at it from every train window on the coast run. Greywacke Point, with a dotted line running into its base like a vein.

"How do you know about this cave?" I asked. "If it only opens once a decade, who's been telling people it exists?"

Okonkwo's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a small recalculation, the look of a woman deciding how much of her hand to show. "Our backer has historical records. Railway-era documents. He believes there's a wreck inside."

"A wreck. Inside a sealed cave. Behind a door that opens once every ten years."

"That's the claim."

"And your backer's name?"

She slid a second card across the table. This one was heavier than the first. It probably had its own insurance policy.

**ALDOUS VEY.** And beneath the name, in smaller type: *Vey Maritime Trust.*

I'd heard the name. You can't work the ghost lines for six years without hearing it — Vey money sat behind half the salvage operations on the coast, buying up decommissioned rolling stock, funding inquiries that never published, endowing museum wings full of railway artifacts nobody was allowed to touch. A name that came up in conversations and then politely left the room when the conversation got specific.

"He pays well," Okonkwo said. "He pays triple what the job's worth, if you want the truth of it. Which makes some people nervous, and makes other people rich. Which are you?"

I should have said no. Let me be clear about that, because everything that came after started here, and I've had six years and one very long winter to examine the moment from every angle. I should have finished my coffee, walked out into the drained and stinking harbor air, and caught the midnight freight south. That was the rule. Three nights, never four, because stillness is where the past catches up, and I had built my whole life on outrunning it one closed line at a time.

But she said *triple*, and triple meant eleven thousand dollars, and eleven thousand dollars meant I could pay off Harrow & Finch — no relation to our Finch, the world is small and mean that way — the debt collectors who'd been holding my father's estate in receivership since the funeral. Eleven thousand dollars meant the survey tube in my bag stopped being collateral and started being mine again. Eleven thousand dollars meant I could stop moving.

Or so I told myself. Remember that part. *Or so I told myself.*

"One condition," I said.

Okonkwo waited.

"I want to see the historical records. Before I commit. Whatever documents your backer used to locate this cave — I want ten minutes alone with them."

Something flickered across her face then, quick as a signal lamp. Surprise, maybe. Or recognition. As if I'd asked for exactly the thing she'd been warned someone might ask for.

"That's not standard," she said.

"I'm not standard. I'm the guide who knows the closed lines, and you're paying triple because you can't find anyone else who does. Ten minutes. Alone. Or I catch the midnight freight and you can explain to Mr. Vey why his once-in-a-decade window is closing with no surveyor."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the kid's mother had given up shouting and come down onto the flats herself, and now they were both out there in the dying light, turning over the bones of the harbor.

"I'll ask," she said finally. "He's arriving tomorrow on the morning boat. He'll want to meet you himself anyway. Vey meets everyone personally." She stood, tucked her chair in with a precision that told me she'd served on naval vessels, not just dive boats. "Nine days, Ravensworth. Don't leave town."

"I always leave town."

"Not this time."

* * *

Vey arrived on the morning boat the way weather arrives — you saw it coming from far off and it changed everything anyway.

He was smaller than I expected, silver-haired, dressed like a man who had chosen expensive clothes so carefully they looked cheap to anyone paying attention. Two men came off the boat with him and stayed exactly one step behind him at all times, and neither of them carried anything, which was its own kind of statement.

He found me at the harbormaster's office, where I'd gone to check freight schedules I had no intention of using. He didn't introduce himself. He just set a leather document case on the counter between us and opened it.

"Ms. Ravensworth. Mara says you wanted to see these."

Inside, flat and yellowed and protected by archival sleeves, were survey charts. Coast Survey originals, 1950s vintage, the beautiful hand-drafted kind they stopped making when the machines took over. And I knew before I touched them. I knew the way you know a voice through a wall.

My father's hand. Younger, steadier, but his — the distinctive way he drew depth soundings like little commas, the tiny annotations in the margins, the private shorthand we used to play games with when I was small enough to sit on his knee in the survey office while he worked. *What does that mark mean, Papa?* *It means the water is lying, Jonah. Water lies all the time. Our job is to make it tell the truth.*

There it was, in the margin of the oldest chart, in his handwriting, in his shorthand, the words I'd only ever seen in his stolen notes:

*The tollgate.*

And beside it, a date. Three weeks before he died.

The harbormaster's office went very quiet and very far away. I was aware of Vey watching me with the patient attention of a man confirming a purchase, and I was aware that my hands were steady, because my hands are always steady, and I hated them for it.

"Where did you get these?" My voice came out level. Good.

"Acquired," Vey said, which is what certain kinds of men say instead of *bought from someone who shouldn't have sold them.* "Elias Ravensworth was a fine surveyor. One of the best the coast office ever employed. It's a shame his career ended the way it did."

"You know how his career ended."

"I know what the record says." He closed the case gently, like a lid on a jar of something perishable. "The record and the truth are different documents, Ms. Ravensworth. I'm in the business of the second one. Triple rate. Nine days. Greywacke Point." He slid a contract across the counter, already typed, already dated, my name already printed in the guide line — which meant he'd known I'd say yes before I did, and that should have frightened me more than it did.

I signed it because my father's handwriting was in that case, and because eleven thousand dollars was freedom, and because I told myself — remember, I told myself — that this time, taking the money would be the same thing as taking control.

Vey smiled like a man closing a deal with the tide.

"Wonderful," he said. "We sail for the Point tomorrow. And Ms. Ravensworth — when we find what's inside, you'll understand why I needed someone whose name was already on the manifest."

I didn't know what he meant.

I would.
