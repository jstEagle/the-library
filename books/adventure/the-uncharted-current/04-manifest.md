## 4. Manifest

The mail car came out of the cave in pieces, and it came out wrong.

Not broken-wrong. Sealed-wrong. Mara's divers spent the first hour of the second slack tide cutting away rockfall that had been stacked, not fallen — stones fitted into the gap behind the car with a mason's care, then mortared with silt and time until the whole thing looked like an accident of geology. Jonah watched them work from the ledge above the waterline, her lamp beam on their bubbles, and felt the shape of the lie settle over her like weather.

Somebody had buried this car on purpose. Somebody had wanted it found by no one.

"Jonah." Mara's voice in her earpiece, tinny through the rebreather comm relay. "You're going to want to see this before we move anything."

They brought up the strongbox first — a riveted iron case the size of a coffin lid, crusted white with mineral scale, its lock long since rusted to decoration. They set it on the staging tarp among the dive gear, and the whole expedition went quiet around it in a way that made Jonah's skin prickle. Six people, one cave mouth breathing tide at their backs, and nobody saying a word.

Vey wasn't there. He'd stayed topside with the radio, "coordinating," which Jonah had already learned meant monitoring a channel that never crackled and asking questions he already knew the answers to.

"Open it," she said.

Mara looked at her for a long moment. Then she took the pry bar off Finch's belt and worked it under the lid herself.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job for seventy years, were papers. Hundreds of them. Mail sacks split open across the bottom of the box, letters fused into bricks of pulp, but beneath those — dry, deliberate, packed like someone had expected them to matter — were the railway's own documents. Ledgers. Inspection reports. A maintenance schedule for the coastal trestles, annotated in three different hands.

And a manifest.

Jonah lifted it out with both hands because her hands insisted on doing something. The paper was stiff as bark but legible, typed columns under a letterhead she knew from childhood: *Corvane Coast Railway, Freight & Post Division.* She'd grown up with that eagle stamped on everything in their house — ticket stubs, her father's lunch pail, the tin cup she drank from until she was twelve.

She read down the columns. Crates of mail. Two cars of freight. A crew list.

Her father's name was the ninth line from the top.

*R. Ravensworth — Signalman, Grade II.*

"He wasn't supposed to be on this run." Her voice came out flat, strange to her own ears. "He was a signalman. He worked the tower at Kiln Point. He wasn't — this is a mail car manifest. Why is his name here?"

Mara took the page without touching Jonah's fingers, careful the way you're careful with something that might shatter. "Maybe he rode along. Signalmen sometimes—"

"He hated riding the line. He told me once the trestles sang wrong in high wind and he didn't like hearing it." Jonah heard herself talking and couldn't stop. "I was nine. I thought it was a joke. It wasn't a joke, was it. He heard them singing wrong because they *were* wrong."

Nobody answered. Finch shifted his weight; the tarp crinkled. Somewhere back toward the entrance, water dripped in slow metronome time, counting down the tide.

Jonah set the manifest down and picked up the ledger underneath it.

* * *

It took her twenty minutes to understand what she was holding, and she would spend the rest of her life unable to un-know it.

The ledger covered fourteen months of inspections on the Corvane coastal section — the same section her map traced, the ghost spurs, the closed lines, all of it built on the bones of this one disaster. The inspection entries started routine: load ratings, timber condition, bolt torque. Then, eight months in, the handwriting changed and the numbers started lying.

A trestle near Graywater Bend rated for forty tons, logged at forty tons, while a separate column — a column somebody had tried to make look like part of the original table — recorded actual load tests at nineteen. The same entry repeated month after month, the real number sinking while the reported number held steady like a man holding his breath. Freight kept rolling across it. The mail car ran that route twice a week.

At the back of the ledger, folded into the binding, was the order itself. One page. Typed, official, bearing the division superintendent's stamp:

*In light of interim findings, Section 9 trestle is to remain in unrestricted service pending full structural review. No service interruption will be authorized. This order supersedes prior inspection recommendations.*

And below the signature line, in smaller type: *Witnessed.*

Two signatures there. The superintendent's, a confident slash of ink.

And beneath it, in handwriting Jonah had last seen signing her birthday cards — the same hand that had taught her to letter a survey chart, the R with that little kick at the base she'd copied into her own maps without ever deciding to —

*R. Ravensworth.*

"I don't understand." Finch said it quietly, hat in his hands now, which was not like Finch at all. "If he signed it, isn't he—"

"He witnessed it." Mara was reading over Jonah's shoulder, and her voice had gone very still, very precise, the voice of a woman navigating a reef in the dark. "Look at the wording. Witnessed. Not authorized, not countersigned. Witnessed. You put a witness signature on an order when you're not agreeing to it. When you want proof later that someone saw what was being done."

Jonah stared at the page until the letters swam.

Six years. Six years of hopping closed lines and sleeping in signal sheds and telling herself the story everyone else had told her: that her father had been on that run because he'd been careless, or desperate, or drinking, or all three — that the railway had washed its hands of him and the town had nodded along, and that the disgrace was hers to carry out of town ahead of her like luggage. She'd built her whole life around that story. Three nights in any place, never longer, because places remembered, and what they remembered about the Ravensworths was not kind.

But here was the other story, in ink, in the railway's own hand.

He'd found the failing trestle. He'd logged the real numbers. And when the order came down to keep the trains running anyway, he hadn't signed it as a co-conspirator — he'd signed it as the man who refused to let it happen unseen. Someone had to witness. He made himself the witness. And then he'd been put on the next run over Section 9, a signalman who hated riding the line, listed on a mail car manifest where he had no business being.

The car went into the sea. The witness went with it. The ledger went into a hole in the rock behind fitted stones, and the story got rewritten, and a nine-year-old girl grew up wearing the blame.

"Oh," Jonah said. Just that. It came out of her like something leaving a wound.

Mara's hand settled on her shoulder, brief and hard. "Jonah."

"The tide." Jonah blinked, dragged herself back into her body, into the cave, into the sound of the water. "How long do we have?"

"Forty minutes to slack's end. Maybe less — barometer's been falling since dawn."

"Then we take all of it. Every page. The ledger, the order, the manifest, the inspection reports. Everything dry goes in the waterproof cases, everything wet gets photographed twice." She was moving now, kneeling at the strongbox, sorting with hands that shook and did not slow. "This doesn't go back in the dark. Whatever Vey thinks we came here for — this is what we came here for."

Finch glanced toward the cave mouth. "You think Vey doesn't know what's in here?"

"I think," Jonah said, "that a man who hires a guide at triple pay for a cave that opens once a decade knows exactly what's in here. I just haven't figured out yet whether he wants it found or wants it gone."

Mara met her eyes across the strongbox, and something passed between them — quick, unspoken, a first hairline crack in whatever each of them was pretending.

"Then we figure it out fast," Mara said. "Because if he wants it gone, the safest place for these papers just became the bottom of the sea. And the most dangerous place in the world is going to be standing between him and the surface with them in your pack."

Jonah zipped the case shut over her father's signature and slung it across her chest.

Above them, far up the flooded chimney, the light from the entrance dimmed a shade — clouds moving in off the water, though none of them turned to check the sky.
