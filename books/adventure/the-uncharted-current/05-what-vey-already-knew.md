## 5. What Vey Already Knew

Mara Okonkwo had run salvage on three coasts and one inland sea, and she had learned long ago that the money in a job was never where the contract said it was. The contract said *recovery of historical artifacts, condition unknown, site access limited*. The money said otherwise. The money said *burn rate*.

She noticed it the second night back at the harbor house, going through Vey's expense authorizations. She'd asked for a decompression table for the deep runs — standard kit, four thousand dollars. Vey's office had approved it in eleven minutes. But when she'd requested archival-grade document cases for the paper recovery, the reply had come back with a clause she'd read three times before it made sense: *recovery containers to be supplied by the client.*

Supplied by the client. Nobody supplied their own evidence cases. You bought those at any marine outfitter, off the shelf. Unless you wanted them to be a particular kind of case — fire-rated, incinerator-lined, the kind that went into a furnace and came out as ash with paperwork attached.

Mara sat in the chart room with the authorization printout and listened to the storm building offshore. It had been building for two days, a low gray pressure that made the barometer fall like water down a drain and made everyone in the crew short-tempered and quiet. Jonah Ravensworth had spent most of it at the window, watching the rail yard through a gap in the curtains like a woman counting exits.

The client's cases had arrived that morning. Six of them. Mara had opened one, telling herself she was doing inventory.

Inside, packed in foam cut to precise specifications, were thermite ignition units. Not flares. Not signal devices. Thermite, rated to two thousand degrees Celsius, with ceramic containment shells so they could be placed against paper and steel and set off clean, no shrapnel, no fire spreading beyond the burn radius. Military surplus. The kind of thing you couldn't buy without a license unless you already knew someone who didn't ask questions.

She had closed the case and sat with her hands flat on the lid for a long time.

* * *

Here was what Mara knew about Aldous Vey: he paid on time, he never micromanaged a dive plan, and he had once kept her crew whole through a bankruptcy season by fronting six months of operating costs against nothing but his signature. He had found her when she was thirty-one and unemployable, blacklisted from the commercial fleets after a salvage master's negligence got two divers bent and her name on the report because she'd been the one who logged the safety violations. Vey hadn't cared about the blacklist. He'd said, *I hire people who tell the truth about wrecks,* and he'd meant it, and for nine years that sentence had been the load-bearing wall of her career.

So she did what she always did with a discrepancy. She worked it like a wreck. Systematic. Top to bottom.

The manifest itself was the first problem. Vey had filed the expedition under *historical recovery*, which required an insurance rider listing expected artifacts. The rider listed the mail car's contents as *postal effects, degraded, nominal value.* Nominal value. Mara had pulled the 1950s railway valuation schedules out of the harbor library's reference room — she'd spent a bored afternoon there while the storm built, and now she was glad of it — and postal manifests from wrecks of that era were insured at auction rates. A sealed mail car, intact, was never *nominal value*. Unless the insurer already knew the contents would never reach an auction block.

The second problem was the tide schedule. Vey's original survey request had come eleven months ago. The decade-low tide series had been published fourteen months ago. Anyone planning a legitimate first-look salvage would have moved within weeks of the announcement; the cave would not open again for years. Eleven months wasn't caution. Eleven months was waiting for something else — waiting for the inquiry commission's records embargo to lapse, maybe, or waiting until the last living signatory on some old document had died quietly in a care home up the coast. Mara had no way to know which. She only knew that legitimate urgency didn't look like that.

The third problem was the crew list. Vey had approved every diver she'd proposed except one — he'd insisted on adding his own man, a quiet, competent deckhand named Sorrel who handled lines well and never dove past forty feet. Mara had assumed he was a spy for the insurance underwriters. Lots of backers sent watchers. But Sorrel had spent the entire cave operation topside, managing the surface line, and now she remembered exactly where he'd positioned himself during the recovery: not at the dive flag, but between the boat and the road. Watching the approach. And on the day they'd brought the document crates ashore, Sorrel had photographed each crate before it went into the harbor house. Not for inventory. For proof of destruction later — a record that everything recovered had been accounted for, so that when the originals vanished, no one could claim anything else had ever existed.

She put it together in the chart room at two in the morning, with the rain starting against the windows, and when it was assembled she sat very still and let the shape of it settle over her.

Vey hadn't hired them to recover the mail car's contents. He'd hired them to locate it, raise it, and destroy it. The thermite was the payload; the divers were the delivery system. And the crew — her crew, Finch and Delacroix and young Ibarra and Jonah Ravensworth with her dead father's map — were witnesses to a crime that had been planned longer than any of them had known the cave existed. Witnesses who were supposed to come home believing they'd done honest work, and keep believing it, because the alternative was admitting they'd helped bury a disaster twice.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Nine years of loyalty, and her hands were steady.

* * *

She almost told Jonah the next morning.

Jonah was in the galley at dawn, mending a tear in her survey map's waterproof sleeve with waxed thread, working the needle with the same economy she applied to everything. She didn't fidget. Mara had watched a lot of people wait out storms, and most of them came apart in small ways — checking phones, pacing, talking too much. Jonah just sat and sewed and watched the yard, still as ballast, and Mara understood that this was what six years of running looked like when it finally stopped moving: not relief. Readiness.

"Your stitch spacing's uneven," Mara said, because she needed to say something ordinary.

"It holds." Jonah didn't look up. "That's all stitching has to do."

There it was, then. An opening, if she wanted it. *Jonah, the client's cases are full of thermite. Jonah, your father's ledger is scheduled for cremation. Jonah, we're all disposable.* Say it fast, say it plain, the way she logged safety violations — the way that had gotten her blacklisted once already, because telling the truth about wrecks had a cost and she'd paid it before and survived.

But Vey's face was there too. Vey in the harbor office nine years ago, saying *I hire people who tell the truth about wrecks,* and meaning it — or meaning something adjacent to it, something that had seemed identical until this week. If she burned him now, on suspicion, on inference, on thermite cases and tide mathematics — what if she was wrong? What if there was a version of this where the client was paranoid instead of criminal, where the incinerator cases were overkill rather than intent? Accuse a man like Vey falsely and she lost more than a contract. She lost the only patron who'd ever bet on her, and the crew lost their pay, and the truth — whatever the truth actually was — stayed underwater anyway.

And underneath that, quieter, worse: a small cold voice asking whether it mattered. Whether telling Jonah changed anything. Vey had men in town — Mara had seen them, two of them, too well-dressed for the weather, walking the seawall in shifts. The documents were in a locked room with Sorrel outside it. The storm had closed the roads. Even if Jonah ran tonight, she ran with nothing but a memory of a ledger, and memories didn't survive contact with men like the ones on the seawall.

So Mara said none of it. She drank her coffee, and complimented the stitch work after all, and carried the empty cups to the sink, and hated herself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

Complicity, she thought, rinsing the cups. That was the word. Not betrayal — betrayal required an act, a choice made against someone. This was just standing in the current and letting it carry her, knowing where it went. The first betrayal that wasn't quite a betrayal yet. There was still time for it to become something else. There was still time, and every hour she didn't speak, the thermite cases sat in their foam beds like ordnance in a bomb bay, patient as tide tables.

* * *

She went to see Vey that afternoon.

He received her in the harbor house's best room, a high-ceilinged parlor with a view of the gray water, and he looked exactly as he always looked: silver-haired, unhurried, a man whose cufflinks probably cost more than her boat. He poured tea without asking how she took it, because he'd remembered from nine years ago, and that small act of attention — that was the hook, wasn't it. That was how you kept people. You remembered how they took their tea and then you asked them to help you burn history.

"The storm's peaking tomorrow night," Vey said. "I want the final recovery run done before it lands. Everything from the car consolidated, catalogued, and crated."

"We've already consolidated it." She kept her voice level. "One question first. The client-supplied cases. They're incinerator-lined. Rated past two thousand degrees."

"Yes."

Not a flicker. Not a denial, not a deflection, not even the small mechanical pause of a man composing a lie. Just *yes*, delivered the way he'd deliver confirmation of a fuel order, and somehow that was worse than any cover story would have been. He wasn't going to insult her. That was its own kind of message: *you're smart enough to have figured it out, which means you're smart enough to understand why you'll go along.*

"The contents are historically significant," Mara said. "A ledger proving the railway ran a condemned trestle. Signatures. Names."

"I'm aware of the contents." He set down the pot. His eyes, when they met hers, were not cruel. They were tired, almost, the way a surgeon is tired. "Mara. Do you know what happens when that ledger reaches an inquiry? Sixty-year-old liabilities reopen. Pension funds that settled decades ago get clawed back. Descendants sue. Rail workers' families who took buyouts learn they were cheated, and towns that built themselves around the apology learn the apology was purchased. I have watched what documents do when they surface. They don't heal anything. They detonate."

"And my crew?"

"Your crew will be paid generously and thanked publicly for a salvage of modest historical interest. The recovered materials will be assessed, found deteriorated beyond conservation, and disposed of according to protocol. No one will ever need to know they were here." He smiled faintly. "You included. Especially you included."

"You're asking me to destroy evidence."

"I'm asking you to complete the contract." He slid a revised authorization across the table — triple the original fee, plus a completion bonus with a number that made her breath go shallow despite everything. "The tide closes the cave for years after this week. Whatever isn't resolved by Friday resolves itself. Think of it as the sea keeping its own secrets."

She looked at the authorization. She thought about Ibarra's daughter's surgery fund, and Finch's mortgage, and the boat payment, and nine years of a man remembering how she took her tea. She thought about Jonah sewing stitches in a map sleeve, holding things together with whatever was at hand.

She took the authorization. She said, "I'll brief the crew tonight," and walked out on legs that felt borrowed, and stood a long moment in the rain letting it hit her face, because it was cleaner than what she deserved.

She hadn't said yes. She also hadn't said no. She had bought herself until tonight, and she knew — with the same certainty she felt reading a failing hull — that tonight was the last night the word *complicity* would still be accurate. After tonight it became either *betrayal* or *rescue*, and the difference between them was a conversation she had to start with a woman who trusted her, in a house with Vey's man outside the document room, with a storm coming in that would close every road and every mouth.

She found Jonah on the porch, watching the rail yard, the mended map sleeve folded in her jacket pocket like something alive.

"Mara," Jonah said, without turning. "You've been circling me for two days. Either say it or stop spending my oxygen."

Mara looked at the seawall, at the two men in good coats walking their shift in the rain. Then she reached into her own pocket and put the revised authorization on the rail between them, face up, where the numbers could do their own talking.

"There's something you need to know about what Vey sent us here to do," she said. "And there's something worse you need to know about what happens Friday."
