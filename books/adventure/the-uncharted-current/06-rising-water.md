## 6. Rising Water

The barometer on the *Meridian*'s deck told the story before the sky did. Jonah had learned to read pressure the way other people read faces — her father taught her that a falling needle was a promise the sea always kept — and by the time the first swell lifted the tender against its lines, she was already counting heads on the shingle below the cliff.

Nine. They were nine. They should be eleven.

"Okonkwo and Reyes are still in the deep chamber," Mara had said an hour ago, before she went back down herself with a spare bottle. "The mail car's sealed tighter than a bank vault. She wants the ledger photographed before we move it."

Jonah had looked at the water then, and the water had looked back the way it always did at the turn of a tide — flat, patient, lying. Slack water lasted ninety minutes in the Tollgate on a normal day. She'd written that in her father's margin herself, in pencil, three summers ago, when the cave was a rumor and she was a woman with a map and no reason to use it.

But the swell coming up the coast was not a normal day.

"Everyone out of the entrance chamber," she said. "Now. Take nothing you can't carry at a run."

Finch laughed at her. "Tide's not due for—"

"Look at the water line." She pointed at the black seam on the cave wall, the kelp mark, the place where the sea had signed its name on stone. "That's where it sits on a calm day. That swell is two meters of storm surge arriving early, and when it lands, this entrance shuts like a door."

The crew moved. That was the one thing she'd give them — Vey's money had bought a crew that moved when the guide said move, even Finch, grumbling, hauling the document cases up the sloping shingle toward the high shelf where the lanterns hung. The entrance chamber was a cathedral of wet rock, and the sea came into it through a throat maybe four meters wide, and Jonah stood at the throat and watched the horizon do arithmetic.

The first swell came in like a train.

She'd spent six years riding trains, so the comparison came naturally, and it was exact: a long low rumble you felt in your teeth before you heard it, then the mass of it arriving, black water stacking itself against the cliff, shouldering into the cave mouth and slamming against the far wall hard enough to shake grit from the ceiling. The lanterns swung. The water in the throat rose a meter in a breath and did not come all the way back down.

"Okonkwo!" she shouted at the water, knowing it was useless. "Reyes!"

The second swell came four minutes later. The third came three minutes after that. The intervals were collapsing, and the throat was filling, and the flat calm of slack tide was a memory from another century.

* * *

Mara Okonkwo had been diving wrecks for nineteen years, and she knew the sound of a cave closing. It was not dramatic. It was a change in the way the water breathed — the slow tidal inhale and exhale through the entrance gone wrong, replaced by a churning, confused surge that pushed her sideways against the mail car's hull while she was clipping a case to her harness.

She put her light on Reyes, twenty meters back, working the last of the loose documents into a waterproof satchel. He gave her a thumbs-up. Young, competent, too calm — the calm of a man who hadn't yet learned what the sea could take from him.

Then her depth gauge ticked, and the water temperature dropped two degrees, and she felt the pulse of it: a big swell arriving from open water, rolling through the throat behind them, and the pressure wave it shoved ahead of itself came through the tunnel like a fist.

The silt came up all at once. Nineteen years had taught her that too — a cave that had sat sealed for seventy years held seventy years of fine gray silt on its floor, and when a pressure wave hit it, the water turned to smoke. Her light became a lamp in a fog. She found Reyes by his torch beam, grabbed his harness, and pointed up and back — the only direction that wasn't death — and they swam blind through a tunnel that was trying to become a pipe organ, the surge playing it, a low groan rolling through the rock.

The entrance chamber, when they reached it, was a wall of churning gray.

Mara hung at the edge of visibility and did the math she hated. The throat was at four meters and closing. The surface above the shingle was a chaos of backwash. A diver could punch through it, maybe — on a lucky breath, in a lucky second between swells. Two divers, with cases, in zero visibility, in a collapsing interval? She'd buried friends for trying less.

She surfaced in the one pocket of air that still existed against the high wall of the chamber, dragging Reyes with her, and the first thing she heard was Jonah Ravensworth's voice cutting through the roar, flat and certain, from the shelf above.

"Okonkwo. Can you hear me?"

"Reading you!"

"The throat's gone. Don't try it." A pause, and then, and this was the thing Mara would remember for years: no panic in it at all. "There's another way out. My father's survey. There's a chimney — a fault shaft — sixty meters back along the north wall, past the mail car. It runs up through the cliff. His notes say it vents at the top, above the high-water line."

"His notes say?" Mara shouted back. "You've never been up it?"

"I've never been in this cave. I've read the map a thousand times."

Mara looked at Reyes. Reyes looked at the gray wall of water where the entrance had been. Somewhere above, faintly, through forty feet of rock, they could hear the storm arriving.

"Well," Reyes said. "Better than the map we don't have."

* * *

The problem, as Jonah laid it out on the shelf with her father's chart weighted down by four lantern globes, was that a chimney is not a staircase. The survey showed the fault shaft as a dotted line and a question mark — her father's question mark, his small neat *?* that she'd traced with her finger so many times the paper had worn thin there. Vertical, maybe twenty meters, flooded from the tidal surge upward, venting somewhere in the cliff face or possibly not venting at all.

"We go up on air," she said. "Okonkwo leads. I've got the survey gear — the old rail line instruments. My father's kit."

Vey's expedition had come with modern dive computers and modern everything, but the crew had also hauled her father's brass and mahogany survey instruments up the shingle because Jonah had insisted, and because Vey had liked the romance of it for the photographs. Now the romance was going to keep two people alive.

"The theodolite has a plumb line and a spirit level," she said. "I can lower it down the shaft on the wire and find the true vertical. If the chimney bends, we'll know where, and which way to lean. The barometer tells me if the top is open — if there's a vent, the pressure at the surface of the water column will breathe with the swells. If it doesn't breathe, the shaft is sealed, and we're not sending anyone up it."

"And if it breathes?" Mara asked.

"Then it breathes all the way to daylight, because a sealed shaft doesn't feel the storm."

She rigged the wire to an iron mooring ring some railwayman had driven into the rock seventy years ago — the railway had been here, her father's map said, the railway had been everywhere — and paid the theodolite down into the black water on the north wall, sixty meters back, exactly where the dotted line said the chimney began. The crew gathered around the instrument in a half circle, lantern light and rain and the boom of the sea filling the entrance chamber like artillery.

Jonah watched the plumb line. She watched the spirit level bubble. She watched the barometer needle, the one her father had held, do the thing she was praying for it to do.

It breathed.

A tiny shudder, up and down, in time with the swells hammering the cliff. The shaft was open to the sky.

"Okonkwo," she said. "It's alive. Go."

* * *

They went up one at a time, Mara first, Reyes second, each on a bottle with a guide line clipped to the survey wire, and the rest of the crew on the shelf listening to the regulators hiss and then hearing nothing at all, just the storm and the water and the needle of the barometer trembling like a pulse they were all holding in their hands.

Jonah held the wire. She felt Mara's weight on it, a small rhythmic tug, meter by meter, up through the dark. Twenty meters of flooded chimney. At the top, Mara would surface into whatever the vent held — a blowhole, a crack, a throat of its own — and if the vent was underwater at high swell, if her father's question mark had been a question with a bad answer, then the tug on the wire would stop, and Jonah would feel that too.

The tug climbed. Fifteen meters. Eighteen.

It stopped.

The whole shelf went silent. Even Finch.

Then the wire went slack — not the slack of a dead line but the slack of a released one, clipped off at the top, and thirty seconds later, faint and impossible, drifting down the chimney and out of some crack in the cliff face above them, they heard a whistle. Three notes. The diver's signal. *Clear. Surface. Come up.*

Reyes went next. Jonah paid out the wire with her hands shaking and her voice steady, and when his three notes came down the shaft, she sat down on the shingle and let herself shake where only the chart could see her.

* * *

They came down off the cliff an hour later, around the headland, to the vent — a fissure wide enough for a man, breathing storm spray, in the face of the cliff above the old rail cut. Mara and Reyes stood on the rocks soaked and gray-lipped and grinning the way people grin when the sea has looked at them and let them go.

The document cases came up on the wire last, hauled hand over hand by the whole crew, and Jonah counted them twice, and then a third time, because the ledger was in the second case and the ledger was the whole reason any of them were still breathing this air.

It was Finch who said it, quietly, watching her count.

"All that's down to your map. Your old man's map."

Jonah looked at the chart in her hands — her father's chart, the only record of the Tollgate's throat, its intervals, its chimney, its question mark. The thing that had brought them in and the thing that had brought them out. The thing Vey had already asked to see twice.

She thought about the way Vey had watched her at the manifest. She thought about the barometer falling, and how early the swell had come, and whether the sea had really broken its schedule — or whether someone who read tide tables as well as she did had simply read them first, and chosen the day, and chosen it for reasons that had nothing to do with salvage.

The storm was coming ashore behind them, and the road back to town ran along the rail line, and somewhere ahead of them on that road, she knew, there would be a car with its lights on, waiting.

"Pack it deep," she said, and folded her father's chart into her coat, against her skin. "Nobody talks on the walk back. Not about the cave. Not about the ledger. Not to anyone."

She looked up at the cliff, at the small dark mouth of the vent breathing against the storm sky, and she understood the price of the day exactly. The cave would close at tomorrow's high tide and not open again for years. The chart in her coat was now the only map to it in the world.

Which meant that from this moment on, everyone who wanted what was in that cave — Vey, the railway, whoever had sent the storm's timing down the wire — would only ever want one thing from her.

And she had three nights in port to decide what it was worth.
