## 7. Three Nights in Port

The first night, Jonah told herself it was the storm.

That was a lie she could live with. The *Cormorant* had come ashore two days after they'd pulled Finch and Okada out of the flooded chimney, and it sat on the coast like a fist, driving rain sideways up the harbor road and tearing shingles off the cannery roof in long wet ribbons. No train ran in that. No train should. Even the ghost lines — especially the ghost lines — respected a storm like that.

So she stayed at the Saltbox Hotel with the rest of the crew, and she told herself: one night. Weather delay. It happened to everyone.

She unpacked nothing. She never unpacked. Her bag sat by the door with its strap coiled for lifting, the survey map rolled inside a length of PVC pipe beside it, and every time the wind slammed something loose against the window she found herself looking at that bag the way other people looked at exits.

Three nights. That was her rule. Six years of hopping dead spurs and closed coastal branches, six years of never sleeping under the same roof four times running, and the rule had held through everything — through the winter she'd ridden log flats through the Siskiyou cut, through the season she'd lived out of a caboose on a line scheduled for scrap. Three nights anywhere, and then gone before the place learned her name.

The Saltbox was learning her name.

"You want the same room again, Miss Ravensworth?" the desk clerk asked on the second morning, and the question went through her like a fishhook.

"Just until the roads clear," she said.

"Sure." He slid the key across, and there was something in his face — not suspicion exactly, but attention. The beginning of recognition. A town this size, a stranger who stayed, people started to build a story around you whether you gave them one or not.

She took the key. She hated how easily her hand took it.

* * *

By noon of the second day the storm had eased to a hard gray drizzle, and Jonah walked down to the rail yard to see what was still standing. The yard was a graveyard with pretensions: three dead sidings furred with rust, a water tower leaning like a drunk against its own legs, and the locomotive shed where the crew had stashed the equipment they'd hauled out of the cave — the dive gear, the document cases, the sealed evidence tubes Mara had insisted on cataloging twice.

Two men stood at the yard gate.

They weren't pretending to be anything. One leaned against the gatepost under an umbrella he clearly didn't need anymore; the other sat on a crate with his collar up, smoking, watching the shed door with the patience of a man being paid by the hour. When Jonah came down the gravel road they both looked at her, and neither moved, and that was the message. Not a threat. An announcement.

*We know where your things are.*

She kept walking, past them, close enough to smell the cigarette, and neither man said a word. At the shed she checked the padlock — intact, hers — and made a show of it, letting them watch her count the hasp, test the chain. Then she turned around and walked back up the hill, feeling their eyes between her shoulder blades the whole way.

Vey wasn't trying to steal the evidence. Not yet. He was trying to make sure nobody left with it.

Back at the Saltbox, she found Mara in the narrow second-floor corridor, towel around her neck, coming up from the bathhouse.

"You saw them," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"At the gate."

"And?"

"And they're not going anywhere. Neither are we, if Vey's got men on the only road out." Jonah leaned against the wall, arms crossed, and made herself say the next part plainly, because dancing around it would cost more later. "Mara. Your backer put watchers on a rail yard in a town with one road. That's not salvage money behavior."

Mara wrung out the towel, slowly, not meeting her eyes. "Vey's thorough."

"He's something." Jonah watched her. Six days underwater together, sharing air hoses and fear, and now there was this thin film between them, like oil on a tank. "When the weather breaks—"

"When the weather breaks," Mara said, "we'll talk about it."

She went into her room and shut the door, gently, which was somehow worse than slamming it.

* * *

The second night, the past arrived wearing a good coat.

Jonah was in the hotel's ground-floor parlor because it was the only room with heat that worked, sitting under a hissing radiator with a cup of coffee gone cold, working the tide tables. The cave wouldn't open again for months — the almanac said eleven weeks minimum, and that assumed no more storm swells — but she ran the numbers anyway, over and over, because running numbers was a form of motion, and motion was the only prayer she knew.

Aldous Vey came in without knocking on anything, because doors opened for him.

He was older than his photographs in the shipping journals, silver at the temples, dressed like a man who'd been caught in weather on purpose. He crossed the parlor, took the chair across from her without asking, and set a leather folder on the table between them.

"Miss Ravensworth."

"Mr. Vey."

"I wanted to speak with you before I spoke with anyone else." He folded his hands. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. "Your father worked for me."

The words landed somewhere behind her ribs and just sat there.

"That's not possible," she said. "My father drove maintenance-of-way for the Coast Range & Pacific. Thirty-one years. He never—" She stopped, because Vey had opened the folder, and inside was a photograph, old, curling at the corners, and the man in it was unmistakably her father, thirty years younger, standing on a trestle deck in a hard hat, and beside him, arm raised mid-gesture like a man selling the horizon, stood a much younger Aldous Vey.

"Coast Range & Pacific was mine," Vey said quietly. "You knew that. Everyone on this coast knows the Vey family built half those lines. What you don't know is that Elias Ravensworth reported to me directly for the last two years of his life. Survey work. Condition assessments." He tapped the photograph. "He was the best eyes I ever employed. And he was the one who told me the Meridian trestle was failing."

Jonah's hands had gone flat on the table without her deciding to do it. "Then he tried to stop the mail run."

"He tried to stop the mail run," Vey agreed. "And I overruled him. And when the car went into the water with nine men aboard, I did not report the failure. I reported a rogue wave. I closed the spur, I buried the inquiry, and I paid Elias Ravensworth to sign a statement saying he'd inspected the trestle and found it sound." His eyes were steady, and that was the worst part — no cruelty in them, just arithmetic. "He signed it as a witness to my inspection. Not to the truth. I've always told myself that distinction mattered to him."

The radiator hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard popped with the cold.

"You're telling me this why?" Jonah said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Why confess to me, in a hotel parlor, in front of God and the desk clerk?"

"Because you have the ledger, and I have a choice to make, and I'd rather you heard it from me than reconstructed it from paper." He leaned forward. "Here is what I'm offering, and I'll only offer once. The ledger and everything recovered from that car comes to me. In exchange: the debt on your name is cleared. All of it. Every creditor from here to Portland. Plus passage anywhere, and a stake to start over. You could stop running tonight, Miss Ravensworth. Isn't that what you want? To stop?"

And there it was — the thing she'd been circling for six years, laid out on a table between cold coffee and a lie about a bridge. Freedom, priced. He'd done his homework. He knew about the debt. Of course he did; he'd probably known since before he hired her, since the moment her father's handwriting on that old survey chart had made her say yes to a job she should have walked away from.

Her father's handwriting. Her father's signature. Her father, bought and silenced, working all along for the man now offering to buy her.

"No," Jonah said.

Vey didn't flinch. "You haven't heard the amount."

"It's not an amount." She stood, and her legs were steadier than she deserved. "My father spent the last years of his life carrying what you made him carry, and it killed him slow, and you think the cure is money." She picked up her tide tables. "You built the line. You let it fall. You drowned nine men and called it weather. And now you're going to sit there while I decide who my loyalty protects — and it isn't you, and it isn't the creditors, and it sure as hell isn't the version of my father you're selling me."

For a long moment Vey simply looked at her, and she saw something move behind the arithmetic — not respect, exactly. Recalculation.

"Eleven weeks," he said, rising, straightening his coat. "Until the tide opens that cave again. I don't need eleven weeks. Think about what you can live with, Miss Ravensworth. Most people can live with more than they believe."

He left the folder on the table. The photograph of her father on the trestle, young and whole, arm raised toward a future that was already rotting underneath him.

Jonah sat back down and stared at it for a long time, and for the first time in six years she understood that running hadn't been keeping the past away. It had been keeping her too busy to read it.

* * *

The third night, she knocked on Mara's door.

It was past midnight. The storm had finally died to a whisper, and the whole hotel creaked like a ship coming off heavy seas. Mara opened the door already dressed, already awake, and something in her face said she'd been waiting.

"You talked to him," Mara said.

"Last night. He offered to buy the ledger and erase my debts." Jonah stepped inside. The room was stripped clean except for a seabag and a chart case, and spread across the bed, in neat rows, were copies — pages photographed in the cave's work light, the ledger, the cover-up order, the manifest with nine names and her father's signature at the bottom like a wound. "How long have you known what he really sent us in there for?"

Mara held her gaze. "Since before we dove."

"Say it."

"Vey's instructions were explicit. Recover the mail car's contents, verify condition, and prepare them for destruction on site. The crew were to be told it was archival salvage." Mara's voice was flat, diver-calm, the voice she used below forty feet. "I followed the first two orders. I stalled on the third. Then the chimney flooded and the decision got taken out of everyone's hands."

Jonah looked at the rows of paper. Nine names. Her father's hand. Six years of running, and the truth had been sitting in a flooded rock the whole time, waiting on a tide.

"Finch knows," Mara said. "I saw him copying pages two nights ago. He's been making calls from the depot office whenever he thinks no one's watching."

"Selling to Vey?"

"Selling to whoever pays. There's a difference, but it won't matter to us." Mara began stacking the pages, squaring their edges with the precision of a woman building something that had to hold weight. "Weather breaks tomorrow. Vey's men are on the road and the yard, but they're not on the tracks — nobody watches track that's scheduled for demolition. There's a spur out of here, north along the bluffs, abandoned since '61. Your kind of line."

"The ghost line," Jonah said.

"The ghost line runs tomorrow night, or it never runs." Mara met her eyes. "But Jonah — if we take that evidence out of this town, there's no putting it back. Vey doesn't lose gracefully. Whatever happens on those tracks, we don't get to be bystanders anymore."

Outside, far off, a locomotive whistle sounded through the clearing dark — long, low, testing itself — and Jonah felt something in her chest answer it, steady and terrible, like a tide turning.

"Get the crew ready," she said. "Tomorrow night, we find out who's on board."
