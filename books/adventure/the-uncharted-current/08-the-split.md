## 8. The Split

Mara Okonkwo had spent eleven years in salvage learning one rule above all others: you never lie to your crew about the water. You could lie to them about the pay, the client, the odds — they'd forgive all of that. But the water told its own truth eventually, and if you'd been dishonest about it, you drowned alone.

She sat in the back room of the Harbormaster's Arms with Vey's orders open on her knee, and understood that she had been lying about the water since the day she signed his contract.

The orders were typed on a single sheet, no letterhead, no signature beyond a set of initials that might as well have been a fingerprint: *A.V.* The instructions were precise in the way only cowardice is precise.

*On recovery of materials from the tollgate site, all documents are to be destroyed by fire at first opportunity. Photographic record of destruction is required. Crew is not to be informed of the nature of the materials recovered. Any crew member who views or removes said materials is to be reported immediately upon return.*

*Reported.* Not *dealt with*. Vey was careful like that. He let the word do its own work.

Mara had read it four times now, and each time her eyes went to the same line — *crew is not to be informed* — and each time she heard it in Vey's voice, smooth as bilge oil: *You're the lead, Ms. Okonkwo. Leads carry weight so their people don't have to.*

She had told herself it was standard confidentiality. Salvage clients were paranoid; half of them were sitting on something illegal and all of them knew it. She had told herself the sealed mail car was just cargo, that the ledgers Jonah had pulled from the flooded compartment were just paper, that the way Vey's man Reyes had gone pale when Jonah spread them out on the galley table was just a man tired from diving.

She had told herself all of it because the money was real, and because a crew of six trusted her, and because it is astonishing how much a person can not-see when seeing would cost her everything.

Now Finch was dead asleep in the next room, snoring like a man with no secrets, and the storm had pinned them all in Port Calloway for a third night, and Mara was holding a piece of paper that made her crew expendable.

Jonah Ravensworth came down the stairs without a sound. That was the thing about her — a woman built entirely of quiet movement, like she'd learned early that being heard was dangerous. She crossed the room, glanced at the paper in Mara's hands, and stopped.

"You went through my kit," Mara said. It wasn't an accusation. It was an acknowledgment.

"Your kit was in my boat." Jonah sat across from her, elbows on knees. Up close her face had the particular grayness of someone who hadn't slept properly in years and had stopped pretending otherwise. "I wasn't looking for anything. I found it anyway."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Rain worked at the windows like it had a grudge.

"He sent us in blind," Jonah said finally. "He knew what was down there. He knew what my father's name was doing on that manifest. And he sent divers who can't read a tide table into a cave to burn it."

"Yes."

"And you knew."

"I suspected." Mara heard how thin it sounded and hated it. "I told myself I didn't know. There's a difference, on paper."

"On paper," Jonah agreed. Her voice didn't rise. That was worse than if it had. "Finch has been running messages to the rail yard office every morning. Did you know that too?"

Mara's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "No."

"Well." Jonah leaned back. "One of us is honest, then."

* * *

They woke Finch at five in the morning, which was cruel, but Mara wanted him groggy. Groggy men told the truth before they'd decided which truth to tell.

He came down rubbing his eyes, saw the two women sitting shoulder to shoulder at the table, saw the typed sheet between them, and Mara watched the sleep fall off his face like a mask coming loose.

"That's private," he said.

"It's addressed to me," Mara said. "Sit down, Finch."

He didn't sit. He stood by the door instead, which told her everything — a man who positions himself near exits is a man planning to use them. "Vey pays me direct," he said. "Separate from the crew cut. Has since the dock briefing. It's not — look, it's not personal, it's just insurance. His word. Insurance."

"For what?"

"For whoever cracks first." Finch's jaw worked. "And it cracked first, didn't it? You two, sitting here at dawn with the evidence laid out like a wake. I'm supposed to report this. Tonight. Reyes already flagged the ledger copies going missing from the strongbox."

Jonah didn't move. "You've seen what's in those ledgers."

"I've seen what's in it for me." But his eyes flicked away when he said it, and Mara had spent enough years reading men underwater to know what that flick meant. He'd looked at the documents. Of course he had. Everyone had. Forty-one names on a disaster list, and a railway that had run a rotten trestle knowing it would fail, and a witness named Ravensworth whose signature proved he'd tried to stop it and been buried along with the mail car's history.

Nobody looked at that and stayed clean. Finch just hadn't figured out yet which side of himself he lived on.

"The last ghost line runs tomorrow night," Jonah said quietly. "The spur out of Calloway, scheduled for demolition behind it. Vey thinks we don't know the schedule changed. We split the evidence three ways — originals with me, certified copies with Mara for the inquiry, photographs everywhere. We torch a decoy set in the yard furnace where Reyes can watch it burn, and we're on that train before anyone counts what's ash and what isn't."

Finch laughed. It was an ugly sound. "And Vey? Vey doesn't lose, Ravensworth. Men like him don't even play. They just keep the board."

"Then we take the board off the table," Jonah said. "We make the truth public before he can buy it back. Every paper, every name, printed under my father's byline. He can't silence a story that's already out."

"You think ink protects anybody?" Finch's voice cracked upward. "You think Vey cares about *printed*? He'll care about who printed it. He'll care about the six people on that train, and he'll start with whoever talked." He pointed at Mara, hand shaking slightly. "She's supposed to report you. Both of you. That's the job. That's what the extra pay was *for*, and everybody in this room knows the crew are witnesses, and witnesses on Vey's jobs have a way of—"

"Stop," Mara said.

Finch stopped. Maybe because her voice had the same tone she used underwater, flat and absolute, the tone that meant the situation had passed beyond argument.

"You're right about one thing," she said. "The crew are witnesses. So am I. And I'm telling you now, in front of both of you, what I should have told everyone in the galley the night we surfaced: Vey hired us to destroy evidence that forty-one people died carrying. If we help him, we're not witnesses anymore. We're the second wreck."

The rain filled the silence.

Finch looked at the typed sheet for a long time. When he raised his head, something had settled in his face — not agreement, nothing so useful as that. Calculation.

"I need to think," he said.

"You've thought," Jonah said. "Since the dock briefing, you've thought. What you need is to decide."

"And if I decide wrong?" He was looking at Mara when he asked it. Asking her, specifically. *Which way do I jump, boss? Give me the word and I'll be loyal to it.*

It would have been easy. One sentence — *stay with me, Finch, and I'll protect you* — and she'd have bought another few hours of his uncertainty. Maybe that was all any of them needed: hours.

But she had lied about the water once already this expedition, and the water always collected.

"I can't choose for you," Mara said. "That's the whole point of what Vey did — he chose for all of us and called it employment. Decide on your own. But decide before the train leaves, because after that there's no deciding. There's only what you did."

Finch stood there a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, like a man closing a ledger of his own, and went up the stairs without looking back.

* * *

Mara waited until his door closed overhead before she spoke.

"He'll sell us."

"Maybe." Jonah was gathering the documents back into their oilcloth, her hands steady, her face doing something complicated around the edges. "Or maybe he's downstairs in six hours telling us he's in. People aren't tide tables. You can't read them off a chart."

"You can read Finch. I've dived with him two years. He flinches toward money the way most men flinch from cold." Mara rubbed her eyes. "Here's what we do regardless. We assume he talks. We build the plan so that even if Reyes knows everything by noon, the split still works — decoy burns whether or not the real papers move, train leaves whether or not the decoy fools anyone. No single point of failure except us."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then it goes wrong at speed, on a train, in a storm, on a line scheduled for demolition." Mara almost smiled. "Which, if I understand your history, is roughly your natural habitat."

Jonah didn't smile back. She held the oilcloth bundle against her chest the way other people held children.

"My father signed that cover order as a witness," she said. "Trying to stop it. And the railway buried his name so deep that his own daughter spent six years thinking he died disgraced." She looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were dry and terrible. "Vey knew that. He read the manifest before we ever dove. He hired the daughter of the man he helped bury to guide him back to the burial site. That's not business. That's cruelty as a hobby."

"Then we finish it," Mara said. "Tomorrow night. Ghost line. Everything splits, everything moves, nobody waits to be reported."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the creak of Finch's floor. Back and forth. Back and forth. A man pacing out the dimensions of whatever he was becoming.

Mara listened to it and felt the old diver's certainty settle over her, cold and clear: somewhere above them, right now, Finch was writing the message. Or he wasn't. Either way, by morning the rail yard would know, or it wouldn't, and the only thing left to control was how fast they moved when the answer came.

Outside, the storm shifted direction, and far off down the coast, a locomotive that officially did not exist began its night's work in a shed that officially was not there.

Twenty-nine hours until the last ghost line ran.

Mara started counting.
