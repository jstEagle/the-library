## 9. The Ghost Line Runs

The rain came sideways off the water, and the old Porter engine answered it with a sound like a man clearing his throat after forty years of silence.

Jonah stood in the cab beside Finch's replacement — a kid named Ruiz who'd signed on for the ghost line run because he'd never been told what he was signing on for. She'd fixed that in the last hour, quietly, and he hadn't quit. That was something. That was maybe everything.

Behind the locomotive: one flatcar carrying the mail car's documents sealed in oilcloth and a crate of survey equipment that had once belonged to her father. Behind the flatcar: nothing. They'd left the passenger coach at the yard, uncoupled in the dark, because Vey's men were watching the rail line south of town and a full train was a target and an empty train was a rumor.

"Line's scheduled for demolition tonight," Ruiz said, shouting over the rain. "You sure about this?"

"No," Jonah said. "Open her up."

The throttle went down and the ghost line ran.

It wasn't really a line anymore. It was a memory of a line, six miles of rail that the Coast & Northern had abandoned in '61 and never quite gotten around to tearing up, running along the bluff above the sound through country that had forgotten trains were ever possible. Salmonberry swallowed the ballast. The ties rotted soft under the wheels. Every quarter mile there was a culvert or a cut or a curve where the map in Jonah's head said *slow* and her hands said *faster* because behind them, back at the yard, headlights were swinging onto the service road — three vehicles, maybe four, moving parallel to the track the way Vey's men had moved all week, patient as tide tables.

Mara crouched in the lee of the tender, checking straps on the document crate. Rain sheeted off her hood. "Vey's not following us," she said. "He's following the *line*. He knows where we have to go."

"Then he knows I know," Jonah said.

"That's supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be true."

The storm hit the coast like a debt collector. Wind drove the rain horizontal, and somewhere inland the thunder rolled long and low, and ahead of them the track curved out along the bluff toward the one structure on six miles of dead rail that still stood whole enough to cross.

Carrow Trestle.

Two hundred feet of timber and iron spanning the Carrow Gorge, built in 1949, condemned in 1953, kept standing anyway because demolition cost money and nobody official wanted to look too hard at why it needed demolishing. Her father had surveyed it eleven times in two years. His notes called its condition *marginal* in ink so careful it looked apologetic. The last entry in his field book, dated nine days before the cave, before the mail car, before everything, read: *They will not close the line. Ask me again and I will say so publicly.*

Nine days later his boat was found swamped off Point Alder, and the railway's report said *tragic accident, experienced mariner, sudden squall*, and Jonah had spent six years running from the shape of those words.

Now she was driving a stolen train straight at the place they'd been written.

"Carrow's four minutes out," she called back. "When we stop—"

"*If* we stop," Mara said.

"When we stop, you take the crew up the north bank trail. Documents stay with me."

"The hell they do. Vey wants them burned, Jonah. If you're alone on that trestle with him—"

"He won't burn them." Jonah watched the gorge open up ahead, black and breathing mist. "Burning them proves they existed. He needs them *discredited*. Lost. An accident on a condemned bridge, tragic, experienced guide, sudden squall." She heard her own voice go flat and cold and recognized her father's handwriting in it. "He's done it before."

Mara was quiet for three rail joints. Then: "I'm staying with you."

"You're getting the crew out."

"I'm *staying with you*, Ravensworth. You've spent six years making sure no one ever had to choose between you and the truth. Tonight somebody finally gets to make the wrong choice on purpose."

Jonah didn't answer. There wasn't time. The trestle came out of the rain like a sentence she'd been reading her whole life without understanding.

* * *

She brought the Porter onto the span at walking speed, because the map in her head — the map she would surrender tomorrow, the map that had been her whole life for six years — said the third span from the north end had shifted two inches since her father's last survey, and even a dying engine deserved better than trust.

Halfway across, she stopped her.

The gorge roared below, swollen black with stormwater, and the trestle groaned around them like a ship in weather, and Jonah set the brake and stepped down into the rain with the ledger under her coat and felt, for the first time in six years, absolutely still.

Not trapped. Not caught. *Still.* There was a difference, and the difference was that she had chosen the coordinates.

The headlights came up the service road on the south side within minutes — Vey's men, fanning out along the abutment, and then a single figure walking the ties toward her through the rain without a flashlight, because Aldous Vey had never needed light to find what he wanted.

He stopped ten feet away. Older than his photographs. A man built like a ledger himself — every column balanced, nothing wasted.

"Miss Ravensworth." Even shouted, his voice didn't rise. It simply arrived. "You're standing on a bridge scheduled to come down tonight."

"So are you."

"I came to offer you a ride home." He glanced past her at the engine, the flatcar, Mara descending from the tender with a pry bar held casually like a conversation piece. "The documents are railroad property. Pre-inquiry material, subject to chain-of-custody review. You've taken possession of stolen assets during a severe weather event. I'm prepared to characterize your involvement as coerced. Everyone walks away. Everyone is compensated. This is the generous version, Miss Ravensworth. I don't travel with the other version."

"My father worked for you."

Something moved behind Vey's eyes — not surprise. Recognition. The small satisfaction of a man watching a sum finally total correctly.

"Your father," Vey said, "was the best surveyor the Coast & Northern ever employed, and the most expensive mistake I ever approved. He found the mail car in '53. He documented the trestle. He wrote to the board. And when the board declined to act, he told me he was going public." Vey took a step closer, rain streaming off his jaw. "I didn't kill him, Miss Ravensworth. I want that understood. I simply told him what would happen to his daughter's future if he did. And he chose the water rather than choose between us. So whatever you think you're settling tonight — settle it accurately."

Jonah pulled the ledger from inside her coat. Held it out between them, rain hammering the oilcloth.

"Then read it accurately," she said. "Page forty-one. Witness signature, cover-up order, March 1953. He didn't stay silent, Vey. He signed his name to the truth and someone buried the paper instead of the man — until my cave opened it back up. He wasn't disgraced and he wasn't a coward. He was a witness. And witnesses get published."

"And you'll die proving it," Vey said, "on a bridge that comes down in—" 

He stopped. Because he heard it too.

Below the storm, below the gorge roar, a deeper sound: engines. Not cars. Heavy equipment, coming up the south service road, and along the north bank a flare arced up red against the rain — the demolition crew, early, working ahead of the weather, torches already moving along the north abutment spans.

"They're cutting the north end first," Mara said, suddenly beside Jonah, voice tight. "Jonah. The north end. We're on the *south* side of the shift."

The trestle shuddered. Somewhere above them a span joint screamed, and the whole structure leaned its argument into the wind.

Vey looked at the ledger. Looked at the burning north end. And for one unguarded moment, Aldous Vey looked like a man doing arithmetic that wouldn't balance.

"You can't publish that," he said. "You're a drifter with a stolen train and no byline. No court will—"

"She won't have to," Mara said, and pulled the copy from her jacket — the duplicate, the split, Finch's betrayal bought and paid for and already mailed from the Port Hardy depot two hours ago, registered post, addressed to the provincial inquiry. "It's already gone. You're not chasing evidence anymore, Mr. Vey. You're chasing a *story*, and stories move faster than you do."

The north span let go.

It didn't collapse so much as *leave* — two hundred tons of timber folding into the gorge with a sound like the world's largest door closing, and the shockwave ran up the remaining spans under their feet, and Jonah grabbed Mara's arm and shouted the only order that mattered:

"South! Run the engine south — NOW!"

Ruiz had the Porter reversing before she finished the word. They ran the ties alongside it, Vey's men hauling their boss back off the track, and the trestle came apart span by span behind them like a sentence being erased — but the south abutment held, and the engine cleared solid ground as the last span behind them dropped into the flood and the Carrow Trestle ceased, after seventy years of standing witness, to exist.

Jonah stood in the rain on the south bank, soaked, empty-handed except for the ledger, and watched the place where her father's story had ended disappear into the water.

It didn't feel like an ending.

It felt like a door opening.

"Port Hardy depot," she said to Ruiz, climbing back into the cab. "Registered mail travels by the morning coastal freight. Which means by sunrise, the inquiry has page forty-one — and by noon, every paper on the coast has the rest."

Mara dropped into the cab beside her, breathless, grinning despite everything. "And you? What does Jonah Ravensworth do at noon?"

Jonah looked back once at the gorge, at the flood, at the wrecked and burning dark where a bridge used to be.

"For the first time in six years," she said, and put the throttle down, and the ghost line carried them south through the storm toward morning, "I'm going to be exactly where I said I'd be."

But twenty miles south, at the Port Hardy yard, the night agent was already running up the platform with a lantern, waving both arms at a train that had no business existing — because the coastal freight had come through early, ahead of the storm, and it was carrying more than mail.

It was carrying a passenger who had read page forty-one.

And she was waiting for them.
