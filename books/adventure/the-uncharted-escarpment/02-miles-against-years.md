## 2. Miles Against Years

The rail-yard tavern in Corven's Bend had a name once, painted on a sign that now hung by one chain and read only THE ______, the letters long since peeled by wind off the flats. Sable liked it for that. A place that had given up its name had given up pretending, and he trusted things that had stopped pretending. He took the corner table by the window that faced the yard, ordered a beer he wouldn't finish, and laid his belongings out on the scarred wood like a man preparing for surgery: the stone fragment, wrapped in his spare shirt; Tomas's map, folded along creases gone soft as cloth from twelve years of handling; and his own field notebook, which he kept closed, because opening it meant reading what he'd written the day everything ended.

Outside, a switching engine moved freight cars with the patience of something that had done nothing else for a century. Iron sang against iron. The sound had been the soundtrack of his whole adult life, and he had never decided whether it was a comfort or a sentence.

He unwrapped the fragment.

It was smaller than his fist, a slab of grey limestone sheared clean on three sides, and across its face ran the marks: the double-baseline with the offset tick, the numeral 7 struck through and rewritten, the small hooked arrow that meant *proceed against the grain of the slope*. Surveyor's shorthand. But not surveyor's shorthand the way the Corps taught it, with its tidy federal conventions. This was a private dialect, invented by two boys with a surveyor's chain and a stolen level, working the coulees behind their father's farm, marking trees and fence posts and the graves of dogs. Tomas had drawn the hooked arrow wrong on purpose, once, and when Sable corrected him, Tomas had said, *Wrong is just a signature nobody else can read.* And then he'd kept drawing it wrong for the next thirty years, through his licensing exams, through his professional maps, through the last set of stakes he ever set.

The marks on the fragment were Tomas's. Not similar. His.

Sable turned the stone over. On the reverse, cut shallow but sure, was a string of characters he recognized with a feeling like a hook set behind his sternum: *K—7 / 114*. Kilometer post seven, chainage one-fourteen. He knew that numbering system. He had helped build it. The Kestrel Line.

He set the stone down and pressed both palms flat against the table until the wood's cold climbed into his wrists. Around him the tavern went on with its small noises — the tapman's rag squeaking the counter, a domino clacking, the radiator ticking as the afternoon cooled — and he let all of it recede, because what was coming now had to come alone, the way it always did.

* * *

The mountain had been beautiful that morning. That was the detail he could never make anyone understand, the detail that made listeners shift in their chairs: that the day the ridge came down was the finest day of the season, air like cut glass, the snowpack gleaming above the Kestrel work camp so white it looked deliberate, arranged, as if the range itself had set a table.

They were re-surveying the bench above camp because the original figures had come back inconsistent — a discrepancy of centimeters, nothing, the kind of thing that gets noted and filed and forgotten. Sable was on the theodolite. Tomas was walking the line below him with the stadia rod, and their father's hooked arrow was inked on the back of Tomas's left glove, a joke between them that had survived into a federal survey, and Sable remembered looking at that glove through the scope and feeling, absurdly, at home.

The benchmark reduction was his job. He'd done it a hundred times. But he was tired — they were all tired, three weeks of weather holds — and there was a transcription, a carried digit, a sign error in the vertical offset that he would not discover until later, much later, when discovery was a wound instead of a correction. The error said the slope was stable. The error said: *proceed.*

He signed the reduction at 11:40. The foreman radioed the camp at noon that the bench was cleared for the blasting crew to move up.

At 12:17 the slope above the old camp let go.

He heard it before he understood it — a sound like the range clearing its throat, deep and wrong — and then the white came down the coulee and he was running and Tomas was not running, Tomas was standing at the radio shack waving both arms at the men on the bench, waving them back off the route, and Sable screamed his name into a wall of moving snow and the world went white and then silent and then, minutes or hours later, white again, and he was digging with his hands.

They found eleven men. Tomas was among the last, at the radio shack, his arms still raised toward the bench, the left glove torn away and never recovered. The official report — Sable's own figures, uncorrected, entered into evidence — placed the failure as an act of God, an unforeseen slip plane, weather. The report also noted that the route survey had been supervised by A. Marchetti, and that the blasting authorization had been signed on the basis of his reduction, and a second line, in a clerk's careful hand, noted that foreman Emil Voss had ordered the crew up.

Emil Voss took the blame the way a mountain takes weather. He said nothing at the inquiry. He lost his ticket, his pension, and within two years, quietly, his health, and he died in a valley town with the avalanche's name attached to his own. His son Renn had been seventeen.

And Sable had taken the other thing — the thing nobody offered and nobody named. He had taken the error, folded it small, and buried it under twelve years of closed lines and cutoff surveys, mapping railways nobody would ever build, measuring distances because distance was the one honest thing left to him. *Miles against years.* That was the phrase he used, in his own notebook, in the privacy of his own shorthand. He measured miles so he wouldn't have to measure anything else.

* * *

The beer had gone flat and warm. He drank half of it anyway, for the ritual, and spread Tomas's map across the table, weighting the corners with his tin cup, the stone, his knife.

The map was the last one Tomas had drafted before the Kestrel survey — a working sheet of the escarpment country, annotated in that same private dialect. Sable had studied it a thousand times and seen only a dead man's tidy hand. Tonight he saw it. Tonight the fragment sat on the map like a key set into its lock.

The hooked arrow on the map pointed up a coulee on the seaward flank of the Uncharted Escarpment, and beside it, in Tomas's struck-through 7, was the notation *K—7 / 114*. The same chainage. The same wrongness. And below it, in pencil gone faint with handling, a line Sable had read a hundred times without ever reading: *Tide gate. Below the old cutoff. Only at the sea's lowest hand.*

A tide gate. The cave he had stood inside that morning, with cold brine still dripping from its roof, exposed by the lowest tide the coast had logged in nine years. Tomas had known it was there. Tomas had marked it, surveyed it, left his signature inside it — and then had gone to the Kestrel Line and died, and the cave had waited under the sea for twelve years like a letter in a dead letter office.

Sable's hands were not steady. He watched them, almost with detachment, as they took up his pencil and began to work.

Kilometer post seven of the Kestrel Line lay beyond the escarpment's spine, on the abandoned seaward leg — track pulled up in the fifties, tunnel portals blasted shut, the whole corridor erased from the operating maps and closed by order of the valley towns, who did not want the mountain's history reopened any more than Sable did. There was no road. There was no trail. There was exactly one way through, and it was the way the map's margin noted in Tomas's hand: the glacier route, the ice corridor between the escarpment's shoulders, passable in the narrow season between thaw and calving — a window that the glacial service bulletins in the tavern's rack, which Sable had read that morning without knowing why, said would close within the month. Early calving, the bulletin said. Unstable year.

He sat back. The switching engine outside gave a long sigh of brakes and fell silent, and in the quiet he could hear his own pulse, and beneath it, faint and persistent, something he had not heard in twelve years: not grief exactly. Grief had been his furniture for so long he no longer felt it as feeling. This was different. This was the old current, the surveyor's current, the thing that had made two boys walk the coulees with a chain and a stolen level — the pull of an unknown distance that demanded to be measured.

Tomas had left him a mark. His brother, who had died standing between the mountain and other men, had left a mark in a cave under the sea, on a chainage of a closed line, and had keyed it to a tide so rare it came once in a decade. That was not sentiment. That was a survey. Tomas had *meant* something by it, and Sable Marchetti had spent twelve years refusing to mean anything, and the two facts collided in him now with the force of the avalanche itself.

He thought of the reasons not to go, and they were the usual reasons: the season, the closure, the danger, the cost. He thought of the real reason, the one under all of them, and it was this: if he went, he might find out what his brother had wanted him to know. And if what Tomas wanted him to know had anything to do with the Kestrel reduction — with the carried digit, with the sign error, with the eleven men and the glove and the raised arms — then going would mean unburying the thing he had spent twelve years burying.

He looked at the stone fragment in the lamplight, at the wrong-drawn arrow, *wrong is just a signature nobody else can read.*

"Then read it," he said aloud, to the empty table, to the dead.

He closed the map along its soft creases. He pocketed the fragment. And he understood, with the clarity that comes only after a decision is already made, that the route ran through country no one crossed alone — that he would need permits he didn't have, gear he couldn't carry, and ice knowledge that lived in other people's heads. He also understood, with the same clarity, that there was exactly one person in Corven's Bend who had been asking the tavern keeper, all afternoon, in a foreign accent and an unhurried voice, about the escarpment's ice cores.

She sat two tables away, a woman with a field case at her feet and a topographic sheet spread beside her plate, and she had been there long enough, he now realized, to have seen everything he'd laid out on his own table.

She looked up. Their eyes met.

Sable did not move to cover the map. Some part of him — the part that had been numb for twelve years and had cracked open that morning on a tidal flat — understood that the hiding was over, one way or another. The only question left was the price.
