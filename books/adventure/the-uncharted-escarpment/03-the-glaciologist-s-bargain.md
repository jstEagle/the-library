## 3. The Glaciologist's Bargain

Ines Okafor had learned to read a room by its maps.

The tavern in Kestrel Junction smelled of creosote and spilled porter, and its walls were papered with the dead language of the range — survey plats, elevation charts, the ghost-ink of lines that had carried coal and timber and men before the mountain closed its accounts. She had been nursing the same tea for two hours, watching the window fog and clear, fog and clear, like a lung deciding whether to breathe. Her ice cores were still in crates in the freight shed. Her permits were still a rumor. Her funding was still a thing she did not say out loud.

And in the corner booth, the man she'd come to find was doing something forbidden.

She noticed it the way she noticed everything now — glaciologically, by change. For an hour he had been just another shape in the smoke, a broad-shouldered surveyor's frame drinking alone with the economy of a man who'd learned not to waste anything, including motion. But then he'd opened a leather roll on the table, and the bartender had looked over, and looked away again, and Ines understood that what lay on that table was not meant for public light.

She carried her tea across the room and sat down across from him without asking.

He looked up. His eyes were the color of slate after rain, and they did the thing she'd been warned about in three separate letters from three separate people: they measured her. Not her face. Her mass, her center of gravity, her probable load. Sable Marchetti, she thought. He surveys people the way he surveys slopes.

"You're sitting in my light," he said.

"You're reading a map of the Kestrel Line in a public house." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used with drill crews before telling them the ice was going to fight back. "Either you're brave, or you're careless, or you've decided it doesn't matter anymore. I've spent four days trying to guess which."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. He turned the map face down with two fingers, unhurried, the way a man closes a door he already knows is unlocked.

"Dr. Okafor," he said. "The glaciologist."

"You know my name."

"I know your crates. Forty-one of them, stenciled with a university I've never heard of, sitting in a freight shed that charges by the week." He took a slow pull of his porter. "Ice cores. The escarpment. You want the pre-calving stratigraphy before the face collapses and rewrites twelve years of record."

It was her turn to recalculate. "That's a precise summary for a railroad man."

"I read the freight manifests. It's a habit." He set the glass down. "The question is what you're doing in my light, Doctor."

* * *

She had rehearsed this conversation on the train north, in the dark of a sleeping berth, watching her own reflection ghost over the passing pines. She had rehearsed it because she was good at rehearsing — because she had spent eleven years learning that science in this country ran on permission, and permission ran on money, and money ran on families, and the family paying for her expedition had a name that opened doors and closed mouths in equal measure.

Voss.

She had not yet said it to anyone in the valley. She had let the university's letterhead do the talking, let the crates do the rest, and told herself — the way she told herself every season — that the source of a thing did not change its truth. Cores were cores. Data was data. The ice did not care who signed the checks.

But the ice was three hundred miles north and this tavern was here, and the man across from her was the only living soul who knew the closed routes over the Uncharted Escarpment, and the calving season would begin in weeks, and after it began, the glacier route would seal like a wound closing over a splinter — and her splinter was a column of ice, four hundred meters of undisturbed accumulation, that would tell her whether the escarpment's slope had been failing before the avalanche or only after. Whether the mountain had been dying on its own schedule, or whether a man's arithmetic had killed it early.

She had not come all this way to be stopped by a bartender's glance.

"I need a route," she said. "The old Kestrel traverse. Over the shoulder of the escarpment to the accumulation basin. Before the season closes."

"Nobody crosses the escarpment."

"Nobody has a reason to. I have one." She reached into her coat and laid a folded paper on the table, on top of his face-down map. "Survey permits. Coring permits. Wilderness transit for three, signed by the regional office. They took eleven weeks and two hundred pounds of correspondence to get, and they are worthless without a guide who knows where the crevasses sleep."

He didn't touch the paper. He looked at it the way he'd looked at her — measuring.

"And in exchange," he said slowly, "you want my route knowledge. Which I have. And my company on the ice. Which you also want, though you haven't said so."

"I want the man who surveyed those routes. The routes are in your head, but the head comes attached."

He almost smiled. It was a small, dry movement, like a crack forming in lake ice — visible, audible if you listened, promising nothing good. "Flattery by way of anatomy."

"Accuracy by way of logistics." She folded her hands. "I've made inquiries, Mr. Marchetti. You've spent twelve years walking closed lines. You know every cut, every culvert, every stretch of track the mountain has been slowly swallowing. You are, by the testimony of three stationmasters and a very drunk bridge inspector, the only person alive who could get a coring rig to that basin and back. And I am, by the testimony of my own stubbornness, the only person offering you permits to do it legally."

The tavern noise seemed to recede around them — the clink of glasses, the hiss of the stove, a card game going wrong in the back room. He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the wood, thinking, and she watched the thinking happen in his hands before it reached his face.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire the guide everyone else has refused to hire, and we die on the ice in an interesting way, and my cores stay in their crates, and you go back to drinking alone with maps you shouldn't have." She paused. "I'd rather not. I've read the casualty reports from that crossing. I know what the interesting way looks like."

* * *

What she did not say — what she had folded up small and sewn into the lining of herself, where she kept all the things that made her hands unsteady — was the name on the second page of her funding letter.

*The Voss Family Trust, in memory of Aldous Voss, 1851–[the year of the slide].*

She had read that line forty times. She had read it on the train with the window rattling, read it in freight sheds and station hotels, and every reading it grew heavier, the way a core sample grows heavier the deeper the drill bites. Aldous Voss had died in the avalanche. Aldous Voss's death had been blamed — she had learned this in the archives, in the margins of the official report, in the careful passive voice of men protecting other men — on a guide's judgment. On a Voss, in fact. Renn Voss, the son, the other survivor, the man whose name still tasted of ash in the valley's mouth.

The Trust wanted her cores. That was the arrangement beneath the arrangement. The Trust wanted the ice to say something — she did not know exactly what, and she had stopped asking, because the answers made the money sound like a noose — and in exchange the Trust had bought her permits, her crates, her season. She told herself the ice would say what it would say, regardless of who was listening. She told herself that a scientist who refused tainted money was a scientist with no expedition. She told herself these things in the firm, clean voice she used on drill crews, and mostly the voice worked.

It was not working now. Now, across the table, the man whose survey reports had buried the Voss name was studying her permits for the fine print, and she could feel the omission sitting between them like a third drink neither of them had ordered.

He would find out. On the ice, with three hundred miles of nothing, he would find out. And she had decided — had decided on the train, in the dark, in the honest hour when the reflection in the window is more truthful than the person sitting in the berth — that she could live with that. That the cores mattered more than the manner of their taking. That everyone on this mountain was using everyone else, and the only sin was pretending otherwise.

"You're hiding something," Sable said.

It was so direct that she laughed — one short breath, surprised out of her. "So are you."

"Everyone on this mountain is." He turned the permits over, found the Trust's name on the second page, and she watched him read it. Watched his face do nothing at all, which was its own kind of information. When he looked up, the slate eyes had gone flat and still, like water over something deep.

"Three people," he said. "You, me, and a guide of my choosing."

"Agreed."

"And the route is mine to call. You core where I say the ice is safe, and not one meter where I say it isn't."

"Within reason."

"There is no reason on the escarpment, Doctor. There's only the mountain and whoever's listening to it." He slid the permits into his coat, and the face-down map with them, and stood, dropping coins beside his glass. "There's a guide in Thorndike. Renn Voss. He's the last one who'll cross, and he'll tell you no, and then he'll say yes, and you won't know why either time."

He was at the door before she found her voice.

"Mr. Marchetti. Why are you doing this? You don't need my permits to walk a closed line. You never have."

He stopped with his hand on the door, and for a moment the lamplight caught him edge-on, and she saw what the measuring eyes had hidden: a tiredness so old it had become architecture, load-bearing, the thing the whole man was built around.

"Because there's something past the escarpment I have to see," he said. "And I'd rather see it in company."

The door swung shut behind him. Ines sat alone with the stove's hiss and her cold tea, and understood, with the particular chill of a woman who has just signed something she cannot unsign, that she had not hired a guide.

She had hired a man walking toward something. And she had just promised to follow him there — carrying, in her crates and her conscience, a name that was going to meet him on the ice.

She ordered another tea she didn't want, and began, in the back of her mind, drafting the letter she would never send: *Dear Trust. Your money is on the mountain. So is your son.*
