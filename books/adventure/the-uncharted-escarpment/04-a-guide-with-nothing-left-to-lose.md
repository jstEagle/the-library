## 4. A Guide with Nothing Left to Lose

The letter came to Renn Voss the way most things came to him now: late, water-stained, and asking for something he'd already decided to give.

He read it twice in the doorway of his gear shed, letting the wind off the escarpment push at his shoulders like an impatient client. *Dr. Ines Okafor, glaciologist. Party of two. Requires passage across the Kestrel shoulder before calving season. References enclosed.* The references were good — too good, the kind of paper that meant money behind it, and money behind that. He'd learned to smell institutional money the way other men smelled weather coming. It had a sweetness to it that always went sour above the snowline.

He looked up from the letter to the mountain itself.

The Uncharted Escarpment stood against the morning the way it always did — not beautiful, exactly, but *present*, the way a debt is present. Its eastern face caught the first light and held it, a long rampart of gray granite stitched with icefields, and below it the glacier lay in its valley like something sleeping that was better left asleep. Twelve years ago he had come down off that ice carrying his father's name in his mouth like a stone. The official report said Aldous Voss had misread the slope. Had cut the route too close beneath the cornice. Had led eleven people into a trap of his own making and then died in it, conveniently, so no one could ask him why.

Renn had been nineteen. He remembered the funerals more clearly than the avalanche itself — that was the cruelty of it, memory keeping what hurt most and losing what mattered. He remembered standing at the graveside while men who had never laced a boot above the tree line shook their heads over his father's *error*. He remembered his mother's face going carefully blank, the way she'd trained herself to go blank whenever anyone mentioned the mountain, which was constantly, because the town lived off the mountain and hated it in equal measure.

And he remembered Sable Marchetti.

The surveyor had been pulled out of the slide on the third day, half-frozen, one arm broken, frostbitten so badly they'd taken two fingers. Renn had seen him in the aid station — a young man wrapped in wool, staring at the ceiling with an expression Renn had spent twelve years trying to name. Not grief. Grief he knew; grief was honest. This was something else. This was a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished.

Now that man wanted to cross the escarpment. With a glaciologist. Before the season closed.

Renn folded the letter along its creases and put it in his jacket pocket, where the other letters lived — the ones he'd never answered, the coroner's preliminary findings, the retired inspector who'd once bought him a drink and said, quietly, *your father's figures were clean, son, I never could make them dirty* — and went inside to check his rope.

* * *

He found Okafor first, because she was easier. She'd taken a room at the rail-yard tavern, the same tavern where the expedition teams used to drink before the line closed, and she sat at the corner table with her cores' shipping manifests spread out around her like a card hand she was losing gracefully. She was younger than he expected, or carried it differently — a compact woman with close-cropped hair and hands that moved when she talked, drawing shapes in the air as if the air were data.

"You're the guide," she said, before he'd fully sat down. Not a question. "Renn Voss."

"I'm the last guide," he corrected. "There's a difference this year."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning everyone else with a permit has either read the season reports or has something to live for." He turned his cup over, inspecting it out of habit — old guide's trick, checking for cracks before you trusted a vessel. "You want the Kestrel shoulder. That's closed terrain. Officially it's been closed since the slide."

"Officially," she agreed, and met his eyes without flinching. He liked that. He distrusted it. People who didn't flinch were usually the ones with the most to hide.

"Your funding," he said. "Who's behind it?"

Something crossed her face then — quick, controlled, gone. A flinch after all, just filed somewhere he couldn't see it. "A consortium. Academic interests. Why does it matter?"

"Because names matter on that mountain. There are families up there under the ice, Dr. Okafor, and some of them have living relatives who'd rather their dead stayed surveyed than disturbed. If your consortium has a name I should know, I'd rather know it now than on the ice."

She considered him for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere down the platform a coupling clanked — the sound the yard made all night, the sound Renn had grown up thinking of as the mountain breathing through its iron lungs.

"The Voss Foundation," she said finally. "Among others."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped piton. His own name, worn by strangers, spending money he'd never seen, attached to a legacy he'd spent twelve years refusing to inherit. His grandfather had endowed it before the slide — before the report, before the blame — and the family lawyers had kept it running like a heart-lung machine, pumping respectability into the Voss name while the actual Vosses bled out quietly in private.

"My family's money," he said.

"Your family's money," she confirmed. "Which is why I need you and not your permission. The permits are cleaner if a Voss is on the roster. And you're the only Voss who knows the shoulder."

He almost laughed. Almost. It came out instead as a slow exhale, fogging the window between them and the street. So they wanted his bloodline as a passport and his knowledge as a map, and they thought those were the same thing. Everyone always had.

"I'll take you across," he said. "One condition. I set the pace, I pick the camps, and when I say turn back, we turn back. No exceptions for funding schedules."

"Agreed."

"And your other party member. The surveyor. Marchetti." He watched her face as he said the name, and there it was again — that flicker, that careful stillness. "What's his stake in this?"

"Route knowledge. Old line maps. He's been surveying the closed corridors for years." She gathered her manifests into a stack, aligning the edges with unnecessary precision. "Why? Do you know him?"

"We've met," Renn said, and left it there, and watched her decide not to press, and understood that they had just made the same bargain with each other: *I will not ask about your secrets if you do not audit mine.*

* * *

Sable Marchetti arrived three days later, walking up from the coast with a surveyor's pack and a gait Renn recognized from a hundred trailheads — the walk of a man who measured distance professionally and therefore never hurried, because hurry was a form of imprecision.

They met on the platform, because Renn wanted neutral ground, and because he wanted to see the man move before he saw him speak. Up close, the years had done their work. The missing fingers on the right hand were the obvious ledger, but Renn read the rest: the way Sable's eyes went to the escarpment every few seconds and then away, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. The way he stood slightly angled away from the mountain, even while looking at it.

"Voss," Sable said. His voice was flat, careful, a voice that had been sanded down by disuse.

"Marchetti." Renn let the silence sit. Let him feel it. "Twelve years."

"Twelve years."

"You don't look surprised to see me."

"I'm not." Sable's gaze came off the mountain and settled on him, and for a moment — just a moment — Renn saw beneath the flatness, down to whatever furnace fed it. "I requested you specifically."

That landed harder than it should have. Renn had assumed he was incidental — the last body with a permit. But requested. Which meant Sable had thought about him, had chosen him, and Renn could not for the life of him decide whether that was an insult or a confession.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because you're the best guide on this side of the range, and because you know the shoulder better than any report records it." Sable paused. Wind came down the platform and moved the dust between them in little traveling spirals. "And because I owe your family a debt I've never been able to itemize."

The words were so precisely wrong that Renn knew instantly they were precisely something else. A debt you can't itemize is a debt you've already counted. He'd said things like that himself, at the graveside, to the inspectors, to anyone who would listen — sentences built to sound like humility while concealing their actual cargo.

"Then we'll settle accounts on the ice," Renn said. "We leave Thursday. Calving season won't wait for grief, and neither will I."

* * *

Thursday came gray and windless, the worst kind of morning — the kind that gave nothing away. Renn loaded the sleds in the dark, checked the clients' harness fittings twice without being asked, and led them out past the last functioning signal post onto the old Kestrel access road, where the rails ran green with twelve years of moss and the ties had gone soft as bread.

Behind him he heard them talking — Okafor's quick, data-hungry questions, Sable's answers coming slower, each one weighed before release, like a man paying out rope he might need later. Renn kept his eyes forward and his thoughts on the slope above the old camp, which he could not see yet but could feel, the way you feel a splinter.

Here was the thing he had told no one, the thing he would tell no one: he hadn't taken this job for the money, or the permits, or even the strange gravity of being requested. He had taken it because of a number. Three winters ago, going through his father's effects at last — unable for twelve years to open the trunk, unable longer to leave it shut — he had found a field notebook, water-damaged, its final pages fused together. And on the last legible page, in his father's small, exact hand, a benchmark calculation dated four days before the slide. An elevation figure. Corrected. Cross-checked twice, initialed, flagged for transmission to the survey office.

A figure that, if it had ever reached the survey office, would have changed the route.

His father had known. Four days before the mountain came down, Aldous Voss had known the slope was failing, had written it down, had sent it — and the report that came back afterward, the official report, the one signed by the surviving surveyor, had blamed *him* for the error his own notes disproved.

Unless the notes were wrong. Unless grief had forged them. That was the wound Renn carried up the mountain every time: not certainty, but the possibility of it. He needed the ice to say it. He needed cores, dates, strata — a glaciologist's testimony, cold and unimpeachable, that the slope had failed on its own schedule regardless of any man's route. And if the ice contradicted the notebook — if his father *had* erred — then Renn wanted to be standing on the slope when he learned it, so the mountain could bury the hope in front of him properly, once, instead of leaking it into him year after year like meltwater.

Either way, the mountain owed his family a sentence. He had come to collect it.

He glanced back. Sable Marchetti was looking at him — had been looking, he realized, for some time — with that same unreadable arithmetic in his face. And Renn understood, with the sudden clarity of high altitude arriving early, that the surveyor was carrying a notebook of his own. That they were two men climbing toward the same wound from opposite sides, each believing the other held the blade.

"Keep moving," Renn said, and turned uphill, into the white silence, where the truth of twelve years ago lay sealed under forty meters of ice — waiting, patient as a benchmark, for someone to finally measure it.
