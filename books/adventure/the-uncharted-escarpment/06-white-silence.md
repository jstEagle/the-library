## 6. White Silence

The storm came down on us like a verdict.

I had watched weather all my life from the safe side of instruments — barometric traces, satellite loops, the polite abstractions of data. But nothing in a decade of ice cores had prepared me for the way the Uncharted Escarpment announced itself: not gradually, not with warning, but with a single concussive exhalation off the glacier that turned the afternoon white. One moment Renn was thirty meters ahead, a dark figure against the blue-gray ice, picking our line through the crevasse field below the old Kestrel tramway; the next he was a rumor, and the world was a wall of moving snow that screamed like something alive.

"Signal hut!" His voice reached me as if through water. "Two hundred meters! Rope tight!"

Sable's hand closed on my pack strap from behind, and I understood then why Renn had insisted we rope together despite my protests that glaciologists are not mountaineers and should be trusted to fall only into things they can measure. The three of us moved as one blind animal, counting steps aloud, Sable's voice flat and steady behind me — *forty-one, forty-two* — a metronome keeping time against the wind's chaos. Once I felt the snow underfoot give that hollow, drum-like tremor I'd read about in accident reports and never wanted to feel, and Sable's grip on my strap became something close to tender, steering me left without a word.

Then wood. Renn's boot broke through a drift and struck planking, and we were clawing at a door frozen in its frame, and then inside — inside was silence so total it rang.

The derelict signal hut sat on a shelf of rock above what had once been a siding on the Kestrel Line. It was smaller than a chapel and about as reverent: one room, a rusted stove, bunk frames bolted to the walls, a signal lever mounted by the window like an artifact from a drowned civilization. Frost furred the inside of the glass. Through it, nothing. Not the glacier, not the escarpment, not the sky. Just white, moving, endless — the mountain erasing itself.

Renn had the stove lit within ten minutes, working with a competence that bordered on contempt for the hut, as though he'd done this before, here or somewhere exactly like it. Sable sat on a bunk with his instrument case across his knees, and I noticed — filed away, the way I file everything — that he did not open it. A surveyor separated from his instruments is a priest without his sacraments; most men would have checked them after a crossing like that. He just held the case and looked at the frosted window.

"We're pinned," Renn said. It wasn't a question. He fed split planking — pried, I suspected, from the bunk frame itself — into the stove. "Could be six hours. Could be two days."

"The calving season," I began.

"Doesn't care about us." He wiped his hands on his thighs. "Storm buys us time, maybe. Slows the meltwater. Or it loads the slope above the icefall and takes the whole route out early. We'll know when we know."

I set up my cores along the far wall, wrapped and numbered, fourteen cylinders of ancient air and compressed summers, each one a page torn from a book no one had read. They were the reason I had lied to Sable Marchetti in a rail-yard tavern, and lying in proximity to them made my chest tight. Outside, the wind found some flaw in the hut's cladding and worried it, a rhythmic metallic keening, like a hinge on a door someone had died behind.

We ate. Melted snow. Said little. And then, because there was nothing else to do — because a storm is a kind of confessional, sealing you in with strangers until the truth leaks out — Renn Voss began to talk.

* * *

It started with the stove. He was feeding it another plank when he said, almost to himself, "My father built this hut. Restored it, anyway. Summer of the year before." He turned the plank over in his hands. "He believed a signal line should never go dark. Even a dead one. Especially a dead one. He said a dark signal lies to people — tells them nothing's coming."

Sable's head came up slightly. I pretended to inventory my core wraps.

"He kept it stocked," Renn went on. "Candles, fuel, biscuits. Every season, until—" He stopped. Set the plank down. "You were both on the Kestrel crew," he said. "Twelve years ago. The survey party."

"I was," Sable said. His voice was level. Too level, the way ice is level right before it isn't.

"I wasn't. I was sixteen. But I heard everything, afterward. Every inquiry, every hearing, every drunk in every tavern who thought the son couldn't hear him." Renn sat back against the wall, arms folded, and looked at Sable with an expression I could not read — grief worn so long it had become architecture. "They say the camp failed at dawn. That the cornice above let go and took the slope with it, and that there was no warning."

"That's the official finding," Sable said.

"They say my father ignored the warnings he was given. That his benchmark reports cleared the camp site, and when the mountain disagreed, the mountain was treated as the anomaly." Renn's laugh had no humor in it; it was a sound like the wind taking the cladding. "Eleven men. My father signed the report that put them there."

The stove ticked. Snow hissed against the window. I found I had stopped breathing normally and made myself resume.

"That's not how it happened," Renn said quietly. "I know it isn't. Because my father came home the week before it happened, and he wasn't sleeping, and he wasn't eating, and he told my mother the numbers were wrong. Not the site. The *numbers*. He said he'd flagged it, that he'd sent a correction down-mountain, and that nobody upstairs wanted to hear it because the season was funded and the funding had a schedule." He looked at Sable directly now. "Your name was on the schedule, Mr. Marchetti. Junior surveyor, fastest stake-work the district had seen in years. Everyone said so."

Sable did not move. But I saw his thumb press against the brass corner of the instrument case, hard enough that the skin went pale around it.

"The official account says the correction never existed," Sable said. "There's no record of it."

"No," Renn agreed. "There isn't. Which is strange, isn't it? Because my father wrote everything down. He annotated grocery lists. A man like that sends a correction, there's paper. Unless the paper went where inconvenient paper goes." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the escarpment, toward the slide zone buried under twelve years of new snow. "Or unless someone needed it not to exist."

"You're suggesting a cover-up," Sable said. "By whom?"

"I'm suggesting," Renn said, with terrible gentleness, "that you were twenty-six years old and fast, and that fast men sometimes write the number they expected instead of the number they got. And that my father spent the last week of his life trying to fix it, and died under eleven men because the fix arrived too late — or was made to arrive too late." He stood, opened the stove, fed it fire. "That's my side of it. That's the version they didn't print."

The silence afterward was the loudest thing in the hut.

I should have spoken. Some part of me understood that a scientist in the room has obligations — that when two accounts diverge, someone must ask which is true. But I looked at Sable Marchetti's face and saw not guilt, exactly. Something worse. I saw a man listening to his own obituary being read in a language he almost didn't recognize, checking it for errors, unable to decide whether to correct them.

And I said nothing.

Because I had brought my own lie up this mountain, sealed in funding agreements and a patriarch's name I hadn't mentioned — the Voss family money in my expedition account, old Aldous Voss's death the reason the ice cores mattered to anyone with power — and a liar recognizes the posture of another liar the way one dog recognizes another across a yard. If I opened my mouth, mine might come out too.

So I did what I do instead of confessing. I worked.

* * *

While Renn slept — he took first rest with the untroubled conscience of a man who believes he's already told the truth — and while Sable sat watch by the window, motionless as the frost on the glass, I opened his field notes.

Not his instrument case. I wouldn't touch that; it was locked and sacred and beside the point. The field notes were in his rucksack's outer pocket, canvas-wrapped, and I told myself it was practicality: storm-bound parties cross-check their routes. I am a good enough liar to believe my own cover stories, which is, I suspect, the definition.

His handwriting was beautiful. That surprised me. Precise, upright, an engineer's hand trained into calligraphy — dates, elevations, stake readings, barometric corrections, all of it immaculate. Twelve years of closed lines mapped with the tenderness of a man writing letters he never intended to send.

I went backward. Twelve years ago. The Kestrel survey, spring season.

The entries were there — the camp site assessments, the benchmark series, the slope stability readings. And they were wrong. Not forged — nothing so crude. Wrong the way a translation is wrong: faithful to something, but not to the original. The benchmark figures in these notes matched the official report, the one that cleared the site, the one that blamed Renn's father. But the *margins* —

In the margins, in the same beautiful hand but lighter, faster, pressed less deeply into the paper, were other numbers. Corrections. Recomputations. Little annotations in shorthand I half recognized from district survey conventions: *re-run, dawn light better*, *stake 9 displaced?, re-shoot*, and once, twice, three times, a single word that stopped my breath:

*TOMAS.*

Tomas Marchetti. The brother. The one who'd died in the slide — the name Sable had given us in the rail-yard like a coin tossed to buy passage, a dead man offered as motivation. In the margins of the field notes, next to the corrected benchmark figures, Tomas's initials appeared again and again, paired with dates in the final week. As if the two brothers had been conducting a second survey inside the first — an honest one, hidden in the margins of the official one.

And on the last page, dated four days before the avalanche, one line, boxed in the old surveyor's convention for a critical flag:

*Benchmark 7 fails re-check. Slope will not hold a camp. Correction sent down-mountain 06:40. T.M.*

Correction sent down-mountain. The correction that did not exist in any record. The correction Renn's father had supposedly failed to make — except here it was, in another man's hand entirely, sent by a junior surveyor's younger brother, four days before eleven men died.

Which meant either Renn's story was true and the record had been doctored — or Sable's notes were a fabrication so elaborate it amounted to madness — or the truth was some third thing, some arrangement between the brothers that neither survivor had ever spoken aloud.

I closed the notebook. My hands were not steady, and I am a woman whose hands do not shake over ice cores drilled from thousand-year-old ice.

Outside, the storm had changed its mind about something. The keening against the cladding dropped a register, deepened, became a pressure more than a sound — the mountain shifting its weight. Renn stirred in his sleep and frowned. Sable, at the window, spoke without turning around, and his voice was quiet and stripped and aimed at no one:

"Tide's out tomorrow. If the wind holds from the north."

I didn't know what he meant. I noted it anyway, in my own log, in my own careful hand, beneath the day's readings — because I had come to understand that Sable Marchetti measured everything, and that whatever he was measuring, he had been measuring it for twelve years, and tomorrow, if the wind held, we would find out whether the mountain had been keeping the receipt all along.

Above the hut, unseen through the white, the glacier groaned — long, tectonic, patient — and I lay back on the bunk with his notebook's last line burning behind my eyes.

*Correction sent down-mountain 06:40. T.M.*

Four days before the slide. Eleven men. One surviving surveyor who had lied to me about why he came, one dead guide's son who had lied to himself about why he led us, and one glaciologist whose funding depended on never asking whose money walked beside her.

The storm would break by morning. So, I suspected, would everything else.
