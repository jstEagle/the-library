## 7. What the Ice Keeps

The glacier face at dawn was the color of old bone, and it breathed.

Renn had grown up with that sound — the slow exhalation of compressed air escaping the ice, a sigh so deep it seemed to come from under the world. His father used to say the escarpment glacier was a sleeping animal, and you didn't wake a sleeping animal, you learned its rhythm and moved when it moved. Renn had been moving to its rhythm for three days now, and he hated how easily his body remembered, how the mountain fit him like an old coat he'd sworn never to wear again.

He stood apart from the others at the edge of the corrie, watching Ines work. She'd staked her drilling site on a shelf of blue ice below the serac line, close enough to the face that the morning light came through it green, like light through bottle glass. The core drill chattered and whined. Steam rose from her breath and from the borehole both, two kinds of heat arguing with all that cold.

Sable was down there too, holding the tripod legs steady while she fed the barrel. He worked without wasted motion, the way surveyors do, hands that knew their own weight. Twelve years ago Renn had watched those same hands — younger, quicker, cockier — chalk figures on a slate at the Kestrel Line staging camp while his father read them aloud and argued with the sky about them. *Your numbers are wrong, Marchetti,* his father had said, laughing, not laughing. *These numbers will bury us.*

They had buried him. That much the official record agreed on. Everything else in the record, Renn had spent twelve years wanting to dig up.

* * *

Ines called them together at midmorning, when the first core came up whole.

She laid it on a canvas sheet across the ice: a column of frozen history, banded like agate, each layer a year or a season or a single catastrophic week. She read it the way Sable read contour lines — fluently, hungrily, her gloved fingers hovering above the bands without quite touching.

"Here." Her breath fogged the words. "See this layer? Ash-grey banding, silt inclusion, fractured structure. This is failure ice. This is what a slope looks like after it's already let go."

Renn crouched. "That's the avalanche layer."

"That's the avalanche layer," Ines agreed. "Twelve years ago, give or take a season. But look above it."

Above the grey band, the ice changed character gradually and then suddenly — a zone of disturbed crystal, of melt-refrozen lenses, of debris flecks that had no business being there. Renn didn't have the vocabulary for it, but he had the eye. You couldn't grow up hauling freight over the Kestrel Line and not know what unstable snowpack looked like.

"The slope failed before the slide," Ines said. "Days before. Maybe a week. Small releases, loading events, creep fractures — the mountain was telling everyone it was going to come down. And then—" she tapped the grey band, "—it did."

"Everyone knew the slope was bad," Renn said slowly. "That was the argument. My father wanted the camp pulled back. The company wanted the line driven through. The survey data was supposed to settle it."

"And the survey data said the slope was stable." Ines looked up at him, and behind the frost on her lashes her eyes were careful. "That's what I've been cross-checking against the archived reports. The benchmark elevations. The load calculations for the cut above the camp."

She unrolled a sheaf of papers she'd carried up from the signal hut — photocopies of documents older than some governments, softened at the folds, annotated in her small fierce handwriting. Renn recognized the header. Kestrel Line Extension, Final Survey Report. He'd seen that report once before, at fourteen, held in his mother's shaking hands.

"The numbers are elegant," Ines said. "Whoever drew them up was good. Very good. Consistent methodology, clean reductions, defensible error margins." She paused. "Except for one benchmark. Benchmark Seven, set on the shoulder above the camp. Its elevation is off by four meters. Not a transcription error — the reduction chain is internally consistent all the way through. It's a calculation error made at the source, early, and then everything downstream inherited it. Four meters of phantom stability. Enough to make a failing slope look like bedrock."

The wind moved through the corrie, lifting fine spindrift off the face, and for a moment the glacier's breathing was the only sound.

"Who ran the calculations?" Renn asked. His voice came out flat, which surprised him. He'd expected it to shake.

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked past him, down the shelf, to where Sable stood at the drill rig with his back to them, checking the barrel alignment against the sun.

"You already know," she said quietly.

* * *

He did. He had known since the signal hut, maybe since the tunnel, maybe since the first night on the ice when Sable had recited the route from memory — every cache, every anchor point, every deadfall — with the intimacy of a man revisiting a grave he'd dug himself. Renn just hadn't let the knowing finish itself. He'd kept it half-formed, the way you keep a sentence you're afraid to hear spoken aloud.

Benchmark Seven. He could see it in his mind's eye as clearly as if he were standing on it: the brass pin his father had helped set, the shoulder of grey rock above the camp, the view down onto the tents where twenty-two men slept. His father had trusted those numbers because he'd trusted the man who made them. *Marchetti's boy is the best calculator on the coast,* he'd told Renn once, almost fondly. *Numbers don't lie.*

Numbers didn't lie. Men did. One man had, one afternoon, with a slip of arithmetic no bigger than a fingernail, and twenty-two men had gone into the dark because of it.

And then — Renn had read the final report a hundred times — the blame had landed elsewhere. On the weather. On the company's schedule pressure. And finally, in the appendix, in language soft as snowfall, on the *site judgment* of the senior guide. On Aldous Voss, who had signed off on a camp placement based on numbers he had no reason to doubt, and who had died in the slide along with everyone else, unable to defend a decision that had never really been his.

They'd given his father a plaque in the valley station. *A prudent man overtaken by events.* Renn had been fourteen. He'd read the plaque and understood, with the terrible clarity children sometimes get, that the mountain hadn't killed his father. A piece of paper had.

Now he watched the man who'd written that paper steady a drill tripod on the ice, patient and competent and quiet, and something in Renn's chest closed like a fist.

* * *

He said nothing.

Not that day, nor the next, as they moved the drill to the second site and Ines pulled cores deeper, into ice older than the railway, older than any of their griefs. Not when Sable handed him tea at the evening fire, wrapping Renn's numb fingers around the tin cup with a care that felt, now, obscene. Not when Sable talked route logistics in his low even voice, laying out the crossing plan for the icefall, the timing windows, the margins.

Vengeance deferred by necessity — that was the arithmetic Renn lived by now. There were two men between him and the valley floor, and one of them was the only person alive who could find the way through the calving ice, and the other was the only person who could make the science mean anything to anyone who mattered. He needed them both. He needed the liar most of all, and that need sat in him like swallowed glass.

So he did the only thing he could do. He rearranged his nights.

The first evening he pleaded a bad boot sole and moved his sleeping bag to the windward wall of the tent, six feet from where he'd been. Sable noticed — Renn saw him notice, saw the flicker in his eyes, quick as a bird's shadow — but Sable said nothing either. Two men keeping silence in a tent the size of a coffin, listening to the glacier breathe.

By the third night Renn had stopped pretending. He simply took his bag outside and dug into the drift beside the gear sled, bedding down alone under the stars with the cold coming up through the ground like a rising tide. It was brutal and it was honest and it was the only place he could sleep, because lying six feet from Sable Marchetti, feeling the warmth of another body in the dark, hearing him breathe the way living men breathe — that had become unbearable. Twenty-two men had stopped breathing on the shoulder above the Kestrel camp. His father had stopped breathing. And the man responsible lay warm and guiltless-seeming ten feet away, handing out tea, memorizing escape routes, planning his descent.

Unless. Unless there was more to it. Renn turned it over in the dark, night after night, the way you worry a loose tooth. Because Sable had come back to this mountain. Nobody comes back to a thing like this by accident. And there was the brother — Tomas Marchetti, the other name on the report, the co-surveyor who'd died in the slide, whose field notes Renn had never been allowed to see. What role had Tomas played? What had the official record left out?

The mountain keeps what it keeps, his father used to say. Snow falls, ice forms, and everything that happens up here gets written down in layers, and someday somebody reads it back.

Ines was reading it back. Core by core, band by band, the glacier was testifying. And somewhere ahead of them, past the icefall, at the end of the route only Sable knew, there was supposed to be a cave — Sable had let that much slip, one night, thinking Renn asleep — a cave with markings in it, a cave Sable needed to reach before the season shut.

At midnight on the fourth night, lying in his drift-hole with his breath freezing on the wool around his face, Renn heard the tent flap open. Sable stepped out into the starlight, stood a moment looking north toward the icefall, and Renn, motionless in his hollow, watched him press one hand flat against his own chest — a gesture Renn had seen dying men make, though Sable wasn't dying, or wasn't yet — and then go back inside.

The next morning the horizon over the icefall was wrong. The clouds stood up in white towers along the ridge, and the glacier's breathing had changed key, gone shallow and fast, and far off under the ice something groaned like a ship breaking loose from its moorings.

Ines stood at the corrie rim with her binoculars up, and when she lowered them her face had gone the color of the ice.

"It's starting," she said. "The calving. Early." She turned to Sable. "How long do we have?"

Sable looked at the icefall for a long time, reading it the way he read everything, and when he answered, his voice was steady and his eyes were not.

"One day," he said. "Maybe less. We move at first light."

Renn shouldered his pack and said nothing, and followed the man whose numbers had killed his father toward the only way home — which ran, all of them knew now, directly beneath the failing ice.
