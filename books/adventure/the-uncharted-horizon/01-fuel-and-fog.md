## 1. Fuel and Fog

The fog came off the river at dawn the way it always did in the interior—slow, then all at once, like a held breath finally released. Petra Sterling watched it swallow her airstrip from the open door of her shack, coffee cooling in her hand, and did the arithmetic she did every morning: fuel on hand, jobs on the board, payments behind.

Three. She was three payments behind on the de Havilland.

The Beaver sat at the top of the strip where she'd chained it the night before, a forty-year-old bush plane with fabric patches on the cowling and a heart that refused to quit. Her whole life fit inside that airplane—the medicine runs, the mail contracts, the occasional tourist too cheap for the big operators out of Port Amara. Without it she was just a woman with a shack and a debt.

She drained the coffee, shouldered her flight bag, and walked out into the wet gray morning to prove, one more day, that she was still a pilot.

* * *

The medicine run to Kettle Falls was routine in every way that mattered. Two crates of antibiotics, a box of insulin packed in dry ice, a nurse named Odile who slept the entire ninety minutes with her mouth open. Petra flew the valley route low under the cloud deck, following the river's brown ribbon through country so green it looked wet even in drought, and landed on Kettle Falls' grass strip with the kind of touch she didn't let herself feel proud of anymore. Pride cost money. Money was the thing she didn't have.

It was on the return leg, refueling at Halfway Creek—a gravel bar with a drum of avgas and an honesty jar—that she saw the boat.

It came upriver against the current, a black aluminum skiff with a covered console, moving with the deliberate economy of something that had somewhere specific to be. Petra had spent enough years in the interior to read boats the way farmers read weather. This one said trouble.

She topped off the tanks faster than she needed to, capped them, and was rolling the drum back into the brush when the skiff nosed onto the gravel thirty meters downstream. A man stepped out. City shoes—clean leather loafers, absurd on the stones—and a rain jacket too new to have ever been rained on properly. He had the build of someone who worked out in a gym rather than a world, and he smiled before he spoke, which in Petra's experience meant the smile was the tool.

"Petra Sterling?" He said her name like he'd rehearsed it.

"Depends who's asking."

He produced a laminated folder from inside the jacket and held it up, not offering it. "Dane Kessler, Meridian Recovery Services. Acting on behalf of Cascade Pacific Credit." He let the name hang there. "You're familiar with Cascade Pacific."

"I'm familiar with the concept of a phone call."

"We called." His tone stayed pleasant, conversational, the way a man talks when he's decided the outcome already. "Fourteen calls, by our records. Left messages with the trading post at Loon Narrows, the mission office, the fuel dock at Port Amara. You're a difficult woman to reach, Ms. Sterling."

"I'm an easy woman to reach. I'm a hard woman to catch. There's a difference." She wiped her hands on her coveralls, buying time, keeping her voice level. "I'm three months behind. Maybe four. I've been flying this contract since March and the pay comes slow. It'll come."

"It always does," Kessler agreed. "That's what everyone says right up until the aircraft gets winched onto a barge." He glanced past her at the Beaver, and his eyes did something appraising and cold, the way a man looks at livestock. "Nice old girl. '52 Beaver? Those hold their value. That's the problem, if you follow me. You're not late because you're poor, Ms. Sterling. You're late because the bank knows exactly what you're sitting on."

The fog had burned off while they talked, and the sun lay flat and white on the water. Somewhere upstream a loon called, that wild laughing sound, and Petra thought about how many mornings she'd heard it and felt lucky. Luck was a currency too, apparently, and hers had run out.

"So what happens now?" she asked. "You winch her off a gravel bar?"

"Now?" Kessler checked a watch that probably cost more than her annual insurance premium. "Now I file my report. The order says repossession at your base of operations or any point of convenience. Halfway Creek is convenient." He smiled again, wider this time, showing teeth. "But here's the thing about orders. They're paperwork. Paperwork takes time. And time is the one thing you might be able to buy."

"How much time?"

"Fly me downriver to the junction. I've got a meeting at the Halvorsen Concessions camp tomorrow—I do work for more than banks, Ms. Sterling, everybody needs recovery services—and if you get me there tonight, I can be generous with my report. Say the aircraft was unlocatable. Weather. Fog." He gestured at the sky, which was insultingly clear. "A man can only report what he sees."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. The offer was a leash dressed as a favor, and they both knew it. Fly him now, owe him later. Every favor from a repo man compounded like interest.

"I've got a charter booked," she lied. "Evening run. Can't take passengers."

"Then I'll wait," Kessler said pleasantly, settling himself on a driftwood log as though he owned the creek. "I'm very patient. It's the job."

* * *

The charter was real, as it happened—or half real, which in the interior amounted to the same thing. The radio at Halfway Creek was a crackling single-sideband bolted inside a plywood shelter, and by four o'clock the trading post at Loon Narrows had relayed a message up the chain: a party at the old mission outpost above the Sorel confluence needed transport out, tonight, cash, no questions, double rate.

No questions. Double rate. In twenty years of bush flying, Petra had learned that those two phrases together were either a gift or a warning, and usually both.

She told Kessler the charter had come through after all, and watched his face do a small, satisfied thing, like a fisherman watching a trout rise to the fly. Then she fired up the Beaver and climbed away from Halfway Creek with a repo agent standing on the gravel bar getting smaller beneath her tailwheel, and she told herself the tightness in her chest was just the climb.

The mission outpost sat on a bench of land above the Sorel River, a cluster of tin-roofed buildings that had been a church school once and was now mostly a fuel cache and a place where the sick came when the river was their only road. Petra set down in the last light, wheels chirping on the short grass strip, and saw the man waiting before she'd finished taxiing.

He stood alone at the edge of the strip, leaning on a walking stick made from a ski pole, and even at a hundred meters she could see he was gaunt the way winter-starved animals are gaunt—not thin but hollowed, the body eating itself from the inside. An old canvas pack sat at his feet. Behind him, a young missionary woman in a faded blue dress hovered at the clinic door, arms crossed against the evening chill, watching with an expression Petra recognized: the look of someone who has argued with a dying man and lost.

"Vane," the man said when she shut the engine down. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Elias Vane. You the pilot?"

"Petra Sterling." She climbed out and offered her hand. His grip was dry and startlingly strong, a prospector's grip, the kind that came from forty years of rock and rope. Up close he was worse than he'd looked at distance—skin gone the color of old tarpaper, cheekbones standing out like ridge lines, a week of beard shot through with gray. But his eyes were clear. That was the terrible part. Dying men usually went cloudy. His eyes were sharp as survey instruments.

"You're the double-rate no-questions flight," he said.

"That's me."

"Good." He coughed then, turned aside, and when he straightened there was blood on his knuckles, bright red against the gray skin, and he wiped it on his trousers without looking at it, the way a man wipes off mud. "Port Amara. Tonight. Can you do it?"

Petra looked at the sky. The last light was going amber over the western ridges, and between here and Port Amara lay two hundred kilometers of rising terrain, a pass that funneled weather like a chimney, and night coming on fast. Every rule she'd ever been taught said no. Every rule she'd ever been taught had been written by people whose planes weren't three payments from a barge.

"Double rate," she said. "Cash."

"Triple, if we don't stop talking about it." Vane picked up his pack, and the effort cost him; she saw the tremor go through his shoulders as he swung it up. The missionary woman came forward then, quick, trying to take it from him, and he shook her off with a gentleness that somehow made it worse.

"He shouldn't fly," the woman said to Petra, low, urgent. "Whatever he's paying you—it isn't worth it. His lungs are finished. The altitude, the cold—"

"Marta." Vane's voice wasn't angry. It was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day. "I've been dying for eight months. I'd like to do it somewhere of my own choosing, among my own business. You've been kind. Let me be done being grateful."

Marta looked at Petra. *Please*, the look said. *Talk sense into him.*

Petra had flown men out of the bush with arrows in them and men out with hearts giving out, and she had learned that sense was the one cargo no bush plane could carry. What she said instead was: "There's a blanket in the back. Get him warm. We're burning daylight."

* * *

They lifted off into the last of the light and chased the dark eastward.

The Sorel Valley ran gold and violet below them, the water braided and shining, and for twenty minutes it was almost beautiful enough to make her forget the man in the passenger seat breathing in short, careful sips, the way you breathe when each breath is a negotiation. Petra kept the climb shallow, milked the valley for its lift, and started up the pass with the sun a red smear on the ridgeline ahead.

Halfway up, the weather found them.

It came out of the west without announcement—a wall of cloud boiling over the pass like smoke through a keyhole, swallowing the red light, and suddenly her windshield was gray wool and her wings were rocking in air that smelled like iron. She dropped the nose, hunted for the valley floor, and flew the next hour by memory and river-shape, instruments flickering, rain hammering the fabric skin of the old Beaver like thrown gravel.

Beside her, Elias Vane never made a sound.

She'd expected groaning, maybe worse—she'd carried dying men before and they rarely went quiet. But when she glanced over during a lull in the turbulence, he was sitting perfectly upright, eyes closed, one hand flat on the oilcloth-wrapped bundle in his lap as though it were a sleeping animal, and his lips were moving. Not praying, she didn't think. Counting, maybe. Or naming things. Men in the mountains did that sometimes, near the end. Named everything they could remember so it wouldn't be lost.

Lightning stitched the western sky. She put her hand back on the yoke and flew.

They broke out of the weather forty minutes from Port Amara, into a night washed clean and enormous, stars in sheets from horizon to horizon, the city's lights a smudge of gold on the black coast ahead. Vane opened his eyes at the first glimpse of them and made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"Never thought I'd see it again," he said. His voice was thinner now, worn down by the altitude like stone under a waterfall. "Port Amara. Forty years I've been staying away from that town."

"Why come back now?"

He was silent long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Rain ticked on the windscreen. Below, the river caught the starlight and held it.

"Business," he said finally. "Old business. The kind you leave too long and it goes rotten on you." He shifted, and she heard the wet rattle in his chest, and he pressed on before she could say anything about hospitals. "You got a place to stay tonight, Miss Sterling? A hangar? Somewhere the plane sits quiet till morning?"

"I sleep in the shack by the strip. Plane's on the tie-downs."

"Good. Because I need you again tomorrow. Day after that, maybe longer." He reached inside his jacket, slow, careful, and brought out an envelope thick with bills, folded once, and laid it on the seat between them without ceremony. "That's triple. And there's more where it came from, if you can keep your field shut and your wheels turning. No radio calls about your passenger. No names to the fuel dock. Nobody knows Elias Vane came out of the Sorel. Can you do that?"

Petra looked at the envelope. It was more money than she'd seen in a year—more than enough to catch up the payments, more than enough to buy herself a season of breathing room. Enough, maybe, to stop running the arithmetic every dawn.

She thought of Dane Kessler on his driftwood log, patient as a spider. She thought of fourteen phone calls unanswered. She thought of how easy it would be, tomorrow, for a man in clean shoes to find out exactly where a de Havilland Beaver slept at night.

"No questions," she said. "That was the deal."

Vane studied her in the dim green glow of the instrument panel, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to satisfy him, because he nodded once and settled back and closed his eyes, and within minutes his breathing went ragged and shallow, and she flew the last miles to Port Amara listening to it and telling herself it wasn't her problem. A passenger was a passenger. A fare was a fare. She'd hauled stranger cargo than a dying man and a bundle of oilcloth.

But when she flared the Beaver onto the dark strip at Port Amara and felt the wheels kiss the gravel, Vane's hand came down on her arm—light, weightless, a bird landing—and he said, in a voice almost too quiet to hear over the engine:

"Sterling. Funny thing, that name. I knew a Sterling once. Long time ago." A pause, and then, softer, almost to himself: "Small world. Small, small world."

The taxi light swept the strip ahead of them, and beyond it the night stood wide and black and full of things she didn't yet know were coming for her.
