## 2. The Passenger Who Knew Her Name

The fog burned off by the second hour, and the world below unrolled itself in green and brown like a map nobody had finished drawing.

Petra flew at nine thousand feet where the air was smooth, the de Havilland Beaver droning its one honest note, and for a while she let herself pretend this was still a medicine run. Insulin and antibiotics lashed in the back, a cooler of plasma wedged behind the copilot's seat, and one gaunt old man breathing shallowly beside her instead of the usual chatty nurse or government clerk. That was all that had changed. Just cargo with a heartbeat.

She glanced sideways. Elias Vane sat very straight for a dying man, hands folded on his knees like a man in a church he didn't believe in, watching the ridgelines scroll past beneath them with an expression she couldn't read. Not wonder. Pilots saw wonder in passengers' faces all the time — the soft-mouthed awe of people seeing the interior from above for the first time. This was something else. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man checking landmarks against a memory forty years deep.

"You've flown this corridor before," she said. It wasn't a question so much as an offering. Conversation kept passengers calm, and calm passengers didn't grab the yoke when the turbulence came.

"Once or twice." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Long time ago."

"Survey work?"

Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under water. "You could say that."

Petra let it go. She'd learned long ago that the interior collected men the way eddies collect leaves — prospectors, fugitives, dreamers, men running from wives or debts or their own names — and the polite thing was never to ask which. She checked her gauges out of habit rather than need. Fuel burn normal. Oil pressure steady. Outside, a river braided itself silver through a valley so wide it looked like the earth had exhaled.

It was another twenty minutes before he spoke again, and when he did, it wasn't to her. He was looking down at the confluence ahead — two rivers meeting in a Y that any pilot who worked this country knew the way they knew their own signature — and he said, quietly, almost fondly:

"Sterling Junction."

Petra's hands went cold on the yoke.

She didn't say anything. She told herself not to say anything. But her eyes flicked to the junction anyway, down and left, the involuntary glance of a woman hearing her own name called in a crowd, and she knew — she *knew* — that he'd seen it.

"They named it after a man," Elias said. "Pilot. Came up here in the late eighties flying survey support. Ran supplies into camps no one else would land at." He turned his head slowly, and his eyes were pale gray, the color of weather coming. "Sterling. Thomas Sterling."

"I don't know the name."

The lie came out flat and fast, and it was a bad lie, and they both knew it. Her voice had done the thing voices do when they're defending ground already lost — pitched slightly too high, slightly too quick, a door slammed a half-second after the intruder's foot is already inside.

Elias Vane smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse.

"No," he agreed softly. "I don't suppose you do."

* * *

The turbulence found them over the divide, as it always did, thermals rising off the sun-heated rock and slapping the Beaver around like a toy in a dog's mouth. Petra tightened the harness, trimmed nose-down, and worked the controls in small corrections, keeping her eyes moving — horizon, gauges, horizon, terrain — because that was the discipline, that was the whole religion of bush flying: keep your eyes moving, keep your hands busy, and the fear has nowhere to sit down.

But her hands were shaking.

Not much. A tremor, fine as a hairline crack, running from the heel of her palm into the yoke. She watched it happen the way you watch a stain spread through cloth, unable to stop it, unable to look away. Twenty years of flying and her hands had never once shaken in rough air. Turbulence didn't scare her. Turbulence was honest — it pushed, you pushed back, and when it was over it was over.

This wasn't turbulence.

"Sterling," Elias said again, over the drumming of the wind on the struts. Not accusing. Testing. Saying the name aloud the way a man taps a wall to find the hollow place behind it. "Tom Sterling. Flew a Turbo Beaver, tail number ending in four-one-one. Had a daughter he used to bring to the mission strip at Kalaung in the summers. Little girl with a scuffed-up flight bag sitting on the fuel drums, watching him preflight."

"Stop."

The word came out of her before she'd decided to say it, raw and too loud in the cockpit, and the plane bucked in a thermal right on cue as if the sky itself wanted emphasis. Petra swallowed. Kept her eyes forward. The mountains rolled past, indifferent, ancient, covered in forest older than every name anyone had ever pinned to it.

"There was a lot of pilots," she said, quieter now. "A lot of Beavers. A lot of little girls on fuel drums. You've got me confused with somebody else, mister, and I'd thank you to sit back and let me fly."

"I watched him load floats at Kalaung in June of '89," Elias said, relentless as water finding a seam. "Last time I saw him. He was carrying a crate of core samples out for the survey crew — our crew — and he asked me to look after something if anything happened to him. I told him nothing was going to happen to him. He laughed." The old man's breath caught, wet and rattling, and he pressed his lips together until the coughing passed. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone almost gentle. "He laughed and said, 'Nothing ever happens to Sterling.'"

Petra flew.

She flew the way she'd taught herself to fly through everything — through the year the bank took the hangar, through the winter she ate rice and tinned fish three meals a day, through the night her mother died and she'd flown a medevac run four hours later because the schedule didn't care and neither, she'd decided, would she. Hands level. Eyes moving. The past tapping on the windshield glass, and her job was to keep flying and not turn her head.

But the glass was fogging from the inside.

Because she remembered the fuel drums. God help her, she remembered them — the warm black rubber baking in the sun, the smell of avgas and creosote, the way the drums made a perfect row of seats for a seven-year-old girl whose whole world was a man in a grease-stained shirt crouched under a propeller saying *now, Pet, what does this one do?* She remembered the tail number. Four-one-one. She had known it before she'd known her times tables.

"You said 'our crew,'" she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone else in the cockpit. "'Our crew.' You were on the '89 survey."

Elias was quiet a moment. Below them the land opened into a high plateau, burnt umber and gold-green, stitched with game trails. Somewhere down there, probably, were the bones of a camp.

"Geological survey," he said finally. "Contracted through a company that doesn't exist anymore under a name nobody remembers. Six of us in the field, two aircraft support. We were mapping mineral claims along the upper tributaries." He paused. "Except we weren't only mapping claims. And in September, the second plane went into the mountains in weather it should never have flown in, and Tom Sterling went with it, and the search found wreckage and no answers, and the company closed the file in eleven days because closing files is what companies do." Another pause, longer. "And I have spent thirty-five years being sorry about a great many things, Miss — "

"Don't." Her knuckles were white. "Don't say my name."

"Petra," he said anyway, softly, the syllables landing in the cockpit like stones dropped down a well. "Petra Sterling."

The name hung there. The engine droned. Far ahead, storm light was gathering over the western ranges, a bruise-colored smudge building along the horizon, and Petra stared at it and thought about turning south, about climbing, about doing anything other than sitting in this sealed metal room with a ghost she'd spent half her life pretending had no face.

"My father disappeared," she said at last, each word placed carefully, like footing on rotten ice. "That's all anybody knows. Plane went down, no bodies recovered, file closed. My mother got a letter from some lawyer and a box of his things and that was the entire estate of Thomas Sterling. So whatever story you've built in your head about me knowing something, or caring something, or—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated him for hearing it. "I'm a charter pilot. I fly insulin and fish crates and occasionally coughing old men who pay double for silence. That's the whole of it. There's no more."

"No," Elias agreed. "There isn't. Not anymore. Not since September of '89." He shifted in his seat, and she heard the wet rattle of his lungs again, and despite everything — despite the anger climbing her throat like floodwater — her eyes cut to him, measuring, the way she'd measure a failing gauge. He was grayer than he'd been at the strip. His lips had a bluish tinge.

"How far to the mission?" he asked.

"Ninety minutes, weather holding."

"And is it holding?"

Petra looked west at the bruise on the horizon, then north at the clean blue gap between cloud layers along their route. "Holding," she said. "Barely. Which is more than I can say for you. When we land, I'm radioing ahead for the medic whether you like it or not."

"I'd expect nothing less." He settled back, closed his eyes, and for a few blessed minutes the cockpit held nothing but engine noise and wind and the ordinary arithmetic of flight. Petra felt her pulse come down by degrees. Her hands, she noticed, had stopped shaking — or maybe she'd just stopped noticing, which amounted to the same thing in her line of work.

Then, without opening his eyes, Elias Vane said:

"He talked about you, you know. In the letters."

The words went through her like a propeller through a bird — no impact, at first, just the terrible whiteness afterward.

"What letters."

"To the mission. Kalaung. He wrote two, three times a year — money for the school, mostly, but notes too. Personal notes. After he stopped flying regular runs he'd ask the brothers to pass word along the river network whenever a pilot came through headed your way." Elias opened his eyes now, and they were fixed on her profile with an attention that felt almost physical, a hand on the back of her neck. "He never stopped asking. Every letter, same line at the bottom: *anyone seen my girl? Is she flying yet?*"

"Stop the plane," Petra said absurdly, to no one, meaning *stop everything*, meaning *stop 1989*, meaning *stop*. "You're lying. You're a sick old man spinning stories to—"

"Why would I lie?" And there it was again, that gentleness, worse than cruelty, patient as erosion. "What could I possibly want from you, Miss Sterling? I'm a dead man with a paid ticket. I have nothing to sell you and nothing to take. I just thought—" His breath hitched; he mastered it. "I thought you'd want to know you weren't forgotten. That's all. That's the whole of it."

Petra flew.

The plateau ended. The land folded upward into the first real ranges, granite shoulders shrugging into cloud, and she began the slow climb to clear the saddle, easing the throttle forward, trimming, watching the manifold pressure the way her father had taught her to watch it — *gently, Pet, she's not a racehorse, she's an old mule with wings, and you love her accordingly.*

She loved him accordingly. She had loved him accordingly for thirty-five years, in the only language she had left, which was altitude and fuel loads and perfect landings nobody saw.

"The second plane," she said suddenly. She hadn't planned to say it. It simply came out, pulled out of her like a splinter. "You said it went in during weather it shouldn't have flown in. Whose decision was that?"

Elias was silent so long she thought he might not answer, might have slipped away altogether — she actually reached to check his breathing, and his hand came up and briefly touched hers on the throttle, dry and light as paper.

"That," he said, "is a question I've been carrying since before you were born. And it's why I'm in your airplane."

Ahead, the saddle between the peaks stood open and blue, a doorway of clean air, and beyond it the mission valley lay hidden in its fold of forest. Behind them, the western bruise darkened and swelled, eating the horizon ridge by ridge. Petra set her heading, split the difference between speed and comfort, and flew.

Her hands were steady now. Steadier than they'd been in years, in fact — steady the way things get steady after they break, once all the shaking is done.

"Kalaung strip," she said into the radio, twenty minutes later, voice level, professional, a stranger's voice. "Beaver four-seven-niner, inbound from the east, one passenger, medical priority. Request someone meet us with a stretcher."

The reply crackled back, tinny and distant. She acknowledged it and put the radio down.

Beside her, Elias Vane had produced something from inside his coat — she caught it in her peripheral vision, oilcloth, wrapped tight and tied with cord, handled with the unconscious reverence of a man touching scripture. He didn't offer it. He just held it in his lap, both hands, and looked out the windshield at the approaching valley, at the strip coming up to meet them, grass silvered with seed heads, exactly where a certain little girl on a certain row of fuel drums had watched a certain man touch down a hundred times.

"Almost home," he murmured. Petra couldn't tell if he meant the mission, or himself, or her.

She flared over the trees, bled the speed, and set the Beaver down on Kalaung's grass like setting down something fragile — the best landing she'd done all season, smooth as poured water, and she didn't know who she was proving it to.

Figures were already moving at the tree line. Elias slid the oilcloth bundle beneath his coat again and gripped her forearm as she taxied, sudden, surprisingly strong.

"When I'm settled," he said, "we need to talk about what I'm leaving you. And you're going to tell me no. Everyone tells me no." He smiled that terrible gentle smile. "But you'll change your mind. Your father always did."
