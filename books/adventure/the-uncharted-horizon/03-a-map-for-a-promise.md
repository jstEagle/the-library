## 3. A Map for a Promise

The storm found us forty minutes out from the mission at San Isidro, rolling up the valley like a grey tide, and I flew into it because there was nowhere else to go.

Elias Vane had stopped talking somewhere over the second ridge line. I'd glance sideways and see him slumped against the harness, chin on chest, and every time the plane bucked his head would loll and come up again, slow, like something being reeled in from deep water. The blood he'd coughed onto my passenger seat back at the strip had dried to a brown crescent. There was more of it now, fresher, spotting the knee of his canvas trousers where he'd pressed his fist against his mouth.

"San Isidro," I said, loud enough to carry over the engine. "Twenty minutes. They've got a nurse. Father Anselmo can radio through to Port Amara, get a medevac—"

"No medevac." His voice came out of him like gravel shifting in a chute. "No hospitals. No names."

"You're coughing up your lungs, old man."

"I know what I'm doing." He turned his head and looked at me, and even half-dead his eyes had a weight to them, the kind of look that made you understand this man had stared down things in the bush that would have folded most people in half. "You want your money. You'll get it. But you fly me to San Isidro and you land quiet, and after that what happens is between me and God and you."

Rain hit the windshield in fistfuls. The wiper on the pilot's side had died two months ago and I'd never fixed it, because fixing things cost money and money was a language I'd stopped speaking fluently. I swept the glass with my palm uselessly and put the nose down toward the river bend where the mission sat — a cluster of tin roofs and a chapel spire and a grass strip that was more suggestion than runway, cut into the bank above brown water that was already starting to swell with the early rains.

"Strip's going to be slick," I muttered, mostly to myself.

"I've landed worse," Elias said, and then he laughed, or tried to, and it turned into a wet, tearing cough that went on so long I thought he might die right there in the seat, thirty feet above the trees.

* * *

San Isidro materialized out of the rain all at once, the way places do when you're flying low and blind — one moment grey murk and black-green canopy, the next a river bend, a cross, a strip of red mud with two figures standing at its edge waving us off, arms crossed over their heads, which in any language means *don't land*.

I landed anyway.

The de Havilland touched down long, hydroplaned through a sheet of standing water, and I stood her on her toes with the brakes until we shuddered to a stop twenty yards short of the chapel fence, tail swinging, propeller throwing rooster tails of red mud. The two figures came running. One was a young brother in a soaked cassock, the other an older woman in a nurse's smock who reached my door before I'd finished shutting down the engine.

"He's sick," I said, climbing out into the deluge. "Coughing blood. He wouldn't let me divert anywhere else."

The nurse got the other door open and leaned in, and whatever she saw in Elias Vane's face made her go very still. Then she was all motion — shouting for the brothers, for a stretcher, for someone to fetch Father Anselmo — and hands were pulling Elias out into the rain, and he weighed almost nothing, I realized. A man built of wire and leather and stubbornness, and the stubbornness was the only part still holding weight.

They carried him under the eaves of the mission house, and I would have stayed by my plane, because staying by my plane was what I did — planes didn't ask questions, didn't hold dying men's hands, didn't get involved. But Elias caught my sleeve as they set the stretcher down, and his grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"A word," he rasped. "Before your conscience catches up with you and you fly off."

"My conscience and I aren't on speaking terms."

"So I've gathered." He waved the nurse off — *five minutes, Sister, give me five minutes* — and she went, reluctantly, and then it was just the two of us under the dripping eave while the rain came down like the sky was trying to drown the whole interior out of existence.

* * *

They moved him inside eventually, to a narrow cell of a room with a cot and a crucifix and a window fogged with damp, and it was there, an hour later, with the storm still hammering the tin roof like applause from an enormous indifferent audience, that Elias Vane made me his offer.

I should have said no. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I should have taken my double fee, gassed up at dawn, and been gone before the fog lifted, and lived the rest of my life as a woman who once flew a dying prospector to a mission and never asked why. That was the smart play. That was the Petra Sterling special: get in, get paid, get gone, leave nothing behind that anyone could follow.

But he had said my father's name over the canyon country, back in the clean air before the storm, and the word *Sterling* had lodged in me like a splinter working its way toward the heart. So I stayed. I told myself it was about the money. I've always been good at telling myself things.

Father Anselmo had given him something for the pain, enough that Elias could prop himself up on one elbow and look at me sitting on the room's only chair, and what he looked at me with wasn't gratitude. It was appraisal. The way a man looks at a horse he's deciding whether to trust with his life.

"How far behind are you?" he said.

"That's not your business."

"The repo man at your strip this morning. Grey sedan, city shoes, clipboard. He'll be back within the week, and next time he won't come alone." He said it without malice, the way you'd report the weather. "Three payments. Maybe four."

My spine went cold. "How do you—"

"Petra." He said my name carefully, tasting it. "I've been asking about you for six months. You think you're invisible out here, but nobody's invisible. Not you, not me, not the men who work for Halvorsen Concessions, and certainly not—" He stopped himself. Coughed, dryly this time, into a handkerchief already stiff with old blood. "Not your father's daughter."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than I meant it to. "Whatever you knew about Frank Sterling, whatever story you've got, I'm not buying. My father is a closed account."

"Is he." Elias studied me. Rain drummed. Somewhere down the hall a brother was singing vespers badly, off-key and unbothered. "Then you'll want to hear none of this. Good. That makes it simpler." He reached beneath the cot, slowly, wincing, and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, no bigger than a Bible. He laid it on the blanket between us like a man laying down cards.

"What is it?"

"A map."

"I don't want a map."

"Nobody wants a map, girl. Maps are trouble. Maps say *here*, and *here* is always where somebody doesn't want you to go." His mouth twitched, which I suppose was his version of humor. "This map says *here* louder than most."

I didn't touch it. "What's it worth?"

"To Halvorsen? Enough that they'd burn this mission down around our ears to keep it from reaching the wrong hands. To you—" he shrugged, a small terrible movement, "—to you it's worth whatever your promise is worth. Which is the only currency I've got left, so I'm gambling everything on it."

"Promise for what?"

"For delivery." He pushed himself up higher, and the effort cost him; I watched sweat stand out on his forehead despite the chill in the room. "There's a woman in Port Amara. Marisol Vane. She's my daughter. She doesn't know I exist — or rather, she knows, and wishes she didn't, and she's right to." Something moved behind his eyes, some old grief worn smooth as river stone from years of handling. "I left when she was four. Her mother kept her away after that, and God forgive me, I let her. I told myself the bush was no place for a child, and then I told myself she was better off, and then I just told myself anything at all, because lying to yourself is the one surveying error no instrument can catch."

I said nothing. Outside, the rain eased fractionally, the way storms do when they're only reloading.

"She works boats. Marine mechanic, they tell me. Good with engines, bad with fathers — and I earned that, every bolt of it." He tapped the oilcloth bundle with one yellowed finger. "Five weeks. The concession permits on a certain parcel of land expire in five weeks. After that, the claim lapses, the land reverts, and Halvorsen files their paper and everything under that ground — everything this map leads to — becomes theirs forever, legal as a hymn book. But if the claim is renewed, if it's filed properly, in time, with the right names attached..." He trailed off, breathing hard.

"With whose names?"

"Mine. Hers." He looked at me. "And one more."

The splinter in my chest turned a quarter turn. "No."

"You haven't heard the name yet."

"I don't need to. Whatever game this is—"

"Frank Sterling." He said it gently, the way you'd handle a wound. "Survey crew, 1989. The expedition that walked into the upper valley and never walked out. The plane they sent looking for him came back empty too. Official record says weather. Official record says a lot of things." His voice dropped. "Official record was written by Halvorsen."

The room seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a horizon tilts when your inner ear gives up on you. I gripped the chair's arm. "You're lying."

"I'm dying, girl. Lying's expensive at my stage. I can't afford it." He took a breath that whistled. "I was on that expedition. Junior surveyor, nineteen years old, wet behind the ears. Your father taught me how to read country. How to find water in limestone. How to be a man, though he'd have laughed himself sick to hear me say it." He closed his eyes. "When the expedition broke apart, I took something with me. Something that proves what really happened up there. And I have spent forty years keeping it safe and doing nothing with it, because I was a coward, and cowards call their cowardice *patience*. Now the permits are expiring and my patience has run out along with everything else."

"Why me?" My voice sounded strange to me, thin and far away. "Why not deliver it yourself? Why not hire anyone — anyone who isn't—"

"Because Halvorsen owns everyone else. Because you fly under their radar because you barely register on anyone's radar. And because—" he opened his eyes, and they were clear and terrible and kind, all three at once, "—because you deserve to know what happened to your father, and you will never, ever go looking for it on your own. I've watched you run from that name for six months. Someone has to make you stop running. Dying men get to make requests like that. It's in the contract."

"There is no contract."

"There is now." He nudged the oilcloth bundle an inch toward me. "One promise. Take it to Marisol Vane in Port Amara before the permits lapse. Five weeks. That's all. She decides what happens after — whether she comes looking, whether she burns it, whether she throws it in the harbor. Her choice. I only ask that it reaches her hands while there's still time for it to matter."

"And if I say no?"

He smiled, and it transformed his whole ruined face for a moment into something younger. "Then you fly home tomorrow, the repo man takes your plane Thursday, and you spend the rest of your life wondering. I know the breed, Petra. I'm the same breed. We'd rather bleed out than ask directions." He coughed again, softer. "But you won't say no."

"Confident."

"Desperate people recognize each other." He lay back against the pillow, spent, the bundle still sitting on the blanket between us like a live coal. "Take it. Hold it. Feel how heavy a piece of paper gets when forty years sit on it."

I should have said no. Instead I reached out and picked up the bundle, and it was heavier than it looked, the way he'd promised, oilcloth slick and cold from the damp, cord biting into my fingers. Forty years. I turned it over once, twice, feeling the stiff rectangular shape inside — a map, folded, maybe papers with it — and my heart was doing something I didn't authorize, hammering like a kid's.

"Swear it," Elias whispered. "On something that matters. Pilots swear on their ships, I've heard. Swear on the de Havilland."

"That's a terrible thing to swear on. She's held together with baling wire and denial."

"Then she's honest, at least. Swear."

I looked at him — this gaunt, stubborn ghost of a man who had known my father, who had carried a secret through four decades of bush and silence, who was spending his last days arranging the world's affairs like a man setting his table for guests who hadn't been born yet — and I felt the trap close around me, soft and warm and shaped exactly like my own curiosity.

"On the plane," I said. "I swear. It reaches Marisol Vane before those permits expire. What she does with it is her business."

"And mine," Elias murmured, settling deeper into the pillow, "is done." His eyes drifted toward the fogged window, toward the rain. "She has her mother's temper. Don't let her throw you off the dock."

"I've been thrown off better docks than hers."

He laughed — a real laugh, brief and rusty and painful to hear — and then the pain took it away, and the nurse came back in, and I understood I was dismissed.

At the door I stopped. "Elias. The expedition. My father — did he—"

"Not tonight." His eyes were already closing. "Tonight you take the first step. The rest of the road tells itself as you walk it. That's the only kind of map worth carrying."

* * *

I slept in the chapel's back pew because the brothers offered and because the alternative was my plane's cold cabin and I couldn't face the passenger seat with its brown crescent of blood. Around midnight the storm broke apart and the moon came through the broken clouds and lit the chapel's dust motes like drifting snow, and I lay there staring at the rafters with the oilcloth bundle tucked inside my jacket, against my ribs, heavy as a second heartbeat.

Five weeks. Port Amara was a day's flight south. Marisol Vane was a stranger who hated her father, and I was a stranger sworn to hand her the reason why.

Somewhere down the hall, faintly, I heard Elias coughing, and then not coughing, and then coughing again, and I lay in the moonlight and told myself the same lie I'd been telling myself since the strip: *just the money, just the job, just until delivery.*

Down the hall, the coughing stopped for good this time — no, I checked later; he held on, stubborn to the last — and I rolled over and closed my eyes, and dreamed of a valley I'd never seen, and a plane going down in it, and a name on the wind that sounded like my own.

In the morning there was fog on the strip thick as wool, and a telegram form waiting for me at the mission office, and Father Anselmo watching me with kind, tired eyes as I wrote out the address of a marine workshop in Port Amara and a message I rewrote four times:

**HAVE SOMETHING OF YOUR FATHER'S. IT CANNOT WAIT. COME TO SAN ISIDRO OR I WILL BRING IT TO YOU. — P.S.**

I handed it to the brother at the wireless, and before the key had finished clicking, I already knew I wasn't flying home.
