## 4. Inventory of a Small Life

The rain found the shack's one weakness within an hour of her return, as it always did—a seam above the window where the tar paper had lifted, letting in a thin, persistent drip that landed on the tin plate she'd positioned there years ago. *Tink. Tink. Tink.* Petra had stopped hearing it most days. Tonight it sounded like a countdown.

She hung her flight jacket on the nail by the door and stood dripping on the packed-dirt floor, watching Elias Vane's map lie on her table where she'd left it, still wrapped in its oilcloth like something surgical. The kerosene lamp threw her shadow long across the room, over the cot, the two crates that served as shelves, the dented kettle on the single burner. Twenty-two years old when she'd built this place with salvaged tin and stolen lumber, and it still looked like what it was: a way station for someone who hadn't decided to arrive.

Her father had taught her to build things that lasted. She'd made a career of the opposite.

Petra lit the second lamp because one wasn't enough for what she was about to do, then sat down at the table and unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a woman defusing something. The map came out stiff and smelling of linseed and decades, hand-inked on survey linen gone the color of weak tea. Elias Vane's drafting hand was beautiful—she'd noticed that on the flight down, the way his fingers had trembled around the paper but never lost their precision. Rivers drawn with hatched banks. Elevation contours stacked like fingerprints. Marginal notes in a cramped engineer's script: *fall line unreliable after rains*, *gravel bar shifts seasonally — do not trust '61 sketch*, *water high in June, dead low by October*.

And in the lower right corner, boxed in a cartouche like a signature on a painting:

**SURVEY PARTY 1989 — R. HALVORSEN, PRINCIPAL. FIELD CREW: E. VANE, D. OKONKWO, M. FERREIRA, J. STERLING.**

*J. Sterling.*

The lamp hissed. The drip hit the plate. *Tink.*

Petra didn't move for a long time. Outside, the monsoon wind worked at the tin roof, peeling up a corner and laying it back down, peeling and laying, like something trying to get a grip.

---

Her father's initials were everywhere in her childhood, if she was honest, though she'd spent twenty years pretending they weren't. *J.S.* scratched into the handle of the machete she still used. *J.S.* on the inside cover of the navigation manual she'd learned from, the margins dense with his handwriting—*always know your escape heading before you need it, Petra-glass, always.* He'd called her Petra-glass because she'd been born during a storm and he said she was the only clear thing in it.

James Sterling had flown a de Havilland Beaver off strips like this one, hauling survey crews into country that didn't want them, and he'd come home smelling of avgas and river mud and lift her onto his shoulders so she could see over the crowd at the airstrip dances. She remembered the exact weight of his hands on her ankles. She remembered being seven years old and absolutely certain that the world was a machine her father understood completely.

Then 1989. The expedition. The plane that went out and the man who didn't come back, and the official finding—*aircraft lost, presumed down in the high country, pilot error possible, search suspended*—and her mother going quiet in a way that never reversed itself, and Petra learning at eleven years old that the sky takes payments too.

She'd told herself the story so many times it had worn smooth: he flew into weather he shouldn't have, the mountain took him, the mountain takes everyone eventually, end of story, fly on. It was a good story. It fit in a pocket. You could carry it through twenty years of debt and anonymity and small charter runs for people who paid cash and asked nothing.

Elias Vane had taken that story out of her pocket tonight and turned it over, and underneath, where the wear didn't reach, were words she'd never let herself read.

*"Your father didn't crash, girl. Your father found something."*

That's what he'd said on the flight, coughing blood into a rag, watching her fly with those sunken eyes that missed nothing. She'd snapped at him—*you don't know anything about my father*—and he'd smiled with cracked lips and said nothing else for two hundred miles, which was worse than any argument.

---

Petra got up, crossed to the crate shelf, and pulled down the biscuit tin.

Not biscuits. Never biscuits. Inside, wrapped in a plastic bag gone brittle with age: her father's last letter home, postmarked three weeks before the expedition's final flight. She'd read it maybe fifty times in her life, the first dozen sobbing, the rest like taking a vaccination—small controlled doses of the same wound. She knew it word for word and read it anyway, because grief doesn't want information, it wants ritual.

*...Vane says the geology doesn't match the reports Halvorsen's sending south. Says the ore values are being written up out of thin air to keep the concession money flowing. I told him to keep his head down but you know Elias. I'm flying the west leg again Thursday. If the weather holds we'll be done in ten days and I'll take you both to the coast, Petra-glass can have the whole beach...*

The letter ended there. Ordinary. A man planning a beach.

She put it back in the tin. Her hands were steady now, which is how she knew she was in trouble; they only went steady when the adrenaline had somewhere to go.

---

She spread the map flat and weighted the corners with four stones from the doorstep, and made herself look at it the way she'd look at an unfamiliar strip from the air—twice, slow, once for the shape and once for the traps.

The cache coordinates were marked in red pencil near a tributary confluence, annotated: *cache site — sealed drum — contents listed separately*. Contents listed separately. Elias had been specific about that on his deathbed under the tarp: *the map gets you there, but the drum tells you why*. She'd assumed gold. Everyone assumes gold. Prospectors die of gold the way pilots die of weather, and she'd filed his treasure under the same heading as everything else in her life—something valuable that belonged to someone else.

But now she looked at the route lines. The original 1989 survey track ran across the map in faded black ink, and Elias had added to it—not recently, the ink was the same age—but in places the black line simply *stopped*, mid-valley, west leg, and beside the stop was a small annotation in that beautiful cramped hand:

*J.S. departed here 14 June. No further contact.*

West leg. *I'm flying the west leg again Thursday.*

Petra sat back. The lamp flame leaned in some draft she couldn't feel, and for a moment the whole shack seemed to lean with it.

The map didn't just show where a cache was. It showed where her father had last been seen alive—or rather, last been *heard* from, which was a different thing and a crueler one. And Elias Vane had carried this knowledge for thirty-five years, drafted it, preserved it, guarded it—and in his last hours, dying in a stranger's airplane, he'd chosen to put it in *her* hands. Not mailed it. Not left it to a lawyer. Handed it to a bush pilot three payments behind on her own aircraft who happened to be James Sterling's daughter.

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He'd watched her name on the charter manifest, or heard it, or recognized her face—the Sterling jaw, her mother always said, you have your father's jaw—and he'd hired her specifically, paid double for silence, and died holding the one thing she'd spent her whole life not looking for.

*Tink,* said the drip on the plate.

"Shut up," Petra told it.

---

She inventoried her life the way she inventoried an aircraft, because that was the only method she trusted: coldly, item by item, no sentiment.

Fuel money: enough for one more fill and not two. Debt: fourteen months behind on the Beaver, repo agent already sniffing at the strip, probably a letter waiting in the Port Amara post office box right now with a date in it. Food: rice, beans, coffee, half a tin of condensed milk she was saving for no reason she could name. Prospects: none that didn't involve selling the plane, and selling the plane was selling her legs, and a woman without legs in this country was just scenery.

Against that ledger, a new entry: one promise to a dead man. Deliver the map to Marisol Vane, Port Amara, before the concession permits expired in five weeks. Five weeks. She turned the phrase over. Concession permits. Halvorsen Concessions—R. Halvorsen, Principal, printed right there in the cartouche, the same name, the same family, still carving up the same country thirty-five years later while her father lay in some unnamed valley because he'd *found something*.

She thought about the promise the way she thought about weather. There was the honest forecast and there was the forecast you wanted, and confusing the two killed people. The honest forecast said: this is not your problem. The honest forecast said: find the daughter, hand over the oilcloth, collect whatever gratitude converts to cash, pay the bank, stay invisible. That was the flight plan any sensible woman would file.

But her hands wouldn't agree to it. That was the trouble. She kept seeing Elias Vane's fingers on the map, trembling and precise, and hearing him say *contents listed separately* in that voice like gravel underwater, and understanding—with the specific dread of a woman reading her own instruments—that he hadn't given her a delivery job. He'd given her a heading. And headings, her father's own handwriting reminded her from the manual's flyleaf, are commitments: *always know your escape heading before you need it, Petra-glass*—and she had never once in her life known hers, because every escape heading she'd ever flown led away from the same coordinates, and those coordinates were a valley in the high country and a letter that ended mid-sentence about a beach.

---

Sleep didn't come. Around two in the morning she gave up on it, dressed, and went outside into the rain because the walls were too close.

She stood under the eave and watched water sheet off the tin roof into the mud, and let herself remember the funeral-that-wasn't—no body, no coffin, just a memorial service in a mission hall where everyone said *he's in a better place* and eleven-year-old Petra had thought, with a clarity that felt like sin: *he's in a specific place, and nobody went to look.* Search suspended. Those two words had built the shack she lived in. Every year of invisibility, every cash job, every debt, every night alone with a drip hitting a tin plate—all of it founded on the premise that looking was closed, that the case was shut, that the sky keeps what it takes.

Elias Vane had just handed her a key to a door labeled *search resumed*.

And she was afraid. Not of enforcers or rivers or five-week deadlines—she'd live through those or she wouldn't. She was afraid of the other outcome. That she'd go to that valley, or send someone, or learn something, and the answer would be ordinary. Pilot error. Weather. The story she already owned, confirmed at last, closing the account forever. Because as long as the question was open, some part of her father was still flying, still out there past the last radio contact, still coming home—and she had built her entire survival on never landing that plane.

"Tough," she said aloud, to herself, in the rain. "You don't get the luxury."

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't argue with: Elias Vane had spent thirty-five years carrying her father's name alongside his own secret, and in his final hours he'd trusted it all to her. She owed money—that was simple, that was survivable, that was Tuesday. But she owed a dead man a delivery, and debts to the dead accrued interest no bank could forgive. His daughter was out there not knowing her father had died with her name in his mouth. Her own father was out there not knowing—had never known—whether anyone would ever come looking with honest eyes.

Petra went back inside, dried off, and cleared the table. She laid the map flat, weighted the corners, and began copying the route into her kneeboard notebook in her own hand—waypoints, bearings, the confluence, the red-penciled cache site, the annotation that read *J.S. departed here 14 June*. If the map got destroyed, stolen, soaked, seized—there would be a second copy, in her handwriting, in her head. Her father's rule, learned at nine years old with a pencil stub: *never trust one copy of anything that matters.*

By lamplight she worked until the kerosene ran low, transcribing a dead man's lines, and somewhere past three in the morning she wrote the last item on a fresh page, plain and unadorned, the way you file a flight plan you're actually going to fly:

*Port Amara. Find Marisol Vane.*

She stared at the name a long moment. Then, beneath it, because stubbornness was the only inheritance she'd ever claimed outright, she added:

*Then ask her about the west leg.*

Outside, the rain kept working at the roof. But the drip over the window had stopped, the seam having finally soaked through to some new equilibrium, and the silence it left was louder than the ticking had been. Petra blew out the lamps and lay down on the cot fully clothed, boots on, the copied pages zipped inside her jacket on the hook by the door—ready, without quite admitting it, to move.

Five weeks. One daughter. One valley with her father's initials at the edge of it.

At dawn she'd wire Port Amara. At dawn she'd stop being a woman who ferried strangers and become, whether she liked it or not, a woman on a heading.

The first gray light was already seeping under the door when she finally slept, and in the last moment before it took her she was seven years old again, on her father's shoulders, above the crowd, seeing everything.
