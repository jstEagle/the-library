## 5. Men in Clean Boots

The rain had stopped an hour before they arrived, and Petra would remember that later—the way the world went too quiet, the way the puddles on her strip held the sky like small grey mirrors, and how the first thing she noticed about the two men was not their faces but their boots.

They were clean. That was the thing. Nobody came out to a bush strip forty miles past the last graded road with boots that clean. The mud here got into everything—into the treads of your soul, her father used to say—and these men's boots were dark leather, polished at the toe, unmarked by so much as a fleck of the red clay that stained everything else in the interior a permanent rust color. They stood at the edge of her strip beside a rented Land Cruiser with dealer plates still on it, and they looked like two accountants who had wandered off a golf course and gotten lost.

Petra was under the Beaver with a grease gun when she heard the crunch of gravel. She slid out on the creeper, blinking against the flat white light, and saw them standing there, one tall and narrow as a fence post, the other shorter, broader, with the kind of face that looked like it had been assembled from spare parts and then forgotten.

"Morning," the tall one said. He had a voice like dry paper. "Petra Sterling?"

She stayed on the creeper for a moment longer than necessary, which was a habit of hers—a way of telling people that her time was her own. Then she stood, wiped her hands on a rag that only made them dirtier, and walked over.

"Depends who's asking."

The tall one produced a card from his breast pocket with the smoothness of a magician producing a dove. *HALVORSEN CONCESSIONS LTD.* — *Resource Development. Community Partnership. Sustainable Futures.* Below that, embossed: **R. Calloway, Regional Liaison**.

Regional Liaison. Petra had lived out here long enough to know that companies didn't send liaisons into the bush. They sent lawyers to towns and muscle to the middle of nowhere, and whatever this man was, he'd been sent.

"Calloway," she read aloud. "That a real name or a job description?"

Something flickered behind the tall man's eyes—not anger, just a recalculation, the way a man recalculates when the terrain turns out rougher than the map suggested. "It's the name my mother gave me," he said mildly. "This is my colleague, Mr. Dree."

Mr. Dree did not offer a card. Mr. Dree offered nothing at all except a slow look around the strip—the shack with its rust-streaked tin roof, the fuel drums stacked three high, the windsock hanging limp and faded as an old dishrag, and beyond it all the wall of green that went on until the world curved. His gaze lingered on the hangar. On the padlock. On the Beaver's cowling, still open, tools laid out on a cloth like surgery.

"We're looking for a passenger," Calloway said. "Flew through here—" he consulted a small notebook, a gesture so bureaucratic it was almost funny out here where paper rotted in a week "—four days ago. Older gentleman. Ill. Flying to a mission outpost upriver of the Kessel confluence."

Petra felt something cold slide down between her shoulder blades, the way a drop of rain finds its way inside a collar no matter how you hunch.

"Flew through here," she repeated. "Lots of people fly through here."

"Not lots." Calloway smiled. It was a good smile, professionally maintained, the kind of smile that had never once reached his eyes. "Fuel records show a Sterling Air charter departing this strip four days ago, destination logged as the San Rafael mission. One passenger. We're very thorough, Ms. Sterling. It's what we do."

*Thorough.* She turned the word over and found hooks in it. Thorough men found things. Thorough men found oilcloth bundles and mission registers and old pilots who talked too much for the price of a drink.

"I don't discuss passengers," she said. "Medical confidentiality. Charter rules."

"Was it medical?"

"You'd have to ask him."

"We tried." Calloway's tone didn't change, but something underneath it did, some subsonic note that made the hair on Petra's arms lift. "He passed away, we understand. At the mission. Very sad. A man like that accumulates a great deal of... material, over a long career. Papers. Correspondence. Personal effects." He paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before naming a price. "We'd be prepared to make a generous payment for any such items that may have come into your possession. As a courtesy. As a gesture of goodwill between neighbors."

"Neighbors," Petra said. "Halvorsen's nearest operation is two hundred miles east."

"For now." The smile again. "The company has ambitious plans for this region. Development. Roads. Jobs." He said *jobs* the way other men said *God*, with the automatic reverence of someone reciting something they'd been paid to believe. "We find that relationships with local operators are... valuable. To both parties."

Petra looked at Dree. Dree was looking at the hangar again. Not idly. There was a particular quality to how he looked at it—the way a man looks at a lock he intends to open eventually, cataloguing the hasp, the hinges, the age of the wood. She had seen that look before, years ago, on repo agents and customs men and once, memorably, on a thief in Port Amara who'd taken the radio out of her Cessna while she ate lunch ten feet away.

She thought of the map rolled in oilcloth, wrapped in a shirt, shoved beneath the floorboards of her shack not six hours after Elias Vane had pressed it into her hands and made her swear. She thought of the promise, and of the five weeks ticking down on it, and of the telegram she'd already sent to Port Amara that might or might not find a woman who hated her father's name.

"I don't have anything of his," she said.

And there it was—the lie, coming out thin and quick, skidding across the morning like a stone across ice. She heard it herself. She was a bad liar and always had been; her father used to say she wore her tells like jewelry. Her eyes had gone, just for a fraction of a second, toward the shack. Toward the floorboards.

Calloway saw it. Of course he saw it. Men like Calloway were built out of noticing.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "Perhaps it'll turn up. Things do, out here. Turn up, I mean." He took the notebook back out, unhurried, and wrote something in it. "You know, Ms. Sterling, aviation is a difficult business. Fuel costs. Maintenance. Insurance premiums that would bankrupt a bishop." He glanced at the Beaver, at the patched fabric on the left aileron she hadn't gotten around to recovering yet. "A woman alone, running charters out of a strip like this—it must be hard to keep everything... current."

The word *current* landed on her like a hand on the back of her neck. Three payments behind. The notice folded in her flight bag, soft as cloth from handling. The repo agent in Port Amara who knew her schedule better than her own mother would have, if she'd had one worth mentioning.

"I manage," she said.

"I'm sure you do. For now." Calloway closed the notebook. "The offer stands. Any papers, any effects, anything the old man might have left behind—we pay well, and we ask no questions. And should you change your mind about larger matters..." He let his gaze travel over the strip again, appraising it now, valuing it, the way a man values a thing he expects to own. "...the company is always interested in acquiring local assets. Strips. Hangars. Operators willing to work within a structure."

"There it is," Petra said.

"Pardon?"

"The sales pitch. Took you a while to taxi around to it."

For the first time, Dree moved. It wasn't much—he shifted his weight from one immaculate boot to the other—but it carried a sudden coiled quality, like a hinge under load, and Petra understood that the big man was not decoration. Calloway raised one finger, barely, and Dree went still again.

"No pitch," Calloway said. "An invitation. Halvorsen prefers partners to obstacles. But I'll be honest with you, since you strike me as a woman who values honesty." He stepped closer, close enough that she could smell him—soap, wool, and underneath it something faintly chemical, like solvent. "The passenger you flew. Elias Vane. He spent thirty-five years interfering with this company's business. Thirty-five years of filing complaints, blocking surveys, writing letters to ministries, stirring up river communities against projects that would have fed thousands of families. A selfish old man, in short, who cared more about his grudges than about progress. He's dead now. Good. What we cannot abide is his dead hand still meddling. Papers can be dangerous, Ms. Sterling. Old papers especially. They mislead people. Get hopes up. And hopes, out here, get people hurt."

The rain started again. Softly at first, ticking on the tin roof of the shack, then harder, drumming on the Beaver's wing above them, running in sheets off the trailing edge. None of the men moved. Water beaded on Calloway's shoulders and he ignored it as if weather were a service provided for lesser people.

"So think about it," he said, raising his voice just enough to carry under the drumming. "Any papers. Fair price. No questions. Or—" and here the smile returned, wider, showing teeth that were very even and very white, "—or don't, and we'll all find out together what kind of neighbors we are. Dree will leave a number where you can reach us. Day or night. We're very responsive."

Dree produced a card, set it on the rail of the fuel stand, and weighted it down with a stone, precisely, deliberately, the way a man marks a claim.

Then they were walking back to the Land Cruiser, unhurried, boots pristine despite the mud that sucked at every other footprint on the strip. Calloway paused at the door and half-turned.

"One more thing. The daughter. Marisol Vane, Port Amara. Has she contacted you?"

The question came out of nowhere, or seemed to, and Petra's heart performed a maneuver she'd have failed her on in any checkride. "Who?"

"Mm." Calloway studied her for a long moment, rain streaming down his face, expression unreadable as a closed cockpit door. "No one. Forget I mentioned it."

The Land Cruiser pulled away through the puddles, throwing fans of brown water, and shrank down the track until the green swallowed it.

* * *

Petra stood in the rain for a long time after they were gone, letting it soak her through, because moving felt dangerous, like movement might confirm something.

Then she went to the shack, barred the door, and knelt on the floorboards with her heart going like a failing engine. The map was where she'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped in flannel, wedged beneath the joist beside the cache of ammunition and the photograph of her father she couldn't bring herself to hang. She sat back on her heels and stared at it.

*Any papers. Fair price. No questions.*

They knew about Elias. They knew about the flight, the mission, the death. They knew about Marisol—knew her name, her city, and were asking whether she'd been contacted, which meant they hadn't found her yet either, which meant the race Petra had told herself she wasn't running was, in fact, already underway, and she was losing.

And they knew about her debt. Calloway hadn't needed to say it outright; he'd said it in the grammar of the thing, in *current*, in *for now*, in the way he'd looked at the Beaver like a line item. Which meant Halvorsen had talked to the finance company, or owned it, or would soon, and the repo notice in her flight bag had just changed species. It wasn't a bill anymore. It was a leash.

She should take the money. Part of her—the part that had done the math on her fuel bills every night for three years, the part that lay awake listening to the roof tick—laid it out plainly: sell them the map, buy the plane free and clear, pocket the difference, forget the whole thing. Elias was dead. Promises to dead men were just stories people told themselves. The land office wouldn't miss one expired concession among hundreds. The river communities would go on being poor and patient, the way they'd always been, and Petra Sterling could go on being nobody, invisible, safe.

But she kept seeing the old man's hands on the oilcloth, cracked and yellowed and steady as bedrock, and hearing his voice through the ruin of his lungs: *Not the gold. Never was the gold. It's what's written down.*

And she kept seeing her father's initials on the survey crew list, inked sixty years ago by a man who had then walked into the green and never walked out, and the question that had been eating her alive since the night she'd unrolled the map: *what did he write down?*

Petra wrapped the map tighter, pushed it deeper, and dragged the trunk over the boards on top of it.

Then she did the thing fear demanded and defiance required: she packed. Fuel cans, tools, the good machete, dried food, the .303 and a box of shells, her father's compass with the cracked crystal. Enough to move fast and live rough. Because Calloway had seen her glance at the floorboards, and men like that didn't leave loose ends—they scheduled them.

By dusk the rain had settled into a steady monsoon-grey roar, and the strip was a lake with delusions. No flying tomorrow, maybe not for days. She stood in the doorway watching the water sheet off the tin roof and understood, with the clarity that comes only after the adrenaline drains out, exactly how badly she'd lied today, and what it had bought her: time measured in days, maybe hours.

Out past the tree line, headlights swept once through the rain. Slow. Deliberate. Watching.

Petra killed the lamp, loaded the .303, and sat in the dark with the map against her chest, doing the only math that mattered now: how far to Port Amara, how much fuel, how soon before the men in clean boots came back for what they'd decided was theirs.

First light, she decided. First light, she'd send a second telegram, and then she'd fly low and dirty through weather no sane pilot would touch, and she'd put this map in Marisol Vane's hands if she had to land on the street to do it.

The headlights swept again, closer this time.

Petra began, quietly, to plan.
