## 6. The Daughter in Port Amara

The telegram found Marisol Vane on a Tuesday, wedged under the wiper blade of a customer's trawler like a parking ticket.

She read it twice, standing in the yard of Amara Marine with diesel on her hands and a socket wrench still hooked in her back pocket, because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense the first time through.

ELIAS VANE DECEASED STOP I FLEW HIM OUT STOP HE LEFT SOMETHING FOR YOU STOP MUST DISCUSS IN PERSON STOP COME TO MISSION DOCK FRIDAY STOP — P. STERLING.

*Deceased.* The word sat there like a fishhook. She read it a third time and felt nothing she could name—no grief, exactly. Grief was for people you mourned. This was more like the sound a hull makes when it takes on water somewhere you can't see: a low, wrong note under everything, telling you the integrity of the whole structure had been compromised years ago and you'd just been pretending not to hear it.

"You going to stand there all day, or are you going to tell me why you look like somebody died?" asked Benny Okafor, the yard's owner, from under the hull of the *Cormorant*, where he'd been fighting a seized shaft bearing since morning.

"Somebody did." Marisol folded the telegram into quarters and shoved it in her coverall pocket. "Guy named Vane."

"Relative?"

"Something like that."

Benny slid out on his creeper, gray hair plastered to his forehead, and gave her the look he gave engines that had been run too hard by owners who wouldn't admit it. "The something-like-that kind of relative doesn't usually send telegrams through bush pilots, Mari."

"He didn't send it. Somebody else did." She turned back to the trawler, because the carburetor wasn't going to rebuild itself, and because her hands needed work the way other people's hands needed prayer. "Forget it."

She didn't forget it. That was the problem. All afternoon the telegram sat in her pocket like a stone, and the carburetor came apart under her fingers with a precision she wasn't feeling anywhere else in her body, and by the time the yard lights buzzed on against the early dark, she'd already argued with Elias Vane three times in her head and won all three.

He'd walked out when she was six. She had the whole architecture of it memorized: the duffel by the door, her mother's face turned to the window, the smell of the river on his jacket when he crouched down—she could still summon it, that smell, diesel and pine and something wilder underneath—and said, *Be good for your mother, little fish.* Little fish. As if a nickname could be a life raft. As if six years of being *little fish* could be cashed out later, decades later, by a telegram from a stranger.

He'd come back once, when she was nineteen. She knew because her mother told her, in the hospital, in the last week, the way dying people hand you the heavy things first because there's no time left for the light ones. *He came by the shop. You were out on a delivery. He sat in the waiting room for two hours and left a envelope.* Marisol had never opened the envelope. It was still in her mother's lockbox at the bank, and she paid the rental fee on that box every year with the same grim discipline she paid her tool insurance, and she had never once, in eleven years, asked to see what was inside.

So no. She owed Elias Vane nothing. The arithmetic was simple and she'd done it a thousand times and it always came out the same.

She went home to her room above the chandlery, ate cold rice out of the pot, and lay on her bed staring at the ceiling beams, and somewhere around midnight she admitted to herself that she was going to the mission dock on Friday. Not for him. For the *something*. If the old man had left money—and men like him, men who vanished into the interior for thirty years, usually hadn't, but if—then there might be back rent, and a new lathe for the shop, and maybe enough to buy out Benny's share of the yard before Halvorsen Concessions bought the whole waterfront out from under everyone and turned the harbor into a staging depot for their dredges. That was the story she told herself. Money, not answers. She was a mechanic; she believed in parts you could hold.

The lie held until Friday.

* * *

The mission dock was at the south end of the harbor, past the fuel barge and the fish cannery, where the water went the color of weak tea and the pilings wore a beard of green weed down to the low-tide line. Marisol walked out at four in the afternoon because arriving early would look like eagerness and arriving late would look like she didn't care, and she cared about looking like she didn't care more than she cared about almost anything.

The plane was tied up at the far end. A de Havilland Beaver, floatplane, paint gone chalky, a patch of fresh fabric on the left wing that hadn't been painted to match. Even from thirty meters Marisol's eye started cataloguing—corroded exhaust stack, tie-down lines rigged wrong, a drip under the number-two cylinder that meant the gasket was living on borrowed time. It was a working plane flown by someone who loved it and couldn't afford it. She knew the type. She *was* the type, one propeller over.

The woman standing beside it was younger than Marisol expected—late twenties, maybe—wiry, sun-browned, with a pilot's jacket that had been mended more times than the wing and dark hair scraped back in a tail that had given up on vanity long ago. She was pacing. Actually pacing, four steps and turn, four steps and turn, like a dog that had been left.

When she saw Marisol coming down the dock she stopped, and something went through her face too fast to read, and then she planted her feet the way you plant feet when you've decided to hold a position.

"Marisol Vane?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Petra Sterling." She crossed the distance with her hand out. Marisol looked at the hand, then at her, and didn't take it. Sterling let the hand fall without embarrassment, which Marisol grudgingly noted was either confidence or practice. "I flew your father out of the interior. Eleven days ago."

"And now you're flying telegrams around telling people he's dead. That's a hell of a business model."

Sterling's jaw tightened. "He was dying when I picked him up. Lung, from the look of it, and years of it. He knew. He paid me to fly him to the mission at Kessala so he could—" She stopped herself, recalibrated. "He didn't make it easy on himself at the end. I'll say that."

"Did he ask about me?"

The question came out before Marisol could stop it, and she hated it instantly, hated the nakedness of it, hated the way Sterling's expression flickered with something that wasn't quite pity. Pity she could have handled. This was worse. This was recognition.

"He asked me to find you," Sterling said. "That was the whole job. He traded me a map for the flight, and the map came with a condition: I deliver it to his daughter before the concession permits expire. Five weeks. That's why the telegram. That's why I'm here."

"A map." Marisol laughed, and it came out ugly. "A map. Thirty years of nothing—not a birthday card, not a phone call, my mother dies and he can't even send flowers—and now he's dead and he sends me a *map*. Like a treasure hunt. Like I'm eight years old and he's going to come home if I follow the clues."

"He wasn't well," Sterling said. "He was coughing blood into a rag in the back of my plane and he still spent the whole flight talking about—"

"Don't." Marisol's voice came out flat and hard, the voice she used on customers who tried to haggle over work she'd already done. "You knew him for one flight. I knew him for six years and then I knew him for thirty years of *nothing*, and I'm not going to stand on a rotten dock and listen to a stranger tell me what a complicated man he was. I've heard it. My mother heard it. Everybody always says it like it explains something."

Sterling was quiet for a moment. Out in the harbor a Halvorsen dredge barge groaned past, riding low, its floodlights already on against the dusk, and both women watched it with the involuntary attention of people who lived in the shadow of that company's money.

"I'm not going to tell you what kind of man he was," Sterling said finally. "I only met him once. But I'll tell you what I know, which is that he was scared. Not of dying. Of not getting there in time. He made me repeat your name back to him four times. Marisol. He had it right, no hesitation, like he'd said it to himself a lot over the years." She shrugged, awkward, a woman more comfortable with machines than sentences. "I don't know what that's worth to you. Probably nothing. But it's what I have."

Marisol looked at her. Really looked, the way she looked at an engine that came in making a sound the owner couldn't describe—listening past the obvious complaint for the real one underneath. And what was underneath Sterling's competence and her careful pilot's flatness was the same thing Marisol had seen in her own mirror for thirty years: a person holding a door shut with their shoulder and calling it standing guard.

"You're doing the same thing I do," Marisol said. "You've got your arms crossed over something and you're daring me to ask what it is."

Sterling blinked. "That's not—"

"Fine. Don't tell me. But here's what's going to happen." Marisol pulled the folded telegram from her pocket and held it up between two fingers. "I'm going to look at this map. Once. Because I want to know if there's money in it, because I'm three months behind on my own rent and the shop I work in is about to be swallowed by that barge you're staring at. And then I'm going to hand it back to you and wish you luck, and you're going to fly out of my harbor and that'll be the whole story. Elias Vane's daughter declines to participate in his final adventure. The end."

"And if it's not money?" Sterling said. "In the map."

"Then it's nothing."

"See, I thought that too." Sterling's voice dropped, and the flatness cracked just enough to show the thing under it. "I thought whatever he left me was nothing, just a job, just a debt. And then I looked at it closer. And I'm telling you, Vane—it's not nothing. I need another pair of eyes on it, and I need them to belong to somebody who's not me, because I've been staring at it for eleven days and I can't be objective anymore. That's the only reason I tracked you down. Not for him. For me." She hesitated, then, plainly forcing herself: "And because I think there's something in it about my father too. He went down in '89. A survey flight. Elias Vane was on that expedition."

The dock seemed to shift under Marisol's feet, the pilings creaking, the tea-colored water slapping black against the boards. She'd spent her whole life being the daughter of a man who left. It had never once occurred to her that the leaving had a shape, a direction, a *where*—that somewhere out there was a point on a map where her father's life had forked, and that other people had been standing at that fork their whole lives too, staring down the other road.

"Your father," Marisol said slowly.

"Thomas Sterling. He was the pilot. Elias was on his crew." Petra Sterling said the names like she'd only recently learned to say them in the same breath. "The expedition vanished. Nobody ever found the plane. Nobody ever found him."

Marisol looked at the chalky Beaver with its mismatched wing patch, at this woman who had crossed a territory to deliver a dead man's map and was now standing on a mission dock admitting, in the flattest voice available, that she'd been orphaned by the same silence that had raised Marisol.

The tide was going out. The weed on the pilings gleamed wet and dark. Somewhere behind the cannery a generator kicked on, and the Halvorsen barge's floodlights swept the water like a search party that had already given up and was just going through the motions.

"Tomorrow," Marisol said at last. "There's a place on Cordova Street that does coffee you could patch a hull with. Nine o'clock. You bring the map, I'll bring a straightedge and a magnifier, because if I'm doing this, I'm doing it properly." She held up a finger before Sterling could speak. "One time. One look. And if it's a treasure hunt, I walk, and you take your dead man's errand somewhere else."

Sterling nodded. She put her hand out again, and this time Marisol took it—briefly, callus against callus, the handshake of two people who worked for a living and trusted grip over words.

"Nine o'clock," Sterling said.

Marisol walked back up the dock without looking behind her, and she told herself the whole way that the stone in her chest was just the telegram, just paper and ink, just a dead man's handwriting reaching out of the ground to hook her one last time.

She was almost at the chandlery when she realized she'd forgotten to ask the only question that mattered: *if it wasn't money in that map, what did he think it was?*

She stopped under the flickering streetlight, hands in her pockets, and admitted the truth she'd been outrunning since Tuesday: she was going to Cordova Street at nine o'clock not for the money and not for the shop and not even for the stranger with the mended wing.

She was going because Elias Vane had said her name four times to a dying man's stranger, and she needed to know what else he'd said.

She needed to know what else was in the map.

She turned toward home, and behind her, out on the dark water, the Halvorsen floodlights swept the harbor one more time—and this time they seemed to pause, just for a second, on the chalky Beaver tied at the mission dock, as if the barge itself had taken an interest in who was asking questions.
