## 7. What the Oilcloth Hid

**Petra**

The telegram office in Port Amara smelled of mildew and carbon paper, and the clerk had looked at her like she was a stray dog asking for a table at a restaurant. *MARISOL VANE. MARINE MECHANIC. HARBOR DISTRICT.* That was all Petra had — a name Elias Vane had coughed out between wet, rattling breaths, along with the map and the promise.

Finding the woman had been easier than Petra expected. Finding her had been the easy part. Everything since — the slammed hatch cover, the words thrown like wrenches, the long silence on the dock while gulls screamed overhead — that was the hard part, and it wasn't finished yet.

Now they sat across from each other in Marisol's workshop, which was really half a boat shed with a workbench, a hot plate, and a single hanging bulb that swung whenever the harbor wind pushed through the gaps in the boards. The oilcloth bundle sat on the bench between them like a patient neither wanted to examine.

"You said he was dying when you flew him," Marisol said. She hadn't touched the bundle. Her hands were busy instead with a rag and a socket wrench she kept turning over, as if the conversation were something she could torque down and make behave.

"Coughing blood. He knew what it was. He'd been carrying it a while."

"And he didn't go to a hospital."

"He went to the interior. To finish something." Petra hesitated. "He paid me double to keep quiet about the flight. I think he didn't want anyone knowing where he'd been."

Marisol's laugh was short and ugly. "That sounds like him. Twenty years of not telling anyone anything, right up to the end." She set the wrench down. Picked it up again. "So why are you here? You got paid. The man's dead. Deliver the map to the next of kin — congratulations, you're looking at her. Consider it delivered."

"Because I read enough of it to know it isn't just coordinates." Petra reached for the bundle, then stopped, her fingers an inch from the oilcloth. "And because he made me promise. Not to deliver it. To deliver it *before the permits expire*. Five weeks. There's a claim attached to this, Ms. Vane, and if nobody files it, the land goes back into the open pool and men like Halvorsen swallow it by lunchtime."

The name landed. Petra watched it land — watched Marisol's jaw tighten, watched the rag stop moving.

"Halvorsen Concessions," Marisol said quietly. "They called the shop last month. Wanted to buy Papa's old survey papers. Said they'd pay 'generously for family documents.'" Her eyes came up, and for the first time there was something in them besides anger — something colder and more awake. "I told them he didn't have any family documents because he didn't have any family worth mentioning."

"They came to my strip too. Two men, clean boots, offering cash for 'any papers' a passenger might have left."

The wind shoved through the shed walls. The bulb swung. Shadows slid across the oilcloth bundle like hands reaching for it.

Marisol put the wrench down and did not pick it up again.

"All right," she said. "Open it. But understand something, Sterling — I'm not doing this for him. Whatever's under that cloth, I want to see it so I can know exactly how big the lie was. That's all."

"That's enough," Petra said. "For now, that's more than enough."

She untied the cord.

* * *

**Marisol**

The oilcloth came away in layers, stiff with age and smelling of paraffin and river mud, and underneath it the map unrolled across the workbench like something breathing out after holding its breath for thirty-five years.

It wasn't one map. It was three sheets, drawn on different paper, stitched together at the edges with waxed thread — a surveyor's field composite, the kind her father used to make before he stopped making anything at all. The top sheet was the cache: a hand-inked grid reference in the high country north of the Serrano watershed, annotated in Elias's cramped, slanted hand. *Dry cairn below twin falls. Iron box. Do not trust the 1991 resurvey.*

The middle sheet made her breath catch.

It was a route line. Not the published route — Marisol had grown up hearing about the famous '89 expedition the way other kids grew up hearing about saints, back when she still heard from him at all, back when the postcards came with survey-station return addresses and sentences like *proud of you, mija* that meant nothing because pride doesn't file child-support paperwork. The official route had run east along the Serrano headwaters. Everyone knew that. It was in the company histories, in the anniversary articles Halvorsen Concessions ran every decade like clockwork.

This route ran south. Through country no published account ever mentioned, down a corridor of unnamed tributaries, past a bend marked with a small, precise X and four words in her father's handwriting:

*Second plane. Two souls.*

"Papa drew this," she said, and hated how her voice cracked on the second word. "This is his drafting hand. I'd know it anywhere. He used to draw me treasure maps when I was six. This is — this is the same hand."

Petra leaned closer. Up close, Marisol could see the exhaustion on the pilot — the hollows under her eyes, the way she held herself like someone braced against weather that hadn't arrived yet. "Two souls," Petra repeated. "A plane went down in '89 that isn't in any record. And your father drew exactly where."

"Why would he hide it?" Marisol asked, though even as she asked it, she felt the answer forming in her chest like a bruise. "Why hide your own expedition's crash?"

"Maybe because it wasn't supposed to be findable." Petra's finger traced south along the route line, past the X, to where the ink changed character — became denser, more urgent, margins crowded with notes. "Look at this section. The notes aren't survey notes. They're — warnings. *Do not file this segment. Route falsified upstream. Coordinates shifted two kilometers east.*"

Marisol read it. Read it again. The shed seemed to get colder.

"He was saying the official maps were wrong," she said slowly. "Deliberately wrong."

"I think your father spent thirty-five years sitting on proof that somebody moved a map," Petra said. "And I think that's why he died alone in the interior with an oilcloth bundle and a stranger for a pilot."

* * *

**Petra**

They spread the third sheet under the lamp, and that was when Petra stopped being able to pretend this was someone else's inheritance.

The bottom sheet was older paper, water-stained, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. It was a fragment — a corner of a larger survey chart, torn along a fold line decades ago. And in the lower margin, in a hand she hadn't seen since she was nine years old but would know blindfolded, in the dark, underwater:

*R. STERLING — SURVEY CREW — FIELD VERIFICATION*

Her father's initials. R.S. Beside them, a date: 14 August 1989. Eleven days before the expedition's official end date. Eleven days before the record said everyone had come home safe.

Eleven days before Robert Sterling walked into the bush on a fuel run and never walked out.

"Petra." Marisol's voice came from far away. "Your face just went white as deck paint. What is it?"

"My father was on this crew." She heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone else. "Elias Vane told me a name on the flight up. Sterling. I told him he was mistaken. I told myself he was mistaken." Her finger hovered over the initials without touching them, the way you hover a hand over a stove you already know is hot. "There were two planes on that expedition. The support plane and the survey plane. The record says the support plane went down in '94, years later, mechanical failure, no survivors — that's the story. But this—" she tapped the X on the middle sheet, *second plane, two souls* — "this says otherwise. This says the second plane went down in '89, on a route that doesn't officially exist, carrying people who were never reported missing."

"Because they weren't lost," Marisol said. "They were erased."

The word hung in the lamplight. Erased. Petra thought of her mother's face at the funeral-that-wasn't, the closed casket of nothing, the insurance settlement that arrived suspiciously fast and suspiciously generous, the way her mother never spoke his name again. She thought of herself at nineteen, taking the only job that let her live above the cloud layer where nobody could ask questions. She thought: *I have been flying away from this map my entire life without knowing it existed.*

"The permits expire in five weeks," she said. "If we file the counter-claim with this documentation, the land gets protected. But that's not — Marisol, that's not the whole of it. This route passes within forty miles of where my father disappeared. If your father surveyed it, if he drew it, then he may have known what happened out there. He may have written it down."

Marisol was quiet for a long time. Outside, a foghorn moaned in the harbor, once, twice, like something large turning over in its sleep.

"You want to go," she said finally. "To the cache. Along this route."

"I promised Elias I'd deliver the map. The map says the claim has to be filed in person at the land office with the field journals as supporting evidence — he wrote that on the top sheet. *Claim void without original documentation.*" Petra met her eyes. "But it's your father's map. Your father's claim. I'm not going anywhere you haven't agreed to."

"Don't do that." Marisol's voice sharpened. "Don't hand me the decision like it's a courtesy. You've got a stake in this bigger than mine and we both know it now. Your father's initials are on that sheet. So don't stand there being noble."

"Fine." Petra exhaled. "Then yes. I want to go. For the claim, and for the truth, and because I've spent twenty years running from a question and I'm tired of paying rent on the fear."

* * *

**Marisol**

Marisol turned back to the middle sheet, to the route line threading south through country she'd never seen, and told herself a story. It was a good story, well-worn, the kind of story you could live inside for years if the walls held.

*I'm going for closure,* she told herself. *I'm going because a dying liar left a piece of paper and I want to see the lie with my own eyes. I'm going because Halvorsen wants this destroyed and I have spent my whole life watching that company take things — wages, rivers, my mother's health, my father — and I am done letting them take without a receipt.*

It was a good story. Almost good enough.

What she did not tell herself — what she kept in the sealed compartment at the bottom of her chest where she kept everything else about Elias Vane — was that the route line ended near a set of coordinates her father had mentioned exactly once, in a postcard when she was eleven, a postcard she still owned, hidden in a coffee tin with the others: *Someday I'll show you the falls where the water runs green, mija. Best place I ever surveyed.* A place he'd loved. A place he'd never shown her.

The cache sat below twin falls. She'd checked the annotation twice.

She poured coffee from the pot on the hot plate — burnt, bitter, the color of the harbor at low tide — and handed Petra a tin cup. Their fingers brushed on the handoff, and Marisol noticed the pilot's hands: scarred knuckles, a tremor that wasn't cold.

"Conditions," Marisol said. "One: we split everything fifty-fifty. Decisions, risk, whatever we find. Two: nobody dies being noble. Three—" she paused, and the truth slipped out sideways, the way truth does, "—three: when we find out what happened to the second plane, we say it out loud. No matter what it is. No editing. I've had thirty-five years of edited."

Petra wrapped both hands around the cup. "Agreed. All three." A pause. "The coffee's terrible."

"It's the best thing in this building."

"Then we're well matched." Petra almost smiled — the first time Marisol had seen anything on her face besides wariness and grief. "We leave tomorrow morning, ahead of that storm system building over the divide. Weather window's maybe eight hours. After that, monsoon season opens early this year, if the barometer's honest."

"You trust the barometer?"

"I trust that bad weather keeps bad company." Petra drained the cup, grimaced, and began rolling the map back into its oilcloth with the care of someone handling a reliquary. "Halvorsen's floatplane has been circling the harbor district twice today. They know the map exists. They may not know we have it yet, but they will."

Marisol looked at the bundle disappearing into its wrappings — her father's cramped handwriting vanishing fold by fold — and felt the strange, unwelcome sensation of a door opening onto a room she'd boarded shut decades ago.

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "I'll need an hour to close up the shop."

"What do you tell people?"

"Nothing. I've gotten good at leaving without explanations." She met Petra's eyes. "Family trait, apparently."

Outside, the foghorn sounded again, and somewhere over the black water a floatplane engine droned through the dark, patient as a shark, and neither woman said anything about it, because saying it would make it real, and they had one night left of pretending the journey would be simple.

Petra paused at the door of the shed, the oilcloth bundle under her arm.

"For what it's worth," she said, "he watched you grow up. In the mission letters. He wrote about you."

Marisol stood very still. "You read his letters?"

"There was a packet in his kit. I didn't read them — he asked me to burn them unread, and I did. But he talked. On the flight. About a daughter who could fix anything with an engine." Petra's voice was quiet. "He talked about you the way people talk about the north star. Like something they navigate by and can never reach."

The door closed behind her. Marisol stood alone in the swinging lamplight, surrounded by engines she understood and one man she never had, and told herself, one more time, firmly, *closure, just closure* — and began packing tools.
