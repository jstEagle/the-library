## 8. Departure Window

The storm showed itself first as a bruise on the western horizon, a smear of purple-black that hadn't been there at dawn. I stood on the apron at Port Amara's back field with my coffee going cold in one hand and the weather telex in the other, and I did the arithmetic every bush pilot knows by heart: distance, load, fuel, and the closing speed of a front that meant business.

Six hours, maybe five, before it ate the valley route.

"Storm's coming," Marisol said behind me. She'd said it three times that morning, each time like she was testing whether I'd heard, whether I'd change my mind, whether I'd give her an exit that wasn't there.

"Storm's coming," I agreed. "Which is why we leave now."

The Beaver sat on the floats at the dock, fueled to the tabs, loaded lighter than I'd have liked to fly into weather but heavier than I'd have liked to fly with a shadow on my tail. Two crates of supplies, the oilcloth map in the map case against my knee where it had lived for a week, and Marisol's duffel, which she'd packed like a woman expecting not to come back. I'd noticed that. I hadn't said anything. We were both fluent in the language of things left unsaid.

She climbed into the right seat without being asked, which was its own kind of statement. A week ago she'd have ridden in the back, arms crossed, as far from me and my dead man's map as the fuselage allowed. Now she settled in, pulled the harness tight, and put her hand on the door handle like she'd been flying floatplanes her whole life.

"Checklist," I said.

"You do it. I'll watch."

"You're going to learn it."

"I'm going to survive the flight. That's my whole ambition today."

I ran the checks anyway, talking through them because she was listening even if she pretended not to—mixture, props, flaps, controls free and correct—and then I looked west one more time. The bruise had spread. Lightning flickered inside it now, silent at this distance, like a lamp switching on and off in a house down the valley.

"Halvorsen's floatplane is still at the main dock," Marisol said. She'd been watching it all morning the way you watch a dog you don't trust. "Blue and white. Two men aboard since six."

"I saw."

"They're not going fishing."

"No," I said. "They're going to wait until we're airborne, and then they're going to follow us, and they're going to find out where we go, because that's worth more to them than the map itself. The map they can reprint. The destination they can't guess."

"So don't let them follow."

"That's the plan."

"And if the plan is stupid?"

"Then it's my stupid plan, and you're a passenger." I smiled at her, and to my surprise she almost smiled back, and that small almost was worth more than I let it show.

I swung the Beaver out into the channel, ran the engine up against the dock line, and when the gauges stayed green I turned her downwind and gave her the throttle. The hull slapped water, once, twice, the floats biting, and then we were off the lake and into the gray morning with the town falling away behind us and the storm front standing on the horizon like a wall someone had painted there.

I didn't climb. I stayed at two hundred feet, down in the murk where the river ran silver through the green, because altitude is visibility and visibility is a gift you don't give to a tail.

* * *

For the first forty minutes there was nothing behind us but our own wake dissolving on the water. Marisol navigated off the old survey sketch, calling bends in the river before they came, and I flew the map between her voice and the windshield. She was good at it. Better than good—she read terrain the way I read gauges, instinctively, her finger tracking the contour lines while her eyes checked the real country outside. A mechanic's mind. Her father's mind, though neither of us would have said so.

"There," she said, at the same moment I saw it.

The floatplane came out of the haze behind us and a thousand feet up, blue and white, holding our line with the lazy patience of something that knows it doesn't need to hurry. It didn't close the distance. It didn't have to. It just sat there in my mirrors like a debt.

"Same one from the dock," Marisol said.

"Same one." My mouth had gone dry. "Halvorsen's charter outfit runs two of these. I've seen the other one. This is the fast one."

"What's the plan, then? The stupid plan?"

I looked at the terrain ahead. The river bent north into the hills, and the hills closed in—the Kessel Narrows, the old surveyors called it, a slot of water between walls of black granite where the valley pinched down to a quarter mile and the fog pooled in the morning like milk in a bowl. No room to turn around in there. No room for a floatplane to maneuver above us, either, if we flew it low enough. Low enough was the whole trick. Low enough was also the whole risk.

"The map shows a canyon shortcut," I said. "Cuts two hours off the lake route. The survey crew used it in '89 because it was faster and because nobody else used it."

"And now nobody else uses it because—"

"Because it's a canyon with a river in it and fog on the walls and no second chances. But a floatplane can't follow us through it without painting its wingtips on the granite, and the pilot knows it."

Marisol looked at the storm, then at the shadow behind us, then at me. "And if he's a better pilot than you?"

"Then I'm going to have a very bad day."

I rolled into the turn toward the narrows, gentle, unhurried, the kind of turn that says *just following the river, nothing to see here.* Behind us, the floatplane adjusted its line to match. They'd bought it. Or they were letting me think they had.

The walls rose on either side. The fog came down to meet us.

* * *

Flying the Kessel Narrows is like threading a needle in a room where someone keeps turning the lights off. The river made three S-bends in the first mile, and the fog sat on the water in layers, and I flew it by memory and by the map and by the sound of the walls—by the way the engine note came back off the granite, telling me how close it was, the way a blind man reads a room by echo. Two hundred feet became one hundred. One hundred became fifty. The floats were down where the mist thickened, and the black walls slid past close enough that I could see the water stains running down the stone, the green moss in the seams, a hawk's nest wedged in a crack with the hawk standing on the rim watching us pass with what I swear was contempt.

Marisol had gone quiet. Not scared-quiet. Working-quiet. She had the map open on her knee and she called the bends: "Left, hard, then it opens—no, it doesn't open, it fakes, there's a wall past the point—right, then down the middle, the sketch shows a shoal on the left."

"You're reading sixty-year-old pen strokes," I said between bends, "drawn by a man who was probably drunk."

"He was my father."

"I know. I'm sorry. Left shoal it is."

The floatplane didn't follow. I checked the mirrors at every gap in the walls and the sky above us stayed empty, and by the third bend I understood why: they'd have to be insane. Or they'd climb, run ahead, and come down on the far side of the canyon to pick us up when we emerged. Which is exactly what I'd have done in their seat, which is why I flew the canyon the way I did—fast, committed, and out the far end before their climb-and-run could pay off.

The walls fell away. The river widened into a chain of lakes strung together like dropped beads, and the storm front was closer now, close enough to hear it—a low continuous grumble rolling along the underside of the clouds, the sound of a sky clearing its throat.

"We lost them," Marisol said.

"We bought time. That's not the same thing."

"How much time?"

"Depends on how good their pilot is." I climbed to a legal altitude, finally, breathing easier. "Their pilot is probably very good."

She looked at me sideways. "You're not going to tell me it'll be fine."

"It'll be fine or it won't. Telling you doesn't change the odds. I'd rather spend the words on something useful. There's a lake ahead, Lac Verre, the map shows an old survey camp on the east shore. We'll stop there, check the fuel, eat something, and push on before the front hits. We should make the cache by tomorrow midday if—"

I never finished the sentence, because that was the moment the engine coughed.

* * *

A cough from a radial engine can be nothing. Water in the fuel. A fouled plug. A hiccup. I'd flown eleven years with a coughing engine and learned to hear the difference between *nothing* and *something,* and this was *something*—a stutter in the rhythm, a missed beat, then another, the kind of irregularity that makes your hands go cold before your brain catches up.

"Fuel pressure's low," I said, already switching tanks, already running the drill. "We picked up water or debris at the dock, or the boost pump's dying. Either way—"

The engine caught again, ran rough for ten seconds, smoothed out. Then rough again. Then it caught with a bang that I felt in my teeth.

"Lac Verre," Marisol said. Not a question. She'd read it in my face.

"Lac Verre. East shore, the old camp. It's a mile. Maybe two."

The engine ran long enough to give me hope, which is the cruelest thing an engine can do. Then it gave a shudder like a dying animal and the power fell away in steps, and I put the nose down and traded altitude for distance and picked my water. The lake came up gray-green through the mist, and I could see the east shore, and I could see that the old survey camp was a ruin of two collapsed tents and a dock that had mostly rotted to matchsticks, and I could see—too late, always too late—that the wind had come around with the front and was putting a chop on the water that the glassy-calm morning had promised would never exist.

"Flaps," I said. "Brace."

"I'm not—"

"Brace."

She braced. The hull hit the first swell hard enough to slam my spine, skipped, hit again, and the second impact came with a sound from the nose that I will hear for the rest of my life—a crack, a grind, the sound of metal meeting water at an angle metal was not asked to meet it at. The Beaver slewed left, dug a float, and for one long second we were sideways and slowing and I was certain we were going over, and then she settled, wallowing, and the engine died with a wheeze, and there was nothing but the rain starting to hit the windshield and our own wake rolling out behind us and the sound of my own breathing.

"Are you hurt?" I said.

"No." Marisol's voice was steady. Her hands, on the armrests, were not. "You?"

"Ask me in a minute."

I got out on the float in the building rain and walked the length of the hull with the lake slapping at my boots, and I found it at the nose of the left float's strut line: the propeller. The tip of one blade was bent forward, a smooth curl of aluminum like a peeled apple skin, maybe two inches of it, and the blade behind it was nicked and scarred where it had kissed the water on the second impact. The engine had thrown the bent blade out of balance and shaken itself into the fuel-pressure fault, or the fault had caused the landing, or both. It didn't matter. The math mattered.

I stood in the rain and did the math.

A bent prop tip on a Beaver is not a death sentence. It's a death sentence *here.* You can straighten a tip with a hammer and a block of wood if you're desperate and lucky—Marisol might manage it, Marisol could probably manage anything with metal—but a blade that's been bent and hammered straight is a blade that will shake the engine apart at cruise power, and the nearest propeller that fits a Pratt & Whitney R-985 was in Port Amara, six hours' flight and a storm front away, on the far side of the canyon we'd just flown to escape a floatplane that would be looking for exactly that.

"We're grounded," Marisol said. She'd come out on the float beside me, hair plastered to her face, staring at the bent metal like it had personally betrayed her.

"We're grounded."

"For how long?"

"Until the front passes. A day, maybe two, if the rain lets the lake drop and the wind lays down enough to work on the prop. Then we fly out on a propeller I've straightened with a rock, or we don't fly out at all."

She was quiet for a moment. The rain came harder, drumming on the hull, filling the silence.

"And the floatplane?"

"Will have watched the weather too. They'll know we're down somewhere between the narrows and the cache. They'll be looking." I looked east, toward where the cache lay hidden under the map's careful ink, and then west, toward the storm, and understood that I'd put us in the one place that was both. "They'll be looking."

Marisol climbed back into the cabin and came out with the tarp and the tool roll, and she knelt on the wet float in the rain and opened the tool roll like a surgeon laying out instruments, and when she spoke her voice had changed—harder, calmer, the voice of a woman who fixes things because fixing things is the only prayer she trusts.

"Then we'd better be gone before they find us. Hand me the hammer."

I handed her the hammer. Behind us, the storm rolled in over the hills, and somewhere inside it, I was sure, a blue-and-white floatplane was turning toward the sound of our mistake.
