## 9. Grounded

The lake had a name on no map I'd ever seen, which seemed fitting, because the plane in it was named too—*Wren*, stenciled on the tail in paint gone chalky—and it sat half-sunk to its wheel struts like a bird that had misjudged the ice. The propeller told the rest of the story. One blade curled at the tip like a fern frond stepped on by something heavy.

"You landed on gravel," I said.

"I landed on *water*," Petra said. "The water was lying about being water."

We'd been stranded for six hours. Long enough for me to learn three things about Petra Sterling: she carried a tool roll that would embarrass a machine shop, she swore in a low, conversational register as though swearing were a form of navigation, and she had not once looked directly at the bent propeller without her jaw tightening, which meant she'd already done the math and hated the answer.

I did my own math. The tip was chewed, not cracked—the aluminum had folded rather than split, which was the difference between a bad afternoon and a funeral. You could dress a folded tip. You could file it back to profile, balance the blade against its twin, and fly out of here at reduced RPM praying the whole way, which described roughly ninety percent of every engine I'd ever loved.

"Give me the file," I said.

She handed it over without argument, which told me more than any speech could have. A person who hands over their tools to a stranger they met two days ago is either desperate or paying attention. With Petra I suspected both ran on the same fuel line.

I waded in. The lake was snowmelt-cold even under the high summer sun, cold enough to make my forearms ache, and I worked with the blade braced against my knee while Petra stood on the float and held a tarp overhead against a sky that couldn't decide whether to threaten rain or just sulk about it. The filing itself was simple work, meditative almost—stroke, check, stroke, check—but the silence between us kept snagging on things.

On the flight up here she'd been all business: headings, fuel burn, weather cells stacking on the horizon like furniture being moved upstairs. On foot, following the map's old route to the wreck yesterday, she'd been all caution: hand signals, sightlines, that habit of hers of standing where two exits were always visible. But now there was nothing left to do but file aluminum and wait for weather, and the silence started asking questions neither of us wanted to answer.

"Your father flew this route," I said, because apparently I was going to be the one who broke first. "In '89. And you never went looking."

Her jaw did the tightening thing again. "It's a big country."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I've got." She shifted the tarp; a bead of water ran off the corner and hit the float with a sound like a clock ticking. "You want to know why I didn't go looking? Because looking is how you find things. And I've spent thirty years making sure I don't find anything."

I turned the blade, checked my angle against the light. "That's the stupidest sentence I've ever heard from someone who flies airplanes for a living. Flying is literally finding things. Finding runways, finding weather holes, finding—"

"Finding people who need finding." She said it flatly, like reading off a manifest. "Yeah. I know what flying is. I just prefer the version where nobody's waiting at the other end."

I stopped filing. Water dripped off my elbows into the lake, each drop a small cold punctuation. "Somebody's waiting at the other end right now," I said. "Me. I'm the somebody. In case you've forgotten the entire reason we're doing this."

"I haven't forgotten." Something moved behind her eyes—not guilt exactly, but the shape guilt leaves when it's been lived with so long it stops being a guest and starts being furniture. "Believe me. I count everything I owe. It's the only arithmetic I'm good at anymore."

* * *

We broke for food around what I guessed was midafternoon, though the sky had gone the color of wet slate and made a liar out of the sun. Petra produced tinned fish, hard biscuits, and a thermos of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration. We ate sitting on the floats with our boots dangling over the water, and I watched her watch the tree line the way other people watch a door in an argument.

"Say it," I said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you keep almost saying. You've got a whole speech loaded behind your teeth. I can see the hammer cocking every time you look at me."

She laughed—one short, surprised note, quickly put away. "You're your father's daughter. He did the same thing. Read a room like a chart."

"Don't." The word came out harder than I intended, and I let it stay hard. "Don't do the thing where you tell me what he was like. You knew him for—what, a few weeks? I knew him for zero weeks. Neither of us gets to be the expert."

"Fair." She turned the thermos cup in her hands, around and around. Then, quietly, in the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge: "Can I tell you something instead? About me?"

The change in register caught me off guard. I'd spent two days cataloguing Petra Sterling—competent, closed, allergic to questions—and here she was opening a door herself. I nodded.

"I owe eleven thousand dollars on this plane," she said. "Eleven thousand and some change, plus penalties. Three payments behind. There's a man named Ostrander who works for the finance company, and his whole job is showing up at strips like this one and taking airplanes away from people who are one bad season from finished. He came for the Wren four days before Elias walked into my life. I talked him off it with a story about a charter contract I didn't have. Then I took a dying man's money to fly him somewhere quiet, and I told myself it was mercy, and it was also rent." She looked out at the middle distance, at nothing. "Every good thing I've done in the last three years has had a bill attached. I've gotten so I can't tell the difference anymore."

The lake lapped at the floats. Somewhere across the water a loon called, that long descending note that sounds like grief if you're in the mood for it.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because you asked why I don't look for things." She shrugged, a small motion, like setting down something heavy for just a moment. "This is what I find when I look. Debt and a name I use wrong. Sterling isn't even—it was my mother's name. My father's name I dropped the day I turned eighteen, same week I learned he wasn't coming back. I built a whole person out of the parts that were left, and the person works fine as long as nobody checks the paperwork." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were steady in a way that cost her something; I could see the cost. "And then a dying man hands me a map with my father's initials on it, and suddenly the paperwork's getting checked whether I like it or not."

I should have said something generous. That was the moment for it—I knew that even then. Instead what came out of me was the thing I'd been carrying since Port Amara, since the telegram, since the dock:

"He wrote letters."

Petra went still.

"Elias. To the mission, mostly—Father Anselm told me when I tracked down the records. Monthly, for years. I read three of them. Do you know what they said?" My voice was doing something I didn't authorize, going thin and fast. "'My daughter is well. My daughter is strong. Tell my daughter I think of her.' Third person. Like I was a report he filed. Twenty-six years of thinking-of-her and not one envelope with my actual address on it. Not one." I hurled a pebble at the water; it skipped twice and sank, which felt about right. "So when you say you build a person out of leftover parts—at least yours left on purpose. Mine got abandoned by a man who thought about me the way you think about weather. From a distance. With concern."

"That's not—" She stopped herself. Started again, slower. "Marisol. The mission letters. Did Father Anselm say who they were addressed to?"

"The mission. Obviously."

"Did he say *why* the mission?"

"No. Why does it matter?"

Petra was quiet long enough that the loon called again, closer now. When she spoke, her voice had gone careful, the way mine went careful around a seized bearing—you didn't force it, you eased it. "Elias paid for my schoolbooks. Did I mention that? When I was nine, ten. My mother scraped at the mission clinic and there was a pilot who came through every couple months with supplies, and somehow there was always a little extra in the supply crate. Paper. Pencils. Once, a secondhand flight manual that I still have." She turned the cup again. "He watched me grow up, Marisol. From the air, from the margins. I think maybe writing to you directly was the one thing he couldn't make himself do, so he did the next thing instead. It doesn't excuse it. But it might explain it."

"And you know this how? Because you were there? Because you were the girl with the schoolbooks?"

"Because I was the girl with the schoolbooks." Her mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "Neither of us got the version of him we needed. We just got different wrong versions."

* * *

I went back to the prop because my hands needed a job that wasn't shaking. The light was failing and I worked by feel and by the small flashlight Petra held in her teeth, and the absurdity of it—two women crouched on a float in the dark, dressing a wound in a piece of metal—cracked something loose in my chest that might have been laughter or might have been the other thing.

"Balance check," I said finally, sitting back. My knees were soaked, my fingers were cut in three places, and the dressed tip looked, in flashlight glow, like a scar that would hold. "It'll fly. Not fast, not high, and if you spin it past twenty-two hundred RPM it'll shake itself apart and take us with it. But it'll fly."

Petra took the flashlight out of her mouth. "How do you know how to do that? Marine engines aren't props."

"Boats teach you everything teaches you everything. Corrosion is corrosion. Imbalance is imbalance. Everything that goes in water or air is just fighting the same three enemies." I wrapped the file back in its cloth. "My turn for a confession, since we're trading."

"We're trading?"

"Aren't we? That's what this has been. You gave me the debt thing, I gave you the letters thing. Vulnerability for vulnerability. Fair market rate." I heard myself and winced, but I didn't stop, because stopping was the family disease and I was tired of inheriting it. "I came on this trip telling myself it was about closure. Closure—that great word people use when they mean *I want to yell at a dead man*. And then we found the wreck yesterday, and I stood in that clearing looking at sixty-year-old aluminum, and you know what I felt? Nothing. No closure. Just cold feet and mosquitoes. Because closure was never the point. The point is I don't know how to be near anyone. He taught me that by example, and I learned it so well I've run a boat shop for eight years where the longest conversation I have in a day is 'she'll be ready Thursday.'"

The words hung there over the black water. I hadn't planned any of them. They'd just been sitting in me, pressurized, and the night had found the seam.

Petra didn't offer comfort, which I ended up being grateful for. What she offered was better: her own truth, again, deeper down.

"I stay invisible," she said, "because the last time I was visible—if you have a name, a face, a plane people recognize—people can find you. And the people who found my father found him because he was visible. He filed surveys. He signed reports. He existed on paper, and paper is how they erased him." She looked at the Wren, at the patched prop catching the last gray light. "So I fly cargo at dawn. I pay cash. I keep my head down and my tail number dirty. And I told myself that was survival, but honestly? Some nights I think it's just hiding with a schedule."

The loon again, nearer, its cry sliding across the lake and breaking apart against the far shore.

"So what happens," I said, "when we get to the land office? File a claim in both our names? That's the opposite of invisible. That's signing your name on the loudest piece of paper in the territory."

"I know." She said it simply, without drama, and I understood that she'd already done this math too, days ago probably, somewhere over the canyon with Halvorsen's floatplane in her mirrors. "I know exactly what it costs. I'm going to pay it anyway."

I looked at her—this woman I'd known for three days, this stranger whose father had bought my schoolbooks, whose debts and dodges and stubbornness fit against mine like mating flanges—and I felt the strange vertigo of recognizing yourself in someone you have no history with. We were both running. We'd just picked opposite directions, and the map had thrown us together, and now the river was going to make us run the same way or drown trying.

"Thursday," I said.

"What?"

"My boat shop. 'She'll be ready Thursday.' That's the longest conversation I have most days." I stood, joints cracking, and offered her a hand up onto the float. "Starting tomorrow, we're going to need longer conversations. Weather's coming, that prop's held together with prayer and a file, and there's a river downstream with our names on it. So. Longer conversations."

She took my hand. Her grip was calloused and certain, and for a second neither of us let go, and the loon cried once more, and then we let go, because there was work to do and a storm building in the west like a debt coming due.

Later, bedded down under the wing with the tarp pulled tight, I heard her voice in the dark, barely above the wind off the water.

"Marisol. The margin notes on the map. Have you actually read them? All of them?"

"Some. Why?"

A pause long enough to worry me.

"Because there's something in them," Petra said, "that I think was written for you. And I don't think I'm the one who should show you."

Across the lake, thunder cleared its throat, and the first fat drops began ticking against the tarp like a countdown.
